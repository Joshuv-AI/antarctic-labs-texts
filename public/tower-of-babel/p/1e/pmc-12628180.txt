A phase 1B trial of vocimagene amiretrorepvec in patients with advanced solid tumors: Safety, tumor homing, and immune modulatory effects
Authors: Jaime R. Merchan, Jordi Rodon, Shree Venkat, Arthur Donahue, Amber Thomassen, William P. Accomando, Maria Rodriguez-Aguirre, Deukwoo Kwon, Cornelia Bentley, Daniel J. Hogan, Olivia Roos Rodrigues, Sharon Yavrom, Derek Ostertag, Jolene Shorr, Gerald S. Falchook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vocimagene amiretrorepvec (Toca 511) is a replicating retroviral vector (RRV) encoding cytosine deaminase that converts 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC) into 5-fluorouracil in the tumor microenvironment. This study investigated the safety and immunomodulatory effects of systemically administered Toca 511 and Toca FC in advanced cancer patients. Eligible subjects received three daily intravenous (i.v.) injections of Toca 511, followed by intratumoral (i.t.) injection of Toca 511, and a 7-day course of oral Toca FC given every 4–6 weeks. Among 21 enrolled patients, the most common treatment-related adverse events were diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and fever. Correlative studies confirmed tumor homing after intravenous virus administration. Addition of Toca FC reduced tumor myeloid cells, Tregs, and exhausted T cells, and increased CD8 T cells. Systemically, a shift in T cells from naive to effector phenotypes, expansion of CD4 memory T cells, and increases in B cells were observed. Treatment was associated with a disease control rate of 61% and median overall survival of 9.6 months. The above results confirm feasibility of systemic administration of Toca 511 in combination with oral Toca FC, successful tumor targeting, and tumor and systemic immunomodulation. Further investigation of Toca 511/Toca FC in combination with checkpoint inhibitors and/or targeted agents is warranted.

Introduction

There is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic options for patients with advanced, refractory solid tumors. Viral and gene therapy approaches are under development and show great promise, as they can be engineered to selectively target tumor over normal tissues and “armed” to deliver therapeutic payloads for therapeutic gain.1,2 Vocimagene amiretrorepvec (Toca 511) is a replicating retroviral vector (RRV) derived from a cloned Moloney murine leukemia virus, pseudo-typed with the amphotropic envelope gene, enabling the virus to infect human cells.3 Toca 511 selectively targets tumors by infecting actively dividing cells,4,5 with viral replication further restricted by innate and adaptive immune responses, defective in malignant cells but intact in normal tissues.4 Additionally, a modified yeast cytosine deaminase (FCY1) gene has been inserted into this vector that functions as a cell killing component.5 Toca 511-induced expression of cytosine deaminase (CD) catalyzes the conversion of the prodrug Toca FC (5-fluorocytosine [5-FC]), to the antineoplastic drug, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) within infected tumor cells. Preclinical studies in xenograft and syngeneic immunocompetent models of brain and colon cancer have demonstrated significant antitumor activity and long-lasting antitumor immune responses in mice treated with Toca 511 and Toca FC.6,7 These responses have been associated with decreases in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages, with subsequent influx of effector T cells within the tumor.7,8

Locally administered Toca 511 in combination with Toca FC has demonstrated safety and early evidence of antitumor activity in patients with advanced high-grade glioma. In a phase 1 trial reported by Cloughesy et al., Toca 511 administered directly into the resection cavity wall during surgery, followed by Toca FC given orally in patients with recurrent high-grade gliomas, was associated with durable complete responses.9 A recently published phase 3 trial of Toca 511/Toca FC in recurrent high-grade glioma did not meet the study’s primary endpoint of overall survival (OS) in the intention-to-treat population.10 A subgroup analysis of this trial suggests a possible survival benefit in patients at second recurrence.3,10 The combination of Toca 511 with Toca FC is now under development by Denovo Biopharma as DB107-RRV and DB107-FC. Two clinical trials are actively recruiting, including a biomarker-driven study in patients with recurrent high-grade glioma patients (NCT06264388) and a trial in newly diagnosed high-grade glioma patients (NCT06504381).

While previous reports have demonstrated feasibility of local administration of Toca 511 in high-grade gliomas, the clinical and biological effects of systemically administered Toca 511/Toca FC in patients with advanced solid tumors (outside high-grade gliomas) have not been characterized. The current study reports on the safety, feasibility, and pharmacodynamics of systemic and local Toca 511 administration in combination with Toca FC in patients with advanced solid tumors.

Safety profile of systemic Toca 511 and Toca FC administration

i.v. administration of Toca 511 was well tolerated. Seventeen patients received Toca 511 intratumor administration during procedure 1 (Figure 1). The most common sites of intratumoral virus administration were metastatic sites in the liver (11/17) and abdominal wall (3/17). Other sites (one each) included lymph node, lung, and kidney. The most common organ system adverse events (AEs) related to Toca 511 or Toca FC (Table 2) were gastrointestinal, with diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain grades 1 and 2. Other common grade 1/2 AEs included pyrexia, fatigue, chills, and decreased appetite. Two patients experienced a serious AE of colitis grade 3, related to Toca FC. No grade 4 or 5 treatment-related toxicities were observed. No clinically significant hematologic toxicity was observed in the treated individuals. Seventeen of the patients had blood collected once at two time points (pre and post), and six completed all five time point collections. Mononuclear cells were isolated from peripheral blood for flow cytometry analysis of the major lymphocytes and monocytes subsets, as well as a panel to focus on T cells. Clinical activity

Evaluation of efficacy was not the primary objective of the study, and patients were allowed to receive standard anticancer therapies in combination with Toca FC after the completion of cycle 2 (Figure 1). Of the 18/21 patients eligible for efficacy evaluation (3 patients did not have post baseline imaging), best response per RECIST 1.1 was partial response for one patient, stable disease for ten patients, and progressive disease for seven patients (Table 3). The disease control rate was 61.1%. Median progression-free survival (PFS) was 4.6 months (95% CI, 3.0–10.9), and median OS was 9.6 months (95% CI, 6.3–16.4) months. A swimmer plot depicting duration on study for all enrolled patients, as well as PFS and OS, is shown in Figure 5. Among patients with prolonged benefit from treatment were three patients with KRAS wild-type metastatic CRC, who progressed on ≥ 3 lines of treatment, including prior anti-EGFR antibody therapy. Discussion

While advances in targeted and immunotherapies have significantly improved clinical outcomes in advanced cancer, many patients deriving initial benefit from these treatments eventually develop treatment resistance and succumb to progressive disease. This underscores the urgent need to develop novel strategies to overcome such resistance and improve treatment outcomes. Viral gene therapy strategies represent promising therapeutic interventions with the potential to fill this gap. Retroviral-based gene therapy (including lentiviral) strategies are being used in the development of cellular (e.g., CAR-T cells) therapies and for the treatment of non-oncological diseases.2,11,12,13,14 Toca 511 is an RRV expressing the suicide gene CD, which, in combination with Toca FC, has demonstrated tolerability and clinical activity after i.t. administration in high-grade glioma patients.3,10,15,16 The clinical and biological effects of systemically administered Toca 511 and Toca FC in patients with solid tumors have not been previously characterized. In this report, we present the results of a phase 1b, multicenter, open-label clinical trial of systemically administered Toca 511 and Toca FC in patients with advanced solid tumors.

Several important clinical and translational observations can be derived from this study. First, we demonstrate for the first time that i.v. administration of Toca 511 in solid tumor patients is feasible. Patients tolerated three i.v. infusions of Toca 511 followed by oral Toca FC, with the most common AEs being grade 1 and 2 diarrhea, nausea, fever, chills, and fatigue. The majority of events were considered mild to moderate in severity. No infusion reactions were observed, and no grade 4 or 5 treatment-related AEs were reported. Subsequent i.t. Toca 511 administration was well tolerated without significant procedure-related AEs. Importantly, no significant hematologic toxicity or immunosuppression was observed in the treated patients. This offers a potential therapeutic advantage of this treatment (through preservation of endogenous antitumor immunity), allowing Toca511/Toca FC to be combined with checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapy strategies.

The safety and feasibility of i.v. and i.t. administration of Toca 511 and Toca FCs in solid tumor patients is encouraging and opens the door for future studies using systemically administered Toca 511 and Toca FC in combination with other anticancer therapies. Our data are consistent with previous reports that demonstrated safety of non-replicating and RRVs administered locally or systemically.17,18,19,20

Second, successful virus-tumor targeting after i.v. administration was demonstrated by positive Gag signal on tumor biopsies (Figure 2). In addition to confirming consistent viral delivery via i.v. injection, we also observed a decrease in Gag+ staining between the first and second biopsies, suggesting destruction of viral-infected tumor cells likely as a result of Toca FC administration and conversion to 5-FU. While prior clinical studies have shown tumor targeting after systemically administered oncolytic viral vectors, such as adenovirus, vaccinia, coxsackie, and reovirus,21,22,23,24 the current report is the first, to our knowledge, to demonstrate successful tumor distribution of a systemically administered RRV in patients with solid tumors.

Correlative tumor studies demonstrated that Toca 511/Toca FC treatment modulated the local tumor microenvironment, as evidenced by a generalized reduction in CD11b+ cells (innate immune myeloid cell marker association with immune suppression) between first and second biopsies (Figure 2). Additionally, in patients with decreased Tregs, there was a concomitant increase in CD8+ T cells and a reduction of exhausted T cells, consistent with enhanced immune activation following treatment (Figure 3). These findings align with preclinical studies showing that Toca 511/Toca FC reduces MDSCs through intratumoral conversion of 5-fluorocytosine to 5-fluorouracil via CD activity.6,7 Given that anti-PD-1 therapy, while enhancing effector T cell responses, can also lead to Treg expansion or activation in certain tumor settings25,26 potentially limiting its efficacy, the ability of Toca 511/Toca FC to deplete immunosuppressive cell populations such as Tregs and MDSCs may help overcome this limitation and improve the therapeutic activity of anti-PD-1-based combinations, as supported by recent preclinical data.27

Peripheral blood immunophenotypic studies showed that Toca 511 and Toca FC induced a systemic immunomodulatory response (Figure 4), including T cell shifts from naive to effector phenotypes, suggesting that such immune cells are encountering their target antigens: expansion of CD4+ memory T cells creating a memory for tumor antigens to enable more rapid immune responses in the future, and increased quantities of B cells, suggesting that the body is preparing for antibody production. The local and systemic immunomodulatory effects observed after Toca 511 and Toca FC treatment support the concept of viral gene therapy-mediated immunomodulation, which may be further enhanced by combinatorial therapies using checkpoint inhibitors and other new-generation immunotherapeutic agents.

While treatment efficacy was not a primary endpoint in this phase 1 study, encouraging clinical activity was observed in this heavily pretreated patient cohort, with one patient achieving a partial response and ten achieving stable disease. This clinical benefit was translated into a median OS of 9.6 months in the treated-patient cohort. Of interest, prolonged benefit was observed in selected patients with treatment-refractory (including anti-EGFR-based therapies) KRAS wild-type CRC, upon initiation of standard panitumumab in combination with Toca FC (allowed by the protocol, as in materials and methods). This early evidence of clinical benefit is encouraging and warrants further characterization of this regimen in future clinical studies in CRC and other tumor types.

This study has several limitations, including the sample size (n = 21), with 18 patients evaluable for response), and a heterogeneous patient population, limiting our ability to make firm conclusions regarding the clinical activity of this combination. The correlative findings, while noteworthy, need to be further validated in future studies with larger numbers of patients, in other tumor types, and with other technologies to comprehensively analyze the effects of the treatment on the tumor microenvironment. Such studies will lead to a better understanding of the virus-tumor host interactions and mechanisms of immunomodulation. While the observed clinical activity, especially in heavily pretreated patients, is encouraging, these results are hypothesis generating and require confirmation in larger clinical studies using the viral vector-prodrug platform in combination with checkpoint inhibitors and/or targeted agents.

In conclusion, the current study is the first report demonstrating that systemically administered Toca 511 and Toca FC in patients with advanced solid tumors is safe and feasible. Viral tumor targeting and treatment-induced tumor and systemic immunomodulation were demonstrated, as well as early evidence of treatment benefit in heavily pretreated malignancies. The above results warrant further clinical evaluation of Toca 511 and Toca FC in combination with novel immunotherapies in patients with advanced solid tumors.

Materials and methods

Patient population

The study was conducted from July 2016 to December 2019 at the University of Miami Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center (Miami, FL), Sarah Cannon Research Institute (Denver, CO), and MD Anderson Cancer Center (Houston, TX). This study included patients with advanced solid tumors or lymphoma for whom curative options were not available. The full clinical protocol is available in the supplemental information. Eligible patients had adequate performance status (ECOG 0–1; KPS > 70%); hemoglobin > 10 g/dL; platelets > 100,000/μL; absolute neutrophil count > 1,500; total bilirubin < 1.5 × the upper limit of normal; ALT < 2.5 × ULN (<5 × ULN in patients with liver metastases); and estimated glomerular filtration rate >50 mL/min. Participants had measurable disease by RECIST 1.1, Lugano, or McDonald criteria, depending on the tumor type, and tumors amenable to safe core needle biopsy and i.t. injection of Toca 511. There was a washout period of 2 weeks for prior chemotherapy or investigational anticancer therapies and 4 weeks for prior antibody therapy. Exclusion criteria included inability to recover from toxicities associated with anticancer therapies within 2 weeks of treatment initiation, uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, inability to swallow oral medications, allergy or intolerance to flucytosine, HIV infection, inability to stop anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents prior to i.t. procedures, and pregnancy or breastfeeding status. Patients had next-generation sequencing (NGS) of their tumors, by commercially available NGS platforms.

Trial design, treatment, and procedures

The Toca 6 study was a multicenter, open-label phase 1b trial of Toca 511 and Toca FC. It was registered as NCT02576665. Following screening, eligible patients were given Toca 511 by i.v. injection (4.6 × 109 TU) on days 1, 2, and 3, consecutively (Figure 1) On day 11, patients underwent biopsy or tumor resection (procedure 1) for correlative studies. After the first four patients received i.v. Toca 511, the study was amended to add one i.t. virus administration, and the rest of the patients (N = 17) were administered i.v. and i.t. Toca 511. For those patients who had easily accessible tumors, they received an additional dose of Toca 511 (up to 1.3 × 109 TU) injected directly into the tumor after the first biopsy (up to approximately 4 mL depending on tumor size) (N = 17). On day 34, patients began their first two cycles of oral Toca FC (5 days of treatment per cycle with 2 days off in between cycles). On day 63, patients underwent a biopsy or resection of the previously biopsied tumor (procedure 2) to evaluate post-treatment immune activity, 5-FU concentration, as well as other tumor characteristics. Cycle 3 (7-day treatment) of Toca FC was initiated 4–6 weeks following the start of cycle 2. All subsequent cycles of 7 days of Toca FC treatment were initiated 4–6 weeks following the previous cycle. At the completion of cycle 2 of Toca FC, the primary investigator could consider use of non-investigational anticancer therapies concomitantly with Toca FC. Such use was initiated only after consultation with the medical monitor. Treatment with Toca FC was continued until there was documented disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, patient withdrawal, or investigator decision (see supplemental information for complete protocol document). Additional details on the treatment and protocol procedures can be found in supplemental information.

Tumor correlative studies and immunostaining

Tumor biopsies were collected from patients at both procedure 1 and procedure 2 at Sarah Cannon Research Institute, Investigational Cancer Therapeutics Department, MD Anderson Cancer Center, or University of Miami Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center. All specimens came from metastases of CRC and collected using needle core biopsy. Tumor samples were processed for formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks per institutional protocol, one core needle biopsy specimen per block. FFPE blocks were sectioned at 4-μm thickness onto positive charged slides (Fisher Scientific, one section per slide) at Colorado Histoprep (Fort Collins, CO). Following deparaffinizing, one slide per specimen collected and blocked was stained hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and DAPI nuclear staining. These stains were used to determine suitability, based on quality parameters, for further investigation with three antibody panels. Inclusion in the current analysis required at least two high-quality specimens available from both biopsy 1 and 2. The quality of each specimen was determined by visual assessment H&E and DAPI stains without bias, as this assessment done in a blinded fashion. The quality parameters included loss or folding of tissue section and evidence of drying or deterioration of sample. Six patients’ specimens met quality parameters and were included in this study. Additional antigen retrieval studies were done on these slides including Gag/PanCK panel, myeloid panel, and T cell panel as described below.

Gag/PanCK panel

Following deparaffinization of slides, antigen retrieval was carried out using Antigen Retrieval Reagent-Universal (CTS015, R&D Systems) for 45 min at 95°C, 30 min open air, followed by water rinse then Ready to Use, Phosphate Buffer Saline-Tween (RTU PBST) rinse. Slides were blocked in for 10 min in Blockall (SP6000, Vector Labs) containing 0.1% Triton X-100, then washed two times, 3 minutes each, in RTU PBST. Primary antibody cocktail was prepared in Antibody Diluent for IHC (559148, BD Biosciences): rabbit anti-Pan CK (ab9377, Abcam) at 1:250; rat anti-Gag (77977-3, Genescript) at 1:500. Antibody Diluent without primary antibody was used as control for background staining. After overnight incubation in primary antibody cocktail or control at 4°C, slides were washed four times for 5 min in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Secondary antibody cocktail was prepared in Antibody Diluent for IHC: goat anti-rabbit IgG-AF488 (ab150081, Abcam) at 1:500; goat anti-rat IgG-AF647 (ab150167, Abcam) at 1:250. Slides were incubated in secondary antibody cocktail for 1 h at room temperature, protected from light and then washed four times for 5 min in PBS. Slides were mounted with cover slips in Proong Gold antifade mounting medium (P36930, Invitrogen) containing 1:5,000 dilution of stock Hoechst 33342 (10 mg/mL, H3570, Invitrogen). Slides were scanned at Allele Biotechnology (San Diego, CA). Quantification of Gag signal was assessed using VisioPharm image quantitation software (number of Gag+ pixels per tumor area defined by PanCK+ staining).

Myeloid panel

Following deparaffinization of slides, antigen retrieval was carried out using Antigen Retrieval Reagent-Basic (CTS013, R&D Systems) for 45 min at 95°C, 30 min open air, followed by water rinse then RTU PBST rinse. As described above, slides were blocked, treated with primary and secondary antibody cocktails, cover slipped, and scanned. Primary antibody cocktail was prepared in Antibody Diluent for IHC: rat anti-HLA-DR (MA1-70112, ThermoFisher) at 1:50; goat anti-CD11b (ab62817, Abcam) at 1:100. Secondary antibody cocktail was prepared in Antibody Diluent for IHC (559148, BD Biosciences). Secondary antibody cocktail was prepared in Antibody Diluent for IHC (559148, BD Biosciences), goat anti-rat IgG2a-FITC (PA1-84761, ThermoFisher) at 1:500; rabbit anti-goat IgG-AF568 (ab175707, Abcam) at 1:500. Quantitation of CD11b-positive pixels was assessed using VisioPharm image quantitation software. Because CD11b+ signal did not overlap with HLA-DR signal, CD11b+ signal was used to indicate non-macrophage myeloid infiltration. VisioPharm software was trained to identify areas of tumor cells within the sample based on typical endothelial morphology and size of nuclei. The stromal area, area around tumor cells where myeloid cells may be located, was then calculated by area of tumor (endothelial morphology) subtracted from the total area of sample: stromal area = (area of sample) – (area of tumor). Myeloid infiltration was calculated as CD11b+ pixels/stromal area.

T cell panel

The T cell multiplex panel—CD4, CD8, FoxP3, PD-1—was developed and executed by Cell IDx (HI03A-005, Cell IDx, San Diego, CA). Quantitation of several T cell populations was assessed from scanned slides using VisioPharm image quantitation software: (1) CD8 T cells = CD8+ cells/stromal area; (2) CD4 T cells = CD4+ cells/stromal area; (3) exhausted CD8 T cells = CD8+, PD1+/stromal area; (4) regulatory T cells = CD4+, FoxP3+/stromal area. VisioPharm software was trained to identify areas of tumor cells; and stromal area, area around tumor cells containing infiltrating T cells, was calculated as described above.

Flow cytometric immunophenotyping of peripheral blood

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from venous blood within 48 h of collection (as per Figure 1) using Vacutainer CPT Cell Preparation Tube with Sodium Heparin (BD Biosciences). PBMCs were cryopreserved in RPMI 1640 (Cellegro) with 12.5% human serum albumin (Gemini bio-products) and 10% DMSO (Sigma) and then stored in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen until needed. Samples were de-identified during processing by assigning lab ID numbers and then analyzed in random order by technicians blinded to sample identities. Thawed PBMCs were rested overnight (16–20 h) at 37oC with 5% CO2 with a density of approximately 1E+06 cells per mL in 25-cm2 flasks (Corning) in AIMV medium (Gibco) containing 10% human serum (Sigma) and ≥1,000 U/mL IL-2. After resting, 1E+05 to 5E+05 PBMCs were transferred to each well of 96-well U-bottom polypropylene plates (Costar). To prepare fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls, multiple PBMC samples were pooled from each batch. Single-color controls were prepared using UltraComp eBeads (Invitrogen) and ArC Amine Reactive Compensation beads (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In addition, a healthy PBMC control sample was run with every batch to track variability in results. Samples were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Green Dead Cell Stain kit (Invitrogen), Fc blocked using Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend), surface stained using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (BioLegend, eBioscience, and BD Biosciences, Table S1), fixed, and permeabilized using a Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set staining kit (eBioscience), and then stained intracellularly using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (BioLegend, eBioscience, and BD Biosciences). Data were acquired using a BD Biosciences LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer equipped with a high-throughput sampler and a Fluidics Flow Supply System. At least 10,000 events per sample replicate was acquired using BD FACSDiva software version 8.0.1 (BD Biosciences), and compensation was performed using single-color controls prior to exporting FCS files.

Data analysis was carried out using FlowJo version 10.5.3 (Tree Star). Cell population gating was performed using FMO controls and unstained samples to determine cutoffs for positive and negative events. For dimensionality reduction using t-stochastic neighbor embedding (tSNE), number of events were first standardized using the DownSample plugin version 2.0.0 to reduce live PBMCs to 15,000 events per replicate or live T cells to 10,000 events per replicate. Next, tSNE was applied using the TSne plugin version 2.0.0 applied to compensated parameters using 1,000 iterations with a perplexity of 20, Eta of 200, and Theta of 0.5. Additional statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using R version 3.4.1 or later,28 with data converted from wide format to long format using the reshape and reshape2 packages29,30 in order to facilitate plotting using ggplot2.31

Quantification of Toca 511 from peripheral blood

DNA and RNA were extracted from whole blood using a modified version of Promega’s Maxwell 16 Purification System as communicated by the manufacturer. Viral DNA and RNA detected by qPCR and qRT-PCR, as described previously.20

Statistical considerations

The primary objective of the Toca 6 study was to evaluate changes in immune activity after treatment with Toca 511 and Toca FC. Secondary objectives included assessment of safety and tolerability of Toca 511 and Toca FC as administered in this study, evaluate vector deposition in tumor specimens following i.v. administration of Toca 511, and assessment of efficacy of treatment with Toca 511 and Toca FC, alone or in combination with standard of care therapies based on the following: objective response rate, disease control rate, duration of response (DOR), PFS, and OS. DOR was displayed using swimmer’s plot. Median PFS and median OS along with corresponding 95% confidence interval were estimated using the inverse Kaplan-Meier method. Due to the small sample size, statistical tests were not performed. Statistical software, SAS version 9.4, and R version 4.2.2 were used for analysis.
