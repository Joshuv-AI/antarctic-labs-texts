Programmable digital printing of organic single crystals for integrated electronics
Authors: Chengtai Li, Hui Yang, Congcong Huang, Liangmeng Hao, Aiqing Fan, Bowen Geng, Lihua He, Feng Zhang, Shuming Duan, Xiaochen Ren, Wenping Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise control over orientation and crystallinity in additive manufacturing (AM) of organic semiconductor arrays is critical for achieving high-performance organic integrated electronics. However, achieving such control in conventional AM remains particularly challenging without engineered spatial constraints due to the complex crystallization kinetics of small-molecule organic semiconductors under nonequilibrium ink deposition conditions. Here, we report a simple yet effective mask-free, pattern-free printing of high-resolution organic single-crystal arrays with controlled location, orientation, and aspect ratio. Central to this method is the establishment of a dynamic liquid–crystal area (DLCA) beneath the nozzle, which governs the kinetically coupled interplay of solute transport, solvent evaporation, and nucleation. By tuning DLCA geometry via applied voltage and speed, the random nucleation and anisotropic crystal growth is suppressed. This mechanism, supported by classical theory and transition region theory, is universally applicable to most solution-processable high-performance small-molecule organic semiconductors. The organic single crystal array exhibits highly uniform mobility, with around 12 to 15% variation, representing the most uniform organic single-crystal arrays achieved by electrohydrodynamic printing so far. In addition, the printed organic single-crystal patterns can be successfully integrated into a functional 96 organic field-effect transistor photodetector array, demonstrating their potential for information recognition and organic integrated electronics.

INTRODUCTION

Additive manufacturing (AM) for thin film devices, including techniques such as digital or direct-write printing, has emerged as a cornerstone technology in both scientific research and industrial innovation. Its digitally programmable, mask-free nature enables precise deposition of functional materials under ambient conditions, minimizing waste while allowing complex, geometry-independent patterning. In research, AM has accelerated developments in printed electronics, (1, 2) biointegrated sensors, (3, 4) soft robotics, (5, 6), and energy storage platforms (7, 8). In industry, it supports advances in personalized health care (9), flexible circuit (1, 10–15), lightweight aerospace structures (16), and on-demand device fabrication (17)—contributing to a projected AM market surpassing $50 billion by 2030 (18, 19).

Within this landscape, AM techniques have been extended to a wide spectrum of materials systems, including inkjet-printed perovskites for photovoltaics (20), aerosol jet printing of two-dimensional (2D) materials (21, 22), electrohydrodynamic (EHD) printing of quantum dots for light-emitting diodes (LEDs) (23), and polymer organic field-effect transistor (OFET) (24), and solution-processed metal organic frameworks (MOFs) for chemical sensing (25). Of particular interest are organic semiconductors, which combine mechanical flexibility, tunable optoelectronic properties, and solution compatibility—making them ideal candidates for scalable AM-enabled electronics (2, 20, 24). However, despite these advantages, a critical limitation remains: AM technologies can dictate where material is placed but not how it crystallizes. For functional semiconductors—especially molecular systems whose performance depends on long-range order and crystallographic alignment—this lack of control over the solidification process leads to grain boundaries, misoriented domains, and compromised electronic performance. The longstanding goal of directly printing single crystals with programmable location, orientation, and domain size remains elusive.

Previous strategies to address this challenge have relied on substrate-directed crystallization, such as surface energy patterning, wettability contrast, or lithographic confinement, to guide nucleation (26–33), while such approaches inevitably compromise the flexibility and mask-free nature of AM and are often limited to specific materials or device layouts. Some alignment-based techniques—such as shear-induced ordering or meniscus-assisted growth (26, 28, 34)—can promote long-range orientation and offer control over nucleation and defect density. Even the most advanced inkjet strategies (29, 35, 36) using solvent blends or antisolvent crystallization typically result in polycrystalline thin films due to isotropic evaporation and stochastic nucleation dynamics.

Here, we report a direct-write strategy that enables spatially programmed crystallization of organic single crystals without surface patterning or confinement, using EHD jet printing. A key feature of this process is the formation of a narrow, self-confined dynamic liquid–crystal area (DLCA) beneath the printing nozzle, within which solvent evaporation, solute concentration, and nucleation energetics are tightly coupled. By tuning the DLCA geometry via printing parameters such as voltage of nozzle head and printing speed, we modulate supersaturation kinetics and reduce the entropic cost of ordering. This enables deterministic formation of single-domain crystals governed by principles described by transition zone theory (37, 38) and classical nucleation theory (CNT) (39, 40). Specifically, the DLCA establishes a transient, supersaturated zone that simultaneously promotes nucleation and constrains lateral growth; CNT provides the thermodynamic framework for understanding the free energy barrier for nucleation, the critical radius, and nucleation rates; while the dynamic confinement in DLCA reflects a kinetic modulation strategy long recognized in controlled crystallization environments.

Using this mechanism, we demonstrate sub–10 μm-resolution printing of aligned organic single crystals from multiple small-molecule semiconductors, including C8-BTBT, diF-TES-ADT, and TIPS-pentacene. These printed crystals are integrated into functional devices, including flexible low-voltage OFET arrays on plastic substrates and pseudo–complementary metal-oxide semiconductor (CMOS) inverter circuits. The devices exhibit high carrier mobilities, minimal device-to-device variation, and robust switching characteristics at operating voltages below 5 V. Collectively, these results establish a general and scalable approach for AM of crystalline organic electronics with performance on par with single crystals grown by conventional methods.

RESULTS

Programmable crystallization via DLCA engineering

To overcome the stochasticity and multinucleation challenges inherent to solution-processed organic semiconductors, we developed an EHD jet printing strategy that achieves controllable single-crystal formation without the need for substrate patterning or confinement. At the core of this process is the emergence of a self-confined DLCA, which forms directly beneath the nozzle tip as a result of coupled flow, evaporation, and concentration gradients. The DLCA acts as a transient, geometry-tunable zone where solute accumulation, supersaturation, and nucleation kinetics are tightly modulated. By adjusting parameters such as printing speed and applied voltage, we precisely control the DLCA width and position, which in turn govern the nucleation onset and lateral growth dynamics. This enables the formation of spatially isolated, single-domain crystals with programmable size, shape, and orientation—realizing a long-standing goal in AM of organic semiconductors.

To visualize and validate the crystallization dynamics, we constructed an EHD jet printing platform (Fig. 1A), where both nozzle motion and voltage waveforms are digitally programmed to deliver user-defined deposition paths and profiles. During continuous printing, a self-confined DLCA forms just behind the nozzle tip (Fig. 1B), comprising a diffusion boundary layer (DBL), a solute accumulation zone, and a crystallization front. This confined zone decouples solute transport from nucleation, allowing dynamic modulation of supersaturation and molecular assembly. The DLCA width plays a key role in determining nucleation stability and crystal quality. As shown in Fig. 1C (top), polarized optical microscopy captures the real-time crystallization process under different DLCA regimes, revealing the transition from labile, metastable, to stable nucleation regimes as the DLCA width decreases. In wider DLCAs (>100 μm), uncontrolled nucleation leads to dense polycrystalline networks, while intermediate widths (50 to 60 μm) correspond to partially stabilized fronts with competing grains. Narrow DLCAs (10 to 30 μm) result in thermodynamically favored nucleation and defect-free propagation of a single-crystal front. The corresponding final polarized optical microscopy (POM) images of printed lines in Fig. 1D show a clear correlation between DLCA geometry and crystalline uniformity: Large-area polycrystalline domains are gradually transformed to well-aligned single crystals as the DLCA becomes confined. These results confirm that DLCA geometry governs the transition from stochastic nucleation to deterministic, anisotropic crystal growth.

To demonstrate functional applications, we integrated the printed single crystals into several device platforms. Flexible OFET arrays fabricated on polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) substrates (Fig. 1E) retained uniform performance under bending, leveraging the high crystallinity and alignment of printed semiconductors. The high-resolution patterning enabled by our printing approach also allowed construction of a photodetector array (Fig. 1F) for 1D image scanning. Furthermore, pseudo-CMOS inverter circuits based on printed single-crystal transistors were realized on rigid substrates (Fig. 1G). These proof-of-concept integrations confirm the potential of DLCA-guided printing for flexible organic integrated circuits.

Ink formulation and jetting stability analysis

Next, we investigated the printing process in greater detail to elucidate the underlying mechanisms responsible for single-crystal formation. Notably, unlike conventional EHD (E-jet) inks (2, 20, 41–44) that typically contain metallic nanoparticles and use mixed solvent systems for optimized rheology and charge transport, our ink formulation is nanoparticle-free and composed of small-molecule organic semiconductors and polymer blend dissolved in single-component solvents. This fundamental difference presents a notable challenge: Standard solvent systems widely adopted in commercial EHD printing are not compatible with our formulation and fail to produce a continuous and stable jet under similar conditions; the scanning electron microscopy image of the nozzle is provided in fig. S1.

To address this, we first conducted finite element simulations to model the behavior of ink ejection under applied electric fields. These simulations revealed how solvent volatility, viscosity, and dielectric constant collectively affect cone formation, flow focusing, and jet stability. Because stable and controllable ink ejection is critical for achieving uniform deposition and directional crystallization, the simulations established a feasibility window that guided solvent selection and process parameter tuning. Full simulation setup and results are provided in the Supplementary Text and figs. S2 and S3.

Mechanism of single-crystal formation during direct E-jet printing

To gain insights into the crystallization process, the two images shown in Fig. 2A—captured under optical and polarized microscopy—were combined to reveal the presence of a distinct liquid zone ahead of the crystal growth front. As illustrated in fig. S4, this zone represents the DLCA, where solute accumulation and directional solidification are spatially confined beneath the printing nozzle. Movies S1 to S3, which print at different linewidths, further visualize this process; movie S1 shows the rapid emergence of bright, birefringent crystals following the moving nozzle under crossed polarizers; and movies S2 and S3 show the printing mode changing by wider linewidth, and excessive ink is observed, which means that they have slower evaporation rate, thus multicrystal formed. We further leveraged this directional crystallization to print dense arrays of single crystals with digitally defined trajectories. The polarized optical images in Fig. 2 (B and C) highlight the capacity of the method to fabricate densely packed, high-resolution arrays of organic single crystals with uniform crystallographic orientation. A complete polarized view of the printed array is provided in fig. S5, confirming the long-range alignment and structural coherence across the patterned region. To demonstrate the digital programmability of the printing process, we patterned organic single crystals into both linear and curved geometries (e.g., arcs, circles, and triangles). As shown in Fig. 2C, polarized optical microscopy reveals uniform brightness within each printed feature when the sample is aligned at 0° to the polarizer axis and near-complete extinction when rotated by 45°; zoom-in POM images of the corners of the square and triangle patterns are provided in fig. S6. This periodic intensity modulation—arising from the intrinsic optical anisotropy of the single crystals—confirms that each printed patterns maintains a consistent crystallographic orientation throughout its structure, and then further AFM images in fig. S7 of the turnings of each nonlinear pattern showed higher at the inner edge and lower at the outer edge. This phenomenon is analogous to the differential linear speeds of a vehicle’s inner and outer wheels when navigating a turn (fig. S8). Specifically, the inner edge, which undergoes slower local printing velocity, experiences prolonged solvent residence time accompanied by slightly enhanced evaporation. The slower inner edge experiences a longer solvent-residence time and slightly enhanced evaporation, leading to increased material accumulation and greater crystal thickness. Additional curved array patterns shown in fig. S9 further demonstrate the method’s capability for digitally defined single-crystal patterning.

Conventional ink-jet droplets deposited on untreated substrates evaporate in an uncontrollable manner (fig. S10A). Solvent evaporates fastest at the pinned contact line, driving an outward “coffee-ring” flow (45–47) that convects solute to the perimeter. Supersaturation therefore rises nearly uniformly across the droplet, so the critical nucleation barrier is crossed simultaneously at many uncorrelated sites. Each nucleus grows with an arbitrary lattice orientation until it impinges on its neighbors, yielding a highly polycrystalline film with numerous grain boundaries (fig. S10B).

EHD jet printing fundamentally changes this scenario. A self-regulated micro-meniscus that trails the nozzle—termed the DLCA—confines evaporation and solute transport to a narrow zone (Fig. 3A). By adjusting the applied voltage, we continuously tune the DLCA width from millimeters to tens of micrometers, reshaping both the evaporation kinetics and the thermodynamic pathway to crystallization.

According to transition zone theory (37, 38), solution crystallization proceeds through four coupled regions: bulk ink, a DBL, a melt-like pregrowth intermediate (PGI) and the advancing crystal front xtl (Fig. 3A). Kelvin-driven evaporation vertically compresses the PGI to <1 μm in narrow DLCAs, reducing the entropic cost for molecular ordering. When the DLCA is ≈20 μm wide (left graph in Fig. 3B) the solution reaches its critical supersaturation almost instantaneously; a single nucleus forms and propagates unimpeded, producing a continuous single-crystal ribbon. A DLCA width of approximately 50 μm (Fig. 3B, middle) correlates with increased solvent retention and a mixed evaporation profile, which subsequently induces random nucleation and polycrystallinity. In contrast, a wide DLCA (≈180 μm, right in Fig. 3B) retains solvent far longer, allowing multiple, randomly positioned nuclei to form throughout the PGI. Their growth fronts compete for solute and merge into misoriented grains, converting the downstream xtl segment into a polycrystalline strip.

Although the observed transition from polycrystalline to single-crystalline growth is primarily governed by the DLCA geometry and evaporation kinetics, lattice constant of organic crystals also defines the effective confinement threshold for different organic semiconductors. The area of the ab-plane unit cell represents the minimum repeating unit for crystal propagation. Therefore, we calculated the number of unit cells that can be accommodated across the critical line width as a normalized metric to evaluate the confinement effect, rather than comparing absolute line widths. The calculated unit cell areas (a × b) for the three semiconductors are listed in table S2 in the unit of Å2. A “normalized crystallization width” (NCW) is defined by dividing the printing line width for each material by its respective unit cell area. The results of the relationship between single domain probability and printed line width or NCW are shown in Fig. 3C and fig. S11, respectively.

As shown, TIPS-pentacene exhibits the smallest NCW value, indicating that it requires the most stringent spatial confinement (i.e., it is the most sensitive to line width variations) to achieve single-crystal growth. diF-TES-ADT shows a moderate NCW, while C8-BTBT has the largest NCW, signifying that it has the widest process window for forming single crystals under confinement. This trend aligns with the observed crystallization propensity: C8-BTBT is the most forgiving and can form single crystals over a broader range of line widths, followed by diF-TES-ADT, and then TIPS-Pentacene.

Finite-element simulations prove these observations. When the DLCA radius r exceeds ~50 μm, a printed filament behaves like an isolated sessile drop. COMSOL calculations (Fig. 3D) show that a 1-mm droplet dries in hundreds of seconds, whereas a 30-μm meniscus evaporates within ~100 ms. This equation relates the actual vapor pressure (p) to the saturated vapor pressure (p0) for a flat surface, liquid/vapor surface tension (γ), molar volume of the liquid (Vm), universal gas constant (R), radius of the droplet (r), and temperature (T). It dictates that smaller r raises the local saturated vapor pressure and accelerates solvent loss. Rapid evaporation drives the interior of a narrow DLCA deep into the labile supersaturation regime within milliseconds (Fig. 3D), whereas wide tracks remain in the metastable zone.

CNT (39) further quantifies this behavior. For a cylindrical nucleus spanning the film thickness h, the reversible work of formation isΔG(rn)=2πrnγh−πrn2hΔμΩ(2)yielding a critical radius rc = 2γΩ/Δμ and a nucleation barrier ΔG∗ = πhγ2Ω/Δμ. Because Δμ = kBTσ, the steady-state rateJ(σ,T)=J0exp[−πhγ2ΩKB2T2σ2](3)falls exponentially with 1/σ2. In the DLCA, supersaturation increases with time as σ(t) ≃ (cs − c∞)[1 − exp. (−Dt/r2)], giving an induction time tc ∝ r2/D. Thus, reducing the line width by a factor of 10 shortens tc from seconds to milliseconds, enabling a single nucleus to dominate before secondary nucleation can occur. Experimentally, single-domain coverage exceeds 80% when the printed line width is ≤30 μm but falls rapidly for wider tracks (Fig. 3C). This trend is universal across TIPS-pentacene, diF-TES-ADT, and C8-BTBT (fig. S12). Printing speed also significantly influences crystallization quality. At narrow line widths where evaporation dominates, the printing speed determines the solute delivery rate per unit area, as shown in Fig. 3E and fig. S13, atomic force microscopy (AFM) cross-sectional profiles reveal that slower printing speeds lead to thicker films due to greater local ink accumulation, whereas faster speeds yield thinner deposits, analogous to meniscus-guided crystallization in the evaporation-limited regime (Fig. 3F). A speed of 0.3 mm s−1 produced films with optimal uniformity and crystallinity when using C8-BTBT, as evidenced by the consistent single-crystal domains in polarized microscopy (fig. S14). Extremely slow speeds tend to result in uncontrolled overgrowth, while excessively fast speeds can produce discontinuous films due to insufficient ink supply (48).

In summary, DLCA engineering—interpreted through Transition Zone Theory (TZT) and CNT—eliminates the need for substrate patterning, surface energy contrast, or confinement structures. In contrast to inkjet or meniscus-guided coating techniques that rely on wetting gradients or predefined templates, our mask-free EHD printing route enables the direct transformation of organic semiconductor inks into spatially programmed single crystals under ambient conditions. This approach offers a scalable strategy for AM of organic electronic devices with long-range molecular order.

Structural characterization of printed organic single crystals

The structural properties and crystallinity of the printed C8-BTBT lines were further examined using AFM, grazing-incidence wide-angle x-ray scattering (GIWAXS), and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED). As shown in Fig. 4A, the AFM topography reveals a uniform printed ribbon with an average thickness of ~43 nm, alongside a characteristic coffee-ring profile indicative of radial solute redistribution during solvent evaporation. High-resolution AFM (HR-AFM) imaging (Fig. 4B) resolves the molecular packing on the crystal surface, with the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT, Fig. 4C) yielding lattice parameters of a = 5.9 Å, b = 8.2 Å, and γ = 89.4°, consistent with previously reported single-crystal data for C8-BTBT (49).

To assess bulk ordering and crystallographic alignment, in-plane x-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed. As shown in Fig. 4D, a distinct (020) Bragg reflection is observed, indicating preferential in-plane orientation of the printed crystal domains. The complementary out-of-plane diffraction profile is provided in fig. S15. 2D GIWAXS mapping (Fig. 4E) further confirms the highly ordered nature of the film, with sharp and intense reflections observed along both the out-of-plane (qz) and in-plane (qxy) directions. The presence of (00l) and (11L) diffraction features indicate that the [001] crystallographic axis is oriented perpendicular to the substrate, as further supported by the line profile extracted along qz (Fig. 4F).

To probe the structural coherence at the microscale, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and SAED measurements were carried out on individual printed ribbons. As shown in Fig. 4G, a representative crystal was transferred onto a copper grid, and electron diffraction patterns were acquired at three separate positions (I to III). The resulting SAED patterns (Fig. 4H) are identical across center locations, and more spread SAED patterns of it are provided in fig. S16, demonstrating single-domain crystallinity over the entire structure. The extracted lattice parameters (a = 5.9 Å, b = 7.9 Å, γ = 90.4°) agree well with those of bulk C8-BTBT single crystals, confirming high ordered structure.

In conclusion, AFM, HR-AFM, XRD, GIWAXS, and SAED analyses consistently confirm that the EHD printing method yields highly ordered, well-oriented C8-BTBT single-crystal patterns. This approach allows direct patterning of organic semiconductors into single crystals without requiring surface pretreatment or templates. Moreover, the method is suitable for other small-molecule organic semiconductors (OSCs), as demonstrated by the diF-TES-ADT single-crystal films shown in figs. S17 to S19.

A small fraction of polystyrene [(PS) molecular weight (Mw) ≈ 190,000] was incorporated into the ink to enhance jet stability and improve the semiconductor/dielectric interface. AFM and POM analyses (fig. S20) confirmed a phase separation after printing, indicating that the polymer mainly acts as an interfacial modifier without disturbing the crystal lattice of the semiconductor. Similar strategies have been reported by Duan et al. (34, 50).

Electrical performance of printed single-crystal OFETs

To evaluate the charge transport properties of printed C8-BTBT single crystals, we fabricated bottom-gate top-contact OFETs on SiO2/Si substrates (Fig. 5A). The source and drain electrodes consisted of thermally evaporated Ag (30 nm), with a thin F4-TCNQ interlayer introduced to improve hole injection. The channel length (L) was 100 μm, and the width (W) was defined by the printed crystal line (~40 μm).

A linear array of 35 OFETs was fabricated on a single printed ribbon and characterized under polarized optical microscopy (Fig. 5B). The uniform extinction across all channels at both 0° and 45° to the polarizer confirms consistent crystallographic orientation throughout the array. The transfer and output characteristics of these devices (Fig. 5, C and D) show typical p-type behavior with negligible hysteresis and an on/off ratio exceeding 107. The representative device achieved a field-effect mobility of 8.1 cm2 V−1 s−1. Across the entire 35-device array, the average mobility was 7.8 cm2 V−1 s−1 with an SD of 0.97 cm2 V−1 s−1 [coefficient of variation (CV) = 13.1%], as shown in Fig. 5 (E to G). In addition to its excellent crystallinity, the printed C8-BTBT single crystals exhibit remarkable environmental robustness. Even after 10 months of ambient storage without encapsulation, the OFETs retained nearly unchanged transfer characteristics (fig. S21), confirming the intrinsic air stability of C8-BTBT arising from its deep highest occupied molecular orbital level and dense molecular packing.

To further assess statistical uniformity over larger areas, we measured an expanded set of 120 OFETs, all fabricated from single-crystal ribbons printed under optimized DLCA conditions. As detailed in fig. S22, the mobility distribution remains centered at 7.8 cm2 V−1 s−1, but with a slightly broader coefficient of variation of 15.1%, confirming reproducibility even across multiple substrates and printing batches.

The impact of printed line width on device performance is summarized in Fig. 5C. As the DLCA width increases, the average field-effect mobility systematically decreases while the coefficient of variation rises. Wider lines suffer from increased grain boundary density and structural defects, leading to degraded transport. At narrow widths (~30 μm), single-domain crystal formation dominates, enabling high mobility and low variability.

Compared to OFETs based on inkjet-printed polycrystalline films, our E-jet–printed devices exhibit considerably improved performance and reproducibility. Benchmarking against literature (Fig. 5H) shows that both the mobility and CV values of our devices approach those achieved by state-of-the-art meniscus-guided coating. Notably, this represents a report of direct printing of organic single-crystal transistors without substrate patterning, lithographic confinement, or guided flow fields.

To explore material generality and low-voltage compatibility, we extended this approach to other OSCs and dielectric platforms. OFETs based on printed diF-TES-ADT single crystals yielded an average mobility of 2.4 cm2 V−1 s−1, an on/off ratio of ~107(details in figs. S23 to S25). Furthermore, printing C8-BTBT onto high-k AlOx/Al substrates produced low-voltage OFETs with well-defined p-type characteristics, a mobility of 3.3 cm2 V−1 s−1, an on/off ratio of ~105, and a CV of 12.6% (fig. S25); tested array and its mobility heatmap in fig. S26. These results demonstrate the versatility of DLCA-guided E-jet printing in fabricating crystalline organic electronics with spatial, electronic, and material control.

To harness the spatial precision and material compatibility of E-jet printing, we fabricated optoelectronic and logic circuits based on printed single-crystal semiconductors, including line-scanner photodetectors, flexible OFET arrays, and pseudo-CMOS inverters. These devices demonstrate the potential of this method for direct integration of large-area, high-quality single crystals onto functional substrates.

First, flexible OFET arrays by printing single-crystal C8-BTBT ribbons on PEN substrates with high-k AlOx/Al gate dielectrics (Fig. 6A) were fabricated. Electrical characterization under different bending radii (flat, 27 mm and 14.5 mm; fig. S27, A and B) revealed stable current-voltage relation (I-V) characteristics with negligible hysteresis or mobility degradation (Fig. 6, B and C, and fig. S27C). These results validate the mechanical robustness of the printed crystalline semiconductors and their compatibility with flexible electronics platforms.

Then, we demonstrate a unipolar inverter constructed entirely using printed C8-BTBT single-crystal OFETs. Figure 6D shows polarized optical images confirming the crystallinity and alignment of the semiconductor layer. The device circuit diagram is shown in fig. S28, comprising two p-type transistors in an inverter configuration. Voltage switching characteristic under different supply voltages (Fig. 6E) exhibit sharp switching behavior, with voltage gains exceeding 10 at VDD = 5 V (Fig. 6F). The inverter operates reliably at low voltages, confirming its suitability for energy-efficient logic circuits.

Lastly, a 96-channel organic photodetector array was fabricated using printed diF-TES-ADT single crystals. As shown in Fig. 6G, a 45-mm-long single-crystal stripe was directly printed onto anodized AlOx/Al, where the AlOx acts as the gate dielectric and the underlying aluminum as the gate electrode. Thermal evaporation of top-contact Au electrodes completed the OFET array in a single-step process. Polarized optical images of the crystal ribbon are shown in fig. S29.

Photoresponse characteristics of an individual device under LED illumination are presented in fig. S30. The transfer I-V curves show increasing current under higher light intensities, along with a threshold voltage shift toward more positive values in fig. S30A. The shift in threshold voltage (ΔVth), attributed to photogenerated electron trapping, increases linearly with light intensity and saturates around 1200 μW cm−2 (fig. S30B). At a fixed gate voltage of −0.25 V, the device exhibits dark currents as low as 10−10 A, with photocurrent rising substantially under illumination (fig. S30C), confirming high responsivity and low off-state leakage.

To demonstrate light-based data recognition, we positioned a binary barcode mask over the device and sequentially scanned the 96 OFETs using a custom readout circuit. Pairing two adjacent OFETs to represent a single bit enabled a 48-bit linear photodetection array. By differentiating the output currents between illuminated and masked regions, the system successfully decoded binary patterns from the input mask. The resulting current intensity heatmaps (Fig. 6H) matched the encoded information with high fidelity, confirming the feasibility of printed crystal arrays for optical recognition and data extraction.

Together, these results underscore the versatility of E-jet–printed single crystals in enabling low-voltage, flexible, and optically responsive organic electronic systems. The combination of structural coherence, mechanical robustness, and device functionality makes this platform well-suited for future integration into wearable sensors, photodetectors, and organic logic circuits.

DISCUSSION

This study establishes a generalizable strategy for the direct printing of organic semiconductor single crystals with deterministic spatial control, crystallographic alignment, and device-grade quality. By leveraging the EHD jet (E-jet) process and introducing the concept of a DLCA, we circumvent the limitations of conventional AM techniques that lack control over crystallization pathways. Unlike traditional inkjet or meniscus-guided coating methods, which require prepatterned substrates, confinement geometries, or solvent engineering, our approach enables the spontaneous and programmable formation of single crystals on flat, untreated surfaces at ambient conditions.

The underlying mechanism of DLCA-driven crystallization was elucidated through a combination of finite element simulations, experimental morphology analysis, and CNT. We demonstrate that the DLCA geometry—defined by printing parameters such as speed and voltage—governs solvent evaporation kinetics, supersaturation evolution, and nucleation statistics. Narrow DLCAs (~10 to 30 μm) compress the PGI region, reducing the entropic barrier to molecular ordering and favoring single-nucleus formation. In contrast, wider DLCAs (>60 μm) promote stochastic nucleation and polycrystallinity due to delayed supersaturation buildup and spatially unconfined nucleation fronts. This mechanistic understanding unifies thermodynamic and kinetic perspectives into a predictive framework for crystallization control during printing.

The resulting printed crystals exhibit high structural quality, as confirmed by AFM, GIWAXS, and SAED. The method supports sub–10 μm resolution, long-range orientation fidelity, and centimeter-scale pattern continuity—criteria previously only achievable through manual crystal transfer or epitaxial growth. When integrated into OFETs, the printed crystals enable excellent device performance: mobilities exceeding 11 cm2 V−1 s−1, low variability (CV ~ 15.1%), and low operating voltage (<4 V) on high-k dielectrics. Beyond linear transistor arrays, we demonstrated functional implementations including barcode-decoding photodetectors, flexible OFETs, and pseudo-CMOS logic inverters—all fully fabricated from printed single crystals without any lithographic alignment.

The process is material-agnostic, having been validated on multiple benchmark OSCs including C8-BTBT and diF-TES-ADT, and adaptable to different substrates and gate dielectrics, such as high-k AlOx. Its ambient compatibility, digital programmability, and elimination of prepatterning constraints position DLCA-guided E-jet printing as a scalable manufacturing strategy for high-performance, flexible, and optoelectronic organic integrated circuits.

Looking ahead, this technique opens alternative avenues for constructing large-area monocrystalline arrays with addressable functionality. Potential applications include neuromorphic photodetectors, reconfigurable circuits, and quantum molecular devices. Further integration with machine learning–assisted print parameter optimization and inline crystallinity monitoring could enable real-time feedback control, pushing the limits of additive crystal engineering for next-generation electronics.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

The objective of this study was to develop a generalizable approach for mask-free printing of organic semiconductor single crystals and to elucidate the mechanism that enables deterministic crystal growth. To conclude, we carefully selected a nonconventional organic ink formulation distinct from standard commercial E-jet inks, as the ink chemistry is crucial to achieving stable jetting and subsequent crystallization. Finite element simulations of the nozzle ejection process under applied electric fields were performed before experiments to evaluate the feasibility of stable ink delivery, as reliable fluid dynamics are prerequisite for controlled crystal growth. Guided by these insights, EHD printing was used to directly deposit C8-BTBT ink onto Si/SiO2 and flexible substrates, while in situ–polarized optical microscopy was used to capture nucleation and growth in real time. The printed single crystals were then integrated into transistors and arrays to correlate crystallization control with device performance.

Materials

1,2-Dichlorobenzene (anhydrous, 99.8%), PS (analytical standard, average Mw ≈190,000), C8-BTBT (high-performance liquid chromatography, ≥99%), and Tips-pentacene (high-performance liquid chromatography, ≥99%) were bought from Sigma-Aldrich; diF-TES-ADT(>99%, nuclear magnetic resonance) was purchased from Luminescence Technology Corp. The heavily n-doped Si wafer with thermally grown 300-nm SiO2 was bought from China Electronics Technology Group Corporation. PEN substrates (120 μm) were bought from Good Fellow.

Device fabrication

Top contact bottom gate OFETs were fabricated on the Si/SiO2, glass substrate, and PEN substrates, respectively. The substrates were cleaned in detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol for 5 min separately in ultrasonic bath, and the substrate were dried by 99.999% purity nitrogen flow. Printing process was performed with Super Inkjet SIJ-S050, large nozzle. In the case of glass substrates, a 150-nm-thick layer of aluminum was deposited on the substrates as a common gate electrode by thermal evaporation in vacuum. The vacuum chamber pressure was 1 × 10−6 mbar, and the evaporation rate of aluminum was 40 Å s−1. The dielectric layer was then formed by the method of anodization; a constant current of 0.7 mA cm−2 was used, the maximum voltage was set as 15 V, and thus the final thickness of aluminum oxide was 1.4 × 15 + 2 = 23 nm, in which 2 nm was the native oxide formed before anodization. Patterned AlOx/Al was fabricated by metal shadow mask before anodization. OSCs and PS in 1:1 w/w ratio were dissolved into 1,2-dichlorobenzene with a concentration of 20 mg ml−1, and the blend solution was stirred for 1 hour. The blend solution ink was loaded in the commercial nozzle bought from SIJ corp. The inkjet speed was about 0.3 mm s−1 at room temperature. Last, F4-TCNQ and silver were successively deposited by thermal evaporation through the same shadow mask. The vacuum chamber pressure was 1 × 10−6 mbar, the evaporation rate of silver (40 nm in thickness) was 1 Å s−1, and F4 TCNQ (4 nm in thickness) was 0.5 Å s−1.

Characterizations

POM images were captured by Nikon Eclipse Ci-POL polarized microscope with a rotation stage; capturing schematics is shown in fig. S31. AFM images were captured by Intelligent mode using a Bruker Dimension Icon in air. Out-of-plane and in-plane XRD results were obtained using Rigaku SmartLab X-ray diffractometer with highest power of 9 kW. TEM and SAED measurements were conducted on a Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN. The transfer and output I-V curves of the field-effect transistors were measured using a Keithley 4200 CSC semiconductor parameter analyzer in air. The field-effect mobility of OFET at saturation region is calculated from the transfer I-V based on the following equation, IDS = WμCi(VG − Vth)2/2L was used, where IDS is the source-drain current, W and L represent the width and length of the channel, μ stands for the mobility, VG and Vth refer to the gate and threshold voltages, and Ci is the dielectric capacitance per unit area. Ci = 11.5 nF cm−2 is used for all the mobility calculation, considering the polymer layer beneath OSCs due to phase separation, the results are slightly underestimated. In line photodetector, each 32-source electrodes was controlled by an ADG732 chip, three ADG732 chips were used to control each OFET to obtain light current, data were collected by Keithley 2636b, ADG732 and OFET powered and controlled by DAQ, DAQ was controlled by a moderated LabVIEW program by PC. In pseudo-CMOS inverter test, output voltage was measured by Keithley 2110, input was given by Keithley 2636b. All electrical tests were performed at room temperature and ambient atmosphere.
