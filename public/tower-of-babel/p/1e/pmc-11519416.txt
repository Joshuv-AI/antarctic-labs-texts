Re-programming by a six-factor-secretome in the patient tumor ecosystem during nutrient stress and drug response
Authors: M. Tarek Elghetany, Jie-Ling Pan, Karthik Sekar, Angela Major, Jack MF Su, Adekunle Adesina, Kam-Man Hui, Xiao-Nan Li, Wan-Yee Teo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryCancer cells need nutrients to grow and proliferate. During nutrient stress in the microenvironment, it is unclear if or how cancer cells can adopt alternative resources to re-wire and survive in patients. We discovered a 6-factor-secretome remarkably sustains a critical cell mass during nutrient stress in a pediatric embryonal brain tumor, atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (ATRT). Specific ATRT subtypes emerged as secretome-enriched, matching macrophage-enrichment patterns and were high-relapse-risk subtypes. The secretome alters drug response, protects against cell death, and provides pro-survival niches to rescue drugged cells. Secretome-grown tumor cells rearrange into a web-like architecture—stable during drug exposure, suggesting a mechanism for therapy resistance. Secretome prevents tumor cell death in aggressive tumor models, and in cerebrospinal dissemination, suggesting a role in tumor resistance/relapse. Our results unravel, a previously unexplored role of a specific 6-factor-secretome, providing an alternative fuel to sustain cancer cells during nutrient stress, and implications in relapse subtypes.

Introduction

Clinical implications of macrophage density infiltrating tumors vary between different body organs.1 Increased macrophage density has been associated with tumor progression and/or negative clinical outcome in some organs (brain, breast, liver, kidney, ovary, pancreas, thyroid, head and neck1,2,3,4). In colorectal cancer, increased macrophage infiltration was linked to a better prognosis,1 while in cancers of the bone, lung, and prostate, the role of macrophages remains controversial.1 The complexity of the problem is multiplied by the myriad of dynamic macrophage polarization spectrums.1 Over the last decade, infiltrating macrophages are increasingly recognized to predominate brain tumors,5 but there is still no consensus on the clinical significance of macrophage infiltration relevant to patient outcomes in brain tumors.6 Some current strategies to study macrophages infiltrating patient tumors include genomic profiling of micro-dissected regions of macrophages in patient tumors,7 and single cell sequencing of individual macrophages in patient tumors.8,9 These approaches involve tearing apart of the macrophage microenvironment compartment from the tumor cell compartment of patient tumors harvested. Newer approaches such as spatial transcriptomics,10 allow gene expression profiling without disrupting the tumor architecture. However, the common challenge for all these approaches, is that the resected patient tumors are immortalized at a single time-point. The ability to study the in-situ impact of macrophage presence over time is lost. We therefore redirect our focus instead to the factors nested within the macrophage milieu in the tumor microenvironment.

Cancer cells need nutrients to grow and proliferate. During periods of nutrient stress in the microenvironment, it is not well understood if or how cancer cells can adopt alternative resources to re-wire and survive in patients. We postulate that the macrophage milieu may provide an alternative fuel source for cancer cells to survive. Patient tumors are more complex than in-vitro culture systems. Patient tumors comprise a microenvironment surrounding cancer cells. Nonetheless, laboratory testing of drugs still uses in-vitro cancer cell cultures without a microenvironment, which remains an imperfect strategy for drug discovery and translation in oncology. Macrophages have been observed to infiltrate many human tumors and across cancer types. Macrophages in the stroma of patient tumors can be found in large quantities, and are therefore highly attractive and desirable targets in the microenvironment. However, macrophages are dynamic and offer many facets and phenotypes which the field has yet to fully comprehend. In this study, instead of macrophages, we approached the secretome nested in the macrophage milieu of the patient tumor microenvironment, which offers the benefit and advantages of examining a more constant environmental fluid surrounding tumor cells. We hypothesized that various cytokines-chemokines from the secretome derived from macrophages continuously provide a critical niche for tumor progression/development and impact the drug response of each individual tumor. In the absence of nutrients during nutrient stress, this secretome can provide an alternative fuel source for cancer cells to survive. To explore the real-time, dynamic interaction of this secretome in the tumor ecosystem, we developed an assay utilizing human pro-monocytic cells activated to become CD11b+ (pan-macrophage) cells to produce a 6-factor-secretome, to establish the effects of this secretome on the atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumor (ATRT), a pediatric embryonal brain tumor.

Pediatric brain cancer is the leading cause of death in childhood cancer.11,12 ATRT is a pediatric embryonal brain tumor, frequently occurring in infants and young children, accounting for 15–20% of all brain tumors in children less than 3 years of age.11,12 It is an aggressive brain tumor and historical survival outcome is poor (10–20%, 5-year survival for patients <3 years old).13 Standard of care historically involved surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, although none are curative therapies.14 A recently published trial ACNS0333 employing high dose chemotherapy and peripheral blood stem cell rescue15 has improved survival. However, this improved survival was achieved at the cost of treatment toxicity with high dose chemotherapy and the subsequent requirement for peripheral blood stem cell rescue.15 High rates of treatment failure were also observed in this trial.15 More effective drugs16 and a deeper understanding of the tumor microenvironment in ATRTs impacting drug efficacy and tumor biology, are needed.

Results

Macrophages populate some brain tumors

M2 macrophages have been implicated with poorer patient survival in medulloblastoma, another type of pediatric embryonal brain tumor.17 We first examined four patient ATRT tumors for macrophage infiltration with immunohistochemical staining using CD163 (M2 macrophage marker) monoclonal antibody. CD163 was exclusively expressed on alternatively activated or M2-polarized macrophages.18 Patient ATRT tumors depicted an architecture of macrophage compartment interfacing with tumor cell compartment; this macrophage compartment was clearly absent in normal brain tissue (Figures 1A and 1B). Morphologically, CD163 was expressed on macrophages and not tumor cells. Macrophage-enriched compartment with a high density of macrophages was nested at the tumor border (edge of patient tumor resected from normal brain) (Figure 1A). The normal brain (control) was barely infiltrated by macrophages (Figure 1A). Intra-tumoral heterogeneity of macrophage infiltration within each single patient tumor (Patient 1–4) was consistently observed across patient tumors, demonstrating that stroma was distinctly infiltrated by a high number of macrophages compared to tumor compartment (p = 0.022, Paired T-test, Figure 1B). Macrophages sometimes formed a well-defined perivascular ring suggesting origins related to circulating myeloid cells in Patient 4 (Figure 1B). We surveyed a mixed bag of 13 pediatric brain tumors from a single institution and found similar enrichment of CD163 macrophages among a good proportion of patient tumors (Figure S1). We observed that not all patient tumors within each histological type demonstrated high CD163 expression – two ATRT tumors in this cohort, one demonstrated CD163hi expression and the other CD163lo (Figure S1). A 6-factor secretome in patient tumors

Many strategies currently employed to study macrophages among patient tumors, explore the spatial relationship of macrophages in tumors immortalized at a single time-point (Figure 1C), such as genomic profiling of micro-dissected regions of macrophages in patient tumors (Strategy 1), single cell sequencing of individual macrophage in patient tumors (Strategy 2) and spatial transcriptomics (Strategy 3). Challenges of Strategy 1 include omission of scattered, invasive macrophages (Figure 1B) in patient tumors, whilst limitations of Strategy 2 include (1) the high cost to sequence large numbers of single cells, (2) data limited by the number of cells sequenced (cost limitation), and (3) ensuring good representative population of single cells sequenced. The common problem of both Strategy 1 and 2, is that both approaches involve tearing apart the macrophage microenvironment compartment from the tumor compartment. The unifying challenge among all three strategies is that spatial information on macrophages extracted from human tumors lack dynamic data – macrophages are immortalized at a single time-point from resected patient tumors, resulting in a loss of opportunity to study the in-situ, real-time interaction between the tumor and macrophage compartments. To approach this problem, we redirected our focus instead to the factors nested in the macrophage milieu in the patient tumor ecosystem. We activated human pro-monocytic cells to become CD11b+ (pan-macrophage marker) cells to produce a 6-factor-secretome (Figure 2A). To do this, we first treated human-myeloid-derived cells with a PMA-cytokine cocktail to induce CD11b (pan-macrophage marker) expression.19,20 CD11b (pan-macrophage marker) was consistently high at 80–90% (Figures 2A and S2A), while CD163 (M2 macrophage marker) expression was negligible (Figure S2B).Figure 26-factor-secretome derived from CD11b+ macrophages is enriched in specific molecular subtypes of ATRT(A) In-vitro system to derive 6-factor-secretome using CD11b+ human-myeloid-derived cells.(B) Three conditions of macrophage-tumor microenvironment were established in-vitro: (1) Tumor cell factors, (2) CD11b+ cell factors, (3) CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture factors. 36-cytokine-chemokine array analyses performed for above conditioned media and co-culture supernatant demonstrated a pattern of 6-cytokine enrichment related to CD11b+ macrophages in the microenvironment. We next examined which factors could be secreted by these activated CD11b+ cells. Array analyses of 36-cytokine-chemokines were performed to compare the secreted factors in 3 different conditions: (1) tumor cells only conditioned media, (2) CD11b+ cells only conditioned media, and (3) tumor-CD11b+ co-culture. Results demonstrated a distinct enrichment pattern of 6 cytokines-chemokines released by CD11b+ cells in the microenvironment (Figure 2B). We employed the most aggressive ATRT tumor cells (CHLA-06) from our panel of 7 ATRT cell line16 to create the tumor compartment (Figure S3), based on the assumption that the most rapidly growing in-vitro cell line CHLA-0616 will secrete the most complete spectrum of tumor-promoting factors. Importantly, the presence of tumor cells enhanced the production of these 6 specific cytokines-chemokines (CCL2/MCP-1, MIP-1α/MIP-1β, CCL5/RANTES, MIF, IL-1ra/IL-IF3, IL-8) by CD11b+ cells (Figure 2B). Tumor cells in isolated culture did not produce this panel of cytokines (Figure 2B) apart from MIF, indicating that this cytokine panel was distinct to and released by the CD11b+ cell component. Comparing 10 patient tumors, 10 controls (normal brain tissue controls from various brain regions of cerebellum, cerebrum, pons), and 8 patient-derived cell lines in Discovery Set (RNA-seq, n = 28 samples), this 6-factor-secretome was enriched only among patient tumors and not normal brain controls nor patient-derived tumor cell lines (Figures 2C and S4A). Normal brain controls were distinctly secretome-bland (Figure 2C). Remarkably, this enrichment pattern among patient tumors was recapitulated in the expanded 36-cytokine-chemokine panel (all candidates on cytokine array platform, Figure 2B).

MYC- and TYR-atypical teratoid rhabdoid tumors are secretome-rich

We next compared this 6-factor-secretome enrichment patterns among patient tumors of three different ATRT molecular subtypes (MYC-ATRT, SHH-ATRT, TYR-ATRT) in Validation Set (GSE70678; Affymetrix U133 plus 2.0)21 comprising of 49 patient ATRT tumors classified by molecular subtypes.21 MYC-ATRTs were the most enriched for 6-factor-secretome, followed by TYR-ATRTs, while SHH-ATRTs were almost devoid of 6-factor-secretome (Figures 2D and S4B). Similarly, the enrichment pattern of the expanded 36-cytokine-chemokine panel (all candidates on cytokine array platform) recapitulated the 6-factor-secretome enrichment pattern among three ATRT molecular subtypes (MYC>TYR>SHH), and was evident only among patient ATRT tumors and not normal brain nor cell lines, in both Discovery (Figure 2C) and Validation Sets (Figure 2D).

Secretome-rich tumors are enriched in macrophages

Enrichment patterns of 6-factor-secretome mirrored macrophage infiltration density. Quantitative bioinformatic evaluation of macrophage markers among patient tumors, controls, and patient-derived tumor cell lines in Discovery Set (RNA-seq, n = 28 samples), confirmed that patient tumors were enriched in CD11b (pan-macrophage marker) and CD163 macrophages (M2 macrophage marker), but not tumor cell lines nor normal brain tissues (Figure 3A). Control brains were comparably low in CD11b and CD163 macrophage-content. Macrophage markers of various subsets, specifically CD11b and CD163, correlated well among patient tumors but not in tumor cell lines nor normal brain tissues (Figures 3C and S5). Among 3 molecular ATRT-subtypes in the validation set, MYC-ATRTs and TYR-ATRTs were macrophage-enriched tumors (Figures 3C and 3D), matching 6-factor-secretome-rich subtypes, indicating distinct signaling pathways influencing macrophage recruitment and function in patient tumors of these secretome-rich subtypes. We further interrogated additional macrophage markers22,23,24,25 in both Discovery Set and Validation Set, and observed similar trends (Figures S6–S10). SHH-ATRTs were macrophage-low and secretome-low. Tumors displaying M2 macrophage-enriched phenotype (CD163hi) were frequently MYC-ATRTs and TYR-ATRTs and were significantly enriched in the dendritic cell maturation pathway and complement system (Figure 3E). Secretome reinvigorates tumor cells during nutrient stress

To elucidate the effects of the 6-factor-secretome on tumor cell proliferation, we cultured a panel of 6 individual patient-derived ATRT cell lines in all three in-vitro conditions of macrophage-tumor microenvironment we have developed (Figure 2B): Condition 1 - Tumor cell factors, Condition 2 - CD11b+ cell factors, Condition 3 - CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture factors. We contrasted the growth of the 6 tumor cell lines (Figures 4A and 4B) in all 3 microenvironment conditions (using supernatants derived from respective cells cultured in RPMI base media without any serum nor growth factors) against pure RPMI base media (control, without any serum nor growth factors), over a duration of 72 h. Clearly, pure RPMI base media (control, without any serum nor growth factors) was in a state of nutrient stress and was not able to support tumor cell proliferation (Figures 4A and 4B). Supernatants derived from CD11b+ cell factors (Figure 4A) and CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture factors (Figure 4B), resulted in higher tumor cell proliferation compared to tumor cell factors (Figure S11A). Among all 6 ATRT cell lines, 2 cell lines (CHLA-02 and BT-37) displayed an obvious increase in optical density (OD) readings above the baseline OD readings of the 6-factor-secretome (Figure S12). BT-37 was a patient-derived xenograft cell line of ATRT.16 This may suggest certain tumor cell lines can preserve the intrinsic capability to respond to macrophage-related factors. It was interesting to note that the 2 cell lines that demonstrated higher proliferative abilities (CHLA-02, BT-37) were the same cell lines that were marginally secretome-rich compared to all other cell lines (secretome-bland, Figure 2C). This suggests the notion that through in-vitro passaging, most tumor cell lines possibly lose components of microenvironment-responsive factors/receptors in the stroma, as suggested by the lesser growth-inducing response stimulated by exogenous 6-factor-secretome in the other 4/6 cell ATRT lines. These results further indicate that the panel of cytokine-chemokine factors alone was not sufficient to sustain long term tumor growth in-vitro beyond 72 h without standard growth media supplements (standard in-vitro nutrients also need to be refreshed every 48-72 h for standard cell cultures of these cell lines). This suggests that such tumors in cytokine-chemokine micro-ecosystems possibly provide early and temporary niches for initial tumor growth spurt, and an alternative fuel source during a nutrient-stress state when the microenvironment is deprived of nutrients. Supernatants derived from CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture provided the best sustained growth promoting effect at 72 h, compared to CD11b+ supernatants (Figures 2B and 4). Tumor-cell-derived conditioned media was not effective in sustaining tumor growth (Figure S11A), indicating tumor cell factors were not sufficient (Figure 2B). Six factors in combination were effective. Cytokine array results earlier indicated supernatants derived from CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture factors contained higher quantities of these 6 specific cytokines-chemokines compared to CD11b+ conditioned media (Figure 2B). These 6 factors - CCL2/MCP-1,26,27 MIP-1α/MIP-1β,27,28 CCL5/RANTES27,29,30,31, MIF,32,33,34 IL-1ra/IL-IF3,35 IL-831 have been independently described to affect macrophages or tumorigenesis.

Tumor cells are protected from cell death by secretome and re-arrange into web-like architecture

Furthermore, we observed that the distinct combination of these 6 factors was capable of preventing tumor cell death in all four ATRT cell lines tested (CHLA-02, BT-37, CHLA-04, CHLA-06, Figure 4E). This secretome was comparable to standard growth media in preventing cancer cell death up to 48 h, remarkably sustaining a critical mass of cancer cells during nutrient stress. At longer time points 72-96 h, more cell death occurred in the secretome compared to standard growth media (Figure 4E), indicating that the secretome was able to provide initial support, but the secretome alone without nutrient supply was insufficient for prolonged periods. It is particularly interesting to note that secretome was able to prevent cell death for a much longer duration of 72-96 h in CHLA-04, which was derived from a recurrent ATRT tumor obtained at autopsy with widespread dissemination to the brain and cerebrospinal fluid (Figure 4E). This indicates that in this highly aggressive tumor model, although the secretome did not cause a high degree of tumor proliferation (Figures 4A and 4B), instead it was capable of preventing tumor cell death for longer periods, contributing to a mechanism for the tumor to survive and disseminate. A similar ability of secretome in preventing cell death for prolonged periods of 72-96 h (Figure 4E) but not support a high degree of tumor proliferation (Figures 4A and 4B), was observed in CHLA-06, the most aggressive and fast growing ATRT cell line in-vitro.16 These findings in the most aggressive cell lines - the fastest growing in-vitro (CHLA-06), as well as from a recurrent tumor derived at autopsy with widespread dissemination to the brain and cerebrospinal fluid (CHLA-04), suggest a possible mechanism for the secretome in tumor resistance and relapse.

We discovered that tumor cells which normally grow in neurospheres in standard growth media, when instead grown in secretome, after 24 h of nutrient stress, rearrange themselves to form interlacing web-like structures connecting cells linearly over long-distance radius in-vitro (linear patterning) (Figures 4F and 4G; Figures S13–S17). Cancer cells on in-vitro cultures have never been shown to exhibit such a capability in standard media conditions. This observation suggests a cross-talk between cells in a secretome microenvironment during nutrient stress or deprivation, which can be important for tumor cells to survive, re-arrange themselves into a web-like architecture to remain resilient without a stable and continuous nutrient supply.

We postulated that the tumor compartment will be enriched in cell cycle genes during rapid tumor growth and cell division, and therefore cell cycle genes would provide an appropriate target to inhibit growth of the tumor compartment. MELK was expressed among 6/7 ATRT cell lines (Figure S18, BT-37 lower, ATRT-95 no expression). Secretome rescues drugged tumor cells

We found that the presence of a 6-factor-secretome reduced drug efficacy during the initial phase (Figure 5E), providing a pro-survival niche to rescue tumor cells during early drug response. We showed that tumor cells cultured in standard growth media with OTSSP167 exhibited rapid, sustained inhibition of tumor growth indicating a rapidly efficacious drug response in standard growth media (CHLA-02, BT-37, Figure 5E). In contrast, tumor cells cultured in 6-factor-secretome derived from CD11b+ cells conditioned media or supernatants derived from CD11b+ and tumor cell co-culture factors consistently demonstrated a dampened response to tumor growth inhibition by OTSSP167 in secretome microenvironment (Figure 5E). Six factors in combination were effective in dampening the drug response, indicating the drug is less effective in a secretome microenvironment compared to an in-vitro screen employing standard growth media. Within patient tumors, these 6 factors were enriched in the microenvironment, which suggests a lower drug efficacy in a secretome-rich microenvironment in patient tumors.

The 6-factor-secretome is a scalable and clinically relevant resource as a laboratory-stage phenotypic screen for drug testing among cancer cell lines. It can predict the pro-survival contributory component of a restricted repertoire of microenvironment cytokines-chemokines. It serves as a tool to measure drug efficacy in the presence of a minimal pool of microenvironmental factors. In addition, these factors are stable during long term storage at −80°C and maintained their efficacy after long-term storage at −80°C for 3 years (Figure 4D – results of CHLA-02 (Replicate 1) and BT-37 assays (Replicate 1) using supernatants stored for 3 years at −80°C). Our 6-factor-secretome drug screening approach can provide a readily available scalable resource for in-vitro laboratory testing of other cancer types to identify cancers that exploit secretome as an alternative fuel source. This secretome provides a favorable tumor ecosystem that reprograms malignant cells to survive nutrient stress and other selection pressures such as early drug response. Additionally, when treated with OTSSP167, tumor cells maintained their interlacing architecture in secretome (Figures 5H and 5E; Figures S20 and S21), in contrast to tumor cells (CHLA-02) breaking up into smaller clusters when treated with OTSSP167 in standard growth media, suggesting a potential mechanism for therapy resistance provided by secretome.

These findings indicate a pattern of pro-survival niches supported by secretome, which is advantageous for tumor cell survival during nutrient stress and exogenous drug stress. In a native, no-drug state, media nutrients induced a slow gain in proliferative rate, while a 6-factor-secretome microenvironment in a nutrient-poor cancer cell niche provoked a rapid and sustained proliferative rate (Figure 4). This suggests that the secretome provides a superior ecosystem for tumor growth spurt during nutrient deprivation. In a drug-exposed state, tumor anti-proliferative response to OTSSP167 was dampened in the presence of a 6-factor-secretome (Figure 5E), in contrast to the standard media nutrient environment whereby drug exposure resulted in the rapid inhibition of tumor proliferation. This indicates that a drug that is shown efficacious in-vitro in standard growth media (traditional drug testing approach), when tested on the same cell lines in a secretome screen, is less efficacious.

This quadruple mechanism of secretome was capable of (1) inducing rapid early growth spurt and (2) preventing tumor cell death during nutrient stress, simultaneously (3) dampening drug response of tumor cells, and (4) maintaining the stable interlacing web-like architecture of tumor cells when exposed to the drug, together provide strong advantages for tumor cell resilience and therapy resistance (Figures 4G, 5E, and 5H). Some patient tumors are secretome-rich (Figures 2C and 2D), which highlights a gap during the translation of in-vitro effective drugs to clinical trials, whereby there is an unmet need to better accurately match patient tumors that are secretome-rich and potentially less responsive to certain drugs.

High-relapse subtypes are secretome-rich

Another consideration is that these secretome micro-ecosystems can promote tumor relapse. Secretome can provide an alternative fuel source to protect against cell death (Figures 4E and 4F) and accelerate tumor growth spurt (Figure 4D) of residual cancer cells residing near tumor margins post-surgical bulk resection of tumor – a mechanism for tumor relapse. We have observed a macrophage-enriched compartment with a high density of macrophages nested at tumor borders in some patient tumors resected (Figure 1A). This observation is further strengthened by the finding that frequent relapse-subtypes of ATRT (Reddy et al. Presented in abstract. Unpublished data) - MYC-ATRTs, TYR-ATRTs (Figure 6) are secretome-rich subtypes and also macrophage-enriched (Figures 2C and 2D). Data from ACNS033315 reported patients with SHH-ATRTs had no relapses in the first 6 months and a cumulative incidence (CI) of relapse of 37.5% at 4 years, while for TYR-ATRTs and MYC-ATRTs had CI of relapse of 33.3% and 26.7% at 6 months, and 46.3% and 73.3% at 4 years respectively (p = 0.088, (Reddy et al. Presented in abstract. Unpublished data). This trend is interesting, despite the fact that ACNS0333 was not powered to determine prognostic indicators of relapse. Discussion

Macrophage infiltration in ATRTs was reported independently in other smaller datasets,43,44 with subtype-specific differences - previous analysis44 however, reflected infiltration of CD68+ microglia/macrophages were significantly higher in SHH-ATRTs (16 tumors) compared to both TYR-ATRTs (10 tumors, p = 0.0006) and MYC-ATRTs (8 tumors, p = 0.0157), which is in contrast to our current findings which uncovered SHH-ATRTs comprise of a macrophage-depleted tumor-subtype. This also strengthens the challenge we highlighted earlier - when studying macrophages in patient tumors, specimens are harvested and immortalized at a single time-point. During this process, we lose the opportunity to study the real-time dynamics of macrophage and tumor compartments in-situ as an ecosystem. Although the evaluation of CD163 as a marker for M2 macrophages may not be fully elucidated, numerous other studies have consistently used CD163 for that purpose, including studying tumors and cell lines.22,23 In addition, tumor-infiltrating macrophages in the brain comprise of both resident microglial and bone-marrow-derived circulating monocytes entering the brain. While the debate continues about the existence of M1 and M2 microglial,24 our current mechanistic approach of employing the secretome assay circumvents this problem by redirecting the focus instead to the microenvironment milieu during tumor development and concurrently enabling a multi-time-point sampling to study the real-time dynamics in the patient tumor microenvironment.

From our data on BT-37 (Figure 5E), this xenograft-derived cell line supernatant alone can support some tumor growth unlike the other cell line (CHLA-02), suggesting the likelihood of growth-promoting factors contributed by mouse stroma components in xenograft cell lines (BT-37), which may be absent in patient-derived-cell lines (CHLA-02) after long term passaging. In-vivo, OTSSP167 was effective in prolonging animal survival on an aggressive autopsy-derived patient-derived orthotopic xenograft mouse model of ATRT (Figure 5F). But like in most preclinical testing, the mice eventually succumbed to the tumor even though the drug administered was able to slow tumor growth. This is a gap in clinical translation. Microenvironmental factors constantly secreted by other cell types in the host tissue, can provide a critical pro-survival niche for early tumor cells. Tumor cells can be sustained even in the absence of environmental nutrients, which circles into the important challenge of tumor relapses. Our assays demonstrate – as long as there are supporting cell types (such as macrophages) providing a minimal secretome, in a niche with a few surviving tumor cells, such as in conditions after surgical bulk resection/post-therapy, tumor cells can be re-invigorated (Figure 6). Since drug exposure times for in-vitro assays can differ markedly from those achievable in-vivo, our in-vivo data conclusively demonstrated that the therapeutic activity of OTSSP167 can induce tumor growth reduction at OTSSP167 levels achieved in-vivo. Our results indicate OTSSP167 was clinically effective in-vivo on our aggressive autopsy-derived ATRT Model (p = 0.032, Figure 5F). OTSSP167 has been shown to penetrate the blood-brain barrier of another ATRT mouse model.36 Taken together, our data reveals clearly, the functional hallmarks of secretome in tumor microecosystems, which can overcome nutrient deprivation states by providing a constant pro-survival niche. Such a secretome can re-invigorate a small but critical, surviving pool of tumor cells, which can be important mechanisms of relapse and treatment resistance.

Our study addresses the knowledge gap on how a secretome, enriching the microenvironment of patient tumors, can mechanistically impact tumor proliferation during nutrient stress by providing an alternative fuel source. Further, these findings raise further questions on how such secretomes can impact the clinical success of drugs during clinical trial translation since drugs are traditionally tested using standard media in-vitro, but patient tumors can be secretome-rich. We propose a secretome screen as a companion phenotypic assay to identify drugs efficacious in the chemokine-cytokine environment to evaluate the contributory component of tumor microenvironment factors, in conjunction with traditional in-vitro drug screening employing standard growth media, as well as in-vivo preclinical testing. Our approach adds value to the strategy for drug selection and patient stratification, to lower clinical risk of relapse post-resection and post-drug therapy. This is particularly important for high-relapse risk groups of MYC- and TYR-subtypes, which are macrophage- and 6-factor-secretome enriched subtypes.

In summary, we found that a 6-factor-secretome derived from human pro-monocytic cells, enables cancer cells to proliferate in a nutrient-poor state within a cancer cell niche of a pediatric embryonal brain tumor, ATRT, during early tumor development. This secretome is equal to standard growth media in preventing cancer cell death, remarkably sustaining a critical mass of cancer cells during nutrient stress. Specific molecular subtypes of ATRT emerged as secretome-enriched, matching macrophage-enrichment patterns. High-relapse-risk subtypes were discovered to be concurrently secretome-rich and macrophage-enriched. Our results unravel a previously unexplored role of a distinct secretome in providing an alternative fuel to sustain cancer cells when nutrients are depleted, and implications in relapse subtypes. Furthermore, the response of ATRT cells to drugs is altered in the secretome. Clearly, the presence of the secretome reduces OTSSP167 efficacy and protects against cancer cell death during initial tumorigenesis, providing a pro-survival niche to rescue drugged cells during early drug exposure. Drugs effective against cancer cells screened in standard media, may not be equally efficacious in secretome-phenotypes of patient tumors. Strikingly, secretome prevents tumor cell death for prolonged periods in aggressive tumor models and models of cerebrospinal dissemination, suggesting a role in tumor resistance and relapse. We discovered that tumor cells which normally grow in neurospheres in standard growth media, when instead grown in the secretome, rearrange themselves to form interlacing web-like architecture connecting cells linearly over a long-distance radius in-vitro, after 24 h. This observation suggests a cross-talk between cells in a secretome microenvironment during nutrient stress or deprivation, which can be important for tumor cells to survive and remain resilient without a stable and continuous nutrient supply. Additionally, when treated with OTSSP167, tumor cells maintained these interlacing structures in the secretome, which suggests a potential mechanism for drug resistance.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of our study was that only 6 factors (identified out of 36 candidates on the cytokine array) were employed in our in-vitro assays. The threshold for detection was restricted by the minimum quantity of protein detectable by cytokine arrays. The serum-free/cell-free supernatants (Figure 2B) could contain other factors in lower quantities not evaluable through this assay. Using a higher specification protein assay such as mass spectrometry may detect a greater spectrum of chemokines-cytokines. However, our data has proven that patient tumors, particularly MYC-ATRTs and TYR-ATRTs are highly enriched in 36 candidates on cytokine array (Figures 2B and 2C), likely harboring more than 36 cytokines-chemokines. Merely defining all the cytokines-chemokines using high resolution protein assays adds little knowledge to the mechanistic functions of the entire secretome spectrum in the patient tumor. More importantly, we show that using this culture system comprising of a minimum of 6 factors, we were already able to elicit the effects on tumor growth spurt and dampening of drug efficacy. We did consider other potential components in the secretome that can modulate tumor cells, such as exosomes, and therefore employed ultra-centrifuge spin speeds to separate and isolate exosome fractions. Comparing the two fractions, the exosome-fraction did not produce a significant proliferative effect when cultured with tumor cells (data not shown), in contrast to the remaining secretome fraction (containing the 6 major factors) which supported tumor proliferation. This indicates that the current secretome protocol comprising of 6 factors, contains barely/no exosomes in our current experience. The important implication is the direct relevance of this 6-factor-secretome to human patient tumor samples which are also enriched in these 6 major factors in the secretome - this is demonstrated and validated in separate patient cohorts in Figure 2.

Another consideration is that our secretome assay measures up to 72 h. The amount of protein produced by CD11b+ cells in our in-vitro assay was in physiological quantities, and not unlimited. Hence, this assay also parallels the biological concentrations of endogenous secretome production in patient tumors, which our data recapitulated - a small quantity of this mixture of cytokines-chemokines in a patient tumor is sufficient to support an initial growth spurt which is critical during tumor initiation. Within patients’ tumors, the problem is amplified because macrophages residing in-situ in the patient tumor milieu can continuously produce secretomes as alternative fuel sources to sustain a minimum pool of critical cancer cells during nutrient stress until nutrient sources replete through new tumor vasculature. This is important in solid tumors – accessibility to nutrients within solid tumors is regulated by proximity to the vasculature.45 The growth and survival of rapidly expanding solid tumors require a continuous oxygen and nutrient supply which needs to be maintained by the tumor neovasculature. Cells located adjacent to the vasculature use nutrients and oxygen to fuel proliferation.45 However, cells distant from the vasculature have diminished accessibility to nutrients and will need to engage alternative fuel resources in the tumor ecosystem such as secretomes. Therefore, drugs that are efficacious both in nutrient-replete and secretome-based conditions will be highly attractive.

Conceptual Advance/Clinical Relevance

Our strategy has important clinical implications. Many preclinical successes have not been equally paralleled by success at clinical trials. This distinct secretome provides a superior ecosystem with an alternative fuel source for tumor growth spurt during nutrient stress. We discovered that a drug that is shown efficacious in-vitro in standard growth media (traditional drug testing approach), when tested on the same cell lines in a secretome screen, is less efficacious. Some patient tumors are secretome-rich, while in-vitro drug testing conditions are nutrient-rich and secretome-poor. This highlights a gap during the translation of in-vitro effective drugs to clinical trials, whereby there is an unmet need to better accurately match patient tumors which are secretome-rich and potentially less responsive to certain drugs. This problem is amplified in solid tumors - the growth and survival of rapidly expanding solid tumors require a continuous oxygen and nutrient supply which needs to be maintained by the tumor neovasculature. When such nutrient supplies are lacking, the presence of such a secretome can provide the needed milieu as an immediate alternative fuel source to rescue and sustain tumor cells distant to vascular supply. Tumor cells can therefore survive through interim periods before new nutrients can enrich the microenvironment through newly formed tumor neovasculature. Relapse contributes to a major cause of cancer deaths. The ability of such a secretome to propagate the residual tumor cells during disease relapse will serve as an important mechanism in tumor relapse.

Experimental model and study participant details

In vivo animal studies

Orthotopic implantation of patient-derived tumor cells into mouse brains

Rag2/Severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) mice were bred and group-housed (5 mice per cage) in a pathogen-free facility. Both mice genders aged 6–8 weeks were employed for the animal experiments. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance to protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at SingHealth Duke-NUS Academic Medical Center, Singapore. Surgical implantation of tumor cells (1 × 105) into mouse cerebrum was performed.49,50,51 The general recommendations in ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (https://arriveguidelines.org/) were followed in our study. Our sample size was small. We did not use any method to generate the randomisation sequence. The animals were randomly assigned into treatment versus control groups to have approximately equal number of animals per group. We have recently published that gender does not statistically affect treatment outcome,51 and both genders were included in our study. Our animals were housed in the same location in side-by-side cages and managed in a standardized maintenance protocol by the animal unit and our team to minimize any confounding effects. We did not employ any special strategy to minimize other confounders. Drug treatment was delivered to animals assigned in treatment group, cage-by-cage. Blinding was not possible as we have a small team of 2–3 individuals working with the animals and all involved at various stages and day-to-day processes of the animal work. Separate batches comprising of various litters were used, minimizing bias from the same litter effect. We have published using this animal protocol49,50,51,52,53 and is not otherwise deposited in any repository/registry.

Intraperitoneal drug administration

OTSSP167 was dissolved in a solution of dimethyl sulfoxide, Kolliphor EL and sterile water. A daily dose of 5 mg/kg of OTSSP167 was administered to the mice via intraperitoneal injection for 14 days. Ten animals were studied (5 treated, 5 controls).

Clinical staging system to monitor mice fitness

Mice were carefully monitored daily their fitness using a clinical staging system.53 When mice developed severe signs of neurologic deficit or became moribund, they were euthanized according to our protocol.49,50,52,53

Studies involving human participants

Ethics approval

All primary tumor specimens were obtained through an Institutional Review Board approved protocol from patients treated at the Texas Children’s Hospital (Houston, Texas, USA) after informed consent was signed. Paraffin sections from resected ATRTs were used for immunohistochemistry. This study was conducted in accordance and approval by Centralized Institutional Review Board, SingHealth Duke-NUS Academic Medical Center, Singapore. Animal studies were conducted in accordance and approval from Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, SingHealth Duke-NUS Academic Medical Center, Singapore.

Primary tumor specimens

Fresh tumor specimens were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Between 1994 and 2014, a total of 10 primary pediatric ATRTs collected, and a panel of 8 patient-derived ATRT cell lines from international collaborating institutions, were extracted for RNA, used for RNA-Seq and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) in this study. The patient tumor samples were treatment-naïve and obtained at diagnosis (prior to initiation of treatment).

Cell lines and primary cultures

The cell lines were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2. The gender of the patients from which the cell lines were derived: CHLA-04-ATRT (male), CHLA-02-ATRT (male), CHLA-06-ATRT (female) and BT-12 (female); BT-37 (gender unknown), CHLA-05-ATRT (male), ATRT-95 (female). BT-12 obtained from Children’s Oncology Group might have been authenticated, these information were not available to us.

Method details

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells from 7 ATRT cell lines, 4 other embryonal brain tumor cell lines, using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, US) and reverse transcription was performed using high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystem, US), according to manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was performed on C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-rad Laboratories, US) using SensiFAST Probe No-ROX kit (Bioline, UK). Each sample was loaded in triplicates. SYBR-green primers used for the reactions were MELK (H_MELK_1, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and housekeeping gene, GAPDH (H_GAPDH_1, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). PCR was performed with initial polymerase activation at 95 for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 95 for 5 s, annealing at 65 for 10 s and extension at 72 for 20 s). The relative expression for each mRNA was calculated by formula of 2−ΔΔCt.

Proliferation assays (standard growth media)

Tumor cells were seeded in 96-well plates in quadruplicates for each treatment condition (Figure 5B). OTSSP167 was added at the specified concentration range (CHLA-04-ATRT, CHLA-02-ATRT, CHLA-06-ATRT and BT-12 between 0.05 nM and 500 nM; BT-37, CHLA-05-ATRT between 3.125 nM–50 nM. Cell proliferation was determined using Cell Counting Kit-8. Optical density (OD) was measured using Promega GloMax-Multi Detection System.

Flow cytometry

Tumor cells were treated in culture with OTSSP167 at IC50 dose to detect apoptosis (Figure 5C). At each timepoint, cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to manufacturer’s instructions (BD Annexin V FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit I, USA). Stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II, US) at the excitation wavelength of 488 nM (FITC) and 595 nM (PI). Cultured cells were treated with OTSSP167 at IC50 dose to study the cell cycle changes. Cells were harvested and fixed with absolute ethanol. Fixed cells were stained with PI/RNase A mastermix (PI and RNase A) prior to analysis by flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II, US).

Flow cytometry analysis of macrophage marker expression

Human myeloid-derived cells (U937) were treated with PMA (0.1 μg/mL) and different concentration of IL-4 (or different combination of cytokines) and harvested after 48 h of incubation. Cells were stained with PE-conjugated anti-human CD11b antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) or PE-conjugated anti-human CD163 antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) for 10 min according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Isotype control, PE-conjugated REA control antibody was used to confirm antibody specificity. Stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II, USA).

Apoptosis assay of OTSSP167-treated cells cultured in standard growth media versus 6-factor-secretome

Tumor cells were cultured in either standard growth media or 6-factor-secretome for three different duration – 4 h, 24 h and 48 h (Figure 5G). To study the killing effect of OTSSP167, tumor cells cultured in each condition (standard growth media or 6-factor-secretome) were treated with OTSSP167 at IC50 concentration. Cells were harvested at respective timepoints and stained with FITC-Annexin V and/or Propidium Iodide, according to manufacturer’s instructions (BD Annexin V FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit I, USA). Stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACS Canto II, US) at the excitation wavelength of 488 nM (FITC) and 595 nM (PI). A total of 10,000 cells were analyzed per measurement.

Serum-free supernatants

Human myeloid-derived cells (U937) were stimulated with PMA (0.1 μg/mL) and IL-4 (0.02 μg/mL) for 48 h to obtain CD11b+ cells. Stimulated human myeloid-derived macrophages were harvested without trypsinization (Biological replicate 1 in Figures 4D and 5E) and with trypsinization (Biological replicate 2 and 3 in Figures 4D and 5E). These cells highly express CD11b+ with or without trypsinization (Figure S2A). CD11b+ cells were washed twice with PBS. CD11b+ cells were plated with pure RPMI (serum-free) to obtain CD11b+ supernatants (Condition 2). CD11b+ cells (same density) were co-incubated with tumor cells at 1:1 ratio in pure RPMI (serum-free) to obtain co-culture supernatant (Condition 3). Tumor cells were seeded at the same density to obtain tumor supernatant (Condition 1). After 3 h of incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2, supernatants were harvested by centrifugation, verified cell-free (Figure S11) and stored at −80°C prior to further use.

Cell proliferation assay (secretome-based or growth-media-based)

Tumor cells were suspended in 3 types of cell-free supernatants: CD11b+ supernatant, tumor supernatant, and CD11b+-tumor co-culture supernatant. Standard growth media and pure RPMI conditions were used as positive and negative controls respectively. For drug-treated conditions, cells were treated with either DMSO or OTSSP167 at IC50 dose in a 96-well plate. Cell viability was determined by CCK-8 kit according to manufacturer’s manual (Dojindo’s Molecular Technologies Inc, US) at 4 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h time points, over a period of 3 days. Optical density (OD) was measured using Promega GloMax-Multi Detection System (Figures 4A and 4B; Figure S11). All other absorbance (OD) was measured at 450 nm using PerkinElmer 1420 Multi-label Counter Victor3 V. Absorbance of wells with serum-free pure RPMI was used as blank.

Cytokine profiling

Assessment of cytokines present in the cell supernatants were performed using Proteome Profiler Human Cytokine Array (R&D Systems, Inc., #ARY005B). 700 μL of supernatants was used according the manufacturer’s manual. The cytokine spots were visualized by ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-rad, USA).

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) with 1% Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (PPC101) (Sigma Aldrich) on ice. Protein samples were first denatured with 5% β-mercaptoethanol in Laemmli’s buffer at 95°C. Protein concentration was determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit, according to manufacturer’s protocol. Protein lysates were resolved, and equal amounts of denatured proteins was run on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris Protein Gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and transferred onto PVDF membrane. Membrane blots for phosphorylated Histone H2A.X were blocked with 5% Bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 1× TBS-T (10× Tris-buffered Saline, 0.1% Tween 20 (BioRad, USA). Other membrane blots were blocked with 5% skim milk in 1× TBS-T for 1 h and incubated with its respective primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Protein bands were visualized using ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-rad, USA) with Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate, according to manufacturer’s protocol. Primary antibodies used were: Eg5, cleaved PARP, β-actin, cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology), and p53, p-Histone H2A.X, p21 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Secondary antibodies included: anti-rabbit IgG HRP-linked and anti-mouse IgG HRP-linked (Cell Signaling Technology).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunolocalization of CD163 was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded primary pediatric ATRT samples using a commercially available anti-CD163 polyclonal antibody (R&D, Cat #AF1607, 1:50). The Leica BOND-III automated IHC Instrument (Leica Biosystems) was used. The stained sections were reviewed by a pathologist blinded to data/hypothesis associated with the investigation, at the beginning of the study. Sections of human tonsils served as both positive and negative controls, as monocytes stain positively for CD163 while lymphocytes stain negatively for CD163.54

RNA-seq, gene expression microarray and classification of SHH-ATRTs, MYC-ATRTs and TYR-ATRTs

RNA was extracted from fresh frozen patient ATRT tumor samples and a panel of 8 patient-derived ATRT cell lines using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Expression profiles of 10 primary human ATRTs, 8 ATRT cell lines and 10 control brain tissue RNA were generated using RNA Seq according to manufacturer’s protocol (conducted by BGI Sequencing) for the Discovery Set.53 For the Validation Set, CEL files of 49 patient ATRT tumors profiled using Affymetrix HG-U133plus2.0 gene chips (Affymetrix Inc. Santa Barbara, USA), were downloaded from GSE70678.21 Subtype identity of each patient tumor was determined by subtype-specific gene sets published.21 Primary human ATRTs were classified into molecular subtypes, Sonic-hedgehog (SHH)-ATRTs, MYC-ATRTs and TYR-ATRTs.21

Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq)

Genome wide transcriptome analysis was performed for 28 samples (10 primary human ATRTs, 8 ATRT cell lines and 10 control brain tissue RNA) using Illumina HiseqTM 2000 platform.53 Total RNA was extracted and treated with DNase I and mRNA were isolated using Oligo(dT) and are fragmented using the fragmentation buffer and the cDNA is synthesized. The short fragments are purified and resolved with EB buffer and added with single nucleotide A (adenine). The adapter sequences were added to the short fragments and the suitable fragments are selected for the PCR amplification. For QA/QC of the sample libraries we have used Agilent 2100 Bioanaylzer and ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System. The library is sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 4000. Paired end 2 × 100 base pair reads were generated on a HiSeq system (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and on average 8.96 Gb bases of reads was generated from each sample. The low-quality sequencing reads were removed before performing downstream analysis and the clean reads were mapped to hg19 reference genome using HISAT and on average 85.42% reads are mapped to the reference genome. After mapping sequenced reads to reference genome, the transcripts were reconstructed. A total of 23,672 coding genes were identified.

Quantification and statistical analysis

RNA-seq data analysis

Raw reads were subjected to quality control and the filtered clean reads were aligned and mapped to the reference genome using BWA and Bowtie2 (v2.2.5). For gene expression analysis, the clean reads were mapped to reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.2.5). Gene expression levels were calculated with RSEM (v1.2.12). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using Partek Genome Suite to identify genes that were differentially expressed between patient tumors and normal brain controls. The differentially expressed genes were generated using a Benjamini Hochberg’s false discovery rate (FDR), p-value <0.05 with fold change >2 as cut off. Total of 7,313 genes were differentially expressed between 10 patient tumors and 10 normal brain controls with the cut-off of p-value with FDR <0.05 and |FC > 2|.

Microarray data analysis

We downloaded the gene expression microarray data of 49 ATRT patient tumors from GEO (Accession ID: GSE70678.21 Metadata containing tumor subtype identity for the 49 ATRT samples were downloaded. ATRT patient samples of three subtypes (MYC-ATRT, SHH-ATRT and TYR-ATRT) were identified.21 Of 49 patient tumors, 15 tumors were MYC-subtype, 16 tumors were SHH-subtype and 18 tumors were TYR-subtype. The 49 raw CEL files were imported into Partek Genome Suite. RMA background correction and quantile normalization were performed. The gene expression values were log2 transformed and used for downstream gene expression analysis.

Statistical analyses

Student T-Test (unpaired) was used to compare CD11b (ITGAM), CD163, CD68, CXCL10, MELK gene expression between patient tumors. control brain tissues and cell lines. Pearson and Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to identify gene to gene correlation of macrophage markers. The cut-off of p-value <0.05 was used to identify significant Ingenuity Pathway Analyses (IPA) canonical pathways. –log(p-value) greater than 1.3 represents p < 0.05, indicating that the probability of the correlation between an input gene and the mapped pathways in the IPA database did not occur by random chance. The Z score represents the degree of observed changes in gene expression level (increase/decrease) between two conditions that are correlated, to indicate if the expected changes are associated with pathway activation or inhibition. An activation Z score (Z score >2), represented in orange bars (Figure 3E) indicates that the pathway is predicted as activated, while an inhibition Z score (Z score < −2) represented in blue bars, indicates that the pathway is predicted as inhibited.

All data were expressed as the mean ± SD (standard deviation). Statistical analysis was performed using unpaired 2-tailed t test with GraphPad Prism version 8.1.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA), to determine the statistical significance of differences observed between the groups (Figures 3, 4, and 5). For results to be considered statistically significant, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Statistical analyses of animal survival

The survival of each mouse was taken to be the number of days from the date of tumor implantation to the date of death (overall survival), where event = 1 and censored subject = 0 (Figure 5F). Data were censored for mice that were alive at the time of analysis. All Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.1.0 and the survival analysis was performed using Log rank (Mantel-Cox) test.
