Recently Developed Undiagnosed Femoral Arteriovenous Fistulas and Cannulation of the Ipsilateral Femoral Vein for Peripheral Cardiopulmonary Bypass in a Patient Undergoing Redo Open Heart Surgery: Perioperative Implications
Authors: Tony Jose Joseph, Don Jose Palamattam, Thushara Madathil, Nagarjuna Panidapu, Devika Poduval, Rajesh Jose, Praveen Kumar Neema
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Arteriovenous fistula (AVF) results in the shunting of blood from the arterial system into the venous system. The magnitude of the shunt depends on the site, (peripheral vs. central) and the size of the communication(s). The large central shunts are invariably associated with low diastolic blood pressure and congestive heart failure but the smaller ones are challenging to diagnose as they do not have overt clinical features. We describe perioperative implications of recently developed femoral AVFs in a patient undergoing redo aortic surgery (Bentall Procedure) where the ipsilateral femoral vein (femoral vein involved in arteriovenous communications) was used for the establishment of peripheral cardiopulmonary bypass. The patient developed acute limb ischemia and renal failure in the immediate postoperative period and could not be saved. The diagnosis of femoral AVFs was made during the reexploration of the femoral artery for acute limb ischemia.

INTRODUCTION

An arteriovenous fistula (AVF) leads to the shunting of blood from the high-resistance arterial system into the low-resistance venous system, with a subsequent increase in venous return and cardiac output.[1] The communication of the arterial system with the low-resistance venous system results in low diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and the stealing from the arterial system. The hemodynamic effects are often subtle with small and distal fistula but more marked with central fistula.[2] We describe the implications of recently developed AVFs following elective coronary angiography (CAG) in a patient scheduled for redo cardiac surgery. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s relatives to publish this case report.

CASE REPORT

A 63-year-old male, who had undergone aortic valve replacement 12 years before with a bioprosthetic valve, was found to have dilated aortic root and ascending aorta. The heart rate (HR) and blood pressure at the time of admission were 76/min and 110/70 mmHg. Transthoracic echocardiography showed dilated aortic root and ascending aorta, concentric left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, a peak/mean gradient of 32/19 mmHg across the aortic valve prosthesis, trivial AR, good LV and right ventricular systolic function, and grade 1 diastolic dysfunction. The patient underwent CAG via a 6F sheath placed in the right femoral artery (RFA). A few hours after CAG, the sheath was removed; at that time, no swelling or bleeding was noted at the puncture site. The arterial puncture and sheath insertion were landmark-guided. Two days after CAG, the patient was scheduled for Bentall procedure. The HR and blood pressure recorded the day before surgery was 88/min and 110/45 mmHg.

In the OR, monitoring showed HR of 86/min, radial arterial blood pressure (ABP) of 120/40 mmHg, and SpO2 of 98%. Induction of anesthesia and endotracheal intubation were uneventful. For hemodynamic monitoring, a central venous and a pulmonary artery catheter were placed in the right internal jugular vein; an arterial catheter was placed in the left femoral artery under ultrasound guidance (USG). A 5.5 Fr catheter was inserted into the right femoral vein under USG guidance for the insertion of a peripheral venous cannula for establishing peripheral cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Back bleeding of relatively bright blood was noted during the guide-wire insertion in the femoral vein; the position of the guide wire in the femoral vein was rechecked and confirmed by ultrasound, and a cannula was inserted and secured. After cannulation of the right axillary artery, a peripheral venous cannula was inserted through the right femoral vein, and under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance, the cannula was directed into the right atrium; CPB was initiated and midline-sternotomy was performed. The patient was cooled to 18°C and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) was established. The patient underwent a Bentall procedure with a 23-mm bioprosthetic valve and a 28-mm ascending aorta graft. The patient was weaned from CPB with infusions of epinephrine 0.02 µg/kg/min and norepinephrine 0.04 µg/kg/min. Post-CPB, the HR and ABP were 86/min and 120/43 mmHg; TEE examination showed normal biventricular and prosthetic valve function.

Monitoring in the intensive care unit showed a sinus rhythm with HR 80/min, SpO2 100%, and ABP 117/45 mmHg. Over two hours, the left lower limb became warm, but the right lower limb remained cold. The right popliteal, dorsalis pedis, and anterior and posterior tibial artery pulses were feeble. The vascular surgeon advised heparin infusion @10 units/kg/h. Overnight, ABP remained at 120/40–45 mmHg, the right lower limb pulsation remained feeble, and the limb remained cold. A repeat examination of the right lower limb vessels using Doppler (Huntleigh Health Care) continued to show weak pulsations in all the right limb vessels and the RFA showed monophasic flow. A diagnosis of acute limb ischemia with RFA obstruction was considered and the patient underwent emergency exploration of the RFA. A 4-cm × 3-cm pseudoaneurysm of the superficial RFA was identified. The pseudoaneurysm was opened after achieving proximal and distal control. Continuous bleeding was noted from the pseudoaneurysm, and arteriovenous communications were identified. A thrombus was also found in the distal superficial RFA. Thrombo-embolectomy of the superficial RFA and the right popliteal artery was performed, the arteriovenous communications were ligated, and the pseudoaneurysm was repaired. Post-repair, the right lower limb remained cold and pulsations couldn’t be elicited even on Doppler interrogation. Over the next few hours, the right foot became mottled. A fasciotomy of the right lower limb was performed. Later, the patient developed hematuria and a decrease in urine output; a nephrology opinion was sought and hemodialysis was initiated. During hemodialysis, the patient developed hypotension and bradycardia. Hemodialysis was discontinued, and sustained low-efficiency dialysis was initiated, but the patient developed intractable hypotension and died.

DISCUSSION

The temporal sequence of the events indicates that the pseudoaneurysm and AVFs formed following CAG. The fact that the right lower limb did not show signs of ischemia before the surgery indicates that the limb perfusion was adequate. Further, many redo cardiac surgeries are performed with axillary artery-femoral vein CPB with rarely reported limb complications. Seemingly, the presence of AV communications compromised limb perfusion. Kato[3] in an experimental study described narrowing of the distal and proximal arteries immediately after creating a femoral AVF. They further state that the blood flow to the limb is usually restored over two weeks by forming collateral networks.[3] Conceivably, the limb perfusion was compromised before surgery, and it deteriorated further during CPB and DHCA which is supported by the fact that in the ICU, the left lower limb warmed over a few hours but the right lower limb remained cold. Moreover, the medical and surgical measures to improve the right limb perfusion did not improve the flow, and the limb continued to deteriorate. Thrombus formation in the pseudoaneurysm and its embolization in the distal femoral arterial tree could be another possible reason for acute limb ischemia, but the limb perfusion did not improve even after embolectomy.

The key question is how to identify the presence of femoral AVF and how to prevent this adverse outcome. The back bleeding with relatively bright blood was noticed at the time of right femoral vein cannulation, and the presence of low DBP could have warned of the presence of AVF. We did suspect that something was amiss when back bleeding of relatively bright blood was noticed at the time of femoral vein cannulation; hence, we rechecked and confirmed the position of the guide wire by ultrasound. We could have performed a blood gas analysis of the femoral venous blood and transduced the femoral vein pressure; the presence of pulsatile flow and relatively high PO2 in femoral venous blood would have led to the diagnosis of the presence of AVF. The only clinical feature that could have helped in the preoperative diagnosis of the presence of AVF was a severe decrease in DBP after CAG. However, a low DBP is generally not investigated as it does not manifest with overt clinical signs. Moreover, a low DBP (<60 mmHg) is not specific to AVF, and low DBP is often seen in old age, in patients receiving antihypertensive medications, and in the presence of anemia, aortic regurgitation, patent ductus arteriosus, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome. Further, patients with chronically low DBP, such as those with AVF for hemodialysis, patent ductus arteriosus, and aortic regurgitation, adapt to the lower DBP and do not show adverse outcomes in the perioperative period. The knowledge that the distal limb perfusion following AVF formation normalizes over two weeks[3] would have indicated a delay of at least two weeks before the redo surgery, if the femoral vein involved in AVF is to be used for peripheral CPB. The development of iatrogenic AVF and pseudoaneurysm following CAG is described.[456] On ultrasound examination and Color Doppler, a Ying-Yang sign is described in the presence of a pseudoaneurysm.[7]

To summarize, the presence of back bleeding of relatively bright blood from a femoral vein puncture in a patient who had recently undergone CAG using a femoral artery of the same side suggests a possibility of AVF formation. The presence of recently developed low DBP further supports the possibility of the presence of AVF. The presence of pulsatile flow on transduction of the venous pressure and a relatively higher PO2 on blood gas examination will confirm the presence of AVF. Ultrasound and Color Doppler examination of the puncture site can further identify the presence of fistulous communications and pseudoaneurysm. If an AVF is found, it should be repaired before elective surgery and it may be prudent to delay the redo cardiac surgery for at least two weeks if the ipsilateral femoral vein is to be used for peripheral CPB.
