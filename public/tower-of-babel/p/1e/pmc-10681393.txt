Clinical efficacy evaluation and potential mechanism prediction on Guizhi-Shaoyao-Zhimu decoction in the treatment of gouty arthritis based on meta-analysis, network pharmacology analysis, and molecular docking
Authors: Pengda Qu, Haiyang Wang, Wei Wang, Qian Hu, Shiyu Du, Zhaorong Peng, Xiaohu Tang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Guizhi-Shaoyao-Zhimu decoction (GSZD) is a Chinese herb formula. Previous studies have reported that the clinical symptoms and laboratory indicators of gouty arthritis patients could be improved by GSZD. However, no previous study has evaluated and analyzed its efficacy, safety, underlying mechanisms, and the relationship between related ingredients of herbs and targets of gouty arthritis.Methods:Randomized controlled trials of GSZD for gouty arthritis were retrieved from various databases. Meta-analysis was performed by Stata 17 software. Galbraith plot was used to find studies with possible heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed by Egger test and funnel plot. The related ingredients of herbs and the targets of herbs and gouty arthritis were obtained from several databases, such as TCMSP, HERB, and DrugBank. The protein-protein interaction network was conducted by the STRING platform. DAVID database was used to perform GO and KEGG analysis. Molecular docking and visualization of docking results were carried out by AutoDock and PyMOL software.Results:Twenty studies with 1633 patients were included. Meta-analysis indicated that GSZD could better improve the clinical efficiency and visual analogue scale score, and reduce the level of blood uric acid and inflammatory biomarkers (including C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, interleukin 6, interleukin 8, and tumor necrosis factor-α) than conventional treatment. In addition, we retrieved 157 active compounds, 517 herb target genes, 3082 disease targets, and 295 intersection targets of herb and disease. The results of network pharmacology analysis showed that the core related ingredients included quercetin, kaempferol, sitosterol, luteolin, catechin, etc. The core intersection targets contained AKT1, TNF-α, TP53, IL6, etc. And the critical signaling pathways included IL-17, HIF-1, TNF, PI3K-Akt, etc. Among the 56 molecular docking results, only 8 results had binding energy values greater than −5.0 kcal/mol.Conclusion:GSZD could be a satisfactory complementary and alternative therapy for treating gouty arthritis. However, it should be verified by further studies. Future research on gouty arthritis could be conducted from the active components including beta-sitosterol and sitosterol, the targets including TNF-1, IL1B, and ESR1, and the signaling pathways including IL-17 and HIF-1.

1. Introduction

Gouty arthritis (GA) is one of the most common forms of inflammatory arthritis worldwide,[1] which leads to substantial economic burdens and productivity losses.[2,3] In addition, it was also an independent risk factor for chronic kidney disease, hypertension, cardio-cerebrovascular disease, and diabetes, as well as an independent predictor of premature death.[4] According to the 2017 global burden of disease study, the global prevalence rate of GA was 0.52% and increased every year.[5] Moreover, the rate in China was 1.1% and also increased progressively.[6] Although both prevalence rates were increasing, the rate changed more rapidly in China.[7] Therefore, research on the treatment of GA has received increasing attention. Currently, medications used to treat GA include allopurinol, febuxostat, colchicine, benzbromarone, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, etc, according to the recommendations of the American College of Rheumatology and Chinese Society of Endorinology.[1,8] Although the use of the above drugs has achieved relatively satisfactory clinical efficacy, their adverse reactions cannot be ignored, such as allopurinol-induced hypersensitivity and benzbromarone-induced explosive liver necrosis.[9,10] Consequently, it is necessary to find alternative strategies to treat GA more safely and effectively.

In China, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been considered an important complementary and alternative therapy to treat GA. Because it has been used to treat rheumatic and joint diseases for thousands of years, which has accumulated a large amount of satisfactory experience. Guizhi-Shaoyao-Zhimu decoction (GSZD) is a formula of TCM, which is composed of 9 herbs, including Radix Aconiti Lateralis (Fuzi), Radix Saposhnikoviae divaricatae (Fangfeng), Rhizoma Atractylodis macrocephalae (Baizhu), Rhizoma Zingiberis Recens (Shengjiang), Herba Ephedrae (Mahuang), Rhizoma Anemarrhee (Zhimu), Radix Paeoniae Alba (Shaoyao), Ramulus Cinmomi (Guizhi), and Radix Glycyrrhizae (Gancao). In the theory of TCM, GSZD has the effect of dispelling wind, removing dampness, clearing yang and dispersing cold, which is closely related to the etiology and pathogenesis of GA. In addition, previous studies reported that the clinical symptoms and laboratory indicators of GA patients could be significantly improved by GSZD, including the degree of pain and swelling, blood uric acid, c-reactive protein, and interleukin 6.[11–13] Several studies found that GSZD therapy had fewer adverse reactions than the primary therapeutic strategy.[14–16] Accordingly, GSZD is considered an essential option for the treatment of GA. However, there are few evidence-based conclusions due to the small sample sizes and some controversial results of available clinical trials. Moreover, even if it is verified to be effective and safe, what is not yet clear is the underlying mechanism and the relationship between the related ingredients of GSZD and the targets of GA. Accordingly, it is significant to evaluate and explore the efficacy, safety, underlying mechanism, and relationship between the ingredients and the targets of GSZD in the treatment of GA.

In recent years, the efficacy, safety, underlying mechanism, and relationship between the ingredients and the targets of TCM have been further confirmed and clarified with the development of evidence-based medicine, network pharmacology analysis, and molecular docking. Meta-analysis is an important method for evaluating the efficacy and safety of TCM prescriptions. Network pharmacology analysis is an important means to study the underlying mechanisms and the relationship between the ingredients and the targets of Chinese herbals. Molecular docking is widely used to predict the binding stability of the ingredients to disease targets. As a result, their combination enables research not only to synthesize clinical data but also to explore underlying mechanisms and the relationship mentioned above. This could provide a more scientific reference for the selection of alternative drugs to treat GA. In this study, we first evaluated the efficacy and safety of GSZD in patients with GA by meta-analysis, then explored its underlying mechanism as well as the relationship between the related ingredients and the targets by network pharmacology analysis, and finally predicted the binding stability of the partial ingredients and targets by molecular docking.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Meta-analysis of the efficacy and safety of GSZD for GA

2.1.1. Protocol registration.

This meta-analysis was conducted and reported according to the guidelines of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).[17] Research protocol (No. CRD 42023391335) was registered in the PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/).

2.1.2. Database and search strategy.

The databases included Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane, Embase, Wanfang database, China national knowledge infrastructure (CNKI), and China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP). Studies were searched from the establishment of the database until February 1, 2023, with language limited to Chinese or English. The search terms were MeSH terms combined with the keywords “guizhi shaoyao zhimu” and “gouty arthritis.” Article references were further searched for additional relevant publications. The specific search strategy of PubMed was shown in Table S1 (Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K719).

2.1.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: The type of study was a randomized controlled trial. Research subjects were diagnosed with GA according to the diagnostic criteria of the 1987 American Rheumatology Association guidelines or the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria.[18,19] The experimental group was treated with GSZD or GSZD combined with conventional treatment, including etoricoxib, febuxostat, allopurinol, benzbromarone, etc. The control group was treated with conventional treatment only, including etoricoxib, febuxostat, allopurinol, benzbromarone, etc. The outcome indicators included effective rate, blood uric acid (BUA), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 8 (IL-8), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), visual analogue scale (VAS), or adverse events (AEs). Primary outcomes included BUA, CRP, ESR, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. Secondary outcomes included effective rate, VAS, and AEs. Exclusion criteria were as follows: Experimental group with acupuncture, tuina, acupoint application, or other external therapies of Chinese medicine, Duplicate publications, Studies with incomplete data, and Unable to extract data for research.

2.1.4. Data extraction.

After excluding irrelevant studies by reading titles and abstracts, 2 independent reviewers collected data from the included articles using a pre-designed form. The extracted information included: the investigator, year of publication, number of cases, age, intervention measures, duration of intervention, result measurement, and adverse events. Any controversy was resolved by discussion with XT.

2.1.5. Bias assessment.

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated independently by 2 reviewers using the Cochrane Collaboration tool.[20] Three levels, low risk, high risk, and unclear risk, were used to evaluate 7 items: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blind method, blind evaluation of results, incomplete result data, selective reporting, and other biases. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with XT.

2.1.6. Statistical analysis.

Meta-analysis was carried out by Stata 17 software (Stata Corp., College Station, TX). Weighted mean difference (WMD) or standardized mean difference was used to represent continuous variables, and risk ratio was used for binary variables. WMD was used when the same measures or units were applied to the same intervention effect. Otherwise, standardized mean difference was used. All variables were expressed with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The heterogeneity was tested by the Q test. The fixed effects model was used when I2 was less than or equal to 50%. When I2 was >50%, the random effects model was used, and sensitivity analysis was conducted. Subsequently, we used the Galbraith plot to find studies with possible heterogeneity. After these studies were removed, the meta-analysis was re-performed. The results of the meta-analysis were expressed by forest plots. Publication bias was assessed by Egger test and funnel plots when the number of relevant studies was greater than or equal to 10.

2.2. Network pharmacology analysis of GSZD for GA

2.2.1. Screening of related ingredients and targets.

The related ingredients of GSZD were obtained from TCMSP (https://tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) under the condition of oral bioavailability (OB) greater than or equal to 30% and drug-likeness (DL) greater than or equal 0.18.[21] The targets of the related ingredients were collected from the Swiss Target Prediction database (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) and HERB (http://herb.ac.cn/).[22] The targets of GA were retrieved from DrugBank (https://www.drugbank.ca/), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (http://omim.org/), and TTD (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). The intersectional targets of GSZD and GA were extracted by Venn diagram (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html). The herb–component–target network was drawn by Cytosacpe software.[23]

2.2.2. Protein-protein interaction and gene enrichment analysis.

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction analysis of the overlapping targets was conducted by the STRING platform (https://string-db.org/),[24] and the result of STRING was imported into Cytoscape software for PPI network construction. Gene enrichment analysis of overlapping targets was conducted on the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp).[25] After that, we screened the top 20 biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways according to the P value of gene enrichment analysis results. The results of GO and KEGG were visualized by SRPLOT (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/srplot), an online platform for data analysis and visualization.

2.2.3. Molecular docking.

The structures of key targets were obtained from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), and the structures of key ingredients were obtained from TCMSP or PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Molecular docking and the calculation of binding energies was conducted by AutoDock software, and the visualization of the docking results were then performed by PyMOL software.[26] It is generally accepted that binding energies below −4.25 kcal/mol indicate specific binding activities of ligands to receptors, below −5.0 kcal/mol indicate better binding activities, and below −7.0 kcal/mol indicate robust binding activities.[27]

3.1.2. Bias assessment.

All studies mentioned randomization, and 7 studies described the method of randomization as a random number table,[12,14,28,32,35,37,38] and 2 studies described the method of randomization as a hospital visit or order of participation.[11,33] Therefore, in terms of selection bias, the 7 studies were rated as low risk, the 2 studies were rated as high risk, and the rest were rated as unclear risk. Only one research performed allocation concealment.[38] The partial outcome data of 5 studies were incomplete,[11,13,32,38,39] so they were rated as high-risk in the aspect of attrition bias (Fig. 2). Overall, the quality of included studies was not high.

Risk of bias assessments of included studies. (A) Overall risk; (B) Detailed risk.

3.1.3. Effective rate.

Nineteen studies reported the effective rate.[11–16,29–41] There were 777 patients in the treatment group and 764 in the control group. Meta-analysis indicated that the effective rate of GSZD treatment was significantly higher than conventional treatment (I2 = 54.75%, random effects model, RR = 0.18, 95% CI [0.12, 0.24], P < .001, Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis showed that the significant heterogeneity might be caused by 1 study,[36] and it was decreased after this study was excluded (I2 = 42.76%, fixed effects model, RR = 0.13, 95% CI [0.09, 0.16], P < .001) (see Fig. S1, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K721). We conclude that GSZD treatment could be more effective than conventional treatment.

Forest plot of effective rate.

3.1.4. Blood uric acid (BUA).

Sixteen studies reported BUA.[11,12,14–16,28–30,32,34–38,40,41] There were 679 patients in the treatment group and 666 in the control group. Meta-analysis indicated that GSZD therapy had a better effect in lowering the level of BUA compared with conventional therapy (I2 = 98.20%, random effects model, WMD = −1.55, 95% CI [−2.45, −0.66], P < .001, Fig. 4). Sensitivity analysis revealed that the significant heterogeneity might be caused by 3 studies,[14,15,36] and it was reduced after these studies were excluded (I2 = 63.92%, random effects model, WMD = −0.74, 95% CI [−0.95, −0.54], P < .001) (see Fig. S2, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K722). Together, these results provide important insights that the level of BUA could be better reduced by GSZD than conventional treatment.

Forest plot of blood uric acid (BUA).

3.1.5. C-reactive protein (CRP).

Eleven studies reported CRP.[12,14–16,29,30,34,35,37,38,41] There were 436 patients in the treatment group and 432 in the control group. Meta-analysis showed that GSZD therapy could significantly reduce the level of CRP than conventional treatment (I2 = 73.57%, random effects model, WMD = −0.43, 95% CI [−0.70, −0.17], P < .001, Fig. 5A). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the significant heterogeneity might be caused by 2 studies,[15,38] and it was decreased after these studies were excluded (I2 = 18.36%, fixed effects model, WMD = −0.31, 95% CI [−0.46, −0.16], P < .001) (see Fig. S3, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K723). Overall, these results provide significant evidence that the level of CRP could be better lowered by GSZD than conventional treatment.

3.1.6. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).

Eleven studies reported ESR.[12,14–16,29,30,34,35,37,38,41] There were 436 patients in the treatment group and 432 in the control group. Meta-analysis revealed that GSZD treatment had a better effect in reducing the level of CRP than conventional treatment (I2 = 76.65%, random effects model, WMD = −0.51, 95% CI [−0.79, −0.23], P < .001, Fig. 5B). Sensitivity analysis showed that the significant heterogeneity might be caused by 3 studies,[15,30,38] and it was decreased after these studies were excluded (I2 = 13.34%, fixed effects model, WMD = −0.31, 95% CI [−0.47, −0.15], P < .001) (see Fig. S4, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K724). Taken together, these results suggest that the level of ESR could be better reduced by GSZD than conventional treatment.

3.1.7. Interleukin 6 (IL-6).

Four studies reported IL-6.[12,28,31,41] There were 166 patients in the treatment group and 166 in the control group. Meta-analysis indicated that GSZD treatment could significantly reduce the level of IL-6 than conventional treatment (I2 = 29.57%, fixed effects model, WMD = −0.49, 95% CI [−0.71, −0.28], P < .001, Fig. 5C). Overall, these results show that GSZD treatment could better lower the level of IL-6 than conventional treatment.

3.1.8. Interleukin 8 (IL-8).

Four studies reported IL-8.[28,31,32,41] There were 192 patients in the treatment group and 190 in the control group. Meta-analysis revealed that GSZD treatment had a significant effect in lowering the level of IL-8 than conventional treatment (I2 = 38.48%, fixed effects model, WMD = −1.12, 95% CI [−1.33, −0.90], P < .001, Fig. 5D). The results in this section illustrate that the level of IL-8 could be better lowered by GSZD than by conventional treatment.

3.1.9. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).

Three studies reported TNF-α.[28,31,32] There were 206 patients in the treatment group and 204 in the control group. Meta-analysis showed that GSZD treatment could significantly lower the level of TNF-α than conventional treatment (I2 = 0%, fixed effects model, WMD = −0.64, 95% CI [−0.86, −0.41], P < .001, Fig. 5E). In summary, these results indicate that the level of TNF-α could be better lowered by GSZD than conventional treatment.

3.1.10. Visual analogue scale (VAS).

Three studies reported VAS.[14,16,35] There were 148 patients in the treatment group and 143 in the control group. Meta-analysis indicated that GSZD treatment had a better effect on lowering the score of VAS than conventional treatment (I2 = 93.91%, random effects model, WMD = −1.25, 95% CI [−2.29, −0.21], P = .02, Fig. 6). We speculated that the heterogeneity was due to the subjective measurement of VAS in different studies (see Fig. S5, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K725). Overall, these results show that the score of VAS could be better reduced by GSZD than conventional treatment.

Forest plot of visual analogue scale (VAS).

3.1.11. Adverse events (AEs).

Six studies reported AEs.[11,12,15,32,35,41] There were 254 patients in the treatment group and 251 in the control group. The AEs included palpitation, rash, dizziness, diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, etc. These symptoms were mild, tolerable, and could be relieved automatically. Meta-analysis revealed that GSZD treatment had a lower incidence of AEs than conventional treatment (I2 = 30.55%, fixed effects model, RR = −0.77, 95% CI [−1.44, −0.10], P = .02, Fig. 7). Taken together, these results indicate that GSZD may be safer than conventional treatment.

Forest plot of adverse events (AEs).

3.1.12. Publication bias.

Effective rate, BUA, CRP, and ESR were evaluated for publication bias. The funnel plot and Egger test results indicated that there was a publication bias in the effective rate (Egger test: P < .001, Fig. 8A) and BUA (Egger test: P < .001, Fig. 8B). However, CRP (Egger test: P = .0814, Fig. 8C) and ESR (Egger test: P = .1063, Fig. 8D) did not show publication bias. Overall, we hold the review that the results of the effective rate and BUA should be treated with caution.

Funnel plot of publication bias. (A) Effective rate (Egger test: P < .001); (B) Blood uric acid (BUA) (Egger test: P < .001); (C) C-reactive protein (CRP) (Egger test: P = .0814); (D) Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (Egger test: P = .1063).

3.2. Results of the network pharmacology analysis

The analysis in this section was based on the 9 herbs of GSZD mentioned in the introduction.

3.2.1. Analysis of related ingredients and targets.

We collected 157 related ingredients of GSZD (see Table S3, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K726), 517 targets corresponding to the related ingredients (see Table S4, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K727), and 3082 targets corresponding to GA (see Table S5, Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K728). There were 295 intersection targets of GSZD and GA (Fig. 9A), and details were provided in Table S6 (Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K729). The network of the 157 related ingredients and 517 targets was shown in Figure S6 (Supplemental Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K730), and the network of the related ingredients and 295 intersection targets was shown in Figure 9B. According to the degree value of the related ingredients and intersection targets, we obtained the top 20 related ingredients (Fig. 9C), including quercetin, beta-sitosterol, Kaempferol, etc.

3.2.2. PPI and gene enrichment analysis.

According to the degree value, we obtained the top 20 targets from the PPI network (Fig. 10A), such as AKT1, TNF, and IL6 (Fig. 10B). The results of gene enrichment analysis showed that the main BP items (Fig. 11A) included positive regulation of gene expression, negative regulation of apoptotic process, inflammatory response, etc. The main MF items (Fig. 11B) included enzyme binding, protein kinase activity, transcription factor binding, etc. The main CC items (Fig. 11C) included plasma membrane, extracellular space, extracellular region, etc. The main KEGG pathway items (Fig. 11D) included the TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway, etc. The results in this part show that the related ingredients of GSZD may play a role in the treatment of GA by multiple targets, signal pathways, etc.

3.2.3. Analysis of molecular docking.

Seven targets and 8 ligands, selected from the top 20 targets and the top 20 related ingredients, were used to calculate the binding energy value. The binding energy results showed that the lowest value was −9.18 kcal/mol, the highest value was −4.19 kcal/mol, and the average value was −6.31 kcal/mol (Table 2). Subsequently, ten combinations with low binding energy were visualized (Fig. 12), among which TNF-1_beta-sitosterol docking had the lowest binding energies (−9.18 kcal/mol), followed by IL1B_beta-sitosterol docking (−8.78 kcal/mol) and TNF-1_sitosterol docking (−8.78 kcal/mol). Taken together, these results indicate that most of the combinations we choose for molecular docking are relatively stable.

4. Discussion

In the first part, to evaluate the efficacy and safety of GSZD for GA, we conducted a meta-analysis with 1633 subjects in 20 articles. After that, we explored the underlying mechanism of GSZD in treating GA as well as the relationship between the related ingredients of drugs and targets of GA by network pharmacology analysis. Finally, we used molecular docking to predict the binding stability of the partial ingredients and targets. The current research found that, compared with conventional treatment, GSZD treatment could improve the clinical efficiency and VAS score and reduce the level of BUA and inflammatory biomarkers, including CRP, ESR, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. The rate of adverse reactions of GSZD treatment may be lower than that of conventional therapy. The network pharmacology analysis results showed that the core related ingredients included quercetin, kaempferol, sitosterol, etc, and the core intersection targets included AKT1, TNF-α, TP53, etc. According to KEGG results, GSZD may play a role in the treatment of GA through IL-17 and HIF-1 signaling pathways. Another important finding was that the binding of our selected related ingredients and targets was stable, especially the 10 combinations visualized in the molecular docking results. Collectively, GSZD could be used as an important alternative therapy for GA. Its therapeutic effects may be exerted by multiple related ingredients, targets, mechanisms, and signaling pathways.

There are 5 possible reasons why GSZD could be an important alternative therapy. First, the level of uric acid could be reduced by herbs or herbal extracts included in GSZD. GA is closely related to the excessive deposition of urate crystals in joints and kidneys caused by the high level of uric acid in the body.[42] Urate lowering is important for the treatment of GA. Many scholars hold the view that the level of uric acid, in vitro or in vivo, could be reduced by the ethanolic extract of ramulus cinmomi,[43] cinnamaldehyde,[44] and total glucosides of paeony.[45,46] According to Ying and Huang,[47] the level of serum uric acid could be reduced by ephedrine in hyperuricemia model rats by regulating urine pH. In addition, inhibition of xanthine oxidase (XOD) activity is critical for urate-lowering. Because XOD is a crucial enzyme in purine catabolism that catalyzes hypoxanthine and xanthine to uric acid in human metabolism.[48] Studies have shown that XOD activity could be inhibited by ramulus cinmomi oil,[49] cinnamaldehyde,[43,44] and total glucosides of paeony.[46] One study by Cao et al[50] suggested that licoflavonol may decrease the level of uric acid in adenosine-induced renal tubular epithelial cell NRK-52E, possibly via XOD.

Second, herbs or herbal extracts included in GSZD could exhibit anti-inflammatory effects. In addition to crystal deposition, GA is also closely associated with the inflammatory response to crystal production.[51] The mechanism is that monosodium urate crystals induce inflammation by activating neutrophils and inflammasomes to release similar proinflammatory cytokines.[52] Previous studies have reported that GSZD could exhibit anti-inflammatory effects in vitro and in vivo by inhibiting the activations of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and NLRP3 inflammasome.[53–55] And it could also play an anti-inflammatory role by decreasing NLRP3 and ASC expression in macrophages and neutrophils.[54,55] Zhou et al[53] found that GSZD could significantly reduce the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, NLRP3, and caspase-1 in the peritoneal exudates of mice. According to Shin et al[56] ethanolic extract of ramulus cinmomi could inhibit NLRP3, nlrc4, etc, inflammasome activation, reduces IL-1β secretion, and consequently suppresses inflammatory responses in GA mice. In addition, total glucosides of paeony were found to suppress inflammation in THP-1 macrophages by regulating MALAT1/miR-876-5p/NLRP3 axis and TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway.[57]

Third, related ingredients in GSZD have urate-lowering or anti-inflammatory effects, and most of these related ingredients could act on the targets or signaling pathways of GA. The details can be seen in the following discussion of related ingredients.

Fourth, adverse effects appear less frequently in GSZD therapy compared with conventional treatment. The present study suggests that patients treated with GSZD had fewer adverse effects than conventional therapy. Adverse effects were reported in 6 studies, which include palpitation, rash, dizziness, diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, etc. Most symptoms were mild, tolerable, and could be relieved automatically.

In addition, some of the results of this meta-analysis is consistent with those of Zhang et al[58] They conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 outcome indicators in 13 studies on GSZD treatment for GA. The consistent findings included that GSZD could improve clinical efficiency and reduce the rate of AEs and the level of BUA, CRP, ESR, and IL-6. And there was a publication bias in BUA. However, part of their results differed from ours, such as no heterogeneity in effective rate and CRP, and no publication bias in effective rate. Nonetheless, the reliability of our conclusions was not particularly affected. This may be linked to the fact that, compared with their study, we included 20 studies and 9 outcome indicators, and used the Q test for heterogeneity testing and the Galbraith plot to search for studies with possible heterogeneity. These could provide support for the conclusions of our research. In summary, these reasons indicated that GSZD could be a satisfactory complementary and alternative therapy for GA.

Turning now to the results on network pharmacology analysis, we found that the key related ingredients included quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, catechin, etc, which possess anti-inflammatory or urate-lowering effects. Quercetin was reported to exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 production, inflammasome activation, NF-κB activation, or inflammasome components mRNA expression.[59–61] And it could also abolish cartilage degeneration and decrease chondrocytes apoptosis by attenuating oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and associated apoptosis.[62] Feng et al[63] found that quercetin could diminish the expression of IL-6, IL-17A, and IL-17F in the IL-17 pathway. In addition, Mo et al[64] concluded that serum urate levels could be decreased by quercetin by inhibiting XOD activity. Recent evidence suggests that luteolin exerted anti-inflammatory effects by altering M1/M2 polarization of macrophages and further down-regulating p-STAT3 B and up-regulating p-STAT6.[65] And luteolin could also be anti-inflammatory by inhibiting the production of TNF-α, MMP-2, MMP-8, and MMP-9 and the expression of MMP-1, MMP-3, and MMP-13.[66] In the same vein, Yang et al[67] found that luteolin diminishes human neutrophil inflammatory responses by inhibiting Raf1-MEK-1-Erk. In addition, luteolin could decrease the level of serum urate by inhibiting XOD activity.[64] Kaempferol was found to exert anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting COX-1 and 2 enzymes in vitro cell-free assay systems.[68] Moreover, it could also act in an anti-inflammatory role by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway to reduce the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, COX2, NOS, and TNF-α.[69] Similarly, Al-Ishaq et al[70] found that kaempferol decreased the RhoA/Rho kinase-induced pro-inflammatory pathway in normal rat kidney-52E cells and renal proximal tubule epithelial cells. Moreover, Mo et al[64] argued that kaempferol could decrease serum urate levels by inhibiting XOD activity. Previous evidence suggests that catechin could protect against gout attacks by regulating the modulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation.[71] In addition, catechin could also enhance the excretion of uric acid.[72] Kuang et al[73] speculate that catechin plays an anti-inflammatory effect by mediating ferroptosis on RAW 264.7. Similarly, Jung et al[74] found that catechin could downregulate the level of TNF-α and IL-6 in RAW 264.7 cells, and the phosphorylation of MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathway-related proteins. In summary, quercetin, luteolin, kaempferol, catechin, etc, may be the main related ingredients involved in GSZD treatment for GA.

The current research found that the core targets included AKT1, TNF-α, TP53, etc, which are closely related to inflammatory reaction and uric acid lowering. In vivo experiments such as that conducted by Reyes-Gordillo et al[75] have shown that activation of AKT1 could lead to inflammation and proliferation. Kim et al[76] found that AKT1 deficiency can aggravate renal damage and increase renal tubular cell apoptosis and inflammatory reaction. Differential regulation of AKT1 can change the pro-inflammatory microenvironment to an anti-inflammatory environment by inducing macrophage polarization and regulating the expression of NF-κB.[77] As one of the most important pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α is involved in vasodilation and edema formation, and can also lead to oxidative stress at the site of inflammation and indirectly induce fever.[78] In addition, the TNF superfamily plays a crucial role in many inflammatory and autoimmune diseases, including stimulating lymphocyte activation, increasing lymphocyte survival and function, inducing cell death, and driving inflammatory responses.[79–82] Amaral et al[83] found that TNF-α, in vivo, can drive the expression of pro-IL-1β mRNA and IL-1β protein, and its transmembrane form can initiate MSU-induced inflammation. In the same vein, Zhang et al[84] indicated that the production of IL-1β and TNF-α is positively correlated with the development of gouty inflammation. Interestingly, one of the key related ingredients in our study, quercetin, was observed to modulate TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 to treat GA.[63] GA is associated with apoptosis of articular chondrocytes. TP53 could influence stress responses and target gene expression in a variety of cells, and induce cell senescence and apoptosis by regulating cell division and proliferation.[85,86] TP53 is closely related to the level of cellular immune infiltration, which directly affects human immune response capacity.[87] Gain of function mutations of TP53 play a crucial role in inflammation. One study by Ham et al[88] reports that the gain of function mutations of TP53, via NF-κB signaling, could upregulate CCL2 and TNF-α expression. Together, these studies indicated that AKT1, TNF-α, TP53, etc, may be the main targets involved in GSZD treatment for GA.

According to the results of KEGG, we held the view that IL-17 and HIF-1 signaling pathways may be critical pathways for GSZD to treat GA. The main symptoms of GA include enhanced local inflammation accompanied by joint redness and severe pain.[89] IL-17, an important proinflammatory factor, plays an important role in inflammatory and immune responses.[90] And it is closely related to the progression of GA inflammation.[91,92] The mechanism may be that IL-17 binds to its receptor and activates downstream pathways, including MAPK and NF-κB, thereby inducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α.[90,93,94] There is a consensus among Chen et al[95] and Li et al[96] that IL-17, IL6, and IL-1β may play an important role in the formation and development of GA, according to the results of RNA network analysis. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that there was a positive correlation between the severity of GA symptoms and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-17, and TNF-α.[92,97] Pinto et al[98] found that intra-articular injection of IL-17 can increase the production of TNF-α and IL-1β, mediate the recruitment of neutrophils, and induce hyperalgesia and arthritis. Notably, the inflammatory response induced by IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α could be amplified by the synergistic action of IL-17 presence at the painful site of inflammatory arthritis.[99] The hypoxia-inducible factor is an important transcriptional regulator of cell metabolism and cell stress adaptation caused by oxygen deficiency. In the HIF-1 signaling pathway, it can participate in the activation of NF-κB and glycolysis, when inflammation causes local environmental hypoxia and HIF activation.[100] Several studies have revealed that glycolysis could strongly induce and support proinflammatory macrophage functions in distinct methods.[101–103] And glycolysis is also crucial for IL-4-induced macrophage activation.[104,105] HIF-1α, a subunit of HIF, plays a key role in treating GA. Because HIF-1α could directly or indirectly inhibit the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β secretion in bone marrow-derived macrophages.[106] In most rheumatoid synovium, HIF-1α is abundantly expressed by macrophages, mainly close to the intimal layer and in the subintimal zone.[107] Westra et al[108] suggested that inflammatory cytokines, in HIF-1 expression, may be one of the important inducers. Likewise, Zamudio-Cuevas et al[94] held the view that after stimulation with MSU crystals, the expression of many factors is increased in patients, such as HIF-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-8. In addition, studies have shown that the level of IL-17, HIF-1, etc, can be reduced by the active components included in the GSZD, such as beta-sitosterol and luteolin.[94,109,110] The others relevant studies came up for example in the discussions of the related ingredients of GSZD. Overall, there seems to be some evidence to indicate that IL-17 and HIF-1α signaling pathways may be the main pathways involved in GSZD treatment for GA. However, the specific mechanism and pathway need to be further verified.

Finally, the results of 56 molecular dockings showed that most of the combinations of core related ingredients and core targets might form stable structures, especially beta-sitosterol and sitosterol, with lower binding energies to considerable targets. This was why we mainly visualized the molecular docking of beta-sitosterol and sitosterol. We found that the lowest binding energy of the combination was TNF-1_beta-sitosterol, followed by TNF-1_sitosterol, IL1B_beta-sitosterol, and ESR1_beta-sitosterol. Several studies have revealed that beta-sitosterol may possess anti-inflammatory effects.[111,112] Many researchers have argued that sitosterol could reduce the expression of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, and promote the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, IL-17, MMP-2/9, and IL-10.[113–115] Moreover, there is a consensus among de Souza et al[116] and Ryz et al[117] that the symptoms of GA could be alleviated by sitosterol, such as foot edema. However, very little is known about these combinations, so further experimental investigations are needed to verify molecular docking results.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that integrates meta-analysis, network pharmacology analysis, and molecular docking to evaluate and explore the efficacy, safety, underlying mechanisms, and relationship between the ingredients and the targets of GSZD in the treatment of GA. The meta-analysis was performed on 20 articles that addressed the specific study question. Based on the results of the meta-analysis, network pharmacology analysis and molecular docking were conducted to reveal the possible CC, BP, MF, KEGG pathways, and the relationship between the related ingredients and the specific targets. However, several limitations need to be considered. First, the articles included in this study span several decades (published between 1998 and 2022). During this period, significant changes may have occurred in the testing methods, sensitivity of laboratory indicators, etc. Second, sensitivity analysis showed significant heterogeneity in BUA, CRP, ESR, and VAS. Despite the results of CRP and ESR being robust after removing the literature that may have contributed to the heterogeneity, we were unable to explain these differences in detail. Third, the overall quality of the included studies was not high. Of the 20 included studies, most studies mentioned randomization only in words. This may be related to the characteristics of TCM syndrome differentiation and treatment.

5. Conclusion

The evidence from this study suggests that GSZD could be a satisfactory complementary and alternative therapy for the treatment of GA, especially in improving the clinical efficiency and VAS score and reducing the level of BUA and inflammatory biomarkers, including CRP, ESR, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. However, given the quality of the included studies, further research is needed to improve the scientific credibility of the literature. By network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis, we hold the view that future research on GA could be conducted from the active components including beta-sitosterol and sitosterol, the targets including TNF-1, IL1B, and ESR1, and the signaling pathways including IL-17 and HIF-1. Further research is needed to verify these results.
