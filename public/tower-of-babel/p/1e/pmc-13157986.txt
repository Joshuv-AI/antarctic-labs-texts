Field-Based Mesocosm Evidence for the Role of Natural Nutrient Sources in Cyanobacterial Blooms
Authors: Yong-Jun Kim, Jae-Hun Lee, Young-Cheol Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harmful algal blooms (HABs) dominated by toxigenic cyanobacteria represent a growing threat to freshwater ecosystems and drinking water security. While phosphorus is widely recognized as a key driver of cyanobacterial proliferation, the ecological roles of naturally derived nutrient sources remain poorly understood. In this study, field-based mesocosm experiments were conducted to compare the effects of various nutrient sources originating from both autochthonous and allochthonous origins—sediments, cyanobacterial scums, benthic periphyton mats, and agricultural soils—on cyanobacterial and Microcystis growth, as well as microcystin (MC) production. All nutrient treatments were adjusted to a uniform total phosphorus concentration (0.4 mg/L), and nitrogen limitation was excluded. Cyanobacterial biomass, assessed via chlorophyll-a and phycocyanin fluorescence, was consistently higher in all nutrient-amended mesocosms than in the inorganic phosphorus control. Notably, periphyton mat extracts promoted sustained biomass accumulation during the latter incubation stages. MC concentrations were elevated in all nutrient-amended treatments relative to the control, although no significant differences were observed among the various nutrient sources. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that while total Microcystis abundance did not differ significantly across treatments, the abundance and relative proportion of toxigenic Microcystis (mcyA gene) were significantly higher in periphyton mat–amended mesocosms. In contrast, MC production normalized to toxigenic cell abundance showed no significant variation among treatments, indicating that strain-specific traits exert a stronger influence on toxin production than the nutrient source type. These findings demonstrate that organically derived nutrient sources, particularly periphyton mats, play a critical role in sustaining HABs and selectively promoting toxigenic strains. Consequently, effective HAB mitigation strategies should extend beyond simple total phosphorus reduction to include the management of internal nutrient sources and phosphorus speciation within aquatic systems.

Introduction

Most reservoirs and river-type aquatic systems have experienced progressive eutrophication due to continuous population growth and the expansion of industrial and agricultural activities, which have increased nutrient loading into aquatic environments [1]. Consequently, harmful algal blooms (HABs), dominated by toxigenic cyanobacteria, have occurred repeatedly during the summer season [2,3]. In Korean freshwater systems, the cyanobacterial genera Microcystis, Anabaena (Dolichospermum), and Aphanizomenon are most frequently observed, with the genus Microcystis showing pronounced dominance under high-temperature summer conditions [4]. The massive proliferation of cyanobacteria not only disrupts aquatic ecosystems by reducing water transparency, depleting dissolved oxygen, and causing fish kills, but also poses serious operational challenges for drinking water sources, including increased treatment costs and filter clogging [5,6,7]. Furthermore, some cyanobacteria are known to produce potent toxins such as microcystins (MCs), anatoxin-a, and cylindrospermopsin. Among these, MCs are hepatotoxic compounds that can cause severe adverse effects in humans and livestock [8,9,10].

Worldwide, concerns regarding drinking water safety associated with cyanobacterial toxins have been continuously reported, making the prevention and mitigation of HABs a critical issue in water resource management [11]. The relationship between Microcystis growth and toxin production has yielded conflicting results. Some studies have shown that high concentrations of inorganic nutrients enhance the expression of MC biosynthesis genes, whereas others suggest that toxin production is more strongly influenced by the growth phase, cellular physiological status, and environmental stressors rather than by nutrient conditions alone [12,13,14]. Nevertheless, there is a general consensus that nutrient availability contributes more to an increase in the total toxin load in the water column by promoting cyanobacterial biomass accumulation, rather than directly regulating the rate of toxin biosynthesis.

Numerous studies have investigated the environmental conditions that promote HABs. Cyanobacterial growth is regulated by a combination of physical factors, including water temperature, light intensity, hydraulic residence time, and pH [15,16], as well as chemical factors such as nutrient availability, particularly nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) [13,17]. In freshwater ecosystems, phosphorus is widely recognized as the primary limiting nutrient for cyanobacterial growth. Long-term field studies have demonstrated that phosphorus reduction is one of the most effective strategies for controlling cyanobacterial proliferation [18]. Because some cyanobacteria possess the ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen, phosphorus often acts as the primary growth-limiting factor [18,19]. Cyanobacteria can physiologically benefit from a “luxury uptake” strategy, whereby excess dissolved inorganic phosphorus (DIP) is assimilated and stored intracellularly, allowing continued growth under subsequent P-limited conditions [20,21]. When DIP becomes depleted, cyanobacteria secrete alkaline phosphatase (APase), enabling the hydrolysis and utilization of organic phosphorus (OP) as an alternative source [22,23,24]. Consequently, once cyanobacteria become dominant, their growth may persist despite reductions in external phosphorus inputs, sustained by internally derived OP. Nutrient inputs into reservoirs are generally categorized into external and internal loading. External loading originates from point sources, such as wastewater treatment plants, and non-point sources, including agricultural runoff [25]. Internal loading is typically defined as the release of nutrients accumulated in sediments into the overlying water column in response to changes in physicochemical conditions, such as temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, flow velocity, and benthic microbial activity [26]. In addition to sediment-derived loading, the senescence and decomposition of aquatic organisms can substantially increase nutrient concentrations, particularly OP, in the water column [27].

Recent studies have reported that only a small fraction of total phosphorus (TP) is present as biologically available DIP, while a substantial proportion exists as OP associated with organic matter [28]. This OP accumulates as internally derived nutrient pools, including biogenic organic matter produced after cyanobacterial senescence. In particular, sediments and biological debris have been identified as major contributors to accelerated nutrient recycling by promoting phosphorus release under hypoxic conditions [29,30].

During spring and early summer, green algae and diatoms typically dominate under relatively low water temperatures [31]. As water temperatures exceed approximately 25°C, these algal groups often decline rapidly, leaving behind persistent organic matter in the aquatic environment. Additionally, floating benthic periphyton mats frequently develop in littoral zones and near artificial macrophyte islands; their subsequent decomposition during the summer can release substantial amounts of nutrients into the water column [32].

Floating periphyton mats differ from cyanobacterial scums in that they are structurally larger and more cohesive, making them relatively amenable to physical removal. Therefore, the proactive removal of floating periphyton mats during the spring may serve as an effective management strategy to mitigate the intensity and frequency of summer HABs. Recent studies have further indicated that benthic periphyton mats can function as significant nutrient sources that fuel cyanobacterial proliferation [33].

In this study, a series of field-based mesocosm experiments were conducted to compare the effects of diverse nutrient sources derived from both autochthonous and allochthonous origins—including sediments, cyanobacterial scums, periphyton mats, and agricultural soils—on the growth of cyanobacteria and Microcystis, as well as MC production. Based on these findings, this study aims to propose practical and cost-effective management strategies for mitigating HABs in freshwater systems.

Materials and Methods

Collection of nutrient source samples

To compare and analyze the growth characteristics of harmful cyanobacteria in response to different nutrient sources, four types of samples were collected from both autochthonous and allochthonous origins. Floating benthic periphyton mats, cyanobacterial scum, and sediments were selected to represent internal loading and biomass decomposition-derived sources, while agricultural soil was used as an external loading source.

Benthic periphyton mats were collected from Giori in Daechung Reservoir (36°20′18″ N, 127°33′54″ E). Surface sediments were sampled from the Seohwacheon inflow area of Daechung Reservoir (36°21′33″ N, 127°34′03″ E) using a grab sampler, targeting the upper sediment layer. Cyanobacterial scum dominated by Microcystis spp. was collected from Dodongnaru in the Nakdong River (35°41′10″ N, 128°22′19″ E). Agricultural soil was collected from farmland near Chusori in the Daechung Reservoir watershed (36°21′16″ N, 127°33′33″ E).

Nutrient extracts preparation

Periphyton mats and sediment samples were dried at 50°C for 3 days, ground, and stored at −70°C. Sediments were homogenized using a glass rod prior to drying. Cyanobacterial scum samples were settled for one week at 4°C under dark conditions to separate the scum from reservoir water; the floating fraction was then collected, dried, and stored at −70°C. Agricultural soil samples were cleared of plant debris, dried, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and stored until use.

To minimize variability in nutrient release, sediments were extracted using a combination of ultrasonication and moist heat treatment. Dried sediments were ultrasonicated at 80 W for 30 s (five cycles), stirred at room temperature for 1 h, and autoclaved at 121°C for 30 min. After settling for 24 h, the supernatant was collected for experiments. Cyanobacterial scum and periphyton mats were ultrasonicated under the same conditions to disrupt cell walls and simulate in situ senescence and decomposition, followed by stirring for 1 h and settling for 24 h. Agricultural soil samples were stirred for 1 h without additional physical treatment to simulate rainfall runoff and allowed to settle for 24 h. All extracts were stored at 4°C until use [33]. Rainfall-driven runoff is a major pathway for nutrient export from agricultural soils to aquatic systems. When rainfall exceeds soil infiltration capacity, surface runoff mobilizes dissolved nutrients as well as particulate-bound phosphorus associated with eroded soil particles, often resulting in a pronounced "first-flush" effect during high-intensity precipitation events [34]. In the present study, this rainfall-driven runoff process was simulated to extract nutrients from agricultural soils.

Field-based mesocosm experiments

Field-based mesocosm experiments were conducted using reactors installed near an artificial macrophyte island in the Munui area of Daechung Reservoir (36°30′34″ N, 127°30′13″ E). Each mesocosm had an effective working volume of 100 L and was mounted on a floating frame constructed from aluminum profiles (40 mm × 40 mm) with Styrofoam floats, ensuring that the reactors remained buoyant at the water's surface (Figure 1). The mesocosms were positioned at a fixed depth of approximately 50 cm below the water surface. To minimize contamination prior to cyanobacterial inoculation, the mesocosms were initially filled with reservoir water for a 10-day pre-equilibration period, followed by thorough rinsing.

Each mesocosm was filled with 95 L of reservoir water, to which four types of nutrient source extracts were added to achieve a target total phosphorus (TP) concentration of 0.4 mg/L. To minimize dilution effects, the extracts were prepared at concentrations at least 20-fold higher than the target TP concentration, ensuring that the added volume did not exceed 5% (5 L) of the total mesocosm volume. In the control mesocosm, a K₂HPO₄ solution was added to achieve the same TP concentration (0.4 mg/L). Duplicate mesocosms were used for the periphyton mat treatment, whereas a single mesocosm was utilized for each of the other three nutrient treatments and the control.

To prevent nitrogen limitation, NaNO₃ was added to all mesocosms at identical concentrations to ensure sufficient nitrogen availability. Cyanobacterial inocula were collected during bloom periods from cyanobacterial scum dominated by Microcystis spp. near Baekje Weir in the Geum River (36°19′27″ N, 126°57′03″ E). Following nutrient amendment and inoculation, 1 L samples were collected at 4–6 day intervals for physicochemical analyses, biomass determination, intracellular MC quantification, and molecular analysis to compare the effects of phosphorus speciation and nutrient sources. In this study, triplicate samples were collected from each mesocosm at each sampling interval; these were analyzed independently, and the resulting mean values are presented.

Environmental monitoring and analytical methods

Meteorological and hydrological data were obtained from the Water Resources Management Information System (WAMIS; http://www.wamis.go.kr), including precipitation and hydrometeorological records from the Gadeok monitoring station (36°34′41″ N, 127°34′38″ E). During the experimental period, water temperature, pH, and precipitation were monitored. Water temperature was recorded at 30-min intervals using automatic temperature loggers (HOBO® Water Temp Pro v2, Hobo, USA) installed inside and outside the mesocosms. pH was measured in situ at each sampling event using a portable pH probe (PHC30101, Hach, Korea).

Changes in cyanobacterial biomass were assessed using fluorescence measurements of phycocyanin, a cyanobacteria-specific pigment [35]. Samples were ultrasonicated (40 W for 20 s) prior to analysis, and fluorescence was measured using a fluorometer (Promega, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 595 nm and an emission wavelength of 670 nm. Results were expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU). Physicochemical parameters analyzed included TP, dissolved TP (DTP), total nitrogen (TN), dissolved TN (DTN), and chlorophyll-a (Chl-a). All analyses were conducted according to the Standard Methods [36].

For MC analysis, samples were filtered through Whatman GF/C filters, and toxins were extracted by adding 6 mL of 5% (v/v) acetic acid followed by vortexing for 1 min. Samples were extracted under dark conditions at 4°C for 24 h, centrifuged at 7,010 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected and purified using Sep-Pak C18 cartridges. Purified MCs were eluted with methanol, completely evaporated, re-solubilized in 1 mL of methanol, and filtered through a 0.2-μm PTFE syringe filter prior to HPLC analysis. An RP-18 column was used with a mobile phase consisting of distilled water, acetonitrile, and trifluoroacetic acid (68:32:0.5, v/v). MCs were detected at 238 nm using a photodiode array detector (Waters 2998, Waters, UK) [37].

DNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Samples were filtered through polycarbonate membrane filters, and DNA was extracted using a combination of enzymatic digestion and physical disruption (freeze–thaw cycling) [37]. Extracted DNA was quantified using the Quant-iT™ PicoGreen™ dsDNA Assay [38]. The distribution and abundance of Microcystis and toxigenic Microcystis were analyzed via quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) targeting the Microcystis-specific 16S rRNA gene and the mcyA gene [37,39].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). Differences among treatments were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test when significant differences were detected [40]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Meteorological factors and pH dynamics during incubation

During the incubation period, the daily maximum and minimum water temperatures were 32.9°C and 23.9°C, respectively. Until September 8 (day 30), the temperature remained above 25°C throughout the day, providing optimal conditions for cyanobacterial growth [41]. A total of nine rainfall events occurred during the experimental period. The highest daily precipitation (25 mm/d) was recorded on August 25 (day 16), followed by 11 mm/d on day 27 and 8 mm/d on day 24. The remaining six rainfall events were minor, with daily precipitation amounts below 5 mm. Following the peak rainfall event on day 16, the average daily water temperature in the mesocosms decreased from 28.9°C to 27.1°C. Thereafter, the water temperature stabilized at around 28°C but showed a consistent decline after the rainfall event on day 27. After day 30 of the incubation, the average water temperature dropped below 25°C, and the daily maximum temperature did not exceed 26°C after September 15.

The pH of the control ranged from 10.01 to 10.89 (mean: 10.51). Mesocosms amended with nutrient sources exhibited average pH values ranging from 10.21 to 10.50. A one-way ANOVA indicated no significant difference in mean pH among treatments (F = 1.36, p = 0.27).

Concentrations of nutrients

TN concentrations ranged from 8.53 to 12.78 mg/L in the nutrient-treated mesocosms. The highest TN concentration was observed in the cyanobacterial lysate–amended mesocosm, whereas the lowest concentration was recorded in the agricultural soil extract–amended mesocosm. The coefficients of variation in the nutrient-amended mesocosms ranged from 5.7% to 7.2%, indicating that differences in TN among treatments were not significant during the incubation period (Figure 2a). DTN concentrations decreased over time (Figure 2b). Initial DTN concentrations in the field mesocosms ranged from 8.09 to 9.36 mg/L depending on the nutrient source, whereas final concentrations ranged from 1.54 to 3.18 mg/L. In the control, DTN decreased by 59.4%, while greater reductions of 80.9% and 70.9% were observed in the agricultural soil extract– and periphyton mat extract–amended mesocosms, respectively. According to Baldia et al. [42], the half-saturation constant (Kₛ) of Microcystis aeruginosa for NO₃⁻-N is 0.530 mg/L. Although DTN was not speciated into NH₄⁺-N, NO₂⁻-N, and NO₃⁻-N in this study, DTN in the surface waters of reservoirs predominantly exists as NO₃⁻-N [43]. Since the DTN concentrations remained higher than the reported Kₛ value for NO₃⁻-N, nitrogen was not considered a limiting factor for cyanobacterial growth.

Initial TP concentrations in the agricultural soil extract– and sediment extract–amended mesocosms were 0.406 and 0.395 mg/L, respectively, and decreased to 0.293 and 0.291 mg/L by the end of the experiment (Figure 2c). TP concentrations in the periphyton mat extract–amended mesocosm decreased from 0.399 to 0.307 mg/L. After 36 days of incubation, TP concentrations had declined in all nutrient-amended mesocosms. Notably, the attachment and growth of the green alga Spirogyra spp. were observed on the mesocosm walls after day 20. Therefore, the periphytic growth on the walls—which was excluded from sampling—was considered to have influenced the TP measurements. During the first 15 days, DTP concentrations decreased rapidly (Figure 2d).

Changes in biomass according to nutrient sources

Chl-a concentrations in the nutrient source–amended mesocosms were higher than those in the control mesocosm at all sampling points, except for day 12 (Figure 3a). On day 26, Chl-a concentrations declined or plateaued in all nutrient treatments, with the exception of the sediment extract-amended mesocosm. Compared with values on day 21, Chl-a concentration in the sediment extract–amended mesocosm increased by 36.3% (from 289.8 to 395.0 mg/m³), whereas the periphyton mat extract–amended mesocosm showed a marginal increase of 2.6%. In contrast, Chl-a concentrations decreased by 6.9% in the agricultural soil extract–amended mesocosm (from 230.2 to 214.3 mg/m³) and by 2.1% in the cyanobacterial lysate–amended mesocosm. After day 30, Chl-a concentrations increased in all treatments, with particularly rapid growth observed in the periphyton mat extract– and cyanobacterial lysate treatments.

Phycocyanin concentrations exhibited trends distinct from Chl-a, increasing continuously until day 16 (Figure 3b). In the sediment and periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosms, phycocyanin concentrations remained in a plateau phase until day 31, whereas a plateau was observed until day 26 for the agricultural soil and cyanobacterial lysate treatments. During this stagnation phase, the average phycocyanin concentration was lowest in the control (5,732 RFU), while higher values were recorded in the cyanobacterial lysate (9,566 RFU), periphyton mat (9,259 RFU), agricultural soil (10,654 RFU), and sediment extract-amended mesocosms (10,963 RFU). Except for day 36, phycocyanin levels in all nutrient-amended mesocosms were consistently higher than those in the control.

To further evaluate biomass dynamics, the experimental period was divided into growth and stagnation phases. During the growth phase (days 0–16), the rate of phycocyanin increase was lowest in the periphyton mat treatment (453 RFU/d), followed by the agricultural soil (505 RFU/d), sediment (524 RFU/d), and cyanobacterial lysate treatments (568 RFU/d; n = 4). The growth rates in all nutrient-amended mesocosms exceeded those of the control. One-way ANOVA performed on mean phycocyanin concentrations during the stagnation phase revealed significant differences among treatments (F = 38.87, p < 0.01). Tukey’s HSD post hoc test indicated that the control differed significantly from all nutrient-amended mesocosms (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the periphyton-amended mesocosm showed significantly higher values than the sediment-amended mesocosm (p < 0.05), while no significant differences were detected among the other nutrient-amended treatments.

Microcystin analysis

Analysis of MCs in the field mesocosm experiment revealed the presence of three MC variants: MC-LR, MC-RR, and MC-YR. MC concentrations in the nutrient-amended mesocosms were consistently higher than those observed in the control (Figure 4). The maximum MC concentrations reached 24.00 µg/L in the cyanobacterial lysate-amended mesocosm, 18.53 µg/L in the agricultural soil extract-amended mesocosm, and 17.65 µg/L in the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosm. In contrast, the sediment extract-amended mesocosm showed a comparatively lower maximum concentration of 6.70 µg/L. Except for the sediment-amended treatment, MC concentrations in all nutrient-amended mesocosms increased continuously throughout the incubation period. In the agricultural soil and cyanobacterial lysate treatments, MC levels increased gradually until day 26, followed by a sharp increase thereafter. Conversely, MC concentrations in the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosm showed a sustained increasing trend throughout the experiment. In the sediment extract-amended mesocosm, MC concentrations peaked on day 26 and subsequently declined.

Consistent with earlier laboratory studies involving two toxigenic Microcystis spp., this field mesocosm experiment found no significant differences in total MC concentrations across nutrient treatments [33]. This suggests that strain-specific traits play a more dominant role in MC production than the specific nutrient source composition.

Number of Microcystis and toxigenic Microcystis

Analysis of Microcystis 16S rRNA gene copy numbers revealed that the agricultural soil extract–amended mesocosm showed a continuous increase until day 31, reaching a maximum of 1.21 × 10⁷ copies/mL (Figure 5a). In the cyanobacterial lysate–amended mesocosm, the highest Microcystis 16S rRNA abundance was observed on day 12 (1.30 × 10⁷ copies/mL), while the periphyton mat extract–amended mesocosm reached a maximum of 8.86 × 10⁶ copies/mL; subsequently, both treatments exhibited a continuous decreasing trend. In contrast, the sediment extract–amended mesocosm exhibited a peak abundance of 1.32 × 10⁷ copies/mL on day 21. Although the timing of maximum Microcystis 16S rRNA abundance differed among nutrient sources, all treatments, with the exception of the agricultural soil-amended mesocosm, showed a consistent decline following the peak.

Analysis of the mcyA gene revealed that, unlike other nutrient sources, the agricultural soil extract–amended mesocosm exhibited the highest mcyA gene copy number on day 41 (1.83 × 10⁵ copies/mL). In contrast, the cyanobacterial lysate– and sediment extract–amended mesocosms reached their maximum mcyA gene copy numbers on day 31, with values of 2.44 × 10⁵ and 2.31 × 10⁵ copies/mL, respectively. Notably, the periphyton mat extract–amended mesocosm showed the highest mcyA gene abundance on day 21 (3.52 × 10⁵ copies/mL), maintained elevated levels from days 7 to 21 before exhibiting a gradual decline thereafter (Figure 5b).

To evaluate the changes in the total abundance of Microcystis following various nutrient treatments, 16S rRNA gene copy numbers were analyzed. The results indicated no statistically significant differences among the treatment groups (one-way ANOVA; F = 1.78, p = 0.17). In contrast, the copy numbers of the toxin-producing gene, mcyA, showed significant differences across the nutrient-amended mesocosms (one-way ANOVA; F = 3.60, p < 0.05). Subsequent Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests revealed that a significant difference existed only between the control and the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosms (padj < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in other pairwise comparisons (padj > 0.05).

The ratio of toxigenic strains (mcyA) to the total Microcystis population (16S rRNA) from day 7 to day 31 varied distinctly by treatment (Table 1). The proportion in the control mesocosm was 9.80 ± 2.46%. Similarly low proportions were maintained in the agricultural soil extract- (13.32 ± 4.69%), cyanobacterial lysate- (19.70 ± 13.48%), and sediment extract- (15.46 ± 8.30%) amended mesocosms. In contrast, the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosm exhibited a significantly higher occupancy of toxigenic strains at 46.46 ± 21.58%. One-way ANOVA confirmed that the mean differences between mesocosms were statistically significant (F = 5.81, p < 0.01). Post-hoc analysis further indicated that the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosm was a significantly higher proportion than both the control and other nutrient-amended treatments. These findings suggest that components derived from periphyton mats serve as a key factor in promoting the selective growth of toxin-producing Microcystis.

To evaluate toxin production efficiency, MC concentrations were normalized to the abundance of toxigenic Microcystis (expressed as MCs per mcyA gene copy) for samples collected from day 7 to 31. In the control mesocosm, the mean normalized concentration was 12.94 ± 15.88 fg/mcyA gene copy (Table 2). In comparison, the mesocosms amended with agricultural soil extract, cyanobacterial lysate, sediment extract, and periphyton mat extract showed mean values of 5.54 ± 3.31, 4.89 ± 1.54, 3.77 ± 1.09, and 2.18 ± 1.44 fg/mcyA gene copy, respectively, with the lowest efficiency observed in the periphyton mat extract-amended treatment. One-way ANOVA was performed after excluding an outlier from the control group on day 31 (36.65 fg/mcyA gene copy), which was markedly higher than other samples. The analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in normalized toxin production among the treatment groups (F = 1.73, p = 0.20). Previous studies have reported that toxigenic Microcystis carries a single copy of the mcyA gene per cell [39]. Furthermore, WHO data indicate that the average MC production per toxigenic cell is approximately 200 fg/cell [44]. When converting the mcyA gene copy numbers to cell counts in this study, the average toxin production per toxigenic Microcystis was 4.24 ± 2.17 fg/cell, a value considerably lower than the WHO-reported average.

Discussion

This study represents one of the first comprehensive investigations evaluating how naturally derived nutrient sources influence the occurrence and persistence of HABs using a field-based mesocosm approach. The results clearly demonstrate that cyanobacterial biomass, MC production, and the growth of toxigenic Microcystis are not governed solely by TP concentrations, but are jointly regulated by the specific characteristics of the nutrient sources. Following amendment with four nutrient sources (agricultural soil, cyanobacterial scum, sediment, and periphyton mat), Chl-a and phycocyanin concentrations were consistently higher than those in the control mesocosm amended with IP (K₂HPO₄). Notably, growth was particularly pronounced in the periphyton mat extract-amended mesocosm during the later stages of incubation. Elevated MC concentrations were detected in all nutrient-treated mesocosms compared to the control, suggesting that OP sources are more effective in promoting MC production than IP. Furthermore, analysis of the toxin-producing gene (mcyA) revealed a higher abundance in the periphyton mat treatment than in other nutrient-amended mesocosms, indicating that periphyton mat-derived components may selectively facilitate the growth of toxigenic Microcystis.

In a previous study, the TP concentrations of three nutrient sources—periphyton mat, cyanobacterial scum, and sediment—were reported as 1.92, 1.66, and 0.076 mg/g (dry weight), respectively. Among these, dissolved TP (DTP) accounted for 26.0%, 36.7%, and 50.0% of the TP, respectively [33]. In addition, orthophosphate (PO43--P) comprised 22.9%, 31.9%, and 39.5% of the TP in each source. In the present study, the TP concentration added to each mesocosm was standardized at 0.4 mg/L. Accordingly, the concentrations of dissolved OP (DOP; calculated as DTP minus orthophosphate) were estimated to be 0.013, 0.019, and 0.042 mg/L for the periphyton mat, cyanobacterial scum, and sediment treatments, respectively, corresponding to 3.1%, 4.8%, and 10.5% of the total added phosphorus. Although DOP constituted only a small fraction of the TP, its addition resulted in enhanced MC production compared with the IP-only control. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying this effect, further molecular characterization of the DOP composition is warranted.

External nutrient sources exerted substantial effects on cyanobacterial biomass and growth rates in this study. Mesocosms amended with extracts of periphyton mat, cyanobacterial scum, sediment, and agricultural soil consistently exhibited higher cyanobacterial biomass, as reflected by phycocyanin concentrations, than the IP-only treatments. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that both internal and external nutrient loads function not merely as nutrient reservoirs, but as ecologically active drivers of bloom development [45,46,47]. In stratified or polymictic lakes, such as large reservoir systems, both internal phosphorus loading and watershed-derived inputs promote cyanobacterial blooms; specifically, internal loading plays a critical role in sustaining blooms even after external inputs are reduced [47,48].

Periphyton mats can act as potential internal nutrient sources by storing substantial amounts of nutrients and releasing phosphorus and organic carbon during decomposition under high-temperature conditions [49,50]. In this study, the mesocosm amended with periphyton mat extract exhibited relatively low initial growth rates, followed by stable and sustained biomass accumulation, ultimately resulting in high final biomass. This pattern suggests that biologically derived nutrient sources provide a more complex and persistent nutrient environment than single-form phosphorus additions. Similarly, the elevated biomass observed in the agricultural soil and sediment extract treatments indicates that watershed-derived inputs can promote HABs when transported into aquatic systems through physical and chemical processes. Agricultural intensification and soil erosion increase phosphorus and organic matter loads to downstream waters, potentially enhancing both the intensity and duration of HABs in reservoirs and rivers [51,52].

With respect to MC production, the present study indicates that strain-specific characteristics of Microcystis exert a stronger influence than the nutrient source type. No significant differences in MC concentrations were observed among the nutrient source treatments, and the MC-to-mcyA gene copy ratio did not differ significantly across treatments. These results suggest that MC production is primarily governed by the intrinsic toxin-producing capacity of specific Microcystis strains rather than by the nutrient sources themselves. Gobler et al. [53] similarly reported that while diverse nutrient sources, such as fertilizers and sewage, can increase Microcystis bloom intensity and toxin concentrations, the magnitude and toxicity of blooms vary depending on strain composition and environmental conditions. Therefore, effective HAB management strategies must address not only biomass reduction but also the monitoring and control of toxigenic strains.

Recent nutrient management studies have further demonstrated that nitrogen and trace elements interact with phosphorus to influence bloom development and toxicity [54,55]. Alterations in N:P ratios can promote Microcystis dominance and MC production, while micronutrients such as Fe and Zn have been shown to affect both growth efficiency and toxin synthesis [54,56]. These findings highlight the limitations of single-nutrient control strategies and underscore the need for integrated nutrient management approaches.

Conclusions

This study experimentally demonstrates the effects of naturally derived nutrient sources on the development and persistence of HABs. OP contained in these sources can increase both the intensity and longevity of blooms. In contrast, MC production appears to be predominantly determined by the characteristics of toxigenic strains rather than by the nutrient source type. These findings suggest that HAB mitigation strategies should integrate biomass control, regulation of watershed-based nutrient inputs, and targeted monitoring of toxic cyanobacterial strains. Importantly, the results emphasize that reducing TP alone is insufficient; effective management of phosphorus speciation and watershed-derived nutrient sources is essential for fundamentally mitigating HABs.
