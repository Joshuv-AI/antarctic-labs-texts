A unique symmetrical variation of the median accessory anterior digastric muscle
Authors: Daniel Andrew Martinez, Everett Johnson, Nicole Marie Zipay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA median accessory anterior digastric muscle was identified during a routine dissection of the submental triangle in an embalmed 86-year-old Caucasian female cadaver. The muscle had a digastric and a mylohyoid variant. The digastric variant was triquetral, symmetrical, with a mandibular apex attachment at the symphysis between the digastric fossae and two base attachments at the right and left intermediate tendons, approximately 3 mm apart. A midline raphe connected the base fibers, extending cranially toward the apex. The mylohyoid variant presented as two thin muscle strands originating from the cranial mylohyoid near the raphe, and inserting into the intermediate tendons, parallel to the mylohyoid nerve’s path. No enlarged lymph nodes were noted. This accessory muscle, a potential landmark in neck surgeries, may be mistaken for the digastric, risking incomplete tissue resection. This report describes its anatomy to guide surgeons in neck dissections.Keywords: Accessory anterior digastric muscle, Digastric variation, Submental triangle, Neck surgery

Introduction

Understanding the submental triangle is imperative to distinguish normal structures from variants or pathology for head and neck surgeries. The anterior digastric muscle serves as a landmark in procedures such as dissection for malignancy, adipose and lymph nodes removal, or in reconstructive surgeries involving osseomyocutaneous flaps [1]. Cervical lymph nodes of this region have shown to be common sites for metastatic extension arising from the oral cavity, and paranasal sinuses [2]. Normal nodes on computed tomography are typically homogenous and their signal attenuation is roughly equivalent to that of skeletal muscle [3]. Variations in this region are often asymptomatic but hold clinical significance because an accessory digastric muscle may be mistaken for the digastric itself, potentially leading to errors in assessing the submental triangle’s extent or resection of diseased tissue. The digastric muscle also aids in identifying the mylohyoid’s free edge, locating the lingual nerve, and submandibular duct during gland resection [4]. The digastric muscle is usually manipulated for face and neck reconstructive surgeries [5].

This case report describes the anatomy of a unique variation of the anterior digastric muscle as a reference for surgeons in neck dissections. The report will also discuss anatomy, function, and innervation of the normal anterior digastric muscle, and compare this variation with similar variation described in the literature.

Case Report

A median accessory anterior digastric muscle was discovered during a routine dissection in a formalin-embalmed 86-year-old Caucasian female cadaver. Standard measurements were taken, and a plasticine clay model was constructed to illustrate the muscle’s configuration. The findings included digastric and mylohyoid variants. The digastric variant was triquetral and symmetrical, originating between the digastric fossae and inserting into the bilateral intermediate tendons, approximately 3 mm apart (Fig. 1). The base formed an arch rising 2 mm from the tendon insertions to its midpoint (Fig. 2). Fibers from the apex extended 2.5 mm caudally, superficial to and parallel with the mylohyoid’s midline raphe, fluting bilaterally by 1 mm as they merged with the arch (Fig. 3). Normal configuration of the anterior belly from a study by Zdilla et al. [6] of 23 (11 female) Caucasian cadavers similar to the cadaver in this case, measured approximately 3.5 cm (Fig. 4), falling within range of the mean (3.87±0.74 cm). By comparison, this variant is approximately 1 cm shorter. The mylohyoid variant presented as two thin muscle strands originating from the cranial mylohyoid near the raphe and inserting into the intermediate tendons, paralleling the mylohyoid nerve (Fig. 1; open arrowheads). The mylohyoid nerve emerged anterior to the submandibular gland, traveled medially to the anterior digastric muscles; then cranially to the mylohyoid muscle, and a smaller portion branched medially from the insertion to the digastric to innervate the accessory belly at the bilateral base arch midway to the merging point with the apex (Figs. 5, 6).

Discussion

Anatomy and function

The digastric muscles coordinate with other head and neck muscles during jaw opening, swallowing, chewing, and speech. They are comprised of anterior and posterior bellies, originating from the mandibular digastric fossa and mastoid process, respectively, and inserting into the hyoid’s intermediate tendon. They elevate the hyoid during swallowing, protect the airway, and assist in jaw opening [7]. In addition, the suprahyoid muscles play a vital role in speech by rapidly adjusting the position of the mandible during articulation [8]. Most of the cranial muscles involved in jaw movement, including speech, receive descending corticobulbar projections from the primary motor cortex of both hemispheres [9]. In contrast, the digastric muscles on each side are usually activated in unison [10]. The variant of the donor was symmetrical and midline-originating, it is plausible, then, that it shares the normal actions of the anterior digastric muscles, given its innervation by the mylohyoid nerve and lack of associated mandibular or muscular abnormalities upon observation.

Innervation

The anterior belly of the digastric develops with the mylohyoid from the first branchial arch. Both are innervated by branching of the mylohyoid nerve such that the mylohyoid nerve travels between the muscles before separating inferiorly to supply the anterior digastric belly from its deep surface and superiorly to supply the mylohyoid from its superficial surface. Based on these relationships and innervation patterns, Sakamoto and Akita [11] proposed a hypothesis to classify the aberrant muscle bundles within the submental triangle region.

When remnants are left between the two layers as intermediate primordia, they develop into aberrant muscle bundles. These were classified as either unilateral or crossover types and had a development of digastric or mylohyoid origin. The unilateral type had the origin and insertion of the bundles attached to the mandible or the hyoid bone ipsilateral. The intermediate primordium superficial to the mylohyoid nerve develops into a digastric pattern, while that lying deep develops a mylohyoid pattern. The crossover type variants arose from one side of the mandible or mylohyoid muscle and inserted contralateral to the hyoid bone. The bundles develop bilaterally and join the median line with double innervation from each side. When superficial and deep primordia join, they become a hybrid pattern combining the anterior belly and the mylohyoid [11]. The variant accessory muscle found in this case presented with origins and insertions that made it difficult to classify according to its innervation.

Comparison with other variations

Digastric muscle variations began to be reported at the end of the 20th century. Doubling of the anterior belly is a common variation, and the anterior bellies of opposite sides may be united by conversion of the fascia that typically passes between them into muscular tissue [12]. Since this variant is symmetrical, this report will limit its comparisons to variations that originate at the midline, and/or inserts bilaterally to the anterior digastric muscles or into the intermediate tendons. Zdilla et al. [13] described the common features of the arrowhead variation: “(1) two sheet-like muscle bellies that do not appear to have the bulk of a typical anterior belly digastric muscle, but rather are flattened like the mylohyoid; and (2) muscle bellies oriented in the shape of an arrowhead, diverging from the mylohyoid raphé and inserting posteriorly at the intermediate tendon of the digastric muscle. Sargon et al. [14] in 1999 described an accessory variant as “originating from the middle digastric fossa and bifurcating into the right arm inserting into the right anterior belly and the left arm inserting into the left anterior belly.” Additionally, the arrowhead-shaped musculature frequently has an accessory belly that oftentimes projects from the midline of the arrowhead to the mandible. The arrowhead-shaped musculature has also been reported to occur in conjunction with a mentohyoid muscle, in which case the “arrowhead” lies deep to the mentohyoid muscle.” The mentohyoid was first described by Macalister [15] in 1875 as a muscle extending between the body of the hyoid and the symphysis of the mandible.

Sargon et al. [14] described a similar midline-originating muscle inserting into the anterior digastric bellies, while this case inserts directly into the tendons. The accessory belly that projects from the arrowhead to the mandible is like the anatomy in this case. The exception is that the “arrowhead” is not connected to the mylohyoid except for the two singular stands at the right and left cranial end of the mylohyoid. Despite this similarity, it is the description by Sargon et al. [14] that most closely resembles the present case. Both have an origin in the midline; however, this variation inserts into the ipsilateral intermediate tendons while the case described by Sargon et al. [14] inserts into the normal digastric muscle bellies. The feature that makes this case most unique from others and make it difficult to categorize are the two singular strands of muscles fibers that arose from the mylohyoid and inserted into the intermediate tendons along with the base of the triquetral variation. Speculation may include that this variation may have originated as an arrowhead variation with an accessory belly and then separated from the mylohyoid except for the two singular thin strands. This hybrid configuration challenges existing classifications, combining digastric and mylohyoid patterns.

In conclusion, a unique variation of an accessory digastric muscle was discovered during a routine dissection of the submental triangle during a gross anatomy course. The features that make this finding unique are that the variation had a digastric and a mylohyoid variant. The digastric variant was triquetral in shape with an origin at the midline mandibular symphysis and insertions at the right and left intermediate tendon. The mylohyoid variant presented as two singular strands of muscles fibers that arose from the mylohyoid and inserted into the intermediate tendons. This variation highlights the need for surgeons to be aware of such anomalies to ensure accuracy during head and neck surgeries.
