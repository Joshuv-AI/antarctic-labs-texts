Repurposed hnRNPC binds mature mRNAs and safeguards the mitotic transcriptome
Authors: Liat Lev-Ari, Sandra Laster, Andrea Atzmon, Daniel Blumenkrants, Daniel Benhalevy, Orna Elroy-Stein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpon entry to mitosis, RNA metabolism is broadly suppressed. While mechanistic understanding is limited, the mitotic transcriptome is generally preserved, allowing daughter cells an economical start. We used highly effective cell cycle synchronization to specifically characterize mitotic heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein C (hnRNPC), an abundant nuclear RNA-binding protein known for intron binding and splicing regulation. Mitotic hnRNPC exhibited a density shift, forming low- and high-density (LD and HD) complexes. RNA-seq analysis combined with fluorescent crosslinking and immuno-precipitation (fCLIP) revealed hnRNPC binding to 17.1% and 8.7% of expressed genes in LD and HD complexes, respectively, with most sites mapping to protein-coding genes. Mediated by its known cooperative interaction with U-rich motifs along ≳700 nt-long targets, mitotic hnRNPC acquired prevalent interactions with exons, predominantly within the 3′ untranslated region of mature mRNAs. Mitotic hnRNPC also retained intron interactions, predominantly in LD-hnRNPC RNPs, which comigrated with both the spliceosome and mono-ribosomes through a density gradient. HD-hnRNPC, which co-migrated with poly-ribosomes, predominantly interacted with mature mRNA complexes comprising new targets specific to mitosis. Downregulation of hnRNPC elicited a global negative effect on the abundance of its mitotic targets. The data point to the global role of mitotic hnRNPC as a stabilizer of pre-mRNA and mRNA.

Introduction

Nuclear envelope breakdown, a fundamental process of cell division, results in a transient loss of subcellular compartmentalization. This event is coupled to the temporary suppression of RNA metabolism, including transcription, splicing [1–5], and translation [6–10], and it is not entirely understood how dividing cells preserve their transcriptome during this phase [9, 11–14]. Many nuclear RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) participate in splicing during interphase, while their roles following nuclear envelope breakdown remain elusive. Several other RBPs have been shown to participate in distinct biological functions in different cellular contexts [15–24], but comprehensive information about all RBPs is still lacking. Our previous work using quantitative proteomics has shown that mitotic poly-ribosomes associate with a subset of proteins involved in RNA processing, with hnRNPC being the most enriched [25]. This observation prompted us to investigate the role of hnRNPC during mitosis. hnRNPC is an evolutionarily conserved, ubiquitously expressed, and predominantly nuclear RBP first identified as a component of the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) core particle [26]. It interacts with intron sequences and accompanies newly transcribed pre-mRNAs during their post-transcription maturation process [26, 27]. A previous study applying the crosslinking immuno-precipitation (CLIP) methodology on nonsynchronized cells revealed that hnRNPC, known as a regulator of splicing, binds mostly U-rich sequences within introns, organizing pre-messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) into hnRNP particles. This spatially structured binding enables hnRNPC to enhance or repress exon inclusion depending on its position relative to splice sites, highlighting a direct regulatory role for hnRNP particle architecture in splicing control [28]. Here, we used a highly effective cell cycle synchronization to specifically characterize mitotic hnRNPC at metaphase. We discovered that mitotic cells contain low- and high-density (LD and HD) hnRNPC ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPC–RNPs) co-migrating on a sucrose gradient with spliceosomes/monosomes and poly-ribosomes, respectively. Our CLIP data showed that mitotic hnRNPC interacts significantly with mature mRNAs, predominantly within HD-hnRNPC–RNPs, recapitulating its molecular preference for U-rich binding. Interestingly, LD-hnRNPC also interacted with exons of mature mRNAs characterized by short coding sequences, in agreement with mono-ribosome loading, while it continued to interact with introns associated with spliceosomes. By focusing on mature mRNA targets, we confirmed hnRNPC’s minimal length-dependent binding, consistent with previous findings [29, 30] and supporting the concept that hnRNPC–RNA interaction is highly cooperative also during mitosis, with tri-tetramers forming the 19S “triangular complex.” Importantly, we found that downregulation of hnRNPC elicited a global negative effect on the abundance of its mitotic targets. The data points to the global role of mitotic hnRNPC as a stabilizer of pre-mRNA and mRNA.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human cervical carcinoma HeLa S3 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U/ml penicillin (all Biological Industries) at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Single thymidine block (STB)-proTAME cell synchronization to metaphase

Cells were seeded at a density of 8 × 106 cells per 15 cm plate or 6 × 104 per well of a 24-well plate. After 24 h, cells were treated with 2 mM thymidine for 18 h (single thymidine block, STB), washed three times with PBS to remove excess thymidine, incubated for 7.5 h with regular medium, and supplemented with 12 μM proTAME (Cayman Chemicals, #25835) for an additional 3 h. Metaphase-synchronized cells were then washed twice with PBS before harvesting or fixing the cells for further analyses.

Sucrose gradient

Cells synchronized to metaphase in a 15 cm plate (see above) were incubated with 100 μg/ml cycloheximide (CHX; Sigma, #C7698) for the final 5 min of incubation, followed by washing with ice-cold PBS supplemented with 100 μg/ml CHX, and UV-illumination of the slightly wet cells (0.15 J/cm², Stratalinker, Stratagene). Crosslinked cells were then lysed on the plate with “optimized” buffer, as detailed in [31], followed by loading onto a 10%–50% sucrose gradient for polysome profiling as detailed in [25], and collection of 0.6 ml fractions.

Immunoblot analysis

Cells were harvested in 2× Sample buffer (4% SDS, 220 mM DTT, 20% glycerol, 180 mM Tris, pH 6.8, and traces of Bromo Phenol Blue) and subjected to SDS–PAGE and immunoblot analysis using RαHistone H3 (Abcam ab1791) and RαPhospho-H3 (Abcam ab5176). For sucrose gradient fractions’ analysis, the fractions were concentrated using StrataClean Resin (Agilent Technologies, cat#400714-61), and samples equivalent to 10% volume of each fraction were analyzed using MαhnRNPC (clone 4F4, Sigma R5028), MαPABP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-32318), RαRPL26 (Abcam ab59567), RαSF2/SRSF1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-38017), MαRPS6 (Cell Signaling #2317), and HRP-conjugated secondary αRabbit IgG and αMouse IgG antibodies (Jackson Laboratories).

Immunofluorescence, image acquisition, and quantitative analysis

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates on pre-coated coverslips (1 μg/ml Poly-D-Lysine (PDL) for 2 h at 37°C, followed by two PBS washes and synchronization to metaphase (see above), and subjected to immunofluorescence staining as detailed in [25]. Essentially, MαhnRNPC (Clone 4F4, Sigma R5028, 1:5000) and Alexa Fluor™ 448 DαMouse IgG (Abcam ab150109, 1:2500) were used as primary and secondary antibodies, respectively. DNA and actin staining were performed using Hoechst (Sigma #B2261, 1:10 000) and Phalloidin (Thermo Scientific #A12380, 1:250) dyes, respectively. Image acquisition was performed using a 3i Marianas (Denver, CO) spinning disk confocal microscope. Images were analyzed using SlideBook 6.0 (Intelligent Imaging Innovations). For each cell, a single midplane optical section was selected to visualize and quantify the spatial distribution and co-localization of the labeled proteins. Only cells in metaphase were included in the analysis based on morphological features. Cell boundaries were manually segmented, and the total cell area (in μm²) was quantified using SlideBook’s area measurement tool. Four subcellular regions were defined within each cell: (i) “DNA region,” segmented using the Hoechst signal; (ii) “Near DNA,” defined as a region occupying 20% of the total cell area, surrounding the chromatin mask; (iii) “Periphery,” defined as a region comprising 33%–36% of the total cell area, adjacent to the cell boundary; and (iv) “Remaining,” defined as the area not included in the previous three compartments. All regional masks were manually adjusted to reflect consistent geometry across cells. Mean intensity for each fluorescence channel was calculated within each region by dividing the total signal intensity by the area of that region. Enrichment was defined as the ratio between the regional mean intensity and the mean intensity across the cell (calculated as the total cellular signal divided by the total cell area): Enrichmentregion= [Mean intensityregion]/[Mean intensitycell].

smFISH, image acquisition, and quantitative analysis

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates and synchronized as described above, fixed for 10 min with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, washed twice with PBS, and incubated overnight in 70% ethanol at 4°C. Single-molecule RNA detection was performed using DesignReady Stellaris™ RNA FISH Probe Sets (Biosearch Technologies) designed for RBM3 (Quasar 670, VSMF-2624-5) or POLR2A (Quasar 670, VSMF-2295-5), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 50 nM for each probe and a 4 h incubation at 37°C. Coverslips were mounted on slides using ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen P36965), and images were acquired as detailed above. For each cell, image deconvolution was performed using the nearest-neighbor algorithm across a full z-stack, and a single midplane optical section was selected for further analysis based on maximal cell cross-section. Cell outlines, and the “Periphery” compartment were segmented based on phalloidin staining, where “Periphery” was defined by selecting an inward band corresponding to ∼8%–10% of the total measured cell diameter. The width of this region was estimated individually for each cell and adjusted to maintain consistency across the dataset to comprise ∼33%–36% of the total cell area. The “Cytosol” compartment was defined as the remaining cellular area after subtracting the manually defined periphery from the total cell mask. RNA spot detection was performed manually using the Define Objects function and minimum object size (SlideBook) for each cell after cell-specific intensity threshold application to exclude background signal while retaining discrete RNA spots. Detected spots representing single mRNA molecules were binarized, and their xy-coordinates retained. To quantify RNA distribution, the coordinates of each RNA spot were intersected with the mutually exclusive “Periphery” and “Cytosol” masks, and the number of RNA spots in each region was counted per cell. Counts were standardized by the relative area fraction of each compartment, and enrichment was calculated as: Enrichmentregion= [%RNA − spotsregion]/[%arearegion]. This enrichment metric was used to quantify the preferential localization of RNA molecules per compartment area. All measurements were performed on a per-cell basis.

Fluorescent crosslinking-immunoprecipitation (fCLIP) of mitotic hnRNPC

Fluorescent crosslinking-immunoprecipitation (fCLIP), library preparation, and basic data processing were performed as previously described [32] with several modifications. Synchronized HeLa S3 cells were UV-crosslinked on ice (0.15 J/cm²), lysed, and separated by sucrose gradient centrifugation as detailed above. A sample of the total cell extract was removed for RNA extraction (RiboEX reagent, GeneAll), rRNA depletion (NEB, E7405), library preparation (NEB, E7760), and Illumina sequencing (NextSeq2000, 100 bp, single read) to generate the STB-proTAME RNA-seq dataset.

Pooled Low-Density #6-9 fractions, and pooled High-Density #11–17 fractions were used for hnRNPC–RNA complexes immunoprecipitation (IP) using MαhnRNPC (Sigma, R5028) pre-bound to Dynabeads Protein G (Invitrogen) (14.3 μg antibody per 4.3 mg/143 μl beads, per 1 ml of sample). IP was performed overnight at 4°C, followed by sequential washes in IP buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, and 1% NP-40), high-salt buffer (IP buffer containing 500 mM NaCl), and RIPA buffer. Bead-bound hnRNPC–RNA complexes were partially digested with RNase I (0.015 or 0.15 U/μl, for 10 min at 22°C), then 3′-ends of the protected RNA fragments were dephosphorylated with QuickCIP (NEB, M0525S), and ligated to a fluorescent 3′ adapter (T4 RNA ligase 2 truncated K227Q, NEB, M0351). The ligated product was washed and phosphorylated on-beads with T4 PNK (NEB), followed by elution with 2× sample buffer, and SDS–PAGE. hnRNPC–RNA 3′-ligated complexes were visualized using a fluorescent imager and excised (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Gel slices were centrifuged through gel-breaker tubes, and proteinase K digestion was applied to release the 3′-ligated RNA footprints, then recovered by phenol–chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. The adapter was ligated to the 5′ end of the RNA (T4 RNA ligase, Thermo), followed by reverse transcription (SuperScript IV, Thermo) and 5-cycles of PCR amplification. The product was size-selected using Pippin Prep and a 3% agarose cassette, and a second PCR was performed using Illumina-indexed primers. Final libraries were purified (Zymo DNA Clean & Concentrator), assessed, and quantified by TapeStation, and Illumina-sequenced (GEO, GSE307226).

Paralyzer-defined “groups” with #overlapping reads ≥ 5 were defined as binding sites, and further filtered to include only sites along autosomal (chr 1–22) and chr X protein-coding transcripts (intron, 5′UTR, CDS, and 3′UTR annotations). Sites were further filtered to include only those with #groups per gene ≥ 2 Supplementary Fig. S2B, Supplementary Table S3). hnRNPC non-targets were defined as expressed genes (RNA-seq reads > 0) not bound by hnRNPC (#groups = 0). Raw data visualization was performed using IGV [35]. Statistical analyses and plotting were performed using the GraphPad software (Prism version 10.4), with P-values determined by 2-way-ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test (Fig. 1C), 1-way-ANOVA and Sidak's multiple comparison test (Fig. 1D), Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (Fig. 3C, S6B), Kruskal-Wallis (Figs. 3E, 4B-D, S3C, S6A), paired t-test (Fig. 5).

Results

A subset of hnRNPC–RNPs acquires higher density during mitosis

To study mitotic hnRNPC interactions with optimal sensitivity and specificity, we aimed to work with cells synchronized to a specific time point, when relevant RNPs of interest are most enriched. Since hnRNPC predominantly localizes to the cellular cortex in mitotic cells [25], we assumed this subcellular location reflects functional engagement of mitotic hnRNPC with RNP complexes. Therefore, to utilize this unique subcellular localization phenotype as a readout for mitotic hnRNPC function, we first determined the time of its full appearance along the mitotic process. While nuclear egress of hnRNPC into the cytoplasm initiated at prophase, its peripheral localization was fully acquired only at metaphase. hnRNPC localization to the cell cortex then persisted through anaphase and telophase, followed by nuclear re-entry during cytokinesis, in concert with nuclear envelope reformation (Fig. 1A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S1A). Quantitative analysis of immunofluorescent signal confirmed hnRNPC co-localization with nuclear chromatin during interphase, and its exclusion from the DNA region at metaphase (Fig. 1C). Spatial quantification of four different subcellular areas confirmed hnRNPC-specific enrichment at the cell periphery at metaphase (Fig. 1D).

To extract RNPs from cells at metaphase, we used proTAME for cell synchronization, given its high effectiveness in synchronizing cells to this specific sub-mitotic timepoint [49]. Our optimized synchronization procedure, termed STB-proTAME, included a single 18 h thymidine block (STB) followed by 3 h proTAME treatment and consistently attained >86% of cells at metaphase (Fig. 1E and Supplementary S1B). The higher enrichment of mitotic cells obtained by STB-proTAME relative to the DTB-mitosis procedure [25] was confirmed by RNA-seq analysis comparing gene expression between DTB-mitosis, DTB-G1, and STB-proTAME synchronized cells. Principal component and clustering analyses of VST gene expression clearly demonstrated the higher efficiency of STB-proTAME relative to DTB-mitosis in cell synchronization. Differentially expressed genes (n = 4525) were clustered into three distinct sets (Supplementary Table S1, and Supplementary Fig. S1C and D). A cluster of highly expressed mitotic genes (cluster #1) combined with the distinct differential expression pattern of the genes in clusters #2 and #3, collectively indicate that STB-proTAME-synchronized cultures (“Metaphase”) were highly enriched with mitotic cells, while DTB-mitosis cultures presented a less pronounce trend of mitotic gene expression, due to higher content of non-mitotic cells (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E).

We hypothesized that hnRNPC performs its mitosis-related functions while it is associated with specific RNP complexes. Therefore, we employed fractionation of RNPs on a sucrose gradient, as traditionally used for polysome profiling [25], using extracts of nonsynchronized and STB-proTAME-synchronized cells to identify mitosis-specific hnRNPC-containing fractions. Immunoblot analysis was applied to the fractions using antibodies specific for SF2 (spliceosomes marker), RPS6, RPL26 (small and large ribosomal subunit markers, respectively), PABP (translation complexes marker), and hnRNPC (Fig. 1F). In line with translation downregulation during mitosis [6–10], a smaller polysomal peak was exhibited by cells synchronized to metaphase. hnRNPC was hardly detected in cytoplasmic fractions in nonsynchronized cells, as expected from a nuclear protein involved in splicing. Moreover, cells at metaphase exhibited predominant co-sedimentation of hnRNPC with splicing factor SF2 in sub-polysomal fractions containing the spliceosome complex [50] and mono-ribosomes. Complexes within these fractions were termed LD-RNPs. Surprisingly, at metaphase, hnRNPC also migrated to the denser fractions that harbor mRNAs loaded with poly-ribosomes, and do not contain SF2, nor are known to comprise the splicing machinery. Complexes within these fractions were termed HD-RNPs (Fig. 1F).

Identification of RNA sequences bound by hnRNPC within LD- and HD-RNPs in metaphase

To identify RNA sequences bound by hnRNPC in metaphase, we used the fluorescent fCLIP method [32]. Cells synchronized to metaphase were exposed to UV (254 nm) for protein–RNA crosslinking, followed by cell lysis and sucrose gradient fractionation to isolate the distinct subpopulations of hnRNPC–RNPs (Fig. 2A and B). Based on the presence of hnRNPC along the sucrose gradient (Figs 1F and 2), two samples were generated by pooling the LD and HD fractions, excluding two intermediate fractions for improved separation. In accordance with their differential density, LD and HD samples varied in composition. As shown in Fig. 2B, LD hnRNPC-containing RNPs co-sedimented with monosomes and spliceosomes, while the HD-RNPs pool also included heavy polysomes. The LD- and HD-RNP pools were each subjected to immunoprecipitation using an antibody specific for hnRNPC and analyzed by fCLIP to capture hnRNPC–RNA interactions. hnRNPC–RNA crosslinked complexes were size-selected by SDS–PAGE, excised, and the bound RNA was recovered, further processed, and sequenced (Fig. 2C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S2A). The fCLIP analysis identified 28 862 and 12 521 binding sites (a.k.a “groups” [32, 51]) in LD- and HD-hnRNPC fractions, respectively. Among these, 77% of LD-hnRNPC and 68% of HD-hnRNPC binding sites mapped to protein-coding genes (Supplementary Fig. S2C), which were used for downstream analyses. The complete list of binding sites, including those detected along noncoding RNAs (pseudogene, snRNAs, miRNA, lncRNA, and more), is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Integrating fCLIP and RNA-seq analyses revealed that 8.7% and 17.1% of expressed mRNAs are bound by hnRNPC within the HD-RNP and LD-RNP pools, respectively. As a preliminary assessment of the fCLIP procedure, we examined whether LD- and HD-hnRNPC binding sites recapitulate the well-established preference of hnRNPC for binding U-rich sequences [28, 52–54]. To this end, we performed k-mer analysis (k = 6) to identify 6-mer motifs enriched in hnRNPC-bound sites relative to control sequences. This analysis indicated a clear preference of both LD- and HD-hnRNPC for poly-U tract binding, starting from 6-mers comprising 4 consecutive Us and peaking at the U 6-mer (UUUUUU) (Fig. 3A). This result confirms capture of authentic hnRNPC interactions and indicates that the mitotic-specific HD-hnRNPC also preferably interacts with U-rich sequences.

Intronic and exonic binding sites are differentially distributed between LD- and HD-RNPs

LD-hnRNPC RNPs co-sediment with the spliceosome marker SF2 to densities previously shown to harbor spliceosomes [50]; therefore, it is reasonable to assume that at least part of LD-hnRNPC-containing RNPs are involved in splicing. However, given that the LD fractions contain a mixture of spliceosomes and mono-ribosomes, while mitotic hnRNPC also migrated to denser, poly-ribosomal fractions (HD-hnRNPC) that lack the spliceosome marker SF2, we hypothesized that some of the LD-hnRNPC might interact with mature mRNAs engaged with mono-ribosomes. To test this hypothesis, we assumed that for splicing-related interactions, hnRNPC will target pre-mRNAs, predominantly at introns, as previously shown [28]; and for post-splicing -related interactions, hnRNPC will target exons, comprising mature mRNAs. Dissecting the exonic and intronic nature of hnRNPC binding sites showed that, in agreement with its co-migration with SF2, LD-hnRNPC has more binding sites within introns than HD-hnRNPC (64% versus 44%, respectively). While exonic binding of LD- and HD-RNPs (36% and 56%, respectively) is distributed along the entire mRNA, 38% of HD-hnRNPC’s binding sites are within 3′UTRs compared to 21% of LD-hnRNPC’s binding sites (Fig. 3B), indicating that a larger portion of HD-hnRNPC targets were loaded with ribosomes, hindering CDS interactions [55, 56]. To further compare the functionality and specificity of LD- and HD-hnRNPC targets, we tested whether intronic targets were previously reported as retained introns [40], which therefore may be included in mature mRNAs that co-sedimented with polyribosomes. While only 10% of the introns bound by HD-hnRNPC were identified as retained introns, their frequency was 2.5-fold higher among HD-hnRNPC than LD-hnRNPC-bound introns (Supplementary Fig. S3A), thus HD-hnRNPC further exhibited a higher preference for mature mRNAs.

Next, we utilized the quantitative nature of fCLIP to compare the binding intensities of LD- and HD-hnRNPC with introns and exons. As shown in Fig. 3C, while LD- and HD-hnRNPC interacted with exons at comparable intensities, LD-hnRNPC interactions with introns exhibited an order of magnitude higher intensity, relative to HD-hnRNPC–intron interactions, suggesting that a large portion of detected HD-hnRNPC intronic binding is at minimal levels and at least in part representing false positives due to fCLIP’s high sensitivity. Transcriptome-wide positional meta-analysis similarly indicated that only LD-hnRNPC quantitatively interacted with introns (Fig. 3D), and k-mer analysis of intronic and exonic targets confirmed that all are characterized by poly-U tract binding (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Thus, collectively, our analyses indicate that HD-hnRNPC interacts preferentially with mature mRNAs, while LD-hnRNPC exhibits a more complex pattern of interaction with both exonic and intronic RNA sequences.

LD-hnRNPC mixed interaction with exons and introns, and its co-migration with spliceosomes and mono-ribosomes (Fig. 1F) raises the question whether LD-hnRNPC complexes comprise a mixture of spliceosomal pre-mRNAs and mRNAs encoding relatively small proteins, whose shorter coding sequence (CDS) are more likely to associate with a single ribosome. To clarify this insight, we compared the CDS lengths of LD-hnRNPC targets to those of HD-hnRNPC targets and to expressed non-hnRNPC targets. Notably, only LD-hnRNPC exonic targets were characterized by a selectively shorter length of CDS (Fig. 3E). This finding is consistent with LD-hnRNPC comprising a mixed RNP composition of mono-ribosomes loaded with mature mRNAs [57], and spliceosomal pre-mRNA complexes, unlike the HD-hnRNPC, which predominantly binds mature mRNAs loaded with poly-ribosomes. Although co-sedimentation does not necessarily reflect direct association, possible hnRNPC association with mono- and poly-ribosomal mRNAs brings up the possibility that these hnRNPC-bound mature transcripts may either be actively translated or engaged with stalled ribosomes, given that translational states are affected by mitotic suppression [6, 10].

To verify the mitotic-hnRNPC specificity of this novel observation, we employed our analyses to hnRNPC iCLIP data obtained in non-synchronized cells [28], followed by comparing the findings to mitotic-hnRNPC fCLIP (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Like our current finding, interphase hnRNPC bound predominantly protein-coding transcripts (77%, Supplementary Fig. S4A, left), with poly-U tract binding preference (Supplementary Fig. S4B). However, in stark contrast to hnRNPC binding in metaphase, 93% of the binding sites in interphase were in introns, exhibiting > 2 orders of magnitude higher intensity compared to hnRNPC–exon interactions (Supplementary Fig. S4A, right, and S4C). This contrasts with the hnRNPC binding intensity in metaphase (exons > introns, Fig. 3C). Since nonsynchronized cells include a portion of mitotic cells, the small minority of exonic sites may represent hnRNPC interactions in that cell subset, and indeed, we could identify a U-rich specificity also in the minority of exonic binding sites (Supplementary Fig. S4B), a feature not originally reported by Konig et al. [27]. Quantifying the intensity of hnRNPC binding along exon and introns in nonsynchronized cells indicates that interphase hnRNPC interacts with introns at a higher intensity than exons (Supplementary Fig. S4C), highlighting the metaphase-specificity of hnRNPC interactions with mature mRNAs. Another observation in alignment with a mitosis-specific hnRNPC function is that while the majority of mitotic-hnRNPC intronic targets were also bound by interphase-hnRNPC (77%), its exonic interactions mostly targeted novel genes (61%) that did not associate with interphase-hnRNPC (Supplementary Fig. S4D).

Mitotic hnRNPC interaction with mature transcripts enabled in-cellulo confirmation of its cooperative length-dependent binding with RNA

hnRNPC–RNA interaction is known to be highly cooperative, with single hnRNPC tetramers serving as the core binding unit, and tri-tetramers forming the 19S “triangular complex,” binding an RNA stretch of ∼700 nt [29, 30] (see Fig. 4A, adapted from Fig. 12 of Huang et al., 1994 [30]). The tri-tetramer hnRNPC - ∼700 nt complex is considered the functional in vivo form of hnRNPC; however, since to-date, hnRNPC has been shown to interact mostly with pre-mRNAs, this assumption could not be tested in cells due to the considerably higher than ∼700 nt intrinsic length of introns. The finding of mitotic hnRNPC interaction with mature mRNAs now provides an opportunity to test the length-dependent cooperative hnRNPC–RNA interaction model. To this end, we focused exclusively on mitotic hnRNPC exonic targets, representing mature mRNAs, and tested for length dependence. As shown (Fig. 4B), while expressed hnRNPC non-targets were of lengths down to 350 nt, hnRNPC-bound mRNAs were selectively above 620 nt for LD-hnRNPC and 670 nt for HD-hnRNPC. These findings are in striking agreement with the previous findings, supporting the notion that hnRNPC–RNA interaction is highly cooperative also during mitosis, with tri-tetramers forming the 19S “triangular complex.”

hnRNPC–mRNAs interactions exhibit a preference for the 3′UTR

To probe the functionality of mitotic hnRNPC–RNA interactions, we leveraged the apparent minimal-length binding property of hnRNPC. We therefore asked whether, in exon-targets, is it the CDS, 5′UTR, or 3′UTR lengths that limit hnRNPC association. Our analyses indicated that the length of the 3′UTR, but not that of the CDS and 5′UTR, is relevant for exonic binding of hnRNPC, and thus for its interaction with mature mRNA (Fig. 4CD, and Supplementary Fig. S3C). In accordance, meta-transcriptomic positional analysis demonstrates a clear preference for hnRNPC interaction with the 3′UTR (Fig. 4E), aligning with the higher occurrence of U-rich binding sites within many 3′UTRs [58]. This pattern is characteristic of cytoplasmic RBP–mRNA interactions, which are often more prevalent along the 3′UTR, while CDS regions are loaded with ribosomes and less available for RBPs to interact [55, 59]. Next, given that hnRNPC has until now been mainly associated with splicing, we aimed to explore the functional significance of hnRNPC interaction with mature mRNA.

hnRNPC functions to protect the mitotic transcriptome from RNA degradation

Since hnRNPC acquires cell cortex localization during metaphase, we first wanted to corroborate a link between this unique sub-cellular localization and mitotic hnRNPC interaction with mature mRNAs. To this end, we selected two candidate mRNAs for smFISH analysis that are expressed at similar levels at metaphase: RBM3, an exon-exclusive target of hnRNPC, and POLR2A, an hnRNPC non-target (see IGV visualization of RBM3 and POLR2A in Supplementary Fig. S5). As shown in Fig. 5A and B, RBM3 mRNA was enriched at the cell periphery, in correlation with mitotic-hnRNPC localization, while POLR2A mRNA was depleted from the cell periphery (Figs 5A and B, and Fig. 1A). This result strongly suggests that at least part of the mitosis-acquired hnRNPC interactions and putative functions are taking place at the cell cortex.

In a previous attempt to probe for mitotic hnRNPC’s role in post-transcriptional gene regulation, we generated gene expression (RNA-seq) datasets in DTB-synchronized cells, in the presence of DOX-induced shRNA-mediated hnRNPC depletion. These conditions could only achieve ∼50% reduction in hnRNPC levels, and similarly, cell synchronization was effective for ∼50% of cells [25]; however still provided valuable insight. Here, we crossed mitotic differential mRNA expression in hnRNPC-depleted cells with hnRNPC mitotic targets (this study), to assess whether hnRNPC might affect the cellular levels of its specific mRNA targets. Evidently, even with only ∼50% hnRNPC depletion, the cellular levels of its targets were compromised, with no effect on levels of non-hnRNPC targets (Fig. 6A). Specifically, upon hnRNPC depletion, ∼60% of LD-hnRNPC and ∼70% of HD-hnRNPC exonic targets exhibited reduced steady-state RNA levels (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6A). Similarly, 65% of LD-hnRNPC intronic targets also reduced their steady state RNA levels in hnRNPC-depleted cells (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6B, left panel).

To assess whether this observation is specific to mitosis, we conducted a similar analysis using RNA-seq data from hnRNPC-depleted, nonsynchronized HeLa cells [28]. Despite a higher depletion efficiency in this dataset, no reduction in RNA levels was observed for the mitotic hnRNPC target transcripts. In fact, these transcripts exhibited a slight increase in abundance relative to non-targets (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These results suggest a previously unappreciated mitosis-specific requirement for hnRNPC in maintaining transcriptome stability. To further investigate this hypothesis, we examined the effect of mild hnRNPC depletion on the stability of 218 transcripts annotated as “mitotic” (cluster #1, Supplementary Fig. S1D), of which 53 were hnRNPC-targets. A destabilization effect was observed for the 53 hnRNPC targets within this cluster compared to the 165 non-target transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S6B). While the modest reduction in stability observed among non-targets (solid black line) likely reflects background variability inherent to the experimental system, the significantly greater instability of hnRNPC targets supports a critical role for hnRNPC in preserving transcript integrity during mitosis.

To also probe whether hnRNPC affects the loading of its mitotic targets with ribosomes, we crossed mitotic hnRNPC targets with ribosome footprinting (Ribo-seq) data in response to hnRNPC depletion [25]. Our analysis indicated a lack of major effect of mitotic hnRNPC on the number of ribosome-protected fragments (RPF) along both its exonic and intronic targets, with a similar trend observed for non-hnRNPC targets (Fig. 6B). This observation hints at a more complex view (discussed below) stemming from the multistep nature of mitosis, where hnRNPC nuclear egress may either precede or follow the onset of RNA metabolism arrest, as well as the loading of recently matured mRNA with the translation machinery, which involves ribosome protection.

Collectively, the data point to the global role of hnRNPC as a mitotic pre-mRNA and mRNA stabilizer. To search for additional insights related to specific genes, we looked in hnRNPC-depleted cells for hnRNPC targets showing ≥ 20% reduction in RNA abundance, accompanied by a different change in the RPF value. For example, the RNA and RPF values of TAF1D decreased by ∼25% and ∼15%, respectively; and SLC30A1 exhibited a reduction of ∼23% in RNA level and an increase of ∼12% in RPF. While the latter is a rarer case, it points to an interesting possibility, which is discussed below.

Discussion

Precise cell cycle regulation requires an accurate gene expression via multiple regulatory mechanisms, which include RNA stability. The decrease in mRNA degradation during mitosis is supported by multiple observations, including a decrease in global deadenylation and the regulatory role of PABPC in controlling poly-A tails of specific mRNA targets (reviewed in [14]). The degradation of ∼200 cell cycle-related genes at mitosis exit [60] is consistent with the concept that a wave of mRNA degradation at the mitosis-to-G1 transition resets the transcriptome required for the new cell cycle. We anticipate that additional mechanisms take a key role in controlling the stability of specific mRNA populations in mitosis, one of which involves hnRNPC, as presented in our current study.

Upon entry to mitosis, RNA metabolism is broadly suppressed by a significant decrease in transcription, splicing, RNA degradation, and translation [1–10, 14]. This suppressed RNA metabolism likely enables the cell to redirect essential resources towards proper chromatin separation, the central event in mitosis. The scheduled degradation of specific pre-mRNA/mRNA populations at the mitosis-to-G1 phase transition [60] raises the question of how the rest of the transcriptome is protected upon nuclear envelope breakdown. RBPs evolved to interact with their specific RNA recognition elements (RREs); however, despite the specificity at the molecular level, there are examples of the multifunctionality of a given binding specificity in regulating distinct biological functions along the mRNA life and at different cellular contexts [15–24]. Here, we used an unbiased approach to specifically focus on hnRNPC, an abundant nuclear RBP that interacts mainly with introns and is largely involved in splicing regulation during interphase. An iCLIP experiment using nonsynchronized cells revealed that only 9% of hnRNPC’s binding targets are within exons [28]. While our analytical pipeline using the latter data confirmed this observation (Supplementary Fig. S4A, left), the current results, obtained using cells synchronized to metaphase, show that between 36% and 56% of hnRNPC’s binding sites are within exons (Fig. 3B). We found that while mitotic hnRNPC continues to interact with its established U-rich recognition element [28, 52–54], it acquires distinct targets, in addition to those previously reported (Supplementary Fig. S4D).

Known as a regulator of alternative splicing during interphase, hnRNPC was also implicated in the sporadic cases of 3′UTR binding and stabilization of urokinase receptor (ukR) and amyloid precursor protein (APP) mRNAs, thereby enhancing their translation [61, 62]. Here, we exposed a global role of mitotic hnRNPC as a general RNA stabilizer. We show that downregulation of hnRNPC elicited a negative effect on the abundance of 60%–70% of its mitotic targets (Fig. 6). This shared effect of interactions with both pre-mRNAs and mature mRNAs, indifferent to the distinct complexes harboring these transcripts, aligns with the necessity to keep the entire mitotic transcriptome stable upon the inhibition of RNA metabolism and loss of compartmentalization at M-phase, before the degradation of specific RNA molecules towards mitosis exit occurs [60]. Due to the limitation of cell synchronization, we could not show RNA stabilization directly by introducing transcription inhibitors such as Actinomycin D [63]. However, the natural suppression of transcription during mitosis [5] may effectively replace transcription inhibition, rendering steady-state mRNA generally representing its stability.

Using sucrose gradient fractionation of metaphase-synchronized cells, we separately characterized HD-hnRNPC, which exhibited predominant interaction of hnRNPC with the 3′UTR of spliced mature mRNAs, that co-sediment with poly-ribosomes. In addition, we characterized LD-hnRNPC, which co-sedimented with mono-ribosomes and spliceosomes. Our analyses indicate that LD-hnRNPC interactions represent the blended nature of this fraction. Specifically, exonic targets of LD-hnRNPC were predominantly mature mRNAs with shorter CDS lengths, predisposed to loading with single ribosomes. The LD-hnRNPC intronic targets co-sediment with spliceosomes, as would match hnRNPC-bound pre-mRNAs. Importantly, we confirmed that the binding sites within all analyzed subsets of hnRNPC targets display their established U-rich recognition element (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3B). Three additional key findings strongly support the authenticity of our conclusion that mitotic hnRNPC acquires interactions with mature mRNAs, in addition to unprocessed pre-mRNAs. These include: (i) the length dependence feature of hnRNPC exonic interactions is in accord with its reported cooperative binding property (Fig. 4B); (ii) the 3′UTR being the predominant binding site of hnRNPC exonic targets (Figs 3B and 4E) is in accord with authentic interactions with mature mRNAs engaged with ribosomes along their CDS; (iii) the finding that it is the 3′UTR length that limits hnRNPC cooperative binding (Fig. 4C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S3C). Notably, post-transcriptional control in the context of RNA stability/translation, by binding of RBPs to specific motifs (often in the 3′UTR), was previously assigned to various hnRNPs, including hnRNPD/AUF1, A1/A2, E1/E2, I, and K [64–69].

To obtain our observations, we were highly invested in optimal cell synchronization. Our efforts were designed to attain preparative amounts of cells, at highly homogenous synchronization to the metaphase step. These enabled hnRNPC fCLIP analysis in previously unexplored conditions that minimize interphase-contamination and sub-M-phase diversity. While our results may reflect a snapshot representing a single time point in mitosis, they revealed a dramatic functional shift in detected hnRNPC interactions. Such a functional change might be shared by other RBPs, including highly abundant nuclear RBPs during mitosis. Such dynamic remodeling of protein assemblies on RNA in response to physiological signals in living cells, thereby affecting the function of RBPs, was recently demonstrated [70], though not in a cell cycle-specific context.

Further work will be required to assess the ubiquity of our observations. In particular, it will be important to determine whether hnRNPC promotes transcriptome maintenance in other rapidly dividing cell lines and, more challengingly, in cancer. Extending this analysis to other abundant nuclear RBPs and defining their mitotic RNA targets and functions may reveal broader principles of context-dependent RBP activity, including potential “moonlighting” roles of nuclear RBPs that are transiently “unemployed” and redistribute to the cytoplasm. These would complement a major limitation of this study, namely the intrinsic challenges of robust hnRNPC depletion and cell synchronization to mitosis. While CRISPR knockout or degron-mediated depletion specifically at mitosis is not feasible for hnRNPC due to loss of activity upon tagging, other future biological systems may enable these approaches and provide additional perspectives on mitotic transcriptomics.

hnRNPC exists as two isoforms, hnRNPC1 and hnRNPC2, produced through alternative splicing. Both isoforms form a stable tetramer in vivo, predominantly in a (C1)3 (C2)1 ratio [71]. Importantly, the 13-amino-acid insertion in hnRNPC2 influences RNA-binding affinity and specificity [72]. The antibody used in this study does not discriminate between both isoforms, thus allowing us to capture an integrated, physiologically representative profile that likely reflects the natural hnRNPC binding landscape within the mitotic cell. Post-translational modifications of hnRNPC isoforms, such as phosphorylation and sumoylation, affect their binding capability to RNA and other protein partners [61, 73–75] and may also affect tetrameric composition, which remains to be studied in the context of mitotic hnRNPC function.

Previous reports linked the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification to the promotion of hnRNPC–RNA interaction [76, 77]. The sparse cases where hnRNPC positively influences mRNA stability in an m6A-dependent manner[78–80], led us to probe whether mitotic hnRNPC–RNA interactions correlate with mitotic m6A distribution [43]. Our analyses did not indicate robust dependence between these two molecular events, with only 6% of hnRNPC binding sites in the vicinity of mitotically m6A-modified sites, in agreement with what has been observed in interphase (7%, [77]). K-mer analyses showed the reduced frequency of RRACH motifs relative to U-rich motifs along hnRNPC binding sites (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Interestingly, the subset of hnRNPC binding sites that do include an RRACH motif is highly enriched with transcripts encoding specifically cytosolic ribosomal proteins (Supplementary Fig. S7B), though we did not further pursue this observation. As previously postulated, proximal U-rich and RRACH motif along RNAs, often display putative stem-loop folding potential, which was shown to enable m6A-induced hnRNPC binding. The PRDX1 transcript, which decreased by ∼10% upon hnRNPC depletion, serves as an example for such a putative mitotic hnRNPC target (Supplementary Fig. S7C, Supplementary Table S3).

Our early work has shown that despite the significant reduction in active translation in cells synchronized to mitosis using DTB, they are generally immune to stress granule formation. In addition, we found that despite the decrease in translation, DTB-synchronized cells harbor heavy poly-ribosomes, of which some are actively translating, whereas others are attenuated/stalled [6]. Although the mechanism governing transcript-specific active or attenuated elongation is unknown, the findings imply that ribosome association with mitotic transcripts leads to their protection. The current study informs that hnRNPC depletion negatively affects the abundance of its mitotic targets but does not significantly impact their RPF value (Fig. 6AB), possibly implying that the RNA protection property of mitotic hnRNPC is more significant for the transcripts that are not fully engaged with ribosomes. In addition to its global function as an RNA stabilizer, mitotic hnRNPC may also exert gene-specific regulatory effects on translation of individual targets. For example, TAF1D transcript shows a decrease by 25% in RNA abundance and by 15% in RPF upon hnRNPC depletion (Supplementary Table S3, IGV map presented in Supplementary Fig. S8A). This could reflect the higher vulnerability of TAF1D mRNA sub-population that is not sufficiently protected by ribosomes, supporting the possibility that hnRNPC regulates the translation of specific TAF1D splice variants. Given that TAF1D downregulation leads to a reduction in gene products important for cell division (such as CDK1 [81]), some TAF1D transcripts may be actively translated during mitosis. Another example is SLC30A1 mRNA, which, upon hnRNPC depletion shows a decrease in RNA abundance by ∼23% and an increase in RPF by 12% (Supplementary Table S3, IGV map presented in Supplementary Fig. S8). This could reflect an increased binding of ribosomes upon hnRNPC depletion, implying a possible inhibitory role for hnRNPC on active mitotic translation of SLC30A1 mRNA by blocking ribosome association. Future experiments will decipher the functional roles of mitotic hnRNPC by identifying the different hnRNPC–RNPs partners, their specific post-translational modifications, and the signals that govern their dynamic changes.

Importantly, hnRNPC dysregulated activity correlates with poor prognosis in multiple malignancies [82]. Our results expand the known functions of hnRNPC, proposing a model in which, aside from splicing, mitotic hnRNPC promotes global transcriptome maintenance during mitotic suppression of RNA metabolism, which can explain its specific contribution to rapidly dividing cancer cells. Evolvement of preservative mechanisms to maintain transcriptome integrity during this sensitive phase in the cell cycle to provide the daughter cells with an economical and efficient start as they enter G1 phase may represent a broader manifestation and vulnerability of cancer cells. Future studies should be designed to provide additional insights into hnRNPC’s multifunctional roles during mitosis.
