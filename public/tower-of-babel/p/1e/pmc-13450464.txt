Recurrent ophthalmic arteries supplying posterior meningiomas: embryologic background and 3D anatomical visualization
Authors: Kohei Ishikawa, Nobuyuki Watanabe, Naoki Kato, Kyoichi Tomoto, Hirokazu Koseki, Yudo Ishii, Toshihide Tanaka, Yasuharu Akasaki, Toshihiro Ishibashi, Yuichi Murayama, Kentaro Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeRecurrent branches of the ophthalmic artery rarely function as feeding arteries to posteriorly located meningiomas. This study describes two such cases and examines the embryologic basis and collateral pathways that enable this atypical supply. The utility of three-dimensional (3D) simulation for delineating skull base vascular relationships is also assessed.MethodsTwo patients with posteriorly located meningiomas supplied by recurrent ophthalmic artery branches underwent detailed angiographic evaluation. The embryogenesis of the superficial recurrent ophthalmic artery (SROA) and deep recurrent ophthalmic artery (DROA) was reviewed based on established developmental models. Contrast-enhanced MRI, rotational angiography, and MRI CISS sequences were fused using GRID software to visualize vascular courses relative to the cerebellar tentorium and cranial nerves.ResultsIn both tumors, the SROA or DROA continued distally as tentorial arteries and contributed to tumor vascularization. Fusion 3D simulation enabled clear visualization of these recurrent branches, their tentorial trajectories, and their anatomical relationships with the surrounding dura and cranial nerves. These findings demonstrate that persistent embryologic communications between the ophthalmic artery and adjacent ICA dural branches may supply lesions far from the orbit.ConclusionRecurrent ophthalmic artery branches can function as feeding vessels to posterior meningiomas through embryologically derived anastomotic pathways. Integrating 3D simulation into preoperative hemodynamic assessment enhances understanding of such vascular variants and supports safer treatment planning.Keywords: Superficial recurrent ophthalmic artery, Deep recurrent ophthalmic artery, Posterior cranial fossa, Surgical simulation

Introduction

Meningiomas typically exhibit characteristic feeding arteries depending on their site of origin, and their hemodynamic patterns generally follow predictable configurations [1]. However, in highly vascular tumors, potential anastomoses that are usually not evident may become prominent, allowing unexpected vessels to function as major feeding arteries. Although several reports have described recurrent ophthalmic arteries as feeders to posterior fossa lesions, their detailed anatomical course and relationship to the tentorium have not been evaluated using modern three-dimensional (3D) simulation techniques. Here, we report two rare cases in which recurrent ophthalmic arteries supplied posteriorly located meningiomas, one arising from the posterior convexity and the other from the posterior cranial fossa. Using a combination of angiography, MRI, and 3D reconstruction, we provide a detailed anatomical analysis together with a discussion of the embryological background of these unusual collateral pathways.

Case report

Imaging acquisition and 3D reconstruction

For 3D reconstruction, contrast-enhanced MRI and constructive interference in steady state (CISS) sequences were obtained using a 3.0-T MRI scanner. CISS images were acquired with a slice thickness of 0.9 mm and a field of view of 200 × 200 mm. Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images were obtained with a slice thickness of 1.0 mm. Rotational angiography was performed using a biplane angiographic system (Artis icono; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), and three-dimensional datasets were reconstructed for fusion with MRI. Image fusion and 3D reconstruction were performed using GRID software version 1.1.9 (Kompath Inc., Tokyo, Japan) running on a Microsoft Windows 11 Home workstation. Tumor dimensions and estimated tumor volumes were obtained from the 3D segmentation models generated using the GRID software.

Case 1

A 48-year-old man presented with hearing loss. Preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a left petrous meningioma measuring approximately 39 × 46 × 34 mm, with an estimated tumor volume of 25.8 cm³. Digital subtraction angiography of the left internal carotid artery demonstrated that the tumor was supplied exclusively by the superficial recurrent ophthalmic artery (SROA). The feeder, measuring approximately 0.8 mm in diameter on angiography, passed through the superior orbital fissure, coursed posteriorly on the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus, and reached the tumor via a trajectory similar to that of the inferolateral trunk (ILT) and the meningohypophyseal trunk (MHT). 3D reconstruction clearly demonstrated the relationship between the vascular course and the tentorium, revealing that the feeder followed the posterior course along the tentorial surface immediately before entering the tumor, consistent with the trajectory of the lateral tentorial artery.

Preoperative embolization was not performed because the SROA was the sole feeder and demonstrated limited tumor blush. In addition, embolization through the ophthalmic artery was considered to carry a potential risk of reflux into the internal carotid artery or ophthalmic circulation through collateral pathways involving the ILT and MHT. The patient underwent gross total resection without postoperative complications, and histopathological examination confirmed a WHO grade I meningioma (Fig. 1).

Case 2

A 55-year-old man presented with headache. Preoperative contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated a right posterior convexity meningioma measuring approximately 50 × 59 × 54 mm, with an estimated tumor volume of 83.5 cm³. The primary dural attachment was considered to arise from the posterior convexity dura rather than the tentorium, based on the presence of adjacent occipital bone destruction and the absence of a distinct tentorial dural tail. Preoperative right internal carotid angiography identified a deep recurrent ophthalmic artery (DROA), arising from the first segment of the ophthalmic artery and contributing to tumor supply, in addition to the occipital artery, the posterior convexity branch of the middle meningeal artery, and the posterior cerebral artery. The DROA measured approximately 0.6 mm in diameter on angiography, and its contribution to tumor vascularity was relatively minor. The vessel was observed coursing laterally to the internal carotid artery within the cavernous sinus and entering the tumor via a route analogous to that of the ILT and MHT. 3D reconstruction demonstrated the relationship between the feeder and the tentorium, revealing that the vessel traveled along the medial tentorial surface immediately before entering the tumor, consistent with the course of the medial tentorial artery. Intraoperatively, the tumor was found to be attached primarily to the posterior convexity dura rather than the tentorium.

Preoperative embolization using Embosphere microspheres followed by proximal coil occlusion was performed for the main feeders arising from the occipital artery and middle meningeal artery. In contrast, embolization of the DROA was not attempted because its contribution to tumor vascularity was limited and catheterization through the ophthalmic artery was considered to carry a potential risk of embolic migration into the internal carotid or ophthalmic circulation. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and histopathological examination confirmed a WHO grade I meningioma (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Embryology of the ophthalmic artery and collateral pathways

We presented cases in which a recurrent ophthalmic artery served as a feeding artery to posteriorly located meningiomas. As demonstrated by Padget and later by Lasjaunias and colleagues, the development of the ophthalmic artery involves a complex process of reconstruction based on two embryonic systems: the primitive dorsal ophthalmic artery (PDOA) and the primitive ventral ophthalmic artery (PVOA) [2–4]. When the embryo is 4–5 mm, the PDOA appears, arising from the bifurcation of the primitive internal carotid artery, and when the embryo reaches 9 mm, the PVOA also arises. Both arteries elongate in accordance with the ventral shifting of the optic cup, with the PDOA giving rise to the future lateral posterior ciliary artery, whereas the PVOA gives off the future medial posterior ciliary artery. When the embryo reaches approximately 18 mm, the ophthalmic artery enters a process of reconstruction accompanied by migration of its origin. Padget interpreted this process as reflecting the predominant persistence of the PDOA, whereas Lasjaunias et al. proposed that the PVOA contributes more substantially to the definitive ophthalmic artery.

During the same developmental period, the stapedial artery (SA) of the external carotid system becomes an important source of orbital supply, giving rise to the maxillomandibular branch and the supraorbital branch. The maxillomandibular branch anastomoses with the primordium of the external carotid artery to form the middle meningeal artery (MMA) [5, 6]. The supraorbital branch enters the orbit through the superior orbital fissure and anastomoses with both the PDOA and PVOA, forming a circumferential anastomotic ring around the optic nerve. In late embryonic stages, the ventral portion of this ring regresses, establishing the definitive course and branching pattern of the ophthalmic artery. Moreover, regression of the lateral supraorbital branch results in incorporation of the lacrimal artery into the ophthalmic artery. As a consequence of these developmental processes, remnants of the embryonic communications between the ICA-derived ophthalmic system and the ECA-derived MMA may persist along the lateral orbital wall and around the superior orbital fissure [5].

In the adult, the recurrent meningeal artery (RMA) can be regarded as an embryonic remnant of the anastomotic network formed by the PDOA, PVOA, and the supraorbital branch of SA, representing a potential communication between the ophthalmic artery and the middle meningeal artery. Thus, the RMA constitutes an embryological basis for anomalous orbital hemodynamics, as well as for the reflux of embolic agents into the ophthalmic artery during endovascular embolization. On the other hand, the recurrent branch of the ophthalmic artery that served as a feeding artery in our case is not the RMA. In the present study, the ophthalmic artery was divided into three portions. The first portion was defined as the segment extending from the entrance into the orbit to the first bend of the artery, where it courses inferolateral to the optic nerve. The second portion was defined as the segment between the first bend and the second bend, where the artery crosses to the superomedial aspect of the optic nerve and turns anteriorly. The SROA arises from the proximal portion of the lacrimal artery in the second segment of the ophthalmic artery, courses through the lateral part of the superior orbital fissure, and supplies the roof of the cavernous sinus. Owing to its potential anastomosis with the marginal tentorial artery, it may function as a feeding artery to tentorial tumors and has been referred to as the ‘ophthalmic artery origin of the marginal tentorial artery’ [2, 7]. The SROA is considered a remnant of the supraorbital branch of the SA [3].

The DROA, in contrast, arises from the first segment of the ophthalmic artery, turns posteriorly, and passes through the medial portion of the superior orbital fissure to supply the lateral wall of the cavernous sinus. The DROA potentially anastomoses with the anteromedial branch of the ILT and, at times, with the cavernous sinus branch of the MMA or the accessory meningeal artery, and is considered a remnant of the PDOA [1]. Only a limited number of reports have noted the DROA functioning as a feeding artery to posterior cranial fossa tumors, but the fact that approximately 8% of ILTs and MHTs share a common trunk, and that the superior branch of the ILT anastomoses with the MHT, highlights the close potential collateral relationship between the ILT and MHT. These anatomical considerations suggest that, in Case 2, the DROA may have contributed to the tumor supply as a tentorial artery through the MHT [8, 9].

Although their recurrent courses often lead to confusion with the RMA, the SROA and DROA are embryologically distinct entities. Through their potential anastomoses with the ILT and MHT, they may continue distally as tentorial arteries and can, as demonstrated in our cases, serve as feeding arteries to posteriorly located tumors far removed from the orbit.

Hemodynamic characteristics of meningiomas

The feeding arteries of meningiomas are generally well defined, and it is uncommon for remote or atypical collateral pathways to develop. In cerebellopontine angle meningiomas, the most frequent feeding arteries include the tentorial artery, the dorsal meningeal artery, the petrosal branch of the MMA, and the ascending pharyngeal artery (AphA) [10]. The contribution of the MMA to the skull base is primarily limited to the anterior cranial fossa and the lateral portion of the middle cranial fossa, and its involvement in the tentorium is restricted to its site of dural attachment [3]. In the posterior cranial fossa, collateral circulation is usually maintained through a balance between the AphA and the occipital artery [11]. In our case series, however, both tumors exhibited poor supply from the AphA, and the MHT and ILT—branches arising directly from the internal carotid artery—were hypoplastic. These anatomical conditions likely promoted the recruitment of collateral flow from the ophthalmic artery. Assessment of collateral circulation requires careful hemodynamic evaluation based on findings from both external and internal carotid angiography.

Clinically, it is essential to recognize the possibility of potential anastomoses between feeding arteries and the ophthalmic artery during preoperative embolization. Tonetti et al. reported a case of a tentorial arteriovenous fistula in which retrograde filling of the ophthalmic artery occurred during angiography of the MHT—the primary feeder [12]. Although it is well known that particle and liquid embolic agents may reflux into neural supply through collateral channels, it should also be appreciated that, even in lesions of the posterior cranial fossa or occipital convexity, collateral pathways may extend as far as the orbit via the ophthalmic artery. Moreover, it has been assumed that ophthalmic artery supply to posterior fossa tumors is more common in hypervascular lesions. However, in Case 1, the sole feeder was the SROA, and the angiographic tumor blush was not prominent. Therefore, in large meningiomas as shown in the present study, the ophthalmic artery may contribute to blood supply to the tumor regardless of vascularity. In such situations, posterior surgical approaches place devascularization at the final stage, potentially influencing the overall surgical strategy, including the need for preoperative embolization or modification of the operative plan. These considerations highlight the importance of thorough preoperative evaluation, including assessment of hemodynamics, in cases of giant meningiomas.

Visualization of skull base vasculature using 3D simulation

We routinely perform 3D simulation prior to surgery. Although angiography remains the gold standard for evaluating the precise course of intracranial vessels—including variants such as the SROA and DROA—recent advances in angiographic systems and imaging quality have enabled even more detailed assessment of vascular anatomy. However, maximum intensity projection slab images obtained from 3D angiography, while useful for clarifying the relationship between bone and vessels and for identifying skull base foramina, provide limited information regarding the relationship between these vessels and the tentorium. Because the tentorium is a thin membranous structure, direct extraction and 3D reconstruction from imaging studies has traditionally been difficult. To address this limitation, we developed a method for reconstructing the cerebellar tentorium from preoperative CT and MRI CISS sequences using the 3D simulation application GRID [13].

In the present cases, fusion of contrast-enhanced MRI with 3D angiography enabled clear visualization of the SROA and DROA coursing distally as tentorial arteries along the cerebellar tentorium and contributing to tumor supply. It should be noted that the dura reconstructed using this method preserves the overall anatomical relationships but has an average thickness of approximately 1.2 mm. Previous studies have reported the thickness of the convexity dura to be 1.05 ± 0.22 mm and that of the petrous apex dura to be approximately 0.36 mm; therefore, particularly at the skull base, the dura appears thicker than its true anatomical thickness [14, 15]. Furthermore, it should be noted that this method cannot reproduce the petroclinoidal fold, which extends anteriorly from the petrous apex, continues into the tentorium, and transitions into the cavernous sinus wall. In the present cases, the reconstructed vascular trajectories were consistent with angiographic anatomy, and their relationships to the tentorium were qualitatively compatible with the intraoperative surgical impressions. However, formal validation of the reconstructed tentorial anatomy against intraoperative findings was not performed, because the surgical procedures were focused primarily on tumor resection rather than direct verification of the tentorial surface and vascular course. Therefore, the anatomical concordance described in this study should be regarded as qualitative rather than quantitative.

The SROA is also known to supply the intradural part of the third and fourth cranial nerves, and fusing these nerves into the 3D model further enhances understanding of their anatomical relationships [1]. Historically, the functional vascular anatomy of intracranial arteries—including variants such as the SROA and DROA—was delineated primarily through combined angiographic and cadaveric studies in the 1970s, and these findings remain fundamental today. With recent advances in AI-based imaging and 3D simulation technologies, it has become possible to fuse the dura into vascular models, allowing clarification of their relationships to skull base structures such as the tentorium—relationships that cannot be fully appreciated on angiography alone. Although angiographic interpretation remains essential, the integration of 3D simulation provides significant additional value for understanding the courses of such vascular variants and the surrounding surgical anatomy.

Conclusion

The ophthalmic artery exhibits potential anastomotic connections that extend beyond adjacent vessels such as the ILT and MHT, reaching the cerebellar tentorium and occipital convexity, thereby allowing it to serve as a feeding artery to posterior lesions far from the orbit. Comprehensive preoperative hemodynamic assessment is essential, and the integration of 3D simulation enables detailed evaluation of the relationships between these vascular structures and the surrounding anatomy.
