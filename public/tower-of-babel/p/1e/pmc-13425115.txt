Dietary and non-dietary factors of undernutrition using Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure in children aged 6–23 months in Democratic Republic of the Congo: the 2023 National Nutrition Survey
Authors: Richard Makaba Ma Eloko, Marcel Mubelo Aspro, Emérite Disuemi Bisita, Botomba Ntambwe Steve, Bernard-Kennedy Nkongolo, Mayavanga Jean Baptiste Bazana, Nahimana Damien Gitebo, Bindama Senge Bruno, Mapatano Mala Ali, Marie-Claire Muyer Muel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUndernutrition remains a significant public health challenge in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). This study aims to identify dietary and non-dietary factors associated with undernutrition among children aged 6–23 months using the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure.MethodologyThe secondary analysis was conducted using data from the 2023 National Nutrition Survey, a nationally representative cross-sectional study. The final analytical sample consisted of 5396 children, stratified into two age bands 6–11 months (n=1792) and 12–23 months (n=3604). Bivariate analysis (p<0.2), Multivariable logistic and stepwise regression models (p<0.05) were used to identify factors associated with undernutrition.ResultsThe overall prevalence of undernutrition was 47.4%, rising significantly from 37.4% in infants aged 6–11 months to 52.6% in children aged 12–23 months. Stepwise logistic regression identified distinct risk profiles: for the 6–11 month group, male sex (adjusted OR (aOR)=1.7; 95% CI 1.3 to 2.1) and larger households with >2 children (aOR=1.3; 95% CI 1.1 to 1.6) increased the undernutrition, while acceptable minimum meal frequency (MMF) was the primary protective factor (aOR=0.12; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.19). Among the 12–23 month, high dietary diversity (aOR=0.5; 95% CI 0.4 to 0.7), acceptable MMF (aOR=0.6; 95% CI 0.5 to 0.8) and maternal education significantly reduced undernutrition. Conversely, rural residency, recent diarrhoea (aOR=1.4; 95% CI 1.2 to 1.7) and poor hand hygiene remained major factors associated with undernutrition.ConclusionTraditional indices significantly underestimate the true nutritional burden in the DRC. These findings underscore the need for a National Nutrition Programme to implement multisectoral interventions that prioritise maternal literacy and the integration of Water, Sanitation and Hygiene protocols to address the diverse drivers of undernutrition.

Introduction

Undernutrition is a major public health problem. Globally, undernutrition affects one in three children under 5 years of age.1 This condition encompasses wasting, stunting and underweight, which impact 6.6%, 23.2% and 5.6% of children aged 6–59 months worldwide, respectively.1 2 In sub-Saharan Africa, the situation is particularly critical; it is estimated that 35%, 14.8% and 5.1% of children under 5 are affected by stunting, underweight and wasting, respectively.3 In specific regions of Central Africa, the cumulative burden of undernutrition reaches as high as 61%, highlighting severe regional disparities.4 In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), recent data from the 2023 National Nutrition Survey (ENN) and the 2023–2024 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) reveal an alarming rise in undernutrition. Stunting prevalence reached 47.7% (ENN) and 45% (DHS), signalling a concerning upward trend compared with previous years’ failure.5

According to the UNICEF and WHO conceptual framework, undernutrition results from three levels of causality. First, the immediate causes include diseases and inadequate dietary intake. Second, underlying causes involve household food insecurity, poor healthcare access and unsanitary environments. Finally, basic causes encompass broader socioeconomic factors such as poverty, unemployment and low agricultural productivity.3 6

Research conducted in Bangladesh, India, Ethiopia and Tanzania has consistently revealed a high prevalence of undernutrition, with notable variations between rural and urban settings. These studies highlight a strong association between undernutrition and various risk factors, such as low birth weight, household poverty, increasing child age, lack of maternal or parental education, living environment and factors related to feeding practices and dietary diversity.7–10

While traditional anthropometric indices (stunting, wasting and underweight) provide specific information on the type of undernutrition, they fail to account for the overlap of these conditions in the same child.11 12 The Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF) addresses the distinction between children with at least one form of undernutrition and those without undernutrition. Unlike conventional isolated indices that may mask the true severity of undernutrition, the CIAF successfully identifies children suffering from multiple simultaneous deficits, providing a comprehensive assessment of the nutritional burden.7 8 13 14 A study conducted using data from the 2018 Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) showed that undernutrition based on the CIAF in the DRC was 47%, almost one in two children suffered from at least one form of malnutrition.15

Despite growing interest in this indicator, several significant gaps remain in the literature. First, most studies using the CIAF focus on children under 5 years of age as a whole, without specific analysis of the most vulnerable age groups.16 Second, data remain limited in some regions, particularly in Central Africa, where few studies have examined CIAF.11 17 Finally, analyses specifically targeting the 6–23 months age group remain scarce. Most existing evidence is either concentrated in East Africa18 19 or restricted to localised health zones.20 Focusing on children aged 6–23 months is essential, as this window of opportunity represents a critical phase of heightened biological vulnerability for both brain development and linear growth.21 Within the context of the DRC, undernutrition during the transition to complementary feeding is frequently irreversible, leading to permanent cognitive and physical impairments.18 Using the CIAF as the primary analytical framework, the aim of this study is to identify the specific dietary (infant and young child feeding, IYCF) and non-dietary factors associated with undernutrition. By isolating these drivers in this age group, the study provides the National Nutrition Programme (PRONANUT) with evidence-based insights to refine multisectoral interventions and improve nutritional outcomes during this critical developmental phase.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This was a secondary analysis of the 2023 ENN data. This survey was a nationally representative cross-sectional survey. The methodology adopted was based on the guidelines of the Standardised Expanded Nutrition Survey (V.1.3, March 2012), ensuring a rigorous and structured approach.22 The study was conducted across the 26 provinces of the DRC, which is administratively divided into 145 territories and 519 health zones. Spanning the equator in Central Africa, the DRC covers a total land area of 2 345 409 km². As of 2023, the national population was estimated at 102 262 808 inhabitants, reflecting the immense scale and demographic complexity of this national-level nutritional assessment.23

Study population

Data were collected from mothers/caregivers who had children aged 6–23 months with complete data on anthropometric index variables. Mother/caregivers whose children were obese or overweight were excluded from this study.

The size required for each stratum was calculated from the estimated prevalence from the 2017–2018 MICS-PALU survey by province.24

A total of 18 252 households were surveyed, which resulted in 20 176 children’s.

In secondary study

The study population was derived from the 2023 ENN database, which initially included 22 056 children under 5. For this analysis, we focused specifically on the 6–23 months age group. After excluding records with biologically implausible anthropometric data (EAA) and children whose parents were absent or had resided in the household for less than 4 months, the final analytical sample consisted of 5396 children. This sample includes 1792 infants aged 6–11 months and 3604 children aged 12–23 months. Sampling technique

In the 2023 ENN survey

A three-stage cluster random sampling was used. 26 strata corresponding to the 26 provinces were defined. In each province or stratum, 60 clusters (including six reserve clusters) were selected according to the number of stages of sampling. Primary units: In urban areas, these are the districts of communes; in rural areas, sectors or chiefdoms; Secondary units: Corresponding to streets, avenues or villages; Third stage of sampling: Use of the exhaustive list of households counted in each selected unit. This census was carried out during the passage of the investigators in each cluster, 13 randomly selected households were to be surveyed per cluster, for a total of 18 252 households.22

In this study

Data from children aged 6–23 months were extracted based on the defined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data collection

Following approval by the Ethics Committee of the Kinshasa School of Public Health (KSPH) and authorisation from PRONANUT, data were extracted from the 2023 ENN. A standardised household questionnaire captured sociodemographic characteristics, IYCF practices and health-related indicators.

Variables

The dependent variable

The dependent variable was undernutrition based on the CIAF, which derived from stunting (height-for-age), wasting (weight-for-height) and underweight (weight-for-age).3 Each anthropometric index was calculated by creating two categories z-score <−2 (undernutrition) and z-score ≥−2 to ≤1 (not undernutrition). The CIAF categories are classified in table 1. Undernutrition based on CIAF was defined as any child with at least one form of undernutrition, compared with children without undernutrition.

Independent variables

Independent variables were grouped into three categories. The following sociodemographic factors were considered: residential environment (urban and rural), mother’s education level, marital status, household size, number of children under 5, child’s age and position in the sibling group.

The following IYCF data were included: Dietary diversity: Adapted from the WHO Minimum Dietary Diversity indicator, these variables measure the types of food consumed by the child in the previous 24 hours. We classified it into three categories: high dietary diversity (consumption of foods from more than six distinct groups), average dietary diversity (consumption from four to five food groups) and low dietary diversity (consumption from fewer than four food groups, specifically lacking meat and/or legumes). Minimum meal frequency (MMF): Defined according to WHO standards based on the child’s age. It was divided into two categories: minimum acceptable feeding frequency (having consumed at least two meals a day of solid, semisolid or soft foods, appropriate for the child’s age) and minimum unacceptable feeding frequency (having consumed fewer than two meals a day, excluding breast milk).25

Finally, the hygiene/interventions: albendazole deworming, measles vaccination, the child having had diarrhoea in the last 2 weeks, acute respiratory infection, fever and the hand washing sequence.

Potential bias: non-response bias, like a risk of selection bias related to individuals who did not respond to the initial survey. Bias related to cluster representativeness: data collected did not initially reflect the demographic weight of each cluster. Mitigating bias: data weighting to minimise variations due to probability of individual selection. Selection bias was limited by excluding children with abnormal anthropometric data. Study base: the 2023 ENN, a nationwide cross-sectional survey using two-stage random cluster sampling.

Data processing and analysis

Data was processed on Microsoft Excel 2019 and imported into Stata V.17.0 software for a second cleaning. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). To ensure the representativeness of the findings and to account for the complex survey design of the 2023 ENN, all analyses were conducted using the svy command suite. This approach integrated the primary sampling units (clusters), stratification and sampling weights provided in the national database.

A descriptive analysis was first conducted to characterise the study population. Categorical variables, including maternal characteristics and IYCF indicators (MMF and Dietary Diversity), were presented as weighted frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, such as child age, were expressed as means with SD and 95% CIs.

To calculate anthropometric indices (weight-for-age, weight-for-height and height-for-age), we used the WHO Anthro WHO202006.26 The primary outcome was undernutrition, defined as the presence of at least one anthropometric failure according to the CIAF. By using a binary classification (Yes: B to Y and No: A, expressing no failure), the analysis was stratified into two age-specific cohorts to account for distinct developmental and nutritional milestones: infants aged 6–11 months (n=1792), who are most vulnerable during the transition to solid foods,20 and children aged 12–23 months (n=3604), who face increased pathogen exposure and higher dietary diversity requirements.19 This approach ensures that the identified determinants of undernutrition are precisely tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of each developmental stage.19 20 Bivariate analyses were performed to identify potential predictors (using χ² test: p<0.2).27

The independent factors associated with undernutrition were identified in two distinct modelling stages for each age stratum: the Model 1 (multivariate logistic regression) included all variables showing a significant association in the bivariate analysis (p<0.2), estimating their ORs and adjusted ORs (aORs). The Model 2 (using stepwise regression) was applied using a significance threshold of p<0.05 to identify the most parsimonious set of predictors for undernutrition in each age group. The statistical significance was set at p<0.05 and the results were reported with their respective aOR and 95% CI to indicate the strength and precision of the associations. The multicollinearity test was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF); ‘continued breastfeeding’ and measles vaccine were excluded due to their high VIF (11.6 and 12.4). After its removal, the models stabilised with mean VIFs of 1.8 (6–11 months) and 2.4 (12–23 months), ensuring the reliability of the aOR.

Results

Prevalence of undernutrition based on the CIAF and other traditional indices

This section presents the overall undernutrition prevalence. To provide a comprehensive assessment, undernutrition was first evaluated using traditional indices (defined as wasting, underweight and stunting) and subsequently compared with the CIAF. The overall prevalence of wasting, underweight and stunting among children aged 6–23 months was 9.8%, 23.7% and 42.1%, respectively. Across all traditional forms of undernutrition, the most affected group consisted of boys and children aged 12–23 months (9.7% wasting, 25.5% underweight and 47.5% stunting). Using CIAF, undernutrition prevalence was 47.4% (n=5396) (figure 2). This highlights that relying on a single index, such as stunting alone (42.1%), underestimates the true extent of undernutrition by 5.3% (11.2% of the total undernutrition group). As illustrated in figure 2, undernutrition was significantly higher among males (50.8%) compared with females (44.3%). The burden also increased sharply with age: 37.4% (n=1792) of infants aged 6–11 months, rising to 52.5% among those aged 12–23 months. The CIAF allowed for the identification of overlapping deficits.

Factors associated with undernutrition

Factors associated with undernutrition in children aged 6–11 months

For infants in the first phase of complementary feeding, biological and demographic factors predominated (table 4). In the multivariate analysis (Model 1), living in urban areas was significantly protective, reducing the odds of undernutrition by 40% (aOR=0.6; 95% CI 0.4 to 0.8; p=0.002). Male children were 1.7 times more likely to experience anthropometric failure compared with females (aOR=1.7; 95% CI 1.3 to 2.3; p=0.001). The most critical protective factor identified was MMF; infants receiving an acceptable meal frequency had an 88% lower risk of undernutrition (aOR=0.12; 95% CI 0.1 to 0.8; p<0.045). The stepwise model (Model 2) confirmed these associations while identifying household size as an additional driver. Specifically, having more than two children in the household increased the risk of undernutrition (aOR=1.3; 95% CI 1.1 to 1.6; p<0.01), while secondary maternal education (aOR=0.8; 95% CI 0.6 to 0.9; p<0.05) and MMF (aOR=0.2; 95% CI 0.1 to 0.8; p<0.05) remained significant protective factors.

Factors associated with undernutrition in children aged 12–23 months

In older children, the influence of maternal education, dietary quality and morbidity became more pronounced (table 5). According to Model 1, an educational gradient was observed: children of mothers with primary (aOR=0.6; p=0.001), secondary (aOR=0.5; p<0.001) or upper-level education (aOR=0.3; p=0.031) were significantly less likely to be undernourished than those whose mothers had no formal schooling. High dietary diversity (aOR=0.5; 95% CI 0.4 to 0.8; p=0.001) and acceptable MMF (aOR=0.6; 95% CI 0.5 to 0.8; p=0.001) served as major dietary safeguards. Conversely, a recent episode of diarrhoea increased the likelihood of undernutrition by 40% (aOR=1.4; 95% CI 1.1 to 1.8; p=0.021). The stepwise analysis (Model 2) reinforced these findings, highlighting the persistent protective role of urban residency (aOR=0.7; 95% CI 0.6 to 0.9; p<0.01) and high hand hygiene practices (aOR=0.7; 95% CI 0.6 to 0.9; p<0.05). The model also showed that children of widowed mothers had lower odds of undernutrition (aOR=0.8; 95% CI 0.7 to 0.9; p<0.01) compared with single mothers, while maternal education remained a primary determinant across all levels.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the specific dietary (IYCF practices) and non-dietary factors associated with undernutrition in children aged 6–23 months based on the database of the 2023 ENN in DRC. The results of this study reveal an alarming prevalence of undernutrition based on the CIAF in the DRC in 2023. The increased vulnerability of boys and children aged 12–23 months highlights the differentiated impact of undernutrition according to age and sex. Several factors were identified as being significantly associated with undernutrition, including rural environment, unacceptable food frequency, low dietary diversity, lack of maternal education as well as diarrhoea and hand hygiene.

Prevalence of undernutrition

The evaluation of traditional indices reveals a critical public health situation: stunting (42.1%) and wasting (9.8%) have reached and surpassed the emergency thresholds set by the WHO. These findings underscore that undernutrition in the DRC is not only widespread but represents a severe crisis requiring immediate, large-scale intervention.17

However, isolated metrics inherently underestimate the true nutritional burden. By using the CIAF, this study uncovers a higher, more realistic overall undernutrition prevalence of 47.4% among children aged 6–23 months. The critical added value of the CIAF lies in its capacity to capture multiple concurrent anthropometric deficits, ensuring no undernourished child is overlooked.28 The spatial distribution of undernutrition highlights a critical geographical disparity, with the highest prevalence concentrated in the Haut-Lomami and Nord Kivu. Furthermore, age-stratified analysis highlights a worsening trajectory: undernutrition increases significantly from 6 to 11 months to 12–23 months. This age-specific escalation, consistent with findings from the 2018 MICS survey,13 emphasises that older children become increasingly vulnerable due to the inadequate management of complementary feeding.19

Children with concurrent forms of undernutrition face exponentially higher mortality risks than their well-nourished peers. Evidence from a multicountry study spanning three continents demonstrates that children experiencing both wasting and underweight are 4.7 times (95% CI 3.1 to 7.1) more likely to die. Crucially, this mortality risk escalates to a 12.3-fold increase (95% CI 7.7 to 19.6) for those suffering simultaneously from stunting, wasting and underweight.29

Factors associated with undernutrition

The stratified analysis for the 6–11 month group highlights the predominance of biological and structural factors during the introduction of complementary foods. Boys exhibited a higher risk of undernutrition. This aligns with literature suggesting a greater biological vulnerability of male infants to environmental stress and higher metabolic demands during early growth.20 These results highlight the need for multisectoral interventions to address child undernutrition in the DRC.30 An integrated approach is essential to stabilise the nutritional situation.31

The household environment often acts as a significant barrier to optimal nutrition and quality healthcare in developing countries. Our findings confirm that children aged 6–23 months in rural areas face a higher risk of undernutrition compared with their urban counterparts.32 This disparity stems from restricted access to diverse food markets, healthcare facilities and educational resources, all of which exacerbate household poverty.33 Furthermore, while urban mothers generally benefit from better access to nutrient-dense foods, children in rural settings suffer from increased susceptibility to illness due to poor sanitary conditions and limited preventive care.7 The stepwise model specifically revealed that having more than two children (from 6 to 11 months) in the household increased the chance of undernutrition, suggesting a dilution of resources and caregiving time in larger families.13

Maternal education protects children from undernutrition at different ages. Educated mothers know good feeding practices, allowing them to adopt diets suited to their children’s growth. Uneducated mothers are often influenced by dietary traditions that limit access to nutritious foods, exposing their children to an increased risk of undernutrition.6 7 9 20 This trend was confirmed in the DRC through the 2022 Access to Primary Healthcare (ASSP) Project data.17 There is therefore a need to facilitate access to education, particularly among young girls, and to campaign against early marriages, which are the major causes of school dropout.34

Across both models and age groups, an acceptable MMF emerged as the primary protective factor against undernutrition. For children aged 12–23 months, high dietary diversity was equally critical, reflecting the increasing need for a broad micronutrient profile. At this stage, continued breastfeeding alone is insufficient, necessitating high-quality complementary feeding to support optimal development.35 These factors are in the delivery of undernutrition and indirectly affect children.36

In the 12–23 months group, recent diarrhoea remained a persistent risk factor, illustrating the deleterious infection-nutrition cycle. Poor hand hygiene serves as a primary vector for enteric pathogens, triggering diarrhoeal episodes that lead to nutrient absorption and increased metabolic needs.37 38 This is further supported by Model 2, where high hand hygiene emerged as a significant protective factor. These findings highlight the critical necessity of integrating Water, Hygiene and Sanitation (WASH) interventions within nutritional programmes to break the link between faecal-oral contamination, illness and subsequent undernutrition.39

These findings support a shift toward multisectoral nutrition-sensitive policies.40 Improving feeding practices through robust community initiatives is essential to address immediate nutritional needs while ensuring long-term sustainability and equity within food systems, as emphasised by the ninth Annual International Summit on Nutrition and Health.41 Consequently, strengthening PRONANUT programmes in the DRC requires a multisectoral approach that transcends simple IYCF promotion. Key interventions must integrate maternal literacy, the institutionalisation of hygiene practices within routine healthcare,12 42 43 and the scaling of nutrition-sensitive agriculture. Such integrated strategies are vital to bolstering household resilience and ensuring sustainable food security, particularly in the vulnerable rural regions of the DRC and all African regions.44

Strengths

A major strength of this study lies in its use of data from the 2023 ENN, which provides a comprehensive, nationwide representation of the DRC. With a robust sample size of 5396 children, the findings offer high statistical power and broad generalisability across the country. Furthermore, the parent survey’s utilisation of the Standardized Monitoring and Assessment of Relief and Transitions (SMART) methodology, a globally recognised and validated standard for nutritional assessments, ensures the reliability and accuracy of the anthropometric data analysed here.

Limitations

Data limitations: The cross-sectional nature of the 2023 ENN data precludes the establishment of direct causality between the identified factors and undernutrition.

Conclusion

This study reveals an alarming prevalence of undernutrition among children aged 6–23 months in the DRC. By using the CIAF, we demonstrated that traditional indices significantly underestimate the nutritional burden, failing to account for children with multiple concurrent deficits. The stratified analysis identified distinct risk profiles: infants aged 6–11 months are primarily vulnerable due to biological factors and low meal frequency, whereas those aged 12–23 months are affected by poor dietary diversity, maternal education gaps and the deleterious cycle of diarrhoea and poor hygiene.

To reverse these trends, it is imperative for PRONANUT to transcend traditional awareness programmes by implementing multisectoral interventions. These must prioritise maternal literacy, the integration of WASH protocols into routine care and the promotion of nutrition-sensitive family farming. Only a comprehensive approach that addresses both immediate dietary needs and underlying socioeconomic determinants can ensure a healthier and more equitable future for Congolese children.
