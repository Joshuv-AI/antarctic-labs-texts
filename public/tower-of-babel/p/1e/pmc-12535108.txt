Background

The surgical technique of sequential lung transplantations has not significantly changed since its original description [1]. A careful dissection of the recipient bronchus with minimal denudation is recommended to minimize ischemic complications. Although the length of donor or recipient pulmonary artery is rarely a problem, on occasion, the donor left atrial cuff may require reconstruction due to inadequate length preservation during procurement. In the recipient, the pulmonary veins are divided using a vascular stapler load proximally to the lung parenchyma. This provides sufficient atrium length to place a vascular clamp at the time of anastomosis. Preparing the recipient atrial cuff can present challenges due to anatomical drainage variability or early intrapericardial branching. Leveraging a small diameter four-row reload may help by providing access to tighter spaces with reduced stapled area while maintaining adequate hemostasis [2]. In this report, we describe our experience using Signia™ Small Diameter Reloads (SDR) (Medtronic, Inc.) for lung transplant recipient pneumonectomies.

Methods

The case series presented is a subset of a larger thoracic cohort from an observational study (NCT05095935) of which the use of SDR was evaluated. All patients within this case series were consecutively enrolled between May 2022 and March 2023 and underwent bilateral lung transplant procedures using a clamshell incision at a single institution performed by a single operating surgeon. Patients who were, or were expected to be, inaccessible for follow-up, and patients also enrolled in, or planned to enroll in, any concomitant drug or device study that may have confounded results within the study were excluded. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were kept intentionally broad to reduce bias and ensure a wide patient population was included. Each SDR staple-line, applied to pulmonary vasculature using a manual stapler handle, was visually evaluated for hemostasis [2]. Any intervention applied was described and characterized as clinically necessary or standard of care/preventative. All patients completed their 30-day follow-up; no protocol deviations occurred. All patients waived informed consent, which was approved by the Western-Copernicus Group (WCG) International Review Board (1324230) in December 2021. Where appropriate, the mean, the standard deviations, or the counts of available patients and percentages described the study outcomes using SAS 9.4 (SAS, Cary, NC).

Results

Thirty-five patients underwent bilateral lung transplants, where 138 firings (136 firings on pulmonary veins and 2 firings on pulmonary arteries) were analyzed. Twenty-three of the patients were males (65.7%), mean age was 59.6 ± 12.6 years, and mean BMI was 25.7 ± 4.3 kg/m2. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis was diagnosed in 57% and chronic obstructive lung disease in 29% of the patients. Mean operative time was 7.5 ± 2:04 h and the mean stapler reload use per patient was 4 ± 1 reloads. No stapler-related complications were observed intraoperatively and postoperatively, and no hemostatic maneuvers were necessary. Every SDR firing was ranked a 1 out of 5 on the hemostatic Likert scale meaning that there was no bleeding at the site after initial blotting of staple-line. Due to the intrinsic steps of a lung transplant, the SDR staple-lines in this study were only intact until the preparation of the left atrial cuff for implant (approximately 60 min). There were only two occasions where the superior trunk of the right pulmonary artery was stapled permanently with SDR to allow for a more favorable distal anastomosis; in both instances, there were no postoperative complications associated with the SDR staple-lines.

Discussion

Preparing the left atrial cuff can present technical challenges either at the time of donor procurement or due to recipient anatomical or geometrical variability. An appropriate donor explant, particularly when removing the heart, is mandatory to reduce the need for complex reconstruction [3]. In the recipient, particularly on the right side where anatomical variability is more frequent, maximizing extra pericardial pulmonary veins length is necessary to allow for vascular clamp placement [4]. SDR provides approximately 4 mm of extra length compared to a 12 mm stapler because of its lower profile and reduced width. As progression to minimally invasive surgical techniques continue, there may be advantages of this approach acknowledging anatomical variation is unavoidable. The narrow profile of SDR allowed for optimal maneuverability in tight spaces and secure transection of vasculature to increase the length of the pulmonary stump thereby reducing or eliminating the need to reconstruct the atrial cuff (Fig. 1). Figure 1 characterizes a side-by-side comparison of a small diameter 8 mm four-row stapler and a standard 12 mm six-row stapler. The small diameter four-row reload not only allows accessibility into narrow spaces, but when articulated, significantly improves maneuverability of the device. Given the smaller profile of staple deployment, recipient tissue preservation makes for better mobility of the atrial cuff, which can reduce the need for complex cuff reconstruction. The controlled nature of lung transplantation surgical approach allowed us to evaluate the hemostatic quality of a four-row staple- line when used to divide the large pulmonary vessels during recipient pneumonectomies. Although it is compatible with a powered handle, the four-row reload was easily deployed using a manual handle.

A growing body of literature describes minimally invasive approaches to bilateral lung transplantation with the utilization of a robotic platform [5]. Given the technical characteristics of SDR, these reloads would allow surgeons to safely maneuver around critical structures while downsizing incision size from the traditional clamshell incision to several 8 mm trocars and simultaneously maintaining adequate length for reconstruction.

Conclusions

This case series demonstrates the clinical utility of a small diameter four-row stapler reload for preservation of left atrial cuff length in recipient pneumonectomies. These findings may potentially apply to other surgical intervention because of the presented patient complexity, specifically patient ages and baseline lung diseases.
