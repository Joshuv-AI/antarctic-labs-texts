Ciprofloxacin hydrochloride-loaded ocular silk fibroin liposomes: Formulation, characterisation, in vitro cytotoxicity, and antimicrobial activity
Authors: Umer Mushtaq Bhat, Nisar Ahmad Khan, Syed Naiem Raza, Mohammad Ali, Seema Mehdi, Ishfaq Mohiuddin, Faiyaz Shakeel, Zulfikar Ali Bhat, Ghulam Nabi Bader, Ishtiaq Ahmad Chashoo, Shahid Ud Din Wani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ocular drugs have low absorption because of the unique environment in the eye, making ocular drugs one of the most challenging pharmaceutical initiatives. Liposomes have shown to be a promising ocular drug delivery system over the years because of enhanced drug absorption, biocompatibility, and biodegradability. Utilising a mucoadhesive material alongside liposomes could be a promising strategy to increase the therapeutic efficacy of ocular drugs. The present study aimed to develop a silk fibroin (SF)-coated liposomal formulation as an ocular drug delivery system. Regenerated silk fibroin (a novel biopolymer) was coated on ciprofloxacin hydrochloride-loaded liposomes (CPH-SFLs). Studies were carried out on the morphology, drug encapsulation efficiency, and in vitro drug release. Human corneal epithelial cells (HCEC) were used to examine the cellular adherence and cytotoxicity of CPH-SFLs. CPH-SFLs had an average particle size of 183 ± 3 nm as opposed to 169 ± 4 of blank SFLs. CPH-loaded SFLs lacked the endothermic peak of CPH at 150 °C, indicating that the CPH molecules were trapped in the SF polymeric grid. In the case of the formulations, 25 %–45 % of the medicine was released at a relatively fast pace over the course of the first 4 h and then at a slower rate over the course of the next 12–24 h. CPH-SFLs demonstrated sustained drug release and high in vitro ocular penetration of CPH. The MTT test was conducted to gauge the viability of HCECs, cell viability was higher than 85 %, demonstrating that CPH-SFLs had no adverse effects on HCEC. The observed CPH-SFL adhesions to HCECs were swift and persistent, like the cellular uptake of CPH-SFLs by HCECs. When compared to CPH-solution, the produced formulation CPH-SFLs demonstrated significantly (P < 0.0001) greater susceptibility. The studies concluded that SF-coated liposomes could be the most viable ocular drug delivery in comparison to conventional eye drops.

Introduction

Ocular drugs have low absorption because of the eye's distinctive structure and defensive mechanisms. A high tear turnover rate along with lacrimation and tear dilution drastically reduce the efficacy of ocular drug delivery systems in view of quick wash-off of the drug from the surface of the eye. Additionally, the tear film, corneal and conjunctival epithelia work together to form a compact barrier that deters drug absorption into the intraocular area [1]. To achieve more effective ocular delivery, researchers have concentrated on two strategies: enhancing drug transport over ocular barriers or increasing drug residence time on the exterior of the eye. Due to their clinical compliance and ease of administration, liquid eye drops are the optimal dosage forms for ocular delivery. To obtain the intended therapeutic effect, regular instillation of eye drops becomes necessary in view of limited ocular bioavailability. This frequent installation at the ocular surface causes pain, toxic side effects, and cellular damage [2,3]. To achieve more effective ocular delivery, researchers have concentrated on improving drug transport over ocular barriers and augmenting drug residence time on the exterior of the eye [4]. Numerous drug delivery systems have been developed to enhance ocular drug transport, including micro- or nanoemulsions [5,6], micro- or nanoparticles [5,6], micro- or nanoparticles [5,7], hydrogels [7], and liposomes [8]. Over the past ten years, liposomes have emerged as viable formulations for ocular drug administration due to the desired benefits of biocompatibility, biodegradability, and increased drug absorption [9]. Additionally, liposomes can enclose both hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules because of the phospholipid bilayers and watery cavities. Numerous drugs have been formulated as liposomal formulations for ocular distribution, which has led to high trans-corneal permeability and low tear-driven dilution in the conjunctival sac. Examples include ganciclovir [10], ciprofloxacin [11], and tropicamide [12].

Normal liposomes, on the other hand, demonstrated a comparable in vitro drug release profile and precorneal retention period to drug solution when administered topically [8]. To ensure controlled drugs and longer retention of the drug on the ocular surface, mucoadhesive polymers must be used as viscosity enhancers to increase the contact length between formulations and the corneal/conjunctival epithelium [13]. Swan et al. made the initial suggestion of methyl cellulose as a means of extending eye contact [14]. Preparations containing polymers, such as chitosan, hyaluronan, polysaccharides, and cellulose derivatives, have shown extension in their residence time in the precorneal region as a result of interactions with the mucus layers of ocular tissues [15]. In vitro drug release patterns were also extended in polymer-coated liposomes due to greater drug molecule diffusion barriers [8,15,16].

Bombyx mori mulberry silkworms produce the naturally occurring protein polymer known as silk fibroin (SF), which is made up of a heavy chain (350 kDa) and a light chain (25 kDa) joined by a sericin coating [17]. The highly immunogenic sericin covering can be removed by degumming SF to create a liquid form. A variety of carriers, including regenerable fibres, particles, membranes, films, gels, and porous matrix, can be made from the liquid silk protein with additional processing [18]. These processes frequently cause random coils and a-helices, which are in a water-soluble state, to change structurally when subjected to heat or organic solvents, resulting in β-sheet formation, which is in a water-insoluble form and has good mechanical properties and different drug delivery behaviors. Numerous investigations have been conducted on SF properties, such as mechanical superiority, nontoxicity, non-immunogenicity, and proteolytic degradability [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Formulations associated with SF that demonstrated improved cell adhesion and uptake behaviors were predicted to be the primary characteristics of cutting-edge ocular pharmaceutical delivery systems [23,24]. To our knowledge, no research has yet been published on the benefits and applications of SF-based nanoparticulate systems for the administration of ocular drugs.

The present study aimed to develop a silk fibroin (SF)-coated liposomal formulation as an ocular drug delivery system. Regenerated silk fibroin (a novel biopolymer) was coated on ciprofloxacin hydrochloride-loaded liposomes (CPH-SFLs). Studies were carried out on the morphology, drug encapsulation efficiency, and in vitro drug release. Human corneal epithelial cells (HCEC) were used to examine the cellular adherence and cytotoxicity of CPH-SFLs.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Sericultural Department, SKUAST (Srinagar, India) provided the gift sample of silk cocoons. Ciprofloxacin HCl was provided by Embiotic Laboratories (Bengaluru, India). Dialysis membranes were procured from HIMEDIA, India. Lithium bromide was obtained from Axiom Chemicals in Gujarat, India. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/F12 (DMEM/F12, HyClone, Thermo Scientific, USA) and foetal bovine serum (FBS), both from (#RM10432, Himedia, India), were used for cell culture. Human corneal epithelial cell line (PCS-700-010, ATCC, USA) and cell culture medium: corneal epithelial basal medium (Cat No: 700-030, ATCC). All other chemicals used were of analytical grade.

Preparation of regenerated silk fibroin

Aqueous SF solution was prepared as previously reported [25] with a few minor changes. Briefly stated cocoons were divided into minor fragments and degummed in boiling boiled 0.02 M Na2CO3 solution for 30 min. This process was repeated, and then the cocoons were thoroughly washed with deionised water. A 9.3 M lithium bromide solution was used to dissolve the degummed SF for 5 h at 60 °C. The resulting solution was dialysed for 72 h using dialysis membranes (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against deionised water to remove salts. The aqueous SF solution was centrifuged for 20 min at 9000 rpm while maintaining a temperature between 2 and 8 °C. The final SF content was ascertained by evaluating and weighing the dried SF that was obtained after drying at 60 °C for 12 h. For further studies, aqueous SF solutions were stored at 4 °C.

Preparation of liposomes

Using a rotary evaporator and dried thin film hydration method, liposomes containing soybean lecithin were created. In a 250 ml round bottom flask, known concentrations of the drugs, cholesterol, and phosphatidylcholine were dissolved in a solvent mixture of chloroform and methanol (2:1, v/v). after the organic solvent was evaporated at 40 °C under decreased pressure using a rotary evaporator (Büchi, type R 110, Switzerland), leading to the formation of a thin lipid coating on the walls of the flask. The flask was placed in a vacuum desiccator overnight to ensure that all the remaining organic solvents were completely removed. Sorensen's phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) was used to hydrate the thin lipid film at 37 ± 1 °C. The resulting suspension was then vortexed for approximately 2 min, followed by an incubation period of 2–3 h at room temperature to allow the lipid film to fully hydrate. The liposomal suspension was allowed to mature overnight in the refrigerator (4 °C).

The RPM was kept 60–65 RPM for drug loading, 65–70 RPM for film formation, 50–55 RPM for hydration. The Temperature was kept 40–45 °C for Film generation, 65–70 °C for hydration, and 65 °C for drug loading.

Formulation of SF-coated liposomes (SFLs)

The SFLs were produced using a modified methodology [23]. The formulated SF-coated liposomes (SFLs) were lyophilised at −80 °C without using cryoprotectant. with different dissolving durations were dissolved in PBS at a 5 % (w/v) concentration (pH 7.4) to completely stretch the protein chains. After that, the solutions were left with the proteins for several hours. The SF solutions were then mixed with the CPH-loaded liposome's aqueous dispersion to generate mixes. After 1 h of moderate agitation, methanol (50 % v/v) was added to the dispersions and blended to insolubilize them, and the SF was separated using centrifugation. SFLs were collected and maintained at 5 °C after two washes. By changing the ratio of drugs to polymer, six batches of SF-coated liposomes were developed.

Characterisation of SFLs

Particle size and zeta potential

A Malvern Zetasizer (Neon Series-ZS90, UK) was used to measure the produced CPH-SFL particle size and zeta potential in triplicate at 25 °C at a 90° angle [26,27]. At least three different batches' average values and standard deviations were provided.

Determination of drug entrapment efficiency and drug loading

Ten milligrams of the produced CPH-SFLs were dissolved in 10 ml of a ternary solvent mixture of CaCl2, C2H5OH, and H2O with a 1:2:8 M ratio at a temperature of 54–55 °C for approximately 1 h [28]. To determine drug entrapment after centrifugation, the amount of drug in the clear supernatant was measured (w) using a UV spectrophotometer at 254 nm. The percentage of drugs loaded and the efficiency of entrapment were calculated using the following calculations:%DrugEntrapmentefficiency=Totalamountofdrugaddedduringthepreparation(W)−amountofdruginsupernatanttotalamountofdrugaddedduringthepreparation(W)

By obtaining the drug from the liposome using 0.1 M hydrochloric acid, the amount of drug in the formulation was ascertained. The liposome (10 mg) was stirred into dissolution in 50 cc of hydrochloric acid during this procedure. The drug content was identified using Millipore filter paper and an acceptable dilution, employing UV spectroscopy, at 254 nm. The following formula was used to determine the liposome loading (L) effectiveness:DL(℅)=QnWnx100where Qn is the weight of the liposome and Wn is the amount of drug in the liposome.

Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and DSC analysis

FT-IR spectroscopy (Shimadzu-8400, Tokyo, Japan) was used to record the infrared spectra of pure CPH, SF, CH, SL, PC, Phy mix, and CPH-SFLs (CPH: Ciprofloxacin HCl, SF: Silk Fibroin CH: Cholesterol SL: Soya lecithin PC: Phosphatidylcholine Phy mix: Physical mixture CPH-SFLs: Ciprofloxacin HCl loaded silk fibroin liposomes). The samples were scanned with a 400–4000 cm−1 scanning zone and a resolution of 1 cm−1. The samples of liposomal complex and pure silk fibroin were scanned between 4000 and 500 cm−1.

Using a DSC (Differential Scanning Calorimeter NEXTA DSC Series, United Kingdom), the temperature behaviour of CPH, SF, CH, SL, PC, Phy mix, and CPH-SFLs was examined. The samples (4 mg) were stored in the DSC instrument packed in an aluminium pan. By applying pressure, the study was carried out underneath a nitrogen stream. The samples were heated to a steady rate of 10 °C/min between 35 and 350 °C.

Morphology

CPH-SFL dispersions were placed on a copper grid surface for scanning electron microscopy examination (SEM, JEM-100 scanning electron microscope, Japan) with an accelerating voltage of 30 kV [29].

In vitro drug release

A phosphate buffer solution with a pH of 7.4 served as the release medium for the in vitro release study of CPH from SFLs to mimic the ocular environment within corneal tissue and the physiological pH. In this experiment, phosphate-buffered saline was used to disperse 20 mg of free CPH and lyophilised CPH-loaded SFLs, and the resulting mixture was then filled with medium in a dialysis membrane (MWCO 12–14 kDa). The medium was rotated at 54 rpm for 48 h. The release medium temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C at predefined intervals (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h). To maintain the sink condition, aliquots were removed from the release medium and replaced with the same volume of fresh PBS.

Release kinetics

Drug release from CPH Loaded SFLs was established by adapting data from in vitro studies to various kinetic equations. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model, Higuchi model, first order equation, and zero-order equation were the kinetic equations employed [28].

Cell culture

In vitro cytotoxicity

Primary Corneal Epithelial Cells; Normal, Human (ATCC® PCS-700- 010™) along with the Corneal Epithelial Cell Growth Kit (ATCC® PCS-700-040™), the Corneal Epithelial Cell Basal Medium (ATCC® PCS-700-030™) were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and used in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The ATCC centres distribute authenticated cell lines, following the ANSI standard for human cell line authentication. Corneal epithelial basal medium supplemented with 10 % FBS, 1 % antibiotic-antimycotic solution, and 1 % L-glutamine (200 mM) were used to maintain the HCE cells at 37 °C in an environment of 5 % CO2, 18–20 % O2. Every two days, the cells were sub cultured in a CO2 incubator. Mycoplasma free cells of passage number 04 were used for the current study.

For CPH-SFLs intended for ocular administration, HCE cell lines were chosen as the model cells for the study. The chosen cells were grown in keratinocyte medium devoid of serum. The medium comprised 10 % v/v bovine serum, 500 ng/mL hydrocortisone, 100 ng/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, 0.005 mg/mL insulin, and 10 % v/v bovine serum along with 0.05 mg/mL bovine pituitary extract and 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor. Cells were cultivated in a culture flask at 37 °C in a humidified 10 % CO2 environment after the cells were 80 % confluent. To determine the viability of the HCE corneal cell line, MTT (bromide of 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazole) was used as a cell viability indicator. Cells were seeded using a 200 μl solution of cells at the needed cell density of (2 × 104 cells per well) in a 96-well plate. After a 24-h incubation period at 37 °C, the cells were moved to a 96-well plate and treated with control, CPH, and CPH-SFL solutions at various drug concentrations (0.025–0.4 mg/mL). At 37 °C and 5 % CO2, the plates were incubated for 24 h. After the incubation period, the waste medium was removed from the plates, and MTT reagent was added at a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml of the total volume. To prevent exposure to light, aluminum foil was used to wrap the plates. The plates were returned to the incubator for 3 h. The cells were incubated for an additional 2 h in new media containing 0.25 % MTT after being incubated for 24 h. The liquid was withdrawn, and DMSO was used to lyse the cells. The solubility was enhanced by moderate swirling in a gyratory shaker. A UV–visible spectrophotometer and an ELISA reader were used to simultaneously measure the absorbance at 570 nm. The results of the MTT test are shown as a percentage drop in comparison to untreated (control) cells [30].

Cell uptake study

At a density of 2 × 104 cells/0.2 ml, culture cells were placed in a Nunc™ 96-Well Optical-Bottom Plate and incubated in a CO2 incubator for 24 h at 37 °C. The culture medium used was removed, and the cells were treated with an experimental chemical at a concentration of 50 μg/ml previously conjugated with 50 μg/ml FITC dye in 200 μl of culture medium. The cells were then kept in the dark for 4 h. Prior to treatment, FITC-conjugated formulations were stirred magnetically overnight for a period of 12 h at 37 °C. The medium was removed from each well at the end of the treatment period, and the cells were washed with PBS. A 10 μg/ml solution of Hoechst 33342 was used to counterstain the cells. Under a Carl Zeiss LSM 880 Fluorescence Live Cell Imaging System (Confocal Microscope) and using DAPI and FL1 (FITC) filter cubes, cells were examined and captured. Zen Blue Software was used to examine the images, and ImageJ (FIJI) was used to measure the relative fluorescence intensity values.

After dissolving 1 mg/mL of lyophilised CPH-SFLs in sodium buffer solution (pH 9.0), 1 mg/mL of FITC in DMSO was added while stirring at a ratio of 1:100 (w/w) for FITC: SF. The mixture was extensively dialysed in a cellulose membrane (MWCO 8–14 kDa) using deionised water for three days followed by a 12-h reaction in the dark at 4 °C ambient conditions [35]. Nile red (a common hydrophobic fluorescent model) was encapsulated using the same pretreatment procedures as were utilised to generate SFLs and liposomes with CPH. SF solutions, liposomes, and SFLs were used to label HCECs after they were seeded in vessels and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then completely washed with PBS, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and stained with 40,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution following the necessary incubation times at 37 °C. SF and liposomal formulations were used to study cell adhesion and uptake using confocal microscopy (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany).

Antimicrobial assessment

The cup plate method was used to determine the antimicrobial activity of CPH-SFLs and CPH solution. Under aseptic conditions, sterilised nutrient agar media was prepared and added to sterilised Petri plates containing strains of Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 96), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MTCC 1688) and Escherichia coli (MTCC 443) obtained from Microbial Type Culture Collection and Gene Bank (MTCC), Institute of Microbial Technology (IMTECH), Chandigarh, India. For a uniform distribution of microorganisms, the media was well mixed before being set aside to solidify. Using a sterilised borer, a well (4–5 mm in diameter) was created, and then normal saline (as a control), CPH-SFLs, and CPH solutions were added to the well. The zone of inhibition was determined after the plates were incubated for a period of 24 h at 37 °C.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were performed in triplicate using one-way ANOVA and Dunnett's multiple comparisons test to evaluate the data sets using SPSS software.

Any possible interactions between the drug (CPH) and the polymers (SF), (CH), (SL), and (PC) were examined using FTIR analysis (Fig. 1). In the FTIR spectra of CPH, the stretching bands of N-H and O-H were observed at 3368 cm−1 together with those of C-H at 2984 cm−1, C=O stretching at 1738 cm−1, and C=C stretching at 1492 cm−1. The spectra of SF revealed distinctive bands of crystalline β-sheet domains, demonstrating the stability of the structure of silk fibroin. The bands connected to amides I, II, and III were found at approximately 1615, 1528, and 1228 cm−1, respectively.

The C=O stretching, N-H stretching, and O-H stretching bands of CPH were physically combined, and their properties were preserved in the FTIR spectrum (CPH + SF + CH + SL + PC). It was interesting to note that the FTIR spectra of optimised CPH-loaded SFLs retained the distinctive absorption bands of free CPH and SF. Additionally, when encasing CPH inside SF-NPs, no significant change in the absorption bands was observed, indicating that CPH and SF were compatible.

DSC is commonly used to study the physical characteristics of pharmaceuticals, polymers, and formulations. It determines how samples' heat gain or loss varies with temperature [33]. Fig. 2 displays the DSC thermograms of free CPH, SF, CH, PC, physical mixtures, and optimised CPH-loaded SFLs. Pure CPH reached a noticeable endothermic peak at 150 °C. The thermal breakdown of the chains of silk fibroin was associated with the endothermic melting peak of SF at 286 °C. The compatibility between SF and CPH was shown by the characteristic peaks of each component on a physical mixture DSC thermogram (CPH + SF + CH + SL + PC + CH). It is intriguing that CPH-loaded SFLs lacked the endothermic peak of CPH at 150 °C, indicating that the CPH molecules were disseminated or trapped in the SF polymeric network.Fig. 2DSC analysis of A) Ciprofloxacin HCl B) Silk Fibroin C) Cholesterol D) Soya lecithin E) Phosphatidylcholine F) Physical mixture G) Optimised Ciprofloxacin HCl loaded silk fibroin liposomes.Fig. 2

In vitro drug release

To determine the impact of the SF coating on the release of the drug, an in vitro release study was performed on a solution of SFLs containing ciprofloxacin HCl (CPH) (CPH-SFLs). Particularly in the SFLs, the first burst of drug release seen in CPH solution was less pronounced in liposomal samples. Additionally, as the SF dissolving time increased, the discharge rates increased as well. The initial CPH concentration considerably influenced the percentage of drug released after 4 h, whereas the molar cholesterol (CH) concentration significantly influenced the percentage of drug released after 24 h.

The drug concentrations released from liposomal formulations after 12 h were measured using the dialysis method. In the case of the formulations, 25 %–45 % of the medicine was released at a relatively fast pace over the course of the first 4 h and then at a slower rate over the course of the next 12–24 h (Fig. 4).Fig. 4In vitro release study of CPH from SFLs in PBS 7.4.Fig. 4

In vitro release study of CPH from SFLs in PBS 7.4.

Initially, drug release was rapid and was caused by detachment from the liposomal surface. Later, drug release was delayed and was caused by persistent drug release from the interior lamellae. As a result, the drug's release from the liposomes is slower than when it is free. When comparing the drug release profiles of the formulations with the free drug ciprofloxacin (CPH), one-way ANOVA revealed a significant difference (P < 0.0001). This is due to (Cholesterol) CH's capacity to alter membrane fluidity and lower bilayer permeability, which minimises drug efflux and prolongs drug retention. Liposomes loaded with CPH have better properties than pure CPH, such as regulated or sustained release, protection from drug degradation and clearance, enhanced therapeutic effects, and fewer side effects.

Release kinetics

To determine the release system, in vitro release kinetics were carried out. By using linear regression analysis and the slope of the suitable plots, the release constants were calculated and the regression coefficient (R2) was determined. For formulations, the first-order release values were 0.847–0.946 and 0.808–0.892 for zero order. In contrast, the results for the Korsmeyer–Peppas model were R2 (0.932) & 0.513 (n), and the Higuchi model's values were 0.929 (R2) & 18.23 (kH). It was determined that the exponent values fell between (0.920–0.990). Based on this, the (n) values show a non-Fickian diffusion release because they are higher than 0.5.

The MTT assay was then used to monitor cell viability. It was deemed reasonable to presume that the control sample would include 100 % viable cells to calculate the relative cell viability. Drug-free SFLs did not clearly exhibit any cytotoxicity against any of the tested cell lines, as shown in Fig. 5, indicating the safety of SFLs. However, against both HCEC cell lines, unbound CPH had a minor, concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect. It was interesting to note that CPH-SFLs were not significantly cytotoxic to HCEC cells; even after treatment with 200 μg/ml, cell viability was greater than 95 %. High doses of CPH-SFLs (400 μg/ml) resulted in a (<90 %) reduction in cell viability. Our combined findings highlighted the least cytotoxicity.

Cell uptake study

A confocal microscope was used to observe CPH-SFLs that were FITC-labelled and liposomes that contained Nile red when they were exposed to HCEC. The adhesion behavior of SF to HCECs over a 12-h period, while FITC's green fluorescent cells were discernible after 10 min of exposure to CPH-SFLs, and throughout the following 12 h of incubation, the intensity of the fluorescence steadily increased. The observed CPH-SFL adhesions to HCECs were swift and persistent [23,24]. Liposomes and SFLs were put onto Nile red. Nile red fluorescence appeared brighter in coated liposomes than in uncoated liposomes, suggesting that coated liposomes were rapidly and effectively absorbed by HCECs and retained for up to 4 h. Discussion

SF can be used due to its effectiveness as a controlled drug release excipient because its structure changes throughout formulation processes [24,38]. Silk I, II, and III are the three primary silk polymorphs that have been identified [39]. There are no reports about how SF interacts with liposomes. The liposomes reported were positively charged according to the zeta potential measurements. Since SF is negatively charged and has an isoelectric point between 3.8 and 3.9, it can interact electrostatically with liposomes, resulting in the creation of an SF coating surrounding the liposomes. Additionally, the addition of foreign compounds such as cholesterol may increase the liposome bilayer's mobility, which could boost the adsorption of SF proteins to the liposome surface [40]. Close to the lipid bilayer, the SF hydrophobic chain may insert, functioning as an anchor to keep the two in touch. During the structural transition, SF chains then connect several single vesicular liposomes to form a multivesicular cluster [41].

The results of the in vitro release investigation showed an inverse relationship between the SF concentration and drug release rate. Because the thickness of the SF coating layer functioning as a release barrier grew as the SF concentration rose. Since they adhere to collagen, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans on the surface of cells, SFLs may target keloid fibroblasts. Both the free SF and the SFLs displayed quick and persistent cell adhesion and uptake behaviors in the current investigation [42,43]. The interactions among formulations and the tear film, in addition to the physicochemical characteristics of the drugs and formulation, may affect the mucoadhesion of ocular administration systems [44]. It has been proposed that mucus, which is made up of water, glycol proteins, proteins, lipids, electrolytes, enzymes, and muco-polysaccharides, makes up a large portion of the human tear film [45,46]. Given that SF has a high affinity for binding, its adherence to the outer mucopolysaccharide layer may improve the likelihood that SF-containing formulations will stick to the cell surface in comparison to glycoproteins and proteoglycans [47].

It was interesting to note that CPH-SFLs were not significantly cytotoxic to HCEC cells; even after treatment with 200 μg/ml, cell viability was greater than 95 %. Moreover, neither SF nor SF-coated liposomes showed overt cytotoxicity during the tests [48]. Nile red fluorescence appeared brighter in coated liposomes than in uncoated liposomes, suggesting that coated liposomes were rapidly and effectively absorbed by HCECs and retained for up to 4 h. The cell's ability to survive the experiment demonstrates that SF and SFLs did not directly cause cytotoxicity [49]. The negatively charged bacteria surfaces are more strongly interacted by the cationic CHF-SFLs, which break the membrane and cause internal chemical leakage, killing the microorganisms. The increased antibacterial activity of CPH-SFLs is a result of their increased surface energy, spherical particle structure [42,50,51].

All these findings suggested that SF would be a reliable and safe biopolymer for ocular drugs administration.

Limitations of the study

Short half-lives, storage issues, and low hydrophilic drug entrapment efficiency, are few of the drawbacks of liposomal systems [52]. Because of its structural characteristics, liposomal ocular administration is remarkably biocompatible with tear film. Unsaturated lipids that make up liposomes can hydrolyze or oxidise, making the liposomes chemically unstable. Additionally, modifications to the liposome's properties are necessary for the longer sustained administration of drugs.

Conclusions

SF can be used due to its effectiveness as a controlled drug release excipient because its structure changes throughout formulation processes. CPH-SFLs had an average particle size of 183 ± 3 nm as opposed to 169 ± 4 of blank SFLs. The liposomes reported were positively charged according to the zeta potential measurements. CPH-loaded SFLs lacked the endothermic peak of CPH at 150 °C, indicating that the CPH molecules were trapped in the SF polymeric grid. In the case of the formulations, 25 %–45 % of the medicine was released at a relatively fast pace over the course of the first 4 h and then at a slower rate over the course of the next 12–24 h. CPH-SFLs demonstrated sustained drug release and high in vitro ocular penetration of CPH. The MTT test was conducted to gauge the viability of HCECs, cell viability was higher than 85 %, demonstrating that CPH-SFLs had no adverse effects on HCEC. confirming that SF-coated liposomes did not induce any cytotoxicity.

Moreover, the two stages of the drug transport process were the release of the drug from the SFLs following corneal cell's cell attachment and uptake of CPH-loaded SFLs. The observed CPH-SFL adhesions to HCECs were swift and persistent, like the cellular uptake of CPH-SFLs by HCECs. To explain how formulations adhere to the tear film, various ideas (interpenetration, adsorption, wetting, diffusion, or electronic) have been proposed. Given that SF has a high affinity for binding, its adherence to the outer mucopolysaccharide layer may improve the likelihood that SF-containing formulations will stick to the cell surface in comparison to glycoproteins and proteoglycans. The SF formulation's interior cellular interactions were seen as SF vesicle binding and adsorption processes. The cellular uptake experiment further confirmed the effective and prolonged retention of SFLs in cornea, as indicated by the continuous permeation of CPH in SFLs.

SF-coated liposomes loaded with ciprofloxacin HCl (CPH-SFLs) for ocular drugs administration demonstrated prolonged drug release, fast and constant adhesion, and the uptake of CPH-SFLs in corneal cells during the study without any obvious cytotoxicity. SF-coated liposomes are a promising method of delivering ocular medicine due to all these characteristics.
