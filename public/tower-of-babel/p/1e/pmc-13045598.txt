Returning to Exercise After Valsalva-Induced Carotid Artery Dissection

Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS) is a connective tissue disorder caused by mutations in the COL3A1 gene, which encodes type III collagen,1 and affects approximately 1:50,000 individuals worldwide. In affected individuals, vEDS is characterized by arterial fragility and joint hypermobility, with increased risk of arterial aneurysm and dissection. Internal carotid artery (ICA) dissection (ICAD), caused by tear of the intimal carotid wall allowing blood to enter and separate layers of the artery, occurs at higher rates in those with vs without vEDS, particularly after Valsalva maneuvers or physical exertion.2

Exercise recommendations for post-ICAD patients with vEDS represent a significant clinical dilemma. Traditional postdissection management has emphasized complete, permanent exercise restriction to avoid recurrence or extension of dissection secondary to the increased blood pressure (BP), vascular mechanical stress, and risk of trauma that can occur with physical activity.3 However, complete activity avoidance may be more deleterious than beneficial as it could lead to physical or mental decline in the form of weight gain, loss of blood pressure control, anxiety, and/or depression.4,5

The exact “thresholds” of safe exercise intensity and modality in such individuals have not been identified, with little research into cardiac rehabilitation (CR) after carotid artery dissection. In postoperative management of thoracic aortic dissection, guidelines recommend structured exercise training (ExT) at an intensity of 3 to 5 metabolic equivalents of the task while avoiding strenuous lifting, lifting to exhaustion, or activities that result in maximal exertion.6 Importantly, 71% of people expressed a desire for specific recommendations on safe physical activities after aortic dissection.7 Given the documented cardiometabolic and mental health benefits of regular exercise and the reasonable presumption that ICAD survivors desire clarity on safe vs “unsafe” exercise, there is a clinical need for personalized, data-driven exercise plans developed through shared decision-making (SDM).3 This case report outlines the successful application of an SDM-driven progressive ExT program in a 19-year-old man with vEDS after 2 separate ICADs occurring 12 months apart.

Case Summary

A 19-year-old man experienced 2 separate ICADs 12 months apart. The first occurred in the left ICA during martial arts and was complicated by recurrent transient ischemic attacks and temporal artery stroke that required angioplasty and stenting. One month after discharge, genetic testing confirmed vEDS (COL3A1 mutation); at this time, the patient's neurosurgery team suggested continuing dual antiplatelet therapy for at least 6 months and avoiding strenuous activity and contact sports. Twelve months after the original dissection, the patient presented to the emergency department with sudden onset slurred speech, left-sided jaw pain, and right-hand numbness after sneezing (Valsalva maneuver). A computed tomography angiogram revealed dissection of right ICA with severe stenosis (Figure 1). The left ICA showed adequate blood flow on computed tomography angiography, confirming that the stent was patent. As flow was somewhat preserved in the right ICA, the patient and his family opted for medical management with dual antiplatelet therapy, angiotensin receptor blocker, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist without surgical intervention.

Procedural Steps

Cardiac rehabilitation enrollment and exercise plan

Before the ICADs, the patient was active in baseball and martial arts. However, owing to exercise restrictions and fear of recurrent dissections and transient ischemic attacks with exercise, he adopted a sedentary lifestyle resulting in significant deconditioning (6-minute walk test distance: 417 m, 46% predicted), 11-kg weight loss, anxiety requiring benzodiazepine, and medical withdrawal from college.

Approximately 5 months after the second dissection, through SDM between himself, his physician, the rehabilitation team, and his mother, the patient was referred to CR to guide his safe return to exercise. Addressing continued sedentariness vs returning to exercise, the SDM discussion centered on types of exercise and risk stratification, psychological and quality-of-life factors, symptom recognition, long-term risk reduction, and the importance of a monitored return. After extensive review of the patient's history, the patient was prescribed a moderate-intensity (defined by rate of perceived exertion [RPE]), progressive aerobic and resistance ExT plan focused on improving functional capacity, minimizing vascular stress, and restoring physical confidence through reconditioning. Through CR, aerobic and resistance exercise frequency and duration was progressed, whereas the number of repetitions and sets and “safe” BP and RPE limits were maintained (Figure 4). Initially, the ExT plan was conservative, including moderate treadmill walking and low-load resistance training using bands, machines, body weight, and free weights. BP and RPE were checked immediately after each set to maintain a target RPE of 11 to 15 and a reasonable systolic BP response in the range of 130 to 140 mm Hg. The patient was educated on how body position and movement types affect BP responses, proper warm-up and cool-down, and how to properly breathe through a repetition. Over 22 weeks (36 sessions of CR) aerobic workload and resistance training volumes gradually increased, and the patient was introduced to movements that involved Valsalva maneuvers or “abdominal bracing.” He exercised in the clinic during monitored CR sessions and independently at home, avoiding high-strain isometrics, collision, and any activities that could cause abrupt neck motion. The patient had no significant complications or medical events during the 22 weeks of CR.

Follow-up testing and plan progression

After CR, the patient gained 11 kg in body mass, 6-minute walk test distance increased 58% (417-658 m, 74% predicted), depression severity was attenuated (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 score 4-2), and overall health-related quality of life was improved (Dartmouth Co-Operative Charts score 21-15). Moreover, he discontinued benzodiazepines and re-enrolled in college. One month after completing CR, repeat magnetic resonance angiography showed no evidence of new vascular pathology, confirming a stable vascular status after resuming structured activity (Figure 3). The left-sided pseudoaneurysm, a known finding from his original dissection and stenting, remained stable. These outcomes indicate that the individualized ExT was well tolerated, safe, and enabled a smooth transition to independent exercise beyond rehabilitation. This case highlights how structured ExT through CR can be safely and effectively adapted for vascular disorders such as vEDS.

Potential Pitfalls

Regular exercise improves cardiovascular health, positively modifies cardiovascular risk factors, enhances muscle function and strength, promotes endothelial adaptation and remodeling, and enhances mood and cognitive function. However, the implementation of ExT in people with vEDS remains a debated clinical topic.4,5 vEDS is characterized by extreme tissue fragility that portends persistent and unpredictable risk of arterial or organ rupture. This risk, which is highly individualized and multifactorial depending on the COL3A1 variant and family history of arterial dissection, raises reasonable concern that sudden BP spikes with exercise can increase the chances of aneurysm expansion or rupture.8 Connective tissue fragility also poses risk for joint instability and musculoskeletal injury during exercise. Minimal direct evidence to guide safe, validated ExT for vEDS exists, and current recommendations to limit trauma-prone activities (collision sports, heavy lifting, and high-resistance training) come from case reports and field experts.2,3,7

Owing to the underlying tissue fragility, balancing the rewards vs risk of ExT in people with vEDS is complex. ExT enhances cardiorespiratory fitness and lowers the HR and systolic BP response, making it feasible that structured ExT might lower lifelong recurrent vascular risk during exertion-related and non–exertion-related stresses. Conversely, considering possible “worst case” scenarios, repeat dissection during exercise could lead to stroke with associated neurologic deficits or death. Thus, the initial exercise evaluation must be thorough, and the entire medical team should be aware of the risks when considering CR protocol design.

Importantly, the patient must be capable of self-monitoring symptoms and BP during exercise and have knowledge to adjust exercise intensity to avoid overexertion and injury. This patient had previous recreational sport experience; however, the patient was given specific guidance on emergency action planning based on American Heart Association guidelines for athletes with cardiovascular abnormalities.9 In case of a syncopal event, chest pain, or neurologic symptoms, the patient was instructed to report to the emergency department immediately. He was also instructed to never exercise alone and ensure access to emergency medical services before exercising. This plan aligned with the medical history, goals, and lifestyle of this patient, and had gradual progression with follow-up imaging to monitor artery stability.

Notably, this patient experienced kinesiophobia after his dissection, which is common in such individuals.10 The fear of exercise and movement was managed through SDM and may require management during guided ExT in similar cases. There is potential for pitfalls if SDM is not collaborative and adjustable with an emphasis on safety and adherence to exercise parameters such as BP and RPE thresholds.

Conclusions

Initial low-intensity, gradually progressed ExT is potentially safe and efficacious in people with vEDS after ICAD. SDM-centered CR with continual monitoring and follow-up can result in remarkable improvements in key clinical metrics—a 58% increase in functional exercise capacity, improved quality-of-life, and successful return to normal activity without vascular complications. This approach provides a replicable framework for CR professionals encountering similar complex vEDS cases. Further research should evaluate the safety and long-term durability of structured exercise in this population, emphasizing routine imaging and functional testing to ensure arterial stability, monitor disease progression, and detect complications or recurrent events.
