A novel NYX gene mutation identified in a Chinese Northeast Han family with high myopia and night blindness
Authors: Xiaoyi Xu, Liwen Zhang, Ying Li, Lili Dai, Zheng Xiao, Dongqi Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the pathogenic gene mutation associated with high myopia and congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB) in a family from Northeast China. The objective was to clarify the underlying genetic basis and to determine the inheritance pattern of these ocular disorders.MethodsWhole-exome sequencing was performed on the proband using the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Variants were filtered based on an allele frequency of less than 0.5% in the gnomAD database. Their pathogenic was evaluated using multiple in silico prediction tools, including CADD, REVEL, SpliceAI, SIFT, MutationTaster, PolyPhen-2, and AlphaMissense. The identified NYX mutation was subsequently validated through Sanger sequencing in the proband and six additional family members. In addition, comprehensive clinical evaluations were conducted. These included detailed ophthalmic examinations, assessment of refractive status, and fundus imaging. These investigations were performed to characterize the phenotypic features and to establish correlations with the genetic findings.ResultsA novel frameshift mutation, c.264dup p. (Arf89Alafs *26), was identified in exon 3 of the NYX gene. The proband exhibited extremely high myopia, with a spherical equivalent of less than − 23.00D. This condition was accompanied by typical clinical features of CSNB. Structural analysis indicated significant conformational changes in the mutant protein. Pedigree analysis supported an X-linked recessive mode of inheritance. This pattern was consistent with both the clinical presentation and the genetic findings observed in the affected family.ConclusionThis study is the first to identify the NYX c.264dup mutation as a causative variant of complete CSNB (cCSNB) accompanied by extreme high myopia. This finding expands the known mutational spectrum of the NYX gene. These results provide valuable evidence for improving molecular diagnostic strategies. They also offer important support for more accurate genetic counseling in affected individuals and their families.

Introduction

Myopia has become the leading cause of visual impairment worldwide. Its prevalence is increasing at an alarming rate. High myopia, is projected to rise from 2.9% to 9.8% by 2050 [1]. This condition is strongly associated with severe ocular complications. These include retinal detachment, chorioretinal degeneration, and choroidal neovascularization [2]. Therefore, understanding the underlying mechanisms of myopia is essential for effective prevention and management. Both genetic and environmental factors, contribute to the development of myopia. Key environmental influences include prolonged near work, limited exposure to outdoor activities, and insufficient light conditions [3–6]. In recent decades, extensive genetic research has been conducted. These studies include linkage analysis, whole-exome sequencing, meta-analyses, and genome-wide association studies. Collectively, they have identified approximately 500 candidate genes and 27 susceptibility loci associated with myopia [7–8]. Syndromic forms of myopia represent an important yet underexplored resource for identifying genetic variants involved in myopia development [9]. Several well-characterized ophthalmic syndromes are associated with high myopia. They include Stickler syndrome [10], Marfan syndrome [11], CSNB [12], retinitis pigmentosa (RP) linked to RP2 and RPGR mutations [13–14], Bornholm ophthalmopathy [15], and cone-rod dystrophy [16]. These conditions share overlapping clinical features. They also provide valuable insights into the genetic architecture of refractive error [17].

CSNB comprises a heterogeneous group of non-progressive inherited retinal diseases. These disorders characterized by dysfunction of rod photoreceptors or impaired signal transmission between photoreceptors and bipolar cells. CSNB presents with diverse clinical manifestations. Common features include night blindness, reduced visual acuity, nystagmus, myopia, and strabismus. The condition also exhibits marked genetic heterogeneity [18]. Based on electroretinogram (ERG) findings, CSNB is classified into two principal types: the Riggs type and the Schubert-Bornschein type [19–20]. The Riggs type is characterized by a proportional reduction in both a- and b-wave amplitudes. This pattern indicates dysfunction at the level of rod photoreceptors. In contrast, the Schubert-Bornschein type shows a selectively reduced b-wave under scotopic conditions. This finding reflects dysfunction of signal transmission from the photoreceptor cells to the bipolar cells. The Schubert-Bornschein type can be further subdivided into cCSNB and incomplete CSNB (iCSNB) [21]. cCSNB is characterized by a severely reduced b-wave with a relatively preserved a-wave. This pattern is typically observed under scotopic conditions and indicates dysfunction of ON bipolar cells. iCSNB presents with a reduced but recordable rod b-wave under scotopic conditions and markedly diminished cone responses under photopic conditions, with the 30-Hz flicker ERG often showing a characteristic bifid peak. This profile reflects dysfunction of both ON and OFF bipolar cells [22]. Molecular genetic studies have identified several genes associated with cCSNB. These include NYX [23–24], GRM6 [25, 26], TRPM1 [27–29], LRIT3 [30], and EGFLAM [31]. Among these, NYX is the most frequently implicated gene [32]. According to global variant databases that catalog pathogenic variants and affected alleles, a total of 155 pathogenic variants have been reported in the NYX gene to date [33].

In this study, we recruited a family from Northeast China affected by both high myopia and congenital night blindness. The aim was to identify the underlying causative genetic variant.

Methods

Patient ascertainment

This study examined a four generation Chinese family from Northeast China. The participants were recruited through the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine(YJSKY2024-397). All procedures were conducted in compliance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulatory standards. Comprehensive ophthalmologic evaluations were performed for all participants. These assessments included uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, and fundus examination. All examinations were carried out by the same team of experienced ophthalmologists to ensure consistency. Relevant clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. ERG recordings were obtained an ERG-jet contact lens electrode placed on the cornea after topical anesthesia. The procedures followed the standards established by the International Society for Clinical Electrophysiology of Vision (ISCEV) [34]. ERG findings were interpreted by comparison with established ISCEV reference ranges derived from 407 healthy subjects using the same recording protocol [35]. Detailed medical histories were collected from all participants. A comprehensive family history was also obtained as part of the genetic counseling process.

Whole exome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using standard protocols. Whole-exome sequencing was performed on seven family members (III-2, III-4, III-5, IV-1, IV-2, IV-3, and IV-4) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, USA) at the Wenzhou Genomics Institute. The proband achieved an average sequencing depth of 97.29x using the Twist HumanCore Exome Kit. The raw sequencing reads (150-bp paired-end) were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit, following established best practice guidelines. Identified variants were annotated using ANNOVAR to support downstream interpretation. Population allele frequencies were filtered to retain variants with a frequency of less than 0.5% in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0). In silico prediction tools, were applied to evaluate potential pathogenicity. These included Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD v1.7), REVEL, SpliceAI, SIFT, MutationTaster2021, PolyPhen-2, and AlphaMissence.

Validation and screening of NYX mutation

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples of all available family members using the Qiagen DNA Blood Midi Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The exon sequences and exon-intron boundary regions of the NYX gene were retrieved from the Ensembl Genome Browser. Specific primers were designed using Primer-BLAST. The forward primer sequence was TGCGAGGCGGTCTCCATC, and the reverse primer sequence was TCGGGCACGCTGAAGAGG. All coding regions and splice-site junctions of the NYX gene were amplified and sequenced for each participant. Sanger sequencing was performed on all available family members (III-2, III-4, III-5, IV-1, IV-2, IV-3, and IV-4). The resulting sequence data were analyzed using Mutation Surveyor software (SoftGenetics, USA) to identify potential pathogenic variants.

ACMG variant classification

The NYX c.264dup p. (Arg89Alafs*26) variant was classified according to the joint American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) standards and guidelines for sequence variant interpretation [36]. Evidence strength was categorized as Very Strong, Strong, Moderate, or Supporting. A population frequency threshold of < 0.01 was applied. Multiple reference databases were queried for variant annotation. These included gnomAD, ExAC, the 1000 Genomes Project, ClinVar, HGMD, and dbSNP. Co-segregation analysis was performed based on the number of informative meioses observed within the family pedigree.

Protein structure prediction and comparative analysis

The three-dimensional structures of the wild-type NYX protein and its mutant variant were predicted using the AlphaFold3 platform. Amino acid sequences were submitted to the system for structure prediction. AlphaFold3 applies deep learning algorithms to infer protein conformations directly from sequence data. The reliability and accuracy of the predicted models were assessed using multiple quality metrics. These included predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) scores, Predicted Aligned Error, and Predicted Distance Error. For structural analysis, the models were further processed and visualized using PyMOL. This allowed detailed comparison of the tertiary structures of the wild-type and mutant proteins at the atomic level.

Comparative analysis of amino acid variations

The coding sequence of the mutant NYX protein was analyzed using the ExPASy Translate tool (https://web.expasy.org/translate/) to predict the corresponding primary amino acid sequence. The translated mutant sequence was then compared with the wild-type protein sequence. This comparison was performed to identify mutation sites and characterize amino acid substitutions. This approach enabled precise localization of altered residues. It also provided a basis for assessing their potential structural and functional consequences.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The proband IV-4 was a 21-year-old male. He exhibited high myopia, with a spherical equivalent (SE) of -24.25 D in the right eye (OD) and − 23.50 D in the left eye (OS). Myopia onset occurred before the age of four (SE < -6.00 D). He also presented with typical symptoms of night blindness. These included impaired vision under low-light conditions, difficulty navigating in darkness, and inability to ascend or descend stairs without auxiliary lighting. ERG revealed markedly reduced rod responses in both eyes. Compared with established ISCEV reference ranges [35], the b-wave amplitudes were significantly decreased. The maximal combined response showed a characteristic negative waveform. The a-wave was relatively preserved, whereas the b-wave was severely reduced. This resulted in a b/a ratio below 1.0 (normal range: >1.5). Oscillatory potentials were also reduced in both eyes. Under photopic conditions, cone responses were mildly to moderately reduced. The 30-Hz flicker response was preserved but showed delayed implicit times compared with the normal reference range (normal upper limit: 30.0ms) [35] (Fig. 1). This ERG pattern is characteristic of cCSNB. Fundus examination revealed typical features of high myopia. These included tilted optic discs with peripapillary atrophy, a tessellated fundus, prominent choroidal vessels, and an absent foveal reflex. Peripheral retinal lattice degeneration with pigmentary deposition was also observed. In addition, the proband had cutaneous vitiligo, mainly affecting the hands and scalp. His monozygotic twin brother (IV-3) showed even more severe myopia (OD: -25.50 D; OS: -24.50 D). Fundus examination of the right eye revealed lattice degeneration in the peripheral retina. The left eye showed cystic retinal degeneration, vitreous traction, and pigmentary changes. Corneal topography suggested keratoconus in the right eye. He also presented with symptoms of night blindness. Pedigree analysis indicated that their maternal aunt (III-2) reported symptoms of nyctalopia. However, clinical examination revealed normal visual acuity and a non-myopic refractive status. This discrepancy was likely due to a history of corneal refractive surgery. For this study, individuals were classified as “affected” if they were male and presented with both high myopia (spherical equivalent ≤ -6.00 D) and congenital night blindness. Female carriers were classified separately as “asymptomatic or mildly affected carriers” based on mutation status and clinical presentation.

In this study, ophthalmic examination data were collected from eight family members. Based on the observed maternal inheritance pattern, the proband’s father (III-6) was excluded from genetic sequencing. DNA samples were obtained from seven family members for genetic analysis (Fig. 2). These individuals were selected based on their clinical status and suspected inheritance pattern.

Analysis of WES sequencing results

A novel frameshift mutation in the NYX gene was identified in the proband (Fig. 3). Whole-exome sequencing localized the variant to chromosome Xp11.4 (chrX:41,332,970). The mutation was designated as NM_001378477.1:exon3:c.264dup p. (Arg89Alafs*26). It represents a single-nucleotide duplication that results in a frameshift. This variant alters the codon for arginine at position 89, replacing it with alanine. It also introduces a premature termination codon 26 amino acids downstream. These changes are predicted to produce a truncated protein or trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), likely resulting in loss of protein function.

Segregation analysis confirmed that the mutation was maternally inherited. This finding is consistent with an X-linked recessive mode of transmission. Targeted sequencing of additional family members identified individuals III-2 and III-5 as heterozygous carriers of the variant (Fig. 3).

The NYX c.264dup p. (Arg89Alafs*26) variant was classified according to the ACMG/AMP guidelines. The supporting evidence is summarized below. PVS1 (Very Strong): The variant is a frameshift mutation that introduces a premature termination codon. Loss-of-function is a known disease mechanism for NYX. Therefore, the PVS1 criterion is met. PM2_Supporting (Supporting): The variant is absent from population databases including gnomAD, ExAC, the 1000 Genomes Project, ClinVar, and HGMD. It also lacks an rs identifier in dbSNP. According to ClinGen SVI recommendations, PM2 is applied at Supporting strength; PP1 (Supporting): The variant co-segregates with the disease phenotype within the family. Affected individuals (III-2, III-5, IV-3, and IV-4) all carry the variant. Based on ClinGen SVI quantitative guidelines, four informative meioses support PP1 at Supporting strength. Based on the combination of PVS1 (Very Strong) and two Supporting criteria, the variant was classified as Likely Pathogenic according to ACMG combination rules.

Structural comparison between wild-type and mutant NYX proteins

The three-dimensional structures of the wild-type and mutant NYX proteins were predicted using AlphaFold3 (Figs. 4a-b). As shown in Figs. 4c, the mutant protein retains the N-terminal region (residues 1–88). However, it is truncated at residue 115. This truncation results in the loss of 361 amino acids. It also leads to the deletion of all ten leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains. Sequence alignment revealed that the frameshift mutation produces a series of consecutive amino acid substitutions (Figs. 5). It should be noted that these structural predictions are computational in nature. Therefore, experimental validation is required to confirm these findings.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a novel frameshift mutation (c.264dup, p. (Arg89Alafs*26)) in the NYX gene in a Chinese family with high myopia and CSNB. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, this variant was classified as Likely Pathogenic. This finding expands the known mutational spectrum of NYX and may facilitate molecular diagnosis counseling for families with X-linked CSNB and high myopia.

The NYX gene located on Xp11.4, encodes nyctalopin, a 481-amino-acid protein belonging to the small leucine-rich proteoglycan family [23]. Nyctalopin is expressed on dendritic tips of retinal ON bipolar cells and plays an essential role in localizing and stabilizing the TRPM1 channel at the postsynaptic membrane [37]. To date, more than 155 pathogenic variants in the NYX gene have been reported worldwide, including missense, frameshift, nonsense, splice-site, and large deletions [33]. The frameshift mutation identified in this study (c.264dup, p. (Arg89Alafs*26)) introduces a premature stop codon at position 115, resulting in the loss of 361 amino acids, including all ten LRR domains. Frameshift mutations that generate premature stop codons frequently trigger NMD. In such cases, the mutant transcript is degraded before translation. Therefore, the pathogenic effect of this variant is more likely mediated by loss of function through mRNA degradation rather than production of a truncated protein. Accordingly, the structural predictions in this study should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Experimental validation is required to confirm these findings. This mutation has not been previously reported, thus expanding the known mutational spectrum of NYX.

cCSNB is characterized by a selective ON-bipolar cell dysfunction with preserved OFF-bipolar cell function [18]. Under scotopic conditions, due to complete interruption of signal transmission in the rod pathway, the rod response (DA 0.01) in cCSNB patients is extinguished. The maximal combined response (DA 3.0/10.0) exhibits the typical “negative waveform”, with a relatively preserved a-wave and a severely reduced b-wave (b/a ratio < 1.0). Under photopic conditions, the cone response (LA 3.0) in cCSNB shows a characteristic “square-shaped a-wave”, while the 30-Hz flicker response demonstrates preserved amplitude with only mildly delayed implicit times. These ERG abnormalities are typically stationary, consistent with the non-progressive nature of cCSNB [38]. In striking contrast, iCSNB has a distinct pathophysiological basis involving dysfunction of both ON- and OFF-bipolar cell pathways. The scotopic rod response (DA 0.01) is reduced but recordable. Although the maximal combined response also shows a negative waveform, both a-wave and b-wave amplitudes are reduced compared with normal values. Under photopic conditions, both the cone response (LA 3.0) and the 30-Hz flicker response are severely reduced, with the latter often presenting a characteristic bifid peak. The ERG findings in our proband are consistent with the cCSNB electrophysiological signature. The characteristic negative waveform (preserved a-wave with severely reduced b-wave, b/a ratio < 1.0) under scotopic conditions localizes the defect to the retinal ON-bipolar cell pathway, which aligns precisely with the known function of the NYX gene.

High myopia is a frequent finding in patients with cCSNB. As reviewed by Liu et al. [39], patients with NYX-associated cCSNB universally present with myopia, with a mean refractive error of approximately − 7.50 D reported in a large cohort [40]. The proband and his twin brother in our study presented with extreme high myopia (spherical equivalent < -23.00 D), which is more severe than typically reported for NYX-associated cCSNB. The mechanisms linking ON-bipolar cell dysfunction to myopia development are not fully understood. As reviewed by Zeitz et al. [18], cCSNB represents a valuable model to study myopia because the disruption of inner retinal signaling has a significant impact on refractive eye development. Studies in mouse models lacking Nyx (nob mice) have shown increased susceptibility to form-deprivation myopia and reduced retinal dopamine levels, suggesting that ON-bipolar cell dysfunction may contribute to myopia through dysregulation of dopaminergic signaling [41]. These findings support the hypothesis that ON-bipolar cell dysfunction in cCSNB may lead to myopia through disruption of retinal signaling pathways that normally regulate axial elongation [38]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms linking nyctalopin dysfunction to extreme high myopia remain to be elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based on a single four-generation Chinese family. This restricts the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Second, functional validation of the identified mutation was not performed. In vitro electrophysiological studies and animal models were not included in this work. Third, the structural analysis relied solely on computational predictions generated by AlphaFold3. No experimental validation was performed, such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy. Future studies should address limitations. Larger multicenter cohorts are needed to improve generalizability. Comprehensive electrophysiological assessments, and functional experiments should also be conducted to validate and extend these findings.

The novel NYX mutation identified in this study expands the known mutational spectrum associated with CSNB. Although further mechanistic studies are required to clarify the relationship between nyctalopin dysfunction and axial elongation, the present findings suggest a potential link between retinal signaling pathways and refractive development. This association warrants further investigation. These results may have implications for molecular diagnosis and genetic counseling in families affected by X-linked CSNB and high myopia. However, functional validation in larger cohorts and experimental models are necessary before any clinical application can be considered.
