Severity and distribution of coronary artery disease in lebanese patients undergoing coronary angiography: Impact of gender and age on angiographic characteristics, a cross-sectional study
Authors: Soltan Al Chaar, Adham Al Hajj, Pamela Nakad, Antoine Sarraf, Joseph Halby, Batoul Fakhry, Ahmad Zahwi, Georges Feghali, Mirna N. Chahine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular diseases are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. This study is the first in Lebanon to evaluate gender- and age-related differences in the angiographic patterns and clinical characteristics of coronary artery disease (CAD). This descriptive, multicenter, cross-sectional study included Lebanese patients presenting with acute coronary symptoms and angiographically confirmed CAD who underwent coronary angiography between January 2021 and February 2022. Data were obtained through patient interviews and comprehensive review of medical records, including coronary angiography findings, echocardiographic assessment of left ventricular systolic function, and laboratory data. Patients were stratified by gender and by age into younger (≤55 years) and older (>55 years) groups. The study included 212 patients with CAD (51.4% male; mean age 60.6 years). Male patients more frequently had prior angina, myocardial infarction, and coronary revascularization and exhibited more severe coronary lesions, particularly involving the left anterior descending artery. Among patients with multivessel disease (MVD), males had a greater extent of coronary involvement. Younger patients more often reported a family history of CAD, whereas older patients had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, prior ischemic events, coronary interventions, heart failure, and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. Gender and age are associated with distinct angiographic patterns of CAD, as well as differences in related clinical characteristics. Male gender is linked to more extensive coronary disease, while older age is associated with greater comorbidity burden and impaired ventricular function. Awareness of these differences may support improved risk stratification and individualized management of CAD.

1. Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is highly prevalent in both developed and developing countries and remains a major global public health concern. It is estimated that CAD accounts for approximately 2.2% of the overall global burden of disease and nearly one third (32.7%) of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) worldwide.[1] In Lebanon, CAD affects 13.4% of the population aged ≥40 years, with a higher prevalence of premature CAD compared with developed countries, particularly among males.[2]

The human heart receives its blood supply from the coronary arteries, which originate from the aortic root. The left main coronary artery (LMCA) bifurcates into the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and the left circumflex artery (LCX), while the right coronary artery (RCA) gives rise to the posterior descending artery (PDA) in a right-dominant coronary circulation pattern.[3] Impaired myocardial blood supply leads to ischemic heart disease, which remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide.[3] CAD may present as single-vessel disease (SVD), involving 1 coronary artery, or as multivessel disease (MVD), involving 2 or more coronary arteries. The angiographic pattern of CAD, including the extent and distribution of coronary involvement, is assessed using invasive coronary angiography (CAG), whereas left ventricular systolic function is typically evaluated using transthoracic echo-cardiography (TTE).[4]

Multiple pathophysiological mechanisms contribute to the development of CAD, including atherosclerosis, Kawasaki disease,[5–7] spontaneous coronary artery dissection,[8,9] coronary embolization, coronary artery spasm, and hypercoagulable states.[10] Risk factors for CAD are broadly classified as modifiable and non-modifiable. Modifiable risk factors include dyslipidemia, smoking, diabetes mellitus, overweight or obesity, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, and psychosocial stress, whereas non-modifiable risk factors include advanced age, sex, family history, and race.[11] Age-related differences in CAD presentation have been well documented; younger patients tend to have a higher prevalence of normal coronary arteries, mild luminal irregularities, and SVD compared with older patients.[12,13] Similarly, sex-related differences have been observed, with women often presenting at an older age and exhibiting a less extensive angiographic disease burden despite a less favorable cardiovascular risk profile compared with men.[14]

A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that CAD differs significantly between men and women across multiple dimensions, including epidemiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, response to therapy, and outcomes.[15] For instance, among patients undergoing coronary angiography for chest pain evaluation, women have a lower prevalence of obstructive CAD than men. Data from the Coronary Artery Surgery Study trial further showed that SVD was more prevalent and MVD less prevalent in younger women compared with younger men.[16] Moreover, electrocardiographic ischemic changes during rest and exercise testing are more frequently false-positive in women,[17] and echocardiographic studies have shown a poorer ventricular response to exercise-induced stress among women with CAD.[18]

Because gender has a recognized impact on the occurrence and outcomes of CAD, significant efforts have been undertaken by the U.S. federal government and the American Heart Association to raise awareness and reduce gender-related disparities in cardiovascular research and clinical care.[19] These initiatives have emphasized the need for targeted studies aimed at closing knowledge gaps related to CAD in women, with the ultimate goal of reducing mortality and improving clinical outcomes.[19,20]

In Lebanon, however, data on gender- and age-related differences in CAD angiographic patterns remain limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on CAD prevalence rather than detailed angiographic distribution or disease extent.[2,21–28]

Therefore, the present study was designed based on the hypothesis that gender and age are associated with distinct angiographic patterns of CAD among Lebanese patients undergoing coronary angiography. The study had 2 main objectives. First, to characterize how the angiographic pattern of CAD, including disease severity, extent of coronary involvement, and distribution of affected vessels, varies according to gender and age. Second, to examine whether these angiographic differences are associated with variations in traditional cardiovascular risk factors and left ventricular systolic function in this population.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethical consideration

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Thesis Committee of the Faculty of Medical Sciences at the Lebanese University and by the institutional review boards of the participating hospitals (IRB number: IRB-F01) (Supplementary S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R567). Data collection was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice (ICH Section 3) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion, and a structured questionnaire was completed for each patient (Supplementary S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R567). Measures were implemented to ensure the confidentiality and protection of all patient information.

2.2. Study design and population

This was a descriptive, retrospective, multicenter, cross-sectional study conducted among Lebanese patients according to gender and age (≤55 and >55 years). Participants were recruited from the catheterization laboratory databases of several Lebanese hospitals affiliated with the Lebanese University Faculty of Medical Sciences, including Sahel General University Hospital, Nini Hospital, and Nabih Berri Governmental Hospital.

Eligible participants were Lebanese patients admitted for acute coronary syndrome who underwent coronary angiography during the study period. Patients were identified based on coronary angiography results, and missing clinical or demographic data were completed through direct patient contact when necessary. Exclusion criteria included renal failure (estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), active malignancy, congenital heart disease, and use of cocaine or other illicit drugs.

2.3. Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using the G*Power software. Assuming an alpha error of 5%, a statistical power of 95%, and reported prevalence rates of CAD of 8.3%[29] in men and 6.0%[29] in women, a minimum of 88 patients per group was required to detect a difference between proportions. Accordingly, at least 88 male and 88 female patients were required (total N = 176), as well as a minimum of 88 patients aged ≤55 years and 88 patients aged >55 years to allow stratified analyses by gender and age.

2.4. Data collection

Data were collected between January 2021 and February 2022 and included coronary angiography findings, echocardiographic measurements, laboratory results, and detailed medical and social histories. A structured questionnaire (Supplementary S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R567) was used to collect socio-demographic information (gender, age, height, weight), lifestyle factors (smoking status, water-pipe smoking, alcohol consumption), medical history, angiographic characteristics, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and prior CAD events (history of myocardial infarction (MI) and coronary revascularization), as well as cardiac biomarkers. Questionnaires were completed either in person or via telephone interview.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, Chicago), version 22. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between gender and age groups were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, and Student t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

In addition to bivariate analyses, multivariable regression models were constructed to evaluate independent associations between gender, age category, and angiographic distribution across coronary territories (LAD, PDA, LCX, and LMCA), while adjusting for potential confounders including dyslipidemia, obesity, and diabetes mellitus.

Multicollinearity among covariates included in the multivariable models was formally assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis, with VIF values >5 considered indicative of significant collinearity.

A 2-sided P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

4. Discussion

In Lebanon, few studies have investigated the effect of gender and age on the pattern of CAD.[2,21–28] This study aimed to evaluate gender- and age-related differences in the angiographic pattern (profile and distribution) of CAD among Lebanese patients undergoing coronary angiography. From a public health perspective, understanding gender- and age-specific patterns of CAD in Lebanon is particularly important given the country’s epidemiological transition, high prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, and limited implementation of preventive strategies. Such population-specific data may improve locally adapted risk stratification, screening, and therapeutic decision-making, rather than relying solely on extrapolation from Western cohorts.

4.1. Gender-related differences

Concerning lifestyle-related risk factors, our results showed that smoking was significantly more prevalent among male patients, who also reported higher cigarette consumption and earlier initiation compared with females. These findings are consistent with previous studies. According to Jassem and colleagues, sex differences in smoking behavior may reflect a combination of physiological (particularly hormonal), cultural, and behavioral factors.[30] Moreover, Cosgrove et al demonstrated that nicotine exposure more strongly activates reward pathways in men than in women, as evidenced by neuroimaging studies.[31] In addition to biological and neurobehavioral factors, sociocultural norms and gender-specific stigma surrounding smoking in Middle Eastern societies may lead to underreporting and delayed initiation among women, resulting in misclassification of true tobacco exposure. Such reporting bias and differential exposure patterns should be considered when interpreting gender disparities in smoking-related CAD risk and may partially account for the greater cumulative tobacco burden and more advanced atherosclerotic disease observed in male patients.

Similarly, male patients were more likely to consume alcohol than female patients, in line with previous reports showing lower alcohol use and lower rates of alcohol use disorder among women.[32] Cultural, religious, and socioeconomic factors in Lebanon may influence both alcohol exposure and reporting patterns. Given the established association between excessive alcohol intake and hypertension, cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmias, this behavioral difference may further amplify cardiovascular risk and contribute to earlier and more severe CAD presentation among male patients.

Regarding angiographic findings, our results showed that male patients exhibited a greater atherosclerotic burden, with more severe coronary lesions compared with females. Specifically, men had a higher prevalence of significant LAD stenosis and more extensive multivessel disease. Importantly, the association between female gender and lower LAD involvement remained significant after multivariable adjustment for major cardiometabolic risk factors, supporting an independent relationship rather than confounding by comorbidity burden. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting less obstructive coronary disease among women.[33–36] Several mechanisms may explain these gender-related differences. First, higher estrogen levels in premenopausal women exert protective effects against atherosclerosis by enhancing endothelial repair, inhibiting smooth muscle proliferation, and reducing matrix deposition following vascular injury.[37] Estrogen also improves endothelial function, reduces systemic vascular resistance, and may protect against coronary artery spasm.[38] Second, gender-specific vascular biology, including smaller vessel size, lower coronary flow reserve, differences in vascular remodeling, and functional differences in smooth muscle cells, may contribute to less extensive epicardial disease in women.[39,40]

Beyond hormonal effects, emerging evidence suggests gender-based differences in plaque composition and ischemic mechanisms, with women more frequently exhibiting plaque erosion or microvascular dysfunction rather than plaque rupture. This may result in ischemic symptoms despite less obstructive angiographic findings, underscoring the importance of considering alternative mechanisms of ischemia in female patients.[41,42]

Conversely, gender did not significantly affect lesion distribution in coronary territories other than the LAD (LMCA, LCX, RCA, and PDA), nor did it significantly influence LVEF. These findings are consistent with those of Heydari et al, who reported no significant gender differences in LV function or number of affected vessels.[43] However, other studies have reported differing patterns of vessel involvement by gender.[44] Such discrepancies highlight the heterogeneity of CAD phenotypes across populations and emphasize the influence of referral patterns, diagnostic thresholds, and population-specific risk profiles.

4.2. Age-related differences

Our findings demonstrate that age is an important determinant of the clinical profile of patients with CAD. Patients aged ≤55 years more frequently reported a family history of CAD, suggesting a stronger genetic contribution to earlier disease onset. This observation is consistent with the findings of Hunt et al, who reported a higher incidence of CAD at younger ages among individuals with affected first-degree relatives.[35] These data support the role of inherited susceptibility in premature CAD.

In contrast, patients aged >55 years exhibited a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities and accumulated cardiovascular disease burden, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, prior angina, stroke or transient ischemic attack, MI, coronary revascularization (PCI or CABG), and congestive heart failure. These findings are in line with previous studies demonstrating that hypertension and diabetes mellitus are major contributors to CAD in older populations.[45] Large epidemiological studies conducted in the United States have further shown that the risk of coronary mortality increases with age as a function of blood pressure.[46] In the Middle Eastern context, limited available data similarly indicate a positive association between hypertension, acute MI, and CAD among older patients.[47,48]

Aging represents a major non-modifiable risk factor for atherosclerosis and is a strong predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.[49] Age-related vascular changes, including increased arterial stiffness, lead to elevated systolic blood pressure and reduced diastolic pressure, thereby compromising coronary perfusion. In addition, older individuals may be more susceptible to adverse effects of antihypertensive therapy, such as orthostatic hypotension, further complicating disease management.[50] The accumulation of comorbidities observed with advancing age likely reflects both prolonged exposure to cardiovascular risk factors and progressive vascular and myocardial remodeling. These findings emphasize the importance of aggressive primary prevention strategies at younger ages and careful optimization of medical therapy in older patients to balance efficacy and safety.

With respect to angiographic findings, no significant age-related differences were observed in the distribution of coronary lesions, the severity of atherosclerotic disease, or the number of affected coronary vessels. This contrasts with several studies reporting a higher prevalence of SVD and minimal coronary lesions in younger patients, and more extensive multivessel disease among older patients.[51] Other investigations have suggested age-related differences in coronary distribution, with some reporting predominant LAD involvement in younger patients and greater LCX or multivessel involvement in older patients.[50,52,53] The absence of such differences in our study may be partly explained by differences in age stratification, as we compared patients aged ≤55 versus >55 years, whereas many previous studies used lower age thresholds (e.g., 40 years), potentially capturing distinct disease phenotypes.

In contrast, left ventricular systolic function was significantly affected by age. Reduced LVEF and possible heart failure were more prevalent among patients aged >55 years compared with younger patients. Few studies have specifically examined age-related changes in LV function among patients with CAD. A Japanese cohort study demonstrated that LV wall thickness and mass increase with age, while LV chamber volumes decrease in both men and women.[54] Although LVEF showed minimal variation across age groups in the general population, progressive myocardial remodeling and cumulative ischemic injury may contribute to systolic dysfunction in older patients with established CAD. Additional evidence suggests that age-related myocyte loss, particularly among men, may be related to hormonal changes and reduced physical activity.[54]

Taken together, these findings underscore the complex interplay between aging, myocardial remodeling, and ischemic injury in patients with CAD. Longitudinal studies incorporating advanced imaging, biomarkers, and functional assessments are needed to better delineate age-related myocardial changes and their prognostic implications, particularly within the Lebanese population.

4.3. Study limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, cross-sectional design limits causal inference and is inherently susceptible to recall and information bias, particularly with respect to the timing of symptom onset and clinical presentation preceding coronary angiography. Second, selection bias may be present, as the study population was drawn from patients referred for coronary angiography at tertiary care centers, potentially overrepresenting individuals with more advanced or symptomatic CAD. Third, although standardized criteria were applied to define lesion severity across all coronary arteries, the multicenter nature of the study and the involvement of multiple interventional cardiologists may have introduced interobserver variability in angiographic interpretation, resulting in potential measurement error. Finally, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—including duration and intensity of cardiovascular risk factor exposure, medication adherence, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle behaviors—cannot be excluded and may have influenced the observed associations.

4.4. Perspectives and recommendations

Future studies using prospective designs, larger and more diverse cohorts, and longitudinal follow-up are warranted to better define causal relationships and validate the observed gender- and age-related differences in CAD patterns. Standardized angiographic assessment, including centralized or core-laboratory adjudication, may further reduce variability and strengthen the comparability of findings across studies. In addition, improved characterization of gender- and age-specific differences in clinical presentation, angiographic severity, and functional impairment could enhance cardiovascular risk stratification and support more individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Such evidence may ultimately inform future clinical guidelines by promoting a more personalized approach to CAD management that incorporates both gender and age.

5. Conclusion

This study is the first to evaluate gender- and age-related differences in the angiographic patterns and distribution of CAD among Lebanese patients. The findings demonstrate significant associations between gender, age, and several angiographic, functional, and clinical characteristics of CAD. Lifestyle-related risk factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, differed by gender, whereas older age was associated with a higher prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities and impaired left ventricular systolic function. In addition, gender was independently associated with differences in the severity of coronary obstruction and the extent of vessel involvement, particularly among patients with multivessel disease.

Given that CAD remains a leading cause of mortality, recognizing age- and gender-related differences is essential for improving cardiovascular risk stratification, diagnostic evaluation, and individualized management strategies. These findings support the need for greater integration of gender- and age-specific considerations into clinical practice and guideline development. Further prospective studies are warranted to better elucidate the biological and sociocultural factors underlying these differences, particularly within Middle Eastern populations.
