Pediatricians’ psychopharmacological practice: a pilot survey in Poland and Ukraine
Authors: Igor Martsenkovsky, Barbara Remberk, Inna Martsenkovska, Dmytro Martsenkovskyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMental health disorders significantly affect children’s development and contribute to a substantial global burden, increasing demand for mental health care within primary pediatric settings. This study explores psychopharmacological prescribing patterns among pediatricians in Poland and Ukraine in the context of rising pediatric mental health needs following the ongoing war in Ukraine.MethodsData were collected via an online survey conducted between January and May 2023 among non-representative convenience samples of pediatricians in Poland (n = 43) and Ukraine (n = 161). The survey assessed demographic characteristics, self-reported frequency of and attitudes toward psychotropic medication use, and sources of knowledge influencing prescribing practices. Cross-country comparisons were conducted using descriptive and inferential statistical analyses.ResultsNearly half of pediatricians in Ukraine reported prescribing psychotropic medications within the past 12 months, compared with approximately one quarter of pediatricians in Poland. Prescribing patterns differed between countries: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) were most commonly reported in Poland, whereas neurometabolic medications were frequently reported in Ukraine. Across both countries, self-reported awareness of psychotropic medication licensing and adverse effects was limited.ConclusionsThis exploratory study provides initial insights into pediatricians’ self-reported psychopharmacological practices in two distinct healthcare contexts. The findings highlight potential gaps in training, access to evidence-based resources, and system-level support for pediatric mental health care. Results should be interpreted in light of methodological limitations, including convenience sampling, reliance on self-reported data, and a relatively small Polish subsample.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03243-3.

Introduction

Mental health disorders are among the five most disabling conditions in children aged 5–14 years and represent a substantial global burden, with profound negative consequences for children and their families [1]. In conflict-affected settings, even higher rates of adverse mental health outcomes have been documented among children and adolescents, including those exposed to the war in Ukraine [2–4]. More broadly, the prevalence of mental disorders in children and adolescents has increased markedly worldwide over the past decade, including a twofold rise in conditions such as eating disorders, depression, and anxiety [5, 6].

Over recent decades, pediatric mental health care has undergone a substantial shift from inpatient to outpatient settings, accompanied by increasing demand for psychotherapeutic interventions [7]. At the same time, persistent gaps in access to specialized mental health services have intensified the need to integrate psychosocial interventions into primary care [8, 9]. Globally, only approximately 38% of children with mental disorders receive any form of psychological or psychotherapeutic care, with the lowest access observed in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [10]. In parallel, rates of psychotropic medication prescribing among children and adolescents have increased at both primary and secondary levels of care [11–14].

To address gaps in access to mental health care for both children and adults, the World Health Organization, in collaboration with international experts, developed the Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) to support the delivery of evidence-based mental health interventions in primary care settings [8]. Implementation of mhGAP has been initiated in both Ukraine and Poland.

The increase in pediatric psychotropic prescribing has been closely linked to persistent barriers in access to mental health care. These barriers reflect a complex interplay of factors, including rising demand for mental health services, shortages of trained specialists able to deliver psychosocial interventions, stigmatization of non-pharmacological care, pressures for rapid symptom relief, and features of local reimbursement and healthcare financing systems [15].

Although medication is not typically considered a first-line intervention in child and adolescent mental health care, psychopharmacological treatment represents an evidence-based component of management for a range of psychiatric disorders. Nevertheless, despite the availability of clinical guidelines, psychotropic prescribing practices vary substantially across countries, healthcare settings, and social and cultural contexts, as well as between specific diagnostic groups [16, 17].

Poland and Ukraine share geographical proximity and a partially overlapping historical and health system development trajectory, which has influenced the organization of medical training and mental health services in both countries. At the same time, both healthcare systems face shared regional pressures, including rising pediatric mental health needs, insufficient funding, and longstanding workforce shortages. Prior to the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, both countries reported limited capacity within pediatric mental health services. At the time of the study, approximately 564 child psychiatrists were practicing in Poland, corresponding to around eight specialists per 100,000 children, whereas in Ukraine only about 70 child psychiatrists were practicing nationwide in 2022, representing fewer than one specialist per 100,000 children [18–20].

The ongoing war in Ukraine has further intensified these challenges by substantially increasing demand for mental health care while simultaneously disrupting existing service infrastructure and reducing available human and financial resources [4, 21]. Since February 2022, large-scale population displacement, including millions of women and children, has placed additional strain on pediatric and general healthcare systems both within Ukraine and in refugee-hosting countries such as Poland [22–24].

The current study aimed to explore and compare the frequency and patterns of psychopharmacological prescribing among pediatricians in Poland and Ukraine, as well as their self-reported knowledge of medication-related adverse effects and the information sources and clinical guidelines influencing prescribing practices.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Data were collected online between January 24 and May 17, 2023. Participants from Poland (n = 43) and Ukraine (n = 161) were recruited using a snowball convenience sampling approach through advertisements published on the National Pediatric Associations’ webpages. Notably, the selected recruitment strategy may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. In particular, pediatricians with a greater interest in mental health or psychopharmacology may have been more likely to participate. Accordingly, the resulting sample should be interpreted as exploratory rather than population-representative.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained by the last author from the SI Institute of Psychiatry, Forensic Psychiatric Examination and Drug Monitoring of the Ministry of Health of Ukraine (reference number 5, 2022-12-05). All participants were provided with information about the study’s purposes and procedures before starting the survey. Electronic consent was obtained from all the participants before starting the survey by asking if they wanted to participate in the study. Measures

Participants were asked to complete a study-specific questionnaire developed for the aims of the present study. Item content was informed by existing literature on pediatric psychopharmacological prescribing and reviewed by a small expert group consisting of child psychiatrists and pediatricians from both countries to ensure clinical relevance and clarity. The original version of the questionnaire was developed in Ukrainian and translated into Polish by a bilingual clinician. The translated version was subsequently reviewed for conceptual equivalence, and minor wording adjustments were made following pilot testing with a small group of pediatricians to improve comprehensibility.

The questionnaire included items assessing sociodemographic characteristics, frequency of and attitudes toward psychotropic medication use, and the influence of various informational sources on prescribing practices. Attention checks were incorporated to enhance data quality. The English version of the questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Sociodemographic variables

The socio-demographic variables included age (years), gender, and type of clinical practice.

Knowledge and attitudes toward psychopharmacological medication use

To assess the attitudes of pediatricians toward psychopharmacological use, participants were asked whether they had prescribed psychotropic medication in the past 12 months, how frequently they prescribed such medication to children, whether they prescribed multiple psychotropic medications simultaneously, and whether they considered psychotropic medications valuable in routine clinical practice. Participants were additionally asked whether they were aware of psychotropic medication licensing (i.e., minimal age and approved indications for prescribing). Finally, participants rated their level of awareness of psychotropic medication side effects in children.

Sources of knowledge about psychotropic medication prescriptions

Participants were asked to rate on a 10-point Likert-scale (0 – not at all, 10 – very much) the extent to which their choice of psychotropic medication prescribing relies on the FDA or North American guidelines (e.g. CANMAT), European guidelines, Ukrainian State Expert Centre guidelines (only for Ukrainian sample), information obtained through conferences or webinars, advice from colleagues, or personal clinical experience (i.e. ‘How much does your choice of psychotropic medication prescription to children rely on European guidelines?’, ‘How much does your choice of psychotropic medication prescription to children rely on your own experience?’, etc.). Participants were also asked whether they used the Mental Health Gap Action Program (mhGAP) recommendations [25] in their practice.

Frequency of psychotropic medication use

Participants were asked about the frequency with which they prescribed certain psychotropic medication prescriptions in their routine practice during the past 12 months (i.e., ‘How often did you prescribe fluoxetine to children and adolescents with mental or behavioral disorders over the past 12 months?’, ‘How often did you prescribe risperidone to children and adolescents with mental or behavioral disorders over the past 12 months?’, etc.) with response options were (0 – never, 1 – sometimes, 2 - often, 3 - regularly). The list of medications included antipsychotics (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, haloperidol, and lurasidone), antidepressants (fluoxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram), anticonvulsant/mood stabilizers (carbamazepine, valproic acid, and lamotrigine), non-psycho stimulants (atomoxetine and guanfacine), psychostimulants (methylphenidate), benzodiazepines and their derivatives (lorazepam, clonazepam, diazepam, and gidazepam), neurometabolic medications (cortexine, cerebrolysine, cerebrokurine, gamalate, phenibutum, piracetamum, hopantenic acid, and pyritinol), nootropic (memantine), and circadian rhythm regulators (melatonin). Because some medications were not represented in both countries (e.g., neurometabolic medications other than cerebrolysine were not available in Poland, and lurasidone was not available in Ukraine), prescribing frequency was analyzed and presented at the level of medication groups, dichotomized as 0 – never or 1 – at least sometimes.

Analytic plan

Descriptive statistics were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group differences between the Polish and Ukrainian samples were initially examined using Pearson’s χ² tests for categorical variables, Student’s t-tests for continuous variables with approximately normal distributions, and Mann–Whitney U tests for ordinal or non-normally distributed variables.

Given the exploratory and hypothesis-generating nature of this pilot study and the number of comparisons across medication classes, formal correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., false discovery rate adjustment) was considered but not applied. To enhance the interpretability of group comparisons, effect size estimates were calculated. For categorical outcomes, effect sizes are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Given the exploratory nature of the study and the number of comparisons across medication classes, no formal correction for multiple testing was applied. All p-values were therefore presented descriptively and interpreted in conjunction with effect sizes and confidence intervals.

As sensitivity analyses, logistic or linear regression models were conducted for selected primary outcomes to examine whether observed group differences persisted after adjustment for key background characteristics that differed between samples (age, sex, and primary practice setting). Adjusted estimates are presented in the Supplementary Materials (S2-S6).

Results

Frequency and attitudes of psychotropic prescriptions

Less than half of pediatricians (48.4%) in Ukraine, compared with approximately one quarter (27.9%) in Poland, reported prescribing psychopharmacological medication in the past 12 months (χ2 = 5.807, p = 0.016). Ukrainian specialists also reported prescribing psychopharmacological medication more frequently (χ2 = 4.753, p = 0.022). Overall, prescribing rates remained relatively low in both countries. Among pediatricians who reported prescribing psychopharmacological medications in the past 12 months in Ukraine, 5.1% reported prescribing daily, 24.4% weekly, 29.5% monthly, and 41.0% once every six months. While in Poland, 66.7% of respondents reported frequency as “once per 6 months” and 32.3% “monthly”. The rate of psychopharmacological polypharmacy was 11.5% in Ukraine and 16.5% in Poland, respectively, and didn’t differ significantly between the countries (OR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.12–3.53). The provider’s satisfaction rate with psychopharmacological treatment was high in both Poland (91.7%) and Ukraine (74.4%), with no significant difference between countries (OR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.03–2.35).

Among pediatric specialists who self-reported prescribing psychotropic medications within the past 12 months, prescribing patterns differed between Poland and Ukraine (Table 2). Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) were more commonly prescribed in Poland than in Ukraine (83.3% vs. 28.2%; OR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.39). Similarly, psychostimulants were prescribed more frequently in Poland (66.7% vs. 7.7%; OR = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.01–0.18), as were prescriptions of ADHD non-stimulant medications (50.0% vs. 15.4%; OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.66) and antipsychotic medications (75.0% vs. 30.8%; OR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.04–0.60). In contrast, pediatricians in Ukraine reported markedly higher use of neurometabolic medications compared with those in Poland (92.3% vs. 41.7%; OR = 16.8, 95% CI: 4.1–69.4). No clear differences were observed for mood stabilizers, benzodiazepines, memantine, or melatonin. Detailed comparisons of prescribing frequencies by medication class are reported in Table 2.

Licensing awareness and sources of knowledge toward psychotropic medication

Country-level differences were also observed in licensing awareness and sources of knowledge regarding psychotropic medications among pediatric specialists who self-reported prescribing psychotropic medications within the past 12 months (Table 3). Ukrainian pediatricians reported a higher level of awareness of psychotropic medication licensing compared with their Polish counterparts (92.3% vs. 58.3%, OR = 8.6, 95% CI: 2.1–34.7). In contrast, self-reported knowledge of psychotropic medication side effects was low in both countries, with only 11.2% of respondents in Ukraine and 8.3% in Poland reporting an adequate or higher level of awareness, and no statistically significant difference between countries (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 0.16–12.9).

Use of the World Health Organization’s Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) among all surveyed pediatricians was reported more frequently in Ukraine than in Poland (34.8% vs. 2.3%; OR = 22.4, 95% CI: 3.1–162.0). With respect to informational influences on prescribing practice, pediatricians who self-reported psychotropic medication prescribing within the past 12 months in both countries reported a moderate influence of FDA recommendations, North American or European clinical guidelines, and advice from colleagues, with no significant differences between countries (p > 0.05).

In contrast, Ukrainian pediatricians reported a significantly greater influence of academic congresses on their clinical practice (median = 9.0, range 1–10) compared with Polish pediatricians (median = 5.0, range 1–9; z = 3.037, p = 0.002). Similarly, the perceived impact of personal clinical experience was higher among Ukrainian respondents (median = 9.0, range 1–10) than among Polish respondents (median = 5.0, range 1–8; z = 2.920, p = 0.003). Finally, Ukrainian pediatricians reported a substantial influence of recommendations issued by the Ukrainian State Expert Center on their prescribing practices (median = 8.0, range 1–10).

Notably, adjusted regression analyses controlling for age, sex, and practice setting yielded patterns consistent with the unadjusted findings (Tables S2–S8).

Discussion

As an exploratory, hypothesis-generating survey based on self-reported data, this study provides an initial overview of pediatric psychopharmacological prescribing practices in two distinct healthcare contexts experiencing rapidly increasing demand for child mental health services following the ongoing conflict in Ukraine. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate self-reported psychotropic prescribing practices among pediatricians in countries following the implementation of mhGAP.

In our study, we found that a quarter of pediatricians in Poland and almost half in Ukraine prescribed psychotropic medication during the past 12 months. Both countries reported higher rates of psychopharmacological prescribing done by pediatricians compared with 10.9% reported by McNicholas and colleagues in Ireland [26]. Speculatively, this can be a result of the rapid growth of demands in pediatric mental health care, which in Ukraine were related to massive war-related stress with simultaneous destruction of medical facilities and loss of human resources [21, 27]. At the same time, in Poland, the rapid growth of traumatized refugees from Ukraine [28, 29] may have increased service demand, potentially contributing to greater reliance on pharmacological interventions in the context of system strain, language barriers, and mental health stigma.

Interestingly, despite the growing global trend of primary care physicians’ roles in mental health care [30], the frequency of psychotropic prescriptions among pediatricians in both countries remained relatively low, suggesting the limited engagement of pediatricians with child mental health management. This may reflect a lack of confidence in mental health due to the absence or very limited training of pediatricians in mental health. It is not uncommon for pediatricians in various countries receive very limited or no training in child mental health [31, 32]. For instance, child psychiatry education in Ukraine is limited to a non-obligatory three-month-long subspecialty training after completion of residency in general psychiatry or pediatrics [33] and is not presented in the standard curriculum of pediatric training. Similarly, general pediatric training in Poland provides little or no training in child psychiatry. These statements can be partially supported by the low level of self-reported awareness of licensing and side effects of psychotropic medications found in the current study. At the same time, specialists in both countries reported satisfaction with their experience in using psychotropic medication in children.

We also compared the patterns of psychopharmacological prescriptions between the countries and found that they differed significantly. In Poland, SSRIs were the most prescribed medication, which is in a row with current existing studies of psychotropic pediatric prescription in primary care [11]. In contrast, despite the recent reports of an increase in anxiety and mood symptoms, the most frequently prescribed in Ukraine were neurometabolic medications, which were reported by 90% of pediatricians. While neurometabolic medication was not among the most prescribed in Poland, it was still used by nearly half of prescribers. Notably, these observations reflect self-reported practices and should not be interpreted as verified prescribing rates or as evidence of causal relationships.

With respect to neurometabolic medications, high-quality randomized controlled trials evaluating their efficacy for pediatric psychiatric indications remain limited, and major European and North American clinical guidelines [34, 35] do not consistently position these agents as first-line treatment options. Many such substances are marketed as dietary supplements or so-called “smart drugs or cognitive enhancers” rather than regulated psychotropic medications. Nevertheless, neurometabolic agents are commonly prescribed in pediatric practice in Ukraine, particularly for children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and neurodevelopmental disorders, which may partly contribute to the observed cross-country differences in the use of psychostimulants, non-psychostimulants, and antipsychotic medications. At the same time, published reports have described adverse events associated with certain neurometabolic agents, including cases of poisoning and potential misuse or dependence, underscoring the need for careful clinical consideration [36–38].

These differences in psychopharmacological prescribing patterns between Poland and Ukraine should also be considered within broader institutional and systemic contexts, in addition to individual clinician factors. These include national drug approval, distribution, and reimbursement policies. In Ukraine, access to certain psychostimulant medications, such as methylphenidate, was limited or absent from pharmaceutical networks for many years, which likely shaped established prescribing traditions with a stronger reliance on medications historically embedded in clinical practice. Moreover, the ongoing war has further disrupted healthcare infrastructure and medication supply chains, additionally constraining availability in both clinical settings and the pharmaceutical market [20, 21]. In contrast, prescribing patterns in Poland may more closely reflect medications that are formally approved, reimbursed, and routinely supported within the European Union regulatory framework. Accordingly, observed differences should be interpreted as reflecting complex interactions between clinician decision-making and structural healthcare constraints rather than physician attitudes alone.

Finally, the observed differences in reliance on academic conferences and personal clinical experience should be interpreted within the context of differing information environments rather than as reflecting divergent professional attitudes. Pediatricians in the Ukrainian sample were, on average, older than their Polish counterparts, which may influence preferred modes of knowledge acquisition, including greater reliance on experiential learning and in-person professional meetings. Structural factors may further shape access to evidence-based resources, including variable institutional access to subscription-based journals, differences in the availability and dissemination of national clinical guidelines, and unequal ease of access to English-language scientific literature. In war-affected settings, professional congresses and centrally organized educational events may also serve as key channels for rapidly disseminating clinical guidance, particularly when routine academic infrastructure is disrupted. Accordingly, differences in reported knowledge sources and knowledge acquisition are more likely to reflect a complex interplay of contextual constraints, regulatory histories, and access pathways rather than intrinsic differences in evidence orientation.

The findings also point to specific areas where targeted capacity building may be most beneficial. At the training level, the integration of structured child and adolescent psychiatry modules into undergraduate and postgraduate pediatric education could strengthen pediatricians’ confidence in recognizing, triaging, and managing common mental health conditions. At the clinical practice level, clearer dissemination of evidence-based psychopharmacological prescribing guidelines tailored to non-specialist settings may help standardize care. At the system level, consultation–liaison models that provide timely access to child psychiatry expertise, such as telephone, or online-based child psychiatry access programs, may support pediatricians managing complex cases, particularly in settings with limited specialist availability. Such approaches may be especially relevant in resource-constrained and war-affected contexts, where rapid decision-making and collaborative care models are critical.

This study should be considered through a number of limitations. Firstly, participation in the survey was voluntary and based on convenience sampling, which may limit representativeness and generalizability, particularly given the relatively modest sample size in Poland.

Secondly, the interpretation of cross-country differences should take into account underlying differences in respondent characteristics. Pediatricians in the Ukrainian sample were, on average, older and more frequently employed in inpatient settings compared to their Polish counterparts. These background differences may partially influence prescribing patterns, access to medications, the clinical stage and severity of treated conditions, and reliance on specific information sources. Although minimal adjusted analyses were conducted, residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Accordingly, simple country-by-country comparisons should be interpreted cautiously and understood within the context of differing professional and institutional profiles.

Thirdly, reliance on retrospective self-reported data introduces the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias. Pediatricians may have under- or overestimated the frequency of psychotropic prescribing, their knowledge of medication licensing or adverse effects, or their use of clinical guidelines, particularly in areas perceived as professionally sensitive. Social desirability may have led some respondents to report practices that align more closely with perceived professional norms rather than actual behavior. As a result, the reported prescribing patterns and levels of knowledge should be interpreted as approximations rather than precise reflections of real-world clinical practice.

Accordingly, this study should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and as an initial step toward understanding pediatric psychopharmacological practices in resource-constrained and war-affected settings. Future studies using larger, more representative samples and objective prescribing data are needed to further validate and extend these observations.

Conclusion

Despite its limitations, this study provides an initial, exploratory overview of pediatric specialists’ self-reported involvement in and perceptions of psychopharmacological management of mental health conditions in children and adolescents across two different healthcare contexts. The findings suggest that pediatricians in both countries report relatively low levels of direct involvement in psychopharmacological decision-making and variable confidence in this area, highlighting potential gaps in training, support structures, or role delineation within child mental health care systems. Cross-country differences in prescribing patterns, including the frequent use of neurometabolic agents in Ukraine, should be interpreted in light of broader regulatory, educational, and healthcare system factors rather than as indicators of individual clinical practice quality.

Taken together, these findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and as an initial step toward understanding how pediatric mental health care is delivered in resource-constrained and war-affected settings. They also highlight the potential value of strengthening collaboration between child psychiatrists and pediatricians, for example through joint educational activities, participation of child mental health specialists in general pediatric congresses, or the development of telephone- or online-based consultation support models for primary care providers, such as child psychiatry access models similar to the Massachusetts Child Psychiatry Access Project (MCPAP) [39].
