Psychometric validation of the Turkish version of the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) in emergency department settings
Authors: Azize Aydemi̇r, İskender Aksoy, Ezgi Şahi̇n
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntimate partner violence is a major public health problem affecting women worldwide, and reliable screening tools are essential for early identification in healthcare settings. This study aimed to adapt the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST) into Turkish and evaluate its psychometric properties in terms of validity and reliability.MethodsThis methodological and cross-sectional study was conducted with 191 women presenting to an emergency department. Data were collected using a Participant Information Form and the WAST. The adaptation process included language and content validity assessments followed by evaluations of construct validity and reliability using exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses.ResultsThe participants’ mean age was 28 ± 5 years; 69.6% were married, and 38.2% reported previous exposure to violence. The content validity ratio indicated a high level of expert consensus (CVR = 1.00). Exploratory factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure consisting of seven items explaining 68.67% of the total variance. Internal consistency analysis showed good reliability for the overall scale and both subdimensions (Cronbach’s α = 0.80). Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated that the model had an acceptable fit with the data.ConclusionThe Turkish version of the WAST demonstrated satisfactory validity and reliability. The scale may serve as a useful screening instrument for identifying intimate partner violence among women in emergency healthcare settings and may contribute to improved assessment, early identification, and intervention in women’s health services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04663-0.

Introduction

Violence against women remains a major global public health and human rights concern. Among its various forms, intimate partner violence (IPV) is one of the most common and pervasive forms affecting women worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 30% of women worldwide experience physical and/or sexual violence during their lifetime, most commonly perpetrated by an intimate partner. Furthermore, nearly 27% of women aged 15–49 years who have ever been in a relationship report experiencing physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner at least once in their lifetime [1].

Emergency departments (EDs) represent one of the most important points of contact for women exposed to violence. Women experiencing IPV frequently seek healthcare services for injuries or other health complaints without directly disclosing the underlying cause of the problem. For this reason, healthcare professionals working in emergency settings play a crucial role in identifying victims of violence and initiating appropriate support mechanisms. However, the identification of IPV in clinical practice remains challenging because many women hesitate to disclose abuse due to fear, shame, social stigma, or concerns about further violence [2, 3].

In Türkiye, violence against women remains an important public health concern. National reports and population-based studies indicate that a considerable proportion of women experience emotional, physical, or sexual violence from intimate partners during their lifetime [4]. Despite increasing awareness and policy initiatives aimed at preventing violence against women, the early detection of IPV in healthcare settings remains limited [4]. One of the major reasons for this limitation is the lack of standardized and culturally adapted screening tools that can be easily applied in clinical environments such as ED.

Various screening instruments have been developed internationally to facilitate the identification of women exposed to IPV in healthcare settings. One of the most widely used tools is the Woman Abuse Screening Tool (WAST), developed by Brown et al. in 1996 to identify women who may be experiencing emotional or physical abuse in intimate relationships [5]. The WAST is a brief and practical instrument that has been widely used in clinical and research settings due to its ease of administration and strong psychometric properties. However, for a screening instrument to be effectively used in different cultural contexts, it must undergo a rigorous process of linguistic and cultural adaptation as well as psychometric validation.

Although several studies have examined violence against women in Türkiye, the number of validated screening instruments specifically adapted for Turkish healthcare settings remains limited [6]. The availability of culturally appropriate and psychometrically validated tools is essential for improving the early identification of IPV and supporting healthcare professionals in recognizing women at risk.

Therefore, adapting reliable screening instruments into Turkish is an important step toward strengthening violence detection and prevention efforts in clinical practice. In this context, the present study aimed to adapt the WAST into Turkish and to evaluate its psychometric properties in terms of validity and reliability among women presenting to emergency departments. By providing a culturally adapted and validated screening instrument, this study is expected to contribute to the early identification of intimate partner violence and to support healthcare professionals in detecting and addressing violence against women in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a methodological and cross-sectional study aimed at evaluating the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the WAST.

Participants

The study was conducted in the ED of Giresun Education and Research Hospital between March 1 and July 31, 2023. This study was approved by the Giresun Education and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 13, Date: January 16, 2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

The study population consisted of women who presented to the ED during the study period. The sample included women aged 18 years and older, who had no communication difficulties, were either married or currently in an intimate relationship, and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. In addition, the partner or spouse of the participant was not present during the ED visit to ensure a safe environment for participation.

Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling method. All eligible women presenting to the emergency department during the study period were invited to participate until the target sample size was reached.

In scale adaptation studies, it is generally recommended that the sample size should be at least 5–10 times the number of items in the scale [7–9]. Since the original WAST consists of eight items, a minimum sample of 80 participants was considered adequate. However, to increase the reliability and robustness of the findings, a total of 191 women were included in the study.

Instruments

Participant information form

A Participant Information Form developed by the researchers was used to collect socio-demographic characteristics and information regarding exposure to violence. Considering the time constraints in the ED setting, the form consisted of five questions addressing basic demographic characteristics and experiences related to violence.

Woman abuse screening tool

WAST was developed by Brown et al. in 1996 to identify and assess women who may have experienced emotional or physical abuse by their intimate partners [5]. The screening tool consists of eight items. The first two items assess the level of tension in the relationship and the difficulty experienced in resolving conflicts between partners. These two items are scored using a cut-off scoring method in which the most extreme responses (“a lot of tension” and “great difficulty”) are assigned one point, while other responses receive zero points. The remaining six items evaluate different dimensions of abuse by asking participants to rate the frequency of various experiences using a three-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (often) to 3 (never). For the total score calculation, the scores of the last six items are reversed and summed together with the scores obtained from the first two items. Higher total scores indicate a greater likelihood of abuse. In the original study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the instrument was reported as 0.95 [5]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the Turkish version of the WAST was found to be 0.80, indicating good internal consistency.

Procedure

Translation and cultural adaptation process

Permission to adapt the WAST into Turkish was obtained from the original developer, Judith Belle Brown, via email prior to the study. The translation and cultural adaptation process followed internationally recommended procedures for cross-cultural adaptation of measurement instruments. First, the original English version of the screening tool was translated independently into Turkish by four faculty members from the Department of English Language and Literature who were proficient in both languages.

Subsequently, the translated Turkish versions were back-translated into English by two independent bilingual translators and two professional translation companies who had not seen the original scale. The back-translated versions were compared with the original English version to identify potential inconsistencies and ensure conceptual equivalence.

After this stage, two faculty members from the Department of Turkish Language and Literature reviewed the Turkish version in terms of linguistic clarity, grammatical accuracy, and comprehensibility. To evaluate content validity and cultural appropriateness, the draft Turkish version was further examined by 25 faculty members from the Faculty of Health Sciences and Faculty of Medicine. These experts evaluated each item in terms of clarity, relevance, and cultural suitability. Based on their recommendations, minor revisions were made to improve the clarity of certain expressions. For example, the term “partner” in the original scale was modified as “your spouse or partner” to better reflect common usage in the Turkish context.

In addition, expert opinions were obtained from 10 physicians working in the Emergency Medicine Department to assess the applicability of the instrument in emergency department settings. To evaluate the linguistic equivalence of the translated version, the original English form of the WAST was administered to 50 fourth-year students from the Department of English Language and Literature, who were fluent in both English and Turkish. After a one-month interval, the Turkish version of the instrument was administered to the same group. The one-month interval was chosen to minimize recall bias while maintaining response stability. After completing these procedures, the final Turkish version of the WAST was administered face-to-face to 191 women presenting to the ED.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23 and IBM AMOS software. Descriptive statistics including frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, and minimum–maximum values were used to describe the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the English and Turkish forms during the linguistic equivalence testing. The psychometric properties of the Turkish version of the WAST were evaluated through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Principal Component Analysis was used for factor extraction in the exploratory factor analysis, and Varimax rotation was applied to determine the factor structure. Confirmatory factor analysis was performed to test the model fit of the factor structure obtained in the exploratory analysis. Model fit was evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit indices. Additionally, Bootstrap Maximum Likelihood analysis with 5000 resampling was conducted to assess the adequacy of the model and sample size. The level of statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05. The recruitment and inclusion process of the participants is presented in Fig. 1.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

The mean age of the participants was 28 ± 5 years. Among the participants, 69.6% were married, 36.1% were aged between 24 and 29 years, 44% were university graduates, and 42.4% were housewives. In addition, 38.2% reported a previous history of exposure to violence.

Content validity

Table 2 presents the content validity results of the Turkish version of the WAST based on expert evaluations. Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) and the Content Validity Index (CVI) based on expert evaluations. According to the relevant criteria, the minimum acceptable CVR value for studies involving at least 10 experts is 0.62 [10]. In the present study, the CVR values for all items were calculated as 1.000, indicating complete agreement among the experts regarding the relevance and clarity of the items. Therefore, no item was removed from the instrument. The overall CVI was also calculated as 1.000, demonstrating excellent content validity for the Turkish version of the instrument (Table 2).

Construct validity

Construct validity of the Turkish version of the WAST was evaluated using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA).

Exploratory factor analysis

Table 3 presents the exploratory factor analysis results of the Turkish version of the WAST. Prior to conducting the exploratory factor analysis, the suitability of the dataset was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. The KMO value was 0.823, indicating good sampling adequacy for factor analysis, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was statistically significant (χ²=522.950, p < 0.001), indicating that the correlation matrix was appropriate for factor analysis. In the EFA, the principal component analysis method was used for factor extraction, and varimax rotation was applied. During the analysis, Item 5 was removed from the instrument because it loaded on two factors with extraction values below 0.30. After removing Item 5, the factor analysis was repeated with the remaining seven items. The extraction values of all items were above 0.30, and the diagonal values of the anti-image correlation matrix were above 0.50, indicating that all remaining items contributed adequately to the factor structure. The analysis revealed a two-factor structure. Factor 1 explained 36.83% of the total variance and consisted of items related to relationship quality, conflict resolution, and emotional abuse; therefore, it was interpreted as “Relationship Quality and Emotional Abuse.” Factor 2 explained 31.84% of the total variance and comprised items reflecting physical and sexual violence; therefore, it was interpreted as “Physical and Sexual Violence.” Together, these two factors accounted for 68.67% of the total variance, indicating that the factor structure explained a substantial proportion of the variance in the data (Table 3).

Confirmatory factor analysis

Table 4 presents the confirmatory factor analysis results and model fit indices for the Turkish version of the WAST. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to test whether the factor structure identified in the exploratory factor analysis was supported in the Turkish sample. Prior to performing CFA, the dataset was examined for outliers, missing values, and normality assumptions. The multivariate normality test indicated that the assumption of normality was not met (critical ratio = 41.801). Therefore, Bootstrap Maximum Likelihood estimation with 5000 resamples was applied in the CFA. The results demonstrated that the two-factor model showed an acceptable level of model fit. The fit indices were as follows: χ²/df = 1.764, CFI = 0.981, GFI = 0.966, RMSEA = 0.063, and SRMR = 0.046. These values indicate a good fit between the proposed model and the observed data. In addition, all standardized path coefficients were statistically significant (p < 0.001), providing further support for the validity of the two-factor structure.

Reliability analysis

Table 5 presents the item–total correlation values and reliability coefficients of the Turkish version of the WAST. The item–total correlation coefficients for all items were found to be positive and above 0.30, indicating that all items contributed adequately to the overall scale and no item needed to be removed. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.809. The Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.807 for Factor 1 and 0.809 for Factor 2, indicating good internal consistency and reliability of the scale.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the Turkish version of the WAST possesses satisfactory psychometric properties and can be considered a valid and reliable instrument for screening intimate partner violence among women in emergency healthcare settings.

In accordance with the literature, the KMO measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were conducted to assess the construct validity of the WAST [11, 12]. The construct validity findings support the adequacy of the Turkish version of the WAST and indicate that the scale has a stable factor structure suitable for use in the target population. The factor analytic results obtained in this study are consistent with commonly accepted psychometric standards and support the structural validity of the instrument. Overall, the findings suggest that the dataset was appropriate for factor analysis and that the Turkish version of the WAST demonstrates satisfactory construct validity [13].

The exploratory factor analysis supported a two-factor structure for the Turkish version of the WAST, providing evidence for the construct validity of the instrument. Although the original version of the scale was reported as having a unifactorial structure explaining 85% of the total variance [5], the Turkish adaptation revealed a two-factor structure. Similar differences in factor structure have been reported in cross-cultural adaptation studies and may reflect linguistic, cultural, and contextual differences between populations [15, 16]. Despite this structural variation, the proportion of variance explained by the Turkish version was satisfactory and supports the interpretability of the scale within the Turkish context. These findings suggest that the Turkish version of the WAST retains its ability to assess intimate partner violence while reflecting culturally relevant dimensions of the construct. The conceptual distinction between the two factors may reflect the multidimensional nature of intimate partner violence. While the first factor appears to capture relationship quality, conflict dynamics, and emotional abuse, the second factor reflects more overt forms of physical and sexual violence. This finding suggests that the Turkish version of the WAST may be sensitive to both relational difficulties and more explicit manifestations of violence within intimate relationships, thereby enhancing its usefulness as a screening instrument in emergency healthcare settings.

The confirmatory factor analysis provided additional evidence supporting the structural validity of the Turkish version of the WAST. The model fit findings indicated that the proposed two-factor structure was compatible with the observed data and demonstrated satisfactory psychometric performance. These results support the stability of the factor structure identified in the exploratory analysis and suggest that the Turkish version of the WAST is a valid instrument for assessing intimate partner violence in the target population.

The reliability findings of the present study support the internal consistency of the Turkish version of the WAST. The reliability coefficient obtained in this study was comparable to those reported for the original scale and for adaptations conducted in different cultural contexts [5, 14–16]. These findings suggest that the Turkish version of the WAST maintains a satisfactory level of reliability and can be considered a dependable instrument for screening intimate partner violence among women in emergency healthcare settings.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample consisted of women who presented to a single emergency department, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings or populations. Second, the data were collected using self-report questionnaires, which may be subject to response bias or recall bias. Finally, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, test–retest reliability and predictive validity of the scale could not be evaluated over time.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the Turkish version of the WAST is a valid and reliable instrument for screening intimate partner violence among women in emergency healthcare settings. The psychometric evaluation demonstrated satisfactory validity and reliability, supporting its use in clinical practice. The availability of a culturally adapted screening instrument may contribute to the early identification of women at risk and support healthcare professionals in implementing timely assessment and intervention strategies.
