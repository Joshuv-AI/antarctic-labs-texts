Convertibility of Cultural Capital: A Longitudinal Study of University Students From 2017 to 2024
Authors: Ondřej Špaček
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA defining feature of cultural capital is its propensity for accumulation and the potential of its convertibility. However, there are a lack of studies that would explore how different forms of cultural capital could be employed as an advantage. This paper addresses this gap by exploring the effect of cultural capital on achieving educational outcomes while utilising a detailed array of questions focused on cultural practices and taste. The study is based on two waves of a longitudinal survey of the 2016–18 cohort of students at Charles University (N = 5127/2020). The analysis employs a categorical principal components analysis to measure established and emerging forms of embodied cultural capital. The results show that while institutionalised cultural capital has a significant effect on the likelihood of completing a university education, embodied cultural capital has no such effect, regardless of whether we focus on established or emerging forms. However, the convertibility of embodied cultural capital is substantial when the analysis focuses on study‐abroad experiences during tertiary education. These results can be explained from the perspective of differentiated parallel areas of social space—one that relies on locally powerful state institutions, such as a national education system, and another that is part of the global space of cultural hierarchies centered in core European economic and cultural centers.

Introduction

There is established evidence of differences in cultural practices and tastes between various locations and classes in society (Bennett et al. 2009; Flemmen et al. 2019; Prieur et al. 2008). Inspired by the account of Bourdieu (1984), they demonstrate a persistence of cultural stratification that aligns legitimate culture with middle‐ and upper‐class taste. While cultural practices associated with legitimate culture may change over time, the key assumption is the existence of an enduring distinction, whatever form it takes in different historical and societal conditions. Studies have uncovered transformations in cultural hierarchies, paying attention to aspects such as comedy (Friedman and Kuipers 2013), the value of irony (Peters et al. 2018) and authenticity (le Grand, 2020; Michael 2015). The valuation and evaluation processes of these cultural practices are well documented. However, there are a lack of studies that explore how these new facets of cultural capital could be employed as an advantage. A defining feature of cultural capital is its propensity for accumulation and the potential of its convertibility. Cultural capital should allow its bearers to secure more valued and prestigious positions in society (Jarness 2015; Savage et al. 2005; Warde 2017). This paper addresses this gap by exploring the effect of specific forms of cultural capital in helping students pursue a university degree. The unique contribution of this paper is that it utilises longitudinal data capturing a wide range of cultural practices and tastes as a measure of cultural capital.

There is a rich and long tradition that integrates the concept of cultural capital into empirical models of social reproduction and educational achievement (for major overviews, see Lareau and Weininger 2003; Davies and Rizk 2018). These studies often rely on large longitudinal datasets of school cohorts and a powerful research design allowing researchers to tackle the nature of casual relationships (e.g., Jæger 2011). Nonetheless, their relevance for the study of cultural stratification is limited. Their conceptual assumptions accentuate the operationalisation of cultural capital as highbrow culture participation and/or scholastic competencies, like reading and familiarity with the school environment and academic culture (Dumais and Ward 2010; Jæger 2011; Lareau and Weininger 2003). While these studies often demonstrate the significance of cultural capital, their narrow and school‐orientated understanding of this concept makes them substantively divergent from current research on cultural hierarchies and legitimate culture. My analysis specifically aims to extend this context and use a more nuanced operationalisation of cultural capital.

In this paper, I examine the extent to which forms of embodied cultural capital – such as distinctive cultural practices and taste ‐ can be converted into other forms of capital, such as educational qualifications. Employed data combine the longitudinal tracking of specific participants over time with a detailed array of questions focused on cultural practices and taste. It is based on a longitudinal study of a cohort of Charles University students who started their tertiary education in the years 2016 and 2017. This cohort was initially surveyed at the start of their study period in 2017, with a follow‐up survey taking place seven years later in 2024. Both surveys covered a questions concerning leisure, participation in cultural practices and taste as well as enquiries into family background, education or work. This research design allows us to trace the conversion of young people's cultural capital at critical moments.

There are many ways in which cultural capital can be valued and converted into other forms of capital. Recent research demonstrated this conversion in elite occupations (Friedman and Laurison 2019), among cultural intermediaries (Michael 2015), and in the field of craft production (Scott 2017), to name a few. This analysis focuses on the education system, which continues to play a prominent role in social reproduction in contemporary societies and remains one of the key arenas in which cultural capital is expected to be valued. Specifically, it examines the extent to which students' legitimate cultural practices and tastes at the beginning of their university education predict education outcomes 7 years later. The analysis follows students' trajectories, considering their achieved university degrees and participation in study‐abroad programmes.

The analysis is further corroborated by considering the field of study. There should be a substantial difference both in the entry requirements and effects of cultural capital in fields of study that are more related to a cultural field, that is, in the humanities and social sciences, as opposed to science, technology or medicine. Therefore, I will also ask whether there is a different process of capital conversion in different fields of study.

This paper is organised as follows: the introduction establishes the theoretical framework, focusing on cultural capital's role in the study of social inequality and the discussion of its different forms. Next, the paper examines the key role of cultural capital convertibility and its close connection to the educational system. This is followed by an outline of the longitudinal dataset and the employed analytical approach. The findings are then presented, assessing the predictive value of cultural capital on educational outcomes. Finally, the discussion interprets these findings and suggests avenues for future research on cultural capital in the context of different countries.

Changes of Cultural Capital

The concept of cultural capital focuses sociological interest on the ways in which culture and embodied cultural practices and tastes could be important sources of power and social reproduction in current societies (Bourdieu 1984). In this notion, cultural consumption and taste are far from being unimportant aspects of leisure but are similar sources of inequality, as are money and economic wealth. However, in the face of many cultural trends, there has been ongoing discussion since the 1990s about the persistent relevance of this type of capital. Cultural omnivorousness had a prominent place in this discussion as the ‘most well‐documented empirical generalisation in the sociology of cultural taste’ (Fishman and Lizardo 2013, 214). It states that higher social positions are no longer characterised by narrow elite cultural practices but rather by consumption incorporating a wide range of tastes. This omnivorousness thesis has been the subject of many methodological and conceptual discussions (Hazir and Warde 2016; Lizardo and Skiles 2016; Brisson 2019); however, one of the main takeaways was that cultural capital was never intended to be identified with so‐called highbrow cultural practices like museum‐going or a taste for classical music. The principle of cultural distinction itself presupposes that cultural capital has an ever‐changing nature and that what is a highly valued cultural practice is historically and geographically contingent (see e.g., Flemmen et al. 2018). When a particularly valued practice starts to be more popular and widespread, it leads to its devaluation and the redefinition of its position in cultural space (Prieur et al. 2023).

Savage and his colleagues (Prieur and Savage 2013; Savage et al. 2015) used the term emerging cultural capital to accentuate their claim that observed cultural trends do not signal that cultural capital has lost its role in the structuring process of current societies. Instead, these trends reflect the never‐ending dynamics of the changing content of cultural capital, the shifts and repositions of which cultural practices are deemed legitimate and valued. Savage et al. (2015) connected this new form of emerging cultural capital specifically to the young middle class, showing their enjoyment of contemporary popular music, computer games, engagement with social media and sport. This is associated with a general openness to new cultural experiences (Michael 2017), a knowing mode of cultural appropriation (Prieur and Savage 2015), but also playful orientation and humor (Friedman and Reeves 2020; Pedersen et al. 2018). While emerging cultural capital downplays privilege and disdain for snobbery, it could also be interpreted as an inward reorientation of cultural distinction, which makes it more invisible, internal the markers of hierarchy recognised only by the informed (Prieur et al. 2023). It is also associated with cosmopolitan tastes (Prieur and Savage 2015), an argument which I will discuss below. Flemmen et al. (2018) noticed that established and emerging forms of cultural capital are not exclusive, both are present among those rich in cultural capital. Pedersen et al. dismissed the notion of emerging cultural capital altogether, arguing that among Norwegian elite youth they found “‘old school’ cultural capital … skilfully brought into play by new generations of elites, though in a different context” (Pedersen et al. 2018, 63).

In this paper, I therefore distinguish between established and emerging forms of cultural capital based on their embeddedness in institutions. Cultural practices and tastes associated with both forms are imbued with legitimacy. However, established cultural capital bases its legitimacy on its role in school curricula and canons and its presence in nation‐building institutions like museums and theaters. It is ‘established’ both by its temporal dimension, being there ‘for an age’, but also by its spatial and symbolic centrality in national cultures. The emerging form lacks this grounding. While movies and quality TV series appraised by cultural intermediaries could be markers of cultural savviness and knowledge of design trends for new hipster urban venues could be quickly capitalised (Scott 2017), all of these lack the above‐mentioned support in established institutions. This means that the distinction between established and emerging forms of cultural capital cannot be defined universally outside the context of the cultural and educational institutions of a given time in a given society (see e.g., Fishman and Lizardo 2013).

New constellations of cultural capital are often associated with cosmopolitanism. Cosmopolitanism is often assumed to be one of the defining features of emerging cultural capital (Prieur and Savage 2013; Roose 2015). A cosmopolitan orientation is also delineated as being the set of resources and skills necessary to succeed in a global environment and labeled as cosmopolitan capital (Igarashi and Saito 2014; Weenink 2007, 2008). This phenomenon is not novel, as cosmopolitan attitudes and transnational connections were always an integral part of the lifestyle of cultural elites. However, current trends show an intriguing presence of the valuation of cosmopolitan‐orientated practices in consumption, as could be seen, for example, in food (Cappeliez and Johnston 2013), music (Meuleman and Lubbers 2014) or media practices (Lindell and Hovden 2017). Tensions between local and global (Western) consumption orientations also align with social differences and are one of the key structuring factors in Serbia (Cvetičanin and Popescu 2011) or Turkey (Rankin et al. 2014). International branches of education put cosmopolitanism at the forefront of school curricula (Weenink 2007), also intertwining it with global business culture.

In this paper, I do not assume that cosmopolitan cultural consumption is a part of cultural capital per se. The cultural legitimacy of foreign movies or food is still derived from its cultural appraisal. It is unlikely that watching a global blockbuster or dining at McDonald's would be considered a valued cultural practice despite its ‘foreign’ status in countries outside the United States. This approach does not deny that foreign and transnational‐based practices are often valued in the current cultural milieu. However, it is not applied universally across cultural space, and its valuation is, as always, a result of significant cultural work by cultural producers and intermediaries.

However, cosmopolitanism is also incorporated into new forms of institutionalised cultural capital. It could take the form of degrees from international universities, international awards or study‐abroad experiences. While the former is very uncommon in the context of many countries, the latter is nowadays an important part of tertiary education in many European universities (e.g., as part of the Erasmus program). Therefore, it could be considered one of the valuable outputs of study at the university—a means of acquiring institutionalised cultural capital. It has an institutionalised form, it is a part of structured CVs, and it is sometimes referenced as a rite of passage experience akin to the grand tours of the 17th and 18th centuries (Grabowski et al. 2017).

Convertibility of Cultural Capital

A key theoretical assumption of cultural capital is its convertibility into other capitals and/or status positions. One of the primary places where this conversion between acquired taste and consumption disposition takes place is in the education system. This process was delineated in Bourdieu's classical work (Bourdieu and Passeron 1990) and received detailed attention in many branches of educational research (Lareau and Weininger 2003). First and foremost, schooling represents a classed environment that was created by the middle‐class and prepared for middle‐class children and families (Lareau 2011). Embodied cultural capital brings a wide range of advantages for children in the school system, including linguistic competencies, familiarity with legitimate culture, a sense of entitlement and confidence in negotiation, and an overall feeling of being ‘at home’ in the middle‐class institutional environment of school.

An established form of cultural capital is more readily valued in the institutional setting of the educational system, as this environment gauges its evaluation, to a large extent, on consecrated parts of legitimate culture. However, the inner differentiation of cultural capital should not be overstated. Both established and emerging forms of cultural capital represent secondary differences of culturally privileged positions (Flemmen et al. 2018; Špaček 2018, 2024) rather than being antagonistic to one another. Emerging cultural capital does not presuppose the establishment of a new field of power but is more associated with newcomers to the cultural field (Bourdieu 1996). Therefore, I assume that both established and emerging forms of embodied cultural capital could be valuable resources in the process of capital conversion. Nonetheless, it could be argued that due to an affinity between established cultural capital and the educational institution, the former should be more of an advantage.

This study will ask a question about the extent to which embodied cultural capital can be converted into educational outcomes ‐ that is, institutionalised cultural capital ‐ during tertiary education. This question is addressed by examining the predictive effect of cultural capital at the beginning of tertiary education on successful educational outcomes 7 years later. The research question is elaborated in several ways: first, successful educational outcomes are tracked, both in the form of achieved university degrees and in the form of participation in study‐abroad programmes. Second, the prediction effect of embodied cultural capital is contrasted with the effect of direct transmission of parents institutionalised cultural capital (university degree). And third, I will also seek to find the differentiated effect of embodied cultural capital in different fields of study. The question I ask is whether embodied cultural capital could more readily be converted into an institutional form in fields of study that are more related to the cultural field, for example, in the humanities and social sciences, as opposed to other study areas.

Context and Data Collection

The Czech education system is, to a large extent, public and without admission fees. Nevertheless, there is a high level of educational reproduction due to the existence of a highly selective early academic track (Straková 2007) and the low capacity of schools to compensate for family background advantages (OECD 2023). However, the significant expansion of tertiary education since the 2000s led to high admission rates even at the most prestigious Czech universities. Meanwhile, the entrance barrier is further lowered by the fact that an overwhelming majority of students attend public universities which have no admission fees. Today, it is far more common for university students to come from non‐academic educational tracks than in the past.

This study is based on two waves of a longitudinal survey of a 2016–18 cohort of students at Charles University. Charles University is both the largest and the most prestigious university in the Czech Republic. Over eight thousand new students enter its undergraduate studies each year, which is about 12% of all new tertiary education students in the Czech Republic. The average admission rate to undergraduate study programmes is 33%; however, it could be as high as 60% in STEM or as low as 20% in the field of medicine. Charles University offers a wide range of study programmes, including humanities, social sciences, education, law, medicine and science. Applied technical and economics branches are to some extent under‐represented there, focusing mostly on top‐level academic research in STEM and economics disciplines rather than wider business or technical‐orientated training. While the focus on a single university clearly limits the scope for generalisation, its internal diversity in terms of fields of study and levels of selectivity makes it an excellent case for analysis. Given its prestigious position within the higher education system, one might expect the effects of cultural capital to be particularly pronounced; at the same time, the large size of the student cohort and the wide range of admission rates render the sample broadly representative of Czech tertiary education.

The first wave of the study took place in 2017. All first‐ and second‐year undergraduate students at Charles University were contacted about participation in the online survey. Questionnaires were completed by 5127 participants, which represents a response rate of 39%. There was no sampling bias with respect to the gender or study field of participants (see Špaček 2018). Most of the respondents (84%) entered their email addresses for further follow‐up studies. All participants were questioned seven years later in the second wave of the survey in 2024. More than 46% of them were willing to complete the second questionnaire; therefore, the whole longitudinal dataset consists of 2020 respondents. There is a slightly higher attrition rate among male participants (see Appendix A1 and A2); however, the results of all models are substantively unchanged under IPW adjustment, suggesting robustness to selective attrition.

A subset of the respondents was used in the analysis to achieve a more comparable context of the individual trajectories of capital conversion and education. First, due to important institutional differences in the organisation of educational systems between countries, only students born in the Czech Republic were included in the analysis. 1 Second, the aim of the study requires a comparison of similar life trajectory timings. Therefore, only fresh secondary school graduates at the beginning of their tertiary education were included in the analysis. These respondents were defined as belonging to the age cohort born between 1995 and 1997. These two criteria filtered out more than a quarter of respondents; therefore, the final sample size used in the analysis was 1355 students. No weighting was employed in the data analysis.

Analytical Strategy

The analytical strategy is as follows: economic capital and both forms of cultural capital are measured using variables constructed through a categorical principal component analysis (CATPCA). This approach diverges from the usual Bourdieusian tradition, in which capitals are typically derived using multiple correspondence analysis. However, the aim of this analysis is not to map the social and cultural space, but rather to produce robust measurements of capital for use in subsequent regression modeling.

There are also recent arguments for employing a wider range of statistical methods that may more appropriately reflect the characteristics of specific datasets (Atkinson 2024). CATPCA, in particular, has advantageous properties for identifying latent dimensions from sets of ordinal variables. It is not susceptible to the horseshoe effect, which often distorts the results of multiple correspondence analysis. Moreover, CATPCA preserves the ordinal nature of the input variables while not assuming linear distances between ordinal categories.

Three separate CATPCAs were used to construct three independent variables: economic capital, established cultural capital and emerging cultural capital. Sets of variables used for these CATPCAs were based on theoretical reasoning, and their consistency was confirmed by Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega. High stability of CATPCA loadings was confirmed by bootstrap resampling (see Appendix A4). In contrast to multiple correspondence analysis, this approach prioritises the measurement properties of the constructed scales rather than a holistic mapping of the space of tastes and practices. Accordingly, unlike standard Bourdieusian analyses, it relies only on the key variables most relevant for scale construction. The variables used to measure different forms of cultural capital were derived from both theoretical considerations and previous analyses of the cultural space of Czech youth (Špaček 2018, 2024).

The convertibility of cultural capital during tertiary education was assessed by comparing achieved academic outcomes with the level of cultural capital at the start of the study period. Three different outcomes were measured based on the 2024 survey: obtaining a university degree, obtaining a master's degree and participation in a study‐abroad program. Each of these possible capital conversions was modeled by a binary logistic regression model. Binary logistic regression allows for the comparison of the effect of independent variables on one binary dependent variable (outcome). It assesses how changes in the value of each independent variable affect the probability (or odds) of a given dependent outcome. The results of the regression models are presented in tables with usual parameters alongside average marginal estimates, complemented with a series of plots showing predicted probabilities of outcomes in relation to key independent variables. These plots enable the better evaluation and comparison of the variable size effect, but also reflect the limitation of comparison based only on the model parameters (Mood 2010).

The regression model includes institutionalised cultural capital (parents' education, secondary education track) and family economic capital alongside both forms of embodied cultural capital. These variables are crucial for answering the main research question about the convertibility of embodied cultural capital into an institutionalised form. Regression models also use interaction terms between the field of study variable and cultural variables to assess the hypothesis about the differentiated effect of cultural capital in different fields of study. These interaction terms acknowledge the possibility that the effect of cultural capital varies in the context of different fields of study. Dependent variables are based on the first wave of the study in 2017, whereas the outcome (independent) variables come from the second wave. This longitudinal design allows much stronger empirical conclusions about causation to be made when compared to a typical cross‐sectional survey design.

The analysis assessed the linearity assumption for all continuous predictors using partial‐residual diagnostics and joint significance tests of spline components. These diagnostics indicated a nonlinear association only for established cultural capital in the study‐abroad outcome model (Table A6, p = 0.002). Therefore, a restricted cubic spline was specified for this variable in that model only. Multicollinearity was assessed using generalised variance inflation factors (GVIFs). All predictors were well below common thresholds (< 3), indicating no problematic collinearity among capital indices or controls.

Variables

Three sets of variables were used to construct the measurement of economic capital, established cultural capital and emerging cultural capital. Separate CATPCA were used for each set of variables to quantify input categorical variables and identify their dimensionality. The procedure of CATPCA assesses and quantifies all categories of input variables. This empirical‐based quantification confirmed the reliability of assuming the ordinal nature of all variables.

The set of variables used for economic capital consists of a subjective assessment of the respondent's family wealth on a scale of 1 (‘rich’) to 6 (‘poor’) and four Likert scale statements reflecting family wealth, needs for austerity and financial security. Economic capital achieved a robust McDonald's omega of 0.84 and explained 62% of the variance of input variables (see Table 1).

The set of variables used for established cultural capital consists of three variables measuring the frequency of visits to the theater, galleries and museums, and three variables measuring preferences for classical music, jazz and poetry. The set of variables used for emerging cultural capital measures the frequency of cinema visits, watching movies and TV series, preferences for sci‐fi and fantasy literature and preferences for dubbing in foreign movies and TV series (in reverse scale). Another four variables are based on questions of whether respondents have seen and liked selected movies (Lord of the Rings, Arrival) and how many episodes of selected TV series they have watched (Game of Thrones, Sherlock). These titles represent generally acclaimed cultural products that, on the other hand, are not part of institutionalised established culture. Two variables were also constructed to measure the span of cultural taste, the first of which denotes the number of movies respondents have seen out of a list of 15, and the second of which counts how many TV series they have seen at least one episode of out of a list of 9. Established and emerging cultural capital achieved a similar value of McDonald's omega of 0.74 and explained 44% and 31% of the variance of input variables (see Table 1).

The question asking about liking specific movies (Lord of the Rings, Arrival) also contained a specific category for respondents who have not seen them. Comparison of different CATPCA models confirmed that the category of ‘not seen’ should, in these cases, be considered a more negative position than the highest dislikes among those who had seen the movie.

The intentional separation of all three cultural capital constructs into independent CATCPAs implies that the resulting variables are not constrained to be orthogonal, and correlations between them may therefore occur. A slight correlation is observed between established and emerging forms of cultural capital (r = 0.17), reflecting their shared association with cultural capital in general. 2 Family economic capital is not significantly correlated with either form of embodied cultural capital. However, it is moderately correlated with institutionalised cultural capital (Spearman's ρ = 0.32).

Parents' university education represents family‐acquired institutionalised cultural capital. It is not just an indicator of social status but could also be understood as a discrete status symbol that divides families where university education is part of a common, shared and expected experience and where university‐level education is something distant and alien. First‐generation students have greater difficulties in their study trajectory and have a higher risk of attrition (Spiegler and Bednarek 2013). The variable has three levels based on whether both, one or neither of the student's parents achieved a university‐level education.

A high school track maps the difference between two major types of secondary education: vocational and academic (gymnázium). This separation takes place in schools, where, in most cases, either only academic or only vocational study programmes are present. An academic track puts much greater emphasis on the acquisition of skills and competencies expected at tertiary education levels. It is thus an indication of an individually acquired institutionalised form of cultural capital, formalised as a sign of the type of education that is more valued at universities.

The field of study variable was classified into the major categories offered at Charles University: humanities, social sciences, law, education, STEM and medicine. Field of study is a key variable for testing the differentiated convertibility of cultural capital across different disciplines. Humanities and social sciences are typical areas of cultural capital accumulation. They represent the most intellectual areas of academic education and are closely connected to institutions that define and establish the cultural field. Thus, it could be argued that acquired cultural capital could be more important and necessary for successful graduation in these fields of study than in other branches of tertiary education. This argument is supported by differences in average established and emerging cultural capital among fresh students entering different study programmes due to both selection and self‐selection processes (see Appendix A5). The bigger differences are between volumes of established cultural capital, with the largest gap between humanities and social sciences on the one hand (0.40 and 0.30, respectively), and medicine and STEM on the other (−0.21 and −0.12, respectively). The dichotomous variable used in the regression analysis thus differentiated between the humanities and social sciences (SSH) and all the other study programmes (non‐SSH).

Finally, gender is an important variable with a significant impact on study trajectories. Women have a much higher probability of pursuing academic pathways in the Czech educational system (Smith and Sokolová 2022).

All three outcome variables are based on data from the second wave of the study. They represent a binary mapping of whether desirable academic outcomes occurred or did not occur (see Table 2). The achievement‐of‐any‐degree outcome is considered to have occurred for students who completed at least a bachelor‐level degree between the first and second waves of the study. Those who were still studying in a five‐ or 6‐year master's program 3 were also counted, as many students graduate later than the expected timeline outlined in their study program. This outcome occurred for as many as 90% of participants. The achievement of a master's degree is considered to have occurred for all participants who had completed the master's degree study program by the time of the second wave of the study. This outcome occurred for as many as 79% of participants.

Results

The participants of the study were mostly between 26 and 29 years old during the second wave of the longitudinal study. The majority of them had already completed tertiary education at this time (see Table 3). Only 12% of participants had failed to complete either the study they were taking in 2017 or any other study program. Another 13% graduated from bachelor programmes and didn't pursue master's degrees. As many as 55% completed a master's‐level program, and another 11% continued to do PhD‐level study. Most of the participants were working, and more than half were working at a full‐time job. One quarter of participants were married, and another 51% had a partner.

As already discussed, dropouts from tertiary education were uncommon in the data; over 90% of participants achieved at least a bachelor's degree. Nonetheless, the regression models identified several strong predictors of this successful outcome. First, having both parents with university education almost doubled the chance of successful tertiary education, meaning an average 5% points higher probability. Second, completing an academic secondary education track made students much more accustomed to the university environment and increased the probability of finishing their university education by 9% points. Economic capital in the family also significantly improved these chances. On the other hand, embodied forms of cultural capital, that is, its established and emerging forms, had no significant effect in this model.

A second set of regression models focusing on master's‐level education had a very similar structure of regression coefficients to the previous one. Almost 80% of participants had achieved a master's degree by the time of the second wave of the study. A parent's university education and a respondent's secondary educational track both increased the likelihood of achieving a master's degree. This meant, on average, a 9% point higher probability for respondents with both parents having university education and over 13% points higher for respondents with an academic secondary educational track. Family economic capital had a slight effect, and neither form of embodied cultural capital had a significant effect.

Therefore, with respect to the variables covered in the model, it seems that cultural capital makes little difference in the chances of finishing a bachelor's or master's level of education. The regression models confirmed a high rate of educational reproduction. A parents' university education and academic secondary‐level track highly increased the chances of students completing a higher level of university education. These institutionalised forms of cultural capital contrast with embodied forms mapped by taste and cultural consumption, which seem not to be relevant predictors of educational outcome in these models.

Nonetheless, a third tracked outcome, the experience of studying abroad, presents a more complex pattern. Parental education retains a similar effect as in the previous two models. Having one or both parents with a university education increased on average the predicted probability of studying abroad by 5 and 10% points, respectively. Similarly, having attended an academic secondary education track is associated with an average increase of about 11% points. However, both forms of embodied cultural capital are also significant predictors of this outcome, although their effects vary by field of study, differing between SSH and non‐SSH programmes.

Established cultural capital is associated with a higher probability of study‐abroad experience across all fields of study. The regression model demonstrates its markedly non‐linear effect. As shown in Figure 1, values of established cultural capital below the sample average are associated with only minimal differences in the likelihood of study‐abroad participation, with average predicted probabilities slightly above 20%. In contrast, the probability increases sharply at above‐average levels of established cultural capital: for respondents with a value of +1 (i.e., one standard deviation above the mean), the average predicted probability rises to 36%.

The effect of emerging cultural capital differs by field of study. As shown in Figure 2, no substantive association is observed in non‐SSH fields. In SSH fields, however, emerging cultural capital has an effect comparable in magnitude to that of established cultural capital. The average predicted probability of having a study‐abroad experience among SSH students increases from 30% at −1 standard deviation to 45% at +1 standard deviation of emerging cultural capital.

The results indicate that parental education and attendance in an academic secondary school track are strong predictors of attaining bachelor's and master's degree. Both predictors reflect the endowment of institutionalised cultural capital and indicate the direct incorporation of scholarly culture and academic requirements. This channel of capital transmission is not unexpected, as educational research consistently documents a high level of educational reproduction across countries, including Czechia.

The regression analyses do not provide evidence for an effect of embodied cultural capital, whether in its established or emerging form, on the successful completion of tertiary education. Embodied cultural capital does not appear to significantly influence progression through university, regardless of field of study. By contrast, embodied cultural capital is strongly associated with the study‐abroad experience. Although this outcome is also significantly predicted by parental education and academic secondary education track, higher levels of established cultural capital substantially increase the probability of studying abroad. In addition, emerging cultural capital is associated with study‐abroad experience among students enrolled in SSH fields.

Sensitivity Analysis

Several unobserved factors may plausibly influence the association between established cultural capital and participation in study‐abroad programmes, including language proficiency, faculty‐level mobility policies, or students' academic performance. To assess the robustness of the estimated effects to such potential omitted variables, I apply the sensitivity analysis framework proposed by Cinelli and Hazlett's (2020), implemented using the sensemakr R package. This approach evaluates how strong would an unobserved confounder would need to be in order to render the observed effect statistically insignificant.

For the effect of established cultural capital, an unobserved confounder would need to explain more than 6.71% of the residual variance of both the treatment and the outcome to eliminate statistical significance (see robustness value, Table 7). For emerging cultural capital, the corresponding threshold is 1.75%. To assess the plausibility of such confounding, parental educational attainment and high school academic track are used as strong benchmark variables. Discussion

Studies focused on cultural capital are often not able to trace one of its defining features—the propensity to accumulation and convertibility. In this paper, I employed a longitudinal dataset of tertiary education students to explore the convertibility of cultural capital, specifically how cultural consumption and taste for legitimate culture at the beginning of university study can influence the chance of achieving various educational outcomes.

The longitudinal survey data collected at the largest and most prestigious Czech university bring important insights to the convertibility of cultural capital. First, the analysis showed a stark contrast between the convertibility of institutionalised and embodied forms of cultural capital. Institutionalised cultural capital, indicated by parents' education and an academic secondary education track, had a major effect on the likelihood of completing a university education. On the other hand, embodied cultural capital, measured by a taste for legitimate culture, had no such effect, irrespective of whether we focused on established or emerging forms. This lack of significance concerning embodied cultural capital persisted even when the analysis differentiated between cultural‐orientated fields of study and others. However, the convertibility of embodied cultural capital was clearly visible when the analysis shifted from a focus on achieved education to study‐abroad experience during tertiary education. Embodied cultural capital was shown to have a major impact on the probability that a student will have this experience, which represents one of the institutionalised indicators of transnational and cosmopolitan competences.

This major difference between the effects of embodied cultural capital on different educational outcomes has two different explanations. The first sees this as a shift of status differentiation on different levels. As a result of a general expansion of tertiary education, the achievement of university study programmes ceases to be significantly distinctive in social reproduction. The distinction shifts to another level, that is, study‐abroad experiences, which are profoundly connected with a higher level of embodied cultural capital (see also Lucas 2001). It demonstrates a capacity ‘to stand outside one's own national frame of reference’ (Prieur and Savage 2015, 310), which is directly linked to the valuation of cosmopolitanism.

The second explanation supposes the existence of two parallel areas of social space—one that leans on locally powerful state institutions, such as a national education system, and the other that is part of a global space of cultural hierarchies centered in European economic and cultural core centers. In the case of the former, the direct reproduction of cultural capital inside the institutionalised educational setting can bypass the necessity of embodied cultural capital. At least in the case of the Czech schooling system, there seems to be a very strong direct reproduction of institutional capital that does not have to operate through more subtle areas of embodied cultural capital. This is coherent with other studies that show a very instrumental understanding of legitimate culture among Czech students (Špaček 2024). This reproduction process is strongly embedded in the national framework and is supported by correspondence between the traditional school curriculum, the expectations of parents and the state examination system.

The embodied form of cultural capital is necessary, however, in the case of an ambition to exceed the local nation‐state context of the European semi‐periphery and attempts to achieve success in the transnational field. A similar tension between local and cosmopolitan aspects of social space was demonstrated in Weenink's (2007) study of Dutch secondary education. Two types of education here mirror the diverging fractions of the cosmopolitan and established upper middle class. They represent tension between upwardly mobile upper‐middle‐class individuals orientated toward an international frame of reference and ‘old’ families with a continuous presence in upper‐middle‐class society. Weenink discusses how these factions differ in their reliance on mastering power of place in the case of the upwardly mobile (e.g., international geographical mobility) and power of time in the case of the established (e.g., tradition, ‘cultivation’). The resources of the more cosmopolitan mobile class ‘provide a way to escape the restrictions of space as it allows its owners to exceed the territorial basis of certain areas of social struggle’ (Weenink 2007, 511).

Further research focusing on upper middle class in peripheral countries is needed to assess the plausibility of these explanations. This included inquiry into family strategies of social reproduction, particularly with respect to education, as well as analysis of transitions from education into high status occupational positions (see e.g. Friedman and Laurison 2019). Such work should examine how locally specific and cosmopolitan resources are mobilised as career advantages.

Comparative research on professional education and occupational fields would also be valuable, especially in assessing the extent to which professional success depends on forms of consecration obtained abroad – such as from elite foreign universities, international work experience, or recognition in Western countries – versus reliance on local social network and knowledge of context‐specific institutional rules. Evidence for a dual structure of local and transnational fields would be provided if cultural capital were found to be convertible only within particular professional domains, while other resources proved more decisive in locally embedded professions. This perspective parallels the unequal effect of emerging cultural capital on study‐abroad experience across fields of study (SSH and non‐SSH) identified in present analysis.

While both explanations are similar, they differ in their understanding of local fields of power in peripheral countries. The first explanation assumes one vertical dimension of climbing social positions, where cosmopolitan attitudes and experiences are the next steps above local top positions. The second explanation draws a rather different picture. It shows independent fields in peripheral countries with their own rules and values. These fields could be mirrored in structuring the principle of tension between local and global culture in social space, as shown by Cvetičanin and Popescu (2011) in Serbia. It suggests a need for further inquiry into the social structure of non‐core countries and the implications of this division for the cultural and political fields. It also calls for a nuanced understanding of cultural hierarchies, where parallel hierarchies of local‐embedded culture and transnational global culture could exist side by side. It is important to note that while peripherality is often construed vis‐à‐vis the center (Kuipers et al. 2022), it is also crucial to explore and analyze the conditions and mechanisms that allow for the creation of autonomous spaces in peripheral countries that could diverge from the core and/or transnational definition of legitimate culture.

This paper demonstrated the importance of analysis which allows us to explore the processual and longitudinal character of mechanisms of inequality connected with cultural capital. Differentiation of various mechanisms of convertibility could bring attention to different structuration mechanisms that underlay the static structures revealed in the cross‐sectional design of a cultural mapping of social space. While the presented dataset is limited by a narrow delineation of population, anchored in one university in tuition‐free tertiary education, it demonstrated the relevance of tension between local and global fields of culture and the varied role of different forms of cultural capital.
