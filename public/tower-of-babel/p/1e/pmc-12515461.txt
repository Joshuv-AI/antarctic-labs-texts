How may grant applications change in future because of artificial intelligence? An international and historical perspective
Authors: Christina Gao, Benjamin Cook, Brandon Stretton, Aashray Gupta, Joshua Kovoor, Weng Onn Chan, Stephen Bacchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrant application processes fulfil a vital role by determining the allocation of resources to facilitate medical research. Existing grant application processes run by large governmental institutions have changed over time. Through examination of previous changes to grant application processes and the current challenges facing grant applicants and reviewers, including the increasing role of artificial intelligence, insights may be obtained into future modifications in grant application structures that require consideration.Keywords: funding, research, artificial intelligence, communication

Introduction

The effective allocation of research funding is essential to the progression of medical evidence. While there are multiple possible sources of research funding, large governmental agencies (such as the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC)) provide substantial support for flagship projects and renowned researchers. Such grants are awarded based upon grant applications. Currently, many grant applications are submitted in an entirely written format through online portals, with no oral component to the applications. The exact nature and structure of these grant applications vary by institution, grant opportunity and over time.

The structure of grant applications awarded by governmental bodies and their associated policies and review processes undergo change. This change is to be expected given the dynamic nature of science and technology. One example of previous changes is alterations to the number of resubmissions allowed for US National Institutes of Health (NIH) Grants. 1 In the United Kingdom, another example of such change is the previous limitation of applicants per institution for specific grants with the Medical Research Council (MRC). 2 Previous articles have described that the reduction of paperwork associated with grant applications was a key factor supporting previous changes, 2 although the factors that drive such changes are complex.

In this article, the current structures of and historical changes to premier research funding opportunities from the NHMRC (Investigator Grant), NIH (R01 Research Project Grant) and MRC (Programme Grant) are discussed, with a particular focus on the inclusion, or not, of oral assessments.

NHMRC – Investigator Grant

Since the introduction of NHMRC Investigator Grants in 2019, there have been many substantive policy changes regarding this grant opportunity. In 2020 and 2021, changes were made to peer‐review processes, including the provision of written feedback and the assessment of achievements ‘Relative to Opportunity’. 3 In 2022, a significant policy intervention affected NHMRC Investigator Grants at the mid‐career and senior research levels that involved the institution of a gender quota. A significant response to the low proportion of Investigator Grants awarded to female candidates in 2021 served as the catalyst for this change. 4 This gender quota determined that the proportion of awarded grants should be equal by gender.

Currently, and since conception, Investigator Grants are submitted entirely in a written format. In addition to online‐only components of an application, including investigator, curriculum vitae and career details, an application comprises seven pages of writing (including references). 5 There is no oral component to the application process.

NIH – R01 Research Project Grant

Although they were not named Research Project Grants, extramural grants have been awarded in the United States since 1937. 6 Research Project Grants (coded R01) are the original NIH grant mechanism. 7 In such a long‐running grant application process, there have unsurprisingly been multiple changes over time, particularly relating to information technology. With respect to specific policy changes, one well‐received change in 1999–2000 was the option to exclude detailed costs in advance for applications <$250 000, which 80% of surveyed scientists endorsed and was also supported by peer reviewers. 2 Another substantive change in 2009 involved a ‘two‐strike’ policy, in which a given application could only be submitted on two occasions. This policy was more divisive, with a petition of 2335 individuals ensuing, in response to which data were provided that indicated a reduction in the time to award for both new and established principal investigators (PI). 8

Currently, R01 grants involve a written application, with one page for the ‘Specific Aims’ and the ‘Research Strategy’ not exceeding 12 pages. 9 However, it has been shown that it may be routine for an additional 100 to 150 ancillary pages of content to be included. 10 There is no oral component to the evaluation of the grant.

MRC – Programme Grant

The MRC has a long history dating back to tuberculosis in 1913 with multiple changes during that period of time; however, there have been several recent substantive changes with respect to grant funding processes that require attention. 11 As of 2023, instead of the previous Joint Electronic Submission system, applications will be directed via the Funding Service. 12 The Funding Service was created as a component of the UK Research and Innovation (UKRI)'s Simpler and Better Funding (SBF) Programme, which followed an independent review into research bureaucracy. 13 Among the findings of this review were concerns about the length and complexity of grant funding application processes. The SBF Programme encompasses many substantial changes aimed at improving the funding experience and reducing bureaucracy, including increased harmonisation, a single point of entry and increased responsiveness. 12

Currently MRC Programme Grant applications involve both a ‘pre‐application’ stage and a ‘full application’ stage. The pre‐application stage involves an email with a four‐page description of the programme (including track record and references). 14 Those who are then invited to submit a full application, which are up to 12 pages in length, have specific criteria based upon their area of research.

Discussion

These grant award processes share several commonalities, including that, since inception, all have undergone substantive change. All have also a near‐exclusive focus on written or print communication. Modifications that have reduced the paperwork and bureaucracy associated with grant applications have been well received. With this historical context, a view to potential future modifications in the context of contemporary artificial intelligence (AI) can be considered.

The opportunity cost associated with the time spent preparing subsequently rejected grant applications is substantial. Highly trained academics and clinician‐scientists are a scarce resource, whose time should be used to maximum effect. In a nursing cohort, the time required from a PI to prepare a NIH grant was up to 162.3 h. 15 There were 51 883 NIH R01 applications submitted in 2023, with a 21.3% success rate. 16 Although clearly an oversimplification, extrapolation suggests that 6 627 020 person‐hours (276 125 person‐days or 756 person‐years) were spent on rejected grants, which could have been used elsewhere. For example, time spent rewriting grant applications substracts from time conducting research or spent with patients and trainees. 17 While it may be argued that time spent preparing grant applications hones scientific ideas, a survey of 6081 faculty described that 42% of the time spent on grant applications by PIs was entirely administrative. While it has been studied less rigorously in other grant schemes, similar estimates may be possible based on the number of applications, such as the 1857 NHMRC Investigator Grant applications in 2019. 3

AI is already being used to improve efficiency and reduce administrative burdens in other medical domains. For example, the FDA has described support for the use of validated AI throughout the development and approval process of biological products. 18 Relatedly, there is increasing evidence that AI can assist with administrative burdens in general practice. 19 If AI is gainfully used in these settings to streamline administrative work, then it provides support for the notion that the strategic use of AI could facilitate grant preparation, particularly with respect to the associated administrative work. Such a use case could involve the completion of routine forms or the conversion of a document from one institution into that suitable for another (e.g. when completing templated letters of endorsement).

The burden on, and effectiveness of, peer reviewers when reviewing written applications is another significant consideration. Academics assert that there is not time to read full applications; instead, they must read selectively to answer specific questions. 20 Significant concerns have been raised regarding insufficiently experienced reviewers. 21 Previously, concern was raised regarding the number of reviewers utilised. 22 Strategies to help mitigate the amount of content that requires review could help offset these issues.

With respect to reviewing grants, there are multiple possible use cases for AI. For example, particularly in view of the evidence suggesting reviewers are already reading selectively to answer specific questions, large language models could aid in summarising key information and locating specific parts of a lengthy application. However, currently the use of AI for the task of reviewing grants is explicitly prohibited in multiple settings. For example, the NIH has explicitly prohibited the use of AI for reviewing grants, citing confidentiality concerns. 23 Potential strategies to mitigate such concerns include the development and deployment of local models to support the review process, so that data do not leave the reviewing institution, or specific agreements with proprietary AI providers, which include information protection.

AI, particularly generative AI, will play an increasing role in the production of written content in the preparation of grant applications. In a 2023 survey of 1600 researchers, 15% of respondents endorsed the use of AI in grant applications. 24 It has been suggested that AI may be particularly useful in grant applications involving cumbersome and lengthy documents. 20 Such strategies could enable rapid low‐cost generation of text and facilitate the submission of larger numbers of grant applications. Given the ease with which large quantities of text can now be produced with AI, and particularly noting the strain on reviewers that is already present, there is significant risk that the burden associated with excessive written text will increase. 25

One possible suggestion in response to some of these concerns is to use AI to assist with the scoring of written text grant applications. This strategy conveys the risk of losing sight of medical progress given reduced oversight of submitted content. 26 It is not difficult to envisage a scenario in which a time‐poor grant applicant writes a brief grant application, which is then expanded upon by AI, which is then summarised and/or scored by AI, which is then read by a time‐poor reviewer (Fig. 1).

Another strategy that may have a role to play in addressing these issues is consideration of the integration of oral presentations into grant application processes. By strategically incorporating oral presentations into the grant application process (Fig. 2), it is possible that both applicant and reviewer time may be saved and the role of AI reduced (for now). This approach may have additional benefits. Oral communication, such as at conferences or media engagements, is a key skill for many scientists. 27 Through the integration of oral presentations, an additional component of the skills of grant applicants may be assessed, providing a more comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, oral presentations may provide the opportunity for real‐time questions to be asked of applicants, enabling further elucidation of components of study design or expertise.

Workflow with oral presentations in grant application process.

Possible concerns with the integration of oral presentations include travel costs, blinding, reproducibility and bias. Many of these factors are offset by video conferencing, with which most would be familiar following the global pandemic. For example, it would be feasible to have an applicant present via video call to a group of assessors who are anonymous (e.g. with cameras off). Similarly, if questions were desired, they could be presented to an applicant via an anonymous chat function if there was a desire to avoid the potential for verbal identification. With respect to reproducibility, or a desire for asynchronous evaluation, oral presentations could be recorded.

In future, modifications to grant application processes are likely to continue to occur (as they have in the past in the NHMRC, MRC and NIH), and AI is likely to be a key factor influencing these future changes. Such modifications to the grant process should seek proactively to address concerns regarding the opportunity costs of applicants and availability of reviewers. Possible alterations include supporting the use of AI for administrative processes, the development and deployment of approved local models to assist with review processes, and the incorporation of oral presentations into application processes. It may be feasible to introduce changes in a single grant scheme and evaluate its effect, including on applicant and reviewer experience, prior to integrating a modification across all grant schemes.

Conclusions

Ensuring the effective allocation of funding for scientific research is of societal importance as it directly impacts the advancement of evidence‐based medicine. Major grant processes have historically evolved in response to shifting demands and must continue to adapt. Existing grant processes face several challenges, including responding to the increasing role of AI. Efforts to develop grant application and review processes that both acknowledge the current role of AI and leverage its potential utility should be proactively sought.
