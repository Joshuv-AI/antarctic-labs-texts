The effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on older adults with sarcopenia: a meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaobei Hao, Huigui Hu, Junyu Chen, Qing Wang, Guihua Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study investigated the effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on muscle strength, physical performance, and body composition in older adults with sarcopenia.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and SPORTDiscus for randomized controlled trials on combined exercise interventions for sarcopenia, covering studies up to January 18, 2026. Data were analyzed using Review Manager (version 5.4.1) and Stata 18.0.ResultsThe search identified 24,102 records, from which 14 randomized controlled trials (involving 786 participants) met the inclusion criteria. The methodological quality of the included studies was generally high, and the independent quality assessments by two researchers showed substantial inter-rater agreement (K = 0.80). Meta-analysis showed that combined exercise can improve handgrip strength (MD = 2.68, 95%CI = 1.36-4.00, P = 0.000), the five-time chair stand test (5CST) (MD=-0.43, 95%CI=-0.65–-0.22, P = 0.000), gait speed (SMD = 0.08, 95%CI = 0.03–0.12, P = 0.001), skeletal muscle mass (SMM) (SMD = 0.62, 95%CI = 0.23–1.02, P = 0.002), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) (SMD = 0.42, 95%CI = 0.14–0.71, P = 0.03), and body fat percentage (BFP)(MD=-1.16, 95%CI=-1.92–-0.40, P = 0.003). No significant effects were noted on the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) (MD = 14.51, 95%CI=-20.82-49.85, P = 0.421) and appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) (SMD = 0.16, 95%CI=-0.20-0.51, P = 0.38).ConclusionsCombined aerobic and resistance exercise confers definite health benefits on older adults with sarcopenia. It effectively enhances muscle strength (as reflected by handgrip strength), improves physical performance (gait speed and 5CST), and optimizes body composition (by increasing SMM and SMI, while reducing BFP). Although this combined exercise regimen exerts no significant effects on 6MWT distance, or ASM, the overall findings demonstrate its positive efficacy in targeting the core pathological features of sarcopenia (e.g., reduced muscle mass and impaired muscle strength) as well as improving relevant physical functions. These results provide scientific evidence for the rehabilitation intervention of older adults with sarcopenia, supporting the promotion and application of this combined exercise protocol in clinical practice. Individualized intervention protocol should be formulated based on the specific conditions of each patient.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09647-7.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related syndrome characterized by a progressive, systemic decline in skeletal muscle mass, strength, or physical performance, which often leads to adverse outcomes such as functional impairment, decreased quality of life, and increased mortality risk [1]. Epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of sarcopenia ranges from 8% to 36% in individuals under 60 years of age and from 10% to 27% in those over 60 years [2]. However, the decline in muscle mass and function typically begins around age 40, and sarcopenia often has an insidious onset. Despite this, current global research on sarcopenia in individuals under 60 constitutes only 10% of the total research efforts [2]. Thus, the global prevalence of sarcopenia is likely higher than reported estimates. With the ongoing global population aging, the incidence of sarcopenia continues to rise. Patients with sarcopenia often experience diminished physical function, which is associated with increased hospitalization rates, fall rates, and fracture incidence [3]. Additionally, sarcopenia is closely linked to the occurrence of various diseases, including cancer [4], metabolic disorders [5] and depression [6], thus representing a significant public health issue that has garnered widespread attention from researchers globally. Consequently, delaying the onset and progression of sarcopenia is of substantial importance.

Currently, no specific pharmacological treatment for sarcopenia has been approved [7]. Pharmacological interventions remain experimental, and their long-term efficacy for preventing or treating sarcopenia has yet to be established [8]. Exercise remains the most effective and commonly used intervention for managing sarcopenia [9, 10]. Nonetheless, exercise interventions for sarcopenia vary widely (e.g., resistance training, aerobic exercise, balance training), and evidence regarding the most effective modality remains inconsistent. The specific impact of different exercise types on key patient outcomes remains unclear, warranting further investigation [11, 12]. According to current guidelines [13–15], resistance training has been shown to significantly improve muscle mass, strength, and physical performance in older adults and clinical populations [16]. Conversely, some reports suggest that aerobic exercise is most effective in enhancing muscle strength and physical performance [17].

Previous systematic reviews and network meta-analyses have failed to clearly distinguish the effects of “exercise-only interventions” from “exercise combined with nutritional interventions”, obscuring the independent value of physical activity in sarcopenia management [15, 18–20]. Equally importantly, existing syntheses lack focused analysis of “aerobic combined resistance exercise as a standalone modality”, prior reviews either conflate this intervention with other exercise types (e.g., balance training, whole-body vibration training) or confuse it with nutrition-combined strategies, precluding definitive conclusions on its specific efficacy for sarcopenia. Furthermore, many meta-analyses include heterogeneous populations (e.g., sarcopenic obesity, sarcopenia with frailty) or exclude pure sarcopenia cohorts entirely, further limiting the clinical specificity of their findings for older adults with pure sarcopenia [21–24].

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on pure sarcopenia in older adults through a meta-analysis, so as to provide evidence-based references for selecting appropriate exercise intervention strategies for this specific patient population.

Methods

Study guideline and registration

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [25]. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) on August 21, 2024 (CRD42024577873).

Search strategy

The study performed a comprehensive search across the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, CINAHL and SPORTDiscus. The search was restricted to English-language publications from the inception of these databases until January 18, 2026. Search terms included ‘sarcopenia’, ‘sarcopenic’, ‘exercise’, ‘aerobic exercise’, ‘resistance training’, and related synonyms. The detailed search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Participants met established diagnostic guidelines or criteria for sarcopenia; (2) Age ≥ 60 years; (3) Study design was a randomized controlled trial; (4) The intervention group underwent a combined aerobic and resistance exercise program of proven efficacy; the control group received usual care or was a no-exercise/blank control; (5) Reported at least one relevant outcome pertaining to muscle strength, physical performance, or body composition; (6) The full text was published in English.

The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Participants with specific acute or chronic conditions that could severely limit exercise capacity (e.g., uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, severe osteoarthritis); (2) Studies that did not provide extractable data; (3) Studies that includes nutritional or pharmaceutical supplementation (e.g., protein, vitamin D) as a combined intervention measure; (4) Non-original research such as reviews, case reports, editorials, letters to the editor, comments, conference abstracts, and animal studies; (5) Duplicate publications.

Study screening and data extraction

Three researchers (HXB, HHG and JYC) conducted the initial database search, independently screened for duplicate entries, and assessed the titles and abstracts of studies related to exercise and sarcopenia. Data were collected in qualified studies by two researchers (HXB and HHG) independently, and any discrepancy was settled through negotiation with a third investigator (WQ). Data below were isolated: author, year, sarcopenia diagnostic criteria, country, sample size, age, gender, intervention duration and outcome data (mean and standard deviation of results for all continuous, proportion or event rates for binary outcomes).

Outcome measures

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on muscle strength, physical performance, and body composition in older adults with sarcopenia. To address this objective, we predefined the following outcomes: muscle strength (assessed by handgrip strength), physical performance (evaluated by the five-time chair stand test (5CST), 6-minute walk test (6MWT), and gait speed), and body composition (including appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), and body fat percentage (BFP)).

Risk-of-bias assessment

The quality of the studies was independently assessed and cross-checked by two reviewers according to the Cochrane Handbook. The evaluation content included seven items: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and researchers, blinding of outcome assessors, completeness of outcome indicators, selective reporting, and other biases. Each item included three levels “high risk”, “low risk” and “unclear”. According to the Cochrane Collaboration assessment tool, the overall quality evaluation grade of the study was as follows: A: (low risk of bias) all the evaluation indicators used were correct; B: (moderate risk of bias) one or more indicators are not clear; C: (high risk of bias) One or more indicators are not used or are wrong [15].

Grade of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)

The study used the GRADE quality assessment system to evaluate the quality of evidence and summarize the overall quality of evidence in the studies. The evidence rating starts at high quality and may be downgraded by one or two levels based on any of the following pre-specified criteria: (1) risk of bias (i.e., more than 75% of included studies exhibiting a high risk of bias); (2) inconsistency (i.e., substantial unexplained heterogeneity I2 > 50%); (3) indirectness (i.e., factors limiting the generalizability of the results); (4) imprecision (i.e., the 95% confidence interval suggesting a change if it represents the true effect); (5) publication bias (i.e., small study effects with P > 0.05 significantly affecting the estimates). Each meta-analysis result was assessed as having very low, low, moderate, or high quality [26].

Statistical Analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using Review Manager (version 5.4.1) and Stata 18.0. The mean difference (MD) was used for outcomes measured with identical or highly comparable units (e.g., handgrip strength in kg, 6MWT in meters). The standardized mean difference (SMD) was used for outcomes that were conceptually similar but measured with different tools or in non-directly comparable units (e.g., ASM, SMI, SMM, gait speed). Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was applied if I² ≤ 50% or P > 0.1; otherwise, a random-effects model was used, and potential sources of heterogeneity were explored. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially removing individual studies to assess the robustness of the pooled results. Publication bias was evaluated using Begg’s test and Egger’s test.

Results

Search results

Altogether 24,102 studies were obtained through primary retrieval, among which duplicates were removed, yielding 13,685 articles. After reading the title and abstract, we eliminated 10,298 articles due to irrelevance. Following a comprehensive review of the full texts, the remaining 3,387 pieces of literature were further screened for full-text eligibility, and 3,375 of them were excluded for reasons including reports, non-english, animal experiments, non-randomized controlled study, no combined aerobic and resistance exercise, diagnostic criteria are not clear, no common outcome. Following full-text review, 12 studies were selected for inclusion. An additional 2 studies were identified through manual searches and the reference lists of included articles, bringing the total to 14 studies [1–14] in our meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Inter-rater agreement for screening results between two researchers was substantial (K = 0.89)(Supplementary Material 2).

The effect of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on muscle strength, physical performance and body composition

Effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on muscle strength

Handgrip Strength. Eleven articles [1–10, 14] involving 477 patients evaluated handgrip strength. A random-effects model meta-analysis of eleven studies showed that combined exercise improved handgrip strength compared to the control group (MD = 2.68, 95%CI = 1.36-4.00, P = 0.000) (Fig. 3). The pooled analysis indicates an average increase of approximately 2.68 kg in handgrip strength. However, significant heterogeneity was present among these studies (I2 = 81.4%, P < 0.001). Based on subgroup analyses, the improvement effect of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on handgrip strength in older adults with sarcopenia exhibited a consistent positive trend in statistical terms, which was independent of intervention duration, patient age, diagnostic criteria, and diagnostic tools. (Supplementary Material 4).

The effect of combined aerobic and resistance exercise physical performance

5CST. Two articles [1, 4] involving 107 patients evaluated 5CST. The heterogeneity was non-significant (I2 = 0%, P = 0.787); thus, the fixed-effects model was adopted. The results indicate that combined aerobic and resistance exercise can reduce the time taken by older adults with sarcopenia to complete the five-time chair stand test (MD=-0.43, 95%CI=-0.65–-0.22, P = 0.000) (Fig. 4).

6MWT. Four articles [1, 3, 7, 13] involving 242 patients evaluated 6MWT. Owing to the obvious heterogeneity(I2 = 83.4%, P < 0.001), we selected the random-effects model. The results showed no significant difference in the 6MWT between the experimental group and the control group (MD = 14.51, 95%CI=-20.82-49.85, P = 0.421) (Fig. 5). In response to high heterogeneity, subgroup analysis results indicated that this heterogeneity may be attributed to diagnostic tools, diagnostic criteria, and patient age, but not to intervention duration (Supplementary Material 5).

Gait speed. Eight articles [1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14] involving 489 patients evaluated gait speed. Moderate heterogeneity was present among these studies (I2 = 53%, P = 0.030). The pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed a increase in gait speed compared to the control group (SMD = 0.08, 95%CI = 0.03–0.12, P = 0.001) (Fig. 6). This finding suggests that combined aerobic and resistance exercise increases gait speed by 0.08 m/s in older adults with sarcopenia. Subgroup analysis results revealed that combined aerobic and resistance exercise exhibited a positive trend in improving gait speed among older adults with sarcopenia, which was independent of patient age, intervention duration and diagnostic criteria but potentially associated with diagnostic tools (Supplementary Material 6).

The effect of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on body composition

ASM. Four articles [2, 4, 8, 10] involving 238 patients evaluated ASM. Since heterogeneity was not statistically significant (I2 = 0.0%, P = 0.606), the fixed-effects model was employed to pool the effect sizes. The results showed no beneficial effect of combined aerobic and resistance exercise on ASM (SMD = 0.16, 95%CI=-0.20-0.51, P = 0.38) in older patients with sarcopenia (Fig. 7).

SMM. Three articles [4, 9, 11] involving 178 patients evaluated SMM. Pooled effect analysis showed that patients’ SMM was increased after intervention(SMD = 0.62, 95%CI = 0.23–1.02, P = 0.002), suggesting that combined aerobic and resistance exercise can enhance skeletal muscle mass in older adults with sarcopenia. Moderate heterogeneity(I2 = 43.0%, P = 0.135) was observed across the included studies(Fig. 8).

SMI. Eight articles [1–7, 14] involving 478 patients evaluated SMI. Owing to the obvious heterogeneity (I²=85.4%, P < 0.001), we selected the random-effects model. The results demonstrated a significant increase in the intervention group compared with the control group (SMD = 0.42, 95%CI = 0.14–0.71, P = 0.03)(Fig. 9). Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review is the first and most comprehensive one to date focusing on the effects of combined aerobic and resistance exercise alone for the intervention of older adults with isolated sarcopenia. Based on the 14 RCTs included in the present study, we found that combined aerobic and resistance exercise could improve handgrip strength in older adults with sarcopenia in terms of muscle strength; in terms of physical performance, it exerted positive effects on the improvement of the 5CST and gait speed in these patients, whereas no significant effect was observed on the 6MWT; with regard to body composition, it was positively associated with SMM, SMI and BFP in the patients, while its impact on ASM was not statistically significant. However, due to limited sample size and considerable heterogeneity in some outcomes, the precision of the meta-analysis results is limited. According to GRADE, the overall certainty of the evidence ranged from low to moderate, so the results should be interpreted cautiously, and more large-scale, high-quality trials are needed to confirm them.

Muscle strength

Our findings demonstrate that combined aerobic and resistance exercise enhances handgrip strength in older adults, with an average increase of 2.68 kg in the exercise group. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of this conclusion. These results are consistent with previous studies [27–30]. International guidelines such as the EWGSOP2 have incorporated handgrip strength as a core diagnostic criterion for sarcopenia, emphasizing that low muscle strength is the decisive parameter, followed by muscle mass and physical performance [31]. Handgrip strength is widely recognized as a simple and reliable measure of overall muscle strength and physical function in older adults. Insufficient handgrip strength is closely associated with increased risks of falls, hospitalization, disability, and mortality [32].

Physical performance

Our findings indicate that combined aerobic and resistance exercise was beneficial for gait speed and 5CST, while no significant improvement was observed in 6MWT. Regarding gait speed and the 5CST, previous studies have also shown that resistance training is an effective intervention for improving gait speed, and its combination with aerobic training serves as a moderately effective intervention—as measured by the chair stand test—for enhancing physical performance [33]. This may be attributed to the fact that resistance training can improve neuromuscular parameters, including better recruitment of motor units, intra and inter muscle coordination, and firing frequency, thereby positively impacting both muscle strength and muscle mass. Since physical performance in older adults primarily depends on muscle strength, resistance training significantly enhances muscle strength, and when combined with aerobic exercise, it further improves patients’ physical performance [34].

Regarding the 6MWT, the results of this study are inconsistent with previous research [35]. This discrepancy may be partly related to the limited number of studies included in our analysis that assessed this outcome. Heterogeneity sources for the 6MWT were clarified through subgroup analyses, revealing that its intervention effects are significantly moderated by diagnostic tools, diagnostic criteria, and patient age. Therefore, the results of the 6MWT in this study should not be generalized.

Body composition

The present study demonstrated that combined aerobic and resistance exercise exerted positive effects on whole-body SMM, SMI, and BFP in older adults, whereas no significant improvement was observed in ASM. These findings regarding SMM, SMI, and BFP are consistent with previous literature, indicating that increased physical activity contributes to improved muscle health and body composition in the elderly population, as specifically manifested by reduced body fat percentage and increased skeletal muscle mass and index. The underlying mechanisms may involve the role of aerobic exercise in facilitating the replacement of fat with lean muscle tissue in older adults [36], while resistance training has been proven to effectively stimulate muscle hypertrophy [37]. Furthermore, prior research suggests that performing aerobic exercise prior to resistance training may stimulate muscle protein synthesis through vasodilation, thereby exerting a synergistic effect on muscle mass and strength [38]. Although ASM is a commonly used and readily accessible indicator for sarcopenia assessment, no significant change was observed in this study. This result may be attributed to the limited number of studies included in the analysis of this parameter, highlighting the need for further investigation with larger sample sizes in future research.

High heterogeneity

The results of this study demonstrated considerable heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses indicated that heterogeneity in gait speed may be associated with the diagnostic tools employed, while heterogeneity in SMI appeared to be primarily related to patient age and diagnostic tools. In contrast, heterogeneity in handgrip strength showed no significant association with diagnostic tools, diagnostic criteria, patient age, or intervention duration. Although this meta-analysis has identified several sources of heterogeneity, it has not explored other potentially relevant variables such as exercise intensity, exercise frequency, adherence to training protocols, baseline functional status, and sex-related differences. Future studies are recommended to incorporate these unexplored variables to conduct subgroup analyses, thereby further investigating the sources of heterogeneity. Nevertheless, sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of outcomes for all measures except 6MWT, supporting the overall reliability of the study conclusions.

Comparison of similar studies

Currently, meta-analyses investigating exercise interventions for sarcopenia in older adults have become increasingly abundant; however, most existing studies suffer from limitations such as population generalization or intervention confounding. Shen’s meta [15], which covered diverse comprehensive interventions (including whole-body vibration, resistance training, and mixed exercise), confirmed the efficacy of exercise for geriatric sarcopenia. Yet, the high heterogeneity of intervention types in this study weakened the core conclusion regarding “the role of specific exercise modalities in sarcopenia”, making it difficult to establish clear clinical direction for targeted protocols. Yoshimura’s meta [19] focused on the effects of “protein nutrition combined with (or standalone) exercise”, with outcomes encompassing muscle mass, strength, physical performance, and adverse health events (e.g., mortality and hospitalization). While comprehensive in scope, this study included populations with sarcopenia comorbid with frailty, precluding the isolation of conclusions about the “optimal exercise modality for patients with pure sarcopenia”. Li [21] and García-Alonso [20]’s research centered on “resistance exercise combined with nutritional supplements”, demonstrating that this regimen modestly improved functional performance and muscle strength in older adults. However, the inclusion of nutritional supplements eliminated direct evidence for the independent effects of exercise alone. While Yuan’s meta [22] demonstrated that exercise-only interventions benefit global cognition and most physical functions in older adults with cognitive impairment, their study population did not include individuals with geriatric sarcopenia. Luis [23] and Gonçalves [24]’s meta-analyses separately verified the benefits of standalone resistance training and combined training for older adults with sarcopenic obesity (improving body composition, physical function, and blood biomarkers); nevertheless, their focus on sarcopenia comorbid with obesity—a population with inherent metabolic burdens (excess fat) distinct from pure sarcopenia—renders these findings non-generalizable to the latter group.

In contrast, the present study focuses on the niche area of “aerobic combined with resistance exercise for pure sarcopenia in older adults”: it avoids the limitations of prior research (intervention confounding via combined nutrition, population generalization via comorbid obesity/frailty) while leveraging the unique characteristics of pure sarcopenia (no excess fat-related metabolic burden, lower sensitivity to exercise intensity) to validate the protocol’s precise suitability for this population. Our results indicate that aerobic-resistance combined exercise improves core outcomes in sarcopenic patients (including physical function, body composition, and physical performance). Compared to nutrition-supplemented regimens or complex multi-component exercises, our protocol is more streamlined and feasible: it clarifies the independent intervention value of exercise alone for sarcopenia, and through multi-dimensional literature comparisons, highlights its core advantages of “low intensity, high suitability, and strong feasibility”, thereby providing more precise evidence to advance the niche field of exercise interventions for sarcopenia.

Limitations

Despite a comprehensive literature search, the number of studies meeting the inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis remained relatively small, which reduced the statistical power of certain analyses, particularly subgroup analyses and publication bias assessments. Furthermore, the analysis of several clinically important outcome measures (e.g., 5CST, ASM) was based solely on a small number of trials with limited sample sizes, resulting in low quality of evidence and high uncertainty for the relevant findings, thus compromising the reliability and generalizability of the conclusions. (2) Heterogeneity in body composition assessment methods. Most included studies adopted bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) for muscle mass measurement, whose accuracy is relatively inferior to that of gold-standard reference techniques including computed tomography (CT) and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). In addition, there was a lack of standardized operating protocols for body composition assessment across the included studies. These factors may have introduced methodologic bias into the pooled results, potentially affecting the accuracy and comparability of the findings related to muscle mass indicators. Notably, our analysis found that the measurement method was a strong effect modifier for SMI, highlighting the need for standardized assessment tools in future research. (3) Due to the inherent nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and personnel was generally not feasible. This constitutes an inherent limitation of randomized controlled trials in this field and may introduce performance bias, particularly for subjective or performance-based outcome measures. (4) The included studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity in their training regimens. There were substantial variations in the modality, intensity, progression, frequency, and total volume of both aerobic and resistance exercise components, with a lack of standardized protocols. Although distinct exercise modes (e.g., walking, Tai Chi, virtual reality-based aerobic training, elastic band resistance training, and machine-based resistance training) involve markedly different physiological demands, they were pooled under the broad category of “combined exercise” for analysis. This approach may compromise the precision of the conclusions and limits direct comparability and generalizability across studies. (5) Regarding the study population, the included participants were predominantly from the relatively younger subgroup of older adults and were mainly of East Asian ethnicity. Consequently, the generalizability of the study findings to elderly individuals aged 80 years and above as well as other ethnic groups is limited. Furthermore, the diversity of diagnostic criteria adopted in the included studies has led to high heterogeneity, which also constitutes one of the major limitations of this research. All the aforementioned limitations have impaired the external validity of the study results to varying degrees. (6) Furthermore, this analysis included a small number of studies derived from the same cohort. Although sensitivity analyses were conducted to verify the robustness of the primary findings, the non-independence of these effect estimates may introduce potential bias and potentially inflate the precision of the analytical results.

Clinical implications and perspectives for future research

Handgrip strength is commonly used as an indicator of overall muscle strength and physical function in older adults. Low handgrip strength is associated with various adverse health outcomes, and its improvement holds significant positive implications for patient prognosis. SMI reflects the structural characteristic of muscle atrophy and is considered a fundamental metric for sarcopenia. Enhancements in SMI and SMM suggest a potential reversal of degenerative muscle loss and may indicate deeper metabolic and physiological improvements. Gait speed and 5CST serve as functional markers of mobility and lower limb strength, both predictive of fall risk, independence in daily living, and overall quality of life [39]. Resistance training is a well-established core intervention for improving muscle mass and strength [40]. Combining it with aerobic exercise can further optimize outcomes. For older patients in particular, this combined approach may reduce the injury risk associated with high-intensity resistance training alone and improve adherence.

Future research should focus on the following core directions to enhance the scientific rigor and clinical applicability of findings: (1) Optimize combined exercise intervention protocols (clarifying optimal aerobic-resistance combination modalities and parameter ranges such as intensity, frequency, and duration) and standardize sarcopenia-related outcome assessment methods, criteria, and physical performance evaluation procedures, with subsequent multi-center validation to improve result reliability and comparability. (2) Expand the research population to cover a broader age spectrum, particularly including adults aged 80 years and above, and conduct multi-center, multi-ethnic trials involving sarcopenia patients from diverse regions and ethnic backgrounds to enhance evidence generalizability while exploring age-modulated mechanisms of exercise efficacy and developing age-stratified individualized protocols. (3) Refine study designs, adopt objective measurement indicators to minimize bias, and explore the minimum effective intervention duration for significant skeletal muscle adaptations through extended intervention periods and phased body composition monitoring, thereby providing targeted evidence for the development of precise clinical exercise interventions for sarcopenia.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis confirms that combined aerobic and resistance exercise exerts positive effects on muscle strength, body composition, and physical performance in older adults with simple sarcopenia, specifically reflected in significant improvements in handgrip strength, gait speed, 5CST, SMM, SMI, BFP. These findings further support that combined aerobic and resistance exercise may serve as an effective intervention strategy to mitigate functional decline associated with sarcopenia in the elderly. While this combined exercise modality demonstrates multiple benefits in the aforementioned aspects, its long-term effects remain unclear. Moreover, heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of exercise protocols, diagnostic criteria, and assessment tools suggests that caution should be exercised when interpreting the results of this study.
