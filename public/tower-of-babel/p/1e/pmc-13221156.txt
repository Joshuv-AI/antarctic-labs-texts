Anaphylactic Shock Following Multiple Self-Medication Exposures in a Patient With Rheumatoid Arthritis Receiving Methotrexate: A Case Report
Authors: Mobeen Sajjad, Sammeullah Mirza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a 51-year-old female with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) on long-term methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, and sulfasalazine, who self-administered multiple medications, including diclofenac 50 mg, without medical supervision. She presented with acute-onset shortness of breath, generalized pruritus, angioedema, and palpitations, and was found to be in severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse, with a blood pressure of 60/nil mmHg and a heart rate of 143 beats per minute. Diclofenac was considered the most likely trigger based on temporal association and known nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) hypersensitivity risk; however, causality cannot be definitively proven as several medications were taken within the same exposure window. Investigations revealed leukocytosis, microcytic anemia, reactive thrombocytosis, and elevated alkaline phosphatase. The electrocardiogram showed no ischemic ST-segment changes or conduction abnormalities. Prompt resuscitation with intramuscular epinephrine resulted in hemodynamic stabilization within 15 minutes of the first dose, with complete angioedema resolution within 30 minutes. The patient was managed without intensive care unit admission and discharged in stable condition on day three. This case highlights the diagnostic and management challenges of severe hypersensitivity reactions occurring in the setting of polypharmacy and emphasizes the importance of individualized analgesic planning and careful medication counseling in patients with RA receiving chronic therapy.

Introduction

Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that demands immediate recognition and intervention. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) constitute one of the most common drug classes implicated in anaphylactic reactions, with diclofenac, a widely used phenylacetic acid derivative, ranking among the highest-risk agents within this category [1,2]. The pathophysiological mechanism involves IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation as well as non-immunological cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition, leading to diversion of arachidonic acid metabolism toward leukotriene production and subsequent mast cell activation [3].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by progressive joint destruction and systemic inflammation. Methotrexate, a folate antagonist and cornerstone disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) in RA management, is known to modulate immune function through multiple pathways, including adenosine-mediated immunosuppression and inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation [4]. The concurrent administration of NSAIDs with methotrexate carries well-documented pharmacokinetic risks: NSAIDs reduce renal tubular secretion of methotrexate, thereby increasing systemic exposure and toxicity risk [5].

Despite these established risks, patients with RA frequently self-medicate with over-the-counter NSAIDs for breakthrough pain, often without informing their treating physicians. This case report describes a severe anaphylactic reaction with cardiovascular collapse in an RA patient on long-term methotrexate therapy following self-administration of multiple medications, including diclofenac, and underscores the importance of individualized analgesic management in this population.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old female presented to the Emergency Department of Saad Hospital and Cardiac Care Center, Daska, in the morning hours with a one-hour history of acute-onset shortness of breath, generalized body pruritus, palpitations, and rapidly progressive facial and peripheral angioedema. The patient reported having taken multiple medications at approximately 9:00 AM on the morning of presentation, including diclofenac 50 mg (one tablet), tizanidine 2 mg (one tablet, a centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxant), dexlansoprazole 60 mg, and an antacid suspension (quantity unknown), approximately one hour prior to symptom onset. The preceding night, she had also self-administered tramadol-paracetamol (37.5 mg/325 mg) and dexamethasone 0.5 mg. None of these medications had been prescribed by her current treating physicians.

Her past medical history was significant for rheumatoid arthritis, type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), and hypertension (HTN). She had no documented history of prior drug allergies or hypersensitivity reactions. No analgesic medications had been formally prescribed by her treating physicians for pain management at the time of presentation. The self-administered medications taken on the day of admission were obtained without a prescription and had not been recommended by her current rheumatologist or any other treating physician. Cardiovascular examination revealed tachycardia with normal heart sounds (S1 + S2, no added sounds). Chest auscultation demonstrated bilateral vesicular breath sounds with no added sounds. Abdominal examination was unremarkable. Cervical lymphadenopathy was absent. A clinical diagnosis of severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse was established based on established clinical diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis [6].

Diclofenac was identified as a plausible trigger based on its temporal association with symptom onset and its well-documented association with NSAID hypersensitivity reactions; however, definitive causality could not be established given the multiple concomitant medications administered within the same exposure window.

Resuscitation was initiated immediately with intravenous normal saline 1 L stat, followed by Ringer’s lactate 500 mL, intramuscular epinephrine 0.5 mg (repeated at 15 minutes), intravenous hydrocortisone 100 mg (repeated at 30 minutes), and intravenous pheniramine maleate. The patient demonstrated a rapid and clinically significant response: blood pressure stabilized to 90/60 mmHg within 15 minutes of the first dose of epinephrine, and angioedema completely resolved within 30 minutes. She was subsequently admitted to the general medical ward for monitoring and further management. Urinary catheterization was performed for continuous urine output monitoring in view of hemodynamic instability.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) performed on admission showed no ischemic ST-segment changes or conduction abnormalities (Figure 1).

Diclofenac, tramadol, and tizanidine were discontinued at discharge, and the patient was explicitly advised to avoid these medications going forward pending formal allergy evaluation. Sulfasalazine was continued given its chronic use without prior adverse events; however, the patient was counseled regarding the theoretical possibility of sulfasalazine-associated hypersensitivity and advised to report any new symptoms promptly to her rheumatologist. Chronic medications were reviewed and continued as clinically appropriate. An oral antihistamine and a short prednisolone taper were prescribed to mitigate biphasic anaphylaxis risk, which was documented in the patient counseling records. Glimepiride was added given her uncontrolled glycemia (glycated hemoglobin = 11.4%). The patient was discharged in stable condition on day three with a discharge blood pressure of 130/90 mmHg. Future analgesic selection was recommended to be individualized following physician assessment and consideration of allergy evaluation.

Discussion

This case illustrates a severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse following unsupervised self-administration of multiple medications in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis on long-term methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, and sulfasalazine. Several clinically important dimensions merit discussion.

Suspected trigger and pharmacological context

Although diclofenac represented the most plausible trigger based on its temporal association with symptom onset and its well-documented association with NSAID hypersensitivity reactions, definitive attribution was not possible in this case. Multiple medications were taken within the same exposure window, and other concurrently administered agents, including tizanidine, tramadol, and dexamethasone, could not be completely excluded as contributing factors.

NSAIDs may precipitate hypersensitivity reactions through two distinct mechanisms: IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation and non-immunological cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) inhibition, the latter leading to leukotriene overproduction and subsequent mast cell activation [3,7]. The patient’s long-term sulfasalazine therapy was considered; however, given its chronic use without prior adverse events and the temporal association of symptoms with the acutely self-administered agents, its role as the primary trigger is considered less likely.

Concurrent methotrexate therapy was clinically noted because NSAIDs are known to reduce renal tubular secretion of methotrexate, thereby increasing systemic exposure and toxicity risk [5]. This pharmacokinetic interaction, however, operates through an entirely different pathway from IgE-mediated or pseudo-allergic hypersensitivity mechanisms. No biochemical evidence of methotrexate toxicity was identified in this case, and its contribution to the anaphylactic event could not be inferred from the available data.

Structured causality assessment using the Naranjo algorithm yielded an overall score of +2, consistent with a possible adverse drug reaction (ADR), reflecting the inherent uncertainty of this polypharmacy context (Table 6) [8]. When applied individually, diclofenac scored +2 (possible ADR), supported by its temporal association and well-documented hypersensitivity profile. Tizanidine scored +1 (possible ADR) based on temporal association alone, with limited published evidence linking it to anaphylactic reactions. These individual assessments further support diclofenac as the most plausible trigger while acknowledging that causality therefore remains uncertain.

Self-medication, polypharmacy, and clinical risk

A critical contributing factor was the patient’s self-administration of multiple medications without medical supervision. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis frequently experience breakthrough pain that drives them toward over-the-counter medications [9]. In settings where NSAIDs and other analgesics are dispensed without a formal prescription, patients with complex pharmacological backgrounds may unknowingly place themselves at significant risk.

The absence of a prior allergic history does not preclude anaphylaxis. COX-1 inhibition can precipitate reactions on first or subsequent exposures without classical prior sensitization [10]. Clinicians managing patients with rheumatoid arthritis on complex medication regimens should not rely on a negative allergy history as reassurance against hypersensitivity reactions. Individualized analgesic planning and structured medication counseling at every clinical encounter are therefore essential preventive measures in this population.

Hematological and biochemical findings

The leukocytosis (WBC = 22,400/μL) with neutrophilia (85%) most likely represents stress demargination secondary to the acute anaphylactic event and the underlying inflammatory state of RA, rather than a primary infectious etiology [11]. The absence of a documented infection source supports this interpretation.

Reactive thrombocytosis (platelets = 610,000/μL) is a recognized manifestation of chronic inflammatory conditions, including RA, where elevated interleukin-6 (IL-6) drives thrombopoietin-mediated megakaryocyte proliferation [12]. Microcytic, hypochromic anemia (hemoglobin = 9.3 g/dL; mean corpuscular volume = 67.2 fL) may reflect iron deficiency anemia, anemia of chronic inflammation, or a mixed pattern and would require iron studies for further characterization [13].

The elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP = 445 U/L) with normal transaminases is a notable finding that warrants follow-up, as isolated ALP elevation may represent an early indicator of hepatic, biliary, or bone-related pathology and cannot be attributed to any single etiology without further evaluation, including hepatobiliary imaging [14].

Management and role of early epinephrine

The rapid hemodynamic response observed in this case underscores the paramount importance of prompt intramuscular epinephrine in anaphylaxis management [15]. Blood pressure stabilized to 90/60 mmHg within 15 minutes of the first epinephrine dose, and angioedema completely resolved within 30 minutes. The patient recovered in the emergency room and general medical ward without requiring ICU admission, demonstrating that early epinephrine administration can preclude critical care escalation even in severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse.

Serum tryptase levels were not obtained due to resource unavailability in our setting. The clinical diagnosis was established on the basis of recognized diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis [6], encompassing acute onset, skin and mucosal involvement, cardiovascular compromise, and temporally consistent drug exposure.

Allergy and immunology considerations

Given the polypharmacy context and the inability to definitively identify the culprit agent, avoidance of diclofenac and all NSAIDs was advised at discharge pending formal allergy evaluation. Cross-reactivity among non-selective COX-1 inhibitors is recognized, although the pattern and extent of cross-tolerance vary between individuals and reaction phenotypes [7]. This consideration is clinically relevant in patients with rheumatoid arthritis who may require recurrent analgesic exposure.

Paracetamol at standard therapeutic doses represents a reasonable interim analgesic option pending further evaluation. Selective COX-2 inhibitors should not be initiated without prior formal allergy assessment, given the inability to definitively identify the culprit agent. Referral for allergy and immunology follow-up was recommended to guide structured evaluation, including the possibility of supervised drug provocation testing under controlled conditions following complete clinical recovery.

In our resource-limited setting, formal allergy evaluation may not be immediately accessible. Conservative avoidance of suspected triggers, individualized physician-guided analgesic planning, and patient education regarding early recognition of hypersensitivity symptoms represent a pragmatic and safe interim approach.

Limitations

This case carries limitations inherent to its single-patient nature. Definitive identification of the culprit drug was not possible given polypharmacy. Serum tryptase, skin prick testing, and drug provocation testing were not performed. Urinalysis revealed mild pyuria (WBC = 6-8 per high-power field (HPF)), attributed to uncontrolled diabetes mellitus; urine culture was not performed as the clinical picture was consistent with anaphylaxis. Prior medication history was unavailable, precluding assessment of earlier drug exposures. Methotrexate serum concentrations were not measured; therefore, the pharmacokinetic interaction between methotrexate and concurrent medication exposure could not be directly evaluated.

Conclusions

This case report describes severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular collapse in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis receiving methotrexate following self-administration of multiple medication agents. Definitive identification of the culprit drug was not possible in the setting of polypharmacy, with diclofenac representing one plausible but unconfirmed trigger. Structured causality assessment using the Naranjo algorithm yielded a score of +2, consistent with a possible adverse drug reaction. No clinical or biochemical evidence of methotrexate toxicity was identified, and methotrexate-related contribution to reaction severity could not be inferred from the available case data. Future analgesic plan should be individualized following physician assessment and consideration of formal allergy evaluation. Prompt intramuscular epinephrine remains the cornerstone of anaphylaxis management and can be life-saving even in settings without intensive care facilities.
