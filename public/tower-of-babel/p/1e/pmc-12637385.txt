Tooth-level detection and mapping of dental pathologies on panoramic radiographs using YOLOv11 and RT-DETR
Authors: Sorana Eftimie, Tudor Ileni, Liviu Iacob, Mihaela Hedeșiu, Laura Dioșan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article describes a reproducible method for automated tooth-level detection and mapping of dental pathologies on panoramic radiographs. The workflow integrates two deep learning stages: (1) segmentation of individual teeth and (2) detection and classification of dental lesions. Outputs are combined to assign pathologies to specific teeth, supporting comprehensive radiological assessment. The method was implemented and tested using two datasets: a collection of 1,628 panoramic radiographs annotated with 12 pathology categories, and the public Tufts Dental Database. Several model architectures were evaluated, including YOLOv11 and RT-DETR configurations. The influence of image formatting and preprocessing strategies on performance was also assessed. Results showed that RT-DETR-x achieved the highest mean average precision (mAP@50) for pathology detection, while YOLOv11x produced the most accurate tooth segmentation. The integrated system demonstrated high precision in linking lesions to the corresponding teeth. This method offers a practical framework for developing AI-assisted diagnostic tools in dentistry and can be adapted to other imaging datasets.• Development of a fully automated dental diagnostic tool that segments individual teeth and maps detected pathologies to their corresponding tooth• YOLOv11 and RT-DETR variants are extensively tested for segmentation and detection tasks• Preprocessing and image formatting strategies are systematically comparedKeywords: Artificial intelligence, Lesion detection, Tooth segmentation, Dental pathology, Panoramic radiography

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming multiple industries, including healthcare, where it has shown promise in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, clinical decision-making, and patient care [1,2]. In dentistry and maxillofacial radiology, AI has been applied to automated tooth identification and numbering [[3], [4], [5]] as well as the detection of diverse dental pathologies on panoramic radiographs [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. These developments highlight the potential of AI to streamline workflows and improve the reliability of dental diagnostics.

Our previous work [14] introduced automated dental lesion detection using YOLOv8, successfully identifying several dental pathologies and treatments such as caries, periapical lesions, impacted teeth, root fragments, and prosthetic restorations. However, this approach was limited to lesion detection and did not establish correspondence between lesions and specific teeth. Furthermore, external validation highlighted challenges in generalizability.

To address these limitations, this work builds on the previous article by introducing a two-stage approach, integrating transformer-based architectures that leverage an anchor-free methodology, allowing for both detection and segmentation. In this work, we utilize the newly released YOLOv11 model, which features improved feature extraction, enhancing lesion localization while maintaining efficiency. RT-DETR, a real-time variant of the DEtection TRansformer (DETR), was also selected for its speed advantages while maintaining high detection accuracy. These models were evaluated using the publicly available Tufts Dental Database [15], which provided data for dental segmentation and tooth identification.

RT-DETR [16] is an end-to-end object detector designed for real-time applications. It features a hybrid encoder for efficient multiscale feature processing, IoU-aware query selection for improved precision, and flexible speed–accuracy trade-offs without retraining. It has achieved state-of-the-art performance on the COCO dataset [17], surpassing YOLO versions 5 through 8. YOLOv11 [18] extends the YOLO family with enhanced feature extraction, reduced parameter requirements, and broader support for tasks including detection and instance segmentation.

Method details

A. Proposed approach

In the proposed approach, a segmentation model was first trained to accurately delineate individual teeth from panoramic radiographs, assigning each tooth to one of 32 possible classes. Simultaneously, a pathology detection model was trained to identify and classify 12 different types of dental abnormalities. Once both models are trained, we integrate their outputs to establish a direct association between detected pathologies and specific teeth.

Combining tooth segmentation and pathology detection models is crucial for precise and clinically relevant dental diagnostics. The segmentation model accurately identifies individual teeth, while the pathology detection model identifies abnormalities such as cavities, periapical lesions, or impacted teeth. By integrating these models, we can directly associate detected pathologies with specific teeth rather than providing generalized predictions. The proposed method involves extracting all detected pathologies and overlaying them with tooth segmentation masks to determine which abnormalities correspond to each tooth.

B. Data

Three datasets were used in this study.

1.UMFIH Panoramic Radiograph Dataset:This dataset contains 1,628 panoramic radiographs retrospectively collected between April 2021 and December 2023 at the Department of Maxillofacial Surgery and Radiology, Iuliu Hațieganu University of Medicine and Pharmacy, Cluj-Napoca, Romania. Images were acquired with a Vatech PCH-2500 panoramic imaging system (Vatech, Hwaseong, South Korea). Inclusion criteria were adult patients with permanent dentition and diagnostically adequate images; scans with significant artifacts, excessive blurring, or other conditions obstructing evaluation were excluded. Images were manually annotated using the open source Makesense.ai [19] platform and exported in YOLO format. For training and evaluation, the dataset was split into 1,375 training images, 153 validation images, and 100 test images. Image dimensions ranged from 804 × 415 to 2980 × 1536 pixels (mean 2455 × 1383; SD 459 × 286). C. Architecture selection

For the detection and segmentation tasks, we utilize several variations of YOLOv11 [18] such as YOLOv11-n, YOLOv11- s, YOLOv11-m, YOLOv11-l, YOLOv11-x, and RT-DETR (Real-Time DEtection Transformers) [16] such as RT-DETR- l and RT-DETR-x. While both YOLO and RT-DETR architectures were utilized for pathology detection, only YOLO versions were employed for teeth segmentation. This is because the RT-DETR models were not designed for segmentation. They are primarily object detection models.

The YOLO versions are known for their efficiency and scalability and were chosen due to their proven performance across a wide range of object detection tasks [20]. YOLO models, including YOLO11, produce three main outputs for object detection: Coordinates of detected objects, the probabilities for each class, typically a one-hot encoded vector, and a confidence score indicating how likely a detection is an object. In segmentation tasks, YOLO has an additional segmentation mask output. The maximum number of detections is set by default to 300, but it can predict thousands.

RT-DETR models [16], specifically designed for real-time detection with high accuracy, were applied exclusively to the pathology detection task. RT-DETR is a transformer-based object detector and has two main outputs: a set of predicted object boxes, and classification scores for each detected object. Unlike YOLO, RT-DETR does not use an objectness score. Instead, it relies on the transformer decoder to directly predict object classes and boxes. RT-DETR always outputs a fixed number of detections (queries), usually 100 or 300 (predefined in the model).

All models were initialized with default weights provided by the Ultralytics framework [21], pretrained on COCO [17] and ImageNet [22] datasets. Training was performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel i7-12700H CPU and NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU. Batch sizes were adapted to model size (YOLOv11n/x: 32/8; RT-DETR-l/x: 8/8).

D. Data augmentation

Data augmentation strategies included MixUp (probability 20 %), copy-paste (probability 10 %), and random erasing (probability 30 %) to simulate diverse real-world scenarios. Geometric transformations such as rotation (max 15°), scaling (max 30 %), perspective distortion, translation (max 30 %), and shearing (max 15 %) were applied to improve spatial robustness. Color augmentations included HSV adjustments (hue=0.0, saturation=0.0, value=0.4), horizontal (probability 50 %), and vertical flips (probability 50 %), promoting better feature learning across variations. While panoramic radiographs are grayscale, our augmentation pipeline first converts them to a 3-channel format to match the model's input requirements. The subsequent HSV augmentation, therefore, functions as a brightness and contrast adjustment (by altering the 'Value' channel), which simulates variations in exposure parameters and improves model robustness. The 'Hue' and 'Saturation' components have no effect on the grayscale data.

Additionally, a dropout of 0.3 was used to reduce overfitting, and mosaic augmentation (probability 10 %) helped improve multi-scale feature learning. MixUp is a data augmentation technique where two images are blended together by computing a weighted average of their pixel values and labels. Copy-paste augmentation involves cutting objects (or regions) from one image and pasting them onto another. Random Erasing randomly removes (masks) a part of the image, forcing the model to learn to recognize objects even when parts of them are missing. Mosaic is an advanced data augmentation technique where four images are randomly selected and combined into a single training image.

Effect of input resolution: Increasing the input size from 1024 to 1280 pixels generally improved key performance metrics. For instance, in the non-cropped experiments, RT-DETR-l's mAP@50 improved from 0.7508 to 0.7697. This suggests that higher resolution can enhance object localization and detection consistency. However, this trend was not universal across all models and metrics; for example, YOLO11l saw a slight decrease in mAP@50 from 0.7150 to 0.7070 when resolution was increased.

Model comparison: Among all architectures, the RT-DETR models consistently demonstrated top-tier performance. On the internal test set, RT-DETR-l at 1280 pixels (Experiment 2) achieved the highest mAP@50 (0.7697). The largest model, RT-DETR-x at 1024 (Experiment 1), also performed well with a mAP@50 of 0.7590. While the RT-DETR models excelled in overall accuracy, the highest precision was achieved by YOLO8x (retrained) at 1280 pixels (Experiment 2) with a score of 0.7989, indicating it produced the fewest false positives under those conditions.

Effect of cropping: The results from Experiments 3 and 4 indicate that central cropping (removal of 25 % margins) did not lead to a consistent improvement and often resulted in slightly lower performance compared to using the full image. For example, RT-DETR-l's mAP@50 dropped from 0.7697 (Experiment 2) to 0.7599 (Experiment 4). This suggests that contextual information in the margins contributes to accurate pathology detection in dental radiographs.

A quantitative analysis showed that on our test set, 5.7 % of ground truth boxes were eliminated and 20.8 % were partially cropped. The eliminated boxes were primarily located at the image periphery. We interpret the cropping of the partially touched boxes as a form of realistic augmentation, forcing the model to learn from incomplete objects. Therefore, the performance drop represents a deliberate trade-off, where we sacrifice detection of peripheral findings to create a model that is more focused on the primary diagnostic area.

Model size and generalization: The results suggest that larger models do not always guarantee better performance. For instance, in Experiment 2, the larger RT-DETR-x (0.7542 mAP@50) was outperformed by the smaller RT-DETR-l (0.7697 mAP@50). This trend may reflect overfitting, especially with a relatively small dataset, where smaller or mid-sized models can sometimes generalize more effectively.

External validation: The results on the external validation set follow similar trends to those on the internal test set, but with generally lower scores across the board. For example, the top mAP@50 score on the external set was 0.5476 (RT-DETR-l, Experiment 2), compared to 0.7697 on the internal set. This confirms that while the models have learned relevant features, they still face challenges in generalizing to a completely new dataset, highlighting the difficulty of domain shift in medical imaging.

To clarify the relationship between R_combined and the standard Recall(B), it is essential to understand our two-stage evaluation. A combined True Positive requires both a correctly detected pathology and its correct association with a tooth. The significantly higher R_combined value is primarily driven by the lenient IoU threshold of 0.25 used to determine if a pathology is correctly detected, compared to the 0.5 threshold used for the standard Recall(B). This allows the combined metric to assess the tooth-association across a wider set of plausible detections. Additionally, in the combined evaluation, a False Negative includes both missed pathologies and undetected teeth, ensuring that the entire pipeline is jointly evaluated for its ability to localize and associate both components.

Overall, YOLO11 models demonstrate a substantially lower computational cost compared to RT-DETR, while maintaining competitive accuracy. For example, YOLO11n has only 2.59 M parameters and executes in 19.43 ms, making it highly efficient for real-time applications, though its accuracy is lower than larger models. By contrast, RT-DETR-x requires 67.33M parameters and 111.26 ms per inference, which may limit its applicability in real-time systems.

These results highlight an important trade-off: YOLO11x offers the best balance between accuracy and computational efficiency, making it well suited for deployment in clinical practice. RT-DETR models, while achieving state-of-the-art accuracy, are more resource-intensive and therefore better positioned for offline or cloud-based analysis. The higher memory intensity of RT-DETR-x, despite fewer GFLOPs (232 vs. 258), reflects the transformer architecture’s reliance on self-attention and matrix multiplications, which are difficult to optimize on CPUs. On GPUs, inference time scales more directly with GFLOPs.

From a deployment perspective, even the most demanding models achieve real-time performance on modest hardware. For instance, running a YOLOv8-small or RT-DETR model on a cloud service (e.g., NVIDIA T4 GPU, 16 GB, at ∼$0.35/hour) enables latencies under 50 ms per image. In a typical dental practice handling ∼200 panoramic radiographs per month, the total monthly cloud cost would remain below $5 (including compute, storage, and orchestration fees).

Limitations

The dataset is affected by sample size and pathology imbalance, since certain pathologies naturally occur less frequently than others. This imbalance may bias model training and evaluation, particularly for underrepresented categories, although it reflects the real-world prevalence of dental conditions. In particular, two entities present in the original UMFIH dataset, apical surgery (APS) and root resorption (ROR) were extremely rare in the dataset and were therefore excluded from our experiments, limiting the analysis to 12 pathologies with sufficient representation for reliable assessment.

The most frequent misclassifications occurred for prosthetic restorations and obturations, largely due to their similar radiographic appearances. This was particularly evident in cases involving single full-coverage crowns and extensive coronal restorations (e.g., large composite or amalgam fillings). By contrast, detection accuracy was consistently high for caries, periapical lesions, and root canal treatments.

A further limitation arises from the use of panoramic radiography, which, although valuable for preliminary diagnosis and broad screening, is prone to inherent artifacts such as distortion, unequal magnification, and overlapping anatomical structures [23]. These factors can obscure early-stage or small lesions and reduce diagnostic sensitivity. To improve real-world robustness, our dataset deliberately included both high-quality and imperfect radiographs, reflecting clinical variability (e.g., artifacts, variable contrast, mild distortions). While this strategy reduces the risk of overfitting to ideal images, it also increases noise and may impact model consistency.

It should be noted that the models produced a higher number of false positives. While this is acceptable for the intended role as a screening tool, it may also increase the risk of overdiagnosis, potentially leading to unnecessary follow-up investigations or patient anxiety.

The final limitation concerns generalizability. The training dataset was collected from a single clinical center, which may restrict performance across different populations, imaging systems, or clinical protocols. Broader, multi-center datasets would be necessary to strengthen reproducibility and ensure robustness in diverse clinical settings.
