Thoracoabdominal aortic replacement in the elderly: a retrospective propensity score-matched outcome analysis
Authors: Florian Helms, Heike Krüger, Ezin Deniz, Alina Botezatu, Andreas Martens, Aron-Frederik Popov, Tim Kaufeld, Bastian Schmack, Jan Dieter Schmitto, Alexander Weymann, Arjang Ruhparwar, Morsi Arar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectives:With the overall aging population, the demand for thoracoabdominal aortic repair in elderly patients will continue to increase. With rising frequency, surgeons and physicians will have to decide whether this highly invasive procedure is appropriate and rational for their elderly patients. However, data focused on the outcomes of thoracoabdominal repair in elderly patients is still lacking to date.Methods:We here report a propensity score-matched, single-center analysis of 309 consecutive patients who underwent open surgical thoracoabdominal aortic repair from 2000 to 2024. Of those, 72 patients (23.3%) were 70 years of age or older. Matching for preoperative characteristics, burden of disease, and extent of repair revealed 68 one-on-one matches of patients below or above 70 years of age. These were then analyzed for intraoperative characteristics as well as postoperative events, complications, and mortality.Results:Elderly patients had longer intensive care unit stay times, with 6 days [interquartile range (IQR) 3–14] versus 3 days (IQR 2–8, P = 0.007), as well as higher rates of postoperative complications. Among these complications, respiratory failure (47.1% vs. 25.0%, P = 0.007), acute kidney failure (30.9% vs. 14.7%, P = 0.025), and new-onset atrial fibrillation (14.7% vs. 4.4%, P = 0.041) were found significantly more often in patients aged 70 years or older. In contrast, 30-day mortality did not differ significantly between the two groups (17.6% vs. 13.2%, P = 0.636).Conclusion:Elderly patients were more frequently prone to a prolonged and complicated postoperative course. However, this did not result in excessive short-term mortality. Thus, elderly patients should be critically evaluated before undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic repair, and both patients and surgeons should be prepared for prolonged ICU times. However, age alone should not be an exclusion criterion for thoracoabdominal aortic repair, which can be performed with acceptable short-term mortality rates.

Introduction

Open thoracoabdominal aortic replacement is among the most invasive procedures in cardiovascular surgery, often requiring large-scale incisions and long operation times. Currently available experience reports usually describe mean operation times between 350 and 450 minutes, with cardiopulmonary bypass times well above 120 minutes[1,2]. Furthermore, thoracoabdominal aortic repair is associated with considerable surgical risk and a short-term mortality ranging from 7% to 17% in most reports, depending on the extent of repair[3–5]. Thus, a thorough preoperative patient evaluation and selection are crucial to identify which patients are suitable and, perhaps even more importantly, which patients are unsuitable for open repair. Considering the above-mentioned data, the question arises whether open surgical thoracoabdominal repair is a suitable therapeutic option, particularly for elderly and potentially frail patients who carry a higher surgical risk per se. In larger population-based cohort studies, elderly patients undergoing cardiac surgery showed higher short- and long-term mortality, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays, and higher rates of postoperative complications compared to younger patients[6,7]. However, while these analyses were focused on coronary and valve operations, data on outcomes and survival of elderly patients undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic replacement are still scarce to date. Despite this, the demand for even more complex aortic operations, such as thoracoabdominal repair, in the elderly will continue to increase with the overall aging population[8]. Patients and methods

Patients and propensity score matching

A total of 309 consecutive patients who underwent open surgical thoracoabdominal aortic repair at our tertiary care institution from 2000 to 2024 were included in the study. Data on preoperative patient characteristics and burden of diseases, intraoperative parameters, and operation times, as well as the postoperative course and complications, were collected prospectively in our institutional clinical database and analyzed retrospectively. Long-term follow-up was conducted during regularly scheduled follow-up visits in our aortic outpatient clinic. Data collection and analysis were conducted in accordance with the ethics board approval by the local ethics commission (20 June 2024).

Figure 1 shows a schematic flow chart of the matching process and patient selection. Patients were divided into two groups: group 1 included patients undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic replacement under the age of 70 years at the time of operation, while group 2 included patients aged ≥70 years. Among the initial study population, 72 patients (23.3%) were 70 years of age or older at the time of the operation. Propensity score matching was performed to adjust for preoperative characteristics, burden of disease, prior cardiac or aortic procedures, as well as for the Crawford groups. The full list of predictors used for propensity score matching is shown in Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B228. The match tolerance threshold was set at 0.2. As the primary outcome, overall 30-day mortality was analyzed. Furthermore, outcome analysis included pre-, intra-, and postoperative parameters, as well as in-hospital complication rates. To analyze the possible influence of changes in operative and perioperative care over time on the postoperative outcome, a sensitivity analysis was performed by dividing the matched patient population into a cohort operated on between 2000 and 2010 and between 2011 and 2024. Then, an analysis stratified by surgery date with a combined endpoint of early mortality and adverse postoperative events was conducted. Figure 1.Flow chart. Study variables

The extent of thoracoabdominal aortic repair was categorized according to the Crawford classification of aortic diseases, considering the proximal and distal prosthesio-aortic anastomoses for sorting into the corresponding group[11]. Cerebrovascular disease was defined as any radiographically diagnosed flow-limiting intra- or extracranial cerebrovascular stenosis or occlusion. Likewise, peripheral vascular disease was defined as any radiographically diagnosed flow-limiting peripheral vascular stenosis or occlusion. Coronary artery disease was defined as any treated or untreated flow-limiting coronary artery obstruction diagnosed by coronary angiography. Chronic kidney disease was defined as a decreased glomerular filtration rate of less than 60 mL/min (1.73 m2) for at least 3 months[12]. Acute kidney failure was defined as a three-fold increase in serum creatinine or urine output of less than 0.5 mL per kg body weight per hour for 24 hours (h)[13]. Sepsis was defined according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3)[14]. Low cardiac output syndrome was defined as a cardiac index <2.2 L/min per body surface area in m2 with systolic blood pressure of <90 mmHg or recurrent catecholamine dependency. The necessity of re-intubation or non-invasive ventilation after previous spontaneous breathing was defined as respiratory failure.

Operative technique

Thoracoabdominal aortic replacement was performed as described in greater detail previously[15]. In brief, a left-sided thoracotomy, starting in the fifth to seventh intercostal space depending on the aneurysm location, was used. The incision was then extended to a paramedian laparotomy, accessing the abdominal aorta in the retroperitoneum. After establishing cardiopulmonary bypass and cooling, proximal and distal clamping was commenced, and graft implantation was performed according to the extent of the aortic disease. Intercostal, lumbar, and visceral arteries were reimplanted when needed and suitable. After rewarming, deairing, weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, and achieving hemostasis, the patients were transferred to the ICU for further stabilization.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 1989, 2021). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess continuous variables for normal distribution. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the T-test, while data not meeting normal distribution criteria are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables are reported as absolute numbers (n) and relative percentages. Long-term survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and survival curve comparison was performed using Log-rank testing. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Propensity score matching identified 68 matches (136 patients), representing 44% of the initial study population and including 94.4% of patients aged 70 or older from the demander cases. The mean age of the younger group was 58.8 ± 9.6 years, with a minimum age of 20 years and a maximum age of 69 years, compared to a mean age of 73 years, ranging from 70 to 83 years, in the elderly group (P < 0.001). Concerning the matched preoperative patient characteristics and comorbidities, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups after propensity score matching (Table 1). With respect to the Crawford extents of the repair, no significant differences were found, with the exception of a higher rate of Crawford type III operations in the elderly group (P = 0.009, Table 2). In the early period between 2000 and 2010, adverse events or early mortality occurred in 64.1% of patients younger than 70 years and in 64.6% of patients aged 70 or older (P = 0.955). In the late period between 2011 and 2024, the combined endpoint of early mortality and adverse postoperative events occurred in 63.2% of patients younger than 70 years and in 69.1% of patients aged 70 or older (P = 0.468). While operation and bypass times were comparable in both groups (Table 3), postoperative ICU stay time was significantly longer in patients aged 70 and older, with 6 days (IQR 3–14 days) versus 3 days (IQR 2–8 days, P = 0.007). With respect to respiratory outcomes, longer ventilation times were observed in elderly patients, although this did not reach statistical significance (Table 4). Coherent with that, the rate of respiratory failure was significantly higher in patients above 70 years of age, with 47.1% (n = 32) versus 25.0% (n = 17; Fig. 2A). Likewise, higher rates of acute perioperative kidney failure (Fig. 2B) and new-onset atrial fibrillation were observed compared to younger patients. No differences were found in the rates of neurological complications, with equal postoperative stroke and paraparesis rates in both groups. Wound infections tended to be more frequent in elderly patients, with 8.8% (n = 6) versus 4.4% (n = 3), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. A tendency toward higher rates of postoperative cardiac low output syndrome was noted, and two patients in the elderly group required postoperative extracorporeal mechanical circulation support (ECMO), while no ECMO therapy was required among the younger patients. Discussion

With a median ICU stay of 3 days (IQR 2–8 days), the ICU time in the younger cohort was similar to that reported in an experience report of over 800 patients after thoracoabdominal repair by the Coselli group, who reported a median ICU stay of 4 days in their cohort[16]. In contrast, the median intensive care time was twice as long in elderly patients compared to the younger control group. This finding is consistent with previous reports on valvular and coronary cardiac operations, which identified age as an independent risk factor for prolonged ICU stay times[17,18]. Furthermore, in a large database study by Jones et al, including over 200 000 patients in the British National Institute for Cardiovascular Outcomes Research database, an age over 70 years was also associated with longer ICU times[19]. Our findings suggest that these observations may also be extrapolated to thoracoabdominal aortic repairs. One of the main factors that may have contributed to the longer ICU length of stay is the higher incidence of postoperative respiratory complications observed in patients aged 70 or older at the time of the operation. Previous studies showed that chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and tobacco smoking adversely impact outcomes after thoracoabdominal repair[20]. However, the difference observed in our study cannot be explained by differences in preoperative risk factors or comorbidities, since propensity score matching adjusted for the rate of tobacco smoking and the incidence of COPD, which did not differ significantly between the two study cohorts (Table 1). Likewise, operation and bypass times, which have also been shown to affect respiratory outcomes[21], did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 3). The finding that higher age is associated with adverse respiratory outcomes after thoracoabdominal repair is consistent with findings by Songdechakraiwut et al, who identified an age above 70 years as an independent risk factor for postoperative tracheostomy after thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm repair[22].

New-onset acute renal failure was also observed more frequently in the elderly group, resulting in higher rates of postoperative dialysis compared to younger patients (Table 4). These findings are consistent with a previous report by Ahmad et al, who identified age as an independent predictor for impaired renal recovery after cardiac operations[23]. Saydy et al reported arterial hypertension, tobacco smoking, and a lower left ventricular ejection fraction as risk factors for postoperative renal failure in elderly patients undergoing cardiac surgery[24]. Accordingly, the study groups investigated here have been matched for the rate of hypertension and tobacco smoking via propensity score matching and thus did not differ significantly between younger and elderly patients (Table 1). On the other hand, left ventricular ejection fraction was not specifically matched. Thus, differences in cardiac function might have influenced the renal outcome in this study as well.

The propensity score matching identified 68 one-on-one pairs with overall comparable comorbidity burdens. However, statistically significant differences were present in the rate of Crawford type 3 repairs between the two groups after propensity score matching. This is most likely due to the comparatively large number of predicting parameters included in the matching, as well as the match tolerance of 0.2. This comparatively high threshold for the match tolerance was chosen to generate as many matches as possible for subsequent analysis, while tolerating isolated differences between the groups. Since the Crawford extent 3 is representative of neither a particularly large nor a particularly small operation compared to the other extents, we do not assume that there is excessive bias due to the unequal distribution between the groups.

As expected, long-term survival was lower in the older study population compared to the younger group. While this result is not surprising, the general long-term survival perspective is of interest for elderly patients and relevant to the decision-making process when evaluating elderly patients for thoracoabdominal aortic replacement surgery.

With a 30-day mortality of 13.3% and 17.6%, respectively (P = 0.636), short-term survival was within the upper margin of the expected mortality range for open thoracoabdominal aortic repair[4,5]. However, these rates are comparatively high compared to the mean mortality rates of 10%–11% in other experienced centers[3,9]. This divergence is most likely due to comparatively high rates of comorbidities and especially a higher burden of concomitant cardiovascular diseases in both groups after propensity score matching: over one-third of patients in each group had undergone prior cardiac surgery before thoracoabdominal aortic repair, and around half of the patients had aortic preoperations (Table 1). Likewise, the incidence of coronary artery disease as a comorbidity was extraordinarily high in our matched cohort, with 36.8% for the younger patients and 47.1% for the elderly. In contrast, the vast majority of studies included in a large meta-analysis by Khan et al reported coronary artery disease rates below 30%. In light of these observations, it appears that it is rather the comorbidity burden that is responsible for the comparatively high rates of postoperative mortality, while age ≥70 years alone was not associated with excess mortality.

Limitations

As this is a retrospective single-center analysis of a highly specialized and comparatively infrequent operation, some study-inherent limitations have to be considered. The inclusion period of 24 years is comparatively long, resulting in possible temporal bias due to changes in operative techniques and perioperative care over time. However, no clear indication of temporal bias was found in the time-stratified sensitivity analysis concerning the combined endpoint of adverse postoperative events and early mortality. In addition, a large proportion of patients under the age of 70 were excluded under propensity score matching, resulting in potential selection bias.

Conclusions

Patients ≥70 years of age were prone to a more complicated postoperative course after thoracoabdominal aortic repair, resulting in significantly longer ICU stay times compared to younger patients. However, short-term survival was comparable in both groups after propensity score matching and, thus, adjustment for comorbidities and, within limits, frailty factors. Therefore, we conclude that it is not the age per se but the comorbidity burden that is associated with postoperative mortality after thoracoabdominal aortic repair. Consequently, the decision on whether a patient is a suitable candidate for open thoracoabdominal repair cannot be made based on a simple age cutoff-value. It must, rather, be a patient-specific decision accounting for the individual risk factor profile.
