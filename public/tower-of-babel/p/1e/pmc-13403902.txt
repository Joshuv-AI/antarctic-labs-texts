Multi-level precise regulation of gene transcription in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae based on light-sensitive CRISPR/Cas systems
Authors: Yaokun Liang, Xianghui Qi, Song Gao, Yunjiao Wang, Guoshi Kan, Leonid N Valentovich, Jingjing Guo, Yingfeng An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRegulation of gene transcription based on clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) is a powerful tool for constructing synthetic gene circuits in Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The current CRISPR-based regulatory approaches primarily focus on inhibiting the binding of dCas9 protein to single guide RNA (sgRNA) or blocking target site recognition. However, these regulation strategies are often at a single level, and their sensitivity still needs to be improved. In this study, the gene regulatory approaches at the translational and post-translational levels were integrated with optogenetic control patterns to attain very sensitive multi-level precision regulation of the dCas9 protein, thereby facilitating flexible regulation of transcription levels of target genes. This strategy was used to regulate the transcription levels of fluorescent proteins, resulting in up to 2.58-fold increase in the fluorescence intensity of mCherry compared to that without regulation. This CRISPR-based multi-level optogenetic system should be extremely helpful in understanding gene regulatory networks and in designing robust genetic circuits for synthetic biology.

Introduction

Over the past decade, synthetic biology has shown rapid development across a range of disciplines, including metabolic engineering, medical applications, agricultural innovations, environmental remediation, and biofuel production [1, 2]. To obtain different functionalities, cells are modified with DNA circuits that are often gene transcriptional networks. CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) systems, which originate from the prokaryotic adaptive immune response, have been repurposed for a wide range of biotechnological applications, including the regulation of gene transcription in both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms. Novel transcription factors that can bind almost every DNA sequence are built on dCas9, i.e. the nuclease-deficient version of Cas9 that is realized via two mutations, D10A and H840A, in the HNH and RuvC domains [3]. By fusing dSpCas9 with either activation domains (ADs) or repression domains, up- or downregulation is achieved if dSpCas9:sgRNA targets an appropriate site along a promoter sequence [4, 5]. The yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a model organism for metabolic engineering and protein engineering. CRISPR-based systems have been broadly employed to modulate transcription in S. cerevisiae, enabling both activation (CRISPRa) and repression (CRISPRi) of target genes. Compared with protein-based modulators, CRISPRi offers multiple distinct advantages for synthetic circuit design: it is highly programmable—requiring only guide RNA (gRNA) sequence redesign; exhibits exceptional orthogonality with negligible cross-talk; supports straightforward multiplexing via multiple gRNAs co-expressed with a single dCas9; achieves robust, dose-dependent repression; functions independently of target gene copy number; imposes minimal metabolic burden on the host cell; and can be engineered for reversibility and dynamic, real-time control [3].

Gene expression regulation using CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) has become a powerful approach for constructing synthetic gene circuits in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic systems. However, the absence of cooperative binding in this system has been identified as a key limitation that may hinder the development of dynamic or multistable genetic networks. In the past few years, there has been increasing interest in developing synthetic circuits based on CRISPRi [6–11]. However, effective tools capable of flexible application in gene regulation remain limited [12]. Once synthesized, the regulatory effect of dCas9 ends only when the sgRNA or protein is diluted during cell division or degraded. Several methods have been adopted to control the activities of sgRNAs or dCas9 after their formation. One approach involves the synthesis of phage anti-CRISPR proteins. It was reported that anti-CRISPRs can be utilized to modulate CRISPR-based gene regulation circuits through the implementation of a pulse generator circuit [12]. Type II anti-CRISPR proteins (AcrIIs) can be employed to precisely regulate and inhibit the binding of CRISPR-dCas9 to DNA, thereby enabling controlled modulation of gene transcription [4, 12]. AcrIIA2 and AcrIIA4, identified in Listeria monocytogenes prophages [13], have been demonstrated to effectively abrogate the ability of (d)Cas9 to bind target DNA. Moreover, AcrIIA2 and AcrIIA4 share an identical inhibitory mechanism: they function as structural mimics of DNA, specifically mimicking the PAM sequence, and bind to the sgRNA-loaded Cas9 complex (Cas9:sgRNA) with higher specificity than target DNA, thereby outcompeting DNA binding. However, they are unable to dislodge target DNA once the ternary complex Cas9:sgRNA–DNA has been formed [14–17]. AcrIIA2 and AcrIIA4 are limited by their narrow spectrum of activity, as they specifically target only the II-A subtype and inhibit a small subset of Cas9 homologs. In addition, these two inhibitors hinder DNA association via binding to the PAM-interacting domain and fail to displace pre-bound target DNA from the Cas9:sgRNA–DNA ternary complex (i.e. they function prior to DNA binding). In contrast, AcrIIA5, discovered in the virulent phages of Streptococcus thermophilus [18], functions as a broad-spectrum inhibitor effective against diverse type II CRISPR-Cas systems. AcrIIA5 can suppress gene editing caused by many Cas9 homologs that belong to type II-A, type II-B, and type IIC [19, 20]. Mechanistically, AcrIIA5 does not remove the target DNA once the ternary complex is formed; instead, it binds to the pre-formed Cas9:sgRNA–DNA complex and blocks DNA cleavage while leaving DNA binding unaffected [20]. In addition, it is capable of abolishing the function of CRISPR-dCas9-mediated gene transcriptional regulation in S. cerevisiae [4, 12].

In addition, optical control of Cas9 also helps to confine gene editing or transcriptional regulation to the desired time and place. Various optogenetic systems have been developed in engineered S. cerevisiae to introduce improved synthesis of valuable products [21]. For example, the VPAD-EL222-based OptoEXP transcription system induced by blue light can be utilized to boost the biosynthesis of high-value products in S. cerevisiae [21]. Moreover, the optical control systems of VPAD-EL222 (i.e. VP16-EL222-C120 and VP64-EL222-C120) have been constructed to activate the transcription of the gene [22]. Light-controlled dimerization serves as a leading optogenetic method for CRISPR-based transcriptional regulation. By using precisely timed light stimulation to trigger protein assembly, these systems afford tight spatiotemporal control over endogenous gene expression: temporal resolution is on the order of minutes, and spatial regulation is achieved by projecting defined light patterns onto cell cultures to confine gene activation exclusively to illuminated regions [23, 24]. Such features render these tools well suited for applications including CRISPRa. According to the corresponding research in S. cerevisiae and Pichia pastoris, the EL222 contains an helix-turn-helix (HTH) DNA-binding domain along with a photosensitive LOV domain [25]. In contrast, the use of light-regulated CRISPRi systems in S. cerevisiae is still relatively underexplored. Hence, establishing a strategy for inducible control of dCas9 expression and activity would allow more robust and tunable CRISPRi operation, which is essential for achieving precise and effective gene targeting in this yeast model.

The gene expression can be regulated at epigenetic level, transcription level, post-transcriptional level, translation level, and post-translational level. So far, synthetic biology relies entirely or partially on transcriptional regulation, and in contrast, research on the mechanisms of conditional translation regulation is relatively limited. Translation regulators based on RNA-protein complexes (RNPs) are powerful tools for regulating gene expression [26, 27]. Moreover, many RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in cells can bind to RNAs and alter the function or fate of the RNAs [28, 29]. Light-switchable RBPs have been engineered to bind to specific RNA motifs in a light-dependent style, enabling spatiotemporal control of RNA metabolism either directly or via fusion with RNA effector domains [30–32]. Currently, optogenetic tools applied in bacterial and yeast synthetic biology mainly operate at the transcriptional level to modulate gene expression, but they generally do not incorporate coordinated multi-layer regulatory mechanisms. It was found that an intriguing mechanism of translation regulation exists in archaea, which involves the interaction between ribosomal protein L7Ae and its corresponding structural gene transcripts [33]. The L7Ae:k-turn system involves an interaction between the kink-turn (k-turn) motif in RNA and L7Ae. The interaction between the box C/D k-turn motif and L7Ae has been shown to modulate the translation levels in vitro and in human cells [27], but has not yet been verified in fungal organisms such as S. cerevisiae.

In this study, the CRISPR-based multi-level optogenetic systems of S. cerevisiae have been developed with complex and efficient gene regulatory networks for designing robust genetic circuits.

Materials and methods

Construction of plasmids for development and optimization of light-sensitive CRISPR systems in S. cerevisiae

Escherichia coli strain DB3.1 was employed for plasmid maintenance and cloning, with recombinant strains cultured at 37°C in Lysogeny broth (LB) supplemented with 100 μg/ml ampicillin. All plasmids used in this study were generated using restriction enzyme–based cloning techniques. Briefly, DNA fragments (e.g. promoter variants, EL222, VP16, VP64, and terminator sequences) are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products and the destination vector were digested with the corresponding restriction endonucleases (e.g. EcoRI, BamHI, SpeI, XhoI; New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Digested fragments were purified from agarose gels using a Gel Extraction Kit (e.g. Qiagen or Thermo Fisher). Ligation reactions were set up with a molar ratio of 3:1 (insert:vector) using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) at 16°C for 16 h. The ligation mixture was then transformed into chemically competent E. coli DB3.1 cells. Transformants were selected on LB–ampicillin plates, and positive clones were verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing (the sequences of all constructed DNA fragments are listed in Supplementary Table S2).

Saccharomyces cerevisiae W303-1A (MATa mal can1-100 ade2-1 his3-11,15 leu2-3,13 trp1-1 ura3-1 ssd1-d) was used as initial strain. The PEG/LiAc protocol was used for yeast transformation [34] regardless of the plasmid type (integrative or episomal). Briefly, yeast cells were grown overnight in YPD medium to an OD600 of 0.8–1.0, harvested, and resuspended in transformation buffer containing 40% PEG3350, 100 mM LiAc, and 10 μg of carrier DNA (salmon sperm). Plasmid DNA (1 μg) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 30°C for 30 min, followed by heat shock at 42°C for 30 min. After transformation, cells were plated on synthetic defined (SD) minimal medium supplemented with 2% glucose and 2% agar, and incubated at 30°C for 2–3 days. The SD medium was prepared without the appropriate amino acids to maintain selection pressure for each plasmid.

Detection of fluorescent proteins regulated by the light-sensitive CRISPR systems in S. cerevisiae

For each constructed strain, a single colony was inoculated into 5 ml of appropriate SD minimal medium (lacking the corresponding auxotrophic marker) containing 2% glucose and grown overnight at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm. The next day, the overnight culture was diluted 1:100 into fresh SD-glucose medium and grown until glucose depletion, which was indicated by the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reaching a plateau (typically 3–6 h). To confirm glucose exhaustion, a glucose test strip was used (or the cells were washed once with sterile water). Cells were then harvested by centrifugation (3000 × g, 5 min), washed twice with sterile water, and resuspended in fresh SC-galactose medium containing 2% galactose (without glucose) to a final OD600 of 0.4. The resuspended cells were immediately subjected to light induction.

For blue light irradiation, blue LED panels (460 nm, 5 W·m−2) were placed 20 cm from shaking flasks for cultivating yeast. The LEDs were programmed to deliver a 70 s/30 s cyclic light–dark schedule using a digital timer. Control samples were kept in complete darkness (aluminum foil-wrapped flasks). The cycle of blue light irradiation was set using a Programmable Logic Controller of Digital Timer Switch OHR-PR10. Then, cells from 1 ml of each culture were pelleted via centrifugation and resuspended in 1 ml of H2O. A 200 μl aliquot of the resuspended sample was then transferred to a Black Cliniplate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and fluorescence measurements were conducted using Biotek Cytation 5. The excitation/emission wavelengths for measurement of mEYFP and mCherry were 510 nm/527 nm and 587 nm/610 nm, respectively, with 9 and 10 nm bandwidths and 500 ms measurement time used in each measurement. The OD600 values were measured by BioTek with transparent microtiter plates (Nunc 96F, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell density of each strain, shown as an arbitrary unit was quantified after normalization with the OD600 value. The above experimental values were measured three times, and the standard deviation of the mean for each experiment was calculated during the data analysis. Yeast cells of Strain 17 were grown in SD minimal medium (SD-Trp-Ura-Leu) at 30°C for 16 h and then diluted to OD600 = 0.4 with fresh SC-galactose medium containing 2% galactose. The cells were further cultured with 70 s/30 s blue light/dark cycle or dark treatment for 12 h at 30°C and 200 rpm, then washed twice in ddH2O, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline. The confocal fluorescence images of the cells were taken by Nikon laser scanning confocal microscope with 100× objective lens to show mEYFP and mCherry fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated three times, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Pairwise comparisons between two groups were performed using Welch’s t-test. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 5).

Results

Development of VPAD-based light-sensitive system for transcription regulation

Optogenetics is a type of strategy to regulate cellular processes with a combination of genetic manipulation and light stimulation [35]. The EL222 from the marine bacterium Erythrobacter litoralis is a light-oxygen-voltage (LOV) type protein with photosensitiveness [36]. The rearrangement of EL222 triggered by 450-nm blue light results in dimerization through the exposure of the C-terminal HTH DNA-binding effector, which is concealed on the surface of the photosensory LOV domain [37], allowing its binding to a cognate DNA sequence of C120 (the EL222-binding Clone 1–20 bp) [38, 39]. VPAD-EL222 is a fusion protein composed of VPAD and EL222, where EL222 is a fusion of LOV and HTH, and VPAD (i.e. VP16 or VP64) is a type of protein factor capable of activating the promoter. The constitutively synthesized VPAD-EL222 exists as a monomer under dark conditions, but undergoes conformational changes under the influence of blue light and forms stable dimers through protein–protein interactions. In the dark, the 4α helix of HTH essential to dimerization and DNA binding should be covered due to the binding of the LOV domain to the HTH domain [40], while blue light can trigger the photochemical formation of a protein/flavin adduct within the LOV domain. Under the irradiation of 450-nm blue light, the inhibitory interactions between the LOV and HTH domains will be disrupted, allowing dimerization of EL222 and binding of EL222 to DNA [41, 42]. As a result, the HTH domain in the VPAD-EL222 dimer specifically binds to the PC120 region to achieve the gene transcription with the assistance of VPAD. The VPAD-EL222 systems could control the transcription level merely by adjusting the intensity and duration of blue light irradiation, thus making the operation very convenient.

An EL222 light-inducible system was first constructed in S. cerevisiae via fusion with diverse transcriptional ADs (Fig. 1a). The VPAD-EL222 system, consisting of two variants: VP16-EL222-C120 and VP64-EL222-C120, enables precise light-dependent induction of mEYFP transcription. These two optogenetic constructs are defined as follows: each comprises the blue-light-sensing transcription factor EL222 fused to either the VP16 or VP64 AD, with expression controlled by the C120 promoter, hence named VP16-EL222-C120 and VP64-EL222-C120, respectively (Fig. 1a and b). The efficiency of this regulatory system has been verified experimentally, and the activation efficiency of the VPAD-EL222 system has been visualized. As shown in Fig. 1c, under blue-light irradiation, the fluorescence intensity of mEYFP was increased by 62.34-fold and 74.64-fold for the VP16-EL222 and VP64-EL222 systems, respectively, compared with those under dark conditions. The VPAD-EL222 system efficiently activated the CYC1 promoter in S. cerevisiae. VP64 and VP16 performed similarly in the dark and under light. No significant difference was detected under light (P = .14), with VP64 showing a modestly higher mean. Fusing EL222 with the viral VPAD and introducing the repeated minimal promoter PC120 to activate the CYC1 promoter enables blue-light-induced gene expression in S. cerevisiae. To evaluate whether light-induced upregulation can be reversed following blue-light cessation, a reversal experiment was conducted. Cells were illuminated with blue light for 10 h to induce target expression before being cultured under full darkness for 24 h. As presented in Supplementary Fig. S1, fluorescence intensity was maintained at a low, yet detectable, level after 24 h of dark incubation. It is indicated that the system is largely restored to the original uninduced state upon blue-light removal, while weak residual fluorescence is still retained after 24 h in the dark. Therefore, VP-EL222-driven transcriptional activation is reversible; full recovery to baseline may require prolonged dark cultivation or can be attributed to low-level leaky expression.

Optimization of the VPAD-EL222- and L7Ae:k-turn-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system

The CRISPR system, originally identified as a prokaryotic adaptive immune mechanism, is widely utilized as a robust platform for building RNA-guided synthetic circuits [43]. In addition to the VPAD-EL222 system, the dCas9-based CRISPR systems have also been widely used to regulate the transcriptional levels of genes in S. cerevisiae [3]. However, previous regulation of gene transcription was often limited to a single level and a single mechanism. For instance, many inducible promoters suffer from leaky expression in the “off” state, and permanent genome editing strategies are inherently irreversible [44, 45]. Therefore, the combination of different genetic regulatory elements to construct genetic switches and achieve more precise and sensitive transcriptional regulation should be of great significance. In this study, the CRISPRi system based on dCas9 was constructed to regulate the transcription level of mCherry. In this system, the transcription of mCherry was under the action of the constitutive TEF1 promoter, while sgRNAs were designed to target the core region of the TEF1 promoter. Targeting of the sgRNA–dCas9 complex to the mCherry promoter region led to transcriptional repression. Then the optimal sgRNA was determined by measuring the expression level of the mCherry protein (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Building on the aforementioned VPAD-EL222 systems, a light-sensitive CRISPR platform incorporating L7Ae:k-turn translational regulators was developed. As shown in Fig. 2A, this work reports the first application of the L7Ae:k-turn RNP switch for optically controlled protein expression in S. cerevisiae, extending this translational regulation system from prokaryotes and mammalian cells to a yeast chassis. A 23-nt sequence known as the k-turn motif was fused with the N-terminus of dCas9, and the transcription product of k-turn-dCas9 messenger RNA (mRNA) should form L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 complex by the interaction of L7Ae protein with the k-turn region of mRNA. Under dark conditions, expression of k-turn-dCas9 was driven by the galactose-inducible GAL1 promoter. The sgRNA-guided complex was then directed to the TEF1 promoter, where transcription of mCherry RNA was inhibited, leading to suppression of protein expression. Concurrently, the VPAD-EL222 system was employed to induce expression of the k-turn-binding protein L7Ae in S. cerevisiae. Upon blue-light exposure, L7Ae-P2A-mEYFP was expressed under control of the C120 and CYC1 promoters. The mEYFP reporter served to monitor L7Ae expression and to evaluate the activation efficiency of the VPAD-EL222 system. The L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 complex could inhibit the expression of k-turn-dCas9 at the translation level. Furthermore, transcription of L7Ae could be modulated in a blue-light-dependent manner via the VPAD-EL222 system. This allowed optogenetic regulation of k-turn-dCas9 mRNA and established a tightly controlled, light-inducible CRISPRi platform (Fig. 2b).

The effectiveness of this comprehensive strategy has been verified through the regulation of dCas9 at transcription and translation levels, thereby facilitating the regulation of the transcription level of mCherry. The VP16-EL222-C120-k-turn-dCas9 (Strain 1), VP16-EL222-C120-L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 (Strain 2), VP64-EL222-C120-k-turn-dCas9 (Strain 3), and VP64-EL222-C120-L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 (Strain 4) with or without L7Ae were constructed to detect the effectiveness of regulating mCherry transcription with this strategy. The results showed that for Strain 2 and Strain 4, the mCherry fluorescence levels under blue-light irradiation were 1.57-fold and 1.59-fold higher, respectively, than those under dark conditions, while the difference in mCherry fluorescence levels between Strain 1 and Strain 3 was not significant under the same conditions (P = .85) (Fig. 2c). This result was consistent with expectation, indicating that the formation of L7Ae:k-turn interaction translational regulators could efficiently regulate the expression of the target gene at the translation level, and the construction of the k-turn-dCas9 fusion gene did not affect the regulation function of the dCas9 protein. In addition, unlike the results of the former study [46], there is no significant difference between these two elements (Fig. 2c). Additionally, whether the growth of S. cerevisiae was affected by these regulatory systems was investigated. The results showed that there was no significant difference in the growth tendency of the four strains under either blue-light or dark conditions, indicating that these regulatory systems had no significant effect on yeast growth. However, the growth tendencies of the strains under dark conditions were significantly better than those under blue-light irradiation. A likely explanation is blue-light-induced oxidative stress and respiratory impairment [47]. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that the blue-light irradiation conditions need to be optimized to achieve better regulatory efficiency and more flexible optical control without significantly compromising strain growth (Fig. 2d).

Optimization of the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive regulatory system

Expression of L7Ae in the cells led to concentration-dependent repression of box C/D-mRNA translation [27]. Given the rapid deactivation kinetics of the VP-EL222 system—which terminates transcription with a τ_off of < 50 s upon light cessation [21, 39]—and the relatively low activity of PC120, the expression level of L7Ae under blue-light illumination required enhancement. Studies revealed that augmenting the copy number of the C120 promoter could lead to superior blue-light-dependent transcriptional activation [37, 41].

In this study, the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory systems with optimized PC120 copy numbers (i.e. 2, 4, and 6) were used for transcriptional activation of L7Ae-P2A-mEYFP and recovery of transcriptional inhibition of mCherry (Fig. 3a). As a result, the VP16-EL222-based system increased the fluorescence level of mEYFP by 91.23-fold, 127.09-fold, and 127.19-fold in the presence of 2, 4, and 6 copies of PC120, respectively, while the VP64-EL222-based system increased the fluorescence level of mEYFP by 79.25-fold, 115.78-fold, and 127.94-fold under the same conditions (Fig. 3b and c). This result suggested that the increase in the copy number of PC120 can significantly enhance the activation effect of the VPAD-EL222-based system on the transcriptional level of L7Ae-P2A-mEYFP. Pairwise Welch’s t-tests were conducted to systematically evaluate the effects of two promoters (4 × C120 and 6 × C120) and two effector systems (VP16-EL222 and VP64-EL222) on light-induced activation. Slightly higher fluorescence was detected with the 4 × C120 promoter relative to 6 × C120 in the VP16 background, while no statistically significant difference was found (P = .76). An opposite trend was observed in the VP64 background, where marginally stronger fluorescence was obtained with 6 × C120; likewise, the difference was not statistically significant (P = .19). Increasing the copy number to six did not significantly improve activation efficiency. For the VP64 activation protein, peak activity was reached with six PC120 copies, though no significant enhancement was observed compared to the four-copy construct (Fig. 3b and c).

Optimization of the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system

Gene transcription activation was not effectively enhanced by increasing the PC120 copy number. Excessive tandem repetition of PC120 may also compromise system stability. In the subsequent section, the regulatory performance of 4 × C120 and 6 × C120 was systematically compared within a blue-light-inducible system integrating VP16-EL222 and VP64-EL222 for CRISPRi.

In yeast cells containing the k-turn-dCas9 CRISPRi system, expression of the RNA-binding protein L7Ae was induced by blue light (Fig. 4a). Comparative evaluation of blue-light-responsive systems fused to different activator domains revealed that, relative to controls, mCherry fluorescence was elevated 1.68-, 1.79-, and 1.69-fold in the VP16-EL222 system with 2, 4, and 6 copies of PC120, respectively. Under the same conditions, the VP64-EL222 system showed increases of 1.62-, 1.68-, and 1.73-fold, respectively (Fig. 4b). In the S2 background (VP16-EL222), the 4 × C120 promoter produced a 1.14-fold higher fluorescence recovery than the 1 × C120 promoter under blue light; however, Welch’s t-test showed this difference was not significant (P = .11). In the S4 background (VP64-EL222), the 6 × C120 promoter gave a 1.08-fold increase relative to 1 × C120 (P = .28). Although neither increase was statistically significant, the VP16-EL222 system with the 4 × C120 promoter was selected for subsequent experiments. Therefore, the copy number of PC120 and the choice of VP64 or VP16 were not crucial for the recovery of mCherry transcriptional inhibition. The recovery levels of mCherry transcriptional inhibition were significantly lower than the activation levels of mEYFP transcription, and further optimization should be required to improve the recovery level of mCherry transcriptional inhibition.

It was reported that gene expression levels of light-switchable promoter system can be tuned through variations in illumination protocols. For example, the effects of luciferase expression by modifying the duration of the blue light on/off cycle were investigated [21]. In this study, based on the VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 system (Strain 5), the effects of various blue light/dark cycles (i.e. 1 s/99 s, 10 s/90 s, 40 s/60 s, 70 s/30 s and 100 s/0 s) on the transcriptional activation of mEYFP and recovery of mCherry transcriptional repression were investigated. The results indicated that mEYFP transcriptional activation was strongly dependent on blue light, with fluorescence levels increasing significantly in response to higher light intensity. Under blue-light/dark cycles of 70 s/30 s and 100 s/0 s, mEYFP fluorescence was enhanced by 117.95-fold and 127.09-fold, respectively (Fig. 4c). Meanwhile, recovery from mCherry transcriptional inhibition was also enhanced with higher blue-light intensity. When the illumination period was extended to 70 s/30 s, fluorescence recovery reached 1.81-fold relative to the dark control group, exceeding the performance observed under the 100 s/0 s blue-light/dark cycle (Fig. 4d). The impact of different blue-light/dark cycles on yeast growth was analyzed (Fig. 4e), and the results showed that the growth of yeast weakened with the increase of blue-light exposure time. Although fluorescence recovery levels showed no significant difference between yeast cells subjected to 70 s/30 s and 100 s/0 s blue-light/dark cycles, extended blue-light exposure was observed to exert a measurable negative effect on yeast cell activity. Taking into account the impact on both gene transcription levels and yeast cell growth, a 70 s/30 s blue-light/dark cycle was selected for the subsequent research.

The effect of different L7Ae:k-turns on the light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system

The L7Ae-box and C/Dmini interaction can be incorporated into the ORF region of target mRNAs to effectively repress translation. Such RNP-based genetic circuits, which enable cytoplasmic control of protein production, provide a unique means to study and manipulate cell physiology, fate, or differentiation. According to the principle of L7Ae:k-turn-based translational regulatory in bacteria, the structured 5′ untranslated regions (5′UTR) of mRNA are involved for ligand binding [48, 49], and interaction between L7Ae and k-turn should be very crucial. (Fig. 5a) Therefore, different k-turn motifs, including Kt-3, pbox [27], p1box [27], and 2 × Kt of the 2 × k-turn motif [50], were used to replace the k-turn motif of Strain 5 to construct VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae-dCas9 (without the k-turn motif upstream of dCas9 mRNA, Strain 6), VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:Kt-3-dCas9 (Strain 7), VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:pbox-dCas9 (Strain 8), VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:p1box-dCas9 (Strain 9), and VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:2 × Kt-dCas9 (Strain 10), respectively. The sensitivity of different k-turn-dCas9 constructs to L7Ae was evaluated by comparing mCherry fluorescence levels, while the effect of blue light on restoring transcriptional repression in the CRISPRi system was assessed. Among the variants tested, Kt-3 (Strain 7) demonstrated the most pronounced response. After 10 h of 70 s/30 s blue-light/dark cycles, the mCherry fluorescence intensity was 1.95-fold higher than that of the control without blue light irradiation (Fig. 5b). The expression levels of mEYFP in Strains 5–10 were comparable (Supplementary Fig. S3). When k-turn was replaced by 2 × k-turn motif (2 × Kt, Strain 10), the recovery of mCherry fluorescence intensity even decreased. Although higher fluorescence was observed in yeast cells expressing 2 × k-turn-dCas9 under illuminated conditions relative to other k-turn variants, substantially lower CRISPRi efficiency was recorded in the dark compared to other experimental groups. This reduction in performance may be attributed to the tandem duplication of k-turn motifs upstream of the dCas9 mRNA, which could interfere with its proper expression or functionality. To further validate this hypothesis, additional sgRNAs targeting the TEF1 promoter were tested, yielding results consistent with the expected trend (Supplementary Fig. S4). These findings indicate that the inclusion of excessively complex RNA structural elements upstream of the dCas9 mRNA should be avoided to preserve optimal CRISPRi activity. Therefore, the construct based on the Kt-3 sequence (Strain 7) was used for subsequent studies.

Shifting focus from steady-state measurements to the kinetics of gene expression, how gene expression recovery levels vary with illumination duration was investigated. To do so, the mCherry luciferase activities of Strain7 were measured after incubation for 0–24 h of 70 s/30 s blue-light/dark cycle. As a result, during the 24-h incubation period, the recovery rate of mCherry fluorescence intensity showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing (Fig. 5c). Under the fixed light/dark cycle of 70 s/30 s, the results of the 9–13 h incubation period indicated that the 12-h treatment yielded the optimal outcome (Fig. 5d). In addition, the blue-light irradiation significantly affected the growth of yeast. During cultivation, the OD600 value of yeast under blue-light irradiation was obviously smaller than that of yeast cultured under dark conditions (Fig. 5e). Therefore, the selection of an appropriate duration of blue-light irradiation requires a comprehensive consideration of the effects of blue light on the transcriptional regulation of target genes and on yeast cell growth.

Optimization of the light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system by the introduction of the anti-CRISPR protein

It was demonstrated that proteins interacting with the k-turn motif could be placed under blue-light-dependent control via VPAD-EL222, allowing precise and tunable optical regulation of the CRISPRi system. To further illustrate the system’s broad applicability and improve the sensitivity of the light-controlled CRISPRi platform, the L7Ae protein was replaced with anti-CRISPR proteins, and the functionality of this light-responsive system was experimentally validated in S. cerevisiae.

The VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system was modified based on VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:k-turn-dCas9 (Strain 5) to construct VP16-EL222-4 × C120-dCas9 (Strain 11). To further modify the dCas9-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system, a type II anti-CRISPR protein AcrIIA2, AcrIIA4, or AcrIIA5 was integrated. The AcrIIA2, AcrIIA4, and AcrIIA5 were introduced into the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system based on Strain 11 to construct VP16-EL222-4 × C120-AcrIIA2:dCas9 (Strain 12), VP16-EL222-4 × C120-AcrIIA4:dCas9 (Strain 13), and VP16-EL222-4 × C120-AcrIIA5:dCas9 (Strain 14), respectively (Fig. 6a). It was expected that the transcription levels of the type II anti-CRISPR protein genes should be regulated by the light-sensitive protein VP16-EL222 under irradiation of blue light, and the anti-CRISPR proteins should interfere with the inhibition of mCherry transcription caused by dCas9 through specific binding to the dCas9 protein or the complex composed of dCas9 proteins. The effect of introducing the anti-CRISPR protein on the recovery efficiency of mCherry transcription inhibition was investigated. Among the four copies of the C120 promoter, the strongest inhibitory effect on the dCas9:sgRNA complex was exhibited by AcrIIA4, as reflected by an mCherry fluorescence recovery rate 2.10-fold higher than its corresponding dark control. AcrIIA2 showed the second-highest effect, with a recovery rate 1.71-fold relative to the control, while AcrIIA5 produced only a modest inhibitory effect, indicating substantially lower anti-CRISPR activity under the same conditions (Fig. 6b). As a result, introducing anti-CRISPR proteins into the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system could lead to an improved recovery level of mCherry transcriptional inhibition.

Therefore, the AcrIIA4 was selected for the subsequent research. In order to achieve multi-level regulation, AcrIIA2 and AcrIIA4 were integrated into the VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae:Kt-3-dCas9 system (Strain 7) to construct VP16-EL222-4 × C120-AcrIIA2-L7Ae:Kt3-dCas9 (Strain 15, Fig. 6c), VP16-EL222-4 × C120-L7Ae-AcrIIA4:Kt3-dCas9 (Strain 16, Fig. 6c), and the VP16-EL222-4 × C120-AcrIIA4-L7Ae:Kt3-dCas9 (Strain 17, Fig. 6c). The only difference between the regulatory systems corresponding to Strain 16 and Strain 17 is the relative position of AcrIIA4 and L7Ae in the fusion gene. The mCherry luciferase activities of the optimized light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory systems were measured after incubation for 12 h of 70 s/30 s blue-light/dark cycles. As a result, the Strain 16 and Strain 17, with AcrIIA4 into the VPAD-EL222-based light-sensitive CRISPR regulatory system, could lead to up to 2.41-fold and 2.58-fold improved recovery levels of mCherry transcriptional inhibition, respectively, compared with that of the control (Fig. 6d). The introduction of AcrIIA2 did not substantially restore transcriptional repression mediated by CRISPRi. This finding aligns with prior experimental results, which indicated that AcrIIA2 exhibits weaker inhibitory activity against the dCas9:sgRNA complex compared to AcrIIA4. On the other hand, the expression levels of the mEYFP were not affected by these regulation systems in Strain 11 to Strain 17 (Supplementary Fig. S5). These recovery levels were higher than those of both Strain 7 and Strain 13, indicating that AcrIIA4 and L7Ae:Kt-3 may have a certain cumulative in increasing the recovery level of mCherry transcriptional inhibition. Compared with the negative control Strain 11 without any regulatory system, the integrated regulatory system (Strain 17) achieved 2.61-fold higher recovery efficiency of mCherry fluorescence intensity. To further validate the optimized light-sensitive CRISPR-based regulatory system (Strain 17), after 12 h of 70 s/30 s blue-light/dark cycles or dark treatment, the synthesis of mEYFP protein and the recovery of mCherry transcriptional inhibition were examined using a fluorescence confocal microscope. As a result, only when exposed to blue light, both mEYFP fluorescent and mCherry fluorescent were imaged simultaneously, which was consistent with expectation (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Discussion

We engineered a blue-light-responsive CRISPR system in yeast enabling simultaneous transcriptional repression and translational interference. Using a blue-light-sensitive CRISPR-based system allows for more precise regulation of the transcription levels of key genes by conveniently controlling the duration and intensity of blue light irradiation. However, such irradiation has a certain impact on the growth potential and biomass accumulation of yeast, and the intensity and frequency of blue-light irradiation commonly have a certain impact on cell growth. While our study focused on the design and characterization of specific optogenetic circuits, we acknowledge that blue light may potentially affect the expression of other endogenous genes. Future work should include a systematic transcriptomic comparison (e.g. RNA-seq) of yeast cells grown under blue-light versus dark conditions to fully evaluate the specificity and orthogonality of light-based regulation. Such analysis would also help dissect the contribution of oxidative stress responses to the observed growth phenotype. Therefore, optimizing these factors is obviously necessary.

It should be noted that in our system, blue light inhibits the translation of dCas9 (via the L7Ae-kink-turn switch) but does not trigger degradation of pre-existing dCas9 protein. Consequently, any dCas9 protein that was already present in the cell before light exposure remains active and can still repress target gene expression to some extent. This explains why the derepression of mCherry is incomplete under blue light, and why the system does not achieve full reversal of protein expression levels. Our design focuses on translational control of dCas9, not post-translational control. Future work could address this limitation by fusing a degradation tag (e.g. ecDHFR or ubiquitin) to dCas9 to accelerate its turnover upon light-induced translation inhibition [51].

In this study, it was found that culturing for 0–12 h with 70 s/30 s blue light/dark cycle was beneficial for the recovery of mCherry transcriptional inhibition. Perhaps in the early stages of fermentation, blue light has a relatively small damaging effect on cells, which should be beneficial for regulating the transcription level of the target genes. In addition, this result is also based on the conclusion obtained from fermentation based on a 100-ml shake flask, and further optimization should be needed when using this regulatory system in larger fermentation systems. In addition, the prerequisite for using the blue-light-sensitive CRISPR-based system is that the surface of the fermentation tank or shake flask can transmit blue light, which poses a great challenge for the use of this system in large fermentation tanks. If this system is used during the fermentation process based on the large fermentation tanks, a sophisticated modification of the traditional equipment should be required by introducing blue-light sources inside. For future scale-up in large fermentation tanks, the impact of blue-light penetration should be assessed by measuring light attenuation at different cell densities and tank sizes. Potential solutions include internal LED arrays or optical fibers, but their design would require optimization of light distribution, heat dissipation, and mixing compatibility. These engineering considerations are critical for industrial application.

At present, the regulation of gene expression in most organisms, including yeast, is achieved at the transcriptional level, such as through the binding of dCas9 to target genes or their promoter sequences to inhibit or activate gene transcription. It is known that there are currently no reports on the simultaneous regulation of target genes at the transcriptional and translational levels through the blue-light-sensitive CRISPRi-based system in yeast. In this study, translational regulation of k-turn-dCas9 via the RNA-binding protein L7Ae, combined with the use of anti-CRISPR proteins for target gene modulation, was found to achieve higher efficacy than the application of either strategy alone, suggesting a synergistic interaction between these regulatory approaches. This result provides a new tool for precise regulation of the expression levels of target genes in S. cerevisiae, as well as provides new insights for the modification of other regulatory systems. However, the results showed that the joint use of multiple regulatory components may not necessarily result in synergistic effects, and the influencing factors may be complex. Therefore, it is often necessary to optimize the system when a combination of regulatory components is performed. For example, multiple regulatory components were expressed in series, separated by P2A, and then independent regulatory components should be introduced through the self-cleavage function of P2A. Whether the P2A can perfectly perform self-cleavage and whether the cleaved protein has the correct spatial structure may be important factors that affecting the level of regulation. These results suggested that the regulatory system may have broad application prospects in more fields. In addition, the results of this study may have important reference value for establishing multi-level regulatory systems in other biological cells.

Light has been used to study various complex biological processes in living cells due to its capability of precise temporal and spatial manipulation. Various optogenetic tools have been applied to regulate protein activities, metabolism, and interactions [52]. Among these tools, light-switchable regulation of gene expression represents a versatile strategy that can be widely applied in various systems. In this work, the engineered version of the VPAD-EL222 system was used to control the function of the CRISPR/dCas9 system for the regulation of gene transcription in S. cerevisiae under blue-light irradiation. Previous studies have shown that VP64 is significantly more effective than VP16 in initiating transcription. However, in this study, it was found that in many cases the efficiencies of VP16 and VP64 were not significantly different, and in some cases, VP16 was even better than VP64. These results suggest that the proper VPAD may need to be chosen based on the actual situation. Although there have been many studies on the regulation of gene transcription based on the CRISPR/dCas9 system, there are still few studies on the regulation of gene transcription in S. cerevisiae based on light-sensitive CRISPR.

To sum up, in this study, a blue-light-sensitive CRISPR-based system has been established in yeast cells, enabling precise and tunable regulation of the CRISPRi system through multi-level coordinated control under blue-light illumination. This strategy was used to regulate the transcription levels of fluorescent proteins, resulting in up to 261% increase in the fluorescence intensity of mCherry compared to that without regulation. This technology enables more flexible application of the CRISPR system in S. cerevisiae. Moreover, successful validation using the mCherry fluorescent protein provides a foundation for the future flexible expression of other functional proteins in this organism. This system should be widely used to design genetic circuits for synthetic biology and to understand gene regulatory networks, etc.
