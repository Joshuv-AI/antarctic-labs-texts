SuSha: A multi-model ensemble learning framework for predicting microbial salinity adaptation
Authors: Siwei Ren, Shijie Ren, Hongjian Chen, Wenhao Zhang, Tao Zhang, Hengyao Chong, Zihao Wang, Weiyu Cao, Xiaoyu Yong, Jun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current research on microbial salinity adaptation faces substantial challenges, including the limited predictive accuracy of traditional single-gene models and difficulty in dissecting systemic biological responses to salinity stress in complex natural habitats. To overcome these bottlenecks, the multi-model ensemble learning tool SuSha, which leverages genome-wide amino acid composition features, was developed. By extracting features from the whole-genome data of 123 bacterial and archaeal species with well-defined salinity adaptations, a 24-dimensional feature vector was constructed, comprising the frequencies of 20 standard amino acids and four aggregated functional categories. Based on this, an ensemble model was developed by integrating algorithms such as random forest, bagging, and extra trees. Five-fold cross-validation demonstrated that this 24-dimensional feature-based ensemble model achieved a global accuracy of 0.765 and an area under the curve of 0.941, significantly outperforming individual baseline models. Furthermore, the model was externally validated using 2678 metagenomic samples from six global regions, encompassing freshwater, marine, and hypersaline habitats. SuSha exhibited high robustness, ecological consistency across diverse salinity gradients, and a classification accuracy of over 90% for extreme halophiles, particularly within the extreme halophilic range. By enabling high-precision genotype-to-phenotype predictions using a habitat-adaptive algorithm-switching strategy, SuSha provides a robust computational framework for inferring the physiological potential of uncultivated microorganisms and mining microbial resources in extreme environments.

Introduction

Research on microbial salinity adaptation is currently undergoing a critical transition from traditional single-strain analyses to cross-habitat multi-omics integration [1]. Although the metabolic pathways of model taxa such as Halobacterium have been preliminarily elucidated, understanding the evolutionary logic driving microbial diversity in complex environments (such as saline-alkali soils, hypersaline waters, and deep-sea brine pools) remains a major bottleneck, particularly in the context of increasing global salinization [2]. Recently, large-scale metagenomic studies, such as the Earth Microbiome Project, have identified salinity as the primary environmental determinant shaping the global distribution of microbial communities [3]. However, constrained by traditional cultivation techniques, over 81% of global microorganisms remain uncultured, despite advances in culturomics [4]. Therefore, determining the unique salinity response networks of uncultured microorganisms is an urgent challenge in microbial ecology [5].

To dissect these adaptation mechanisms, previous studies have attempted to predict microbial salinity tolerance by quantifying the abundance of specific marker genes (e.g., the mnh operon for Na+/H+ antiporters and the osmotic regulator gene proP) [6]. However, prediction models that rely solely on functional gene markers exhibit significant limitations and nonlinear deviations when applied to complex natural habitats. For instance, the deep sequencing of soda lake microbiomes by Vavourakis et al. (2018) [7] revealed that highly salt-tolerant taxa lacked significant enrichment in ion transport genes; instead, their primary adaptation manifested as a highly acidic proteome. Furthermore, Gunde-Cimerman et al. (2018) [8] have pointed out that microbial salinity adaptation involves a dynamic trade-off between “salt-in” and “salt-out” strategies; single-gene abundances fail to capture the structural stability and functional maintenance of the entire proteome under high salt stress. Similarly, in Escherichia coli, the metabolic gene content correlates only weakly with the actual metabolic phenotype [9]. The observation that strains harboring “salt tolerance genes” often fail to exhibit a salt-tolerant phenotype [10] suggests that salinity adaptability is a multilevel systems biology trait holistically regulated by genomic coding potential; it is inherently linked to specific regulatory genes and genome-wide amino acid compositions influenced by evolutionary selection pressures.

To overcome the limitations of single-gene models, this study proposes a cross-taxon phenotypic prediction framework that combines genome-scale amino acid composition features with machine learning algorithms. By mining whole-genome data from 123 well-characterized archaeal and bacterial species, key indicators of salinity adaptation were identified; a multidimensional composite feature vector was constructed. The predictive performance of various machine learning algorithms, including ensemble models such as extra trees (ET) and random forest (RF), was systematically evaluated across different feature spaces. Following rigorous cross-validation of accuracy, F1-score, and area under the curve (AUC) calculations, external validation was conducted using metagenomic data from saltpan, marine, and freshwater habitats. Ultimately, this study established SuSha as a robust, multi-model ensemble learning tool for predicting microbial growth salinity based on genome-wide amino acid profiles.

Data collection and preprocessing

Dataset construction followed a rigorous collection and screening protocol. Initially, the genomic data of strains with documented salinity tolerance from the 2023 Manual of Systematic Identification of Common Bacteria and Archaea [11] were utilized to form the training set and categorized into five classes: salt-sensitive, halotolerant, slight halophiles, moderate halophiles, and extreme halophiles. Corresponding whole-genome protein sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database, prioritizing complete and high-quality genomes. To ensure classification accuracy, species with ambiguous growth salinity ranges or those spanning the exact boundaries of the preset categories were excluded to eliminate borderline uncertainty. Supplementary files for the genomic training set are available on GitHub (https://github.com/rsj99-dev/SuSha).

All input features were subjected to unified standardization to facilitate effective learning and accurate prediction [13]. Standardization eliminates biases caused by different unit scales. Such standardization is particularly crucial for distance-based algorithms (e.g., k-nearest neighbors [k-NN], support vector machine [SVM], and logistic regression [LR]), preventing features with relatively large numerical variances from dominating the gradient descent or similarity computations, thereby ensuring equal feature contributions. Tree-based models (e.g., decision trees [DT], RF) are largely scale-invariant. However, StandardScaler was uniformly applied to maintain consistency across the entire data processing pipeline.

Establishment of a classification model

To ensure a comprehensive performance evaluation despite the constraints of a limited dataset size, a progressive modeling strategy was adopted, spanning three hierarchical levels: basic learners, homogeneous ensembles, and heterogeneous ensembles.

First, a baseline system was established using classical algorithms such as LR, DT, k-NN, naive Bayes, linear discriminant analysis (LDA), and quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA). These models offer strong interpretability, require relatively few parameters, perform well with small sample sizes, and serve as reliable benchmarks [14]. To address potential nonlinear data boundaries, SVMs with kernel functions were introduced to map features onto high-dimensional spaces and enhance complex pattern recognition.

Second, to enhance model stability and accuracy, first-generation homogeneous ensemble frameworks were implemented, including RF, gradient boosting decision tree (GBDT), ET, bagging, and adaptive boosting (AdaBoost) [15]. These methods effectively mitigate the overfitting risks associated with single models by aggregating multiple weak learners in parallel or series. Specifically, RF enhances robustness via bagging; AdaBoost targets hard-to-classify samples by dynamically adjusting the weights; and GBDT iteratively fits the residuals by boosting to refine the predictions.

Finally, to exploit model complementarity and maximize generalization, two heterogeneous ensemble models were constructed: Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag and Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA. The former integrates three tree-based strategies to reinforce nonlinear feature learning. The latter combines nonlinear (ET) and linear (LR and LDA) classifiers to capture linear and nonlinear patterns through the synergistic advantages of heterogeneous learners. This hierarchical architecture systematically evaluates algorithm applicability across complexities, establishing a robust technical pathway for highly accurate predictions under small-sample constraints.

Metagenomic reassembly of environmental samples

To ensure data reliability, raw metagenomic data were reassembled and optimized using state-of-the-art bioinformatics pipelines. Quality-controlled FASTQ files retrieved from the SRA database [16] were assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [17]. Sequence alignment and format conversion were performed using Minimap2 v2.30 [18], followed by binning using SemiBin v2.2.1 [19]. Genome quality assessment, functional annotation, and amino acid sequence extraction were performed using CheckM2 v1.1.0 [20] and Prokka v1.15.6 [21], respectively. This pipeline ensured the accuracy of metagenomic bins (contamination < 5%) and completeness of annotations, providing a high-quality data foundation for subsequent predictive modeling.

Model evaluation, cross-validation, and external validation

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using three key metrics: test set accuracy, F1-score, and AUC. Accuracy measures the overall classification correctness; the F1-score provides a harmonic mean of precision and recall (balancing false positives and false negatives); and the AUC index reflects the discriminative ability of the model across various classification thresholds. This multidimensional framework avoids the limitations of relying on a single metric and provides an objective basis for subsequent model selection [22].

To assess generalizability and real-world reliability, optimally assembled metagenomic bins were used as independent external test sets. The models were trained on high-quality whole-genome data, validated on inherently fragmented metagenomic data, and rigorously tested for robustness to variable data quality. This strategy ensures the practical utility and predictive reliability of the model under non-ideal sequencing conditions typical of complex microbial communities.

Results and discussion

Genome pre-analysis

Five key dimensions affecting salinity are required for microbial growth

In this study, 123 microbial species (51 archaea and 72 bacteria) with clear salinity information were screened using the 2023 Manual of Systematic Identification of Common Bacteria and Archaea. Analysis of the frequencies of the 20 standard amino acids within their genomes revealed that halophilic microorganisms possessed significantly elevated frequencies of acidic and basic amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 1). As illustrated in Fig. 1a (using Glu and Arg as examples), the abundance of acidic and basic residues exhibited a clear positive correlation with increasing optimal growth in high-salinity environments across the five microbial categories. The combined proportions of acidic (Asp and Glu) and basic (Arg, Lys, and His) amino acids were markedly higher in the halophilic groups than in their non-halophilic counterparts (salt-sensitive and halotolerant). Furthermore, hydrophilic amino acids were generally enriched in halophiles, whereas the total proportion of hydrophobic amino acids was drastically reduced in extreme halophiles compared with that in the other groups. Consequently, the analysis identified five key dimensional features fundamentally linked to microbial salt stress resistance: the total proportion of acidic amino acids, combined proportion of acidic and basic amino acids, proportion of hydrophilic amino acids, proportion of hydrophobic amino acids, and specific proportion of phenylalanine. Preliminary modeling and validation based on the five key dimensional features

Based on our genomic exploration, the five dimensions exhibiting the strongest correlation with salt stress resistance were utilized for the initial modeling. Feature extraction yielding these five dimensions was performed using 123 genomic datasets. Model performance was subsequently evaluated using five-fold cross-validation repeated 1000 times to map the correlation between amino acid frequency and growth salinity. The results indicated that ET achieved the best performance (global accuracy: 0.654; F1-score: 0.665), demonstrating notable prediction stability and robustness (Fig. 1b). Given the relatively small training dataset (n = 123), 1000 repetitions were performed to ensure statistical stability. Preliminary tests showed that the results stabilized after approximately 50 repetitions; however, to minimize variance in accuracy estimation and prevent overly optimistic performance reporting due to random chance, all subsequent experiments in this study maintained 1000 repetitions. To confirm that a large number of repetitions did not introduce instability, a subset of 50 repetitions yielded nearly identical results (error < 1%), confirming the stability of the calculations.

Building on the five-dimensional baseline, the feature space subsequently incorporated the comprehensive frequencies of all 20 standard amino acids. The top-performing models from the preliminary stage (e.g., ET, RF, and bagging) were selected to form ensemble architectures, which were rigorously evaluated again via 1000-repeat five-fold cross-validation.

As shown in Fig. 1c, the expanded feature space significantly enhanced the pattern-capturing capabilities of the models. The global accuracy of the best-performing ET model improved substantially from 0.654 to 0.762, with the F1-score increasing synchronously to 0.764. The homogeneous tree-based ensemble model (Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag) demonstrated exceptional stability, achieving an accuracy of 0.756 and an AUC of 0.935, underscoring its classification robustness. Concurrently, the heterogeneous ensemble incorporating linear models (Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA) achieved an AUC of 0.937, validating the superiority of multi-model integration for processing high-dimensional biological data. The standard deviation among top-tier models remained consistently low (approximately ± 0.077), indicating that combining comprehensive genomic features with ensemble algorithms elevated the performance ceiling and ensured the high reliability of the predictions.

The predictive accuracy achieved using the full 20-dimensional amino acid frequency vector was superior to that achieved using the five-dimensional subset. To further capture the macroscopic physicochemical properties of the proteome, four aggregated indicators were incorporated: the total proportions of hydrophobic, hydrophilic, acidic, and basic amino acids. A comprehensive 24-dimensional composite feature vector was constructed by combining these indicators with the frequencies of 20 individual amino acids. This expansion aimed to holistically characterize the adaptive evolutionary signatures of microbial genomes under salinity stress by integrating individual amino acid variations and their collective physicochemical behaviors.

Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 2b, all leading models demonstrated exceptional predictive performance for extremely halophilic samples, generally achieving accuracies greater than 0.85. Notably, Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag achieved accuracies of 0.69 and 0.70 in the challenging salt-sensitive and halotolerant intervals, respectively. Composite model optimization based on the 24-dimensional amino acid and physicochemical features

During the evaluation of the 24-dimensional feature space, the QDA model exhibited severe predictive bias. As observed in Fig. 2, although QDA achieved a remarkably high accuracy of 0.98, specifically within the slightly halophilic interval, it performed poorly across most other intervals (e.g., freshwater and extremely halophilic), yielding F1-scores as low as 0.100. This failure stems from the mathematical requirement of QDA to estimate an independent covariance matrix for each class. As the feature dimensionality increased to 24, the number of parameters also increased. In the context of uneven sample distribution, the QDA model was heavily skewed by the majority class in the training set, causing its classification boundaries to shift drastically and misclassify the majority of the test samples as slightly halophilic. Given its instability and imbalanced genomic data, QDA was excluded from the final pipeline. Instead, priority was given to linear models (e.g., LDA, LR) and tree-based ensembles (e.g., ET, Ensemble1), which exhibited a relatively balanced and robust performance across the entire salinity gradient.

External validation of global microbial genomes based on the pre-trained model

To validate the robustness and practical utility of the pre-trained model in complex, real-world environments, the optimal classifiers trained on the aforementioned 123 microbial genomes were exported as serialized “. pkl” files. This predictive framework was applied to a massive external validation set (Fig. 3a) comprising 2678 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) computationally reconstructed from six representative global geographical regions. Accuracy validation of microbial metagenomes in crystallizer ponds with > 35% salinity

To further test the predictive performance of the model in extreme environments, two highly representative metagenomic datasets from high-salinity habitats were selected (Fig. 3b): SRX090229 from the Santa Pola crystallizer pond (SS37) in Spain and SRX5604789 from the Cabo Rojo crystallizer pond in Puerto Rico. According to literature records, both sampling sites are in the hypersaline crystallization stage, with salinities exceeding 35%, representing typical “life-forbidden zones” [23,24]. In this study, 102 high-quality MAGs were extracted and binned from the metagenomes of these two sites, serving as the “gold standard” test set for verifying the discrimination limit of the model.

The test results showed that various models exhibited excellent recognition accuracy when dealing with extremely halophilic communities with highly simplified compositions and extreme adaptations. Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag and Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA showed extremely high capture rates, accurately indicating that the 102 bins mainly belonged to an extremely halophilic group. This finding demonstrates that ensemble learning is robust when processing genomes with strong convergent evolution characteristics (e.g., high proportions of acidic amino acids).

Notably, the LDA model performed impressively, generating predictions highly consistent with environmental realities. This finding indicates that extremely halophilic microorganisms possess distinct linear separability within the 24-dimensional feature space (particularly the acidic-to-basic amino acid ratio), thereby validating the biological relevance of these physicochemical indicators. Over 90% of the bins were labeled as extremely halophilic, well-aligned with ecological observations stating these communities as dominated by highly adapted taxa such as Haloquadratum [23,24]. Crucially, even when analyzing samples from the Puerto Rican saltern during the dry season (characterized by intense evaporation and drastic ion concentration fluctuations), our model resisted environmental noise and accurately pinpointed the halophilic phenotype (Fig. 3b). This finding confirms the successful transition of the model from laboratory pre-training to real-world ecological monitoring.

Accuracy validation of microbial metagenomes in freshwater habitats with salinity ≈ 0.1%

MAGs derived from the sediment metagenome of Lake Baikal (PRJNA521725) were used to evaluate the discriminative power of the model at the opposite end of the salinity spectrum (Fig. 3c). As the largest freshwater lake in the world, Lake Baikal maintains a stable salinity of approximately 0.1%, representing a quintessential salt-sensitive habitat. As microbial genomes in Lake Baikal lacked halophilic evolutionary signatures, this dataset served as a critical test for the ability of the model to prevent false positives and accurately differentiate between salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive phenotypes.

The experimental data consistently showed that Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag and Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA delivered the best performance and successfully classified the majority of MAGs into the salt-sensitive and halotolerant categories. In contrast to linear models (such as LDA), which performed well under hypersaline conditions, ensemble models demonstrated superior discriminative power in freshwater datasets. Although LDA tended to produce ambiguous predictions in low-salinity regions owing to highly overlapping feature distributions, DT-based ensembles successfully captured weak, nonlinear signals in amino acid frequencies, achieving precise classification. Using ensemble models, the predicted abundance of the extremely halophilic group was negligible, whereas the salt-sensitive microorganisms dominated the community. This finding aligns perfectly with the biological reality of Lake Baikal (Fig. 3c) and proves the excellent generalization of the model across the entire salinity continuum, from < 0.1% salinity to hypersaturation (35% salinity).

Accuracy validation of marine microbial metagenomes at ≈ 3.5% salinity

Marine environments (salinity ≈ 3.5%) are the most expansive microbial habitats on Earth. Based on the Global Ocean Sampling Expedition dataset (PRJNA391943) analyzed by the University of Southern California, marine microbial communities are predominantly composed of halotolerant, slightly halophilic, and moderately halophilic taxa (Fig. 3d).

Unlike extreme habitats with highly polarized genomic traits, the amino acid frequency distribution of marine microorganisms is substantially concentrated and nuanced, demanding exceptional local feature extraction capabilities from predictive models. When evaluating 2307 marine MAGs, the bagging algorithm exhibited stellar performance. Consistent with observations from the 24-dimensional pre-training, bagging effectively minimized model variance in intermediate salinity ranges by leveraging the diversity of multiple base classifiers. It accurately identified subtle halotolerant and slightly halophilic signatures inherent in marine samples, yielding a significantly lower false positive rate than individual models.

The multi-model ensembles (Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag and Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA) followed closely. By synergizing the efficient feature selection of ET with the stability of bagging, Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag provided highly balanced predictions for marine habitats. The results indicated that the marine MAGs were predominantly mapped to the halotolerant to moderately halophilic spectrum, conforming to general marine biological paradigms. Together with freshwater and crystallizer pond validation, these results completed a comprehensive salinity gradient assessment. Our findings confirm that the bagging algorithm and its derived multi-model ensembles are optimal tools for determining the salinity adaptability of global marine metagenomes.

Interpretability of the prediction system

To validate the reliability and biological interpretability of the model at the single-genome level, Methanoculleus bourgensis MS2T (GCF_000304355.2), a well-characterized salt-tolerant methanogenic archaea, was selected as a case study [25].

The multi-model prediction results (Fig. 3e and f) revealed that Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag accurately classified the genome as halotolerant, with a high confidence score of 0.91. This prediction is highly consistent with other top-tier models (e.g., k-NN and GBDT), effectively mitigating the risk of incidental errors from any single algorithm.

Subsequent feature attribution analysis illuminated the biological rationale behind the decision of the model: the amino acid fingerprint of M. bourgensis fundamentally differed from the “salt-in” [8] strategy utilized by extremely halophilic archaea, which involves a massive accumulation of acidic amino acids. Instead, it exhibited an enrichment in glutamine and relatively low acidic amino acid levels, a profile highly congruent with the halotolerant control group. This unbroken chain of evidence from high-confidence classification to specific genomic feature attribution confirms the precision of the model in distinguishing halotolerant from halophilic phenotypes; it also proves that SuSha provides transparent, interpretable predictions grounded in genuine biological evolutionary principles.

Applicable boundaries and uncertainty analysis of the pre-trained model

Collectively, the results robustly demonstrate the efficacy of amino acid frequency–based ensemble learning in predicting microbial salinity adaptation. However, to ensure the judicious application of this tool, it is essential to acknowledge its potential limitations.

First, the foundational training data were exclusively derived from pure-cultured microorganisms. Although this ensures the absolute accuracy of the salinity labels, it implies that the foray of the model into “microbial dark matter” relies on extrapolating the unknown from the known. When evaluating microbial taxa that are evolutionarily distant from established reference genomes, the predictions of the model should be interpreted as inferences based on the principles of convergent evolution rather than as absolute physiological certainties.

Second, the feature engineering in this study captured static amino acid preferences within genomic coding regions, which are forged by long-term evolutionary pressures. However, for microorganisms inhabiting highly dynamic environments with frequent salinity fluctuations (e.g., river estuaries), rapid adaptation may rely more on transient regulatory network flexibility (e.g., transcriptomic responses) than on global genomic amino acid remodeling. Such dynamic physiological plasticity falls outside the predictive scope of the current genome-based models.

Third, horizontal gene transfer (HGT) introduces additional uncertainty that the model fails to explicitly account for. Microorganisms can rapidly acquire a set of salt tolerance determinants via HGT without undergoing genome-wide changes in their amino acid composition. Because regions acquired through HGT typically account for only a small fraction of the genome, they hardly alter the global amino acid frequencies in a detectable manner. Consequently, the input features received by the model may appear non-halotolerant or salt-sensitive, whereas the actual phenotype may have already shifted to salt tolerance or even halophilicity, potentially leading to systematic misclassification.

As environmental genomic data continue to expand exponentially, training sets can be iteratively refined to capture the broader diversity of evolutionary lineages. Concurrently, samples identified by the model as ambiguous within intermediate salinity ranges (e.g., the boundaries between slightly halophilic, halotolerant, and salt-sensitive) will undergo in-depth integrative analyses. This will help determine whether undiscovered adaptation mechanisms exist, thereby driving the continuous self-optimization of the SuSha model and fostering new biological discoveries.

Conclusions and prospects

In this study, a multi-model ensemble learning framework was developed to predict microbial salinity adaptation by integrating whole-genome amino acid frequencies with macroscopic physicochemical properties. The excellent generalization capabilities of the models were rigorously validated across diverse global metagenomic datasets. Based on these findings, a dynamic algorithm-switching strategy tailored to environmental salinity gradients is proposed. For broad-spectrum environments, such as oceans and freshwater habitats, the system defaults to the highly robust tree-based ensemble (Ensemble1_ET_RF_Bag); conversely, for extreme habitats approaching salt saturation (e.g., crystallizer ponds), it switches to the linear ensemble model (Ensemble2_ET_LR_LDA), which provides superior discriminative accuracy in that specific feature space. This dynamic strategy ensures stable, high-confidence predictions across the entire salinity continuum, from near-zero to supersaturation.

The SuSha tool has significant scientific and practical value. Scientifically, it bypasses the traditional bottleneck of pure-culture dependency, enabling the direct inference of physiological adaptability from the amino acid sequences of MAGs, which serves as an efficient computational bridge from genotype to phenotype. Fragmented MAGs may slightly underestimate certain amino acid frequencies; however, SuSha substantially reduces this noise by integrating predictions from multiple algorithms.

Halophilic microorganisms are invaluable resources for industrial saline wastewater treatment and the development of salt-tolerant enzymes. By intelligently screening massive genomic datasets, SuSha can precisely pinpoint high-potential taxa, drastically reducing the cost and blind trial-and-error of experimental exploration and establishing a critical technological foundation for mining biological resources in extreme environments.
