Social cognition training improves recognition of individual emotions in schizophrenia disorder
Authors: Samantha Evy Schoeneman Patel, Kristen M. Haut, Hyunkyu Lee, Melissa Fisher, Michael F. Green, Joseph Ventura, Sophia Vinogradov, Mor Nahum, Christine I. Hooker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Impaired recognition of angry, fearful, and emotionless (i.e., neutral) faces is associated with poor social functioning among individuals with schizophrenia disorder (SZ). Limited research evaluates how interventions impact these impairments. Computerized social cognition training can improve overall facial emotion recognition in SZ, but it is unclear whether it improves the aforementioned impairments. We report results from a randomized controlled trial (Trial Registration: NCT02246426) evaluating how computerized social cognition training (SCT) impacts recognition of individual emotions in SZ. Ninety-eight individuals with SZ were randomly assigned to one of two internet-based interventions: SCT or a control condition (computer games without social content) (CON). Participants completed 40 sessions of training over 8–12 weeks. Facial emotion recognition was measured with the Penn ER-40 before training, after 20 sessions, and within 8 weeks of completing 40 sessions. Accuracy and reaction times for each emotion (i.e., anger, fear, happy, neutral, and sad) were analyzed. Linear mixed model analyses and t-tests elucidated intervention effects. A significant Group x Time interaction on accuracy was found favoring the SCT group. Only the SCT group improved recognizing fearful and sad faces; the CON group did not. The SCT group had higher accuracy for neutral recognition than the CON group after training. There was no Group x Time interaction on reaction times. We conclude that, because SCT training improves fear and neutral recognition, it has the potential to improve social functioning in SZ. However, interventions need to be further refined to improve anger recognition and emotion recognition reaction times.

Introduction

Individuals with schizophrenia disorder (SZ) have challenges recognizing facial emotions. Compared to those without SZ, individuals with SZ take longer to recognize facial emotions and have trouble recognizing fearful, angry, and emotionless (i.e., neutral) faces; these impairments have been associated with poor social functioning (Abram et al., 2014;Brittain et al., 2012;Couture et al., 2006;Hooker, Christine and Park, 2002;Huang and Hsiao, 2017;Carter et al., 2009;Hoyland et al., 2017;Kohler et al., 2003;Premkumar et al., 2008;Silver et al., 2009;Wang et al., 2023;Cohen et al., 2009;Krakowski et al., 2022;Villa et al., 2018;Pinkham et al., 2011;Iozzino et al., 2021). It is understandable how trouble recognizing an emotion could impact social functioning. For example, if individuals cannot recognize that they are frightening others, they will not adjust their behavior to make others around them more at ease. Consequently, they experience interpersonal rejection, social isolation, professional challenges, unemployment and ultimately homelessness, all of which are common among individuals with SZ (Lin et al., 2022).

Antipsychotics, the standard treatment for SZ, do not reliably improve social functioning, highlighting the need for efficacious social functioning interventions (Javed and Charles, 2018;Penn et al., 2009). Because facial emotion recognition impairments are associated with impaired social functioning, researchers have argued for the development of interventions that target facial emotion recognition in individuals with SZ (Lee et al., 2022). This intervention approach is further supported by the existence of validated assessments that identify specific impairments in facial emotion recognition and measure changes in facial emotion recognition performance. The existence of these assessments allows for the objective evaluation of a proposed intervention’s efficacy.

However, only a limited number of interventions exist that strengthen facial emotion recognition in individuals with SZ. In addition, overall facial emotion recognition performance is often used to evaluate facial emotion recognition interventions rather than assessing for performance changes in recognizing individual emotions, such as fear or anger. Therefore, it is difficult to know whether these interventions address the particular facial emotion recognition impairments that are associated with impaired social functioning in individuals with SZ. In-person group-based social skills training programs (Wolwer et al., 2005) show promise in improving facial emotion recognition in individuals with SZ, but many individuals with SZ do not have access to such programs; furthermore., there is limited research on how these training programs impact the recognition of individual emotions in SZ. Another promising approach is having individuals with SZ practice mimicking different facial emotions. This approach was actually shown to improve fear recognition in individuals with SZ; however, this is also an in-person program, to which many individuals do not have access (Pancotti et al., 2021).

Neuroplasticity-based social cognition training programs, such as SocialVille (Posit Science, San Francisco, CA, USA), hold particular promise in improving facial emotion recognition. Socialville (SCT) is a computerized social cognition training program that can be accessed by anyone with an internet connection. This program utilizes the principles of neuroplasticity, meaning it aims to engage and strengthen the specific neural networks underlying social cognition through a training program that is intensive, adaptive, and reinforcing in order to promote desired behavioral changes (Dodell-Feder et al., 2015;Nahum et al., 2021). The neural networks underlying social cognition include brain regions involved in emotion processing (i.e., amygdala, somatosensory-related cortex, ventral striatum, and medial orbitofrontal cortex), and theory of mind (i.e., superior temporal sulcus, temporoparietal junction, posterior cingulate, and medial prefrontal cortex) (Dodell-Feder et al., 2015). This program is comparable to other programs that have been shown to engage these networks as seen on functional magnetic resonance imaging (Hooker, Christine I. et al., 2012;Hooker, Christine I. et al., 2013). To ensure the program is adaptive, the program becomes easier or more challenging depending on the trainee’s performance, meaning this program is designed to meet individuals where they are and to address their particular facial emotion recognition challenges. To ensure the program is intensive and continues to consistently engage the brain regions of interest, the program becomes progressively more difficult as trainees improve their performance. Finally, it is reinforcing because it rewards correct responses. Khan et al. and Sacks et al. showed that neuroplasticity-based social cognition training can improve overall facial emotion recognition in individuals with SZ (Khan et al., 2023; Sacks et al., 2013). Schoeneman Patel et al. showed that, when adults without neuropsychiatric diagnoses underwent such training, they significantly improved their ability to recognize the emotions with which they had been particularly struggling: angry and neutral faces; they also became faster at recognizing facial emotions (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022). However, no study has yet examined whether this intervention can help individuals with SZ address their particular facial emotion recognition challenges, which are also tied to their impaired social functioning.

A double-blinded randomized controlled trial looked at how SCT impacted a composite measure of social cognition abilities, including facial emotion recognition, and found SCT significantly improved overall facial emotion recognition from pre- to post-intervention and significantly decreased withdrawal from social interactions in individuals with SZ (Nahum et al., 2021). The current study is one component of this larger study. We hypothesized that SCT would have the same effects in individuals with SZ that it had in individuals without neuropsychiatric diagnoses (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022): 1) improve accurately recognizing emotions with which individuals with SZ particularly struggle: fearful, angry, and neutral and 2) improve participants’ speed in accurately recognizing individual emotions.

Methods

As stated above, the present study focuses on one assessment that was conducted along with other assessments in a multisite randomized controlled trial that examined the effects of social cognition training on social cognitive abilities and functional capacity in individuals with SZ. Below is an overview of the parent study; please note that the complete protocol for the parent study has been published elsewhere (Rose et al., 2015;Nahum et al., 2021).

Participants

Adults with SZ were recruited from the following sites: San Francisco VA Medical Center, University of Minnesota, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles VA Hospital, and Rush University. Study participants met DSM-5 criteria for SZ (assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV; SCID-P38). Eligible participants were between 18 and 65 years of age, had an estimated IQ ≥ 70 based on the Wechsler Test of Adult Reading, and were clinically stable for 8 weeks prior to consent. Additionally, participants were required to have been on a stable regimen of no more than two antipsychotics and/or other concomitant psychotropic treatments for a minimum of 6 weeks before consent. Demographic data, including Age, Gender, Race, Ethnicity (i.e., Hispanic or not Hispanic), and Years of educations were collected for all participants. The following clinical data were also collected for each participant: age at which each participant was first prescribed an antipsychotic, Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) scores (divided into a general score, a positive symptom score, a negative symptom score, and a total score) (Kay et al., 1987)), history of depression according to the SCID, history of substance use disorder according to the SCID, current substance use, and current psychotropics (Nahum et al., 2021). Age at which each participant was prescribed an antipsychotic was treated as the beginning of each participant’s SZ diagnosis and was subtracted from their current age to generate illness duration. Psychotropics were categorized as antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, antidepressants, or benzodiazepines.

Study design

Institutional review board approval was obtained at the coordinating center on October 3, 2014 (WIRB Pro Number 20,141,695; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02246426) and at each trial site. All study procedures were in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. The privacy rights of all the participants were observed, and all participants signed an informed consent form prior to study participation (Nahum et al., 2021).

Participants completed baseline assessments and were then randomly assigned to either SCT or an active control condition (CON) (see below). Both training programs consisted of a total of 40 sessions, each lasting 42 min. Participants in both groups were asked to complete 3–5 sessions per week over 8–12 weeks. Training coaches automatically received and reviewed data on participants’ engagement with the programs. The training coaches interacted weekly by phone with participants from both groups in order to discuss progress. They provided coaching if a participant indicated difficulty in completing training (Nahum et al., 2021). Participants again completed assessments mid-way through training (i.e., after 20 sessions), and after training (i.e., 40 sessions). Immediately after participants completed training, the study staff invited them to complete their final assessments. At times scheduling and logistical challenges arose that delayed participants completing their final assessments. Final assessments did have to be completed within 8 weeks of completing training.

Study interventions

The SCT program was developed by Posit Science (Nahum et al., 2014;Rose et al., 2015). It consisted of 27 unique exercises that target a range of social cognition skills, including visual and vocal affect perception, social cue perception, theory of mind, self-referential style, and empathy; 5 of the 27 exercises specifically targeted facial emotion recognition (Nahum et al., 2021). More information about the individual exercises can be found in Appendix A. As stated above, training exercises were designed in accordance with the principles of neuroplasticity and included socially relevant stimuli in order to target the neural networks that support social cognition. Participants completed their first SCT session in the recruiting laboratories or clinics after their baseline assessments so that study staff could give them instructions and help them troubleshoot any possible technical issues. They were then loaned laptops so that they could complete the remaining sessions in the comfort of their own homes. They received feedback after each trial and at the end of each session (Nahum et al., 2013;Nahum et al., 2021; Dodell-Feder et al., 2015;Rose et al., 2015). The CON program consisted of 13 computer games without social content, such as Solitaire and Sudoku (Nahum et al., 2021). More information on these exercises can be found in Appendix A.

Assessments

The present study is concerned strictly with facial emotion recognition as measured by the Penn Emotion Recognition Task (ER-40) – a computerized facial emotion recognition task that we selected for its high test-retest reliability and high construct validity (Carter et al., 2009). In this task, participants are presented with color photographs of faces and instructed to identify the displayed emotions by clicking on one of the following options with a mouse: happy, angry, sad, fear, or no emotion (i.e., neutral or emotionless). The participants begin with one practice trial after which feedback about accuracy is given. If necessary, participants repeat the practice trial until they choose the correct response. After completing the practice trial, they move onto the actual assessment, which consists of 40 trials (i.e., 8 trials for each of the 5 emotions). Emotion type, emotion intensity, age, gender, and ethnicity of the faces are balanced across trials (Gur et al., 2002;Carter et al., 2009;Kohler, Turner, Gur et al., 2004;Kohler, Turner, Stolar et al., 2004).

As mentioned above, participants completed this assessment at 3 timepoints: before the training (i.e., baseline) (T1), mid-way though the training (i.e., after 20 sessions) (T2), and within 8 weeks of completing training (i.e., after 40 sessions) (T3). The following behavioral variables were collected for each of the 40 trials: response choice, accuracy, and reaction time (RT) (amount of time between stimulus presentation and mouse click response) in milliseconds (ms) (Nahum et al., 2021).

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed with either Microsoft Excel 365 (Build 16.0.17718.42303 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) or SPSS Version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY). The below analyses are based on those conducted in a previous study (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022).

Data for each participant were inspected for outliers and other abnormalities. An outlier was defined as any trial with an RT 3 standard deviations above or below the mean of all reaction times from both groups over all time points. 1.8 % (213/11,760) of trials had an RT 3 standard deviations above the mean and were removed from all analyses. No trial had an RT 3 standard deviations below the mean. 21.8 % (2521/11,547) of the remaining trials were incorrect. Although incorrect trials were included in accuracy analyses, they were removed from RT analyses: only correct trials were included in the RT analyses with the rationale that faster recognition only indicates improvement if said recognition is accurate; therefore, we further removed the 21.8 % (2521/11,547) of the remaining trials because they were incorrect. Thus, a total of 9026 trials were included in the RT analyses.

The following performance variables were calculated at each time point (i.e., T1, T2, and T3): accuracy for each emotion, False Positive (FP) for each emotion, RT for each emotion, and misattribution patterns. Accuracy for each emotion was calculated as the average number of correct trials divided by the total number of included trials for that emotion. More information about analyses of FP rates and misattribution patterns can be found in Appendix B.

Pearson chi-square tests and independent two-tailed sample t-tests were conducted to evaluate for baseline differences between the SCT and CON groups in terms of Gender, Age, Race, Ethnicity, Years of education, Estimated IQ, History of depression, History of substance use disorder, whether they were taking an antipsychotic, whether they were taking a mood stabilizer, whether they were taking an antidepressant, whether they were taking a benzodiazepine, illness duration, PANSS general scores, PANSS positive scores, PANSS negative scores, PANSS total scores, and ER-40 accuracy and RT for each emotion.

To test Hypothesis 1 (SCT will improve accurately recognizing fearful, angry, and neutral faces), a linear mixed model analysis was conducted to evaluate for the effects of Group, Time, Emotion, and the interactions between these three variables on accuracy while accounting for the effects of Baseline accuracy, Gender, Age, Race, Ethnicity, Years of education, Estimated IQ, and Illness Duration, History of depression, History of a substance use disorder, PANSS positive scores, PANSS negative scores, PANNS general scores, and whether patients were taking antipsychotics. We did not include antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and benzodiazepines in the model given how few participants were taking them (Table 2). To test Hypothesis 2 (SCT will improve participants’ speed in recognizing individual emotions), a linear mixed model analysis was conducted evaluating for the effects of Group, Time, Emotion, and the interaction between these three variables on RT while accounting for the effects of Baseline RT, Gender, Age, Race, Ethnicity, Years of education, Estimated IQ, Illness Duration, History of depression, History of a substance use disorder, PANSS positive scores, PANSS negative scores, PANNS general scores, and whether patients were taking antipsychotics. Scatter plots were used to visually confirm linear relationships between the dependent variables and the other quantitative variables included in each model. Homoscedasticity was confirmed for each model by plotting the residuals against the predicted value and confirming there was a linear relationship. The Durbin-Watson test was used to confirm a lack of autocorrelation. Q-Q plots were used to confirm the normality of the residuals for each model. If a significant Group x Time effect was found for a dependent variable (i.e., accuracy or RT), we proceeded with 1) paired two-tailed sample t-tests within each group to look at changes in accuracy for each emotion between all three time points and 2) independent two-tailed sample t-tests to test for between group differences for each individual emotion at each time point. If a Group x Time effect was not found for a variable, we did not proceed with further post-hoc analyses. P < 0.05 (Bonferroni corrected) was regarded as statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Participant characteristics

In this multi-site randomized controlled trial, 212 participants were assessed for eligibility, 147 individuals were randomized, and 109 participants completed the ER-40 at all three time points; trial level data, which was needed to complete the misattribution analyses, was located for 98 of the 109 participants; 11 participants who completed the study were missing trial level data.

Demographic comparison between those who completed the study and those who did not

As stated in a previous publication, Age, Gender, Years of Education, Race, and Ethnicity were comparable between those who completed the study and those who did not provide data beyond the baseline visit (Nahum et al., 2021).

Demographic comparison between those with complete data and those with incomplete data

Between-group analyses of the 98 participants with complete ER-40 data and the 11 who completed the study but were missing data showed no differences in Age (t(107)=−0.082, p = 0.935), Gender (χ2(1, N = 109) = 1.219, p = 0.270), Years of education (t (107)=0.319, p = 0.750), Race (χ2(3, N = 109) = 0.842, p = 0.839), or IQ (t (107)=−0.58, p = 0.422. However, Ethnicity (χ2(1, N = 109) = 5.166, p = 0.023) was significantly different between the two groups: the 11 that were missing data had a higher proportion of Hispanic participants than the 98 with complete data (18 % (2/11) vs. 3 % (3/98), respectively); nevertheless, our linear mixed model analysis that was conducted to test Hypothesis 1 showed that ethnicity did not have a significant effect on accuracy (F (1,90)=0.528, p = 0.469), so it is unlikely that this difference in ethnic composition impacted the below results.

Demographic and clinical characteristic comparison between the SCT and CON groups

The final sample of 98 participants consisted of 49 participants in the SCT group and 49 participants in the CON group. Results from between-group comparisons showed no difference between the SCT and CON groups in terms of demographics (Table 1)

Clinical characteristics were also comparable between the two groups (Table 2). Although some participants had a history of depression, none in this sample met criteria for schizoaffective disorder. No participant had an active substance use disorder although some had a history of a substance use disorder.

Baseline performance

Analysis of performance on the ER-40 task showed that, at baseline, the CON group had significantly higher accuracy than the SCT group in recognizing anger (t(96)= 2.263, p = 0.026) and fear (t(96)= 2.063, p = 0.042). To address these differences, the below linear mixed model analyses did take into account baseline performance. No other significant differences in baseline performance on the ER-40 were found between the CON and SCT groups.

Intervention effects

A linear mixed model analysis showed a significant Group x Time interaction on accuracy (F(2,1344)=7.842, p < 0.001). Please see Appendix C for more information on the effects of Group, Time, Emotion, and covariates on accuracy. There was no significant Group x Time interaction on RT (F(2,1315.10)=2.22, p = 0.11).

Post hoc analyses

Given we found a Group x Time interaction on accuracy, we then ran paired t-tests within each group to look at changes in accuracy for each emotion between all three time points (Table 3). Between T1 and T2 the SCT group significantly improved in their ability to accurately recognize fearful (t(48)= −4.882, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1), neutral (t(48)= −2.741, p = 0.01) (Fig. 2), and sad (t(48)= −2.527, p = 0.015) faces (Fig. 3). Between T1 and T3 the SCT group significantly improved their ability to recognize fearful faces (t(48)= −3.111, p = 0.003). The CON group only had one significant improvement: they improved their accuracy in recognizing neutral faces between T1 and T2 (t(48)= −2.158, p = 0.036).

We also ran independent samples t-tests comparing accuracy in the SCT and CON groups for each emotion at each time point. At T2, compared to the CON group, the SCT group had significantly higher accuracy in recognizing neutral faces (t(96)=−3.047, p = 0.003). At T3, when compared to the CON group, the SCT group had significantly higher accuracy in recognizing neutral faces (t(96)=−2.647, p = 0.010).

Given we did not find a Group x Time interaction on RT, we did not run post hoc analyses on RTs.

Discussion

In this multisite randomized controlled trial comparing social cognition training with an active control condition, we examined how SCT impacted the recognition of individual facial emotions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to look at how this particular intervention impacts recognition of individual emotions in individuals with SZ. As hypothesized, SCT did improve recognition of fearful and neutral faces. SCT also improved recognition of sad faces. However, SCT did not improve recognition of angry faces. In contrast, the CON group only improved their ability to recognize neutral faces. However, contrary to our hypothesis, we did not find that SCT helped individuals with SZ accurately recognize emotions more quickly. We conclude SCT training does improve recognition of individual facial emotions that are known to impact social functioning in individuals with SZ, further re-enforcing that such training has the potential to improve social functioning.

Implications for individuals with SZ

Impaired recognition of neutral and fearful faces is associated with impaired interpersonal communication (Kohler et al., 2003;Mitrovic et al., 2020;Brittain et al., 2012) while the ability to recognize neutral faces is associated with good social functioning (Brittain et al., 2012)6. Therefore, the ability of SCT to improve recognition of neutral and fearful faces suggests that SCT has the potential to improve social functioning. Furthermore the improvement in fear recognition remained significant at T3 despite the fact that some participants were no longer actively engaged in training, suggesting this training can have effects beyond the training period.

Comparison to the effects of SCT in individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis

A study exploring the effects of SCT on recognition of individual emotions in individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis had slightly different results than the present study (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022). Like the individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis, individuals with SZ who underwent SCT improved their ability to recognize neutral faces (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022). Unlike the adults without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis, individuals with SZ improved their ability to recognize fearful and sad faces; the lack of improvement in recognizing these emotions in individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis was likely due to ceiling effects given the individuals without a psychiatric diagnosis who underwent SCT scored above 90 % accuracy for recognizing fearful and sad faces at baseline whereas the individuals with SZ who underwent SCT had accuracies of 70 % and 63 % for fearful and sad faces at baseline, respectively (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022). These improvements that were specific to individuals with SZ speak to the importance of SCT’s adaptive nature: this training meets individuals where they are and allows them to address their individual challenges (Dodell-Feder et al., 2015)30. That being said, the significant improvements seen in recognizing sad faces at T2 lost significance at T3. This may have been due to the fact that at T2 all participants were actively engaged in training but at T3 some participants were no longer actively engaged in the training since they had up to 8 weeks to complete their final assessments. This finding suggests maintenance training may be needed.

SCT did not improve anger recognition and reaction times even though SCT did produce these improvements in individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis (Schoeneman Patel et al., 2022). It is unclear why SCT did not improve anger recognition in individuals with SZ, given, per Table 2, the participants had room for improvement in recognizing anger. It is possible that improving anger recognition in SZ is more difficult than improving recognition of other emotions. A longer training period may be required to improve anger recognition. The lack of improvement in RTs in individuals with SZ was likely due to the fact that processing speed is known to be slowed among individuals with SZ regardless of whether they are encountering social content or not (Gebreegziabhere et al., 2022). Therefore, RT may be harder to improve in individuals with SZ than in individuals without a neuropsychiatric diagnosis. A longer training period may be needed to improve RT. Interestingly, Scoriels et al. studied the effects of an intervention in individuals with SZ that combined exercises targeting multiple cognitive domains including processing speed and social cognition. They had the opposite findings: they found that their intervention improved reaction times for facial emotion recognition but did not improve facial emotion recognition accuracy (Scoriels et al., 2022). Perhaps SCT drove individuals with SZ in the present study to study faces for longer, allowing for an improvement in accuracy but not in RT.

Comparison to studies on other facial emotion recognition interventions

Previous studies have found that other interventions have had more limited effects than SCT on the recognition of individual emotions in individuals with SZ. The caveat is that there is only a limited number of studies that have examined how interventions impact recognition of individual emotions. Byrne et al. showed that a program with facial emotion recognition exercises improved recognition of fear (Byrne et al., 2015). Pancotti et al. showed that an intervention requiring individuals with SZ to practice exhibiting different facial emotions also improved recognition of fear (Pancotti et al., 2021). A major difference between these two interventions and SCT is that SCT included 27 exercises focused on strengthening different domains of social cognition, including social cue perception and theory of mind, whereas the other interventions focused solely on facial emotion recognition, and in one case, the expression of facial emotions. This difference suggests that SCT’s multi-domain focus allowed for greater improvements in facial emotion recognition likely because social cognition domains are inter-related (e.g., recognizing a facial expression, such as a disapproving stare, is also perception of a social cue), and strengthening one domain likely re-enforces the strengthening of other domains.

Future directions

There was some decrease in accuracy in the SCT group between the midpoint of the training and after training, at which point not all participants were consistently engaged in training. Future research should look at whether maintenance training sessions are needed to sustain gains in accuracy, and, if so, what frequency is needed to sustain these gains. It would also be important to evaluate whether further training would lead to even more gains in facial emotion recognition accuracy, including improved anger recognition, and whether further training could improve how quickly individuals with SZ recognize emotions.

Given the strong link between facial emotion recognition and social functioning, future research should continue to evaluate how SCT impacts social functioning. Although Nahum et al. found that SCT only led to some improvements in social functioning (Nahum et al., 2021), it is plausible that more sensitive assessments that track real time social interactions could detect more changes in everyday social functioning. Using ecological momentary assessments to monitor for changes in social interaction quality, as used by Neff et al. (Neff et al., 2021), would be one way to better understand how SCT impacts real world day-to-day functioning. It is also possible that combining SCT with other trainings targeting social functioning could lead to a synergistic effect on social functioning. Similarly, it would be interesting to see if combining SCT with a more robust training to improve processing speed would allow individuals with SZ to recognize facial emotions more promptly, an improvement we were unable to produce in the above study.

SCT’s ability to improve recognition of individual emotions also means SCT can be used as a tool to explicate associations between facial emotion recognition impairments and SZ symptoms. For example, impaired fear recognition is linked to a tendency toward violence (Krakowski et al., 2022), but it is unclear whether this relationship is correlational or causal. One could imagine that someone is less likely to attack a fearful person, which may explain why a blunted ability to recognize fear could lead to violence. To investigate whether impaired fear recognition contributes to violent behaviors, SCT could be used in an enriched population (i.e., individuals with both SZ and violence) to see if improving fear recognition decreases violent behavior.

Limitations

The participants who withdrew from this study scored lower on baseline social cognition assessments than those who completed the study meaning we do not know whether the efficacy of SCT decreases as the level of impairment rises (Nahum et al., 2021). This is unfortunate as the individuals who dropped out likely represent the individuals who stand to benefit the most from a social cognition intervention.

Another limitation is that the participants completed training at 8–12 weeks but not all participants were able to complete their final assessments immediately after their training (they had up to 8 weeks to complete their final assessments). Therefore, we were unable to see how training consistently impacts recognition of individual facial emotions immediately after training in this sample.

Conclusion

We conclude that, because SCT training improves fear and neutral recognition, it has the potential to improve social functioning in individuals with SZ. However, interventions need to be further refined to improve anger recognition and reaction times. In addition, further research needs to examine whether maintenance social cognition training would allow for sustained and perhaps even further gains in recognition of individuals emotions in individuals with SZ.
