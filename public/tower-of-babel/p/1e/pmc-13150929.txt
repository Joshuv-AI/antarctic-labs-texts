High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Levels and Risk of Age-Related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Itay Nitzan, Shlomit Jaskoll, Adi Kramer, Or Shmueli, Jaime Levy, Itay Chowers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels and incident age-related macular degeneration (AMD) in a large cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used electronic health records from 70 U.S. healthcare organizations in the TriNetX network from 2005 to 2025. Adults with at least two HDL-C measurements obtained at least three months apart were classified as having high HDL-C (all values ≥60 mg/dL) or low HDL-C (all values ≤39 mg/dL). Groups were matched 1:1 on demographics, comorbidities, medications, laboratory values, and healthcare utilization. Follow-up began at the second qualifying HDL-C measurement and continued for up to 10 years. Incident nonexudative and exudative AMD were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards analyses, with robustness assessed through nested matching, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and positive and negative control outcomes.ResultsOver 1.29 million person-years of follow-up, high HDL-C was associated with increased risk of nonexudative AMD (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.53; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.32–1.78) and exudative AMD (HR = 1.37; 95% CI, 1.09–1.73). For nonexudative AMD, risk was elevated across early (HR = 1.55; 95% CI, 1.26–1.91), intermediate (HR = 1.82; 95% CI, 1.42–2.33), and advanced atrophic (HR = 1.85; 95% CI, 1.16–2.97) stages. Associations for nonexudative AMD were robust across washout periods, follow-up intervals, mortality exclusion, and stepwise matching. Associations for exudative AMD were consistently positive but less stable. Control outcomes supported the specificity and internal validity of the findings.ConclusionsHigher HDL-C levels were associated with increased risk of incident nonexudative AMD and a weaker, less consistent association with exudative AMD. These findings suggest that sustained HDL-C levels may be relevant to AMD risk assessment and warrant further investigation.

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of irreversible central vision loss among older adults worldwide. The disease is characterized by drusen, lipid-rich extracellular deposits, accompanied by chronic inflammation and dysregulated complement activity, implicating lipid metabolism as a central component of its pathogenesis.1 Genome-wide association studies have identified risk variants in several lipid-related genes, including APOE, LIPC, CETP, ABCA1, and LPL, which are involved in cholesterol transport, lipoprotein remodeling, and reverse cholesterol efflux.2–5 These findings, together with population-based and Mendelian randomization studies linking elevated high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and apolipoprotein A1 to increased AMD risk,2,6,7 underscore the role of lipid pathways in AMD biology.

Despite this biological and genetic rationale, epidemiologic evidence linking HDL-C to AMD remains inconsistent, with a substantial proportion of null findings.8 A 2018 review of 55 studies found that most observational studies (34 of 55) reported no association between HDL-C and AMD, whereas fewer (n = 16) reported higher HDL-C in AMD, and only a small minority (n = 5) reported inverse associations.9 Consistent with this variability, several early case-control and cross-sectional analyses, including the Beaver Dam Eye Study10 and the Cardiovascular Health Study,11 reported no association between HDL-C and AMD. In contrast, a limited number of studies reported inverse associations in specific contexts, including a case-control study demonstrating lower risk of dry AMD with higher HDL-C in a women-only cohort12 and the Blue Mountains Eye Study,13 in which higher HDL-C was associated with reduced risk of advanced AMD despite no association with early disease.

More recent meta-analytic data further highlight this heterogeneity. Updated analyses have shown no overall difference in systemic HDL-C levels between patients with AMD and controls, with substantial between-study variability, whereas subgroup analyses suggest that any association may be stage-specific and more apparent in early and intermediate nonexudative disease.7 At the same time, selected population-based studies such as the ALIENOR cohort2 and large European consortia14 have linked elevated HDL-C with higher odds of early and overall AMD, while genetic studies using Mendelian randomization across European and Asian ancestries support a central role of HDL-C in advanced disease.4 More recently, population-scale resources have highlighted a more complex interaction. The All of Us Research Program15 reported that both low and high HDL-C levels were associated with increased AMD risk, consistent with a U-shaped relationship, whereas metabolomic analyses demonstrated that lipid-related metabolites may exert opposite effects depending on complement genotype.5

These discrepancies likely reflect key limitations of the prior literature, including reliance on cross-sectional or prevalent-case designs,10,11 single-time-point lipid measurements,2,3 variable control for confounding, heterogeneous outcome classification, and limited power for assessing long-term exposure.1,7–9,16 To overcome these limitations, we evaluated incident AMD in a longitudinal cohort derived from electronic health records, classified HDL-C exposure using repeated measurements to capture sustained lipid status, and used rigorous propensity score matching with multiple sensitivity analyses.

Methods

Data Source

Data were obtained from the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, which aggregates de-identified electronic medical records (EMR), including diagnoses, procedures, medications, and laboratory data, from more than 130 million patients across 73 healthcare organizations (HCOs). All analyses were conducted on a single data extract as of August 28, 2025, to maintain consistency within the continuously updated platform. Laboratory data are provided directly by HCOs, with only standardized unit conversions and limited de-identification measures applied to comply with privacy regulations. All data collection, processing, and transfer comply with applicable data protection regulations, including the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation 2016/679) and the United States Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. TriNetX is ISO 27001:2013 certified and operates under a comprehensive information security program designed to safeguard personal and health data. In accordance with institutional policy and ethical standards, the study was exempted from review by the Hadassah Medical Center Institutional Review Board and did not require informed consent. All procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Design and Cohort Selection

This retrospective cohort study examined the relationship between HDL-C levels and AMD. Cohort construction incorporated International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnostic codes, TriNetX laboratory terminology codes for HDL-C measurements, and TriNetX visit codes. HDL-C classification was based on repeated measurements, with individuals required to have at least two HDL-C values at age ≥ 18 years, obtained at least three months apart to ensure consistency of exposure. For the main analysis, participants were stratified into two exposure groups: a High HDL-C group, defined as individuals with all HDL-C measurements ≥60 mg/dL and no measurement below that threshold, and a Low HDL-C group, defined as individuals with all HDL-C measurements ≤ 39 mg/dL and no measurement above that threshold. These cutoffs reflect established cardiovascular risk thresholds and are consistent with recent epidemiological literature on AMD.15,17 To support sensitivity analyses, an Intermediate HDL-C group was also defined, comprising individuals with all HDL-C measurements between 40–59 mg/dL. To reduce confounding by pre-existing ocular or metabolic conditions that independently influence AMD risk or affect the retina through alternative pathogenic mechanisms, we excluded individuals with the following diagnoses: type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus with ophthalmic complications (ICD-10-CM E10.3, E11.3 and E13.3), injury of eye and orbit (S05), chorioretinal inflammation (H30), retinal vascular occlusions (H34), hereditary choroidal dystrophy (H31.2), retinal detachment with retinal break (H33.0), traction detachment of retina (H33.4), pure hypercholesterolemia (E78.0), pure hyperglyceridemia (E78.1), hyperchylomicronemia (E78.3), and lipoprotein deficiency (E78.6). The index date was defined as the second qualifying HDL-C measurement meeting all criteria. Only individuals with an index date on or after September 1, 2005, were included, ensuring sufficient follow-up time while aligning with the platform's 20-year post-index observation window.

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic determinants, medical comorbidities, lipid-lowering medications, and laboratory values, were assessed from 730 days to one day before the index date. Variables were selected to capture sociodemographic factors, metabolic risk profiles, vascular disease burden, systemic inflammation, lifestyle exposures, and healthcare utilization (Supplementary Table S1). Between-cohort differences were evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD ≥ 0.1 considered indicative of a meaningful imbalance. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the platform's built-in logistic regression algorithm, applying 1:1 greedy nearest neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. To evaluate the robustness of the association to increasing levels of covariate adjustment, we implemented a prespecified, stepwise propensity score matching approach with progressive covariate inclusion. Four nested models were constructed: (a) demographics only; (b) demographics plus comorbidities; (c) demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory variables; and (d) a fully adjusted model incorporating all baseline characteristics used in the primary analysis. The covariate matrix was randomized to avoid order effects, and no imputation was applied for missing data. As part of post-PSM baseline balance diagnostics, baseline drusen coding (H35.36) was also evaluated.

Outcomes and Follow-Up

Patients were followed from one day after the index date until the earliest of (a) outcome occurrence, (b) 10 years of follow-up, or (c) their last recorded EMR entry. The primary outcomes were incident diagnoses of nonexudative AMD (ICD-10-CM H35.31) and exudative AMD (H35.32). Individuals with any AMD diagnosis before the index date were excluded. As part of post-PSM validity diagnostics, positive control outcomes included ischemic heart disease (I20-I25) and cerebrovascular disease (I60-I69), where reduced incidence among the high HDL-C group would support known protective associations. Negative control outcomes included appendiceal diseases (K35-K38) and Parkinson's disease (G20), selected due to the absence of plausible biologic links with HDL-C.

Survival and Hazard Analyses

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate cumulative incidence, and between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with the HDL-C group as the exposure variable. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, with P ≤ 0.01 indicating nonproportionality.

Analytics Platform and Statistical Software

All statistical analyses were conducted within the platform's integrated analytics environment. The platform uses R version 4.0.2 (including the survival and Hmisc packages), Python version 3.7 (lifelines, pandas, numpy, scipy, statsmodels), and Java version 11.0.16 (Apache Commons Math version 3.6.1).

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

We conducted a series of prespecified sensitivity and subgroup analyses to assess the robustness of the primary findings, comparing the High and Low HDL-C cohorts unless otherwise specified. First, because nonexudative AMD encompasses biologically distinct stages, we further evaluated stage-specific nonexudative outcomes, including early, intermediate, and advanced atrophic AMD, rather than treating all nonexudative disease as a homogeneous phenotype (Supplementary Table S2). Second, we applied a 360-day washout period after the index date to reduce potential reverse causation and early detection bias. Third, we evaluated associations across prespecified follow-up intervals of three, five, and 20 years to assess temporal consistency. Fourth, we excluded individuals who died during follow-up to examine potential bias from the competing risk of death. Fifth, we stratified analyses by sex. Finally, we compared each of the High and Low HDL-C groups with the Intermediate HDL-C group using the same design and analytic framework as the primary analysis.

Results

Cohort and Matching

Before matching, 1,013,645 individuals with high HDL-C and 494,029 with low HDL-C from 70 of the 73 HCOs available in the platform were included in the analysis. After PSM on demographics, comorbidities, medications, laboratory values, and healthcare utilization, 186,532 patients remained in each group. Before matching, substantial differences were noted in age, sex, race, cardiometabolic burden, and medication use (e.g., mean [SD] body mass index (BMI): 26.2 [5.9] in the high HDL-C group vs. 33.5 [7.7] in the low HDL-C group; SMD = 1.058), as well as in key laboratory values including triglyceride (86.3 [44.8] vs. 191.0 [155.5] mg/dL; SMD = 0.915) and total cholesterol levels (201.0 [37.5] vs. 164.2 [52.0] mg/dL; SMD = 0.812). After matching, covariate balance was achieved across most characteristics (all SMDs <0.1), although moderate residual imbalance persisted for triglycerides (SMD = 0.346) and total cholesterol (SMD = 0.661). As part of post-PSM baseline balance diagnostics, baseline drusen coding was rare and well balanced between groups (0.035% vs. 0.038%; SMD = 0.006). Primary Outcomes: Association Between HDL-C and AMD

After PSM, the mean (SD) follow-up time was 1243.6 (1,117.0) days in the high HDL-C group and 1288.4 (1,144.7) days in the low HDL-C group, yielding a total of 1.29 million person-years of observation. During follow-up, 419 and 285 individuals developed nonexudative AMD, corresponding to 66.0 and 43.3 cases per 100,000 person-years, respectively (P < 0.001), with an HR of 1.53 (95% CI, 1.32–1.78; Fig. 1). Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated early and sustained separation between groups, with higher cumulative incidence in the high HDL-C group (Fig. 2). When nonexudative AMD was further resolved by stage, the association remained evident across early nonexudative AMD (HR = 1.55; 95% CI, 1.26–1.91), intermediate nonexudative AMD (HR = 1.82; 95% CI, 1.42–2.33), and advanced atrophic nonexudative AMD (HR = 1.85; 95% CI, 1.16–2.97; Fig. 1). Exudative AMD occurred in 163 and 124 individuals, yielding 25.7 and 18.8 cases per 100,000 person-years, respectively (P = 0.021), with an associated HR of 1.37 (95% CI, 1.09–1.73; Fig. 3). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a similar trend, with modest but consistent divergence between curves over time (Fig. 4).

Stepwise Matching

To evaluate the robustness of these associations to covariate adjustment, we conducted a stepwise propensity score matching analysis using nested models of increasing complexity. For both nonexudative and exudative AMD, results were directionally consistent across all specifications, with stable effect estimates observed when matching on demographics alone and after sequential inclusion of comorbidities, laboratory values, and lipid-lowering medications (Supplementary Table S3).

Control Outcomes

As part of post-PSM validity diagnostics, we examined two positive and two negative control outcomes. A total of 2173 patients in the high HDL-C group and 2749 in the low HDL-C group developed cerebrovascular disease, yielding 341.7 and 490.5 cases per 100,000 person-years, respectively (P < 0.001; HR = 0.79; 95% CI, 0.76–0.82). Ischemic heart disease occurred in 2437 and 3505 patients, corresponding to 383.1 and 626.1 cases per 100,000 person-years (P < 0.001; HR = 0.69; 95% CI, 0.67–0.71). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of Parkinson's disease (563 vs. 530 cases; 87.1 vs. 94.7 per 100,000 person-years; P = 0.281; HR = 1.08; 95% CI, 0.95–1.21) or diseases of the appendix (548 vs. 582 cases; 84.7 vs. 104.2 per 100,000 person-years; P = 0.121; HR = 0.94; 95% CI, 0.84–1.06).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

The association between HDL-C levels and AMD was consistent across subgroups and sensitivity analyses. Results were consistent after applying a 360-day washout window (HR = 1.45; 95% CI, 1.23–1.72), excluding individuals who died during follow-up (HR = 1.49; 95% CI, 1.27–1.74), and across three-, five-, and 20-year follow-up intervals (HRs = 1.35–1.45). Associations also remained similar when stratified by sex (HR = 1.53; 95% CI, 1.25–1.89 for males; HR = 1.45; 95% CI, 1.15–1.83 for females; Fig. 1). For exudative AMD, sensitivity HRs remained directionally positive (HRs 1.07–1.42), although confidence intervals narrowly included or bordered the null (Fig. 3).

Comparison to Intermediate HDL-C

Finally, we compared each HDL-C group to the intermediate HDL-C group using the same design as the primary analysis. For nonexudative AMD, high HDL-C was associated with a significantly increased risk (HR = 1.12; 95% CI, 1.04–1.21), whereas low HDL-C was not significantly different from intermediate HDL-C (HR = 0.92; 95% CI, 0.82–1.03). For exudative AMD, neither high nor low HDL-C was significantly associated with risk compared to intermediate HDL (HR = 0.89; 95% CI, 0.79–1.00 and HR = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.72–1.02, respectively).

Discussion

In this large, retrospective cohort study of over 370,000 individuals with consistent HDL-C levels, we found that high HDL-C (≥60 mg/dL) was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident nonexudative AMD, including early, intermediate, and advanced atrophic stages, and with a weaker and less consistent increase in risk of exudative AMD, compared with low HDL-C (≤39 mg/dL). These associations persisted after rigorous PSM across a broad set of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and healthcare utilization covariates, and were robust to multiple sensitivity analyses and model specifications. The stronger and more consistent signal across the nonexudative spectrum, compared with the less stable findings for exudative AMD, supports stage- and phenotype-specific associations rather than a uniform relationship across AMD subtypes.

Our findings converge with prior long-term population studies. The Copenhagen population cohorts demonstrated that elevated plasma HDL-C and apolipoprotein A1 were associated with a higher risk of AMD over follow-up periods of up to three decades, whereas lower low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-C was paradoxically linked to a greater risk.3 Similarly, the ALIENOR study identified a more than twofold increase in AMD odds among elderly individuals with high HDL-C, even after adjusting for lipid-related genetic variants such as CETP, LIPC, and ABCA1.2 These results suggest that HDL-C itself, or dysfunctional HDL particles, may contribute directly to drusen biogenesis rather than merely reflecting systemic lipid status. Mechanistically, drusen are highly enriched in cholesterol and phospholipids, accounting for nearly half their volume.1 The “oil spill” model describes progressive deposition of lipoproteins within Bruch's membrane as a nidus for basal deposits, impairing metabolic exchange and fueling drusen growth.1 Although in the current study we measured HDL-C levels only, functional assays have shown that cholesterol efflux capacity is paradoxically elevated in early AMD and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy but not in typical neovascular AMD, correlating with specific HDL particle subclasses.18 This highlights that both the quantity and quality of HDL may influence AMD risk, as HDL particles can undergo oxidative and inflammatory remodeling, shifting from protective efflux functions to pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory phenotypes, thereby providing a biologic substrate for the observed associations.1

Mendelian randomization analyses have further clarified this relationship by reducing confounding and reverse causality. Large-scale genetic studies in both European and Asian populations have found that genetically elevated HDL-C was causally associated with increased odds of advanced AMD, with consistent effect sizes across geographic atrophy and neovascular subtypes.4 A more comprehensive analysis, including eight serum lipid biomarkers, extended these observations, showing that higher HDL-C and Apolipoprotein A1 increased the risk of all AMD stages, whereas LDL-C, ApoB, and triglycerides conferred protective associations, particularly against geographic atrophy.6 Together, these findings highlight a discordance between lipid traits traditionally considered favorable for cardiovascular outcomes and their involvement in AMD.19

Recent metabolomic and pharmacologic data add further nuance. Untargeted plasma metabolomics have revealed enrichment of glycerophospholipids, large HDL subclasses, and very-low-density lipoprotein metabolites in AMD cases, reinforcing the view that lipid transport and remodeling pathways are central to disease biology.1 Importantly, the effect of lipid metabolites appears to vary by complement genotype, with some HDL subfractions showing opposite associations with AMD depending on CFH or C3 alleles.5 This interplay between lipid metabolism and complement activation provides a biological explanation for the heterogeneity across studies. From a therapeutic perspective, meta-analyses in European cohorts suggest that lipid-lowering medications and antidiabetic agents may modestly reduce AMD prevalence,14 although randomized evidence remains lacking. Collectively, these lines of evidence underscore the importance of considering both systemic lipid exposure and local lipid-complement interactions in AMD pathogenesis and may help prioritize future mechanistic and interventional research.

Our study extends prior evidence in several ways. The cohort size (>370,000) exceeds that of prior population-based studies such as ALIENOR2 (∼1000) and the Copenhagen cohorts3 (∼120,000 combined), enabling detailed AMD subtype analyses. In contrast to studies based on a single lipid measurement,2,3 we defined exposure using repeated HDL-C values, ensuring consistency over time and reducing exposure misclassification. We applied propensity score matching across a broad set of demographic, clinical, laboratory, and healthcare utilization covariates, creating balanced cohorts that minimized baseline differences and reduced the likelihood of confounding. Our dataset, drawn from multiple healthcare organizations across the United States, also provides broad demographic representation: after matching, ∼65% of participants were White, ∼16% Black/African American, ∼3% Asian, and ∼4% other race, with ∼7% identifying as Hispanic or Latino. This stands in contrast with the predominantly European ancestry populations of prior cohorts.3,14 Importantly, we also compared each exposure group with an intermediate HDL-C group using the same analytic framework. In that analysis, high HDL-C remained associated with increased risk of nonexudative AMD, whereas low HDL-C was not significantly different from intermediate HDL-C, suggesting that the observed association was driven primarily by sustained high HDL-C. Finally, we separated nonexudative and exudative AMD at scale, and further stratified nonexudative AMD into early, intermediate, and advanced atrophic subgroups, showing a clear and consistent association with nonexudative disease but weaker signals for exudative AMD, supporting subtype-specific pathways of risk.

This study has several limitations inherent to retrospective analyses of aggregated, de-identified EMR data. First, our reliance on ICD-10-CM coding for AMD subtypes assumes accurate diagnostic capture across diverse healthcare organizations. Misclassification across early, intermediate, and advanced disease stages is possible and may result in underestimation or overestimation of true disease incidence. Relatedly, baseline drusen coding, evaluated as part of post-PSM baseline balance diagnostics, was rare and well balanced between groups, suggesting that differential baseline coded subclinical AMD burden is unlikely to explain the observed associations. Second, although we applied extensive PSM, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Substantial baseline differences were evident before matching, and although balance was achieved across most characteristics, moderate imbalance remained for triglycerides, total cholesterol, and BMI. This likely reflects the intrinsic clustering of HDL-C with other lipid fractions and metabolic traits, which cannot be fully disentangled in observational analyses. In addition, important unmeasured covariates, such as dietary intake, physical activity, genetic risk variants (e.g., CFH, ARMS2), and detailed ophthalmic imaging, were not available within the TriNetX platform. Third, we lacked data on laterality, visual acuity, and AMD lesion characteristics, limiting phenotypic granularity. Fourth, medication adherence, health-seeking behavior, and surveillance intensity may differ across lipid profiles, introducing potential detection bias. However, matching on healthcare utilization and socioeconomic proxy diagnoses, together with balanced person-time at risk reflected in the similar follow-up duration between cohorts, helps to partially mitigate this concern. Finally, HDL-C exposure was defined categorically based on repeated measurements, which may not fully account for intra-individual variability over time. Future studies incorporating prospective designs, multimodal retinal imaging, and genetic stratification are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, high HDL-C levels were associated with a significantly increased risk of incident nonexudative AMD across early, intermediate, and advanced atrophic stages, whereas associations with exudative AMD were weaker and less consistent, compared with consistently low HDL-C levels. These findings support subtype-specific effects of HDL-C on AMD pathogenesis. The paradoxical divergence between cardiovascular and ocular outcomes underscores the need for mechanistic studies to dissect HDL-C functionality in the retina and for prospective studies to determine whether HDL-C-modifying interventions can affect AMD incidence.
