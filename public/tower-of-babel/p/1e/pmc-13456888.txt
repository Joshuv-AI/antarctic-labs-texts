Introduction

Renal pseudoaneurysm is a rare but severe vascular complication following percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), typically caused by trauma to renal arterial branches during tract dilation, instrument manipulation, or guidewire passage. Late perioperative bleeding occurs in 0.8–2.6% of PCNL cases, with pseudoaneurysms and arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) comprising the majority[1]. Bleeding severity varies from mild anemia to life-threatening hemorrhagic shock. Unlike true aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms represent a contained arterial rupture by the surrounding tissue, making them highly susceptible to secondary rupture. As the lesion expands, blood leaks into the collecting system, causing gross hematuria[2]. A significant blood loss is typically signaled by persistent bleeding accompanied by a drop in hemoglobin levelof at least 3 g/dL or a hematocrit below 30%[1].

Endovascular embolization is the preferred management strategy due to its minimally invasive nature, rapid hemostasis, and ability to preserve renal parenchyma compared to open surgery[3]. However, its implementation in Indonesia is constrained by high costs and limited technology in regional hospitals. Consequently, patients often require referral to tertiary centers. We present the first documented case in Indonesia of a vascular surgeon successfully treating a post-PCNL renal pseudoaneurysm with selective angioembolization, emphasizing the diagnostic workup and referral pathways in resource-limited settings.

Case presentation

A 64-year-old male presented to the Emergency Department with gross hematuria and intermittent blood clots in his urine, which began 3 hours prior. One month earlier, he underwent a right PCNL for renal calculi. His postoperative course was complicated by recurrent hematuria and flank pain. One week post-PCNL, he required cystoscopy, clot evacuation, and placement of a double-J stent for clot-induced urinary retention. He subsequently experienced multiple episodes of pre-shock, requiring aggressive fluid resuscitation and multiple packed red blood cell transfusions to stabilize his hemodynamics (Fig. 1). Physical examination revealed a palpable right flank mass; other findings were unremarkable. Laboratory evaluations showed severe anemia (hemoglobin: 4.6 g/dL) and acute renal impairment, with blood urea at 90.9 mg/dL, serum creatinine at 3.45 mg/dL, and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 17.7 mL/min/1.73 m2.

CT angiography (CTA) revealed a posteroinferior right perirenal hematoma and an associated parenchymal defect (Fig. 2). Given the CT findings and ongoing hematuria, we suspected a vascular injury. We proceeded with diagnostic angiography, planning to embolize any identified bleeding source. The patient was referred to the vascular surgery team. Concurrently, a urological workup via cystoscopy and upper-tract examination was negative, ruling out active urological pathology. The urology team advised that nephrectomy would serve as the definitive salvage option if vascular intervention failed. Figure 2.Axial (A) and coronal (B) views of the patient’s CT angiography.

Axial (A) and coronal (B) views of the patient’s CT angiography.

Under ultrasound guidance, angiography was performed via a 5 Fr common femoral artery sheath. The arteriogram demonstrated a filling defect and contrast blush originating from the posteroinferior segmental branch of the right renal artery. Super-selective catheterization of the feeding vessel was achieved using an STC-18 microcatheter over a V-18 guidewire. Two 5-mm Vortex coils were precisely deployed into the lesion. Post-embolization angiography confirmed complete occlusion of the pseudoaneurysm with preservation of the surrounding renal vasculature (Fig. 3). Post-procedure, the patient showed immediate resolution of hematuria. At the 1-week follow-up, his hemodynamics were stable, and external genital examination confirmed spontaneous voiding of clear urine. No immediate or delayed post-embolization complications, such as post-embolization syndrome or non-target embolization, occurred. At the 6-month outpatient follow-up, the patient remained free of recurrent hematuria. Furthermore, his renal function significantly improved and stabilized, with his serum creatinine level dropping to 1.2 mg/dL. This case follows the SCARE criteria[4].

Discussion

PCNL is the gold standard for large renal calculi but carries a 21.9% overall complication rate, with 5.5% classified as major (Clavien grade ≥ III)[5]. Although most post-PCNL bleeding is venous and self-limiting, arterial injuries like the renal artery pseudoaneurysm (RAP) occur in 0.86–1% of cases and demand urgent intervention[6]. These lesions typically present as delayed hematuria days to weeks post-surgery and can rapidly become life-threatening[7].

RAP develops when tract dilation or stone manipulation partially disrupts an arterial wall, forming a contained hematoma that retains communication with the arterial lumen[8]. Persistent arterial pressure causes this false aneurysmal cavity to organize and expand, explaining the characteristically delayed presentation rather than immediate postoperative bleeding[8,9]. Our patient’s ongoing hematuria and signs of hypovolemic shock indicated a massive delayed hemorrhage. As noted in previous studies, severe bleeding from a renal pseudoaneurysm can quickly lead to hemodynamic instability, heavy transfusion requirements, and permanent kidney damage[10].

Our patient experienced ongoing bleeding a month after his PCNL. Because his cystoscopy and upper tract evaluations were entirely normal, we strongly suspected a vascular injury. Diagnosing RAP requires differentiation from other vascular injuries. On CTA and digital subtraction angiography, RAP appears as a focal, contained collection of contrast material (contrast blush) originating from an arterial branch. This key feature distinguishes it from an AVF, which shows early opacification of the draining vein during the arterial phase, and from active extravasation, which appears as an uncontained, irregular contrast spread into surrounding tissues[10]. Additional diagnostic challenges in this case included fluctuating hemoglobin levels and preexisting renal impairment, which necessitated cautious contrast utilization.

Selective coil embolization is the gold standard for post-PCNL vascular injuries, offering a minimally invasive, nephron-sparing alternative to open surgery[10,11]. In this case, targeting the specific posteroinferior segmental branch achieved rapid hemostasis while minimizing infarction of the unaffected renal parenchyma[12,13]. This parenchymal preservation was vital, given the patient’s initial renal impairment.

Currently, Indonesia lacks a standardized national protocol dedicated exclusively to post-PCNL vascular complications; management typically relies on adapting international guidelines to local resources. Our institutional protocol mandates that any patient with persistent post-PCNL hematuria and a significant hemoglobin drop undergo immediate CTA, followed by direct referral to vascular surgery or interventional radiology if a vascular lesion is confirmed. Although advanced endovascular suites are concentrated in tertiary Indonesian hospitals, the core principle of this case is highly applicable nationwide. For regional centers lacking these resources, maintaining a high index of suspicion and performing early CTA can expedite the referral pathway to a tertiary center before the patient becomes hemodynamically unstable, thereby averting emergency open nephrectomy.

Our experience contrasts with some literature, where mild or intermittent symptoms delayed diagnosis[10,14]. Our patient suffered an aggressive clinical course featuring severe clot retention, hemodynamic instability, and acute renal impairment, reinforcing the extreme severity that RAP can reach. Our successful outcome also contrasts with a previous Indonesian report by Seno et al, where initial arterial embolization failed and required subsequent venous embolization[15]. This emphasizes that, while selective arterial embolization remains the primary intervention, early failure necessitates prompt follow-up imaging and a flexible interdisciplinary strategy.

Conclusion

Embolization stops post-PCNL vascular injuries while preserving kidney function. In resource-limited settings, early imaging and prompt specialist referral are key to managing severe hemorrhage.
