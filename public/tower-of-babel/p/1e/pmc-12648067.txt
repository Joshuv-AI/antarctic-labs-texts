Comparative evaluation of flattening filter (FF) and flattening filter-free (FFF) radiotherapy beams: melatonin’s radioprotective role on thyroid and laryngeal tissues in rat model
Authors: Şeyma İnan Mahioğlu, Serhat Aras, Esra Erdem, Fatih Hacımustafaoğlu, Dilek Ünal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aimed to evaluate the radioprotective effects of melatonin on healthy thyroid and laryngeal tissues exposed to head and neck radiotherapy using flattening filter (FF) and flattening filter-free (FFF) radiotherapy beams. Forty-eight female Wistar albino rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8): control, only melatonin, FF, FF + melatonin, FFF and FFF + melatonin. A single 16 Gy dose of radiation was delivered to the head and neck region using a linear accelerator. Melatonin (50 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally 15 minutes prior to irradiation. Biochemical parameters including malondialdehyde (MDA), myeloperoxidase (MPO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) were measured from serum samples. Histopathological evaluations of thyroid (inflammation, epithelial hyperplasia, follicular hyperplasia, C-cell hyperplasia, cellular degeneration, follicular cell hypertrophy and cellular necrosis) and laryngeal (general morphological deterioration, inflammation, oedema, epithelial destruction, karyorrhexis and karyolysis) tissues were performed using a semi-quantitative scoring system. FF and FFF beam exposure significantly increased MDA and MPO levels while reducing antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, GPx) compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Histologically, both radiation types induced marked inflammation, cellular damage and morphological degeneration in thyroid and laryngeal tissues. Melatonin administration significantly reversed these effects, improving antioxidant enzyme levels and mitigating tissue damage (P < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed between FF and FFF groups in either biochemical or histological outcomes. FF and FFF beams produced comparable levels of oxidative and structural damage in healthy tissues. Melatonin demonstrated significant radioprotective effects through both biochemical and histopathological pathways, suggesting its potential role in mitigating radiotherapy-induced toxicity in non-target tissues.

INTRODUCTION

Epidemiological studies indicate a large rise in head and neck cancers in recent years [1]. Radiotherapy is a prevalent and efficacious modality employed in the treatment of head and neck malignancies [2]. The primary limitation of the treatment is the difficulties that arise in healthy tissues due to radiation exposure, despite radiotherapy being an effective intervention for malignant tissue. Adverse effects of radiation may manifest both during the treatment and months post-completion. The intensity of these effects is contingent upon several parameters, including fractionation, the interval between fractions, fraction dose, total radiation dose, dose rate and the dimensions of the treatment volume [3, 4]. Technological advancements and treatment methodologies have led to substantial success, particularly in the past decade, despite the complexity of head and neck cancer therapies necessitating a multidisciplinary approach.

Contemporary radiotherapy apparatus utilizing flattening filter-free (FFF) technology, which omits a flattening filter (FF), is commonly employed in treatments as a substitute for conventional linear accelerators equipped with an FF. FFF beams have been employed in next-generation technological equipment to minimize out-of-field doses by attaining rapid dose gradients [5]. FFF beams can attain elevated instantaneous dose rates, hence shortening treatment durations and minimizing scattered radiation, which enhances the protection of adjacent healthy tissues and renders this treatment modality more favorable [6, 7]. Consequently, there are numerous dosimetric advantages associated with the routine clinical utilization of FFF beams. Although numerous in vitro studies indicate the impact of FF and FFF beams on cell viability across different cancer types [8, 9], there is insufficient in vivo research demonstrating the radiobiological effects of these beams on healthy tissues at varying dosage rates. The lethal and sublethal effects of ionizing radiation frequently compromise the thyroid and laryngeal tissues, which are not included in the intended target volume, during head and neck radiotherapy. These repercussions significantly impact patients’ quality of life post-radiation [10–12]. Melatonin (MEL) possesses strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and its capacity to scavenge free radicals and enhance antioxidant enzyme activity has been shown to attenuate radiation-induced tissue damage. Compared with other radioprotective agents such as vitamin E, α-lipoic acid and amifostine, melatonin was selected in this study due to its low toxicity, favorable tissue penetration and proven efficacy in thyroid and laryngeal injury models [4, 10]. The interaction of ionizing radiation with tissues produces free radicals that induce oxidative stress [13, 14]. Due to its radioprotective properties, melatonin has been demonstrated to significantly reduce detrimental effects in thyroid and laryngeal tissues. Histopathological studies and biochemical analyses facilitate the assessment of both acute and chronic effects of radiation exposure. This study analyzed biochemical parameters in rat serum samples after exposure to FF and FFF beams, focusing on malondialdehyde (MDA), myeloperoxidase (MPO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which are enzymes that play a role in mitigating oxidative stress. This study further investigates thyroid and laryngeal tissues subjected to radiation during head and neck radiotherapy, focusing on general morphological histological criteria such as inflammation, necrosis, edema, degradation, karyorrhexis and karyolysis.

The objective is to evaluate, through in vivo experimental rat models, the radioprotective efficacy of melatonin against tissue damage in the thyroid gland and larynx caused by FF and FFF beams, utilizing histological and biochemical analyses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design for experimental rats and melatonin injection

Forty-eight female Wistar albino rats, with an average weight of 220 ± 20 g, were utilized in this study. Their conditions were regulated: room temperature maintained at 20 ± 1°C; humidity at 60 ± 10%; and a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle. Professional staff cared for the rats till they reached 6–8 weeks of age. The Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments at Istanbul Medipol University granted ethical approval for this experiment under E-38828770-772.

The six groups into which the rats were randomly divided each comprised eight animals. The control group (G1) received no irradiation or medical treatment but was administered an equivalent volume of saline via the intraperitoneal (ip) route, similar to the melatonin solution. Rats in groups G2 and G4 received a single dose of 16 Gy radiation at rates of 600 MU/min (FF) and 2400 MU/min (FFF), respectively. Rats in groups G3 and G5 received 50 mg/kg of melatonin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) via the intraperitoneal route 15 minutes before to a single dose of 16 Gy irradiation at dose rates of 600 MU/min (FF) and 2400 MU/min, respectively. Only melatonin was administered to the G6 group, but no radiotherapy procedures were performed. Prior in vivo studies [4, 10, 15–17] informed the melatonin dosage, solution formulation and delivery methods utilized in this research.

Radiotherapy procedures

All experimental rat groups, with the exception of the control group (G1), received irradiation while under anesthesia, administered with 80 mg/kg of ketamine and 5 mg/kg of xylazine. The rats were positioned supinely on a plexiglass tray. All rats, excluding those in the control group, received single doses of 16 Gy radiation to the head and neck region via a Varian TrueBeam linear accelerator configured at 600 MU/min (FF) and 2400 MU/min (FFF). A single dose of 16 Gy was selected based on previous in vivo studies evaluating acute radiation-induced tissue damage in the head and neck region [4, 10]. This dose has been widely used to induce measurable oxidative and histopathological changes in the thyroid and laryngeal tissues during the acute phase. The irradiation field was defined as 5 × 5 cm2 and was planned to encompass the thyroid and laryngeal regions. Due to the non-uniform dose distribution within the field in FFF beams, the prescribed 16 Gy dose was individually defined for each rat by positioning the isocenter over the laryngeal region. In addition, a randomly selected rat underwent computed tomography imaging, and dose verification was performed in the treatment planning system using the same field dimensions and dose parameters (Fig. 1).

Representative isodose distributions and dose volume histogram (DVH) of FF and FFF beams applied to rats.

Prior in vivo studies in the literature guided the selection of the radiation dosage [9]. With the larynx situated at a 100 cm source-to-surface distance, irradiation was administered utilizing the Source Axis Distance (SAD) method. A 10 mm-thick water-equivalent bolus was placed over the irradiation field prior to treatment to compensate for tissue irregularities in the neck area.

Euthanasia protocol

Forty-eight hours after irradiation, intracardiac blood samples from rats under general anesthesia (80 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine) were transferred into serum tubes, allowed to clot for 30 minutes and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 minutes. The resulting serum was collected in Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80°C for biochemical analysis. The larynx and thyroid tissues were meticulously excised and preserved in 10% formaldehyde for histological analysis.

Histopathological evaluation

Thyroid and laryngeal tissue samples obtained 48 hours after the radiotherapy procedure were systematically processed histologically and embedded in paraffin blocks. Three parallel slices, each 4 μm thick, were prepared from these blocks and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for examination under a light microscope. A histologist evaluated the histological examination of thyroid and laryngeal tissues subjected to radiation.

Histopathological parameters were assessed to identify radiation-induced cellular damage, including inflammation, epithelial hyperplasia, follicular hyperplasia, C-cell hyperplasia, degradation, follicular cell hypertrophy and necrosis in thyroid tissue. Inflammation, edema, epithelial injury, general morphological deterioration, karyorrhexis and karyolysis characterize laryngeal tissue.

Histological changes in thyroid and laryngeal tissues due to radiation exposure were evaluated using a standard grading system. The histopathological score of each rat was assessed using a semi-quantitative scale ranging from 0 to 3.

0: No modification whatsoever

1: Little alteration

2: Moderate alteration

3: Profound metamorphosis

In our study, histopathological evaluation was performed using a semi-quantitative scoring system, and all assessments were carried out in a blinded manner by an experienced pathologist who was unaware of the group allocations. Histopathological scoring was conducted on each tissue section from every rat at magnifications of ×5, ×10 and ×20 over 20 randomly selected fields.

Biochemical evaluation

Rat serum MDA, MPO, SOD, CAT and GPx levels were analyzed using commercial kits (YL Biont, Shanghai, China) working on the double antibody sandwich-ELISA principle. The catalog numbers of the kits used were MDA (YLA0029RA), SOD (YLA0115RA), CAT (YLA0123RA), GPx (YLA0119RA) and MPO (YLA0046RA), respectively. All measurements were performed using commercially available and validated kits, and each sample was analyzed in duplicate to minimize intra-assay variability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software. The conformity of the variables to normal distribution was evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, comparisons between the five groups were made with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Tukey post hoc test was applied for pairwise group comparisons. For variables that did not show normal distribution, five-group comparisons were analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis test and pairwise comparisons were analyzed by Mann–Whitney U test. In all statistical tests, P < 0.05 was accepted as the significance limit.

DISCUSSION

This study employed a single-dose treatment regimen utilizing FF and FFF beams for irradiation of the head and neck regions in Wistar albino rat models. Histopathological and biochemical methodologies were utilized to examine the radioprotective effects of melatonin and the acute radiobiological damage caused by FF and FFF beams on the thyroid gland and healthy laryngeal tissue.

The health of individuals undergoing neck radiation treatments is significantly influenced by the monitoring and minimization of radiation doses administered to the thyroid gland and laryngeal tissue. Studies demonstrate that within 5 years post-treatment, structural abnormalities in laryngeal tissue and thyroid dysfunctions impact 20–30% of patients undergoing head and neck radiotherapy [18–22]. Radiotherapy for head and neck cancer can induce inflammation in the laryngeal mucosa, characterized by subepithelial edema, dehydration, vascular congestion and cartilage degradation [23]. Radiotherapy primarily induces the generation of free radicals, leading to adverse effects such as hypothyroidism, Graves’ disease, adenomas and carcinomas in the thyroid gland [24, 25]. Consequently, advancements in technology have enabled the mitigation of these detrimental side effects associated with radiation. Removing the flattening filter from radiotherapy equipment while utilizing FFF beams results in elevated instantaneous dose rates, hence reducing scatter radiation, enhancing beam stability and decreasing out-of-field doses. In addition to providing dosimetric advantages [6, 26, 27], FFF technology facilitates excellent tumor coverage, minimizes normal tissue toxicity and significantly reduces the duration of therapy.

Numerous in vitro studies have investigated the impact of clinical dose rates on cell survival using FF and FFF beams across various cell lines [28–30]. Nonetheless, the literature is deficient in in vivo radiobiological studies that compare FF and FFF beams regarding their impact on cell survival in in vitro cell lines and their effects on healthy tissues. Lohse et al. irradiated T98G and U87-MG human glioma cells, together with V79 Chinese hamster lung fibroblast cells, in vitro using FF and FFF beams. Their analysis revealed that pulse interruptions per dose were associated with slight changes in estimated survival fractions; this discrepancy was observed at 5 Gy but became more pronounced at 10 Gy [31]. Conversely, several studies utilizing diverse cell lines in in vitro experiments have not demonstrated any significant disparity in cell survival between FF and FFF beams [8, 32]. Divergent dosimetric standardization choices and the varying radiosensitivity of the utilized cell lines may account for these conflicting results. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether FFF beams exert a clinical radiobiological effect on the survival of malignant and healthy cells.

This study utilized serum samples from FF and FFF beams to examine the antioxidant activity of endogenous enzymes such as SOD, CAT and GPx, which are implicated in radiation-induced oxidative stress, alongside the quantification of MDA, a byproduct of lipid peroxidation and an indicator of oxidative damage, and MPO, which is crucial in the body’s defense mechanisms and inflammatory injury. In addition to the findings of Lohse et al. [8, 28–30, 32], the acute in vivo radiobiological data derived from the serum of experimental rat models align with prior in vitro research.

Our results in the groups subjected to FF and FFF beams showed a notable reduction in the activation of SOD, CAT and GPx enzymes compared to the control group. Values of SOD, CAT and GPx were statistically significantly elevated in the group administered melatonin prior to radiation. Moreover, whereas melatonin administration significantly decreased MDA and MPO levels, FF and FFF beams substantially elevated MDA concentration and MPO activity. High-dose radiation induced oxidative stress, resulting in elevated MDA concentration and MPO activity while significantly diminishing the activity of antioxidant defense enzymes, as evidenced by biochemical analyses of rat serum samples. Similar findings were reported by Kaya et al., indicating that radiation-induced oxidative stress elevated MDA levels while decreasing GPx and SOD levels [33]. A study conducted by Aras et al. showed that elevated oxidative stress levels due to FF and FFF irradiation led to a decrease in the antioxidant defense enzymes SOD, CAT and GPx in rat blood samples [4]. A similar study identified an increase in MDA levels and MPO activity induced by radiation [34]. The biochemical findings of our study corroborate those of previous investigations, thereby confirming the radioprotective effects of melatonin against radiation-induced damage. Additionally, statistically significant differences were observed in the levels of SOD, CAT, GPx, MDA and MPO, independent of FF or FFF beams.

Key standards for evaluating radiation-induced tissue damage include macroscopic and histological analyses. This study examined several histological changes in laryngeal tissue and the thyroid gland after head and neck irradiation in rats exposed to FF and FFF beams. Additionally, the histological differences among the RT-only, melatonin + RT, melatonin-only and control groups were evaluated to examine the radioprotective and anti-inflammatory effects of melatonin against oxidative stress induced by radiotherapy.

This study examined epithelial hyperplasia, follicular hyperplasia, C-cell hyperplasia, inflammation, follicular cell hypertrophy, cellular necrosis and degradation as consequences of acute radiation-induced injury to the thyroid gland. Early histological alterations induced by radiotherapy at different dosages were also investigated in rat laryngeal tissue, encompassing edema, epithelial degradation, overall morphological degeneration, karyorrhexis and karyolysis. Histological investigations of the radiation groups subjected to FF and FFF beams revealed pervasive general morphological degeneration, inflammation, edema, epithelial degradation, karyorrhexis and karyolysis in the laryngeal tissue. The thyroid gland exhibited inflammation, hyperplasia of epithelial follicular and C-cells, deterioration, hypertrophy of follicular cells and cellular necrosis. In the control groups, the morphology of the thyroid gland and laryngeal tissue was normal; in the FF plus melatonin and FFF plus melatonin groups, the histological structure resembled that of the control group.

In comparison to the FF and FFF irradiation groups, the histological outcomes were less severe. The results established a direct association between thyroid gland damage and laryngeal mucositis, attributed to oxidative stress induced by ionizing radiation during head and neck radiotherapy. Furthermore, melatonin proved crucial for the proper repair of tissue as evidenced by histological analysis. The results indicate that melatonin-enhanced radiation significantly reduced oxidative stress in early-stage rat thyroid and laryngeal tissue damage and demonstrated a radioprotective effect by facilitating histopathological recovery. These findings are consistent with other relevant radiobiological studies [4, 34].

Biochemical and histological evidence indicates that oxidative stress induced in radiation-exposed rats resulted in damage to the laryngeal tissue and the thyroid gland. Additionally, no statistically significant difference was observed between the control group and the group treated with only melatonin. Melatonin administration demonstrated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant radioprotective effects by reducing early-stage ionizing radiation-induced oxidative stress in laryngeal tissue, the thyroid gland and blood samples. This study demonstrated that FF and FFF beams produced comparable radiobiological effects on thyroid and laryngeal tissues. Despite the established radioprotective advantages of many antioxidants, such as melatonin, the radiobiological impacts of high dosage rates of radiation using FFF beams on the thyroid gland and laryngeal tissue are still being investigated [4, 10]. This study is among the rare investigations evaluating the radioprotective effects of melatonin against early thyroid and laryngeal tissue damage in the same rat model, utilizing FF and FFF beams while considering several biochemical and histological parameters. In this study, no significant biological differences were observed between FF and FFF groups in terms of acute tissue responses. However, the potential biological implications of the dosimetric advantages of FFF beams, such as shorter treatment times and reduced peripheral dose, should not be overlooked. In particular, the high dose-rate (dose-per-pulse) characteristic of FFF beams has been discussed in the recent literature for its possible impact on DNA repair processes and oxidative stress responses, indicating that this issue warrants further investigation in future long-term and mechanistic studies.

This study evaluated the radioprotective effects of melatonin on healthy tissues, excluding malignant tissues. Future investigations utilizing various experimental rat models should examine the radioprotective effects of melatonin against high-dose-rate FFF beams in diverse organs. In the present study, the experimental design for FF and FFF beams was limited to the evaluation of normal tissue toxicity rather than tumor response. In future investigations, however, it will be important to assess not only the radioprotective effects of melatonin on normal tissues but also its potential influence on the antitumor efficacy of radiotherapy using tumor-bearing models. The melatonin dose used in this study (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) represents a pharmacologically high level commonly employed in experimental models to demonstrate radioprotective efficacy. However, this dose does not directly correspond to clinically applicable levels and therefore constitutes a translational limitation. Future studies should investigate the efficacy and safety of lower, clinically relevant doses of melatonin. The single high-dose model used in this study does not directly reflect the fractionated protocols applied in clinical head and neck radiotherapy. This limits translational applicability, and future studies employing fractionated irradiation will be necessary to strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings. In this study, only the acute phase of radiation-induced thyroid and laryngeal injury was investigated, and late effects were not assessed. This represents a limitation of our work. Future studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to evaluate both the acute and late radioprotective effects of melatonin.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that laryngeal tissue and the thyroid gland experienced significant damage due to oxidative stress induced by FF and FFF beams. Administration of melatonin before radiotherapy has been demonstrated to reduce oxidative stress levels and repair damage to the thyroid gland and laryngeal mucosa caused by radiation. Moreover, FF and FFF beams exhibited analogous radiobiological effects on thyroid and laryngeal tissues.
