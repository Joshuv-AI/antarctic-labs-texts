NUDT15 Genetic Polymorphism as a Risk Factor for Early Neutropenia During Valganciclovir Prophylaxis in Lung Transplant Patients
Authors: Yurie Katsube, Keisuke Umemura, Yuzuki Urabe, Miori Ono, Yoshiki Katada, Daiki Hira, Akihiro Ohsumi, Daisuke Nakajima, Masahiro Tsuda, Shunsaku Nakagawa, Tomoyuki Mizuno, Miki Nagao, Hiroshi Date, Tomohiro Terada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundValganciclovir (VGCV) prophylaxis effectively prevents cytomegalovirus infection in lung transplant patients. However, VGCV‐induced neutropenia causes early cessation. Nucleoside diphosphate‐linked moiety X‐type motif (NUDT) 15 degrades the ganciclovir (GCV) triphosphate, an active metabolite. We assessed the effects of NUDT15 variants on neutropenia and VGCV cessation in recipients of lung transplants.MethodsWe recruited 28 patients who had received lung transplants and VGCV prophylaxis and genotyped NUDT15 exons 1–3 using Sanger sequencing. Neutrophil counts were monitored from 1 month to 1 year in the wild‐type and NUDT15 reduced‐function variant groups. Cumulative incidences of neutropenia (< 1500/mm3) and neutropenia‐related cessation within 1 year, including late‐onset neutropenia, were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis. A subgroup analysis was conducted focusing on patients with stable renal function, the primary route of excretion for GCV.ResultsOf the 28 patients, nine carried NUDT15 variants (Arg139Cys, Val18Ile, Val18_Val19insGlyVal, Arg139Cys/Val18Ile). The neutrophil count nadir within 1 month of treatment was lower in the NUDT15‐variant group than in the wild‐type group. A higher incidence of neutropenia and VGCV cessation was observed in the NUDT15‐variant group, but without statistical significance. Among 13 patients with stable renal function, all four in the NUDT15‐variant group developed neutropenia and cessation within 60 days, compared with two of nine in the wild‐type group. Covariance analysis showed that NUDT15 variants were associated with decreased neutrophil counts, independent of GCV trough concentration.Conclusion NUDT15 variants increase the risk of early neutropenia during VGCV prophylaxis in lung transplant recipients.

Introduction

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection is a severe and life‐threatening invasive visceral disease in immunocompromised recipients of lung transplants [1], and it is associated with a substantial impact on indirect mortality [2, 3]. The Fourth International Consensus Guidelines on Cytomegalovirus Management in Solid Organ Transplantation advocate prophylactic antiviral therapy over preemptive therapy in recipients of lung transplants, recommending prophylaxis with valganciclovir (VGCV) for 6–12 months post‐transplantation [4]. At our institute, extending VGCV prophylaxis from 6 months to 1 year in patients with CMV serostatus mismatch has shown superior protective effects, and shorter prophylaxis duration was clearly associated with heightened risk of CMV viremia, with leukopenia being the most common reason for early discontinuation [5]. We previously reported that blood concentrations of ganciclovir (GCV), the active metabolite of VGCV, correlate with leukopenia [6], and therapeutic drug monitoring could prevent leukopenia while maintaining target levels [7]. However, some patients develop neutropenia despite normal renal function and GCV levels. Therefore, we focused on potential genetic factors relevant to intracellular GCV metabolism.

GCV, a nucleoside analog, is phosphorylated by the viral UL97 protein kinase to form its monophosphate derivative, which is subsequently phosphorylated by cellular kinases to generate the triphosphate forms [8]. Intracellular accumulation of GCV triphosphate contributes to hematological toxicity [9]. Recent studies have identified nucleoside diphosphate‐linked moiety X‐type motif (NUDT)15 as a key mediator in the dephosphorylation of GCV triphosphate, which cleaves pyrophosphate from triphosphorylated nucleotides [10, 11]. Genetic polymorphisms in NUDT15 are highly prevalent in East Asian populations [12], and the p.Arg139Cys variant leads to significantly reduced enzymatic activity [13]. We hypothesize that NUDT15 variants could be predictive factor of neutropenia under VGCV administration. This study investigated whether NUDT15 variants increase neutropenia risk and affect VGCV continuation in recipients of lung transplants who receive CMV prophylaxis.

Materials and Methods

A total of 28 patients with lung transplants initiating CMV prophylaxis with VGCV were recruited at Kyoto University Hospital between July 2021 and October 2024 and followed for 1 year from treatment initiation. GCV was initially administered intravenously at 5 mg/kg twice daily for 3 weeks post‐transplantation, then switched to oral VGCV at 900 mg once daily. Patients who discontinued VGCV for reasons other than neutropenia or receiving VGCV of 1800 mg/day as treatment were excluded. Patients were divided into wild‐type and NUDT15‐variant groups based on exons 1–3 of NUDT15 genotype determined via Sanger sequencing. The genotyping method is detailed in the Table S1 in Supporting Information. The nadir of neutrophils, white blood cell (WBCs), hemoglobin, and platelets was monitored over 1 year. In our institute, VGCV is administered as long as possible in high‐risk donor+/recipient− patients, prioritizing CMV prevention over hematotoxicity. In some cases, VGCV is discontinued when a decrease in neutrophil count, predictive of a clear and rapid onset of neutropenia or leukopenia, is observed, even if the dose reduction criteria are met. Neutropenia was defined as neutrophils < 1500/mm3, our institutional criterion for dose reduction or discontinuation. Neutropenia‐related events were defined as the incidence rate for both neutropenia and VGCV cessation. Fluctuations in renal function disrupt GCV pharmacokinetics, complicating the interpretation of long‐term observational results due to intra‐individual variations. Therefore, we performed subgroup analysis excluding patients with impaired renal function. Renal impairment was defined as a reduction in renal function of ≥ 25% from baseline in estimated glomerular filtration rate, according to the RIFLE classification [14]. Patients lost to follow‐up or who completed treatment were censored, as indicated in the Kaplan–Meier curves. CMV reactivation and infection were assessed during the observation period.

We focused on the early post‐administration period to evaluate the impact of NUDT15 genetic polymorphisms for 35 days, when GCV trough levels were obtained for all patients. Individual GCV trough levels at the onset of neutropenia were estimated by Bayesian estimation from the closest available measurement using a nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling program based on our previously reported population pharmacokinetic model [6]. The details of the Bayesian estimation are provided in the supplementary document. Observed and estimated values showed no systematic biases or significant discrepancies (Figure S1).

Results

Of the 28 patients, nine (32.1%) carried NUDT15 reduced‐function variants: Arg139Cys heterozygous (*1/*3, n = 5), Val18Ile heterozygous (*1/*5, n = 1), Val18_Val19insGlyVal + Arg139Cys heterozygous (*1/*2, n = 1), Arg139Cys homozygous (*3/*3, n = 1), and Arg139Cys/Val18Ile compound heterozygous (*3/*5, n = 1). No other minor variants were detected in exons 1–3 of NUDT15. At VGCV initiation, the mycophenolate mofetil dose was comparable between the two groups (Table S2). None of the patients received mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, which are known to cause bone marrow suppression. All patients received trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (80 mg/400 mg) on a thrice‐weekly schedule with dose adjustments based on renal function. During the observation, no discontinuation due to adverse events attributed to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole was observed.

The NUDT15‐variant group had significantly lower neutrophil and WBC nadirs within 1 month post‐treatment, but no significant difference in late‐onset hematologic toxicity (Figure 1A, B). The time to onset of neutropenia was significantly shorter in the variant group [median 32 (4–148) vs. 96 (49–340) days; p < 0.05] (Figure 1C). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed higher, although not statistically significant, incidences of neutropenia, VGCV cessation (Figure 1D), and neutropenia‐related events (Figure S2) in the NUDT15‐variant group, compared with those of the wild‐type group. Among patients without ≥ 25% renal function decline, the cumulative event‐free rate of neutropenia‐related events was significantly lower in the NUDT15‐variant group (Figure 1E). No significant difference in the Bayesian‐estimated GCV trough levels at neutrophil nadir onset was observed between groups. Analysis of covariance showed that NUDT15 variants significantly affected neutrophil nadir, independent of GCV levels (p < 0.05, Figure 2).

Among patients who discontinued VGCV, one patient in each group discontinued VGCV at 254 (wild‐type) and 64 days (variant); both developed CMV viremia. VGCV was re‐initiated, and CMV disease did not develop. Two patients with NUDT15 variant discontinued VGCV earlier (within 1 month) owing to hematologic toxicity; one subsequently developed CMV hepatitis and the other CMV enteritis.

Discussion

The impact of NUDT15 variants on VGCV‐related neutropenia in patients who receive lung transplants remains poorly defined. This study aimed to determine the effects of NUDT15 variants on neutropenia following prophylactic administration of VGCV after lung transplantation. Carriers of NUDT15 variants developed neutropenia earlier than wild‐type patients, and the effect was more pronounced in cases that excluded impaired renal function. Notably, two patients with NUDT15 variants who discontinued VGCV early developed CMV disease. Our findings suggest that the NUDT15 genotyping helps to identify patients at high risk of early VGCV discontinuation due to neutropenia in patients with lung transplantation. Since early VGCV discontinuation is associated with CMV disease [4], patients carrying NUDT15 variants with reduced function should be considered for closer monitoring, and the use of alternative antiviral agents with low myelosuppressive properties.

Most patients with NUDT15 variants developed neutropenia within 3 months of initiating VGCV. At 1‐year follow‐up of the recipients of lung transplants in our study, the NUDT15‐variant group tended to have higher cumulative incidences of neutropenia and VGCV cessation owing to neutropenia than the wild‐type group. Bahadur et al. [15] reported that early leukopenia within 60 days of prophylactic VGCV was more common in patients with kidney transplants with NUDT15‐variants than in those with the wild type. These observations indicate that NUDT15 variants consistently predispose recipients of transplants to early VGCV‐induced leukopenia, regardless of the transplanted organ or dosing strategy.

On the other hand, the relationship between late‐onset myelosuppression and NUDT15 variants requires further discussion. Although the association between thiopurine‐induced leukopenia and NUDT15 variants is well established [16, 17], late‐onset leukopenia is less predictable using NUDT15 R139C genotypes in Japanese patients with inflammatory bowel disease [16], and dose reduction of thiopurines in patients heterozygous for NUDT15*3 did not prevent late‐onset leukopenia [18]. DNA thioguanine has been reported as a direct marker of mercaptopurine‐induced late‐onset leukopenia (> 2 months) in patients with inflammatory bowel disease [19, 20]. Furthermore, intracellular GCV triphosphate levels correlate well with neutropenia [9]. Therefore, patients who develop late‐onset myelotoxicity might not be explained by NUDT15 genotype, and other mechanisms for GCV triphosphate accumulation should be considered.

In patients with NUDT15 variants, neutropenia occurred even at low plasma GCV concentrations, suggesting that simple dose reduction may be ineffective. Underdosing of VGCV could raise viral resistance risk [21]. However, when either the recipient or donor had a homozygous deficient NUDT15 diplotype, or when both the recipient and donor had a heterozygous diplotype, CMV control improved during acyclovir use in patients with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [11]. Alternative prophylactic strategies, such as careful dose adjustment of VGCV or administration of letermovir, an agent with low myelotoxicity, are recommended for further consideration.

This study has several limitations. First, postoperative inflammation and transfusion‐related changes in WBC, platelet, and red blood cell counts complicate the accurate assessment of the effect of prophylactic IV GCV administration. Second, therapeutic drug monitoring of mycophenolic acid in recipients of lung transplants has not been performed at our institute, and analysis of the impact of mycophenolate mofetil on bone marrow suppression is insufficient. Finally, in patients with impaired renal function, either clinician adjustment of VGCV dosing or renal dysfunction itself may reflect poor post‐transplant outcomes, which may have complicated the interpretation of the results. In our multivariate analysis, we included the GCV concentration as a covariate to adjust for variables influencing pharmacokinetics. Nevertheless, studies with an expanded sample size are needed to comprehensively examine other factors, such as age, the dosage of mycophenolic acid, and the neutrophil count at the initiation of VGCV administration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that recipients of lung transplants carrying reduced functional NUDT15 variants are at high risk of early‐onset neutropenia after VGCV administration. NUDT15 genotype testing could be beneficial for predicting early VGCV‐induced neutropenia and aiding therapeutic decision‐making for CMV prophylaxis after lung transplantation. Neutropenia occurred even at low GCV trough levels, suggesting that safe plasma ranges may differ according to NUDT15 genotype. Further studies are needed to establish a NUDT15 genotype‐guided VGCV dosing strategy that maximizes CMV prophylaxis while minimizing toxicity.
