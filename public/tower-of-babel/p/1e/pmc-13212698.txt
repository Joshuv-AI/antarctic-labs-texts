Unraveling BCR Repertoire Diversity and its Impact on Glucocorticoid Therapy in Pediatric Idiopathic Nephrotic Syndrome
Authors: Yeping Luo, Chan Zou, Xinjie Yu, Chengxian Guo, Hongmei Dai, Jie Huang, Mai Xun, Cuirong Duan, Song Li, Zhihui Li, Guoping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIdiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS) is the most common glomerular disease in children, but its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Although glucocorticoids (GC) are the first-line treatment, approximately 10% of INS cases are steroid-resistant (SRNS), and 50% may progress to refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS). B cells play a crucial role in INS pathogenesis, treatment, and prognosis. This study aims to explore B-cell receptor repertoire (BCRR) characteristics in INS patients to identify molecular signatures associated with disease outcomes and develop early risk prediction models for RNS and SRNS.MethodsA case-control study was conducted using peripheral blood samples from 40 initial diagnosed INS patients and 56 age-matched healthy controls. BCRR was sequenced using dimer-avoided multiplex PCR, followed by Illumina NovaSeq sequencing. Immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) sequences were mapped and analyzed for diversity, clone expansion and shared clone, isotype usage, class-switch recombination (CSR), IGHV/J gene usage, CDR3 length and somatic hypermutation (SHM). Additionally, a prospective cohort study was conducted on INS patients, with BCRR measured at baseline and follow-up to track changes in response to treatment. Longitudinal analysis was performed to identify specific B-cell clones and predict treatment resistance.ResultsThe BCRR of INS patients exhibited lower evenness compared to controls, with significant increases in the usage of IGHM/D mutated and IgE/IgG12 isotypes. Public B-cell clones, including five specific CDR3 amino acid sequences, were identified across INS patients. NRNS patients showed increased clonal expansion with specific CDR3 sequences characters on IGHV5-51_IGHJ6 sequences and shorter CDR3 lengths, suggesting strong and focused autoimmune responses. In contrast, RNS and SRNS patients exhibited enhanced IgE-mediated immune responses, characterized by increased IgE BCRR diversity, decreased evenness and increased CSR. Early prediction models were developed based on BCRR parameters, successfully distinguishing between RNS and NRNS, SRNS and SSNS patients.ConclusionB-cell-mediated humoral immunity plays a pivotal role in INS pathogenesis, with distinct immune profiles associated with clinical outcomes. Non- refractory nephrotic syndrome (NRNS) patients exhibit clonal expansion with antigen-driven immune responses, whereas SRNS and RNS patients demonstrate persistent IgE-mediated responses that are resistant to GC but sensitive to second-line immunosuppressants. These findings offer new insights into the mechanisms of steroid resistance and provide a basis for early diagnosis and personalized treatment strategies in INS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10875-026-01994-3.Keywords: B-cell receptor repertoire, Glucocorticoid/steroid resistance, Idiopathic nephrotic syndrome, Humoral immunity

Introduction

Idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS) is characterized by significant proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, hyperlipidemia, and edema [1]. Although the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood, the dysregulation of T and B lymphocytes plays a pivotal role in its development [2]. INS is the most common glomerular diseases among children, with incidence rates varying between 1.15 and 16.9 per 100,000 children, and accounts for approximately 30% of pediatric hospitalizations related to urinary disorders [3]. Glucocorticoids (GCs) are the first-line treatment for INS and are administered to all newly diagnosed patients [1].

However, the response to GC treatment varies widely among patients. This variability leads to significant differences in the duration and cumulative doses of GC therapy. While the majority of children respond positively, approximately 10% develop steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), for which standard GC regimens are ineffective. Moreover, among those who initially respond, around 50% progress to frequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome (FRNS) or steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (SDNS) [4]. Refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS) was defined as including steroid-dependent, frequently relapsing, and steroid-resistant NS, consistent with previous reports [5–7] and present a high risk of progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Additionally, prolonged GC therapy is associated with serious side effects, including bone metabolism disorders, life-threatening infections, hypertension, and Cushing’s syndrome, which negatively impact growth and increase mortality risks [1]. While early initiation of second-line therapies, such as mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and levamisole, may benefit certain patients, definitive conclusions are still lacking, despite ongoing clinical trials [4].

Previous studies have examined individual differences in treatment responses among INS patients, but effective biomarkers to predict outcomes for GCs and second-line therapies remain elusive. Notably, the response to GC treatment has emerged as one of the most reliable predictors of clinical outcomes [1]. Recent research has highlighted the role of B lymphocyte-mediated immune responses in the onset, treatment, and prognosis of INS [8]. B-cell activation begins when the B-cell receptor (BCR) recognizes an antigen, leading to the proliferation and differentiation into antibody-secreting plasma cells and memory B cells. The BCR is an antibody anchored to the B-cell membrane, which stimulates activation through intracellular signaling pathways. Composed of two heavy chains and two light chains, the BCR’s genetic components are formed through recombination of variable (V), diversity (D), joining (J), and constant (C) regions [9]. Each mature B cell generates a unique BCR, leading to the production of antibodies with specific antigen-binding capacities [10]. Some B cells undergo somatic hypermutation (SHM) and class-switch recombination (CSR) in germinal centers, generating a diverse array of antibodies [11, 12].

During B-cell development, VDJ gene segments undergo random rearrangements, followed by class switching and SHM upon antigen exposure, resulting in a highly diverse BCR repertoire capable of recognizing a broad range of external antigens [13]. An individual can harbor approximately 10^11 distinct BCRs, reflecting the breadth of immune responses [10]. The complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the BCR heavy chain, particularly CDR3, serve as the primary antigen-binding sites due to their highly variable amino acid composition [14]. Analyzing the BCR repertoire offers insights into humoral immune responses under various conditions, including autoimmune diseases, cancers, and transplant rejection. This type of analysis has proven effective in characterizing immune response disorders and predicting clinical outcomes, shedding light on mechanisms of disease onset and drug resistance [15–17].

In this study, we underscore the significant differences in BCR characteristics between newly diagnosed children with INS and healthy controls, highlighting the humoral immune dysregulation associated with INS. By establishing a prospective cohort of INS patients, we aim to investigate the dynamic effects of GC treatment on BCR repertoires and assess their potential as biomarkers to predict treatment responses, thereby guiding personalized therapeutic strategies.

This analysis focused on the antigen-binding (IgH VDJ) and constant regions of the BCR heavy chain, enabling the evaluation of clone frequencies and BCR molecular character. BCR diversity was assessed and compared between INS patients and healthy controls. Evenness metrics (DE50, Pielou’s and Simpson-evenness) quantify the similarity between the frequencies of lymphocyte populations recognizing the same antigen (i.e., “clones”), which represent clone expansion and immune responses. INS patients exhibited significantly higher Shannon entropy and Gini index values compared to healthy controls, along with notably lower DE50 and evenness values (P < 0.05), consistent with elevated B-cell activation and clonal expansion (Fig. 1A and B). To assess whether INS patients exhibit specific clonal responses to corresponding antigens, we analyzed shared clones across patients. Five common CDR3 amino acid sequences were identified among patients: CARDRYYFDYW, CARGQYYFDYW, CIHYGSGSYSTDYW, CARGGDYW, and CARRYCSSTSCLIDYW. However, these findings likely reflect the heterogeneity of the immune responses in INS (Fig. 1C), indicating a lack of sensitivity and highlighting the diverse humoral immune profiles among INS patients.

INS patients exhibited a significantly higher frequency of mutated IgM/IgD isotype in INS patients (P < 0.05), while unmutated forms were less prevalent (Fig. 1D). Class-switching events between isotypes were also more frequent, particularly involving IgM/IgD, IgE, and IgG12 (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Moreover, IGHE and IGHG12 isotypes were significantly more frequent in INS patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D), indicating that these isotypes play a critical role in immune responses in INS. SHM can increase antibody affinity, and elevated SHM rates were observed across isotypes in INS patients, except for IGHA (Fig. 1F). These results collectively suggest that INS patients mount an antigen-specific immune response primarily mediated by IgM/IgD, IGHE and IGHG12, with evidence of antibody affinity maturation.

Prior studies have suggested that self-antigens, such as podocyte antigens, may trigger humoral immunity in INS, leading to increased autoantibody production. To investigate this, we analyzed the frequency of IGHV gene usage and CDR3 length, both of which are linked to self-reactivity and autoimmune conditions, and can also serve as biomarkers for certain clinical events [14]. IGHV5-51 usage was significantly elevated in INS patients (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1G), and shorter CDR3 segments were more common (Fig. 1H). Specific pairings of IGHV and IGHJ genes were also significantly increased, potentially serving as biomarkers for the disease (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that self-antigens drive clonal B-cell expansion in INS.

Baseline clinical characteristics of RNS and NRNS groups

Based on clinical conclusions, the study categorized patients into RNS and NRNS groups. Clinical characteristics such as average age, presence of hematuria, albumin, cholesterol levels, complement, and immunoglobulin levels were collected (Table 2). BCRR analysis revealed that while both the D50 diversity metrics and Gini index were significantly different in the NRNS and RNS groups compared to the control group, the Simpson evenness index was significantly lower in the NRNS group than in the RNS group. This suggests that both NRNS and RNS patients exhibit B-cell immune responses, but the clonal expansion in NRNS patients may be more concentrated and indicative of a stronger immune response (Fig. 2A). Further analysis of clonal expansion in NRNS and RNS patients showed that the top10, 20, and 50 B-cell clones were significantly more common in NRNS patients (P < 0.05), suggesting that NRNS patients focus their immune response on fewer clones (Fig. 2B and C). To gain insights into the shared B-cell responses, we analyzed BCR clones only shared between NRNS patients. Two specific CDR3 sequences (CARDRYYFDYM and CARGGDYW) were identified, and not specific CDR3 sequences only shared in RNS and CT group (Supplementary Fig. 2A). However, the sensitivity of these specific CDR3 sequences in NRNS patients was relatively low, potentially related to somatic hypermutation during antibody affinity maturation. Considering that proteins with similar sequences may share structural properties, we categorized similar clones based on shared IGHV and IGHJ genes, CDR3 length, and sequence similarity. The comparison revealed that the percentage of similar clone BCRs were significantly more frequent in NRNS patients than in healthy controls (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D), with enrichment of a specific CDR3 sequence (CAR***YYYGMDVW) in NRNS patients (Fig. 2E).

These findings underscore the importance of IGHE isotype B cells in the immune responses associated with INS progression. Clinical characteristics of SRNS and SSNS children at baseline

A total of 40 children with newly diagnosed idiopathic nephrotic syndrome (INS) were enrolled. Peripheral blood samples were collected at baseline, and short-term clinical outcomes were recorded during a one-year follow-up period after onset. Among them, 7 (17.5%) were diagnosed with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS), 33 (82.5%) with steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome (SSNS), 10 (30.3%) with frequently relapsing or steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (FRNS/SDNS), 11 (27.5%) with infrequently relapsing nephrotic syndrome (IFRNS), and 12 (30%) did not relapse. Patients with SRNS, FRNS, or SDNS who did not respond to standard glucocorticoid (GC) therapy or experienced frequent relapses were classified as having refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS). RNS patients were treated with second-line immunosuppressive agents (ISAs) in addition to GCs, including 10 who received FK506 (tacrolimus) and 6 who received mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). One patient with FRNS who did not respond adequately to MMF subsequently received additional FK506 on top of MMF therapy. All SRNS patients achieved remission after receiving second-line immunosuppressive therapy (Table 4).

We analyzed the BCR repertoire between the SRNS and SSNS groups. Our findings revealed a marked increase in IGHE isotype diversity among SRNS patients, as indicated by Shannon, Simpson, and Gini indices (Fig. 4A and B). Additionally, the proportion of IGHE isotype B cells was notably higher in the SRNS cohort (Fig. 4C). CSR analysis also showed significantly greater CSR events involving IGHE and other isotypes (e.g., IGHA, IGHG34, IGHM/IGHD) in SRNS patients, accompanied by a notable increase in somatic hypermutation (SHM) across all isotypes, except IGHA (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4E, F). These results highlight the critical role of IGHE isotype B cells in the immune response involved in SRNS pathogenesis.

These findings suggest that IGHE isotype B-cell responses contribute to SRNS in children. Moreover, other studies have shown that different B-cell subtypes respond variably to immunosuppressive therapies [13], raising the possibility that IGHE-mediated B-cell responses may underlie GC resistance in SRNS patients. We further investigated whether similar B-cell responses to GC tolerance exist in RNS. By tracking persistent B-cell clones before and after GC treatment in the same INS patients, we aimed to identify B-cell clones and immune response features associated with GC tolerance.

Our study tracked the BCR repertoire over one year of immunosuppressive treatment in INS patients. We found that, in SSNS and NRNS patients, baseline BCR diversity (Shannon) and richness (uCDR3) significantly decreased after 1 or 3 months of GC therapy, while evenness increased significantly (increased D50, Simpson evenness, Pielou’s evenness, and decreased Gini index) (supplementary Fig. 3 A and 4 A). These results align with previous findings that GC achieves efficacy in sensitive patients by inhibiting B-cell clone expansion or inducing B-cell apoptosis, thereby reducing the immune response potential [18, 19]. Conversely, SRNS and RNS patients showed no significant change or a slight increase in BCR diversity (Shannon), evenness (D50, Simpson evenness, Pielou’s evenness, and Gini) indices (supplementary Fig. 3 A and 4 A).

Further tracking of specific features revealed that baseline clonal expansion (top10/top20/top50/top100) in GC-sensitive patients (SSNS and NRNS) decreased significantly after 1 month of GC therapy (supplementary Fig. 4 C), and the proportion of persistent clones also dropped markedly (supplementary Fig. 5 A). Interestingly, a specific baseline similar clone (CAR***YYYGMDVW)), which we previously identified, was significantly reduced in frequency and number in GC-sensitive patients after 1 month of treatment (supplementary Fig. 5B and 5 C). In contrast, clonal expansion in GC-tolerance patients showed a continuous upward trend post-treatment (supplementary Fig. 4 C).

Dynamic analysis of Immunoglobulin isotype diversity and proportions showed that the significantly increased baseline IGHE isotype and not decreased IGHE isotype immune response observed in SRNS and RNS persisted through 1 month of GC therapy (during which only GC was administered), but disappeared after 3 months (once immunosuppressive agents were added) (Fig. 5A and B). Furthermore, the proportion of persistent clone BCR was not decreased (supplementary 5 A). Consistently, CSR between IGHE and IGHM_IGHD, which was significantly elevated at baseline in tolerance patients, also persisted through 1 month of treatment but disappeared after 3 months. This pattern aligns with the timing of the addition of second-line therapies (~ 3.9 months) (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, SHM in IGHE not decreased after 1 month of GC therapy in tolerance patients (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results suggest that a high level of IGHE-mediated immune response at baseline may drive subsequent treatment tolerance in newly diagnosed INS patients.

Development of a Predictive Model Based on BCRR Characteristics for Early Treatment Decisions in Children with INS

Our analysis identified significant differences in BCRR characteristics among children with INS at baseline and across various immunosuppressive treatment groups. We utilized these characteristics, measured at baseline, one month, and three months, to construct a model aimed at predicting the need for second-line immunosuppressants.

The model incorporated several key parameters: IGH Simpson evenness, IGHE Shannon index, IGHE Simpson index, IGHE D50, IGHG34 Simpson index, the percentage of IGHE by clone and by reads, and class-switch recombination levels among different immunoglobulin isotypes. Additionally, it included the usage frequencies of specific immunoglobulin heavy chain variable genes, such as IGHV-7–56, IGHV3-11, and IGHV1-69. Our results showed that the baseline BCRR characteristic model effectively distinguished between children receiving GC therapy and those on combination treatments (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Idiopathic Nephrotic Syndrome (INS) is the most common glomerular disease and the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in children; however, its pathogenesis, particularly regarding the immune system’s role, remains poorly understood. In this study, we conducted B cell receptor (BCR) repertoire analysis to explore humoral immune dysregulation in newly diagnosed children with INS and its implications for glucocorticoid (GC) treatment efficacy. Our findings indicate significant differences in BCR diversity, class-switch recombination, and somatic hypermutation rates between INS patients and healthy controls, as well as between patients with refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS) and those with non-refractory nephrotic syndrome (NRNS). In this study, the definition of refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS) adopted a broader perspective, encompassing steroid-resistant, steroid-dependent, and frequently relapsing NS. This classification is consistent with previous clinical studies and reflects the fact that these phenotypes all pose significant therapeutic challenges and require alternative or intensified immunosuppressive strategies. Notably, all SRNS patients in our cohort achieved remission with calcineurin inhibitors, and no CNI-resistant or monogenic SRNS was identified, supporting the appropriateness of grouping SRNS with SDNS and FRNS under the RNS category in our context. This broader definition aligns with clinical practice and facilitates the exploration of immune characteristics in these difficult-to-treat subtypes [5–7]. These results underscore the potential of BCR repertoire characteristics as biomarkers for identifying GC tolerance and predicting treatment outcomes in INS.

Previous studies have established that B lymphocytes, through antibody production, play a crucial role in INS. Antibodies such as anti-Nephrin and anti-Vinculin IgG have been implicated in targeting podocyte antigens, contributing to the pathogenesis of INS [20, 21]. Our study aligns with this, revealing decreased evenness metrics values in INS patients, indicating enhanced B cell activation and clonal expansion. The elevated somatic hypermutation (SHM) rates and increased class-switch recombination further support the notion of an efficient humoral immune response in INS. These immune changes may be associated with disease-specific antigens, such as podocyte antigens, which are believed to trigger autoimmune responses. Moreover, B cell activation and the production of autoantibodies have been shown to promote the progression of INS by disrupting critical structures like the glomerular endothelial barrier and the podocyte cytoskeleton [21, 22]. Additionally, B cells contribute to immune dysregulation through non-antibody mechanisms, including the secretion of immunomodulatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines [8]. Notably, we observed increased usage of specific immunoglobulin heavy chain variable (IGHV) genes, such as IGHV5-51, which has been associated with autoimmune diseases. Prior studies have linked IGHV5-51 to the development of IgA multiple myeloma and colorectal adenocarcinoma prognosis [23, 24]. IGHV5-51 is also involved in the recognition of the diarrhea-specific autoantigen transglutaminase 2, marking diarrhea-specific immune responses [25, 26]. While IGHV5-51 may mediate antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) to reduce the risk of HIV infection, its usage has been shown to increase significantly in patients infected with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) [27]. Additionally, the shorter CDR3 length observed in INS patients may reflect a more focused, antigen-driven B-cell response, potentially linked to autoantibody production [28]. These findings align with previous studies showing biased IGHV usage and altered CDR3 characteristics in B cells in autoimmune diseases, leading to pathogenic immune responses.

Regarding treatment response, the efficacy of GC therapy is closely tied to B cell responses, as GCs have been shown to suppress lymphocyte activity both in vitro and in vivo, correlating with clinical outcomes [29, 30]. Our analysis revealed notable differences in BCR repertoire characteristics between NRNS and RNS patients.

NRNS patients exhibited lower uniformity and a stronger clonal expansion suggesting stronger immune response. Which combined with biased IGHV gene usage and shorter CDR3 lengths, suggesting a autoimmune related dysregulated immune response that could contribute to GC sensitive. In contrast, the notable difference of RNS patients compared to NRNS was the more stronger IGHE B cell immune response. The shared CDR3 sequences found in NRNS but not in RNS patients further highlight the differences in antigen recognition and immune activation between these groups. These shared sequences could serve as biomarkers for predicting GC responsiveness, although their low prevalence underscores the heterogeneity of immune responses in INS. The enrichment of specific CDR3 sequences, such as CAR***YYYGMDVW, in NRNS patients emphasizes the potential role of certain B-cell clones in mediating effective immune responses in these patients. The distinct IGHV usage patterns observed between NRNS and RNS patients, particularly the increased usage of IGHV7-56 in NRNS, further support the notion that BCR repertoire bias may influence treatment outcomes. Previous studies have linked specific IGHV genes to protective or pathogenic immune responses, and our findings suggest that this bias may be relevant in predicting GC responsiveness in INS patients.

While previous research has focused on identifying biomarkers to predict GC efficacy and resistance, limited significant gene mutations affecting GC drug concentrations in INS patients have been identified to date [31]. Cytokines may play a crucial role in GC resistance, with studies showing that levels of specific cytokines, such as interferon-γ, IL-6, and IL-9, are generally elevated in SSNS patients. Additionally, serum levels of ILs, IgG, and related factors in urine have been explored as potential predictors of steroid sensitivity in INS patients [2, 32]. In this study, we established a prospective cohort of children with newly diagnosed INS and analyzed their responses to immunosuppressants based on their clinical outcomes, with a focus on B-cell clonal characteristics. Our findings showed that early GC treatment significantly reduced B-cell clonal diversity in sensitive patients but had little effect on tolerancepatients, suggesting the presence of GC-insensitive B-cell clones in tolerance patients. This is consistent with previous research indicating that pathogenic B cells, including long-lived switched memory B cells, have been found to resist GCs [33]. Research in adult membranous nephropathy indicates that patients who respond favorably to GCs or a combination of GCs with tacrolimus experience a significant reduction in circulating B cell clone post-treatment [34]. Additionally, a prospective cohort study found that high levels of pathological IgM antibodies could predict relapses and steroid dependency in INS, underscoring the importance of baseline B cell characteristics in anticipating treatment responses [29]. Further analysis revealed that GC treatment significantly reduced the diversity of multiple antibody isotypes in sensitive patients, while IgE-mediated immune responses remained activity in tolerance patients after GC treatment. This is in contrast to the self-limiting nature of normal immune responses and suggests an abnormal activation mechanism for IgE B cells in INS patients. The production of IgE is closely related to the activation of type 2 helper T cells (Th2), which are thought to play a crucial role in INS pathogenesis. Additionally, class switching of IgE is associated with the actions of IL-4 and IL-13, which are key cytokines in the development of INS [8]. Elevated IgE levels have also been linked to remission and relapse in minimal change disease (MCD), suggesting its potential role as a biomarker for predicting steroid responses in INS children [35]. High levels of IgE may promote GC tolerance through interactions with other cytokines, leading to frequent relapses.

These findings are consistent with earlier studies indicating that IgE B cells represent a unique B-cell population in INS patients that is associated with GC tolerance. Elevated IgE levels not only correlate with an increased risk of relapse but may also promote frequent relapse and GC resistance through interactions with T cells. While research on GC-resistant B-cell populations is still ongoing, prior literature has reported associations between B cells, such as IgM antibodies and hypo-glycosylated antibodies, and GC dependency [29]. These findings further emphasize the importance of IgE in INS, suggesting that IgE plays a key role in the disease’s pathogenesis and may serve as a novel biomarker for early identification of GC-sensitive or resistant patients, as well as RNS and NRNS providing a theoretical basis for individualized treatment.

Furthermore, the comparison between SSNS and SRNS in our study has important clinical implications. Identifying patients with a high risk of SRNS at the beginning of treatment could facilitate timely genetic testing for monogenic causes, which has been recommended in children over one year of age with CNI-resistant disease or strong suspicion of a monogenic etiology. In our cohort, however, all SRNS patients were older than 3 years at disease onset and achieved remission with calcineurin inhibitors, making a monogenic cause unlikely. Therefore, none of the SRNS patients underwent genetic testing, which is consistent with clinical practice where such testing is typically reserved for infants or for patients who fail to respond to CNIs. This approach balances clinical utility, cost considerations, and the relatively low yield of monogenic findings in older, CNI-responsive children [1]. Early recognition of patients at risk of monogenic SRNS remains critical to minimize unnecessary exposure to steroids and other immunosuppressive agents, improve outcomes, and avoid side effects. Our findings suggest that BCR repertoire characteristics, particularly patterns of IGHV usage, CDR3 features, and IgE-related immune responses, could serve as potential biomarkers for stratifying patients early in the disease course and guiding personalized therapy.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and although age was balanced between groups, no further stratification by age was performed, which may have influenced the results. Second, the analysis focused solely on the immune repertoire level without integrating other omics approaches, such as transcriptomics, which limited the predictive power of the findings. Third, the study explored only the humoral immune characteristics of B cells, without examining their interactions with other immune cells, particularly T cells. Future research could analyze the paired immune repertoires of T and B cell chains from the same samples to better elucidate the role of T-cell responses and T–B cell interactions in the pathogenesis, treatment response, and prognosis of children with INS, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of immune dysregulation across different INS subtypes. Additionally, no in vitro experiments were conducted to investigate the underlying mechanisms in depth. Given the low frequency of IgE B cells in peripheral blood, future studies could employ flow cytometric cell sorting and single-cell sequencing to further explore the mechanisms of glucocorticoid tolerance mediated by IgE B cells in RNS and SRNS patients. Through a systematic analysis of patients’ immune response characteristics, this study revealed a unique immune mechanism involving IgE-related B-cell populations in GC resistance, offering new insights and directions for the precise management and individualized treatment of INS. By deepening our understanding of these biomarkers, we hope to better understand the pathophysiology of INS, improve treatment outcomes, and reduce relapse risk.

Methods

Study Design and Subjects

This case-control study was conducted between October 2021 and May 31, 2022, collecting data from newly diagnosed INS patients at the Department of Nephrology and Rheumatology, Hunan Children’s Hospital. Additionally, age-matched healthy children who underwent routine health check-ups at the Pediatric Outpatient Department of Third Xiangya Hospital between September and November 2022 were included as controls. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committees of both Hunan Children’s Hospital (Approval Number: HCHLL-2021-82) and Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval Number: 22509). Informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all children after thorough communication, with the children’s willingness taken into full consideration. Inclusion criteria for newly diagnosed INS patients were (1) age between 1 and 18 years old; (2) clinical diagnosis of initial-onset nephrotic syndrome (INS) with no prior treatment with glucocorticoids (GCs) or other immunosuppressants; (3) ability of the child or guardians to maintain good communication with follow-up staff; and (4) signed informed consent from the child’s guardian. Exclusion criteria included (1) a family history of chronic glomerulonephritis or nephrotic syndrome, or uremia; (2) secondary nephrotic syndrome (e.g., Henoch-Schönlein purpura nephritis or lupus nephritis); (3) presence of other autoimmune diseases, primary immunodeficiencies, or tumors; and (4) any other condition deemed unsuitable for participation in this clinical study by the researchers. The relevant definitions for inclusion and exclusion criteria were based on the 2021 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [36], which define nephrotic syndrome as massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, hyperlipidemia, and edema, with criteria 1 and 2 being essential for diagnosis. Additionally, 56 age-matched healthy children were recruited from the Pediatric Outpatient Department of Third Xiangya Hospital. Inclusion criteria for the healthy children were (1) no symptoms of fever, cough, cold, or other discomforts; (2) no use of immunomodulatory medications; (3) no history or family history of immune-related diseases or tumors; and (4) signed informed consent from the child’s guardian.

Treatment and Efficacy

All patients received standard treatment protocols. Upon diagnosis of INS, a full dose of glucocorticoids (2 mg/kg/day) was administered for 4 to 6 weeks, followed by gradual tapering. For patients diagnosed with RNS, GC was combined with second-line immunosuppressants (MMF or FK506). Clinical efficacy was evaluated based on the 2020 KDIGO guidelines: (1) Complete Remission: Normal biochemical and urinary test results. (2) Partial Remission: Urinary protein < (+++). (3) No Remission: Urinary protein ≥ (+++). Relapse Criteria: Continuous proteinuria for 3 days with 24-hour urinary protein ≥ 50 mg/kg, or morning urinary protein/creatinine ratio (mg/mg) ≥ 2.0, or morning urinary protein turning from negative to (+++)–(++++), or persistent proteinuria ≥ + for 7 days. (4) Steroid-Sensitive Nephrotic Syndrome (SSNS): Proteinuria resolves within ≤ 4 weeks of full-dose prednisone treatment (2 mg/kg/day or 60 mg/m2/day). (5) Steroid-Resistant Nephrotic Syndrome (SRNS): Proteinuria persists after > 4 weeks of full-dose prednisone treatment (2 mg/kg/day or 60 mg/m2/day). (6) Infrequent Relapsing Nephrotic Syndrome: Steroid-sensitive, with < 2 relapses within 6 months of the first remission or < 4 relapses within any 12-month period. (7) Frequently Relapsing Nephrotic Syndrome (FRNS): Steroid-sensitive, with ≥ 2 relapses within 6 months of the first remission or ≥ 4 relapses within any 12-month period. (8) Steroid-Dependent Nephrotic Syndrome (SDNS): Steroid-sensitive, but relapse occurs within 2 weeks after dose reduction or discontinuation of steroid therapy. (9) Refractory nephrotic syndrome (RNS) was defined as including steroid-dependent, frequently relapsing, and steroid-resistant NS [5–7]: characterized by either persistent proteinuria under adequate steroid treatment or frequent relapses accompanied by other clinical manifestations.

Sample Collection

Blood samples were collected using BD PAXgene tubes (Catalog #762165, BD, USA). For both INS patients and healthy controls, 2.5 mL of peripheral blood was collected at baseline and at each follow-up visit. Information such as age, gender, and BMI of the children was recorded.

RNA Extraction

RNA was extracted from peripheral blood of all participants using the PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (Catalog # 74904, QIAGEN, DE). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One UV spectrophotometer.

Construction of Human BCR Libraries

To construct libraries of human B cell receptor (BCR) heavy chains, we employed RepSeq+-M-PanB-Heavy chain Cassette (iRepertoire, catalog no. iR+7chain-HLRI-C) and iR-RepSeq-plus 7-Chain Cassette (iRepertoire, catalog no. iR+7chain-HLRI-C) for INS and healthy control respectively. RT is performed using Qiagen OneStep RT-PCR mix (Qiagen). The unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were attached during the reverse transcription (RT) step. The first cDNA strand was synthesized, and unused primers were removed using SPRIselect beads from Beckman Coulter. A second round of PCR was performed with a V-gene primer mix to bind and extend the sequences. Both first- and second-strand products were purified using SPRIselect beads. Library amplification utilized primers specific to common sites engineered at the 5′ ends of the V- and C-primers used in both synthesis rounds. The detailed information would be found on iRepertoire Inc website. The final library included Illumina adapters for dual-index sequencing, comprising a 10-nucleotide UMI region and an 8-nucleotide C-gene internal barcode primer.

Repertoire Sequencing and Data Recording

The amplified libraries were multiplexed and pooled for sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, with a 500-cycle kit (250 paired-end reads) through a commercial sequencing service lab (Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). This provided comprehensive coverage of the entire variable region, including CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3. Raw data were analyzed using the iRmap program. Reads were demultiplexed based on barcode sequences from the constant region, and low-quality reads were trimmed. Paired-end reads were joined using a modified Needleman-Wunsch algorithm for overlapping alignment. Reads were mapped to V, D, J, and C germline sequences using the Smith-Waterman algorithm, referencing the IMGT database for CDR3 boundary positions. The resulting CDR3 regions were extracted and translated into amino acids for further analysis.

Clone Analysis

The clone expansion of BCRR was defined by top 10, 20, 50, and 100 of which the accumulated the UMI reads were above 10, 20, 50, and 100 clonotypes, respectively. Public clonotypes were defined as the same clones present in five samples. Figure 2E displays a series of CDR3 sequence logos, which represent the frequency and conservation of amino acids at each position within a multiple sequence alignment. The logos are likely generated to illustrate variations or consensus sequences for a specific protein or peptide family. The similarity clone was defined as identical VJ sequences, matching CDR3 lengths, and CDR3 sequences with 90% similarity, and the proportion reflects such similarity clone occupation in the whole repertoire. And persistence clone was also calculated by similarity clone which limited in one individual rather than a patient group.

IGHM/D Mutation Analysis

This analysis aligned the sample BCR CDR3 to the germline antibody CDR3 sequence. If the local sample cannot be matched to the germline sequence, it is considered mutated; otherwise, it is non-mutated.

Statistical Analysis

The Mann-Whitney U-test which was available on the Python package of SciPy was performed to calculate the statistical differences between different disease groups or the control group. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant between the two groups.
