Polystyrene nanoplastics induce hepatic steatosis by disrupting autophagic degradation of NCoR1 and suppressing PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation
Authors: Yuting Fang, Tingting Wei, Zhuo Zhang, Yang Li, Man Yang, Hanqing Chen, Yajie Wang, Rongzhang Hao, Yanbo Li, Zhiwei Sun, Ji Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicro- and nanoplastic (MNP) pollution is a global public health concern, with inevitable ingestion by humans. The liver functions as a primary target organ, and studies have confirmed the presence of microplastics in human hepatic tissues. However, the hepatotoxic effects resulting from such exposure remain poorly characterized. Although polystyrene nanoplastics (PSNPs) have been implicated in disrupting hepatic lipid homeostasis and inducing steatosis, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain elusive. In this study, C57BL/6J mice were gavaged with PSNPs to establish an in vivo exposure model. Histopathological analysis demonstrated that exposure to PSNPs, in conjunction with a high-fat diet (HFD), facilitated the development of a hepatic steatotic phenotype. Multi-omics analysis revealed significant suppression of the PPARα signaling pathway, mechanistically linked to impaired fatty acid β-oxidation. Further experiments demonstrated that PSNPs inhibited the autophagic degradation of NCoR1. Concurrently, PSNP exposure activated the mTOR/S6K2 signaling pathway, promoting NCoR1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, thereby suppressing PPARα-regulated expression of genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation, ultimately leading to hepatic lipid accumulation. Overall, this study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which PSNPs induce hepatic steatosis through NCoR1 dysregulation, providing a theoretical foundation for nanoplastic-related hepatotoxicity research and advancing health risk assessment related to MNP pollution.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04304-6.Keywords: Polystyrene nanoplastics, Hepatic steatosis, Autophagy, NCoR1, Molecular mechanisms

Introduction

The extensive utilization of plastics in nearly all aspects of modern life has resulted in a global plastic production volume of 400 million tons by 2022 [1]. Regrettably, a substantial proportion of plastic waste remains inadequately managed. An authoritative report indicated that in 2020, approximately 52.1 million tons of plastic waste were directly released into the environment [2]. Projections based on established models suggest that the cumulative volume of plastic waste in the natural environment could approach 12 billion tons by 2050 [3]. Through combined physical and chemical processes, plastic waste undergoes gradual degradation, producing microplastics (MPs, < 5 mm) and nanoplastics (NPs, < 1 μm). These MNPs have been frequently detected in diverse environmental matrices, including the atmosphere, aquatic systems, and terrestrial habitats [4, 5]. They may infiltrate the human food chain through marine and terrestrial organisms acting as vectors. In addition to indirect exposure via food webs, humans also directly ingest MNPs released from food packaging [6] and kitchenware [7], as evidenced by their detection across a broad spectrum of food products. Multiple studies have identified MPs in various foods, including seafood [8], cereals [9], honey, milk, beer [10], and salt [11]. Based on annual beverage consumption, adults in Hong Kong are estimated to ingest approximately 6,200 microplastic particles per year [12]. These findings indicate that human exposure to MNPs is unavoidable. Alarmingly, despite continuous exposure, current knowledge regarding their potential health risks remains limited.

The liver, functioning as a central regulatory organ for metabolic processes, has long been recognized as a critical target of MNP accumulation. A whole blood assay performed on 20 healthy individuals detected MP concentrations ranging from 1.84 to 4.65 µg/mL [13], clearly demonstrating the infiltration of plastic particles into systemic circulation. Since MNPs can circulate through the body via the bloodstream [14], an increasing number of studies have reported MP accumulation in multiple organs, including the brain [15], thrombi [16], placenta [17], liver [18] and lungs [19]. A recent investigation quantified MPs in 52 human liver samples, revealing average concentrations of up to 465 µg/g of tissue [15]. Longitudinal monitoring in mice administered MPs orally for 21 days further confirmed the liver as a primary target organ for MPs [20]. Moreover, the identification of six distinct types of MPs in clinical liver cirrhosis specimens [18] underscores the urgent need to elucidate potential links between MNP exposure and liver pathology. Notably, NPs, due to their smaller size relative to MPs, exhibit enhanced permeability and systemic distribution. Studies have demonstrated that prolonged oral exposure to environmentally relevant concentrations of PSNPs can induce excessive steatosis in murine livers [21]. Therefore, NP exposure is likely to be a contributing factor in the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly referred to as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease).

The global prevalence of MASLD is estimated to be as high as 30% [22]. In the absence of specific and effective pharmacological treatments, identifying disease-contributing factors is essential for developing preventive strategies. Early-stage MASLD is characterized by hepatic steatosis, which arises from an imbalance between lipid acquisition (via circulating lipid uptake or de novo lipogenesis) and lipid clearance (via fatty acid β-oxidation or lipoprotein secretion). Impaired hepatic fatty acid β-oxidation is a central pathophysiological feature of MASLD. Decreased β-oxidation efficiency has been observed in HFD-induced MASLD mouse models [23]. Increasing evidence suggests that PSNPs induce hepatic metabolic reprogramming by simultaneously targeting lipid synthesis [24] and fatty acid oxidation [25], although the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Lipophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of lipid droplets (LDs), represents a key pathway in hepatic lipid catabolism. Hepatic steatosis is histologically manifested by enlarged and proliferated LDs, whose clearance is primarily mediated by lipophagy, a process involving selective fusion of LDs with lysosomes [26]. In HFD-induced MASLD, impaired hepatic autophagy aggravates steatosis by impairing lipophagy [27]. Our previous work demonstrated that PSNPs suppress lipophagic degradation of LDs by inhibiting autophagic flux in L-02 hepatocytes [28]. However, emerging evidence suggests that autophagic dysfunction may occur at multiple stages during MASLD progression [29]. The precise molecular mechanisms underlying this impairment remain insufficiently characterized.

Nuclear receptor corepressor 1 (NCoR1), one of the most extensively studied corepressors in disease pathogenesis, interacts with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) to transcriptionally regulate genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation [30]. A previous study have demonstrated that the suppression of PPARα could be reversed by inhibiting NCoR1 [31]. Consequently, disruptions in the interaction between PPARα and NCoR1 inhibit fatty acid oxidation and promote hepatic lipid accumulation. The protein expression of NCoR1 is dynamically regulated via the autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathway to maintain hepatic lipid homeostasis. Studies have shown that NCoR1 binds to the autophagy adaptor protein GABARAP through its LC3-interacting region (LIR), facilitating its selective autophagic transport to lysosomes for degradation. Liver-specific knockout of the autophagy-related gene Atg7 in mice results in NCoR1-dependent suppression of PPARα activity, leading to impaired fatty acid oxidation [26]. These findings indicate that NCoR1 undergoes autophagy-mediated lysosomal degradation, thereby modulating its protein levels and downstream PPARα transcriptional activity to sustain lipid metabolic balance. The effects of PSNP exposure on hepatic lipid metabolism and the underlying mechanisms remain poorly characterized. Therefore, it is critical to elucidate the impact of PSNPs on hepatic steatosis, particularly in individuals at higher risk, such as those with pre-existing metabolic disorders or HFD consumption.

In this study, we hypothesized that PSNPs exacerbate lipid accumulation in HFD-fed mice. First, the accumulation of PSNPs in murine livers was identified. Subsequently, the effects of PSNPs on hepatic lipid metabolism were assessed. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were then employed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed hepatic phenotypes. Assessments of autophagic function were conducted in the livers of HFD-fed mice following PSNP exposure. Finally, the regulatory mechanism of hepatic steatosis under PSNP treatment in HFD-fed mice was investigated. Collectively, this study provides novel insights into the role of PSNPs in liver disease progression and presents compelling evidence of the influence of environmental pollutants on public health.

Results

Characterization and hepatic distribution of PSNPs

The PSNPs used in this study were characterized as uniformly spherical with smooth surfaces and an average diameter of 49.24 ± 5.31 nm (Fig. 1a and b). Dynamic light scattering analysis confirmed favorable colloidal stability, with consistent hydrodynamic size and zeta potential, indicating good dispersion properties (Table 1). To observe the distribution of PSNPs in vivo, the fluorescence imaging in mice after oral exposure was performed. By gavage of 150 mg/kg·bw red fluorescence-labelled PSNPs into mice, we observed continuous changes in fluorescence signal intensity over 24 h (Fig. 1c). The fluorescence signals tended to disappear after 24 h after a single oral gavage. We then collected multiple organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys and intestine) from mice after 24 h of gavage. PSNPs were clearly distributed in the liver (Fig. 1d).

Establishment of an oral exposure mouse model for PSNPs

Building upon previous findings of hepatic PSNP accumulation through small animal in vivo 3D optical imaging analysis, we conducted a 90-day exposure study to assess PSNP-induced hepatotoxicity. Mice were exposed to either a normal diet (ND) or an HFD with or without PSNPs via daily gavage (Fig. 2a). During the exposure period, food intake, body weight, and fasting blood glucose levels were monitored. Food intake remained unaffected by PSNP treatment (Fig. 2b). However, HFD+PSNP co-exposed mice exhibited transient body weight increases during the first six weeks relative to HFD-only controls (Fig. 2c and d), though final body weights did not differ significantly (Fig. 2e). HFD remained the primary contributor to overall weight gain. In addition, HFD-fed mice displayed more pronounced fluctuations in fasting glucose (Fig. 2f). Notably, HFD+PSNP co-exposure significantly elevated fasting blood glucose after 90 days compared to ND controls (Fig. 2g). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis indicated PSNP accumulation in vesicular structures within hepatocytes after 90 days (Fig. 2h). These results establish effective hepatic accumulation of PSNPs and support their capacity to disrupt metabolic regulation.

PSNPs promoted hepatic lipid accumulation

Following the confirmation of hepatic PSNP accumulation and associated metabolic disruption, we systematically evaluated hepatic lipid alterations. Gross liver weight did not differ significantly between experimental groups (Fig. 3a). Although HFD-fed mice exhibited reduced liver-to-body weight ratios relative to ND-fed controls, PSNP co-exposure did not significantly alter this metric (Fig. 3a). Liver function enzymes , including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), showed no significant differences among groups (Fig. 3b). Histopathological examination revealed pronounced hepatocyte lipid accumulation in HFD+PSNP mice compared to HFD-only controls, whereas ND-fed mice exhibited no pathological changes (Fig. 3c). The NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) indicated that none of the experimental groups met the diagnostic criteria for non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and no inflammation was observed among groups (Fig. 3d). Consistently, neither serum inflammatory factors nor tissue fibrosis staining showed significant alterations (Figure S1). Supporting these findings, Oil Red O staining demonstrated that PSNP co-exposure resulted in larger and more numerous lipid droplets compared with HFD alone (Fig. 3e and f). Biochemical assays demonstrated that triglyceride (TG) levels were highest in the HFD+PSNP group, whereas total cholesterol (TC) and free fatty acid (FFA) levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3g). Notably, PSNP exposure selectively increased free cholesterol (FC) levels in HFD-fed mice compared to ND-fed controls. Among all treatment conditions, the HFD+PSNP group exhibited the most pronounced hepatic abnormalities. These findings indicate that PSNPs exacerbate HFD-induced hepatic lipid accumulation, particularly by elevating TG content and promoting histological alterations.

Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that PSNPs suppressed fatty acid oxidation

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying hepatic lipid accumulation in HFD+PSNP-treated mice, transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling was performed on liver tissues. Transcriptomic analysis identified 236 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 95 upregulated and 141 downregulated genes in the HFD+PSNP group compared to the HFD group (Figure S2a and S2b). The classification and distribution of DEGs across samples are presented in Figure S2c and S2d. Among these, lipid metabolism-related genes were filtered for further analysis to identify potential mechanisms of PSNP-induced hepatic steatosis (Fig. 4a). Notably, pathway enrichment analysis highlighted significant suppression of the PPAR signaling pathway, which governs fatty acid β-oxidation, along with associated steroid biosynthesis and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 4b). Several genes in the PPARα pathway—including Acsl3, Hmgcs1, Fabp4, Fabp5, and Cyp4a31—were markedly downregulated, indicating a compromised capacity for fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 4c).

To validate the transcriptomic findings, untargeted metabolomic profiling using LC-MS/MS and GC-MS was conducted. Principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles between the HFD and HFD+PSNP groups (Figure S3a and S3b). In total, 102 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs, 51 upregulated and 51 downregulated) were identified in the HFD+PSNP group (Figure S3c–e). To further investigate the metabolic disruption, lipid-related metabolites were specifically analyzed (Fig. 4d). As anticipated, the accumulation of long-chain fatty acids—such as tuberculostearic acid, sciadonic acid, and 13-methyltetradec-9-enoic acid —was observed, along with elevated levels of fatty acylcarnitines, including 3-lcosa-8,11,14-trienoylcarnitine (Fig. 4e). These findings corroborate the transcriptomic data and collectively suggest impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation. Taken together, these results indicate that PSNP exposure exacerbates hepatic lipid accumulation in HFD-fed mice by suppressing fatty acid oxidation via the PPARα signaling pathway, consistent with the lipid deposition patterns observed in both histological and biochemical analyses.

NCoR1 upregulation contributed to PSNPs-induced suppression of fatty acid oxidation

To confirm the transcriptomic and metabolomic findings, RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses were performed on liver tissues from the 90-day exposure models. Given the identified downregulation of PPARα pathway genes (e.g., Acsl3 and Hmgcs1), and the known role of PPARα as a key regulator of fatty acid β-oxidation gene transcription, a subset of genes in the PPARα signaling axis was selected for functional validation (Fig. 5a). mRNA levels of Acsl3, Cpt1a, Acox1, and Hmgcs1, as well as protein levels of ACSL3, CPT1A, and HMGCS1, were significantly decreased in the HFD+PSNP group compared to HFD controls (Fig. 5b and c). Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis of genes involved in FFA synthesis, TG synthesis, and FFA uptake revealed no significant alterations, suggesting that PSNPs selectively impair fatty acid oxidation rather than lipid synthesis or uptake pathways (Figure S4). These results provide strong evidence that PSNP-induced hepatic lipid accumulation is primarily mediated through transcriptional suppression of PPARα-dependent fatty acid β-oxidation.

NCoR1 is a critical transcriptional coregulatory protein that interacts with nuclear receptors to modulate the expression of genes involved in both lipid synthesis and catabolism, thereby preserving lipid homeostasis [26, 32]. Prior studies have demonstrated that NCoR1 suppresses PPARα transcriptional activity, leading to reduced expression of fatty acid oxidation-related genes and subsequent lipid accumulation [30]. Notably, liver-specific deletion of NCoR1 has been shown to alleviate hepatic steatosis in alcoholic liver disease by promoting fatty acid oxidation [33]. Given the observed transcriptional suppression of fatty acid β-oxidation, we hypothesized that PSNPs may stabilize NCoR1, thereby inhibiting PPARα activity and exacerbating lipid deposition. To test this hypothesis, Western blot analysis was conducted using whole-cell lysates. Results confirmed a marked increase in NCoR1 protein expression in HFD+PSNP-treated mice compared to HFD controls (Fig. 5d). Immunohistochemical staining further revealed increased NCoR1 accumulation in hepatocytes of PSNP-exposed mice (Fig. 5e), supporting the hypothesis that PSNPs suppress fatty acid oxidation by promoting NCoR1 stabilization and PPARα inhibition.

PSNPs stabilized NCoR1 by inhibiting autophagy-mediated degradation NCoR1

While inflammatory signals and hormones transcriptionally regulate NCoR1 expression [34, 35], autophagy has emerged as the predominant mechanism modulating NCoR1 protein homeostasis through selective degradation. Previous studies have shown that NCoR1 can directly interact with GABARAP family proteins on autophagosome membranes to facilitate its recognition, encapsulation into autophagosomes, and subsequent lysosomal degradation [26]. This process reduces PPARα inhibition and promotes the expression of fatty acid β-oxidation genes [36]. Considering this, the effects of PSNPs on autophagy were first explored in mice fed either an HFD or ND. Fluorescent imaging showed that there was significantly reduced green fluorescence, representing autophagosomes, in HFD+PSNP liver tissues (Fig. 6a and b). The LC3B mRNA and protein levels were then determined. While the Lc3b mRNA levels remained unchanged, LC3B protein levels were substantially decreased in HFD+PSNP livers (Fig. 6c and d), confirming the post-translational suppression of autophagosome formation in HFD-fed mice.

Our previous studies using fluorescently labeled PSNPs to treat L-02 cells and confocal microscopy observation have confirmed that PSNPs can be internalized into hepatocytes [28]. In the present study, immunofluorescence co-staining demonstrated colocalization of NCoR1 with both autophagosomes and lysosomes (Fig. 6e), validating its degradation via the autophagy-lysosomal pathway. Collectively, our findings show that PSNPs inhibit the autophagic degradation of NCoR1, leading to its aberrant accumulation. The preliminary work of this study verified that the PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation process was inhibited (Fig. 5). Notably, PSNP-induced inhibition of autophagosome formation leads to the abnormal accumulation of NCoR1 in the liver of HFD-fed mice, which in turn strongly inhibits PPARα-mediated fatty acid β-oxidation, ultimately inducing hepatic lipid deposition.

PSNPs enhanced NCoR1 nuclear translocation via the mTOR/S6K2 pathway

The subcellular localization and nuclear receptor binding specificity of NCoR1 are modulated by post-translational modifications [37]. Phosphorylation is particularly known to enhance its affinity for PPARα, thereby promoting transcriptional repression of fatty acid oxidation genes [30]. In this study, due to the unavailability of specific phospho-NCoR1 antibodies, immunoprecipitation of NCoR1 followed by probing with a pan-phosphorylation antibody was conducted. This approach revealed significantly increased phosphorylation of NCoR1 in HFD+PSNP liver samples (Fig. 7a). mTOR, a key kinase implicated in NCoR1 nuclear localization [31], displayed elevated phosphorylation in the HFD+PSNP group (Fig. 7b), indicating pathway activation. Activated mTOR subsequently phosphorylates S6K2, which has been shown to exert a more potent effect on ribosomal protein S6 phosphorylation than S6K1 [38]. In the absence of S6K2-specific phospho-antibodies, increased S6 phosphorylation was used as a surrogate marker for S6K2 activity (Fig. 7b). These molecular changes coincided with enhanced nuclear accumulation of NCoR1, as confirmed by immunostaining (Fig. 7c). Together, these findings demonstrate that PSNPs promote hepatic lipid accumulation through activation of the mTOR/S6K2 signaling axis. This activation facilitates NCoR1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, enhancing its association with PPARα. The resulting NCoR1–PPARα complex represses the transcription of fatty acid β-oxidation genes, thereby impairing lipid catabolism and disrupting hepatic metabolic homeostasis.

To further elucidate the causal role of mTOR signaling in regulating NCoR1 nuclear translocation and lipid accumulation, we utilized the specific mTOR inhibitor rapamycin. In lipid-loaded hepatocytes, rapamycin treatment abolished PSNP-induced nuclear accumulation of NCoR1 and alleviated intracellular lipid deposition (Figure S5a and S5b). Western blot analysis further revealed that rapamycin restored the expression of key fatty acid oxidation proteins downregulated by PSNPs (Figure S5c). These findings provide direct pharmacological evidence that PSNPs disrupt hepatic lipid homeostasis mainly through hyperactivation of mTOR signaling, which subsequently promotes NCoR1-mediated transcriptional repression of fatty acid β-oxidation.

Discussion

Plastics are recognized as PBT (persistent, bioaccumulative, and toxic) pollutants that permeate ecosystems globally [39]. Increasing evidence suggests that MNPs, which are derived from the degradation of larger plastic materials, can enter the human body and circulate via the bloodstream [40], posing significant health risks. Due to its dual blood supply and role in xenobiotic metabolism [41], the liver is a primary target organ for environmental toxicants. Our results confirmed that PSNPs, owing to their small diameter of 50 nm (Fig. 1a and b), are capable of penetrating the intestinal barrier, entering systemic circulation, and accumulating in hepatic tissue (Fig. 1c–d). Consistent with this, other studies have shown that MNPs disrupt hepatic lipid metabolism-related genes and lipidomic profiles [42, 43], and promote the accumulation of lipid droplets in hepatocyte cytoplasm [44]. Concurrently, growing evidence has implicated environmental pollutants—including perfluorooctanoic acid, perfluorooctane sulfonate [45], and silica nanoparticles [46]—in exacerbating hepatic lipid deposition and promoting MASLD progression. These findings highlight the urgent need to evaluate the hepatotoxic potential of PSNPs, particularly in the context of metabolic disorders such as MASLD.

Our findings suggest a potential synergistic effect between PSNP exposure and an HFD in disrupting hepatic lipid homeostasis (Fig. 3c-g), potentially accelerating the pathogenesis of MASLD. This is consistent with epidemiological studies showing a positive correlation between global plastic production and rising obesity prevalence [47, 48]. Obesity is the leading risk factor for MASLD [49], with the highest prevalence observed among obese individuals [50] and a relative risk approximately 3.5 times higher than in lean individuals [51]. HFD-induced obesity models are widely employed in MASLD research due to their capacity to induce systemic energy imbalance when consumed over the long term. Excess energy is initially stored in adipose tissue as TGs. However, once storage capacity is exceeded, lipids are redistributed to peripheral organs such as the liver, promoting metabolic dysfunction [52]. As illustrated in Fig. 3e and f, HFD increased hepatic lipid deposition. Notably, we observed a synergistic amplification of hepatic lipid accumulation when HFD was combined with oral PSNP exposure, exceeding the effects of either factor alone.

Our findings demonstrate that combined HFD and PSNP exposure initially manifests as subclinical metabolic disturbance before progressing to structural damage, inducing lipid deposition without inflammatory response (Fig. 3b and f and S1). This phenomenon aligns with the “multiple-hit” hypothesis of MASLD, which posits that hepatic steatosis (the “first hit”) sensitizes the liver to subsequent injurious factors, with inflammation and oxidative stress (the “second hit”) then driving the progression to NASH [53]. Our findings indicate that the combination of PSNPs and HFD acts as a potent “first hit,” leading to intrahepatic lipid accumulation. This metabolic predisposition without concurrent inflammation is consistent with reports on other environmental stressors, such as PM2.5 exposure, which has been shown to induce hepatic lipid deposition without significant inflammatory infiltration in central veins or parenchyma [54]. At this early stage, the metabolic balance of hepatocytes is disrupted, but their structural integrity remains largely intact, which reasonably explains the unchanged ALT/AST levels. To our knowledge, this is the first study reporting that orally administered PSNPs exacerbate HFD-induced hepatic accumulation without phenotypically affecting ND-fed mice. These results support our hypothesis that PSNPs and HFD synergistically impair hepatic lipid homeostasis, accelerating MASLD pathogenesis. On the one hand, HFD increases hepatic metabolic burden by altering lipid-processing enzyme activity; on the other hand, PSNPs disrupt lipid metabolic pathways [55], particularly those involved in fatty acid oxidation and transport. This dual perturbation amplifies HFD-driven lipid deposition.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) are nuclear receptors that play a central role in regulating lipid metabolism. Among them, PPARα governs mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation—the primary hepatic mechanism for fatty acid catabolism [56, 57]. Although species differences in PPARα biology exist, its fundamental role in fatty acid oxidation is evolutionarily conserved in humans [58], with clinical evidence showing that human MASLD livers exhibit reduced PPARα levels [59]. The therapeutic efficacy of PPARα agonists like fenofibrate in treating human hyperlipidemia further underscores the pathway’s clinical importance [60, 61]. Prior research in fish models has shown that dietary PSNPs inhibit AMPK/PPARα signaling and promote hepatic lipid accumulation [25]. To investigate this mechanism in mammals, we performed integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in HFD+PSNP-exposed mice. Our results demonstrated that PSNPs significantly impaired PPARα-mediated fatty acid β-oxidation, as evidenced by downregulation of PPARα target genes and accumulation of long-chain fatty acids and acylcarnitines (Fig. 4c and e). In contrast to studies reporting PSNP-induced lipogenesis [24, 25, 62] or reduced lipid transport [25, 63, 64], we observed no significant changes in lipid synthesis or uptake genes under HFD conditions (Figure S4). This indicates that PSNPs preferentially disrupt lipid homeostasis by targeting PPARα-mediated β-oxidation during lipid overload. Our findings demonstrate that PSNP exposure exacerbates HFD-induced hepatic steatosis primarily by suppressing PPARα-driven fatty acid oxidation. Given the conserved nature of PPARα signaling and the observed suppression (rather than activation) of this pathway, our results identify a novel mechanism through which environmental nanoparticles could contribute to metabolic dysfunction. These findings are particularly relevant to human health, as impaired PPARα activity is associated with human MASLD, and our exposure regimen was based on human-equivalent doses [65]. Therefore, enhancing mitochondrial β-oxidation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy against nanoplastic-induced hepatic steatosis and MASLD progression.

NCoR1 suppresses PPARα-mediated transcriptional activation of fatty acid oxidation genes by forming stable repressive complexes [37]. Genetic ablation of NCoR1 in intestinal epithelial cells has been shown to activate PPARα signaling and upregulate lipid metabolism-related genes [66]. Conversely, under obese or insulin-resistant conditions, aberrant accumulation of NCoR1 intensifies PPARα pathway inhibition and impairs lipid oxidation [36]. In the present study, PSNP exposure significantly elevated hepatic NCoR1 protein levels in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 5d and e). Given the essential role of the autophagy–lysosomal pathway in maintaining NCoR1 protein homeostasis, we further examined its activity. PSNP exposure markedly inhibited autophagosome formation in the livers of HFD-fed mice (Fig. 6a–d), suggesting that impaired autophagic degradation of NCoR1 contributes to hepatic lipid accumulation. This mechanism is supported by multiple lines of evidence. In aged fatty liver, defective chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) impairs NCoR1 degradation and aggravates lipid metabolic dysregulation [67]. Similarly, liver-specific deficiency of class III PI3K/Vps15 leads to excessive NCoR1 accumulation and repression of PPARα target genes, resulting in reduced fatty acid β-oxidation [36]. Glyphosate exposure has also been shown to inhibit autophagic degradation of NCoR1, suppress PPARα signaling, and induce hepatic steatosis in roosters [68]. Although TP53INP2 promotes NCoR1 autophagic degradation to alleviate PPARγ co-repression in brown adipocytes [69], this likewise underscores the critical role of autophagy in regulating NCoR1 turnover. Mechanistically, NCoR1 is selectively recognized by GABARAP-dependent autophagy and delivered to lysosomes for degradation. Loss of Atg7 in liver tissue inhibits this process, resulting in aberrant NCoR1 accumulation [26]. In our study, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed NCoR1 colocalization with both autophagosomes and lysosomes (Fig. 6e), further demonstrating that PSNPs interfere with this pathway by inhibiting autophagosome formation, ultimately stabilizing NCoR1 protein in hepatocytes.

Post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation, and methylation, regulate the subcellular localization and functional activity of NCoR1 [37]. Specifically, phosphorylation enhances NCoR1 nuclear translocation and its interaction with PPARα [70]. Our current findings reveal that PSNP exposure significantly increases NCoR1 phosphorylation in HFD-fed mouse livers (Fig. 7a). To date, several kinases—including mTOR/S6K2 [38], PAK4 [30], and PKB/Akt [70]—have been shown to induce NCoR1 phosphorylation and promote its nuclear localization. Prior work has demonstrated that nanoparticles can activate mTOR via endocytosis [71], which is consistent with our observed increase in mTOR phosphorylation following PSNP treatment (Fig. 7b). Activated mTOR phosphorylates S6K2, which facilitates the physical interaction between NCoR1 and PPARα, thereby repressing the transcription of target genes [38]. Our study confirmed elevated phosphorylation levels of both mTOR and S6K2 in HFD+PSNP livers (Fig. 7b). Although NCoR1 acts primarily as a transcriptional corepressor, its metabolic effects are complex. NCoR1 deletion improves metabolic syndrome by simultaneously repressing lipogenesis and enhancing oxidative metabolism [66]. This dual function arises because NCoR1 can associate with various nuclear receptors, suppressing multiple metabolic pathways. In addition to inhibiting PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation, NCoR1 represses liver X receptor (LXR)-driven cholesterol efflux [72–74] and PPARγ-mediated lipogenesis [75]. In summary, PSNPs exacerbate hepatic steatosis by impairing autophagic degradation of NCoR1 and activating the mTOR/S6K2 signaling cascade, thereby promoting NCoR1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. This was further corroborated with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin. Nuclear-cytoplasmic Western blot showed mTOR inhibition abolished PSNP-induced NCoR1 nuclear accumulation and restored key fatty acid oxidation protein expression (Figure S5). Nuclear-localized NCoR1 interacts with PPARα and transcriptionally suppresses fatty acid oxidation genes. Therefore, targeting NCoR1 expression or disrupting its interaction with PPARα may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating MASLD progression. While this study provides mechanistic insights into PSNP-induced hepatic steatosis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while we identify mTOR as a key regulator, the contributions of parallel signaling pathways and the precise phosphorylation sites on NCoR1 require further mapping. Second, the specific autophagic receptor responsible for NCoR1 recognition remains unidentified. Addressing these points in future work will be crucial for translating these mechanistic insights into a comprehensive understanding of nanoplastic-induced metabolic risk.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the adverse effects of orally administered, environmentally relevant concentrations of PSNPs on hepatic lipid metabolism and provides a comprehensive analysis of the underlying molecular mechanisms. Experimental findings demonstrate that PSNP exposure significantly exacerbates hepatic lipid accumulation in HFD-fed mice. As illustrated in Fig. 8, mechanistic investigations reveal that PSNPs aggravate hepatic steatosis by inhibiting autophagy-mediated degradation of NCoR1, resulting in its accumulation. Concurrently, PSNPs activate the mTOR/S6K2 signaling pathway, enhancing NCoR1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, which facilitates its interaction with PPARα and suppresses transcription of fatty acid oxidation genes, ultimately impairing lipid catabolism. This study not only advances our understanding of the comparative toxicity of nanoscale MNP contaminants but also provides critical evidence for health risk assessments.

Experimental methods

Preparation and characterization of PSNPs

PSNPs (50 nm) were obtained from DK Nanotechnology Co. (Beijing, China) as aqueous suspensions with an initial concentration of 50 mg/mL in sterile deionized water. Two distinct PSNP formulations were employed: red-fluorescent particles (excitation/emission: 620/680 nm) for in vivo distribution studies, and pristine particles for toxicity evaluation. Structural characterization was performed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100, Japan), and the size distribution of 500 particles was analyzed using ImageJ software. Colloidal characteristics were assessed via dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS 90, UK), measuring hydrodynamic diameter, surface charge (zeta potential), and polydispersity index (PDI) in both aqueous and DMEM media (Corning, USA) media. Prior to use, all nanoparticle suspensions were ultrasonicated for 30 min at 160 W and 20 kHz (Diagenode UDC200, Belgium) to ensure uniform dispersion.

Cell culture and treatment

Human normal liver L-02 cells (Nanjing Keygen Biotech, China) were cultured under adherent conditions until reaching 60–70% confluency. Cells were pretreated with 100 µM oleic acid (OA) in serum-free DMEM for 24 h. After aspirating the OA-containing medium, cells were washed twice with PBS and exposed to 25 µg/mL PSNPs (prepared by diluting 10 mg/mL stock solution in serum-free DMEM to a final volume of 10 mL) for an additional 24 h. The PSNPs concentration was selected based on our previous study demonstrating significant cellular viability impacts at this dose without causing overt cytotoxicity [28]. Control groups were treated with serum-free DMEM under identical conditions.

In vivo distribution of PSNPs

Male C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old) were utilized to determine organ-specific PSNP accumulation following a single oral gavage of red-fluorescent PSNPs. Control mice received no PSNP treatment. Each mouse received a single gavage dose of 150 mg/kg·bw PSNPs. Fluorescence intensity was monitored at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-gavage. At 0 h and 24 h post-exposure, mice were sacrificed, and tissues were harvested and rinsed with saline. Organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine) were arranged on black background paper for imaging. Fluorescence signals from the whole body and individual organs were measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 640/680 nm using a small-animal in vivo 3D optical imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) to assess PSNP distribution and liver accumulation.

Animals and experimental design

Male C57BL/6J mice (5–6 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and housed under standardized environmental conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 23 ± 1 °C, 60–70% relative humidity) with ad libitum access to water. Mice were randomly allocated into four experimental groups (n = 10 per group): ND: normal diet + daily oral gavage of equal volumes of ultrapure water (ddH2O); PSNPs: normal diet + 150 mg/kg·bw PSNPs; HFD: high-fat diet (D12492, Research Diets Inc., USA; 459 kcal/100 g, 60% fat, 20% protein, 20% carbohydrate) + ddH2O; HFD+PSNPs: high-fat diet + 150 mg/kg·bw PSNPs.

The murine dose was determined based on the upper range of estimated human microplastic ingestion (0.1–5 g/week/individual) [65]. As described in [76], a standardized interspecies dose conversion factor of 12.3, accounting for allometric and metabolic differences between humans and mice, was applied in the calculation. The resulting dose of 150 mg/kg·bw (for a 20 g mouse) corresponds to a human equivalent dose of approximately 5.0 g/week for a 60 kg adult, which aligns with current projections for potential high-level human exposure. This dosage has been consistently used in prior high-quality mechanistic studies investigating the metabolic and toxicological effects of microplastics in mouse models [77].

Following a 7-day acclimatization period, mice were orally administered PSNPs suspended in ddH2O daily for 90 consecutive days. Weekly assessments included biometric measurements (body weight and food intake), while biweekly metabolic evaluations were performed after 8 h fasting, followed by caudal vein blood sampling for glucose analysis using a clinical-grade analyzer. All physiological parameters were recorded throughout the experimental period to track longitudinal trends. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Chinese National Standards for Laboratory Animal Welfare (GB/T 35892 − 2018) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (Protocol AEEI-2024-013).

Tissue sample collection and blood parameters

Following the 90-day experimental period, mice were fasted overnight and anesthetized using weight-adjusted dosages. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture from fully anesthetized animals prior to liver excision. The collected blood was allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 60 min and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting serum was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis. Hepatic tissues were carefully excised, and organ weights were recorded immediately. For histological examination, a tissue portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while adjacent sections were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and embedded in O.C.T. compound for optimal cryosectioning. Remaining hepatic parenchyma was rapidly vitrified in cryovials cooled with liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C to preserve biomolecular integrity. This stratified preservation approach enabled comprehensive downstream utilization while maintaining both tissue architecture and biochemical stability.

TEM observation of mouse liver tissue samples

Following euthanasia, two mice per group were randomly selected for ultrastructural analysis of hepatic tissue. Subcapsular segments from the dominant hepatic lobe were excised and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 4 h at 4 °C, followed by three phosphate buffer (PB) rinses and cryopreservation. Secondary fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h was followed by three 15 min PBS washes. Dehydration was conducted using a graded ethanol series (30% to 95%, 15 min each), finalized with two 20 min treatments in absolute acetone. Samples were then infiltrated with epoxy resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were prepared using diamond knives and mounted on copper grids. Dual contrast staining involved 4% uranyl acetate (30 min) followed by lead citrate (5 min). After air drying, grids were imaged via TEM under vacuum, with digital micrographs captured at calibrated magnifications to ensure accurate ultrastructural quantification. This protocol preserved subcellular structures while minimizing preparation-induced artifacts.

Histopathological analysis

Fixed hepatic specimens were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio P1110, China) at ambient temperature for 24 h and processed for paraffin embedding. Microtome sections (5 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess overall tissue morphology. A comprehensive NAFLD Activity Score (NAS) was applied to liver sections, systematically evaluating the degrees of steatosis (score 0–3), lobular inflammation (score 0–3), and hepatocyte ballooning (score 0–2) [78, 79]. For lipid-specific staining, cryosections (8 μm thick) from six animals per group were stained using the Oil Red O protocol to visualize intracellular neutral lipid content. Whole-slide imaging was performed using a digital pathology scanner (KF-PRO-005, Ningbo Jiangfeng, China), enabling high-resolution analysis of hepatic architecture and lipid droplet distribution.

Detection of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST)

ALT and AST levels were measured using enzymatic assay kits (Njjcbio C009-2-1/C010-2-1, China) following liver tissue cryopreservation at − 80 °C. Six liver samples per group were homogenized (10 mg per sample) in chilled extraction buffer and centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was subjected to chromogenic substrate reaction under standardized incubation conditions. Absorbance was measured at 505 nm for ALT and 510 nm for AST using a microplate spectrophotometer (Bio-Tek Synergy HTX, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using BCA assay (CWBIO SW0014S, China), and enzymatic activities were calculated as µmol of substrate converted per milligram of total protein.

Measurement of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), free fatty acid (FFA), and free cholesterol (FC) levels

Hepatic lipid content was assessed via enzymatic quantification using commercial assay kits (Njjcbio A110-1-1/A111-1-1; Solarbio BC0595/BC1895, China) following long-term cryopreservation at − 80 °C. Six randomly selected liver specimens per group were homogenized (10 mg tissue/sample) in chilled lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min to isolate lipid fractions. Supernatants were reacted with detection reagents according to manufacturer instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Bio-Tek Synergy HTX, USA) at 500 nm for TG, TC, and FC, and at 550 nm for FFA. Parallel protein normalization was performed using BCA assay (CWBIO SW0014S, China). Final concentrations were calculated based on absorbance values and expressed as µmol/mg protein for TG and TC, while FFA and FC levels were normalized to tissue mass (µmol/g).

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded hepatic sections (5 μm) underwent antigen visualization through standardized immunohistochemical protocols. Following dewaxing and rehydration through graded ethanol series, epitope unmasking was achieved by thermal retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 20 min water bath). Tissue permeabilization employed 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich T8787, Germany) pretreatment before 1 h blocking with 10% normal goat serum (Solarbio SL038, China). Primary antibody incubation against NCoR1 (Thermo PA1-844 A, USA; 1:200 dilution) proceeded at 37 ℃ for 60 min, followed by 30 min exposure to HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit polymer (Boster SA1020, USA). Chromogenic development utilized diaminobenzidine substrate (Boster AR1022, USA) with hematoxylin counterstaining (Boster AR0005, USA) for nuclear contrast. Positive immunoreactivity was characterized by distinct cytoplasmic brown deposition, while negative controls displayed minimal background staining. Digital histomorphometric analysis quantified staining intensity through standardized threshold parameters, ensuring objective interpretation of NCoR1 protein distribution patterns within hepatic parenchyma.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue sections

Paraffin-embedded hepatic Sect. (5 μm) were processed for immunofluorescence labeling. After dewaxing and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using a water bath in citrate buffer (pH 6.0, 20 min). Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich T8787, Germany) and blocked with 10% goat serum (Solarbio SL038, China) for 1 h. Primary antibodies against LC3B (Abclonal A19665, China; 1:200) and NCoR1 (Thermo PA1-844 A, USA; 1:200) were applied overnight at 4 °C in humidified chambers. Following PBS washes, sections were incubated with FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (CST 4412 S, USA; 1:500) for 2 h at room temperature under light-protected conditions. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI)-containing mounting medium (Beyotime P0131, China).

L-02 cells

Cells fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio P1110, China) for 15 min at room temperature were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked in 10% goat serum. Anti-NCoR1 primary antibody (Thermo PA1-844 A, USA; 1:200) was applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (CST 4412 S, USA; 1:500) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. After a 30 min blocking step, sequential immunolabeling was conducted using anti-LC3B (Abclonal A19665, China; 1:200) and anti-LAMP1 (Proteintech 67300-1-Ig, USA; 1:200) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with Fluor® 647 goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Abcam ab150115, USA) for 1 h. PBS washes (3 × 5 min) were performed between each step. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium.

To validate the role of the mTOR pathway in mediating PSNP-induced nuclear translocation of NCoR1, cells were treated with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin (MCE HY-1029, USA) at a concentration of 100 nM, in combination with PSNPs (25 µg/mL) for 24 h. Subsequent experimental procedures were consistent with those described in the previous section. Immunofluorescence staining was performed using an anti-NCoR1 primary antibody (CST 34271T, USA; 1:800), incubated overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by incubation with anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor® 594 (Abcam ab150088, USA) at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. LDs were stained with BODIPY 493/503 (Thermo Fisher, D3922, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with a DAPI-containing mounting medium.

Imaging acquisition

Fluorescent images were acquired using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Nikon Ti2, Japan) equipped with 60× oil immersion objectives. Spectral channel separation and z-stack imaging were conducted using NIS-Elements AR software (v5.21) for high-resolution, three-dimensional analysis.

Transcriptomic analysis

To investigate the hepatotoxic mechanisms associated with nanoplastic exposure, hepatic transcriptomic profiling was conducted on four liver specimens following 90-day PSNP administration. Sequencing was performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw data underwent quality control using fastp software (v1.0) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. High-quality reads were aligned to the murine reference genome using the HISAT2 splice-aware alignment algorithm. Gene expression levels were quantified using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads normalization and HTSeq-count enumeration. Differential expression analysis was carried out with the DESeq2 package, applying statistical thresholds of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2 Fold change| > 1 to identify significantly altered gene expression.

Metabolomics analysis

Metabolomic profiling of hepatic tissue from HFD and HFD+PSNP groups (n = 6 per group) was conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai). Metabolite detection employed integrated chromatography-mass spectrometry platforms, including ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF-MS/MS) and gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), following standardized instrument settings. Raw spectral data were processed using MS-DIAL software, incorporating retention time alignment, mass accuracy filtering (± 5 ppm), MS/MS spectral matching, and isotopic pattern recognition. Compound annotation was achieved using established metabolic databases (HMDB, LipidMaps v2.3, METLIN) supplemented with species-specific resources (LuMet-Animal 3.0, LuMet-GC 5.0). Principal component analysis was used to evaluate metabolic variation and system stability, followed by orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis for intergroup separation. Differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were defined based on an adjusted p-value < 0.05, variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, and |log2 Fold change| > 1.

Western blot analysis

Liver tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo 78510, USA) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Solarbio P0100, China) and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio P1260, China). Homogenization was conducted using a high-frequency sample grinder (Jingxin, 60 Hz/60 s, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA)-based colorimetric assay (CWBIO SW0014S, China) with bovine serum albumin standards. Protein lysates were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min in Laemmli buffer, resolved on 10–15% SDS–polyacrylamide gradient gels, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore RR05505, Ireland) via wet blotting. Membranes were blocked with commercial blocking buffer (NCM P30500, China), incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, and subsequently treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Signal development was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence and visualized on a digital imaging platform (Bio-Rad ChemiDoc™, USA). Band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software (v1.53), using GAPDH (Gene-protein Link P01L081, China) as the internal loading control. Detailed antibody specifications, including catalog number and company, are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

L-02 cells were pretreated with 100 µM OA for 24 h to establish a lipid-loaded state. Subsequently, the cells were co-treated with rapamycin (100 nM) and PSNPs for an additional 24 h. Following treatment, cells were lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer (Abcam ab156034, USA) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (Solarbio P0100, China) and a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio P1260, China). The subsequent procedures for protein quantification, electrophoresis, transfer, and immunoblotting were performed as described in the tissue protein Western blot section above. Detailed antibody specifications, including catalog number and company, are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from liver samples using guanidinium thiocyanate–phenol reagent (Applygene R1030, China) following standard phenol-chloroform protocols. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using PrimeScript™ reverse transcriptase (Takara RR037A, Japan) and oligo-dT primers under defined thermal cycling conditions. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using SYBR Green-based detection (ABI A25742, USA) on a Bio-Rad MyiQ real-time PCR system. Reactions were performed in triplicate for each biological sample (n = 3 technical replicates). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with Gapdh serving as the internal reference. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Immunoprecipitation

Immunoprecipitation was conducted using the BeaverBeads™ Protein A (or A/G) Immunoprecipitation Kit (Beaver Biosciences Inc. 22202, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 30 mg of liver tissue was used per reaction. Initially, the antibody–Protein A bead complex was prepared by incubating the NCoR1 antibody with Protein A beads to enable antibody binding. Residual unbound antibodies were removed using a mild elution buffer. The beads were then equilibrated in antigen-binding buffer. Subsequently, liver tissue extracts were incubated with the antibody-bound beads to allow antigen–antibody interaction. Unbound proteins were removed through multiple buffer washes. The target antigen was eluted from the beads using SDS sample buffer, yielding purified protein for downstream analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
