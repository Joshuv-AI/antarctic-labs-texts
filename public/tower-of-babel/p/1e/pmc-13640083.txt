A novel preclinical strategy for diabetic wound: CITED2-loaded hydrogel spray targeted macrophage reprogramming
Authors: Xichao Jian, Jingjie Shao, Shihan Tang, Xing Hu, Qi He, Miaomiao Wei, Yaping Deng, Yan Zhou, Shune Xiao, Fang Qi, Chengliang Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic wounds resent a considerable clinical challenge due to impaired healing, which results from persistent inflammation and dysregulated macrophage polarization. This study identifies CITED2 as a critical regulator of diabetic wound repair, primarily through its role in modulating macrophage plasticity. We demonstrate that CITED2 exhibits phase-specific expression during normal wound healing, and its downregulation in diabetic wounds disrupts the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages. Local administration of recombinant CITED2 protein accelerates wound closure by promoting the shift of macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis. Transcriptomic analysis reveals that CITED2 potentially mediates the activation of PPAR-γ signaling and the upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes. Further immunofluorescence analysis of wound tissues shows an upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes (IL10, Gpnmb, and Nr1d1) and a downregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α). Additionally, in vitro experiments indicate that conditioned medium from CITED2-treated macrophages significantly enhances the proliferation and migration capabilities of endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Notably, short-term CITED2 treatment (3 days) achieves comparable efficacy to a 14-day rhFGF therapy. To facilitate clinical translation, we developed a fibrin-thrombin-PEG hydrogel spray (Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2) that exhibits favorable anti-inflammatory and hemostatic properties, along with demonstrated biosafety. In diabetic mouse wounds, the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray shows superior wound healing effects compared to CITED2 alone or Fib-Thr-PEG controls. These findings establish CITED2 as a novel therapeutic target and the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel as a potential strategy for diabetic wound management.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04792-6.Keywords: Hydrogel, CITED2, Macrophage, Diabetic Wound Healing

Introduction

Diabetes currently affects over one-tenth of the global adult population, emerging as a critical public health concern associated with various severe complications [1]. Clinical evidence indicates that approximately 25% of diabetic patients ultimately develop chronic wounds related to the disease, which pose persistent challenges in wound management and tissue regeneration [2]. Although current therapeutic interventions—including surgical debridement, infection control, and mechanical offloading—can partially improve local conditions to facilitate healing, the prognosis for diabetic wounds remains unsatisfactory [3]. Under normal healing conditions, the sequential phases of hemostasis, inflammatory response, cellular proliferation, and tissue remodeling proceed in a tightly coordinated manner. However, persistent hyperglycemia disrupts this precisely timed regulation, leading to dyssynchrony between repair phases, particularly characterized by prolonged inflammation [4]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying diabetic wounds and the development of novel therapeutic approaches to modulate the immune microenvironment are therefore crucial for improving diabetic wound healing outcomes.

Our previous study demonstrated that CITED2 (CBP/p300-interacting transactivator with glutamic acid/aspartic acid-rich carboxy-terminal domain 2) plays a significant role in the enhancement of diabetic wound healing by adipose-derived stem cells [5]. CITED2 is a key member of the CBP/p300-interacting transactivator family, functioning by competitively binding to the CH1 domain of the transcriptional co-activators CBP/p300, thereby regulating the activity of various transcription factors [6]. In terms of immune regulation, CITED2 suppresses the function of pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as HIF-1α while promoting PPAR-γ-mediated anti-inflammatory gene expression, thus playing a critical regulatory role in macrophage polarization [7]. Notably, CITED2 dysfunction is closely associated with various diseases. Deficiency or mutation of CITED2 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of congenital heart disease, metabolic disorders, and inflammatory conditions (such as atherosclerosis and obesity), further underscoring its importance in immunometabolic regulation [6]. Therefore, an important question arises: does aberrant expression of CITED2 occur during diabetic wound healing, leading to dysregulated macrophage polarization characterized by a sustained increase in the M1 phenotype and a decrease in the M2 phenotype, resulting in a failure to resolve inflammation in a timely manner and impaired tissue repair [8]? This remains unknown. Consequently, elucidating the specific molecular mechanisms by which CITED2 regulates macrophage polarization during diabetic wound repair will provide a critical scientific foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

This study aims to elucidate the role of CITED2 in facilitating diabetic wound healing through modulation of the M1/M2 macrophage polarization switch. We analyzed the dynamic expression of CITED2 in normal versus diabetic wounds, with a particular focus on the changes in CITED2 levels within macrophages during the healing process. Subsequently, we employed both in vitro and in vivo models to investigate the functional impact of CITED2 on macrophage polarization and wound healing. Additionally, we compared the therapeutic effects of a short-term (3-day) local administration of recombinant CITED2 protein with those of clinical rhFGF. To further explore the implications of local CITED2 administration, we developed a fibrin hydrogel-based delivery system characterized by its anti-inflammatory, hemostatic, and non-invasive properties (see Schematic 1). Our findings suggest that CITED2 is a promising therapeutic target capable of modulating macrophage plasticity, thereby enhancing healing in diabetic wounds.

Materials and methods

Data sources

In this study, we obtained two datasets, GSE460, GSE97615, and GSE154556, from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). GEO serves as a public repository for gene expression data, providing a vast array of gene expression profile resources for researchers worldwide. The single-cell spatial transcriptomic data were sourced from the online tool platform of the Xulanden Laboratory (https://www.xulandenlab.com/tools). This platform offers single-cell spatial transcriptomic data resources that significantly enhance research on the spatial distribution of cells within tissues and their gene expression patterns.

Cell culture

The RAW 264.7 macrophages, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, specifically utilizing passages 3 to 5 for subsequent cellular experiments. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Scientific, USA), and low glucose. The incubator was maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. To simulate the hyperglycemic environment in vivo, the cells were cultured in high-glucose (HG) DMEM (35 mmol/L) for 24 h.

Animal experiments

The studies conducted adhered to the ethical standards and regulations prescribed by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (Ethics Review Number: zyfy-an-2024-0631). Seven-week-old male C57BL/6 mice and seven-week-old male db/db mice were supplied by Huachuang Sino (Jiangsu) Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. These mice were observed in the quarantine room of the SPF Animal Experimental Center of Zunyi Medical University (SYXK (Qian) 2021–0004) for one week without any abnormalities before being transferred to the animal housing facility. A fresh solution of 1% streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted in 0.1 M citrate buffer (pH 4.5) and administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 50 mg/kg for one week. Blood samples obtained from the tail vein of the C57BL/6 mice were measured using a glucose meter (Roche) every two days to assess blood glucose levels and body weight. Animals diagnosed with diabetes exhibited blood glucose levels persistently exceeding 16.7 mM for one week, accompanied by weight loss and polyuria symptoms, thereby qualifying them for subsequent experiments.

Initially, all mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and their dorsal fur was shaved using an electric razor. Subsequently, the dorsal skin was depilated with a depilatory cream and disinfected with alcohol. A circular full-thickness wound with a diameter of 1 cm was created at the same horizontal level on the dorsum of each mouse. Photographs were taken, and the wounds were observed on days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-operation. Additionally, wound tissues were collected on these same days for histological analysis, ensuring a minimum of three diabetic mice per group. The wound healing rates were calculated using ImageJ software with the following formula: wound closure (%) = (A0 − At)/A0 × 100% (where A0 represents the wound area on day 0 and At denotes the wound area without epithelialization at time t).

We established normal wounds in C57BL/6 mice and induced diabetic wounds in C57BL/6 mice using streptozotocin (STZ), as well as in db/db mice. Wound tissues were randomly collected at pre-set time points (0, 3, and 10 days) to monitor the expression of CITED2. To validate the role of CITED2 in diabetic wound healing, wounds were created on the backs of db/db mice, which were then randomly divided into six groups: a PBS group and five groups treated with varying concentrations of CITED2 (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 µg). Recombinant CITED2 protein (purchased from Gene Universal, China) was subcutaneously injected around the wounds on days 1, 2, and 3 to determine the optimal concentration of CITED2. Wound tissues were subsequently collected on days 7 and 14 for further experiments. To compare the therapeutic effects of CITED2 with the clinically used rhFGF spray, after wound creation on the backs of db/db mice, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups: a control group, two CITED2-treated groups (CITED2-3 and CITED2-14), and two rhFGF-treated groups (rhFGF-3 and rhFGF-14). The “CITED2-3” and “rhFGF-3” groups received continuous spray treatment for 3 days, while the “CITED2-14” and “rhFGF-14” groups received continuous spray treatment for 14 days. Wound skin and visceral tissues were collected on day 14 for further analysis. To further investigate the impact of Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray on diabetic wound healing, starting from day 0 after establishing the wound model in db/db mice, diabetic wounds were covered with CITED2, Fib-Thr-PEG, or Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray (100 µL, 0.3 µg per wound).

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining

The collected wound samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 48 h, followed by dehydration in a series of graded ethanol solutions (75%, 85%, 95%, 95%, 100%) and embedding in paraffin. Subsequently, the samples were sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. Standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, as well as Masson’s trichrome staining, were performed. After sealing, the samples were observed and analyzed under a microscope. ImageJ software was utilized to calculate the collagen-positive area and the total tissue area from the tissue sections. The percentage of collagen deposition was determined using the formula: (collagen-positive blue area/total tissue area) × 100%.

Immunofluorescence staining for CD31 and α-SMA was performed to evaluate angiogenesis in the wound bed. Additionally, immunostaining with antibodies against F4/80 and CITED2 was conducted to observe the localization of CITED2 and macrophages in the skin. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining with antibodies against iNOS and CD206, which are markers for M1 and M2 macrophages respectively, was utilized to assess macrophage phenotypic polarization in the wounds. Briefly, dewaxed samples were rehydrated, subjected to heat treatment, permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) PBS/Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with BSA/PBS for 30 min. Subsequently, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against α-SMA, CD31, F4/80, CITED2, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, Gpnmb, Nr1d1, TGFβ, VEGF-A, PDGF, iNOS, and CD206 (Abcam, USA). The following day, after washing, samples were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 40 min, followed by a 3-min DAPI staining and additional washing. Finally, samples were dehydrated in graded alcohols, cleared with xylene, and mounted with neutral resin. Fluorescent images were captured using a microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software.

mRNA sequencing and enrichment analysis

In the wound model of db/db mice, skin tissues from both the PBS group and the group receiving subcutaneous injections of 0.1 µg CITED2 were collected on the seventh day of treatment and sent to Knorigene Technology Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China) for mRNA sequencing. Subsequently, the hypergeometric distribution was employed to conduct enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways for the target genes.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cell and skin tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA utilizing the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo, USA). A SYBR Green-based assay was employed to detect the expression levels of mRNA, with GAPDH serving as an internal reference. Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo, USA) using SYBR PCR Master Mix. The relative expression levels of CITED2, Gpnmb, Nr1d1, and PPARγ were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. The primer sequences are presented in Table 1.

Cell proliferation and scratch assay

EdU proliferation assay: Endothelial cells and fibroblasts in the logarithmic growth phase (2 × 10⁵ cells/ml) were seeded into 96-well plates and stimulated for 24 h with conditioned medium derived from macrophages cultured in high glucose, both with and without CITED2 recombinant protein treatment. Following a 2-h incubation with 10 µM EdU, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, after which a Click reaction was performed in the dark for 30 min. EdU-positive cells were quantified using fluorescence microscopy to evaluate proliferation differences among the treatment groups.

Scratch wound healing assay: Endothelial cells and fibroblasts (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were cultured in 6-well plates for a duration of 6 h. Uniform wounds were created using a 200 µL pipette tip. Following PBS washing, the cells were treated with macrophage-conditioned medium, with or without CITED2 recombinant protein, under high glucose conditions. Wound closure was monitored at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted phase-contrast microscope (Leica, Germany), and quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Preparation and characterization of Fib-Thr, Fib-Thr-PEG and Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2

Fibrinogen, thrombin, and polyethylene glycol (PEG) were all procured from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Initially, the recombinant CITED2 protein was combined with PEG and thoroughly mixed, followed by purification at 4 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, this mixture was introduced into a fibrinogen solution at a concentration of 10 mg/mL to create Solution A (Fib - PEG - CITED2). A thrombin solution at a concentration of 10 NIH U/mL was prepared as Solution B. By combining equal volumes of Solution A and Solution B, a hydrogel (Fib - Thr - PEG - CITED2) was generated. The morphology of the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITED2 hydrogel was characterized using a cryo-scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, SU8010). The adhesion and anti-twisting properties of the hydrogel were evaluated using porcine skin. To ensure the reproducibility of the hydrogel, synthesis was performed at different time points (Days 1, 2, and 3), and the gelation behavior and solidification time were recorded for each batch. For the CITED2 recombinant protein loading assay, Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogels were synthesized and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The protein content was quantified using a BCA assay kit. The loading efficiency (%) was calculated as follows: Loading Efficiency (%) = [(Mtotal− Mfree)/Mtotal] × 100%. For degradation assessment, all hydrogels were incubated at 37 °C, and the remaining mass was weighed at predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, and 6 days) to monitor degradation kinetics. To evaluate protein release profiles, Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogels were immersed in PBS at 37 °C, and the supernatant was collected at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. The CITED2 concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit, and the cumulative protein release rate was calculated based on the initial loading amount.

Bicinchoninic acid assay

To evaluate the effect of PEG on the stability of CITED2, PEG-purified and PBS-dissolved CITED2 were aliquoted into a 96-well plate, with triplicate wells for each group, and stored at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay on days 0, 1, 2, and 3, with the values from day 0 serving as the baseline (100%) for calculating residual percentages. The degradation profiles of both groups were subsequently compared.

Hemolysis assay

Blood samples collected from db/db mice were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 10 min to separate plasma and other cellular components. The pelleted erythrocytes were washed three times with PBS to remove residual plasma proteins. Subsequently, purified red blood cells (RBCs) were resuspended in PBS at a ratio of 5 mL packed RBCs to 95 mL PBS to create a standardized RBC suspension. For hemolysis assessment, CITED2, PEG, Fib-Thr, and Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 conjugates were individually mixed with the RBCs suspension in 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min in a humidified incubator to mimic physiological conditions. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 10 min to pellet intact RBCs. The supernatant was carefully collected, and hemoglobin release was quantified by measuring absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Epoch, Biotek, USA). Each experimental condition was tested in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The hemolysis percentage was calculated using the formula: [(As − An)/(Ap − An)] × 100%, where As, An, and Ap represent the absorbance values of the supernatant, negative (PBS), and positive (0.1% Triton X-100) groups, respectively.

Coagulation assay

To assess the coagulation efficacy of hydrogels, samples (PBS, PEG, Fib-Thr, and Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2) were placed in a 12-well plate and pre-warmed at 37 °C for 10 min. Fresh mouse anticoagulated blood (950 µL) and a CaCl₂ solution (25 mmol/L, 50 µL) were then added to each well. After a 1-min incubation at 37 °C, unclotted blood was carefully removed, and the formed clots were gently washed twice with an equal volume of PBS. The resulting blood clots were subsequently subjected to qualitative analysis. For the mice liver incision model, after anesthetizing the mice, their abdomens were opened to expose the liver. A 0.5 × 0.5 cm incision was made on the liver, and the composite spray was applied directly onto the wound. The time taken for hemostasis was recorded, and a piece of filter paper was placed at the base of the liver to assess blood loss.

Dead cell staining

To evaluate cell viability and late-stage apoptosis, adherent RAW264.7 cells cultured in 12-well plates were processed as follows: The culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were gently washed three times with PBS to eliminate residual medium components. Subsequently, 1 mL of a 10 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) working solution (Servicebio, China) was added to each well. After a 10-min incubation at room temperature in light-protected conditions, the staining solution was removed, and the cells were washed with fresh PBS. The stained cells were immediately imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope, where the nuclei of dead cells or late-stage apoptotic cells exhibited red fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± SD and were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was employed, while one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post-hoc tests was utilized for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

Results

CITED2 exhibits a stage-specific expression pattern during wound healing and its expression is suppressed under high glucose conditions

We first analyzed the expression of the CITED2 gene in non-diabetic wounds using the GEO database. The results indicated that CITED2 exhibited dynamic changes during non-diabetic wound healing: its expression rapidly increased in the initial wound phase, gradually declined during the inflammatory phase, was upregulated in the proliferative phase, and subsequently downregulated in the remodeling phase (Fig. 1A, B). Following this, we further examined the dynamic changes of CITED2 during skin wound healing in normal patients using single-cell spatial transcriptomics data. The single-cell results demonstrated that, compared to day 0, CITED2 expression in macrophages decreased at day 1, increased at day 7, and decreased again at day 30 (Fig. 1C-E, Figure S1A). Further spatial transcriptomics revealed that CITED2 expression in M1-type macrophages decreased at day 1, increased at day 7, and declined again at day 30 (Fig. 1F). In contrast to the expression pattern observed in M1 macrophages, M2-type macrophages within the wound bed exhibited markedly low levels of CITED2 expression at both day 1 and day 7 (Figure S1E). Immunofluorescence analysis of normal mouse skin wound tissues showed significant co-localization between CITED2 and the macrophage marker F4/80 (Figure S1B). Based on existing research evidence and the established association among CITED2, macrophages, and inflammation, we hypothesize that the downregulation of CITED2 expression during the inflammatory phase aligns with the requirements of the inflammatory response in normal wound healing, while its increased expression during the proliferative phase (day 7) promotes the transition of macrophages toward a reparative phenotype. Subsequently, we analyzed the expression of CITED2 in diabetic wounds using the GEO database (GSE154556). The results indicated that the expression of the CITED2 gene was significantly downregulated in diabetic wounds compared to non-diabetic wounds (Fig. 1H). Next, we established wound models in both normal and diabetic mice to observe the expression pattern of the CITED2 gene. The results indicated that, compared to the Control group, wound healing was significantly delayed in the STZ and db/db groups (Fig. 1H, I). On day 3, the markers for M1 macrophages (CD68/CD80) and the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β were upregulated (Figure S1C, D), while the mRNA expression levels of IL-4, IL-10, and CITED2 genes were significantly decreased (Fig. 1J, K, and Figure S1C, D). Further in vitro intervention experiments demonstrated that, compared to the normal glucose group, macrophages cultured in a 35 mM high-glucose medium exhibited M1 macrophage morphology (characterized by a round shape with multiple pseudopods) and showed significantly reduced mRNA expression levels of the CITED2 gene (Fig. 1L, M). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that the expression of the CITED2 gene is suppressed in diabetic wounds. This suppression hinders the transition of M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages during the inflammatory phase, thereby triggering chronic inflammation and impairing wound healing.

CITED2 accelerates diabetic wound closure by promoting epithelialization, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis

Given the downregulation of CITED2 expression in diabetic wounds, we investigated whether topical supplementation of CITED2 recombinant protein could enhance delayed healing. Initially, we analyzed the protein structure of CITED2 (28 kDa), which lacks transmembrane domains and a signal peptide sequence (Figure S2A–C). Subsequently, the amino acid sequence of CITED2 was optimized for expression in an E. coli system. The synthesized gene was subcloned into the pET-22b(+) vector using NdeI and XhoI restriction sites, and the protein was purified and verified via a His-tag (Fig. 2A). The resulting recombinant protein exhibited a purity exceeding 80%, and SDS-PAGE confirmed its molecular weight to be between 25 and 35 kDa (Figure S2D). To explore the role of CITED2 in diabetic wound healing, we established a wound model in db/db diabetic mice. On days 1, 2, and 3 post-wounding, varying doses (0, 0.05 µg, 0.1 µg, 0.2 µg, 0.4 µg, 0.8 µg) of CITED2 recombinant protein were injected subcutaneously around the wound edges. Compared with the control group, all CITED2 doses accelerated wound healing, with the 0.1 µg dose (equivalent to 1 µg/mL, 100 µL per injection) demonstrating the most significant effect (Fig. 2B, C). Therefore, this concentration was selected for all subsequent experiments. On day 7, HE staining revealed that wound width was significantly reduced in the CITED2 group compared with the control. By day 14, HE and Masson’s trichrome staining showed that wounds in the CITED2-treated group were completely re-epithelialized, with abundant granulation tissue and markedly increased collagen deposition. In contrast, the control group exhibited prominent scab formation, incomplete re-epithelialization, and reduced collagen deposition (Fig. 2D–G). Further immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that on day 7, expression of the angiogenesis marker CD31 was significantly increased following CITED2 treatment, whereas α-SMA expression showed no significant change (Fig. 2H–J). However, CITED2 treatment significantly upregulated the expression levels of the pro-angiogenic growth factors VEGF-A, PDGF, and TGF-β within the wound bed (Figure S3). By day 14, both CD31 and α-SMA expression were significantly upregulated (Fig. 2K–M), indicating that CITED2 promotes local angiogenesis in the wound. In summary, CITED2 expression is reduced in diabetic wounds, and topical application of CITED2 recombinant protein accelerates diabetic wound healing by promoting re-epithelialization, increasing collagen deposition, and enhancing angiogenesis.

CITED2 promote the switch of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying CITED2-mediated diabetic wound healing, we performed transcriptomic sequencing analysis of wound tissues from control and CITED2-treated groups at day 7 post-treatment. Volcano plot analysis revealed 422 differentially expressed genes following CITED2 recombinant protein treatment. A heatmap displayed the top 10 upregulated genes, with the anti-inflammatory genes Gpnmb and Nr1d1 being significantly increased (Fig. 3A, B), a finding further validated by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3C) [9, 10]. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis demonstrated that CITED2 enhanced biological processes related to immune system responses (Fig. 3D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis indicated upregulation of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway in CITED2-treated wounds (Fig. 3E), with RT-qPCR confirming elevated PPAR-γ expression (Fig. 3F). Given that PPAR-γ is a master regulator of macrophage M2 polarization, its upregulation suggests that CITED2 may exert anti-inflammatory effects by inducing macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Biological Process analysis revealed robust engagement of immune system processes, including enhanced defense responses and activation of both innate and adaptive immunity (Fig. 3G). Cellular Component analysis identified signaling receptor binding as the most significantly affected function, accompanied by modulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and receptor-ligand activity (Fig. 3G). Molecular Function analysis demonstrated predominant enrichment in extracellular structures, with strong regulation of extracellular regions, matrix components, and collagen-containing matrix structures, indicative of active tissue remodeling (Fig. 3G). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further confirmed upregulation of key biological pathways, including positive regulation of immune response, antigen processing and presentation, and metabolic reprogramming (e.g., cellular lipid metabolism, PPAR signaling, and linoleic acid metabolism) (Fig. 3H). Additionally, enhanced receptor-ligand activity suggested improved cell-cell communication. Collectively, these findings strongly support our hypothesis that CITED2 promotes M2 macrophage polarization. To validate this, we performed immunofluorescence staining of day 7 wound tissues, which revealed increased expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD206 and decreased expression of the M1 marker iNOS in the CITED2-treated group compared to controls (Fig. 4A-C). Furthermore, downregulation of pro-inflammatory genes (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) and upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes (IL-10, Gpnmb, Nr1d1) in CITED2-treated wounds (Fig. 4D-L). These results confirm that CITED2 drives macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, suppresses pro-inflammatory responses, and enhances anti-inflammatory signaling, thereby accelerating wound healing in diabetic mice.

CITED2 modulates M2 macrophage polarization to promote proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and fibroblasts

Extensive studies have established that M2-polarized macrophages critically contribute to tissue repair by secreting a spectrum of growth factors (VEGF, TGF-β, PDGF, and IGF-1) that collectively establish a regenerative microenvironment [4, 11]. These mediators exert coordinated effects on two pivotal cell types: endothelial cells and fibroblasts. M2-derived VEGF potently stimulates endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and neovascularization, while simultaneously secreted TGF-β promotes vascular maturation - processes essential for restoring perfusion to the wound bed. In parallel, the M2-secreted growth factor milieu (particularly TGF-β and PDGF) activates fibroblasts, driving their proliferation and differentiation into ECM-producing myofibroblasts that mediate tissue contraction, while IGF-1 further enhances collagen synthesis and matrix remodeling [4]. Here, we further investigated the effects of conditioned medium (CITED2-CM) from macrophages treated with CITED2 recombinant protein under high-glucose conditions on the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and fibroblasts in vitro (Fig. 5A). First, we examined the impact of CITED2 recombinant protein on macrophage polarization under high-glucose (35 mM) conditions. The results show that macrophages in the 35 mM group exhibited an M1-like morphology, whereas those in the 35 mM + CITED2 group displayed an M2-like morphology (Fig. 5B). Further RT-qPCR analysis revealed that, compared to the 35 mM group, the 35 mM + CITED2 group showed decreased expression of the M1 macrophage marker CD80 and increased expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD206 (Fig. 5C), indicating that CITED2 promotes M2 macrophage polarization. Next, we added conditioned medium from the 35 mM and 35 mM + CITED2 groups to cultured endothelial cells and fibroblasts. EdU assay results demonstrated that, compared to the control group, the CITED2-CM group exhibited enhanced proliferation of both endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 5D, E). Scratch wound healing assays further revealed that the CITED2-CM group significantly promoted the migration of endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 5F, G). Study suggests that CITED2 reprograms macrophages toward an M2 phenotype under high-glucose conditions, thereby enhancing the proliferative and migratory capacities of endothelial cells and fibroblasts, which may contribute to improved wound healing.

Efficacy comparison of CITED2 and rhFGF in promoting diabetic wound healing

In this study, we compared the efficacy of recombinant CITED2 protein with clinical rhFGF in promoting diabetic wound healing. Due to the anti - inflammatory property of CITED2, it only needs to be used for 3 days during the inflammation phase, while clinical rhFGF requires continuous use for 14 days. Thus, we set up five groups: a control group, CITED2–3 (using CITED2 for 3 days), CITED2–14 (using CITED2 for 14 days), rhFGF −3 (using rhFGF for 3 days), and rhFGF −14 (using rhFGF for 14 days). The results showed that all treatment groups healed faster than the control group (Figure S4A). Among them, the CITED2–3 and rhFGF −14 groups had the fastest wound - healing rates, and there was no significant difference between these two groups (Figure S4A, B). HE staining revealed that compared to the control group, the wound widths in the CITED2–3 and rhFGF −14 groups were significantly narrower, and there was no obvious difference between them (Figure S4C, D). Masson staining indicated that both the CITED2–3 and rhFGF −14 groups had increased collagen deposition in the wound compared to the control group, with no statistical difference between the two (Figure S4E, F). In terms of biosafety, HE staining of the heart, liver, spleen, and kidney tissues of the control, CITED2, and rhFGF groups after 14 days of treatment showed no obvious differences among the three groups (Figure S4G). Overall, these findings suggest that a short - term application of CITED2 (3 days) can achieve a similar effect to a long - term application of rhFGF (14 days) in promoting diabetic wound healing, and have good biosafety.

Preparation and characterization of hydrogels

To reduce the pain and infection risks associated with repeated injections and extend the biological activity of CITED2 at the wound site, we successfully developed an external hydrogel spray loaded with recombinant CITED2 protein. The PEGylated CITED2 was added to the fibrinogen solution to form a well - flowing mixed liquid, which, when combined with the thrombin solution, could form the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 hydrogel (Fig. 6A). Considering that the hydrophilicity of PEG could slow down the degradation of recombinant CITED2 protein, we measured the degradation rate after mixing PEG with CITED2. The results showed that PEG indeed slowed down the degradation rate of recombinant CITED2 protein, exhibiting a certain sustained - release effect (Figure S5 A). Next, we tested the coagulation conditions of fibrin hydrogels (10 mg/mL Fib, 100 µL + 10 u/mL Thr, 100 µl) with different proportions of PEG (50/60/70/80 µl) added. The results indicated that the experimental groups with 50, 60, and 70 µl of PEG added could coagulate, while the group with 80 µl of PEG failed to coagulate (Figure S5 B). Further analysis of the coagulation time revealed that the coagulation time of the hydrogel increased gradually with the increase in the amount of PEG added. Specifically, the coagulation time of the 50 µl group was the same as that of the fibrin hydrogel without PEG added (Figure S5 C, D). Quantification of loading efficiency revealed that the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel achieved a CITED2 protein loading rate exceeding 90% (Figure S5 E). Furthermore, the cumulative release of CITED2 from the hydrogel reached 70% within 72 h (Figure S5 F). Based on these results, we decided to use 50 µl of PEG for the subsequent experiments. After successfully preparing the hydrogel (Fig. 6B), to verify the adhesion performance of the hydrogel, we sprayed Solution onto a petri dish respectively. It was found that the dried and coagulated Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 hydrogel could firmly adhere to the petri dish (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, we further tested its adhesion on pigskin. The results showed that the hydrogel could adhere tightly to the pigskin and did not fall off with the morphological changes of the pigskin (Fig. 6D). Degradation assays demonstrated that the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel reached approximately 90% degradation by Day 6, showing no significant difference compared to the Fib-Thr and Fib-Thr-PEG groups (Figure S5 G). The SEM observation revealed that the Fib - Thr group exhibited a continuous, irregular, and interconnected porous three - dimensional network structure. However, when PEG was added, the Fib - Thr - PEG and Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 hydrogel groups showed a non - porous three - dimensional structure (Fig. 6E).

Biosafety of hydrogels

Before applying hydrogel dressings to wounds, comprehensively evaluating their biocompatibility is a crucial step to ensure safe use. The hemolysis test, as an important means of assessing the blood compatibility of biomaterials, can reflect the impact of materials on red blood cells in the blood. This characteristic is of great significance for the in - vivo application of biomaterials. As shown in Figure S6A, in sharp contrast to the obvious red color of the supernatant in the positive control group, the color of the supernatants in the remaining hydrogel groups did not change significantly. This clearly indicates that these hydrogels did not cause obvious damage or destruction to red blood cells. Further analysis of the hemolysis rates of each hydrogel group showed that the hemolysis rates of all hydrogel groups were less than 5% (Figure S6B). This data strongly proves that these hydrogels have good blood compatibility and meet the basic safety requirements for clinical application of biomaterials. The results of dead - cell staining provide further evidence for the biocompatibility of the hydrogels. As shown in Figure S6C-F, there was no significant difference in the dead - cell staining performance between the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 group and the Control group. This means that this hydrogel has no adverse effects on cell viability and survival status at the cellular level, further confirming its good biocompatibility. Fibrin hydrogels play an indispensable role in the wound - healing process due to their unique hemostatic properties. Hemostasis, as the initial stage of wound healing, is crucial for subsequent tissue repair and regeneration. The in - vitro coagulation test is an intuitive and effective method for evaluating the hemostatic ability of hydrogels. As shown in Figure S7A, no obvious blood clots were observed in the PBS group and the PEG group during the test. In contrast, the Fib - Thr group and the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITED2 group showed significant blood clots, and there was no significant difference in the formation of blood clots between these two groups. The above results were further corroborated in a mouse liver hemorrhage model (Figure S7B). This result fully indicates that the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 hydrogel has a hemostatic ability comparable to that of the Fib - Thr hydrogel and can rapidly and effectively promote blood coagulation in an in - vitro environment, providing strong support for its application in actual wound treatment.

Fib-Thr-PEG-CITE2 hydrogel spray accelerated diabetic wound healing in vivo

We established a diabetic wound model using db/db mice and initiated the intervention with the spray on day 0 (Fig. 7A). The experimental results demonstrated that the local application of the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 spray could significantly accelerate the wound - healing process in diabetic mice (Fig. 7B, C). On day 14, we employed HE staining to evaluate the wound structure. As can be seen from Fig. 7D and F, there was no significant difference in the wound structure between the CITED2 group and the Fib - Thr - PEG group, while the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 group exhibited the narrowest wound width. To further investigate the structural changes in the extracellular matrix, we conducted Masson’s trichrome staining. The results revealed that the Fib - Thr - PEG - CITE2 group showed more abundant and mature collagen deposition (Fig. 7E, G). Finally, immunofluorescence results showed that the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITE2 group exhibited the highest expression of α-SMA and CD31 compared to the other groups, indicating greater vascular neogenesis and maturation (Fig. 7H-J). These findings collectively demonstrate that the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITE2 spray significantly enhances diabetic wound healing by promoting collagen deposition, vascular maturation, and structural repair. Furthermore, we performed additional immunofluorescence staining analyses on wound tissues harvested at Day 7 to compare the macrophage phenotypic switch between the hydrogel treatment groups and the controls. As shown in Figure S8, the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel group exhibited a significant increase in the expression of the reparative macrophage marker CD206, accompanied by a marked decrease in the pro-inflammatory marker iNOS, when compared to both the CITED2 and Fib-Thr-PEG groups (Figure S8 A-D). Notably, the Fib-Thr-PEG hydrogel group also showed an increase in CD206 expression, although to a lesser extent than the CITED2 group. We attribute this observation to the mild pro-reparative microenvironment created by the fibrin-based matrix itself, with CITED2 further amplifying this pro-resolving polarization. These data indicate that the M1 to M2 phenotypic switch mediated by the CITED2-loaded hydrogel promotes diabetic wound healing.

Discussion

This study establishes CITED2 as a critical regulator of diabetic wound healing. We demonstrate dynamic expression patterns of CITED2 during wound repair, with its downregulation in diabetic wounds impairing the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages and perpetuating inflammation. Local administration of recombinant CITED2 protein promoted macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, significantly enhancing re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and neovascularization, thereby accelerating wound closure. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that CITED2 potentially mediates the activation of key regenerative pathways, particularly PPAR signaling and immune modulation. Furthermore, we developed a Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray that enables non-invasive delivery, providing rapid hemostatic and anti-inflammatory effects, and presenting a novel therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound management (Scheme 1).

A Novel Preclinical Strategy for Diabetic Wound: CITED2-Loaded Hydrogel Spray Targeted Macrophage Reprogramming. The upper dashed box in the figure illustrates the synthesis process of recombinant CITED2 protein. The middle section outlines the treatment approach. The lower part describes the mechanism by which it promotes wound healing in diabetic mice. (by Figdraw)

Evidence indicates that macrophages dynamically adjust their transition from pro-inflammatory to pro-healing phenotypes in response to spatiotemporal cues during wound healing, establishing a positive feedback loop that shapes an immunomodulatory microenvironment and drives progressive cellular proliferation cascades [4]. However, the hyperglycemic milieu in diabetic wounds disrupts these cellular responses, stimulating excessive secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) by macrophages. This perpetuates M1 macrophage polarization and sustains a vicious cycle of chronic inflammation [12, 13]. Consequently, modulating macrophage heterogeneity has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound healing. Previous studies have implicated CITED2 in macrophage regulation. Research demonstrates that CITED2 deficiency markedly attenuates rosiglitazone-induced PPARγ activity, its recruitment to target gene promoters, and subsequent anti-inflammatory gene expression in macrophages [7]. A research result shows that myeloid-specific CITED2 deletion significantly enhances macrophage recruitment to inflammatory sites in murine models of acute lung injury [14]. Nevertheless, the functional relationship between CITED2 and wound-associated macrophages remains unexplored.

Our study identified CITED2 as a crucial molecular switch regulating macrophage plasticity during wound healing. Through integrated analysis of GEO datasets (GSE460 and GSE154556) and single-cell spatial transcriptomics, we discovered that CITED2 exhibits a characteristic dynamic expression pattern during normal wound healing: rapid upregulation in the initial injury phase (0–1 day), moderate downregulation during inflammation (1–3 days), followed by significant resurgence in the proliferative phase (7 days). This precise spatiotemporal regulation suggests CITED2’s involvement in macrophage phenotype switching across different healing stages. In diabetic wounds, this physiological fluctuation was disrupted, manifesting as sustained suppression of CITED2 expression during the inflammatory phase. Immunofluorescence co-localization experiments confirmed significant co-expression of CITED2 with the macrophage marker F4/80, while high glucose conditions markedly reduced CITED2 expression in cultured macrophages, providing direct evidence for dysregulation of the CITED2-macrophage axis in diabetic wounds. Supplementation with recombinant CITED2 protein significantly reshaped macrophage inflammatory phenotypes. Transcriptome sequencing revealed upregulation of anti-inflammatory genes Gpnmb and Nr1d1 in CITED2-treated wounds. KEGG pathway analysis specifically identified PPAR signaling pathway activation, with qPCR validation showing significantly elevated PPAR-γ expression levels. This finding is particularly significant as PPAR-γ serves as the master transcription factor regulating M2 macrophage polarization. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that under high glucose conditions, CITED2 treatment transformed macrophages from typical M1 morphology (round, pseudopodia) to M2 morphology (elongated, well-spread), accompanied by upregulated M2 marker CD206 and downregulated M1 marker CD80. Notably, CITED2-induced macrophage phenotype switching produced significant biological effects. Immunofluorescence showed increased CD206 expression and decreased iNOS expression in CITED2-treated wounds. This phenotypic conversion was associated with marked improvement in the inflammatory microenvironment: elevated levels of IL-10, Gpnmb and Nr1d1, alongside reduced pro-inflammatory factors IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α. These changes likely form a positive feedback loop: CITED2 promotes M2 polarization via the PPAR-γ pathway, while IL-10 secreted by M2 macrophages further enhances PPAR-γ signaling, thereby maintaining a stable anti-inflammatory microenvironment. This self-reinforcing regulatory mechanism may underlie CITED2’s ability to persistently improve inflammatory status in diabetic wounds.

With the resolution of inflammation, wound healing enters the critical proliferative phase, a process highly dependent on the coordinated action of endothelial cells and fibroblasts [4]. Endothelial cells provide essential oxygen and nutrients to the wound by forming new vascular networks, while fibroblasts are responsible for the synthesis and remodeling of the extracellular matrix, collectively establishing the structural foundation for tissue regeneration [15–17]. In diabetic wounds, persistent hyperglycemia impairs endothelial cell function, manifested as reduced angiogenic capacity, loss of barrier integrity, and increased susceptibility to apoptosis, while fibroblasts exhibit restricted proliferation and migration along with decreased collagen synthesis [18, 19]. These pathological alterations collectively hinder the normal wound repair process. Our study found that CITED2 creates a microenvironment conducive to tissue regeneration by inducing macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype. In vitro experiments confirmed that conditioned medium from CITED2-treated macrophages significantly enhanced the biological activity of both endothelial cells and fibroblasts: EdU proliferation assays showed increased proliferation rates of endothelial cells and fibroblasts in the treatment group; scratch assays demonstrated that endothelial cells and fibroblasts in the treatment group exhibited accelerated migration speeds compared to the control group. These pro-proliferative and pro-migratory effects may stem from alterations in the growth factor secretion profile of M2-polarized macrophages. These in vitro findings were further validated in animal models. Immunofluorescence results showed that CD31 + vascular density, VEGF-A, PDGF, and TGF-β within the wound bedin CITED2-treated wounds was significantly increased by day 7, and by day 14, not only was the number of blood vessels further increased, but the proportion of α-SMA+ mature vessels was also significantly higher, indicating that CITED2 treatment not only promoted angiogenesis but also accelerated vascular maturation. Masson’s staining revealed that collagen deposition in CITED2-treated wounds was significantly increased compared to the control group, with more organized collagen fiber alignment, suggesting better restoration of tissue structural integrity.

Building on the therapeutic potential and clinical translation needs of CITED2 protein, we compared its efficacy with clinically used rhFGF treatment and found that short-term (3-day) CITED2 treatment achieved comparable wound healing effects to conventional 14-day rhFGF therapy. Additionally, designing an appropriate delivery system to avoid injection-related pain and infection while prolonging CITED2’s bioactivity in wounds is crucial [20]. Fibrin, as a natural ECM component, offers excellent biocompatibility and pro-coagulation properties; thrombin enables rapid in situ gelation; while PEG prolongs drug release through hydrophilicity and steric hindrance effects [21, 22]. The synergistic combination of these three materials overcomes the limitations of single components, leading us to develop a Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 sprayable hydrogel delivery system with multiple advantages. This system achieved rapid gelation while maintaining excellent mechanical properties. Scanning electron microscopy revealed a unique non-porous 3D structure, where the dense network combined with PEG’s hydrophilicity significantly delayed CITED2 degradation. Furthermore, this hydrogel system demonstrated good adhesiveness, biosafety, and hemostatic properties. When applied to diabetic wounds, the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray showed superior wound healing efficacy compared to both CITED2 alone and Fib-Thr-PEG control groups. Importantly, when applied to diabetic wounds, the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel spray achieved a wound healing rate comparable to that of subcutaneous CITED2 injection (Figs. 2C and 7C).

Unlike conventional therapies (e.g., rhFGF) that target stromal cells but fail to address the diabetic immunosuppressive microenvironment, CITED2 acts as a master regulator targeting dysregulated macrophage polarization via PPAR-γ signaling to resolve chronic inflammation. Furthermore, the Fib-Thr-PEG hydrogel is not merely a passive carrier; its fibrin matrix intrinsically fosters a pro-reparative niche. The two may exert a synergistic effect —where the hydrogel provides structural cues and CITED2 reprograms immunity—offers a distinct advantage over single-mechanism approaches, enabling effective healing (Figure S8). Indeed, this study has several limitations. First, although the results demonstrate that CITED2-treated macrophages polarize toward the M2 phenotype, the underlying mechanisms remain to be further investigated (such as PPAR-γ). Second, while the Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2 hydrogel exhibits excellent preclinical performance, its long-term safety and efficacy in clinical settings require further validation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CITED2 plays a critical role in diabetic wound healing by modulating macrophage polarization, enhancing extracellular matrix remodeling, and promoting angiogenesis. Our findings reveal that CITED2 expression is dynamically regulated during wound repair, with its downregulation in diabetic wounds contributing to impaired M1-to-M2 macrophage transition and sustained inflammation. Local administration of recombinant CITED2 protein accelerated wound closure by stimulating epithelialization, collagen deposition, and neovascularization, while also shifting macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Transcriptomic analysis further confirmed that CITED2 potentially activates key regenerative pathways, including PPAR signaling and immune regulation. To optimize therapeutic delivery, we developed a fibrin-based hydrogel spray (Fib-Thr-PEG-CITED2) that ensures sustained CITED2 release, robust adhesion, and excellent biocompatibility. Notably, short-term CITED2 treatment (3 days) achieved comparable efficacy to prolonged rhFGF therapy (14 days), highlighting its clinical potential. The hydrogel spray significantly improved wound healing in diabetic mice by enhancing vascular maturation and collagen organization. In summary, our work establishes CITED2 as a promising therapeutic target for diabetic wounds, offering a novel, minimally invasive treatment strategy that combines immunomodulation, tissue regeneration, and practical hydrogel delivery. These findings pave the way for future clinical translation in chronic wound management.
