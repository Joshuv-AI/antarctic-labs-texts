A Framework for Emergency Department–Integrated Hepatitis C Test‐and‐Treat in the United States
Authors: Saeed S Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite the availability of curative, direct‐acting antiviral therapy, hepatitis C virus elimination remains incomplete. Losses across the care cascade continue to limit impact, from initial diagnosis to sustained virologic response. Fewer than 1/3 of individuals ultimately achieve cure. These gaps reflect a delivery system that does not align with the population most affected, many of whom have inconsistent engagement with outpatient care. The emergency department is critical, but underused point of contact, where the burden of undiagnosed and untreated infection is high. This manuscript presents a practical framework for integrating HCV testing and treatment into emergency care within a new United States. It focuses on five domains. Point of care RNA testing allows confirmation of infection during the encounter, reducing delays that contribute to loss to follow up. Treatment initiation is simplified through standardised eligibility criteria and use of pan genomic regimens supported by electronic decision support. A focused safety screen addresses key exclusions, including hepatitis B coinfection, advanced liver disease, renal impairment, and relevant drug interactions. Policy and financial barriers are examined, including prior authorisation requirements, variation of Medicaid coverage, and access to discount pricing programmes. Post treatment follow up is restructured through decentralised approaches such as dried blood spot testing, telemedicine, and linkage to harm reduction services. Implementation will vary across institutions and regions. Regulatory requirements, payer policies, and staffing models remain important constraints. These challenges identify areas for targeted policy reform and prospective study. The emergency department is not traditionally designed for chronic disease management. However, for a curable infection concentrated among patients who rely on episodic care, it may represent the most effective point of intervention.Keywords: care cascade, direct‐acting antivirals, emergency service, hepatitis C, hospital, point‐of‐care testing

Introduction

In 2016, the World Health Organization set an ambitious target of eliminating hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection by 2030. Nearly a decade later, it is clear that the mere availability of a cure is insufficient to bridge aspiration to reality. The discovery of direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs) transformed HCV infection from a disease managed over decades to one curable in 8 weeks with a daily oral regimen. Unfortunately, deployment of this cure remains suboptimal. The HCV care cascade defines sequential steps from screening and diagnosis, linkage to care, treatment initiation, ongoing monitoring, culminating in sustained virologic response (SVR) which signifies a cure. There is significant fracture in the continuum of care, with progressive attrition at each step. A large meta‐analysis found that among persons screened positive for HCV, only 62% attended their first care appointment, 41% initiated treatment, 38% completed treatment, and only 29% achieved cure [1], highlighting the magnitude of this problem.

The emergency department (ED) serves as a vital access point for patients and encounters a higher prevalence of HCV infections compared to other care settings [2]. Given the frequency with which HCV‐infected individuals interact with the ED, re‐conceptualising the care cascade to integrate screening and treatment initiation within this setting, could represent a crucial step toward addressing this public health challenge.

This manuscript proposes a pragmatic framework for ED‐based HCV elimination within the United States healthcare system. The challenges and solutions described reflect the structure of U.S. care delivery, payer models and regulatory conditions. It may not directly translate to settings with different coverage or access constraints. The framework is intended to highlight the need for region‐specific strategies to address gaps in the care cascade. Five domains are addressed: point‐of‐care diagnostics, simplified treatment initiation and eligibility screening, policy and financial barriers, operational considerations and post‐treatment surveillance. Point of Care Testing Innovations

A critical requirement for any ED‐based model is the ability to establish a definitive diagnosis during the encounter itself. Point of care (POC) POC HCV RNA testing offers solutions to current, often cumbersome diagnostic pathways. Many facilities currently use a two‐step testing process: an initial antibody screening via rapid or lab‐based immunoassay, followed by confirmatory RNA testing if positive. These assays are conducted in separate laboratory sections, such as immunology and molecular diagnostics, or sent to external reference laboratories [3], creating turnaround times on the order of days. POC fingerstick testing delivers results in ~1 h [4], allowing diagnosis to be made within the critical window of opportunity where patient retention is most practical and intervention can be initiated promptly. Multiple studies have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for these platforms [5, 6]. On June 27th 2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted market authorisation to the first of these products [7]. The ability to perform and interpret these tests without training in laboratory techniques makes them ideally applicable in ED settings. Widespread implementation, however, would require solutions to existing cost barriers. The Global Fund has successfully negotiated pricing and procurement agreements with Cepheid, the developer of the only FDA‐approved POC HCV RNA test to subsidise the cost of its tuberculosis testing products. Expansion of these agreements to include HCV testing products should also be pursued. Negotiation of bulk pricing by state or federal agencies, provision of subsidies for departments implementing these tests and advocacy for universal coverage of POC HCV RNA testing under Medicaid, Medicare, and private insurance are reasonable funding considerations.

Simplified Treatment Initiation

The initiation of DAAs by non‐specialist providers may seem intimidating, and the concern is not unfounded. Expecting ED physicians to counsel patients on DAA side effect profiles, recognise drug interactions, and independently navigate serologic and fibrosis workups is a genuine cognitive burden in an already compressed clinical environment. Experience from other settings, however, suggests this barrier is surmountable when the right infrastructure surrounds the clinician. The HepCCATT study demonstrated meaningful improvement across the HCV care cascade. This was not achieved by training physicians to function as hepatitis C specialists. Instead, it relied on embedding ECHO‐Chicago tele‐mentoring support and providing frontline clinicians with expert backup [8]. Critically, HepCCATT also illustrates the ceiling of workforce training alone. Despite gains in screening and diagnosis, only 7.7% of confirmed cases were ultimately treated. This finding underscores that individual clinician capacity is not the binding constraint. In the proposed ED model, this limitation is addressed through a different approach. EHR‐embedded clinical decision support (CDS) automates eligibility assessment, reflex laboratory ordering, and DAA prescription generation, reducing the physician's role to confirming eligibility and overriding the system when clinical judgement requires it.

On June 11th, 2025, the FDA approved an expanded indication for glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (Mavyret), making it the first and only oral, pan‐genotypic, 8‐week regimen for the treatment of acute HCV in patients ≥ 3 years without or with compensated cirrhosis [9]. This decision, grounded in the findings of the M20‐350 trial, further strengthens the rationale for ED‐based treatment models. While most cases of acute HCV infection are asymptomatic, up to 20% of individuals develop clinically apparent illness, often severe enough to prompt evaluation in the ED [10]. However, the majority of HCV diagnoses made in the ED represent undiagnosed chronic infection. Distinguishing between these two is often clinically impossible. The stronger argument for ED‐based treatment rests not on the acute indication but on the well‐established efficacy of glecaprevir/pibrentasvir. The ED encounter however brief, represents a rare and often unrepeatable opportunity for intervention.

Supporting this simplified treatment paradigm is the MINMON trial, which enrolled even compensated cirrhotics and HIV‐infected individuals with suppressed viral loads. Adopting a minimalist strategy, researchers omitted pre‐treatment genotyping, scheduled office visits, laboratory monitoring and dispensed the entire treatment course (84 tablets) to participants on enrolment. 91% of participants completed the 84 tablet course, SVR rate was 95% and safety was exceptional with no adverse events necessitating treatment discontinuation or death [11]. The pragmatic approach of MINMON suggests that DAAs can be dispensed from the ED both safely and in a manner that delivers strong value for societal resources. Particularly compelling were SVR rates, which rival those reported in large outpatient and real‐world DAA cohorts [12].

Eligibility and the Minimal Safety Screen

Based on the updated HCV test‐and‐treat algorithm, treatment can be safely initiated during an ED encounter in patients who are DAA‐naïve, not known to be HBsAg‐positive, have no history of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and who lack clinical features of decompensated cirrhosis (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice, or varices) [13]. In this model, each of these criteria is operationalised within the ED encounter through EHR‐embedded CDS to reduce cognitive burden on ED clinicians.

Hepatitis B Virus Status

Before DAA initiation, consideration must be given to coinfection with HBV owing to potential for seroconversion or HBV viral reactivation during therapy [14]. Standard protocols involve assessing serum HBsAg, anti‐HBs, and anti‐HBc prior to DAA therapy initiation to assess whether a patient is immune to HBV, previously exposed, or actively infected. To alleviate cognitive burden on ED physicians, EHR‐based CDS tools should prompt these HBV serologic tests when HCV RNA positivity is confirmed, with reflexive ordering if results are not previously documented. A similar automated workflow has been successfully implemented in EDs, reflexing HCV RNA testing for positive HCV antibody testing on the same specimen [15].

Those HBV‐immune from vaccination [HBsAg (−), anti‐HBc (−), anti‐HBs (+)] can proceed with ED‐based DAA initiation without additional precautions. Patients with resolved HBV infection [HBsAg (−), anti‐HBc (+), anti‐HBs (+)], although at theoretical risk of HBV reactivation, absolute risk is low. In a systematic review of 1317 HBsAg‐negative, anti‐HBc‐positive patients treated with DAAs for HCV across six studies, the occurrence of HBV DNA reappearance and clinical reactivation was 0.91% [16]. Prospective 1‐year surveillance data further address concerns regarding late reactivation, showing zero reactivations among 108 patients with resolved HBV infection. Some 16.7% did experience transient ALT elevations [17], hence a strategy of post‐treatment ALT monitoring is prudent.

Those with active hepatitis B infection [HBsAg (+)] are at risk too high to proceed with simplified treatment. In a pooled analysis of 17 studies, the risk of HBV reactivation in HBsAg positive patients was computed at 24% [18]. Such cases of coinfection require HBV antiviral prophylaxis extending 6–12 months beyond DAA completion [19]. This duration and monitoring burden is fundamentally incompatible with a single‐encounter treatment model.

HIV And Antiretroviral Interactions

While MINMON supports DAA initiation in HIV positive individuals, caution is required. The central concern is that of pharmacologic interaction between DAA regimens and antiretroviral therapy (ART). The proposed model addresses this by stratifying HIV patients into three operational tiers. Patients with documented HIV virologic suppression on a compatible ART regimen may proceed with ED‐initiated DAA therapy. Compatibility can be determined automatically through EHR‐embedded interaction checks. Those with viral suppression but on an interacting ART regimen are not started on DAAs in the ED. They are flagged for expedited referral to an infectious disease specialist. Patients with unsuppressed or undocumented HIV viral load are deferred from ED‐initiated treatment owing to the degree of clinical complexity such management entails.

Renal Impairment

Renal impairment warrants brief consideration given its high prevalence in the ED population and relationship with HCV. HCV infection promotes renal disease through various mechanisms including membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, direct cytopathic injury to podocytes and tubular cells, and cryoglobulinemia [20].

An internal inconsistency exists within current AASLD/IDSA guidance. While instruction regarding renal impairment endorses DAA therapy without dose adjustment across all CKD stages, the simplified treatment algorithm excludes patients with eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2. It reflects historic caution around sofosbuvir's metabolite GS‐331007 which is renally cleared and putatively associated with cardiac toxicity in animal models [21]. Contemporary data supports the safety and efficacy of both sofosbuvir and glecaprevir/pibrentasvir regimens in advanced CKD and dialysis [22]. In the current proposed model, ESRD is not an exclusion. Patients with eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 who otherwise meet criteria proceed with glecaprevir/pibrentasvir, whose biliary clearance removes renal pharmacokinetic concerns.

Cirrhosis and Hepatocellular Carcinoma

The early DAA era furnished signals suggesting accelerated hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) occurrence/recurrence after therapy, particularly in those with cirrhosis or prior history of HCC [23]. Contemporary consensus holds that DAA treatment in cirrhotic patients reduces long‐term HCC risk compared to untreated cirrhosis but does not eliminate it [24]. Ongoing surveillance therefore remains necessary. Decompensated cirrhosis further complicates treatment selection. NS3/4A protease inhibitor‐containing regimens are contraindicated due to impaired hepatic metabolism and the risk of drug accumulation. Instead, management requires sofosbuvir‐based regimens with ribavirin, introducing monitoring demands that are incompatible with a simplified low‐touch framework.

Cirrhosis exclusion in the ED is operationalised through a combinatorial assessment of FIB‐4 and ultrasonography (USG). In the context of HCV infection, a FIB‐4 of < 1.45 has a negative predictive value exceeding 90% for ruling out advanced fibrosis [25, 26]. Patients with FIB‐4 score elevated above this threshold or sonographic features of cirrhosis (surface nodularity, splenomegaly, ascites) would be ineligible for ED‐initiated treatment and should be triaged to expedited specialist referral. FIB‐4 requires only AST/ALT and the patient's age which are already routinely obtained in ED evaluation. Formal USG is available in most US ED's, particularly those affiliated with hospitals where radiology services are staffed. In rural hospitals or freestanding EDs, imaging acquisition is typically performed locally with interpretation support through teleradiology. Together, these modalities represent an immediately implementable cirrhosis exclusion strategy. History of HCC is addressed through history taking and EHR review at the time of eligibility determination. Patients with known prior HCC would also be excluded and referred.

Soon, formal USG may be replaced with transient elastography. Palm‐sized wireless systems capable of B‐mode imaging and liver stiffness measurements have been developed, clinically validated [27], and have already crossed FDA regularly threshold as of February 2024. Scepticism toward this progress would be akin that once directed at POC ultrasound; a technology once doubted as feasible outside of radiology suites but is now a mandated Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education milestone competency in every academic emergency medicine training programme in the United States [28].

Active Case Management for Those Ineligible for Simplified Treatment

Simplified treatment ineligibility should not be conflated with therapeutic abandonment. Patients excluded from ED‐initiated DAA therapy whether due to HBsAg positivity, decompensated cirrhosis, unsuppressed HIV viremia, a history of HCC or complex drug interactions represent the highest risk individuals within the HCV‐infected population. For these patients, an appropriate response is a distinct, high‐priority active case management protocol, modelled on the infrastructure of the CDC's Perinatal Hepatitis B Prevention Program (PHBPP).

The PHBPP is a currently functioning nationally funded programme in which HBsAg positivity in the specific clinical context of pregnancy triggers active case management rather than passive surveillance. This entails proactive outreach, household contact tracing, needs assessment, care coordination and eliminates patient self‐navigating and follow‐up [29]. Spanning 2009 to 2017, the PHBPP case‐managed 100,372 infants born to HBsAg‐positive women across 56 jurisdictions, and over this time, increased timely administration of post‐exposure prophylaxis rates from 94.7% to 97%. Similar improvements were recorded for post‐vaccination serologic testing which increased from 58.8% to 66.8% (p = 0.002), ultimately declining perinatal transmission from 0.9% to 0.4% (p = 0.0006) [30]. However, the PHBPP does not map perfectly to the ED HCV context. The heterogeneity of ineligibility criteria, absence of a third party harm imperative (i.e., risk of harm to a neonate), and lack of a dedicated federal funding mechanism represent important structural differences. It does however establish a key principle. A clearly defined high risk clinical trigger can justify an active case management infrastructure rather than passive referral. Applying this principle to treatment‐ineligible ED HCV patients would ensure that exclusion from the simplified pathway consistently initiates proactive specialist referral and coordinated follow‐up, rather than discharge with instructions alone.

Policy & Systemic Barriers

A review of publicly available Medicaid HCV coverage policies across all 52 U.S. jurisdictions reveals a heterogeneous and frequently restrictive policy landscape (Table 1). As of 2025, 18 jurisdictions (35%) require prior authorisation for initial DAA therapy, including most states that have not expanded Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act. In these settings, 62% of non‐expansion jurisdictions maintain prior authorisation requirements compared with 24% of expansion states. Prescriber restrictions persist in 12 jurisdictions (23%), ranging from “specialist‐only” mandates to continuing medical education (CME) based credentialing requirements. Substance‐use related requirements remain in 6 jurisdictions (12%), now uniformly framed as counselling attestation rather than sobriety mandates. Taken together, at least one barrier directly relevant to DAA‐naive patients presenting to the ED is present in 24 jurisdictions (46%). Feasible ED‐based HCV treatment initiation will therefore require targeted policy interventions alongside clinical implementation. Although prior authorisation removal has been shown to increase treatment rates [32], abrupt universal rollback is a financially non‐viable strategy for most payers given anticipated surges in treatment demand.

A more durable approach would be advocacy for ED‐specific prior authorisation exemptions. A useful precedent is noted in the liberation of buprenorphine prescription requirements during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Prior to 2020, the Ryan Haight Online Pharmacy Consumer Protection Act of 2008 required an in‐person evaluation before prescribing controlled substances via telemedicine. This barrier is structurally akin to the specialist‐only prescribing requirement for DAAs. Federal regulators temporarily waived this requirement for buprenorphine initiation in defined telehealth settings while maintaining prescriber oversight. The impact was immediate. Within the Veterans Health Administration, a national interrupted time series analysis showed an increase of 265 patients receiving buprenorphine in the first month, with continued growth thereafter, even as overall substance use disorder care declined by 6% [33]. Importantly, safety did not worsen. In a propensity score–matched study of more than 23,000 adults, telehealth initiation was associated with a 36% lower risk of opioid overdose, along with better treatment engagement and refill rates, without increases in ED visits or hospitalisations [34]. Though these data are derived from commercially insured patients, the principle remains. Removing a categorical prescribing barrier in a defined clinical setting can expand access without compromising safety. A structured ED‐based protocol can justify exemption from requirements that were designed for routine outpatient care.

Financial Access Barriers and Mechanisms for Cost Mitigation

DAA therapies are now significantly more affordable than their initial entry into the HCV treatment armamentarium. When introduced in 2013, sofosbuvir was priced at $1000 per pill, totalling $84,000 for an 8 week course. Yet, meaningful cost barriers persist across insurance strata and are most acute precisely where HCV burden is highest. Patients with commercial insurance may have out‐of‐pocket costs as little as $5/month. However, patients relying on Medicare Part D historically faced average out‐of‐pocket costs approaching $5351 prior to recent legislative reforms, while uninsured individuals continue to encounter list prices for generic regimens ranging from $22,000 to $36,000 [35]. The Inflation Reduction Act sought to ease this burden by implementing a $2000 annual cap and closing the long‐problematic coverage gap, which left patients exposed to substantial coinsurance after initial benefits [36]. While these provisions are now in effect, recent policy shifts under the current administration introduce some uncertainty regarding the broader trajectory of federal drug pricing reform.

This magnitude of cost is not driven by the complexity of drug manufacturing. Generic price modelling that incorporates active pharmaceutical ingredient costs, excipients, and production in India at a 10% profit margin estimates that a full course of sofosbuvir/velpatasvir can be produced for approximately $85 [37]. In contrast, wholesale acquisition prices for preferred generic regimens have historically exceeded $20,000 per course, with no universal cost mitigation mechanism available outside insured and 340B‐eligible settings [38]. These realities illustrate that cost barriers to HCV elimination in the United States stem from modifiable regulatory and policy constraints.

One promising mechanism to offset DAA costs in the ED is the federal 340B drug pricing programme. If HCV treatment is prescribed and dispensed through an ED affiliated with a 340B‐eligible Disproportionate Share Hospital, medications can be acquired at steeply discounted prices. These savings may be passed on through sliding‐scale pricing or free dispensing to qualifying uninsured patients. Even in rural areas without owned outpatient facilities, 340B pricing can be accessed via contract pharmacy arrangements. While few existing bridge clinic models explicitly leverage 340B pricing, this presents an opportunity for innovation. EDs affiliated with 340B‐eligible hospitals could explore partnerships with nearby pharmacies to extend discounted DAA access beyond the ED encounter. Operationalising this model would require integrated electronic health record systems and dedicated care navigators.

The federal 340B Drug Pricing Program offers a potential solution, but its reach is limited by structure. Under 340B, covered entities can purchase outpatient medications at significantly reduced prices. These savings can be passed on to uninsured or underinsured patients, potentially reducing DAA costs. Eligibility is uneven however. Rural critical access hospitals, freestanding emergency centres, and many community hospitals do not qualify. These are often the same settings where uninsured patients are concentrated. Even within eligible institutions, passing savings to patients depends on local policy and is not guaranteed or mandated [39]. In addition, contract pharmacy arrangements, which have extended 340B pricing into community settings, face ongoing legal challenges following manufacturer restrictions introduced in 2020. This creates additional uncertainty, particularly for rural implementation strategies [40].

For institutions outside the 340B system, no reliable same‐encounter cost mitigation mechanism currently exists for uninsured patients. Manufacturer patient assistance programmes, including AbbVie's myAbbVie Assist, offer free medication to qualifying uninsured patients but require income documentation, provider attestation, and a processing timeline of days to weeks. Addressing this gap will require policy‐level solutions: expansion of 340B covered entity eligibility, federal bulk purchasing mechanisms, or Medicaid expansion in the 13 states where uninsured adults below poverty remain categorically ineligible.

At the systems level, subscription‐based procurement models implemented by Louisiana and Washington show that structural cost reform is achievable when payer infrastructure and political will align. Under these agreements, states pay a fixed annual amount in exchange for broad access to DAAs across Medicaid and incarcerated populations. A synthetic control analysis of Medicaid prescription data found that Louisiana experienced a 534% increase in HCV prescription fills following implementation [41]. Despite this, neither state reached its elimination targets, indicating that improving affordability alone is not sufficient. These findings highlight the need for both care delivery and financial access reform.

Operational Considerations

Beyond regulatory and financial prerequisites, feasibility depends on workflow integration within an already capacity constrained environment. Integrating HCV treatment into ED workflows adds modest physician workload if tasks are appropriately distributed. Serologic and radiographic tests run passively within the existing visit and physician involvement is limited to eligibility confirmation and prescription authorisation which are achievable in minutes with EHR‐embedded CDS. The downstream burden of patient counselling, insurance verification, prior authorisation navigation, and follow‐up coordination is best assigned to pharmacists and care navigators.

Phlebotomy access warrants specific attention in persons serving PWID where poor venous access can extend intake time and impede concurrent patient management [42]. Phlebotomy support and POC fingerstick compatible assays should be incorporated where available.

Staffing requirements for ED‐based HCV initiation have not been prospectively defined and will likely vary by institution. Estimates depend on expected RNA positivity rates, the proportion of patients who meet simplified treatment criteria, and the non‐physician workload required per patient. Data from outpatient programmes provide a useful reference point. The Boston Medical Center primary care HCV programme reported annual staffing costs of $282,770, corresponding to approximately $940 per patient referred and $4160 per SVR [43], with most of the workload carried by non‐physician staff. Across implementation studies, a consistent finding is that successful programmes require protected and dedicated time for pharmacists and care navigators.

Dispensing logistics will vary by institutional context. Large urban emergency departments affiliated with health systems that have on‐site pharmacies offer the most straightforward implementation pathway. In these settings, EHR‐based screening alerts, co‐located pharmacist or navigator support during clinical shifts, and in‐house dispensing can function as a fully integrated model. When an on‐site pharmacy is unavailable, partnerships with community pharmacies provide a practical alternative. Electronic prescription transmission with same‐day or next‐day dispensing can preserve the central goal of minimising the gap between diagnosis and treatment initiation. Rural emergency departments operate under different constraints, including lower patient volumes, limited specialist access, and smaller staffing models. A hub‐and‐spoke telehealth approach is a reasonable adaptation. Point‐of‐care testing and eligibility screening can be performed locally, while treatment evaluation and authorisation are completed through real‐time telemedicine consultation. Although same‐visit dispensing may not always be feasible in this setting, same‐visit eligibility determination with rapid electronic prescribing and pharmacy dispensing within 24–48 h still represents a meaningful improvement over current pathways that often result in no treatment.

Post‐Treatment Surveillance and Cure Confirmation

SVR12, defined as an undetectable HCV RNA 12 weeks after treatment completion, is the accepted endpoint of cure and the target for post‐treatment follow‐up. For patients started on therapy in the ED, reaching this endpoint depends on two steps. A confirmatory sample must be obtained, and the result must be delivered to a patient who may no longer be connected to traditional care.

Dried blood spot testing offers a practical solution for sample collection. A simple fingerstick applied to filter paper avoids venipuncture, does not require refrigeration, and remains stable for months [44].

This allows collection in settings where these patients already engage, including harm reduction sites, opioid treatment programmes, and needle exchange services. For patients with stable housing, mail‐in or community drop‐off options can eliminate the need for a return visit altogether. In real‐world settings, dried blood spot testing has shown high accuracy, with reported specificity of 100% and sensitivity approaching 96% [45]. There are limitations. Variability in sample volume, drying conditions, and processing can affect reliability at very low viral levels, where false negatives are the main concern [46, 47]. These issues can be reduced with standardised collection techniques and validated processing protocols. When feasible, venous sampling remains preferred, but dried blood spot testing is an appropriate alternative when it is not.

The second challenge is ensuring that results reach the patient and that non‐responders are identified. Telemedicine has shown benefit, with studies demonstrating improved SVR rates and engagement compared to standard referral pathways [48, 49]. However, its reach is limited in the same populations that rely on the ED. Access to smartphones, stable internet, and the ability to attend scheduled visits cannot be assumed. Simpler approaches may be more reliable. Asynchronous text messaging programmes have been associated with improved adherence and reduced treatment‐related distress in VA‐based HCV programmes [50]. For patients without dependable digital access, in‐person follow‐up through harm reduction or opioid treatment programmes remains essential.

Reinfection should be anticipated in populations with ongoing injection drug use. SVR does not confer immunity, and recurrence is not uncommon. This does not represent treatment failure. Reinfection can be managed with the same DAA regimens, with preserved efficacy. The appropriate response is retreatment alongside renewed engagement in harm reduction services. Follow‐up encounters for SVR confirmation provide an opportunity to reassess risk, reinforce counselling, and link patients to treatment for opioid use disorder when appropriate. No single follow‐up strategy will reach all patients. Effective post‐treatment care requires flexibility and selection of the approach that best fits the individual.

Conclusion

This framework represents a deliberate shift in how the HCV care cascade is approached. Rather than relying on delayed linkage to outpatient care, it begins at the point of ED contact. Point‐of‐care RNA testing, simplified eligibility assessment, same‐visit treatment initiation, decentralised SVR confirmation, and flexible follow‐up together form a model grounded in the realities of the populations most affected by hepatitis C, particularly those with inconsistent access to traditional care.

The barriers are real. Regulatory requirements vary across states. Financial access remains uneven and, in some settings, legally contested. Staffing these programmes requires institutional investment that cannot be secured through a single policy change. These constraints do not undermine the model. They define the areas where advocacy, pilot implementation, and prospective evaluation should be focused.

Hepatitis C is curable. The tools to diagnose infection within an hour, treat it in weeks, and confirm cure without a clinic visit already exist. What remains is a delivery system that continues to lose patients at each step. The downstream consequences of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and their socioeconomic burden rest like an anvil on the healthcare system. Addressing this will require more than incremental change. It will require rethinking where and how care is delivered. The emergency department is not traditionally designed for chronic disease management, but for a condition defined by silent progression and concentrated among patients who rely on the ED for care, it may be the most consequential place to intervene.
