Stoichiometry modulated tunable optoelectronic properties of Nb-doped WS2 synthesized by chemical vapor deposition method
Authors: Caiyun Wang, Ziyue Liu, Gao Xiong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant progress has been made in the field of two-dimensional WS2, yet the precise control of the doping process to achieve desired properties and the interpretation of complex physical phenomena in these novel materials necessitate thorough research. In this study, Nb-doped WS2 is synthesized using chemical vapor deposition technology, leading to triangular flakes with a side length of 12–20 μm. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis indicates that the NbW substitution defect functions as an electron acceptor. The interlayer and transverse forces of the Nb-doped WS2 flake approach 1 nN and 150 pN, respectively. As the doping concentration increases, the valence band maximum energy of Nb-doped WS2 ranges from 4.06 eV to 4.3 eV. Furthermore, the performance of the photodetector is discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72999-4.Subject terms: Two-dimensional materials, Mechanical properties, Superconducting devices

Introduction

The rapid advancement of science and technology has led to the emergence of significant application potential for two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides (2D TMDs) in various fields such as energy, environmental monitoring, and optoelectronics1–3. Currently, there is a growing interest in manipulating the electronic structure, optical properties, and catalytic properties of WS2 monolayers and its complexes through doping with different elements4.

The introduction of Cu through radio frequency cosputtering resulted in the amorphous phase of Cu within the WS2 matrix, improving wear resistance and extended service life of the WS2 films. In 2016, research was performed on the electronic and magnetic properties of Au-doped WS2 monolayer under varying levels of strain, ranging from − 10% to10%5. The findings indicated that strain had a significant impact on the magnetic moment of the material. In the same year, Shogo et al.6 conducted a study on the photoluminescence (PL) properties of Nb-doped WS2 monolayer at varying temperatures. Their findings indicated that the Nb-doped WS2 monolayer exhibited a lower PL peak energy at room temperature.

To obtain cost-effective 2D TMDs flakes with a large scale, synthesis methods are continuously evolving. In 2019, Mahider et al.7 introduced a method using hydrothermal and thermal injection techniques to synthesize WS2 nanoflowers and CdSe quantum dots. A study has revealed that impurity doping is an effective way to achieve the optical response of semiconductor subgap8, and demonstrated that the neutral impurity center plays a major role in the observed emission. Another study investigated N-doped WS2 monolayers at low temperatures, emission lines in PL spectra and G-factors of spin-splitting subbands in conduction and valence bands are identified9. The double-layer V-WS2 under different doping concentrations were synthesized using liquid-phase assisted precursor chemical vapor deposition (CVD)10. Nb doping of WS2 can alter its crystal structure and electronic properties, benefiting microelectronics, nanoelectronics, and optoelectronics11,12. NbW substitution concentration reaches 1014 cm −2.

Specifically, researchers have implemented an electrolyte-gated WS2 single-layer field-effect structure and observed a notable red shift in the platform under electron or hole doping. This effect is particularly pronounced, exceeding the established exciton manifolds and optical and transport evaluations of degenerate doped atoms in thin quantum materials.

Density functional theory (DFT) has been applied to explain the origin or mechanism of element doping effect on WS213. Vipin et al.14 employed DFT to investigate the electronic, dielectric, and optical characteristics of pristine and Nb/V-doped monolayer WS2. Their findings indicate that doping leads to a modest decrease in the band gap and a transition from n-type to p-type behavior in the pure WS2 monolayer. The introduction of Nb doping in WS2 has been shown to induce p-type electron acceptor by experiment15. A separate study on WS2 crystals demonstrated that the concentration of Nb increases proportionally with molar concentration of Nb in precursor solution, leading to the preparation of a bipolar field-effect transistor12. Additionally, p-type Nb-doped WS2 was synthesized16.

Despite a lot of progress in doping WS2, some challenges remain in controlling the doping process for energy band properties and understanding the complex physical phenomena of these materials. This study focuses on synthesizing Nb-doped WS2 with tunable doping concentrations. The polarized Raman spectrum is used to analyze coordination preferences in Nb-doped WS2, while force curve measurement was employed to evaluate the structure stability.

Results and discussions

As depicted in Fig. 1a, the Nb-doped WS2 flake exhibits a triangular morphology with dimensions ranging from 12 to 20 μm. It is evident that new nucleation occurs along the edges of the flake, indicating that these regions possess a higher chemical reactivity and Gibbs free energy compared to the central distribution. Under unchanged growth conditions, the Nb-doped WS2 material cannot be successfully synthesized. Only by increasing the reaction temperature to 840 oC can the high-Nb-doping WS2 be obtained.

The morphology of Nb-doped WS2 with doping concentration of 4% (a) and 28% (b). (c) AFM image, (d) Force curve, and (e) The polarized Raman spectra of Nb-doped WS2.

Through a large number of experiments, we found that it is difficult to synthesize Nb-doped WS2 with the constant surface roughness using the similar reaction temperature and Ar flux. The WS2 flake from mass ratio of 28% can be obtained only by increasing the reaction temperature to 840 °C, which is 820 °C for other three mass ratio. Furthermore, it is apparent from Fig. 1b that as the doping concentration increases, the triangular WS2 flake displays higher roughness.

The thickness of Nb-doped WS2 was determined through atomic force microscopy, revealing a surface ridge-like morphology with potential applications in bionic devices. The three-dimensional image (Fig. 1c) displays a thickness curve of the flake, indicating layer numbers of 4 at the edge and 1 at the center, with respective thicknesses of 26.2 and 8.8 nm.

The force profile of the Nb-doped WS2 flake, including interlayer and transverse forces, is depicted in Fig. 1d, with interlayer force approaching 1 nN and transverse force close to 150 pN (Supplementary Fig. S1 , S2). The radial force is less than the transverse force, which means that the Nb-doped WS2 has lower stiffness in the radial direction. The radial modulus, referring the ability of the material to resist deformation in the direction perpendicular to the axis line, values of 17,000,000, 20,000,000, 21,000,000 and 19,000,000 kA2/m along a line from one edge to another (Supplementary Fig. S3) indicate that the interior of the WS2 flake is less susceptible to deformation compared to the edges when subjected to radial forces. The Raman spectrum presented in Fig. 1e demonstrates that the peak position of the Raman spectra remains relatively unchanged with increasing doping concentration, suggesting that the structure of WS2 remains consistent following Nb doping.

The polarized Raman spectra of Nb-doped WS2 were measured at various angles (0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, and 90°) and compared to analyze the differences in Raman peaks (Fig. 1e). Eight distinct Raman peaks were observed at each angle, corresponding to Raman shifts of 172, 296, 321, 350, 419, 520, 584, and 697 cm−1. Specifically, the peaks at 297, 350, and 419 cm−1 are attributed to the in-plane vibration modes E1g and E2g, as well as the out-of-plane vibration mode A1g of WS2 flakes, respectively17. The Raman peaks observed at 172, 296, 321, 584, and 697 cm−1 are attributed to the LA, 2ZA, LA + 2ZA, A´´+LA, and 4LA modes18, respectively, within a margin of error of 4 cm−1.

The presence of the second-order mode suggests the synthesis of few-layer WS2, as corroborated by AFM analysis results. The vibration mode demonstrates the regulatory impact of polarization on molecular group vibration in relation to the preferred orientation of incident light angle. As the polarization angle of the incident light increases, the E2g mode improves the most sharply. The vibration of A1g reduced first and then increased (Table S1). This indicates that as the polarization angle increases, the in-plane vibration increases dramatically, while the out-of-plane vibration decreases at first and then increases. Researchers have developed a valley photonic router capable of selectively distributing energy valley information in a directional manner. By adjusting the circular polarization of the incident light, it is possible to selectively excite K or K’ valley excitons for the purpose of achieving directional distribution of valley photons.

Figure 2a exhibits the whole scanning XPS spectrum of Nb-doped WS2, which verifies the characteristics spectra of S, W, and Nb, as seen in the XPS query report card. The signal peak with binding energy of 153.19, 284.8, 528.6, and 979.76 eV are detected, which are attributed to S 2p, C 1s, O 1s, and O kL, respectively. In Fig. 2b, the peaks at 34.4 and 36.26 eV are due to the doublet W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2, respectively. The W 4f5/2 peak at 37.6 eV is from WO3, which is occasionally deposited on the surface of WS2. In Fig. 2c, the peaks at 167.63 eV originates from the S 3p core level. Significantly, the peaks at 192.14 eV are attributed to the Nb 3d binding energy, as indicated in Fig. 2d. The XPS spectra illustrate that the Nb doping in WS2 flake is achieved. The Nb atom entering WS2 lattice is deduced to be substitutional impurity (NbW), i.e., Nb atom substituting the atomic positon of W atom. The valence energy level of Nb-doped WS2 is fitted to be 3.82 eV, as shown in Fig. 2e.

The XPS spectra of Nb-doped WS2. (a) The XPS full scanning spectrum. (b) XPS spectrum of the signal from W 4f core level. (c) XPS spectrum of the signal from S 3p core level. (d) XPS spectrum of signals from Nb 3d core levels. (e) The valence band spectrum of Nb-doped WS2.

The enhancement of Nb doping concentration results in a reduction in the visibility of the S 2p peak (Fig. 3a), an increase in the prominence of the W 4f peak (Fig. 3b), and an increase in the prominence of the Nb 3d peak (Fig. 3c). These findings provide evidence for the synthesis of Nb-doped WS2. When the doping concentration is below 28%, there is no alteration in the binding energy of S 2p and W 4f electrons, attributed to the similar atomic radii of W (1.41 Å) and Nb (1.45 Å), and the absence of significant lattice distortion induced by substitutional impurities. As the doping concentration increases, the valence band maximum energy of Nb-doped WS2 ranges from 4.06 to 4.3 eV (Fig. 3d), indicating that Nb doping increases hole concentration. It is hypothesized that the NbW substitution defect functions as an electron acceptor.

The XPS spectra comparison with different doping concentration of 2, 4, and 28%. (a) XPS spectra of the signal from S 2p core level. (b) XPS spectra of the signal from W 4f core level. (c) XPS spectra of signals from Nb 3d core levels. (d) Valence band spectra of Nb-doped WS2.

In order to examine the energy levels and luminescence mechanism of Nb-doped WS2, the PL spectra were analyzed at three different positions on a flake. The PL peak of Nb-doped WS2 was observed at 618 nm at room temperature, indicating an energy band gap of 2.01 eV and confirming the preparation of monolayer WS2. The PL intensity maximum was found to increase from the center to the edge of the flake, as illustrated in Fig. 4a.

(a) The PL spectra comparison of the positon from edge to center on a Nb-doped WS2 flake. (b) The PL spectra comparison of Nb-doped WS2 about doping concentration. (c) Infrared-visible light absorption and reflection spectrum of Nb-doped WS2.

As the doping concentration increases, the PL peak exhibits center wavelengths of 629, 618, 623, and 628 nm, corresponding to the doping concentration of 2, 4, 8, and 28%. Concurrently, the PL intensity initially decreases and subsequently increases at a doping concentration of 28% (Fig. 4b). The variation in PL peak wavelengths indicates a change in the number of layers of Nb-doped WS2, varying from 1 to 4 layers. The presence of NbW as a nonradiative recombination center results in energy loss through nonradiative processes, precluding photon emission. The substitution of a Nb atom for a W atom in the Nb-doped WS2 lattice results in the loss of three electrons, resulting in the formation of Nb3+ ions. The WS2 lattice gains a free electron as a result. Consequently, Nb doping in WS2 induces p-type doping, aligning with the findings presented in the XPS analysis section.

The comparison of the infrared and visible light absorption and reflection spectra of Nb-doped WS2 with respect to Nb doping concentration (Fig. 4c) reveals no significant variations. Analysis of the light absorption spectra allows for deduction of the energy band, which aligns with previous findings in valence spectra and PL spectra.

The formation of a p-n junction in Nb-doped WS2 flake through electricity injection is depicted in Fig. 5a. The photodetector properties were subsequently measured, revealing that the current of the photodetector under 532 nm laser radiation is 1.4% greater than under white light radiation, as illustrated in Fig. 5b. Additionally, the photocurrent of Nb-doped WS2 with 8% of doping concentration exceeds that of 4% (Supplementary Fig. S4). This observation indicates that an increase in Nb doping is advantageous for enhancing the generation of photoelectrons.

Conclusion

The Nb-doped WS2 was synthesized using molten-salt-assisted CVD technology, resulting in a triangular morphology with a size of 12 μm and ridge-like features. AFM measurements revealed that the interior of the Nb-doped WS2 is more resistant to deformation compared to the edges when subjected to radial force. The presence of Nb impurities induces p-type doping in the WS2 flake, as evidenced by a dominant PL peak wavelength of 616 nm. Further research may explore the potential of Nb-doped WS2 flakes with strong radial modulus for applications in targeted drug packaging. The preferential structure variation is observed based on the polarized angle of the excitation light, suggesting its potential for electronic communication in energy valleys.

Methods

Synthesis of Nb-doped WS2. The raw materials Nb2O3 (Alfa Aesar 99.9%), WO3 powder (Alfa Aesar 99.9%), and sulfur powder (Alfa Aesar 99.9%) were purchased and utilized without additional processing. Subsequently, the WO3 powder was combined with NaCl (Alfa Aesar 99.9%) in a mass ratio of 5:1. Following this, Nb2O3 and the aforementioned mixture were blended in varying mass ratios of 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 28%.

Synthesis of Nb-doped WS2. The raw materials Nb2O3 (Alfa Aesar 99.9%), WO3 powder (Alfa Aesar 99.9%), and sulfur powder (Alfa Aesar 99.9%) were purchased and utilized without additional processing. Subsequently, the WO3 powder was combined with NaCl (Alfa Aesar 99.9%) in a mass ratio of 5:1. Following this, Nb2O3 and the aforementioned mixture were blended in varying mass ratios of 0%, 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 28%.

The Nb-doped WS2 was deposited on a SiO2/p-Si substrate with a 300 nm-thick oxide layer by CVD technology. The two-temperature-zone tube (Hefei Kejing Materials Co., Ltd) furnace was utilized. The SiO2/p-Si substrates were pre-cleaned in acetone, absolute ethyl alcohol and deionized water within an ultrasonic cleaner in turn. Zone I was used with 100 mg Se powder and zone II was used with precursor mixture and substrates. The SiO2/p-Si substrates were on top of WO3 with a space distance of 0.3 cm. The CVD furnace underwent three initial purges, with one purge process involving pumping the furnace to 5.6 × 10−1 mbar and then charging it to atmospheric pressure with Ar. Subsequently, the WO3 was heated at a rate of 15 oC/min before the Se was heated. Only a specific temperature zone was heated, with heat transferring from zone II to zone II. The reaction temperature of WO3 was maintained at 820 oC, with Ar serving as the carrier gas at a flux of 300 sccm. The WO3 mixture and S powder reached their fusion points simultaneously, and the reaction was sustained for 15 min. Following the reaction, the furnace naturally cooled to room temperature.

Morphology and PL characterization. In the measurement of polarized Raman spectra, the sample is radiated by incident light with wavelength of 532 nm. The polarized angle of incident light was controlled by change the direction of aperture. The morphology of the Nb-doped WS2 flake was examined through Scanning Electron Microscopy (Zeiss Gemini 300). AFM image and force curve of Nb-doped WS2 were characterized by atomic force microscopy (Bruker Dimension Icon). Then the force profile was acquired by analysis in software “Nanoscope”. The X-ray photoelectron energy spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific Nexsa) is measured. In contrast, the PL spectrum was monitored using Combined- Raman and fluorescence equipment (Thermo fisher Scientific DXR 2Xi) with excitation wavelengths of 532 nm. The ultraviolet-visible absorption spectrum of the material was measured by spectrophotometer.

Fabrication of Nb-doped WS2-based photodetector. Initially, a fork finger electrode mask plate was fabricated on PP plastic. Subsequently, the mask plate was affixed to the SiO2/Si substrate. Following this, a 50 nm thick Cr electrode was deposited onto the SiO2/Si substrate via sputtering. The WS2 flake was then transferred onto the electrode using a PMMA solution, with a mass ratio of 4% PMMA. During the transfer process, the PMMA was uniformly coated onto the WS2/SiO2/Si substrate at rotation speeds of 3000 r/min and 500 r/min for high and low speeds, respectively. The WS2/SiO2/Si substrate was then put on a hot plate at 50 oC for 15 min. Subsequently, the PMMA/WS2/SiO2/Si substrate was immersed in a NaOH solution with an atomic ratio of 2 mol/L. Next, the PMMA/WS2 flake was dispersed in a solution and subsequently transferred to deionized water to eliminate any remaining impurities and contaminants. The flake was subsequently deposited onto a chromium electrode and dried on a hot plate at 70 oC. Subsequently, the PMMA layer on the PMMA/WS2/Cr structure was removed by immersion in acetone at 70 oC for a duration of 3 h. The electrodes were later connected using copper wire and conductive silver adhesives. The WS2 photodetector was operated at a voltage of 2 V, and the current of the photodetector was measured using a low-power analyzer while the photodetector was irradiated with the excitation wavelength of 532 nm.
