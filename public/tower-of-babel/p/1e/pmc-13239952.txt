Severe Immune‐Related Neurological Adverse Events Associated With Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Treatment: A Retrospective Single‐Center Study
Authors: Anna Grisold, Angelika M. Starzer, Sara Silvaieh, Martin Krenn, Marie‐Bernadette Aretin, Manuela Schmidinger, Julia M. Ressler, Stephan Polterauer, Thomas Berger, Matthias Preusser, Anna S. Berghoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of various malignancies. The resulting activation of the immune system may lead to neurological immune‐related adverse events (n‐irAEs). Here, we retrospectively characterized severe n‐irAEs in a monocentric cohort.MethodsWe screened an in‐house registry of the Medical University of Vienna, Austria, including all patients treated with ICIs between 2007 and 2023. Patients who underwent a neurological evaluation were identified, and their medical records were reviewed. Cases with severe (i.e., grade ≥ 3) n‐irAEs were analyzed. We report clinical and demographic characteristics, neurological manifestations, treatment and outcomes.ResultsTen out of 2043 screened patients (i.e., 0.5%) developed severe n‐irAEs. In most cases (n = 7), neurological manifestations involved the neuromuscular system and/or peripheral nerves, including myasthenia/myositis (n = 5), polyneuropathy (n = 1), and facial nerve palsy (n = 1). Central nervous system involvement included aseptic meningitis, myelitis, and stroke (each n = 1). The number of ICI cycles prior to n‐irAE onset varied, with a median of 3 cycles (range 1–43). The median time from the last infusion to symptom onset was 7 days (range 2–24). n‐irAEs led to ICI discontinuation and/or immunomodulatory treatments in 9 patients. Clinical improvement was observed in 7 of 10 cases. No deaths were attributed to ICI‐related toxicity.ConclusionOur findings indicate that severe n‐irAEs are rare. Nonetheless, clinicians should remain vigilant for delayed‐onset neurotoxicity, even after long treatment exposure. Most patients showed clinical improvement following ICI discontinuation and/or immunomodulatory treatments.

Introduction

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has revolutionized the therapeutic landscape in oncology, enabling effective and sustained treatment responses across a wide range of malignancies. ICIs enhance antitumor immunity by blocking inhibitory immune pathways, including cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte–associated antigen 4 (CTLA‐4), as well as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1) and its ligand PD‐L1 [1]. While these treatments have substantially improved cancer outcomes, the resulting immune activation can lead to serious immune‐related adverse events (irAEs) affecting different organ systems [2].

Among these, neurological irAEs (n‐irAEs) are rare but potentially severe and may be accompanied by increased morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. These complications pose both diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, requiring interdisciplinary collaboration between oncologists, neurologists, and other specialists. Previous efforts to address this clinical need have led to the development of consensus‐based management guidelines [5].

Across different datasets, n‐irAEs of all severity grades have been reported in up to 5% of cases, with combination regimens and anti‐PD1 treatment showing higher rates than anti‐CTLA‐4 inhibitors [3, 6]. However, most n‐irAEs are mild, including headache or dizziness, while the rate of high‐grade (i.e., ≥ 3) n‐irAEs remains below 1% across all ICI agents [7]. Complications affecting the neuromuscular and peripheral nervous system (PNS) occur more frequently than those affecting the central nervous system (CNS) [8]. Overlap syndromes, such as myasthenia with concurrent myositis and myocarditis, pose a particular clinical challenge, as they are associated with higher mortality [9].

By definition, neurological symptoms must occur within 12 months following the last ICI infusion to qualify as n‐irAE, although manifestations between 6 and 12 months after treatment are rare [10]. Most n‐irAE are characterized by a monophasic clinical course [11], however, in up to 50% of cases neurological sequelae or long‐term complications may persist [12, 13, 14].

From a clinical perspective, early recognition of neurological treatment complications is crucial. Accordingly, new‐onset neurological symptoms under ICI treatment should prompt a diagnostic work‐up to rule out tumor progression or infectious causes. Once neurological symptoms are attributed to ICI treatment, management usually includes ICI discontinuation (for n‐irAE grade ≥ 2), treatment with steroids, intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIg), or plasma exchange, while other immunosuppressants may be used in refractory cases [15].

While severe n‐irAEs have been reported, detailed clinical characterizations in real‐world settings remain limited. Most available evidence originates from large pharmacovigilance databases [16, 17, 18], where clinically relevant high‐grade events are often insufficiently phenotyped. In this study, we focus on severe n‐irAEs within a large single‐center cohort to provide a real‐world characterization to improve awareness and management of these serious complications.

Patients and Methods

Patient Selection

In this retrospective cohort study, we screened our in‐house registry of all patients who received ICI treatment (n = 2043), e.g., targeting PD‐1 (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab), PD‐L1 (atezolizumab), or CTLA‐4 (ipilimumab), for various oncological indications between February 2007 and February 2023. These patients were seen at different departments at the Medical University of Vienna (including the Departments of Oncology, Urology, Dermatology, Gynecology and Neurology). We specifically reviewed the medical records of all inpatients and outpatients who underwent neurological evaluation or consultation.

Neurotoxicity Assessment

All individuals meeting the above criteria were systematically assessed for the presence of ICI‐related neurotoxicity. For this purpose, a comprehensive chart review was conducted and detailed information was extracted on patient demographics, medical history, ICI treatment characteristics, clinical presentation, time course, management, and outcome of n‐irAEs.

Immune‐related neurotoxicity was defined as a neurological syndrome of suspected autoimmune etiology occurring during or within 12 months after ICI therapy. Neurological symptoms clearly attributable to tumor progression or to treatments other than ICIs were excluded from the analysis. Neurotoxic events were retrospectively graded based on clinical chart review, according to the National Cancer Institute's Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [19]. This grading scale ranges from 1 to 5, with grades 1 and 2 classified as low‐grade, and grades 3 and 4 as high‐grade neurotoxicity. Grade 5 denotes death directly resulting from the adverse neurological event. As low‐grade neurotoxicity often does not require neurological consultation, our analysis was restricted to high‐grade n‐irAEs (i.e., grade ≥ 3). Moreover, cases of ICI‐related hypophysitis were excluded from the analysis, as this condition was classified as an endocrine rather than a neurological adverse event.

Statistical Analysis

Due to the small number of high‐grade n‐irAEs in our cohort, statistical analyses were limited to descriptive methods. The frequency of severe n‐irAEs was calculated relative to the total ICI‐treated population. Patient characteristics, treatment regimens, and clinical features of affected individuals were summarized using counts, percentages, and medians with total ranges, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Mac, version 29.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

As illustrated in Figure 1, of the 2043 cases treated with ICIs between February 2007 and February 2023, 419 (20.5%) underwent clinical and/or paraclinical neurological assessment; their medical charts were therefore reviewed manually for evidence of ICI‐related neurotoxicity. Of these, 409 cases were excluded due to alternative etiologies, including tumor‐related causes (n = 208), septic, metabolic, or toxic conditions (n = 39), unrelated neurological diseases (n = 29), cerebrovascular events (n = 28), non‐ICI treatment‐related causes (n = 19), CNS infections (n = 4), and unspecific or unknown etiologies (n = 82). After careful evaluation, 10 cases (0.5% of the entire cohort) were identified as having experienced a high‐grade (≥ grade 3) ICI‐related neurotoxic event. Four of these patients were female and 6 were male. The median age at the time of the adverse event was 69 years (total range 34–85).

Underlying cancer diagnoses included renal cell carcinoma and non–small cell lung cancer (each n = 3), as well as squamous cell carcinoma (n = 2), melanoma and choriocarcinoma (each n = 1). All patients had metastatic disease, and brain metastases were present in 2 of 10 cases (20%). None of the patients with n‐irAEs had a prior history of autoimmune neurological disease.

The vast majority of patients were treated with PD‐1 inhibitors, either as monotherapy (n = 8) or in combination with CTLA‐4 inhibition (n = 1). Only one patient with n‐irAEs received PD‐L1 inhibitor therapy, and none received CTLA‐4 inhibitor monotherapy. The number of ICI cycles prior to n‐irAE onset varied widely, with a median of 3 cycles (total range 1–43). The median time from the last ICI infusion to symptom onset was 7 days (total range 2–24).

Severe Neurotoxic Events

In our cohort, all patients were referred to neurological assessments due to newly developed neurological symptoms under ICI therapy. 60% of the patients with n‐irAEs experienced grade 3 events (n = 6), while the remaining were classified as grade 4 (n = 4). Neurotoxicity predominantly involved the neuromuscular system or the peripheral nervous system (PNS) (7 of 10 cases, 70%), whereas the central nervous system (CNS) was primarily affected in 3 patients (30%). The most frequently observed ICI‐related neurological manifestation was myositis, with or without concomitant evidence of neuromuscular junction (NMJ) dysfunction (i.e., myasthenic syndrome). One case had a diagnosis of dermatomyositis.

Furthermore, ICI‐related neuropathies were diagnosed in 2 cases, including one patient with sensorimotor polyneuropathy and one with a cranial neuropathy, manifesting as facial nerve palsy. The 3 CNS‐related diagnoses comprised one case each of myelitis, aseptic meningitis, and combined ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke.

Clinical and Diagnostic Findings

Clinical presentation was heterogeneous but predominantly characterized by generalized muscle weakness (6/10). Ocular symptoms (including ptosis and/or diplopia) were observed in 2/10 patients. Sensory disturbances (paresthesia) occurred in 2/10 cases. Additional neurological findings included hyporeflexia (polyneuropathy), paraparesis (myelitis), facial weakness (facial nerve palsy), dysarthria, and blurred vision (stroke), each of which was observed in 1/10 cases. Pain (muscle or back) was reported in 3/10 patients, and headache was present in 1/10.

Myositis and myasthenia cases (including overlap syndromes) were characterized by marked hyperCKemia (up to > 11× upper limit of normal). Serological testing identified disease‐related antibodies (Abs) in 3 cases, with 2 patients having acetylcholine receptor (AChR)‐Abs and 1 case showing weak anti‐Mi‐2‐Ab positivity.

Electrophysiological assessments included nerve conduction studies in the polyneuropathy case which showed sensorimotor involvement, and the case with isolated facial palsy demonstrated axonal damage of the affected nerve. Cases with myositis and myasthenia had abnormal findings on electromyography and/or repetitive nerve stimulation. Dermatological signs such as Gottron's papules and a heliotrope rash were documented in the dermatomyositis case.

CSF analysis was conducted in 4 cases (polyneuropathy, myelitis, aseptic meningitis, stroke) and showed variable degrees of pleocytosis and/or protein elevation in all of them. Brain MRI was only performed in the 3 cases with CNS involvement, with abnormal findings detected in 2 of them (revealing strokes and myelitis), while brain MRI was normal in the patient diagnosed with aseptic meningitis.

Overall, laboratory, electrophysiological, CSF, and neuroimaging findings supported an immune‐mediated pathogenesis across reported n‐irAE cases.

Additional (non‐neurological) complications likely attributable to ICI treatment were identified in 4 cases, including enteritis, hypophysitis, and pneumonitis. Myocardial involvement was suspected in 3 patients with myositis and/or myasthenic syndromes; however, all 3 also had significant coronary artery disease (CAD), complicating the differential diagnosis. As a secondary complication, severe hyperCKemia led to acute kidney injury due to rhabdomyolysis in 1 case.

Management and Outcome

In our cohort, n‐irAE led to ICI discontinuation and initiation of immunomodulatory treatment in 9 patients. The median time from symptom onset to treatment initiation was 7 days (total range 3–17). Immunomodulatory treatment predominantly consisted of corticosteroids, which were administered in 9 of 10 patients, with 5 receiving oral prednisolone and 4 receiving intravenous methylprednisolone. Additional treatments in steroid‐refractory cases included IVIg in 3 patients and plasma exchange in 1 patient. Symptomatic treatment with pyridostigmine was used in 2 patients with NMJ involvement. One patient with stroke did not receive immunomodulatory therapy, as symptoms resolved spontaneously and were only later attributed to ICI treatment following an unremarkable stroke work‐up.

Clinical improvement was observed in 7 cases, while none of the 10 cases deceased due to n‐irAEs. The median time from n‐irAE onset to the last follow‐up visit was 20 months (total range 0.6–74). In our cohort, no ICI rechallenge was reported following the occurrence of grade ≥ 3 n‐irAEs.

Discussion

Compared with traditional chemotherapies, ICIs act through immune modulation and are associated with a broad spectrum of complications affecting multiple organ systems, including the nervous system [20]. As reports on n‐irAEs continue to expand, our study contributes real‐world evidence from a large hospital‐based registry comprising more than 2000 ICI‐treated patients, with a focus on clinically relevant high‐grade events.

The incidence of severe n‐irAEs in our cohort was 0.5%, which, although at the lower end of the reported range, is comparable to findings from similar studies [21, 22]. 70% of cases in our cohort primarily involved the neuromuscular system or the PNS, with the majority affecting the NMJ and/or the skeletal muscle, as described previously [23]. Among these entities, overlap syndromes such as the so‐called triple M syndrome, characterized by the concomitant involvement of skeletal muscle (myositis), the NMJ (myasthenia), and the heart (myocarditis), are of particular clinical relevance as they are associated with increased mortality [24]. With regard to myasthenic symptoms, published series report a predominance of moderate‐to‐severe clinical presentations (MGFA class III–V), suggesting that clinically overt n‐irAEs typically manifest at CTCAE grade ≥ 3, while only a minority of cases (12%) presented with isolated ocular weakness [25].

In 3 of our reported neuromuscular cases myocarditis was suspected based on elevated cardiac biomarkers; however, significant coronary artery stenoses were detected in all of them, rendering the differential diagnosis challenging. Notably, previous studies have reported that more than 20% of patients with ICI‐related myocarditis have concomitant CAD [26].

Moreover, Abs were detected in 3 of the neuromuscular cases, including 2 with Abs against the AChR and one with Abs against Mi‐2. While Abs in n‐irAE cases have been described in the literature [27], to our knowledge, this represents the first reported case associated with anti‐Mi‐2‐Abs.

The only case of cranial neuropathy in our cohort presented as facial nerve palsy, which has been reported as the most commonly affected cranial nerve in ICI‐related neuropathies [28]. In contrast, CNS events were less frequent and comprised aseptic meningitis, myelitis, and stroke. While aseptic meningitis [29] and myelitis [30] are well‐established n‐irAEs, strokes may occur as part of a broader spectrum of diverse thromboembolic manifestations, once alternative etiologies have been ruled out [31]. Notably, the attribution of cerebrovascular events to ICI treatment remains particularly challenging. Patients with cancer often have multiple vascular risk factors and prothrombotic conditions, which makes the assessment of causality complex and prone to misclassification, especially in retrospective analyses. Therefore, we attributed cerebrovascular events to ICI treatment only if other etiologies had been ruled out by extensive diagnostic evaluation. In the present stroke case, an otherwise negative comprehensive stroke work‐up in temporal association with ICI exposure raises suspicion for an immune‐mediated process. While tumor‐associated hypercoagulability cannot be fully excluded, the absence of an identifiable alternative mechanism makes an ICI‐associated inflammatory pathomechanism the most likely explanation.

It is also worthy of note that published cohorts differ in terms of phenotype distribution. One study has reported a comparably high number of CNS complications, particularly encephalitis with or without neuronal Abs [32]. A potential explanation for this observation is referral bias, as tertiary centers with a focus on autoimmune encephalitis and Ab diagnostics may capture a higher number of such cases. Such differences suggest that institutional expertise can influence the phenotype spectrum of n‐irAEs in studies. However, our analysis was based on a hospital‐wide registry and routine neurological assessments and may therefore be less prone to this kind of referral bias.

Interestingly, our results also indicate a wide range of intervals between initiation of ICI therapy and the onset of n‐irAE symptoms. While most events occurred after the administration of only a few treatment cycles, we also observed late‐onset toxicities in a few cases, with symptom onset up to 43 cycles after treatment initiation. In concordance with recent evidence, these findings underscore the need for continued clinical vigilance regarding n‐irAEs, even during long‐term ICI therapy [33].

Regarding clinical management, 9 out of the 10 patients in our cohort had their ICI therapy discontinued, and immunomodulatory therapy was initiated in most cases, reflecting the current recommendations in guidelines [5]. Treatment comprised corticosteroids in the vast majority of subjects, followed by IVIg and plasma exchange. Symptomatic treatment with pyridostigmine for myasthenic syndromes was administered in specific cases.

Our study has some limitations. First, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of selection and ascertainment bias. We focused on CTCAE grade 3–5 events, as low‐grade symptoms are often underreported and inconsistently documented in routine clinical care. For instance, mild neurological symptoms such as headache or dizziness were not systematically investigated as part of this study and are typically documented as unspecific complaints in medical records. Consequently, our analysis does not capture the full spectrum of low‐grade n‐irAEs, and thus the overall neurological burden associated with ICIs is likely underestimated. Moreover, certain neurotoxicities may be misclassified in retrospective chart review. Establishing a causal relationship between ICI exposure and neurological events is difficult and may result in both over‐ and under‐attribution. Further, the follow‐up period was limited for some patients, which makes the assessment of late‐onset n‐irAEs and long‐term sequelae difficult. Finally, the relatively small sample size of n‐irAE cases limits statistical analyses such as the evaluation of risk factors and associations between ICI classes, phenotypes, and outcomes.

In conclusion, severe n‐irAEs are exceedingly rare but serious complications of ICI therapy, encompassing a wide spectrum of neurological manifestations. Clinicians should remain vigilant for neurotoxicity, particularly in neuromuscular conditions and overlap syndromes. Given the increasing use of ICIs, prospective multicenter studies and registries are needed to improve management strategies and outcomes. Such studies should incorporate systematic imaging, laboratory, and electrophysiological assessments to enable comprehensive phenotyping. In addition, systematic follow‐up data are required to better characterize neurological sequelae in long‐term survivors.
