Chlorine-coordinated iron single-atom nanozymes for amplified ferroptosis in triple-negative breast cancer therapy
Authors: Mingming Yin, Bing-Hao Wang, Huijuan Wang, Jie Ouyang, Xingsheng Hu, Xiong Wang, Yongping Liu, Fenghua Xu, Yi Chen, Shuang-Feng Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents an aggressive breast cancer subtype with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis. Although single-atom nanozymes (SAzymes) show promise in cancer therapy, their ferroptosis-inducing capability remains limited. Herein, we present a rationally designed iron-based SAzyme with axial chlorine coordination (FeN4Cl) that integrates catalytic and metabolic functions to enhance ferroptosis in TNBC. The engineered Fe-Cl coordination strategically modulates the d-band center relative to the Fermi level, resulting in significantly enhanced peroxidase-like activity (2.0-fold increase) and glutathione oxidase-like activity (3.2-fold increase) activities compared to conventional FeN4 structures. Importantly, this electronic modulation triggers NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, establishing an autonomous iron supply mechanism that elevates intracellular labile Fe2+ levels. The synergistic disruption of redox homeostasis coupled with amplified Fenton reactions creates a feedback loop that induces cell death. Encapsulation within red blood cell membranes (FeN4Cl/RBC) improves biocompatibility and tumor targeting. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that FeN4Cl/RBC substantially suppresses tumor growth through effective ferroptosis, presenting a promising approach for developing clinically relevant nanozyme-based therapeutics.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04096-9.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is one of the most aggressive molecular subtypes of breast cancer, characterized by a high rate of metastasis, early recurrence, and poor prognosis. Due to the absence of hormone receptors and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification, TNBC does not respond to endocrine or targeted therapies, leaving cytotoxic chemotherapy as the primary—though often inadequate—treatment option [1–3]. According to the latest global cancer statistics, the global burden of breast cancer continues to rise. For patients with advanced or metastatic TNBC, the five-year survival rate drops below 30%, underscoring the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies [4].

This clinical challenge has sparked significant interest in targeting alternative cell death pathways. Among them, ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation—has emerged as a promising approach to overcoming TNBC’s therapeutic resistance [5–7]. Recent groundbreaking research has further elucidated the complex metabolic networks and in vivo dynamics governing ferroptosis, solidifying its therapeutic potential [8]. The canonical ferroptotic cascade involves glutathione (GSH) depletion, subsequent inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), culminating in fatal disruption of cellular redox homeostasis [9]. Intriguingly, the efficacy of ferroptosis is closely intertwined with that of autophagy, particularly ferritinophagy [10–12]. This process, which is mediated by nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), degrades the iron-storage protein ferritin, thereby releasing labile iron that can fuel the Fenton reaction, amplify ROS production, and potentiate ferroptosis [13–15].

Single-atom nanozymes (SAzymes), which have maximized atomic utilization, well-defined active sites, and potent catalytic activities, are exceptional candidates for inducing ferroptosis [16–19]. The catalytic performance of metal‒nitrogen‒carbon (M‒N‒C) materials is dictated by the electronic structure of the metal center. The local coordination environment directly modulates d-orbital occupancy, which in turn governs the adsorption energies of key reaction intermediates (e.g., ⋅OH, GSH) and thus the overall enzymatic activity [20, 21]. Consequently, engineering the axial coordination of the central metal atom has been identified as a powerful strategy to tune the d-band center (Ed) relative to the Fermi level (Ef) and thereby optimize the catalytic function [22–27]. However, a significant hurdle persists: conventional synthesis methods, such as postmodification doping, often yield inhomogeneous coordination environments and irreproducible active sites, particularly struggling to achieve precise and uniform control over axial coordination, which limits rational design and consistent performance.

Despite their promise, the application of SAzymes in ferroptosis therapy has been largely confined to a single dimension: mimicking peroxidase activity for ROS generation [28]. This singular focus overlooks the synergistic potential of a more holistic approach. An ideal ferroptosis-inducing agent must orchestrate a multipronged attack: not only generating ROS but also concurrently dismantling the cell’s antioxidant defenses (e.g., via GSH depletion) and, most critically, modulating intracellular iron metabolism to self-amplify its therapeutic effect [29]. The activation of ferritinophagy to increase the labile iron pool is a key yet largely unaddressed component for maximizing ferroptotic efficacy, as most SAzyme designs lack the precise electronic and structural features—such as an optimally tuned d-band center and charge distribution—required to initiate this complex metabolic signaling [30]. Beyond the orchestration of metabolic processes within a single death pathway, the strategic synergy between distinct modes of regulated cell death, such as the recently demonstrated co-induction of ferroptosis and pyroptosis, is emerging as a powerful therapeutic concept [31].

In this study, we developed a multifunctional biomaterial platform designed to overcome current limitations in ferroptosis-based cancer therapy (Scheme 1). First, we synthesized an advanced single-atom nanozyme (FeN4Cl) via a precoordination anchoring strategy, enabling atomic-level integration of an axial Cl ligand onto the FeN4 active site. This unique axial Fe–Cl coordination significantly modulates the electronic structure, enhancing both peroxidase- and glutathione oxidase-like catalytic activities. Notably, for the first time, we demonstrated that this electronic modulation can activate NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, initiating a self-amplifying ferroptosis cycle. Finally, the FeN4Cl nanozyme was encapsulated within biomimetic red blood cell membranes (FeN4Cl/RBC), yielding excellent biocompatibility, enhanced tumor-targeting capability, and potent synergistic therapeutic effects against TNBC.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of FeN4Cl sazymes

The FeN4Cl SAzymes were fabricated via a precoordination anchoring strategy designed to achieve precise axial chlorine ligation (Fig. 1a). Initially, hemin Cl was trapped in situ during the self-assembly of ZIF-8, yielding a hemin Cl/ZIF-8 precursor [32]. This precursor retained the characteristic rhombic dodecahedral morphology and crystal structure of the parent ZIF-8 (Figures S1, S2). Following high-temperature pyrolysis, the resulting FeN4Cl SAzyme preserved the polyhedral morphology of its precursor (Fig. 1b). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of FeN4Cl revealed only two broad peaks at 25° and 44°, corresponding to the (002) and (101) planes of graphitic carbon, with a notable absence of any peaks corresponding to metallic iron or other iron-based crystalline phases (Figure S3). The high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image of FeN4Cl (Fig. 1d and e) displayed high-density bright spots of Fe atoms. Furthermore, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1c) revealed a uniform distribution of C, Fe, N and Cl elements in FeN4Cl. In contrast, only N, C and Fe elements were detected in FeN4 (Figure S4). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) measured the iron content in FeN4Cl and FeN4 to be 0.28 and 0.32 wt%, respectively.

Subsequently, the chemical state of FeN4Cl was investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The bonding environment of carbon in FeN4 and FeN4Cl was categorized into three types of C-C, C-N and C-O, indicating the formation of nitrogen-doped graphitic carbon (Figure S5). The N 1 s XPS spectra of FeN4Cl and FeN4 revealed the presence of pyridinic N (398.3 eV), pyrrolic N (400.2 eV), graphitic N (401.4 eV), oxidized N (403.2 eV) and Fe-N peaks (399.2 eV) (Figure S6), confirming the formation of Fe-N bonds in the N-doped carbon. Notably, the pyridinic N, the main bonding sites for FeNx, is considered to be crucial for the oxidase-like activity of SAzymes [33, 34]. The content (23.82%) of pyridinic N in FeN4Cl was higher than that (21.48%) in FeN4 (Table S1). In the Cl 2p spectrum, Fe-Cl bonds in the FeN4Cl SAzymes were detected at approximately 198 eV (Figure S7). The high-resolution Fe 2p XPS spectrum can be deconvoluted to four peaks, positioned at 709.18, 711.71, 722.2 and 725.2 eV, corresponding to the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 electronic states of Fe2⁺ and Fe3⁺, respectively (Figure S8). Compared to FeN₄, the Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 peaks for FeN4Cl shifted to higher binding energies, owing to the strong electronegative interactions between the Cl atom and the Fe center.

Afterwards, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements were conducted to study the coordination structure and chemical state of iron in FeN4Cl. Fe K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) curves of FeN4Cl was measured with Fe foil, Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 as reference samples. The energy value for the Fe K-edge in FeN4Cl was 7122.47 eV (Fig. 1f), indicating that the average oxidation state of iron in FeN4Cl is approximately +2.23 by comparing with the reference samples (Fig. 1g and Table S2). Moreover, the extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) curve was further fitted (Fig. 1h and i) to determine the coordination environment of Fe. The fitting result only showed 2 peak distances of 1.5 Å and 1.71 Å, corresponding to Fe-N and Fe-Cl coordination, respectively. It is noting that no Fe-Fe peak was detected, suggesting the single atom nature of Fe, which was further confirmed by the wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 1j). Moreover, the average coordination numbers were determined to be 3.332 (Fe-N) and 0.833 (Fe-Cl), suggesting one Cl atom coordinating to Fe atom perpendicular to the Fe-N4 plane (illustration of Fig. 1h). Therefore, the FT-EXAFS fitting results and WT analysis confirmed the successful synthesis of FeN4Cl, in which Fe is present as atomically dispersed sites coordinated by four in-plane N atoms and one axial Cl atom.

Having established the unique FeN4Cl atomic structure, we next evaluated its capacity to function as a dual-action nanozyme capable of both generating ROS and depleting glutathione (GSH), two key events for initiating ferroptosis (Fig. 2a). We first assessed peroxidase (POD)-like activity via a 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) colorimetric assay. After optimizing the synthesis and reaction conditions, we found that the FeN4Cl SAzyme exhibited optimal catalytic performance under standard reaction conditions (pH 4.0, 50 µM H2O2) (Figure S9). Notably, FeN4Cl, which followed the pH response pattern of natural enzymes, showed significantly high activity in acidic microenvironment (Figure S10). FeN4Cl rapidly catalyzed the oxidation of TMB, producing a blue product with a maximum absorbance at 652 nm. In contrast, NC showed negligible capability for TMB oxidation, indicating that the atomically dispersed Fe sites are the active centers (Fig. 2b). Besides, oxidation reaction of TMB catalyzed by the FeN4Cl SAzyme was concentration and time-dependent (Fig. 2c and d).

A steady-state kinetic analysis revealed that the POD-like activity of FeN4Cl followed classic Michaelis‒Menten kinetics for both the TMB and H₂O₂ substrates (Fig. 2e and f). Critically, axial Cl-coordination imparted a substantial catalytic advantage over the conventional FeN4 analog. FeN4Cl demonstrated a 2.0-fold higher catalytic constant (kcat) and a 2.2-fold greater catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km) for TMB oxidation. Furthermore, its Michaelis constant (Km) for H2O2 was lower than that of FeN4 (4.23 mM vs. 4.77 mM), indicating a stronger binding affinity for the H2O2 substrate. Interestingly, the Vmax and kcat/Km values of FeN4Cl are higher than those of the overwhelming majority of previously reported SAzymes (Table S3). The results revealed the therapeutic potential of FeN4Cl for the tumor characterized by weak acidity and high H2O2 concentrations.

Considering the strong chelation between thiocyanate ions (SCN⁻) and the metal center catalytic sites, the role of single-atom Fe sites in enzyme-like reactions was evaluated through poisoning experiments (Fig. 2g). The study found that the POD-like activity was significantly inhibited by the blocking effect of KSCN molecules. Since H2O2 is the primary source of •OH generation, the reduction of H2O2 was quantified using the Ti(SO4)2 assay. The decrease in absorbance over time indicated the consumption of H2O2 during the catalytic reaction (Fig. 2h). The specific •OH probe o-phenylenediamine (OPDA) was used to measure the amount of ROS generated during the reaction (Figure S11). Furthermore, the degradation experiment using methylene blue (MB) was conducted to further investigate the ability of FeN4Cl to generate •OH (Figure S12).

On the other hand, tumor cells rely on the high concentrations of endogenous GSH to establish an antioxidant defense system, which not only maintains redox homeostasis but also diminishes the effectiveness of ROS treatment strategies. 5,5’-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) reduction assay indicated that FeN4Cl exhibited the strongest GSHOx-like activity (Fig. 2i). The consumption efficiency of GSH showed a significant positive correlation with both the concentration of FeN4Cl and the reaction time (Fig. 2j and k). The process followed the classic Michaelis-Menten equation (Fig. 2l). Compared to FeN4 (Km = 0.05 mM, Vmax = 1.46 × 10⁻7 M s⁻1), FeN4Cl exhibited a 2.79-fold increase in Vmax (Km = 0.13 mM, Vmax = 4.08 × 10⁻7 M s⁻¹). This result demonstrated that the FeN4Cl SAzyme with GSHOx-like activity could enhance antitumor efficacy by effectively depleting GSH.

The electronic structural properties and catalytic reaction pathways of FeN4Cl were studied through density functional theory (DFT) calculations, with the optimized models provided in the supplementary information. Charge density difference analysis revealed significant electronic rearrangement caused by the axial coordination of Cl atoms (Figure S13), leading to a reduction in the electron density of the central Fe atom’s d-orbitals and d-band center moveing upwards from −1.09 to −0.93 eV (Fig. 3a). This d-band position optimization significantly enhanced the adsorption strength of the Fe site on oxygen-containing intermediates, improving its Fenton-like reaction activity. Bader charge calculations revealed that the effective charge of the Fe atom in FeN4Cl was −1.11 |e|, an increase of 1.83% compared to the −1.09 |e| in the Fe-N4 system, confirming the electronic enrichment effect induced by Cl coordination. This electronic structure modulation reduced the dissociation energy barrier of the O-O bond in the H2O2 molecule, thereby enhancing the generation kinetics of ·OH. As shown in Fig. 3b and Figure S14, the Gibbs free energy profile indicated that the catalytic conversion pathway of H2O2 on the FeN4Cl surface followed an efficient Fenton-like mechanism. H2O2 was adsorbed and activated to form H2O2*, followed by being homolytically split into two OH groups. One OH molecule was desorbed to form ·OH, while the other underwent protonation to generate H2O, completing the catalyst regeneration process. During the homolytic cleavage of H2O2, FeN4Cl exhibited a lower activation energy barrier of 1.17 eV for the rate-determining step (H2O2 → 2OH*), compared to the Fe-N4 system (1.48 eV) (Fig. 3c). It is noteworthy that DOS near the fermi level in FeN4Cl significantly increased and exhibited new d-p hybridized electronic states(Fig. 3d and e), suggesting that the axial coordination of Cl atoms not only induced charge redistribution at the metal center but also enhanced orbital coupling between the Fe-3d orbitals and the reaction substrates, promoting interfacial charge transfer kinetics.

In vitro therapeutic efficacy and biocompatibility

Inspired by the excellent POD- and GSHOx-like activity of FeN4Cl, its therapeutic effect on TNBC was investigated in vitro. First, FeN4Cl was encapsulated with red blood cell membranes (FeN4Cl/RBC) to enhance biocompatibility. The coating of RBC membrane on FeN4Cl can be observed intuitively under a TEM by the thick capping layers around the rhombododecahedron-shaped FeN4Cl (Figure S15). Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis showed that the protein bands of FeN4Cl/RBC completely matched those of red blood cell membrane vesicles (Figure S16), confirming the successful coating of red blood cell membranes on the surface of the nanocatalyst. The change in Zeta potential further confirmed the successful coating of the membrane (Figure S17). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the hydrated diameter of FeN4Cl was approximately 228 nm (Figure S18). After coating with the erythrocyte membrane, the hydrated diameter of the nanomaterial decreased significantly by 50 nm. This reduction can be attributed to the membrane coating altering the thickness of the hydration layer around FeN4Cl in DLS measurements. FeN4Cl forms a thicker hydration layer due to the presence of hydrophobic groups on its surface. In contrast, the erythrocyte membrane coating provides a hydrophilic surface for the nanocomposite, leading to a smaller hydrated diameter in aqueous solution [35]. Since DLS measures the hydrodynamic size of nanomaterials along with their surrounding adsorption layers (including coatings and solvent molecules), the obtained particle size remains larger than that observed in TEM results [36]. In addition, the hydrodynamic diameters of both FeN4Cl and FeN4Cl/RBC remained virtually unchanged after a 5-day incubation in serum-containing buffer (Figure S19). This result indicates that the RBC membrane coating enhances the stability of the nanoparticle delivery system by reducing the adsorption of serum proteins onto the bare FeN4Cl surface, thereby preventing the formation of a protein corona [37]. And the hemolysis rate induced by FeN4Cl/RBC, even at a concentration of 100 µg/mL, remained below the international safety threshold of 5%, underscoring its excellent blood safety profile (Figure S20).

Next, cyanine 5.5 (Cy5.5)-labeled FeN₄Cl/RBC was utilized to study cellular uptake and subcellular localization via confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 4a). After co-culturing for 4 h, FeN4Cl/RBC was efficiently internalized by MDA-MB-231 cells through endocytosis and localized into the lysosomes. Biological TEM images further confirmed that the internalized SAzymes were distributed in the lysosomes (red arrow) and cytoplasm (yellow arrow) (Fig. 4b). The cytotoxicity of the SAzymes was assessed using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. As the concentration of the SAzymes increased, MDA-MB-231 cell viability decreased colrrespondingly (Fig. 4c). Specifically, at a concentration of 150 µg/mL, the cell viability for FeN4Cl/RBC decreased to lower than 10%. Besides, FeN4Cl/RBC also showed superior inhibitory effects on various TNBC lines, such as 4T1 and MCF-7 (Fig. 4d). Live/dead cell staining further affirmed the remarkable capacity of FeN4Cl/RBC to kill TNBC cells (Fig. 4e). Importantly, the SAzymes exhibited negligible effects on the viability of normal HEK293 cells, even at the concentration of 150 µg/mL (Figure S20). To verify the microenvironment-dependent nature of the material’s toxicity, we simulated tumor conditions (pH 6.5, 100 µM H₂O₂) in HEK293 cells. We found that this treatment specifically activated the cytotoxicity of FeN4Cl/RBC (Figure S22), validating its selective killing mechanism. This selective cytotoxicity may stem from the pathological overexpression of H2O2 and acidic microenvironment in the cancer cells (Figure S23, S24).

Subsequently, a fluorescent probe, 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) was employed to detect intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 5a and b). MDA-MB-231 cells treated with FeN4Cl/RBC exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity compared to other groups, indicating robust oxidative stress accumulation due to its enhanced POD-like catalytic activity. Then the downstream effects of elevated ROS were continuously studied.

First, malondialdehyde (MDA), the end product of LPO, was detected to estimate the LPO level in the MDA-MB-231 cells. As displayed in Fig. 5c, FeN4Cl/RBC-treated cells displayed the highest MDA content, suggesting the LPO accumulation. Moreover, GPX4, a GSH-dependent peroxidase that catalyzes the reduction of LPO, can be inactivated due to the GSH depletion. It is observed that the expression of GPX4 for all the SAzymes was downregulated (Fig. 5d). Especially, the cells treated by FeN₄Cl/RBC expressed the lowest GPX4 level due to more GSH depletion, leading to the accumulation of LPO. Based on the GSH depletion capability of FeN4Cl/RBC, the intracellular GSH level in treated tumor cells was significantly reduced (Figure S25). Then BODIPY 581/591 C11, an oxidation-sensitive fluorescent LPO probe, was also used to further verify the LPO level in the tumor cells (Fig. 5e). As expected, FeN4Cl/RBC induced the highest LPO level in the tumor cells. However, pre-treatment with the ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1,) markedly attenuated LPO accumulation induced by FeN4Cl/RBC (Fig. 5e and g), implicating that FeN4Cl/RBC can trigger ferroptosis. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the hallmarks of ferroptosis [38, 39], 5,5′,6,6′-Tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethyl-imidacarbocyanine iodide (JC-1), a mitochondrial fluorescent probe, was utilized to research the variation of mitochondrial membrane potential. FeN4Cl/RBC-treated cells exhibited pronounced mitochondrial depolarization, characterized by intensified green monomer fluorescence and diminished red aggregate fluorescence (Fig. 5f), alongside a 44% reduction in ATP production (Fig. 5h), demonstrating FeN4Cl/RBC can induce tumor cell death by ferroptosis (Fig. 5i).

Moreover, TEM revealed marked accumulation of autophagosomes in FeN4Cl/RBC-treated cells (Fig. 6a), indicating the activation of autophagic pathway. Then, western blot assays of autophagy markers confirmed a significant downregulation of p62 and a concurrent increase in lipidated LC3-II (Fig. 6b and c), which is the hallmark of autophagic flux enhancement. Notably, FeN4Cl triggered NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy that degraded ferritin to release iron. This was evidenced by reduced ferritin heavy polypeptide 1 (FTH1) level and upregulated NCOA4 expression (Fig. 6d and e). Ferrous ion quantification further demonstrated that ferritin degradation was accompanied by an increase in the labile iron pool (Fig. 6f). The results suggested a dual-pathway mechanism (Fig. 6g): FeN4Cl/RBC suppresses GPX4 to initiate ferroptosis, while simultaneously activating NCOA4-dependent ferritinophagy to elevate intracellular Fe2⁺, synergistically amplifying the ferroptosis effect.

Given the excellent tumor cell-killing effects of FeN4Cl/RBC in vitro, we subsequently evaluated its therapeutic potential for MDA-MB-231 xenograft-bearing mice (Fig. 7a). Mice with subcutaneous tumors (~100 mm3) received tail intravenous injection of Cy5.5-labeled FeN4Cl/RBC, followed by real-time biodistribution analysis via fluorescence imaging. FeN4Cl/RBC-treated mice exhibited significantly higher tumor fluorescence intensity with retention up to 48 h (Figure S26), confirming enhanced tumor-targeting specificity and prolonged retention. Pharmacokinetics study demonstrate that FeN4Cl/RBC exhibits a significantly prolonged blood circulation half-life (2.69 h) compared to FeN4Cl (1.13 h), which contributes to its enhanced accumulation at tumor sites via the EPR effect. More importantly, significant enrichment of the nanozyme was observed in tumor tissues compared with normal organs, providing direct pharmacokinetic evidence for its potent antitumor efficacy in vivo (Figure S27).

For the antitumor study, MDA-MB-231-tumor-bearing mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5): (i) PBS, (ii) FeN4, (iii) FeN4Cl, and (iv) FeN4Cl/RBC. The SAzymes were administered intravenously at a dosage of 10 mg kg− 1 every two days for 14 days. Tumor volumes and body weights were monitored. Notably, FeN4Cl/RBC showed the best tumor regression, compared to the other groups (Fig. 7c and f). H&E staining of FeN4Cl/RBC-treated tumors showed extensive vacuolar degeneration and nuclear pyknosis, suggesting significant apoptosis induced by FeN4Cl/RBC, confirmed by TUNEL assays (Fig. 7g). Moreover, upregulated LC3 expression, reduced p62 levels and GPX4 downregulation verified autophagy-mediated ferroptosis activated by FeN4Cl/RBC in the tumor cells (Fig. 7g and h). Finally, systemic safety evaluation showed no body weight fluctuations (Figure S28) or organotoxicity across treatment groups. H&E staining of major organs (Figure S29) and hematological/biochemical analyses (Figure S30) revealed no pathological abnormalities or liver/kidney function impairments in FeN4Cl/RBC-treated groups.

Chemicals

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2•6H2O), iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3•9H2O), and methanol (MeOH) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). 2-Methylimidazole (MeIM), hemin chloride, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), glutathione (GSH), and o-phenylenediamine (OPD) were obtained from Aladdin Industrial Corp. (Shanghai, China). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), Live/Dead Cell Staining Kit, malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kits, and mitochondrial membrane potential assay kits using JC-1 were acquired from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)−6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI) and Liperfluo probes were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The anti-Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4) antibody was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), and primary antibodies against LC3, SQSTM1/p62, NCOA4, FTH1, and GAPDH were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA).

Synthesis of ZIF-8, Fe(NO₃)₃·9 H₂O/ZIF-8, and Hemin Cl/ZIF-8

For the synthesis of ZIF-8, 3.39 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 160 mL of methanol under ultrasonic conditions to prepare solution (A) Then, 3.94 g of 2-methylimidazole (MeIM) was also dissolved in 160 mL of methanol under ultrasonic conditions to prepare solution (B) Subsequently, solution B was added to solution A, and the mixture was sealed in a round-bottom flask, vigorously shaken for 30 s, and then placed in a 60 °C oven to stand overnight. After the reaction, the ZIF-8 product was collected by centrifugation at 6000 rpm, washed three times with methanol, and finally dried in vacuum at 70 °C overnight. The preparation processes of Fe(NO₃)₃·9H2O/ZIF-8 and hemin Cl/ZIF-8 were identical to the above-described synthesis method of ZIF-8, with the only adjustment made during the preparation of Solution A. For the synthesis of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O/ZIF-8, 55.8 mg of iron(III) nitrate nonahydrate was added to solution A; For the synthesis of hemin Cl/ZIF-8, 90 mg of hemin Cl was added to solution A [32].

Synthesis of N-C, FeN4 and FeN4Cl

Powdered samples of ZIF-8, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O/ZIF-8, and hemin Cl/ZIF-8 were individually placed into ceramic boats and subjected to thermal activation in a tube furnace. The thermal treatment was carried out at 1000 °C for 2 h under a continuous nitrogen (N2) flow, with a controlled heating rate of 5 °C/min. The resulting carbon-based materials were designated as N–C, FeN4, and FeN4Cl, respectively.

Synthesis of FeN4Cl/RBC

Mouse whole blood was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min to isolate red blood cells (RBCs), which were washed three times with saline. RBCs were then lysed in 0.25× PBS under hypotonic conditions and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cytoplasmic contents. This process was repeated until the supernatant became colorless, indicating complete hemoglobin removal. For membrane coating, 10 mg of FeN₄Cl nanoparticles were co-sonicated with 10 mg of RBC membranes in PBS. The amphiphilic phospholipid bilayer facilitated hydrophobic interactions with FeN₄Cl surfaces. After three centrifugation–wash cycles to remove excess components, the mixture was extruded through a 200 nm polycarbonate membrane and stored at 4 °C.

The peroxidase-like activities assay

The peroxidase-like activity of FeN₄Cl nanozymes was assessed using a TMB–H2O2 chromogenic system, where FeN₄Cl catalyzed the oxidation of TMB to blue oxTMB, yielding an absorbance peak at 652 nm. For the assay, FeN4Cl (5–30 µg/mL), 20 µL TMB (5 mg/mL), 20 µL 3% H2O2, and PBS were mixed to a final volume of 2 mL. After 5 min at 25 °C, absorbance at 652 nm was measured by UV–vis spectroscopy. For kinetic analysis, mixtures contained 10 µL FeN4Cl, 20 µL 3% H2O2, 150 µL HAc–NaAc buffer (pH 4.0), and 20 µL of TMB at varying concentrations (TMB kinetics), or varying H₂O₂ concentrations with fixed TMB (H₂O₂ kinetics). The peroxidase-catalyzed reaction was initiated by Kinetic parameters including the maximum reaction velocity (Vₘₐₓ) and Michaelis-Menten constant (Kₘ) were derived through linear regression analysis of Lineweaver-Burk plots.

The glutathione oxidase-like activities assay

The glutathione oxidase (GSHOx)-like activity of FeN4Cl was assessed using a coupled chromogenic system with reduced glutathione (GSH) as the substrate and 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) as the thiol-detecting probe. Specifically, 0.1 mg of FeN4Cl was added to 2 mL PBS buffer (pH 6.5) containing 0.25 mM DTNB and varying GSH concentrations (0.02–0.16 mM) to initiate the reaction. The formation of 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB) was monitored at 412 nm via time-dependent UV–vis spectroscopy. Kinetic parameters were determined using Michaelis–Menten analysis based on initial reaction velocities measured within the first 3 min under pseudo-steady-state conditions.

Detection of •OH

O-phenylenediamine (OPDA) was used to detect •OH radicals. FeN4Cl (100 µg mL⁻¹) and 40 mM OPDA were incubated in pH 4.0 NaAc–HAc buffer (0.2 M) for 10 min. The luminescence spectrum was measured by fluorescence spectrophotometry, with intensity indicating •OH levels.

Cell culture

MDA-MB-231 (human breast cancer), MCF-7 (human breast adenocarcinoma), 4T1 (mouse breast cancer), and HEK-293 (human embryonic kidney) cell lines used in this study were obtained from the Cancer Research Institute, Central South University (Changsha, Hunan, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI (Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Genview), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Hemolysis evaluation

Fresh mouse blood (100 µL) was mixed with FeN4Cl (1 mL) at concentrations of 12.5–200 µg mL⁻¹. Water and PBS were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After 4 h at 37 °C, samples were centrifuged, and absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 570 nm. The hemolysis rate (%) was calculated as: Hemolysis rate (%) = (sample absorbance − negative control absorbance)/(positive control absorbance − negative control absorbance) × 100%.

Cellular uptake

MDA-MB-231 cells were incubated in confocal dishes for 24 h, followed by a 4-h incubation with Cy5.5-labeled FeN4Cl. After three PBS washes and 10-min Hoechst staining, samples were imaged using a confocal microscope (LSM980, Zeiss).

Cell viability assay

Nanozyme cytotoxicity was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2, seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/well), and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing nanozymes (0–150 µg mL⁻¹) and incubated for another 24 h. Cells were rinsed twice with cold PBS, followed by CCK-8 assay. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Live/dead staining was performed using calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI). Cells were seeded in confocal dishes, treated with nanozymes, stained with calcein-AM and PI (300 µL each), and imaged using a confocal microscope (LSM980, Zeiss).

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular ROS generated by the nanozyme was detected using DCFH-DA. MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated for 24 h, then treated with 50 µg mL⁻¹ nanozyme for 6 h. DCFH-DA was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After washing with PBS, cells were imaged using a confocal microscope (LSM980, Zeiss).

Bio-transmission electron microscopy (bio-TEM)

For bio-TEM analysis, MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded into culture plates at 2 × 106 cells per dish and treated for 12 h with (a) PBS, (b) FeN4 (50 µg mL⁻¹), (c) FeN4Cl (50 µg mL⁻1), or (d) FeN4Cl/RBC (50 µg mL⁻¹). After treatment, cells were washed with PBS, digested with trypsin, and collected by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min). The cell pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (3 mL) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, then processed for bio-TEM imaging.

Lipid peroxidation (LPO) detection

Remove adherent cells, discard the medium, and wash with PBS. Add Liperfluo premixed in serum-free medium, and incubate at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Wash three times with PBS, then digest with trypsin and collect the cell suspension in a 1.5 mL tube. Centrifuge at 1500 rpm for 5 min, discard the supernatant, and resuspend cells in 150 µL PBS. Keep on ice in the dark until analysis. Perform flow cytometry, collecting 10,000 cells per sample.

Animal model

All animal procedures were approved by the HNU Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No.: HNU-IACUC-2024-110). Female BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from Jiangsu Jiyuan Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. MDA-MB-231 cells (1 × 106 in 50 µL PBS) were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal region. Treatment began when tumor volume reached ~100 mm3. Mice were randomly divided into four groups and treated at 10 mg/kg. Body weight and tumor volume were monitored every other day. At the study’s end, mice were euthanized, tumors were weighed, and major organs and tumor tissues were collected for H&E staining and immunohistochemistry.

Tumor study

When tumor volume reached 100 mm3, nanozymes were intravenously administered at 10 mg kg⁻1. Control mice received PBS (n = 5). Body weight and tumor volume—calculated as (length × width2)/2—were monitored throughout. After 14 days, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation. Blood was collected from the orbital sinus for hematological analysis. Tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) were fixed in 10% formalin or collected for pathological examination.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test for comparisons involving more than two groups, and unpaired t-test for two-group comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Prism 9.0). The threshold for statistical significance was *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we constructed a novel single-atom nanozyme (FeN4Cl) with axial Fe–Cl coordination that enables precise electronic modulation and substantially enhances dual enzyme-mimicking activities. The axial Cl ligand elevates the d-band center of the Fe site, thereby promoting both peroxidase- and glutathione oxidase-like catalytic performance. Mechanistically, the increased oxidative stress induced by FeN4Cl activates NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, establishing a self-reinforcing ferroptotic loop through metabolic iron mobilization. Furthermore, red blood cell (RBC) membrane cloaking endows the nanozyme with excellent biocompatibility, prolonged circulation, and tumor-targeting capacity, resulting in potent ferroptosis-mediated antitumor efficacy in vivo without systemic toxicity. This study provides a paradigm for atomic-level nanozyme engineering coupled with metabolic reprogramming, offering promising translational potential for the treatment of TNBC.
