Succinylation related gene signature enables risk stratification and prognostic modeling in breast cancer patients
Authors: Zhifeng Chen, Mi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BRCA) is a major health threat to women and often carries a poor prognosis. Succinylation is closely associated with cancer metabolism; however, research on succinylation-related genes (SRGs) in BRCA remains relatively limited. This study first identified differentially expressed genes between normal and BRCA groups in the TCGA cohort and intersected them with known SRGs to obtain differentially expressed SRGs (DE-SRGs). Univariate Cox and LASSO Cox regression analyses were used to screen the DE-SRGs, followed by multivariate Cox regression to develop a succinylation-related prognostic model. Subsequently, patients were categorized into high and low succinylation-related risk score (SRS) groups according to the median score. Two groups were then compared for differences in immune microenvironment characteristics, somatic mutation burden, and drug sensitivity. Additionally, the expression levels of the signature genes were validated via qRT-PCR. This study developed a prognostic risk model for BRCA patients based on 13 SRGs (ACOT4, ALDH3A1, B3GALT1, IFNG, MMP1, NFKB2, PCSK6, PTK2, SERPINA1, SHMT2, SIRT7, ST3GAL1, and SUCLA2). A markedly elevated infiltration of immune cells was observed in the low-SRS group, including B cells, Mast cells, and Macrophages. The tumor mutational burden was notably lower in the low-SRS group. Drug sensitivity analysis suggested that the high-SRS BRCA patients might be more susceptible to FTI-277 and GNF-2. qRT‑PCR results showed that ACOT4, IFNG, MMP1, NFKB2, PCSK6, PTK2, SHMT2, SIRT7, and ST3GAL1 were significantly upregulated in human BRCA cell lines, while ALDH3A1, B3GALT1, and SUCLA2 were significantly downregulated. Our findings highlight the prognostic value of SRGs in BRCA, laying the groundwork for its potential use in guiding personalized treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00726-026-03535-z.Keywords: Breast cancer, Succinylation, Bioinformatics, Prognosis, Immunity

Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths in women, with nearly 2.8 million new diagnoses globally each year and mortality reaching about 666,000 (Bray et al. 2024). BRCA is a highly heterogeneous malignancy, comprising diverse subtypes with different biological characteristics, clinical presentations, and treatment responses (Weigelt et al. 2010). Current treatment has established a comprehensive system centered on surgery, combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy (Waks and Winer 2019). Although significant progress has been made in precision treatment for specific populations such as BRCA1/2 mutation carriers (Robson et al. 2017), the prognosis of advanced BRCA remains poor. Once local invasion or distant metastasis occurs, the 5-year survival rate drops to only 31%(Wagle et al. 2025). Therefore, identifying biomarkers that can predict disease progression and treatment response earlier and more accurately has become a key scientific issue in improving the clinical management of BRCA.

Protein post‑translational modifications (PTMs) have become a major focus in cancer research due to their central role in regulating protein function, cellular signaling, and metabolic reprogramming(Duan et al. 2024; Pan and Chen 2022). Common PTMs, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, and ubiquitination, are widely involved in tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Recent advances in mass spectrometry and omics technologies have led to the identification of several novel PTMs. Among them, succinylation—a modification closely associated to cellular metabolism—has emerged as a new area of interest in cancer biology, playing a distinct and important role in tumor metabolism recombination and signaling regulation. Succinylation covalently attaches a succinyl group to specific residues of target proteins, thereby modulating enzyme activity and metabolic pathways, particularly in mitochondrial metabolism, which is critical for cellular metabolism (Liu et al. 2023; Yang and Gibson 2019). Compared to other PTMs, succinylation is characterized by its high modification level, high sensitivity to metabolic fluctuations, and tight connection to cellular energy status. Thus, it is regarded as a key molecular bridge linking cellular metabolism to functional regulation( Hou et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023). Growing evidence indicates that succinylation mediates cancer cell metabolism by changing structure or activity of metabolism-related proteins and is critically involved in metabolic reprogramming (Liu et al. 2023). For instance, CPT1A promotes BRCA cell proliferation via succinylated ENO1 (Kurmi et al. 2018) and enhances metastatic capacity in gastric cancer cells via S100A10 succinylation (Wang et al. 2019). Succinylation regulators may modulate immune cell infiltration and RNA m6A methylation to facilitate the malignant progression of clear cell renal carcinoma (Lu et al. 2021). Additionally, in BRCA cell lines and mouse models, disruption of the SIRT5 gene leads to elevated succinylation and oxidative stress of IDH2 and other metabolic enzymes, as well as impaired transformation and tumorigenic capacity (Abril et al. 2021). Despite these advances, the association between succinylation-related genes (SRGs) and BRCA remains incompletely understood. Thus, this study aims to elucidate the potential prognostic significance of succinylation in BRCA patients.

This study aimed to develop a risk model of SRGs to predict BRCA patient prognosis and validate its prognostic utility in an independent external cohort. We further investigated potential associations between this model and BRCA patients’ tumor microenvironment (TME), somatic mutation characteristics, and drug sensitivity. Collectively, our study identified likely biomarkers for prognostic prediction and therapeutic decision-making in BRCA.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

BRCA-related datasets were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) database, comprising 113 normal and 1118 BRCA samples. GSE20685 (327 BRCA samples) and GSE21653 (266 BRCA samples) datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database as external validation cohorts.

SRGs were collected from the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/) by searching with the keyword “succinylation”, resulting in a final set of 565 genes (Supplementary Table S1).

Differential analysis, prognostic model construction and validation

Differential expression analysis between the normal group and BRCA group in the TCGA cohort was performed using the “edgeR” package( Sarker et al. 2024). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened with the following criteria: |log2FC|> 0.585 (i.e., absolute fold change > 1.5) and FDR < 0.05. This threshold was chosen to balance biological relevance and statistical significance, focusing on genes showing substantial expression changes between the two groups. The resulting DEGs were used for subsequent analysis. The intersection of DEGs and SRGs was obtained to define DE-SRGs. Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed by “survival” R package (Liu et al. 2021) on the DE-SRGs within the TCGA-BRCA cohort to identify genes significantly related to OS (p < 0.05). To reduce multicollinearity and further optimize variable selection, LASSO regression was performed using the “glmnet” R package (Yang et al. 2022). The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined through ten‑fold cross‑validation, and the λ value corresponding to the minimum cross‑validation mean error (λ = 0.00748) was selected to identify key candidate genes. Finally, a multivariable Cox regression analysis based on these candidate genes was used to construct the final prognostic risk‑score model. BRCA Patients were stratified into high- and low-SRS groups according to median score. Subsequently, the “survival” R package was utilized to plot Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and the “timeROC” package (Yan et al. 2024) was employed to plot ROC curves. The latter quantified prognostic accuracy by calculating the AUC at 1/3/5-year intervals. The GSE20685 dataset was employed for validation.

Construction and validation of the prognostic nomogram

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted by integrating the SRS with clinical characteristics (gender, age, TNM stage, and stage) of BRCA patients. The “rms” R package (Li et al. 2023) was employed to construct a prognostic nomogram to predict 1/3/5-year survival probabilities, followed by generating Calibration curves to assess agreement between nomogram-predicted and actual survival outcomes.

GSEA

With normalized expression matrix and risk group labels (high- and low-SRS groups), input files were constructed for functional enrichment analysis using GSEA software. This analysis explored biologically relevant pathways in the KEGG database. Enriched signaling pathways were selected with p < 0.05.

Exploration of the TME

We used “estimate” R package (Qin et al. 2018) to calculate immune, stromal, ESTIMATE, and tumor purity scores for BRCA patients in the high- and low-SRS groups, and drew violin plots. Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was utilized to assess differences in immunocyte infiltration and immune function scores. Additionally, expression differences were also compared for immune checkpoints and HLA-related genes.

Analysis of TMB

Based on mutation data from TCGA-BRCA cohort, the overall mutation landscape was plotted via “maftools” R package (Mayakonda et al. 2018), and the mutation rate of model genes across all samples was calculated. TMB scores were calculated for each sample. Differences in TMB scores were statistically evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the results were visualized using violin plots.

Prediction of potential therapeutic agents

IC50 values for drugs were acquired from CellMiner (https://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer/). Drugs significantly correlated with the signature genes were identified. The “pRRophetic” R package (Zhang et al. 2025) was utilized to predict sensitivity scores for various compounds. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to analyze statistical differences in predicted drug sensitivities.

Cell culture

Human normal breast epithelial cell line MCF10A and human BRCA cell line MCF-7 were both purchased from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China). MCF-10A cells were routinely cultured in DMEM/F12 medium with 5% horse serum, 20 ng/mL EGF, 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone, 10 μg/mL insulin, and 100 ng/mL cholera toxin. MCF-7 cells were routinely cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Both were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the cell lines using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Subsequently, SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA) was utilized to synthesize cDNA. Finally, q-PCR was conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each sample was run in triplicate, and all experiments were independently repeated three times (n = 3). GAPDH served as the reference gene, and RNA expression levels were quantified by the 2−ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The in vitro results are presented as mean ± standard error from at least three independent experiments. After assessing the normality of the quantitative data with the Shapiro–Wilk test, we employed the t-test for statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was completed using GraphPad Prism software. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Construction and validation of the prognostic model

Differential expression analysis between normal and BRCA groups in the TCGA-BRCA cohort yielded 8,763 DEGs (Fig. 1A). The intersection between these DEGs and the SRGs resulted in 299 DE-SRGs (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S2). Subsequently, univariate Cox regression analysis identified 19 genes related to patient survival (Supplementary Table S3). Next, 13 genes were identified using LASSO Cox regression analysis (Fig. 1C, D). Based on 13 signature genes, a multivariate Cox regression model was generated (Fig. 1E).

The BRCA prognostic model based on the TCGA cohort and SRGs. A: Volcano plot of DEGs between normal and BRCA samples. B: Venn diagram of the intersection between DEGs and SRGs. C: LASSO coefficients of genes screened by LASSO Cox regression. D: Gene Identification for prognostic model construction. E: Forest plot of the analysis results using multivariate Cox regression

Subsequently, BRCA patients were categorized into high- and low-SRS groups according to median SRS. Survival analysis showed that the OS rate of BRCA patients in the low-SRS group was markedly higher (Fig. 2A). This survival advantage of the low‑SRS group was also observed in the validation sets GSE20685 and GSE21653 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B–C). Regarding discriminative ability, the AUC values for predicting 1‑, 3‑, and 5‑year survival in the TCGA cohort were 0.73, 0.73, and 0.72, respectively (Fig. 2D). In GSE20685, the corresponding AUCs were 0.67, 0.72, and 0.69 (Fig. 2E), and in GSE21653, they were 0.83, 0.76, and 0.76 (Fig. 2F). To further evaluate the model’s applicability across molecular subtypes, patients in the GSE21653 validation set were stratified into five PAM50 subtypes: Basal, ERBB2, Luminal A, Luminal B, and Normal. Both survival analysis and ROC curves demonstrated that the model maintained good discriminative performance across each subtype (Supplementary Figure S1). These results indicate that the prognostic model we developed exhibits robust predictive performance across multiple independent cohorts and different molecular subtypes.

Validation of the BRCA prognostic model. A–C: Survival differences between high- and low-SRS groups in the TCGA training set (A), GSE20685 validation set (B), and GSE21653 validation set (C). D–F: ROC curves in the TCGA training set (D), GSE20685 validation set (E), and GSE21653 validation set (F)

Development of the nomogram

Based on clinical characteristics (age, TNM stage, gender, and stage) and risk score, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses identified the risk score as an independent prognostic factor for OS in BRCA patients (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, a prognostic nomogram was developed to predict 1/3/5-year survival probabilities (Fig. 3C). Calibration curves of the nomogram demonstrated strong concordance between the predicted values and actual survival probabilities, showing favorable predictive performance (Fig. 3D).

Construction of the nomogram. A-B: Univariate Cox regression (A) and multivariate Cox regression (B) analyses identifying independent prognostic factors. C: Prognostic nomogram for BRCA patients. D: The 1/3/5-year calibration curves of the nomogram

Functional enrichment analysis via GSEA

GSEA was used to conduct KEGG analysis on the differential gene expression profiles between high- and low-SRS groups. The analysis revealed a predominant enrichment of genes involved in immune functions in the high-SRS group, such as G2M checkpoint, unfolded protein response, and mitotic spindle (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). Conversely, in the low-SRS group, genes showed significant enrichment in pathways including IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling pathways, Interferon gamma response, and Allograft rejection (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B).

Functional enrichment analysis. A: GSEA enrichment results of the high-SRS group. B: GSEA enrichment results of the low-SRS group

Evaluation of immune microenvironment

We investigated the relationship between SRS and the TME in BRCA. Compared to the high-SRS group, the low-SRS group showed significantly higher stromal scores (p < 3.4e-11), ESTIMATE scores (p < 2.2e-16), and immune scores (p < 2.2e-16), while tumor purity was significantly lower (p < 2.2e-16) (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, ssGSEA analysis revealed significantly increased infiltration of multiple immune cell types in the low-SRS group, including B cells, Mast cells, CD8+ T cells, DCs, NK cells, Macrophages, Neutrophils, and T helper cells (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the low-SRS group exhibited considerably higher scores for functions such as APC co stimulation, T cell co stimulation, Cytolytic activity, and Type II IFN Response compared with the high-SRS group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Regarding immune checkpoints, expression levels of CTLA4, TIGIT, LAG3, ICOS, and PDCD1 were notably higher in the low-SRS group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5C). Moreover, levels of HLA-related genes (such as HLA-A, HLA-DMA, and HLA-DOA) were also significantly elevated in the low-SRS group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5D).

Analysis of TMB

Mutation data from TCGA-BRCA were summarized, revealing that missense mutations and C > T substitutions were the predominant mutation types (Fig. 6A). Among the 13 signature genes analyzed, PTK2 exhibited the highest mutation rate at 17% (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, waterfall plots visualized the top 30 genes with the highest mutation rates in two SRS groups. Analysis identified the top five mutated genes in each group: high-SRS group: TP53 (44%), PIK3CA (27%), TTN (18%), GATA3 (11%), MUC16 (11%); low-SRS group: PIK3CA (41%), TP53 (23%), CDH1 (18%), TTN (15%), GATA3 (14%) (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, SRS showed a positive correlation with TMB, with high-SRS patients showing considerably higher TMB (Fig. 6E).

C-D: Top 30 most frequently mutated genes in high-SRS (C) and low-SRS (D) patients. E: Differences in TMB between high- and low-SRS patients; Wilcoxon rank‑sum test

Prediction of potential therapeutic agents

To identify possible therapeutic agents targeting the succinylation-related model in BRCA patients, we evaluated treatment efficacy based on IC50 values from the GDSC database. Significant negative correlations were observed between SHMT2 and FTI-277 (Cor = -0.459), and between PTK2 and GNF-2 (Cor = -0.403) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). Patients in the high-SRS group exhibited significantly higher sensitivity to both agents (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7B).

Drug Sensitivity Analysis. A: Correlation analysis between signature genes and therapeutic agents based on IC50 values. B: Comparison of sensitivity to FTI-277 and GNF-2 between high- and low-SRS patients; Wilcoxon rank‑sum test

Validation of signature gene expression

The expression levels of the model genes in BRCA and normal breast epithelial cells were assessed using qRT‑PCR. Compared with the normal group, the expression of ACOT4 (p < 0.0001), IFNG (p < 0.001), MMP1 (p < 0.01), NFKB2 (p < 0.0001), PCSK6 (p < 0.001), PTK2 (p < 0.0001), SHMT2 (p < 0.0001), SIRT7 (p < 0.001), and ST3GAL1 (p < 0.0001) was significantly increased in BRCA cell lines, whereas ALDH3A1 (p < 0.0001), B3GALT1 (p < 0.0001), and SUCLA2 (p < 0.0001) showed significantly decreased expression (Fig. 8).

Discussion

BRCA exhibits significant disease heterogeneity regarding prognosis, biological function, and treatment response(Barzaman et al. 2020; Xiong et al. 2025). This heterogeneity underscores the urgent need for prognostic biomarkers applicable across BRCA patients to enable precise individualized treatment. Succinylation is a broad-spectrum, newly identified, dynamic, and non-enzymatic post-translational modification, playing a crucial role in regulating various tumors(Mu et al. 2021). Therefore, this study employed bioinformatics approaches to develop a prognostic model with SRGs and validated its predictive value for BRCA patient outcomes. This model provides a significant reference for guiding treatment selection in BRCA patients.

Given the vital roles of SRGs in initiation, progression, and prognosis of BRCA, we established a risk signature comprising 13 signature genes (namely ACOT4, ALDH3A1, B3GALT1, IFNG, MMP1, NFKB2, PCSK6, PTK2, SERPINA1, SHMT2, SIRT7, ST3GAL1, and SUCLA2) using multivariate Cox regression analysis. This signature demonstrated independent prognostic predictive power in both the TCGA cohort and the GSE20685 dataset. Among these genes, small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) regulate the pre-metastatic immune microenvironment in BRCA by modulating B3GALT1 expression, resulting in enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration, reduced immune escape, and thereby controlling metastasis (Li et al. 2025). IFNG, encoding interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), orchestrates innate immunity and inflammatory responses within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) (Zhou et al. 2022). In BRCA’s TIME, CD8+ T cells, DCs, M1 macrophages, and neutrophils secreted inflammatory cytokines like IFN-γ to suppress tumor growth (Shihab et al. 2020). Additionally, Alphavirus-driven IFNG expression suppresses tumor growth in orthotopic 4T1 BRCA models (Trofimova et al. 2021). Matrix Metalloproteinase-1 (MMP1), a zinc-dependent endopeptidase, participates in various physiological processes by regulating the extracellular matrix and TME (Akter et al. 2023). MMP1 enhances tamoxifen resistance in BRCA through promoter hypomethylation (Kim et al. 2022). Furthermore, MMP1 facilitates epithelial-mesenchymal transition and confers resistance to sacituzumab govitecan in hormone receptor-positive BRCA via NF-κB activation (Chen et al. 2025). NF-κB signaling regulates BRCA stem cell properties (Yeo et al. 2017). As a pleiotropic transcription factor, NFKB2 is observed with frequent mutation in cancers and linked to aberrant TNF signaling and oncogenesis (Chatterjee et al. 2019), which may function as a prognostic biomarker in BRCA (Limmer et al. 2023). PCSK6 potentially promotes BRCA cell proliferation by interfering with cell cycle arrest and activating the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway (Wang et al. 2018). PTK2, also known as FAK, is a non-receptor protein tyrosine kinase, acting as a key mediator of signals released from integrins and growth factor receptors (Feng et al. 2023). CircRPAP2 regulates PTK2 alternative splicing by binding splicing regulator SRSF1, thereby impacting BRCA development (Yu and Fang 2022). PTK2 expression and activity are upregulated in BRCA and correlate with poor patient prognosis (Chen et al. 2022). SERPINA1 is overexpressed in BRCA, and its overexpression is related to favorable prognosis, increased disease-free survival, and OS (Avalos-Navarro et al. 2024). SHMT2, a key enzyme in serine metabolism, facilitates BRCA tumor growth via VEGF and MAPK signaling pathways (Xie et al. 2022). Downregulation of SIRT7 promotes BRCA metastasis by inducing chromothripsis through LAP2α (Huo et al. 2023). Sialyltransferase ST3GAL1 enhances the migratory capacity of BRCA cells by modulating interactions between EGFR and NRP1 signaling pathways (Fan et al. 2025). SUCLA2 acts as a vital driver of anoikis resistance and metastasis in human cancers, regulating redox balance to drive BRCA metastasis (Boese et al. 2023). However, research on ACOT4 and ALDH3A1 in BRCA is relatively limited and requires further validation. We therefore validated these two genes using qRT-PCR, finding significantly increased ACOT4 expression and downregulated ALDH3A1 expression in BRCA cell lines compared with normal breast epithelial cells.

In the functional enrichment analysis, we observed significant activation of the IL‑6/JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. It is reported that this pathway may contribute to tumor progression in BRCA by promoting cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and forming a positive feedback loop with inflammatory responses (Manore et al. 2022). The TME plays an important role in tumor development and consists of various stromal and immune cells (Seager et al. 2017). With the advancement of technologies such as RNA sequencing, it is now possible to systematically analyze the characteristics of the TME and the functional diversity of tumor‑infiltrating immune cells, thereby predicting patient sensitivity to immunotherapy (Zhang and Zhang 2019). In this study, SRS showed a significant association with immune cell infiltration: the high‑SRS group exhibited lower infiltration levels of B cells, CD8⁺ T cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages. Moreover, the expression of certain immune checkpoint molecules (such as ICOS, PDCD1, TIGIT, CTLA4, LAG3) was significantly upregulated in the low‑SRS group. These results suggest that SRS may reflect differences in the immune microenvironment of BRCA and is associated with immune checkpoint expression, providing an analytical basis for further exploration of the role of succinylation in tumor immunoregulation.

TMB reflects the number of somatic coding mutations, and certain genes frequently exhibit high mutation rates in BRCA. In this study, patients in the high‑SRS group showed higher TMB levels, and the mutation rates of TP53 and TTN were also elevated compared to the low‑SRS group. Research indicates that TP53 is the most frequently somatically mutated gene across all BRCA subtypes, and its mutation has been associated with poorer prognosis (Sandoval et al. 2024; Shahbandi et al. 2020). Another study notes that TTN is also commonly mutated in BRCA (Cui et al. 2023). Additionally, it has been suggested that TTN and TP53 may participate in BRCA progression by regulating shared downstream pathways (Kim et al. 2017). Based on these findings, the prognostic model developed in this study provides a reference for identifying potential immune‑related biomarkers. In the prediction of potential drug sensitivity, the model identified two compounds, FTI‑277 and GNF‑2, with the high‑SRS group exhibiting higher predicted sensitivity to both drugs. FTI‑277 is a farnesyltransferase inhibitor that can restore the E‑cadherin/catenin cell‑adhesion system in human cancer cells and reduce cancer metastasis (Nam et al. 2002). In breast cells, FTI‑277 has been shown to inhibit invasion and migration by blocking H‑Ras activation (Lee et al. 2016). It should be noted, however, that the clinical application of these two compounds in BRCA treatment currently lacks sufficient supporting clinical evidence.

In summary, we have constructed a novel prognostic model based on succinylation to predict the prognosis of BRCA patients and evaluate their potential responses to different treatment strategies, providing a new potential tool for individualized therapeutic decision-making in BRCA. This model offers valuable insights for personalized treatment of BRCA. However, this study also has several limitations that need to be addressed in future work. First, the conclusions of this study are primarily based on retrospective data analysis; therefore, we plan to conduct a multicenter, prospective clinical cohort study to further validate the clinical applicability and robustness of the model. Second, although the model demonstrates good discriminative ability, the pathways for its integration with existing clinical tools (such as Oncotype DX), the feasibility of clinical implementation, and whether it can add value to the current diagnostic and treatment framework require further clarification. Additionally, the sample size in this study is relatively limited, and the stability of the model in broader populations still needs validation through larger-scale external cohorts. More importantly, to deeply elucidate the specific biological functions of the 13 feature genes comprising this model in the development and progression of BRCA, we will conduct a series of experimental studies. We plan to use siRNA or CRISPR-Cas9 technology to knock down or knock out the target genes in BRCA cell lines, systematically investigating their effects on cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and drug sensitivity. Concurrently, we plan to employ co-immunoprecipitation combined with mass spectrometry to explore their interaction protein networks and clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms. If conditions permit, we will further develop transgenic mouse models or utilize patient-derived organoids for in vivo and in vitro functional validation, comprehensively analyzing the roles of these genes in disease progression and treatment responses, thereby providing solid experimental evidence for the biological interpretability and clinical translation of the model.
