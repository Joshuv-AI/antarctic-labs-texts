Nonlinear Association of Occlusion Pressure at 100 MS and Patient Survival
Authors: Sakina Kadomatsu, Tomohiro Shinozaki, Mikio Nakajima, Tadahiro Goto
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the article by Julien Le Marec and colleagues recently published in the Journal (1). The authors demonstrated that abnormally high occlusion pressure at 100 ms (P0.1) values were associated with dyspnea, higher mortality, and prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation, whereas low P0.1 values were not associated with these outcomes. However, other studies suggest abnormally low P0.1 values could be associated with worse outcomes (2, 3). With our interest in extremely low P0.1 values, we would like to raise the following points.

The main finding (Table 3 in Le Marec and colleagues [1]) was based on comparisons among three groups: low (P0.1 < 1.5 cm H2O), normal (1.5 cm H2O ⩽ P0.1 ⩽ 3.5 cm H2O), and high (P0.1 > 3.5 cm H2O). Although the study by Le Marec and colleagues (1) conducted comparisons among groups with low P0.1 values, we noted the absence of a clear differentiation between “relatively low” and “abnormally low” P0.1 values. Despite the presentation of Figure 2B, which extends across a wide range of P0.1 values from 0 to >7.5 cm H2O, indicating the 90-day mortality risk, it is inferred that a precise evaluation of prognoses for patients with particularly low P0.1 values is not fully achieved because of limitations in the modeling approach.

We argue that this trend may be an artifact that merely reflects the characteristics of the model used in their study. Specifically, the adjusted survival probabilities at 90 days in the figure are derived from the estimates of the Cox models using the following steps (4): 1) fit the Cox model including P0.1 as a continuous covariate adjusting for other prognostic variables; 2) from the estimates of the coefficients and baseline cumulative hazard at 90 days, calculate the predicted probability of the value of each patient’s prognostic variables by setting the P0.1 value at a specific value (e.g., 0.1 cm H2O); 3) average the predicted probabilities across all patients in a dataset; and 4) repeat steps 2 and 3 by setting the P0.1 value at different values (e.g., 0.2 cm H2O). As an alternative method, the authors could have predicted the probabilities for the average values of the prognostic variables with P0.1 set at specific values. In either case, the shape of the predicted probabilities across P0.1 values depends on how P0.1 was treated in the Cox model. According to the results in Le Marec and colleagues (1), the Cox model that their adjusted survival probabilities relied on incorporated P0.1 as a linear term. This in and of itself implies a monotonic relationship between P0.1 values and predicted survival probabilities. Figure 1 illustrates how predicted survival probabilities at a specific time point change across values of a continuous covariate when the underlying univariable Cox model includes the variable as a linear term with hazard ratio of 1.18.

Two compatible sets of the estimates of survival probabilities at time t (say, 90 d) for different levels of P0.1 based on distinct univariable Cox models fitted to the same data. Red line: estimates from the Cox model including a linear P0.1 term (a continuous variable), whose coefficient is log(1.18), corresponding to a hazard ratio per 1-unit increase of 1.18. Green line: estimates from the Cox model with linear spline of P0.1, with a hazard ratio per 1-unit increase of 0.50 (when P0.1 < 1.5) and 1.35 (when P0.1 ⩾ 1.5). P0.1 = Occlusion pressure at 100 ms.

As earlier studies have shown that extremely low P0.1 values are also harmful (2, 3), Figure 2B in Le Marec and colleagues (1), based on the Cox model with the linearity assumption, might mislead readers. Specifically, it might incorrectly suggest that lower values of P0.1 are not problematic when, in fact, the relationship between P0.1 and the outcome is nonlinear, such that both excessively high and low P0.1 values are detrimental. To elucidate plateau effects, nonmonotonic relationships, or nonconstant risk increases, the underlying Cox model should have included the P0.1 term in a more flexible way. We respectfully propose using regression spline functions to graphically capture the possibly nonlinear relationship between P0.1 and the prognosis more accurately. This approach could facilitate a more nuanced understanding of the impact of varying P0.1 levels on the outcomes.
