Integrated spatial transcriptomics and pan-cancer XGBoost modeling uncover spatial drivers of immune exclusion and predict immunotherapy response
Authors: Hongying Zhao, Wangyang Liu, Haotian Xu, Lu Wang, Zushun Chen, Yanwu Sun, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment, yet characterizing the spatial complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment remains a challenge. In this study, we established a comprehensive computational framework integrating multi-omics profiling across 27 cancer types to decode immune-related non-coding RNA regulatory networks. Moving beyond traditional bulk analysis, we utilized spatial transcriptomics to dissect the spatial localization of these regulators. We identified the SNHG6–BIRC5 axis as a critical driver of the “immune-cold” phenotype in lung adenocarcinoma. We provide visual evidence that this axis localizes to tumor nests and negatively correlates with T- cell infiltration, elucidating a mechanism of spatial immune exclusion. Validating the clinical relevance of these findings, genome-scale CRISPR-Cas9 screening data confirmed the functional essentiality of these targets for cancer cell survival. Furthermore, pharmacogenomic analysis revealed that high expression of this axis correlates with sensitivity to chemotherapy agents like Vinblastine, suggesting a potential stratification strategy for patients with immune-excluded tumors. To expand the clinical utility to immunotherapy prediction, we developed a pan-cancer XGBoost machine learning model incorporating 14 high-performance regulatory features. This model achieved robust performance in distinguishing immunotherapy responders from non-responders with an AUC of 0.771, outperforming traditional markers such as PD-L1. Collectively, this study highlights spatial determinants of immune exclusion and chemotherapy sensitivity- and presents a generalized machine- learning tool for precision immunotherapy stratification. Introduction

Cancer has a high mortality rate and threatens human life and health [1]. Dysregulation of the immune system is a major cause of cancer development. While immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment strategy by harnessing the immune system [2], only a small fraction of patients (~ 5%) with advanced cancers derive durable benefits, largely due to immune evasion and tumor heterogeneity [3]. Therefore, deciphering the dysregulated gene expression underlying immune resistance is essential for improving therapeutic outcomes [4].

Many mechanisms contribute to gene expression regulation and ensure transcriptional responses to external signals[5]. The competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNA) hypothesis proposes a complex post-transcriptional regulatory network where lncRNAs and mRNAs regulate each other by competing for miRNAs binding via miRNA response elements (MREs). This competition affects the expression levels of various RNAs through MREs, which play a crucial role in cancer development, progression, recurrence, prognosis, and immune regulation[6, 7]. For instance, specific ceRNA axes have been identified as prognostic biomarkers in lung cancer, such as, MLETA1-miR-186-5p-EGFR [8] and AL139294.1-miR-204-5p-BRD4[9]. Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of ceRNA networks in modulating the tumor immune microenvironment (TME), including immune cell infiltration and antigen presentation. For instance, lncRNA LINC00963 regulates antigen processing and presentation via the miR-92a-3p/LY6E axis [10], while SNHG1-miR-448-IDO axis facilitates immune escape immune escape by promoting Treg differentiation[11]. Crucially, ceRNA molecules play a key role in regulating the immunotherapy response and drug resistance. The lncRNA FGD5-AS1 regulates PD-L1 expression to impacts the clinical efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy[12], and the SNHG6-hsa-miR-30e-5p-YSLTR1 axis was associated with prognosis and immunotherapy response in lung adenocarcinoma[13]. Thus, dysregulation of multiple ceRNA expressions has been reported to affect not only endogenous immune surveillance against cancer but also tumor response to exogenous immunotherapy[14].

Despite these findings, our understanding of ceRNA regulation in intratumoral heterogeneity remains limited. The diverse behaviors of cells within the TME are driven by fine-tuning gene regulation, and bulk RNA-seq often masks these cell-specific nuances [15]. Single-cell technologies will greatly enhance the capacity to discover new targets, study mechanisms, explore tissue heterogeneity, cellular variability, and screen rational drugs for immunotherapy[16]. Given that non-coding RNAs are increasingly recognized as key determinants of immunotherapy outcomes[17–19], exploring ceRNA regulation at the single-cell level is crucial.

Therefore, in this study, we constructed a comprehensive computational framework integrating multi-omics and single-cell profiling to decode immune-related ceRNA (ImmCeRNA) networks across pan-cancer. This work will provide important support for predicting the individualized immunotherapy efficacy and elucidating therapeutic resistance mechanisms.

Methods

Data collection

We obtained human immune-related genes, miRNA, and lncRNA from the immport project[20] and immReg[21]. We obtained RNA-seq expression profiles of 32 cancer types and the corresponding clinical information based on the R package “TCGAbiolinks”[22]. We manually collected the immunotherapy datasets of the above cancers from the GEO database, including the bulk dataset and the single-cell dataset. Detailed information about these datasets is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Identification of potential immunology ceRNAs

To identify ImmCeRNA (lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA) relationship pairs in cancer, we analyzed the immune-related lncRNA, miRNA, and mRNA expression profiles described above. Pairs of lncRNA-mRNA that interact with the same miRNA are defined as ceRNAs. In ceRNAs, lncRNAs compete with mRNAs encoding genes to bind miRNAs. Previous studies have shown that an increase in lncRNA expression enhances the expression of the corresponding encoded gene. In addition, miRNAs are expressed in a tissue-restricted manner and are associated with specific biological processes and diseases. Similarly, lncRNAs are expressed in certain tissues and are associated with a variety of cancers. Therefore, it is hypothesized that specific ceRNA interactions occur in cancer. We compared immune-related lncRNA, miRNA, and mRNA expression patterns in 32 cancers. A relationship pair was defined as a candidate ImmCeRNA if, in a given cancer, the expression pattern of the lncRNA, miRNA, mRNA met the criteria established by LncACTdb 2.0 [23]: corr(lncRNA, miRNA) < 0, p < 0.05; corr(mRNA, miRNA) < 0, p < 0.05; corr(lncRNA, mRNA) > 0, p < 0.05. Here, corr(lncRNA, miRNA), corr(miRNA, mRNA), and corr(lncRNA, mRNA) denote the Pearson correlation coefficients based on expression values for lncRNA-miRNA, miRNA-mRNA, and lncRNA-mRNA interactions, respectively. We prioritized this directionality-based filtering strategy over a stringent correlation magnitude cutoff to minimize false negatives arising from tissue heterogeneity and the subtle, fine-tuning nature of ceRNA regulation. In the second step, experimentally confirmed ceRNA relationship pairs were screened. Experimentally confirmed miRNA targets were downloaded from StarBase v2.0[24] and miRTarBase[25], two reliable online miRNA reference databases storing manually curated collections of experimentally supported miRNA targets. After integrating the datasets, a total of 77,460 experimentally confirmed non-redundant human miRNA-mRNA pairs were acquired. We acquired 80,860 experimentally confirmed lncRNA-miRNA relationship pairs from StarBase v2.0 and DIANA-LncBase v3.0 (www.microrna.gr/LncBase). The experimental methods involved included Luciferase reporter assay, Western blot, PAR-CLIP, HITS-CLIP, Immunofluorescent Assay, Immunostaining, MTT assay, qPCR, etc. miRNA gene names are named in the MIR# format in TCGA expression profiles, and miRNAs are named by applying the miRNA mature body naming rules in the target database. Therefore, we downloaded the annotation information of miRNAs from the miRbase[26] database and unified the naming of miRNAs by ID conversion. In summary, our study employed a strict intersection strategy: only relationship pairs that simultaneously satisfied the correlation criteria (Step 1) and experimentally validated physical interactions for both the lncRNA–miRNA and miRNA–mRNA segments (Step 2) were defined as experimentally confirmed ImmCeRNA. This stringent selection process ensures that the identified ceRNA network is supported by both transcriptional co-expression evidence and physical interaction data.

Identification of cell-specific and spatially spot-specific ceRNA networks in single-cell and spatial transcriptome datasets

To verify whether these ceRNA pairs are associated with each other in individual cells/spots, we used a probability-theory-based cell-specific network construction method to identify ceRNA networks in individual cells/spots[27, 28]. The method constructs a cell-specific network (CSN) for each single cell/spatially spot based on scRNA-seq/stRNA-seq data, and the CSN is able to find different gene associations for each individual cell/spot. Functional validaion of ImmCeRNAs using CRISPR screening data

Cell lines were grouped into “high” vs. “low” ceRNA categories according to the median expression of ceRNA-target genes. CRISPR-mediated fitness effects are typically more pronounced in cell lines where the target pathway is highly active. This stratification maximizes our ability to detect significant phenotypic shifts upon knockout. Results

The landscape of ImmCeRNA across cancer types

To systematically identify immune-related competing endogenous RNA (ImmCeRNA) interactions across various cancers, we proposed a three-step computational framework called ImmCeRNA, which analyzed the expression levels and regulatory relationships among long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), microRNAs (miRNAs), and mRNAs across 32 cancer types. First, immune-related lncRNAs, miRNAs, and genes that are correlated with immune-related pathways were collected from the Immunity Project ImmPort and the immune-regulation database ImmReg. Second, to identify the active immune-related ceRNA pairs in a specific cancer, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of each candidate ceRNA pair. A functional ImmCeRNA pair was defined for a cancer if it met the following conditions: there was a significantly negative expression correlation between miRNA and lncRNA as well as between miRNA and gene interactions (with a PCC < 0 and p < 0.05), and a significantly positive correlation between lncRNA and gene interactions (PCC > 0 and p < 0.05) based on expression values. Third, experimentally validated ImmCeRNA interactions through methods including Luciferase reporter assays, Western blotting, and PAR-CLIP were selected (Fig. 1A). Consequently, due to the strict requirement for simultaneous immune-relevance, expression correlation, and experimental validation, robust ImmCeRNA networks were identified in 27 cancer types, which were used for all subsequent analyses.

To elucidate the functional roles of these ImmCeRNA, we conducted comprehensive analyses encompassing several aspects: cancer dysregulation, prognosis, drug sensitivity, regulatory links with transcription factors, and single-cell characterization, including expression profiling of individual cells and spatial spots, as well as dynamic changes along pseudotime trajectories. Additionally, we explored their association with immunotherapy responses, assessed the impact of CRISPR-based ImmCeRNA knockout on cell line survival rates, and constructed a pan-cancer predictive model to assess its utility in distinguishing between immune therapy responders and non-responders (Fig. 1A). Our analysis identified 6,070 ImmCeRNA across 27 cancer types based on expression and experimental validation (Fig. 1B). Notably, a substantial number of ImmCeRNA were identified in testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT, n = 2,045), thymoma (THYM, n = 1,632), and breast cancer (BRCA; 460). This enrichment aligns with the distinct immunobiology of these malignancies: TGCTs are characterized by intense lymphocytic infiltration[29], THYM is intrinsically linked to T-cell development[30], and BRCA is shaped by continuous immunosurveillance mechanisms [31]. The high prevalence of ImmCeRNAs in these tumors suggests that post-transcriptional RNA networks serve as a critical mechanism for modulating immune recognition and establishing an immunosuppressive phenotype.

Furthermore, to ascertain whether ImmCeRNA are cancer-specific or common across cancers, we constructed a cancer-ImmCeRNA network. We discovered that ImmCeRNA in kidney chromophobe (KICH), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) were cancer-specific (Fig. 1C). In contrast, a significant number of ImmCeRNA were shared among TGCT, THYM, BRCA, Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), and prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) (Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings indicate that ImmCeRNA are prevalent in most cancers and exhibit distinct regulatory patterns.

ImmCeRNAs are likely to be dysregulated in cancer

Dysregulation of regulatory factors has been implicated in the development of various tumors. To investigate whether potential ImmCeRNA exhibits expression disturbances in cancer, we systematically analyzed their expression patterns across cancer types with available matched normal tissues (n > 5 per cancer type). A ceRNA was classified as dysregulated if at least one of its three components showed significant differential expression (FDR < 0.05, |FC|> 1.5) in tumor versus matched normal tissue. We adopted this criterion because significant alteration of even a single RNA component is sufficient to disrupt the regulatory balance, leading to potential dysregulation of the entire ceRNA axis[32]. We observed that hundreds of ImmCeRNA displayed differential expression patterns across various cancers (Fig. 2A). Notably, BRCA and COAD exhibited a greater number of specifically differentially expressed ceRNAs compared to other cancers (Supplementary Fig. 2A).

We identified 1,345 ImmCeRNAs that were exclusively dysregulated in a single cancer type, whereas 125 interactions were shared across multiple cancers. We found that ImmCeRNAs with deregulated expression were often cancer-specific in the cases of KICH, LIHC, and thyroid carcinoma (THCA)(Fig. 2B). These results reveal both cancer-specific and shared patterns of ImmCeRNA dysregulation, emphasizing the diverse regulatory landscapes across different cancer types. For example, the ImmCeRNA NR2F1-AS1-MIR25-FGF2 has been identified in five cancer types, lncRNA NR2F1-AS1 and its target gene FGF2 exhibited a significantly positive expression correlation in these cancers (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. 2B–C). The expression levels of both NR2F1-AS1 and FGF2 were significantly downregulated in tumor samples of BLCA, BRCA, COAD, and PRAD compared to matched TCGA normal and GTEx data (Supplementary Fig. 2D). In breast cancer, we observed a significant upregulation of ImmCeRNAs in tumor samples when compared to normal tissue (Fig. 2D). Notably, HOTAIR-MIR221-ACTG1, H19-MIR331-PSMC4, and SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 were highly expressed in breast tumor samples (Fig. 2E; Supplementary Fig. 1E), whereas TBX5-AS1-MIR25-IL6ST, NR2F1-AS1-MIR25-FGF2 were lowly expressed in breast tumor samples (Supplementary Fig. 2F). We identified that HOTAIR can competitively bind to miR-221 and ACTG1 in breast cancer, and this ceRNA was highly expressed in tumor tissues (Fig. 2E), which may be associated with the development and progression of breast cancer. Previous studies have demonstrated that the aberrant expression of lncRNA HOTAIR plays an important role in breast cancer and contributes to cancer progression[33]. Similarly, we found several ImmCeRNAs with dysregulated expression, such as SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 and AC092718.4-MIR185-IKBKG. Both of these ceRNAs were upregulated in LUAD when compared to normal lung tissue (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. 2G). These findings underscore the widespread disruption of ImmCeRNA in cancer, highlighting their potential roles in tumorigenesis and cancer progression.

Furthermore, we specifically explored the activated ceRNAs in cancer. We constructed a cancer-activated ImmCeRNA network and found that 23.9% (389/1,628) of the interactions were specifically activated in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 2G). Notably, consistent with the dysregulation patterns, BRCA and COAD exhibited a greater number of activated ceRNAs compared to other cancers, whereas KICH and LIHC showed minimal activation (Fig. 2H). For example, the SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 axis was identified as a recurrently activated driver across multiple cancers, reinforcing its potential role in promoting tumorigenesis.

It is crucial to maintain the balance among ceRNA transcripts, and their dysregulation may trigger pathological processes including carcinogenesis. Transcriptional regulation of ceRNA network components by specific transcription factors (TFs) may further elucidate the mechanisms underlying cancer progression[34]. To uncover the molecular mechanisms initiating this widespread dysregulation, we integrated ChIP-seq data to map the upstream regulatory landscape. We identified a total of 1,291 TFs potentially regulating 5,675 ImmCeRNAs across pan-cancer types (Fig. 2I). In LUAD, we specifically explored which TFs might orchestrate the expression of the dysregulated lncRNAs identified in our analysis. We found that MAX (Myc-associated factor X) and SMARCA4 emerged as the predominant master regulators within this cancer-specific network, controlling the largest number of upregulated lncRNAs, including AGAP2-AS1, PITPNA-AS1, LBX2-AS1, VPS9D1-AS1, WASIR2, NKILA, PPP1R26-AS1(Fig. 2G). This transcriptional activation may increase the abundance of lncRNA, thereby enhancing their capacity to sponge miRNAs and derepress downstream target genes[35]. For instance, our network identified EP300 (p300) as a key regulator of the lncRNA NKILA. This aligns with seminal findings that NKILA transcription is driven by the NF-kB pathway[36]. Since EP300 acts as an essential transcriptional co-activator recruited by NF-kB to drive target gene expression[37], These results confirm that specific oncogenic transcriptional programs drive the widespread dysregulation of the ImmCeRNA network to promote cancer progression.

Spatial characterization of ImmCeRNAs in the tumor microenvironment

Breakthroughs in spatial transcriptomics map gene expression to tissue location, helping to uncover the mechanisms behind complex diseases[38, 39]. To further explore the immune-related ceRNA regulation at the spatial level, we collected and analyzed recent spatial transcriptome (ST) datasets. While our bulk analysis covered 27 cancer types, we focused our spatial investigation on 12 cancer types where high-quality, matched spatial data were available. In total, we analyzed 89 spatial slices derived from 78 patients (details in Supplementary Table S1). To ensure data quality, we strictly filtered for spatial slices with sufficient tissue coverage (> 500 spots per slice). To ensure the biological validity of the identified networks, we rigorously evaluated their consistency across patients. We defined network consistency based on the recurrence frequency of ceRNA interactions across independent spatial slices. As shown in Fig. 3A, the networks demonstrated high reproducibility in specific cancer types. For instance, in LUAD and BRCA, the majority of identified ceRNA pairs were recurrently detected in > 50% of the analyzed patients, indicating that these interactions represent stable regulatory mechanisms rather than patient-specific noise. For instance, the ceRNA axis SNHG6-BIRC5 was detected in multiple spatial transcriptomic slices of lung adenocarcinoma, and SNHG6-BIRC5 high-expression spots were enriched in tumor regions and spatially distinct from T- cell infiltrated areas (Fig. 3B–E). To rigorously quantify this spatial relationship, we performed a Nearest Neighbor analysis combined with permutation testing (n = 1,000). We observed a significant spatial segregation between SNHG6-BIRC5 hotspots and T-cells. Specifically, the mean Euclidean distance from SNHG6-BIRC5 spots to the nearest T- cell was significantly larger than the random background distribution in analyzed slices (P = 0.008 and P = 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3F–G). These quantitative results confirm that the SNHG6-BIRC5 axis operates in distinct tumor niches, spatially segregated from immune surveillance.

Immunology ceRNAs are correlated with immune cell infiltration and immune-related biological functions

Given the complex composition of the tumor microenvironment, we reasoned that these ImmCeRNAs might regulate immune infiltration through two distinct mechanisms: (1) Tumor-Intrinsic Regulation: expressed by tumor cells to modulate immune infiltration; or (2) Immune-Intrinsic Regulation: directly expressed by infiltrating immune cells to mediate their function. To evaluate the level of immune cell infiltration in each patient, we relied on gene expression data and employed multiple algorithms. Subsequently, we examined the associations between the expression of ImmCeRNAs and the infiltration levels of twelve types of immune cells. As a result, we found that in most of the 26 cancers, the ImmCeRNAs were found to be associated with multiple immune cell infiltrations using several computational methods (Fig. 4A). Notably, 95.26% of ImmCeRNAs were linked to immune infiltration by at least two algorithms, with 24.59% them supported by all seven methods (Supplementary Fig. 3A). For instance, the majority of ceRNAs in renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) were associated with the immune infiltration of B cells (66.67%), CD4 + T cells (64.52%); CD8 + T cells (64.52%), and Treg cells (80.65%) (Fig. 4B). We found that the LINC01140-MIR429-MALT1 were significantly associated with Treg cell infiltration across four cancer types (Fig. 4C). LINC01140 and its target gene MALT1 exhibited a significantly positive expression correlation and significantly downregulated in tumor samples in COAD, and BRCA (Supplementary Fig. 3B–D). It has been reported that MALT1 is important for Treg activities, and loss-of-function mutations in MALT1 in both mice and humans lead to severe deficiencies in Treg cells[40]. To more precisely clarify the characteristics of ImmCeRNA in the immune microenvironment, we then stratified patients based on the level of immune cell infiltration and conducted a comparative analysis. We found that a significant proportion (97.67%) of ImmCeRNA was differentially expressed between the high and low immune cell infiltration groups across 26 cancer types (Fig. 4D). This widespread differential expression indicates that the ImmCeRNA regulatory network is highly sensitive to the tumor immune microenvironment. It suggests that these ceRNAs may actively shape the immunological phenotype, potentially distinguishing ‘immune-hot’ from ‘immune-cold’ tumors. For instance, in LUAD, 53.48% of the identified ImmCeRNAs showed significant differential expression between the high and low CD4 + T cell infiltration groups, 47.06% between those of CD8 + T cells, and 32.62% between those of Treg cells (Fig. 4D). To further dissect the specific interactions driving these patterns, we focused on the core oncogenic axis. The lncRNA SNHG6 and its target gene BIRC5 exhibited a negative correlation with the immune infiltration of CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and Treg cells in LUAD (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Specifically, the ImmCeRNA SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 was significantly upregulated in LUAD patients with low CD4 + T cell infiltration (Fig. 4E–F). This negative correlation, combined with its tumor-specific spatial localization, suggests that the SNHG6 axis functions primarily through a Tumor-Intrinsic mechanism to drive an immune-excluded phenotype. In summary, these findings indicate that ImmCeRNAs are likely associated with immune cell infiltration in cancer, thereby demonstrating their significant role in immunological regulation.

To further gain insights into the roles of ImmCeRNA in cancer, we explored the biological processes and pathways regulated by ImmCeRNA in each cancer. We found that ImmCeRNA regulates a wide range of immune-related biological functions, including B cell activation, differentiation, immune response, etc.[41](Supplementary Fig. 4A–B). ImmCeRNA can regulate T cell differentiation and proliferation in skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) (Supplementary Fig. 4B), a process whose dysregulation is implicated in melanoma progression[42]. Subsequently, we investigated the immune-related signaling pathways associated with ceRNAs in a pan-cancer context. Our analysis revealed that ImmCeRNA was significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including TNF signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway, and T cell receptor signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 4C). These immune pathways are observed across multiple cancer types. In LUAD, 8 ImmCeRNAs regulated antigen processing and presentation pathways by upregulating HSPA8, HSP90AA1, HSPA5 and CALR, and by downregulating HLA-DRB1, altering CD8 + T cell receptor signaling pathway and NK cell mediated cytotoxicity as well as CD4 + T cell receptor signaling pathway through MHC class I and class II signaling pathways. (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Previous work by Luo et al. indicated that these genes regulate immunity through MHC class I and II pathways, corroborating our observations regarding their dysregulation[43–45].

To systematically validate the functional dependency of immune-related ceRNA interactions, we first analyzed the public data of a genome-scale CRISPR knockout screen from DepMap. 141 ceRNA-related genes exhibited significantly lower CRISPR effect scores in high ceRNA- expression cell lines compared to low-expression groups (p < 0.05), indicating stronger negative impacts on cell viability when these genes were knocked out (Supplementary Table S2; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Additionally, we obtained 13 CRISPR RNA-seq datasets of ceRNA-related genes in K562 and HepG2 cell lines from the ENCODE project. Analysis of differentially expressed genes after ceRNA-related gene knockouts revealed significant enrichment in immune-related signaling pathways (e.g., T cell receptor signaling and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction; Supplementary Fig. 5B), further validating the immune regulatory roles of these ceRNA interactions.

Immunology ceRNAs are associated with drug sensitivity

To explore potential therapeutic strategies, we analyzed the correlation between the ImmCeRNA risk score and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of chemotherapeutic drugs. We employed a dual-screening strategy to identify candidate agents specifically effective for patients with high ceRNA risk scores (Supplementary Fig. 6A). First, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed to compare drug response differences between high- and low-risk groups. Second, Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess the linear association between the risk score and IC50 values. A lower IC50 value indicates higher drug sensitivity; thus, a negative correlation suggests that high-risk patients may benefit from the specific drug[46]. We identified that 2,069 ImmCeRNAs were associated with the sensitivities of 60 drugs in 26 cancers (Fig. 5A–B). In the majority of these cancers, ImmCeRNA was associated with the drug sensitivity of Bortezomib, Rapamycin, Dactinomycin, Camptothecin, and Vinblastine (Fig. 5C). This finding is consistent with results from other studies that have implicated ceRNAs in drug resistance mechanisms, which may involve influencing drug efflux, altering cell survival pathways, or modifying the tumor microenvironment[47, 48].

We further characterized the drug sensitivity landscape specifically within the LUAD cohort, identifying 11 ImmCeRNAs were associated with the sensitivity to nine drugs, including established cancer therapies and experimental anticancer agents such as Bortezomib, Buparlisib, and Vinblastine (Fig. 5D). Bortezomib, a potential optional drug for NSCLC patients, inhibits proteasomal degradation of cell cycle checkpoints, thereby blocking the NSCLC cell proliferation[49]. We found that the estimated IC50 values of Vinblastine were significantly lower in the high-risk group based on the ceRNA networks of SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 (Fig. 4E). We found that SNHG6 was highly expressed in LUAD tumor tissues compared to normal tissues and mediates the upregulation of BIRC5 expression through competitive binding to miR-26a1 (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Vinblastine’s clinical efficacy has been widely used in a variety of cancers and is a basic chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of lung cancer, especially advanced NSCLC [50]. Elevation of SNHG6 plays an important role in the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cancer cells. BIRC5 is a key component of the stem cells of lung cancers and glioma stem cells as a novel therapeutic target[51]. BIRC5 is associated with lung cancer progression and drug resistance[52]. Collectively, our findings suggest the SNHG6-miR-26a1-BIRC5 axis as a potential therapeutic target for drug treatment in LUAD.

Population-level analyses provide a generalized molecular landscape, but they often obscure critical inter-patient variations. Even with the same clinical diagnosis, patients can exhibit distinct tumor features driven by specific ceRNA regulations. Therefore, constructing patient-specific networks is essential for understanding tumor heterogeneity[53, 54]. In clinical practice, these individual networks provide a valuable tool for precision medicine. They enable researchers and clinicians to discover personalized prognostic markers and identify specific therapeutic drug targets for individual patients[55]. To facilitate this practical application, we constructed a patient-specific ceRNA regulatory and drug network for each of 9,290 patients in 27 cancers (as queried on the ImmCeRNA database website). To assess the population-level stability of these interactions, we analyzed the recurrence frequency of ceRNA pairs in individual patient networks. Consistent with the high molecular heterogeneity of LUAD, the majority of ceRNA interactions were observed in only a small fraction of the cohort (frequency < 0.5%) (Supplementary Fig. 6C). However, the SNHG6–MIR26A1–BIRC5 axis was identified as a recurrent driver event, present in 11.6% of the LUAD cohort. This recurrence rate may suggest that SNHG6-driven regulation characterizes a distinct molecular subpopulation of patients. To demonstrate the clinical utility of targeting this axis, we analyzed specific cases. For example, in LUAD patient TCGA-55-8621, we observed an association with Vinblastine sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 6D–E). We then constructed a patient-specific ceRNA network for TCGA-55-8621, which comprised seven ImmCeRNAs. Notably, several ImmCeRNAs in this network, including SNHG6-BIRC5, SCGB1B2P-AKT1, and AC092718.4-HSP90AA1, showed elevated expression in this patient (Supplementary Fig. 6F–G). Therefore, targeting these ceRNAs could reverse resistance and improve patient outcomes, offering a promising direction for future research and therapeutic development.

Immunology ceRNA could be a potential biomarker for cancer prognosis

A large number of studies elucidating how various components of the immune system control or promote cancer progression, thus revealing their prognostic value[56]. We considered whether these ImmCeRNA are associated with survival in cancer patients. To assess the clinical relevance of these ImmCeRNA, we integrated clinical data and then performed survival analyses for each ImmCeRNA in 27 cancer types (detailed in supplementary methods). We identified a total of 2312 (38.09%) survival-related ceRNAs, most of which were found in low-grade glioma (LGG,79%), KIRC (81%), and KICH (83%) (Fig. 6A). Most ImmCeRNAs were cancer-specific and were only associated with prognosis in one or two cancer types (Fig. 6B–C). Consistent with published literature, our analysis identified several ceRNA axes known to be associated with cancer progression. Examples include AGAP2-AS1-HDGF[57], KCNQ1OT1-ITGAV[58], and MALAT1-AR[59] (Fig. 6D). The unsatisfactory survival and high mortality rates of LUAD have made the identification of specific biomarkers for the diagnosis and treatment of LUAD patients a pressing issue[60]. We found that SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 were important factors affecting the survival of LUAD patients, and the high-risk group of LUAD patients was significantly associated with a poor prognosis (Fig. 6E). SNHG6-MIR26A1-BIRC5 may be risk factors for LUAD prognosis. Taken together, these results suggest that ImmCeRNA may be associated with patient survival and provide candidate therapeutic strategies for cancer.

Identification of ceRNAs associated with immunotherapy

To evaluate the broader clinical implications of ImmCeRNAs in predicting immunotherapy response, we expanded our analysis across diverse tumor microenvironments. Given the extensive heterogeneity of cancer, we hypothesized that the dominant regulatory mechanisms might vary significantly across different histologies. The rapid growth of cancer immunotherapy, single-cell RNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq datasets provides new opportunities to discover the nature of cancer immunotherapy and explore the mechanisms involved. We analyzed the results using two comparisons: responders (R) versus non-responders (NR) and post-treatment (T) versus pre-treatment (NT). We identified immunotherapy-related ImmCeRNA: ceRNAs expressed in bulk datasets in different immune conditions (R/NR or T/NT), and we performed Pearson correlation tests for lncRNA and mRNA expression in these samples. ceRNA relationships in these samples were retained only when the correlation coefficient was positive, and the P-value was less than 0.05. Across 24 bulk datasets and 15 single-cell datasets, which contain 1318 samples and 123,692 cells, we discovered a substantial number of ImmCeRNA that are associated with immunotherapy, ranging from 1 to 460(Fig. 7A–B). We found that in pan-cancer, the proportion of B- cells decreased after immunotherapy (Fig. 7B–C).

A major analytical challenge in single-cell transcriptomics is the inherent high dropout rate, particularly for low-abundance transcripts like lncRNAs. To ensure the robustness of our CSN-derived networks against data sparsity, we adopted a rigorous cross-validation strategy. Candidate ImmCeRNAs initially identified in scRNA-seq data were systematically evaluated using independent bulk RNA-seq cohorts. Through this integration, we identified ceRNAs present in both data types in all seven cancers (BLCA, BRCA, esophageal carcinoma [ESCA], LUAD, lung squamous cell carcinoma [LUSC], ovarian cancer [OV], and SKCM) (Fig. 7D). Some of these ImmCeRNAs were significantly differentially expressed in different response groups (R/NR) to immunotherapy, such as LINC00511-ZC3HAV1L, and LINC00511-PPIA (Supplementary Fig. 7A–B). We further explored differences in ceRNA networks between responders and non-responders to immunotherapy and found that LINC00511-ZC3HAV1L was only present in responders to immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 7C). Additional detailed examples of these bulk-validated ImmCeRNAs are provided in Supplementary Fig. 7D.

Building upon this pan-cancer landscape of immunotherapy-associated ceRNAs, we next sought to evaluate the clinical predictive utility of specific regulatory axes. Ideally, we aimed to validate the SNHG6 axis in LUAD immunotherapy cohorts. However, given the current scarcity of datasets with sufficient depth for reliable lncRNA detection, we implemented a cross-histology validation strategy by extending our analysis to LUSC. This cross-histology approach enabled us to evaluate the generalizability of the ImmCeRNA framework across distinct pathological subtypes of NSCLC. We identified immunotherapy-associated ceRNA (LINC00511-PPIA) in both the bulk dataset GSE91061 and the single-cell dataset GSE207422 for LUSC. In the bulk dataset GSE91061, lncRNA LINC00511 and target gene PPIA showed positive correlation (Fig. 7E). Both LINC00511 and PPIA were lowly expressed in the responders relative to the non-responders (Fig. 7F), suggesting a potential role in therapeutic resistance. In the single-cell dataset GSE207422, lung squamous carcinoma cells were categorized into 10 cell types, including T cell, B cell, NK cell, neutrophil, and macrophage (Fig. 7G). Consistent with previous studies, neutrophils were the most abundant immune cell type in squamous lung cancer[61] (Fig. 7H). In contrast to the tumor-intrinsic SNHG6 axis in LUAD, single-cell analysis in LUSC revealed an immune-intrinsic mechanism: The LINC00511-PPIA was predominantly present in neutrophils (Fig. 7I), the most abundant white blood cells in human blood, which play a key role in tumorigenesis, cancer progression, and immunosuppression in squamous lung cancer[61, 62]. This highlights a distinct mode of regulation where ceRNAs within infiltrating immune cells directly modulate the therapeutic response. Consistent with the bulk RNA-seq findings, LINC00511-PPIA was also present in large numbers in the NR group (Fig. 7J,K), reinforcing its role as a potential driver of therapeutic resistance. As a key ImmCeRNA, LINC00511-PPIA underwent rigorous validation through bulk data, confirming its robustness and relevance. For further detailed examples of validated ImmCeRNAs, refer to Supplementary Fig. 6D. To gain an in-depth understanding of the dynamic changes of the cell-specific ceRNA network during immune cell differentiation, a cell development trajectory was constructed. We performed trajectory analysis on T cells, B cells, and NK cells in the GSE207422 dataset and explored the role of LINC00511-PPIA in the developmental trajectories of different immune cells (Supplementary Fig. 7E–G). We found that LINC00511-PPIA exhibits high expression at the early stage of the T cell developmental trajectory, indicating distinct roles in T cell differentiation. Conversely, this ceRNA axis shows high expression during the late-stage differentiation of B and NK cells, revealing cell- type-specific ceRNA regulation across immune cell differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 7F).

In addition, we found that T cells in LUSC are not homogenous and may play different roles in cancer therapy (Fig. 7L). Dividing T cells into immunotherapy-responsive (T_R) and immunotherapy-unresponsive groups (T_NR), we identified 6 ImmCeRNA that were significantly expressed in the T_R cells (Fig. 7M; Supplementary Fig. 8A). Previous studies have shown that LINC00511_HSPA8 and AC027031.2_HSPA1B may regulate T cells to affect tumor immunity. Li et al. observed increased expression of HSPA1B in T cells from R (vs. NR) tumors after anti-PD-1 treatment[63]. High HSPA1B expression in T cells may exert anti-tumor effects[64]. Furthermore, we explored two states of T cells and found that XIST and IL7R were significantly highly expressed in T_R cells and that T_R cells significantly regulate immune-related biological processes and pathways (Supplementary Fig. 8B–D). Taken together, our results reveal the alteration of the immune microenvironment during immunotherapy and contribute to the understanding of the role of ImmCeRNA in immunotherapy, providing valuable indicators of immunotherapeutic response during treatment.

Development and validation of a pan-cancer prediction model based on XGBoost

The distinct mechanisms identified in LUAD and LUSC underscore a critical clinical challenge: single biomarkers are often insufficient for pan-cancer prediction due to heterogeneity. Therefore, we developed an XGBoost-based prediction model for pan-cancer immunotherapy response by training ceRNA network features instead of predefining them (see Supplementary Materials and Methods; Supplementary Table S3). We implemented a rigorous feature selection and validation pipeline integrated within a stratified fivefold cross-validation framework. In each fold, the data was first partitioned into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%). Feature selection was then performed exclusively on the training set: each candidate ImmCeRNA regulatory pair was individually evaluated for its predictive ability to distinguish immunotherapy responders from non-responders. The predictive performance of each candidate ImmCeRNA was assessed by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). Only ImmCeRNAs with an AUC greater than 0.7 within that specific training fold were retained as features for that fold. This process was repeated independently across all five folds. The top five most important features consistently identified by XGBoost model were OIP5-AS1-OSMR, MSC-AS1-AVPR1A, AGAP2-AS1-SEMA4C, KCNQ1OT1-IGF1R, and MAPKAPK5-AS1-TMSB10. These ceRNAs that were most frequently selected across all folds (all with AUC > 0.7) were used define the final ImmCeRNA signature (Fig. 8A). Key hyperparameters, including a learning rate of 0.1 and a maximum tree depth of 7, were optimized within the cross-validation framework to enhance generalizability. We evaluated the performance of the ImmCeRNA signature by comparing it with existing biomarkers. The ImmCeRNA signature alone achieved an AUC of 0.737, while combining it with PD-L1 (AUC = 0.758) further enhanced predictive performance, reaching an AUC of 0.771 (Fig. 8B). To validate stability against measurement noise, we subjected the training data to Gaussian perturbation consistent with batch-induced variability[65]. The model retained stable performance (cross-validated AUC > 0.6) across all tested noise intensities (Fig. 8C), indicating high robustness to potential technical artifacts in bulk RNA-seq data. In addition, we developed a user-friendly data portal, ImmCeRNA (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/ImmCeRNA or https://ImmCeRNA.bio-database.com/index.html), to facilitate visualization, searching, and browsing of ImmCeRNA data by the biomedical research community (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive pan-cancer landscape of potential ImmCeRNAs by integrating ImmLnc and lncACTdb methods. We demonstrated that ImmCeRNAs exhibit significant expression perturbations in tumors, correlating with immune cell infiltration, and influence immunotherapy responses, thereby serving as potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. A key insight from our analysis is the extensive molecular heterogeneity of ceRNA regulation. The contrast between the SNHG6-driven exclusion in LUAD and the LINC00511-driven neutrophil suppression in LUSC underscores the extensive heterogeneity of NSCLC, necessitating histology-specific therapeutic strategies. Given this heterogeneity, single subtype-specific biomarkers are insufficient for pan-cancer prediction. Our XGBoost model addresses this by selecting broad-spectrum features (e.g., OIP5-AS1) that differ from the subtype-specific mechanistic drivers (SNHG6, LINC00511). This distinction reflects a multi-layered framework: SNHG6/LINC00511 serve as high-precision mechanistic targets within specific niches, whereas OIP5-AS1 functions as a robust generalizer for pan-cancer prediction.

To ensure the biological validity of these findings, we addressed the challenge of distinguishing functional miRNA sponging from transcriptional co-regulation through a rigorous two-step validation. First, regarding stoichiometry, analysis of absolute expression confirmed that SNHG6 is highly abundant in LUAD (Median TPM = 77.6), satisfying the kinetic requirement for effective miRNA sequestration (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Second, to rule out the co-regulation hypothesis, we investigated HOXA1, a shared transcription factor identified via the KnockTF 2.0 database (Supplementary Fig. 10B). Partial correlation analysis demonstrated that the SNHG6–BIRC5 association remains robust (R = 0.358, P < 0.001) even after adjusting for HOXA1 expression, confirming that the observed co-expression is primarily driven by post-transcriptional ceRNA crosstalk rather than transcriptional confounding. Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis to balance statistical stringency with biological discovery. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10C–E, applying overly strict correlation cutoffs (e.g., R > 0.3) resulted in drastic network fragmentation and the loss of biologically relevant signals. Consequently, we adopted a “dual-screening” strategy: using a baseline threshold (R > 0) to capture subtle fine-tuning signals diluted by tissue heterogeneity, while ensuring specificity by strictly restricting the network to experimentally validated interactions (StarBase/miRTarBase). This approach filters computational artifacts while retaining a comprehensive landscape, as validated by the robust retention of the core SNHG6–BIRC5 axis (R = 0.387) even under stricter criteria.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, regarding the spatial transcriptomics analysis, our investigation was limited to 12 cancer types due to the inherent sparsity of spatial data and the high “dropout” rate of lncRNAs compared to protein-coding genes. Future improvements in spatial sequencing depth will be required to extend this landscape. Second, we candidly acknowledge that transitioning from LUAD to LUSC for immunotherapy validation was driven by data availability rather than our initial design. The current scarcity of high-depth LUAD immunotherapy cohorts preclude direct clinical validation of the tumor-intrinsic SNHG6 axis. Nevertheless, this strategic pivot critically validated our framework: identifying the neutrophil-specific LINC00511–PPIA axis in LUSC demonstrated the versatility of the ImmCeRNA approach across distinct NSCLC histologies. Future histology-matched cohorts are essential to directly validate the SNHG6 axis in LUAD. Third, while we prioritized robustly detected transcripts to mitigate scRNA-seq dropout rates, some low-abundance interactions may remain underestimated.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the functional and regulatory roles of ceRNAs in immunomodulation and immunotherapy. It is important to understand the function of ImmCeRNA in cancer. The identification of potential ceRNAs is a first step towards immunotherapy. Ongoing research on the ImmCeRNAs identified here will significantly enhance our understanding of cancer mechanisms and improve immunotherapeutic strategies.
