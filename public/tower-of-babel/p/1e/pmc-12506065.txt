Introduction

Cerebral atherosclerosis significantly contributes to the global incidence of vascular stenosis, stroke, cerebral small vessel disease and other cerebrovascular diseases [1–3]. It is marked by the build-up of lipid-rich plaques within cerebral artery walls, causing vascular narrowing, reduced blood flow, and a heightened risk of ischemic events. The World Health Organization identifies cerebrovascular diseases, including those driven by atherosclerosis, as leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Grasping the mechanisms behind cerebral atherosclerosis is essential for developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies.

The development of cerebral atherosclerosis involves a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Inflammation is crucial in both the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules attract leukocytes to the vascular endothelium, fostering plaque formation and instability, and inducing secretion of inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-12, and IL-6 from Macrophages and smooth muscle cells [4]. Recent studies underscore the significance of endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress in amplifying vascular inflammation and atherogenesis. In addition, there are some non-modifiable anatomical factors, and the study found that larger carotid bifurcation angles are also associated with cerebral atherosclerosis [5].

Advanced imaging techniques have provided detailed insights into atherosclerotic plaques, revealing their composition and susceptibility to rupture. High-resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography angiography (CTA) are invaluable for assessing plaque burden and tracking disease progression [6]. These imaging modalities also help identify high-risk plaques that are prone to rupture, leading to acute ischemic events.

Despite progress in understanding cerebral atherosclerosis, effective treatment remains challenging. Current therapies focus on managing risk factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus [7, 8]. Statins, antiplatelet agents, and lifestyle changes are the cornerstones of medical management. However, there is an increasing demand for novel therapies targeting the fundamental mechanisms of plaque formation and progression.

Emerging research is investigating the potential of anti-inflammatory agents, antioxidants, and lipid-modifying therapies to mitigate cerebral atherosclerosis. Additionally, advancements in molecular biology and genomics are paving the way for personalized medicine, allowing treatment to be tailored to individual genetic profiles and disease characteristics.

In conclusion, cerebral atherosclerosis presents a significant global health challenge. Ongoing research into its pathophysiological mechanisms is critical for developing innovative and effective interventions. Enhanced understanding and management of this condition will ultimately improve patient outcomes and reduce the prevalence of cerebrovascular diseases.

The role of genetic factors in cerebral atherosclerosis

Cerebral atherosclerosis, a leading cause of stroke and other cerebrovascular diseases, is increasingly recognized as having a significant genetic component. Genetic predispositions can influence the development and progression of atherosclerotic plaques in cerebral arteries, highlighting the necessity for understanding these genetic factors in disease mechanisms. Previous studies have elucidated a significant association between variants in genes such as adiponectin Q (ADIPOQ), ring finger protein 213 (RNF213), apolipoprotein E (APOE), phosphodiesterase 4D (PDE4D), methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR), lipoprotein lipase (LPL), α-adducin (ADD1), and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and the pathogenesis of intracranial atherosclerosis [5]. These genetic elements exert their influence on the development and progression of the disease through various mechanisms, including the regulation of serum adiponectin levels, vascular fragility, high low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels, degradation of cAMP, metabolism and transport of plasma lipoproteins, as well as vascular remodeling and development. However, recent years have seen novel findings in the investigation of genetic factors contributing to intracranial atherosclerosis. This review summarizes the latest research findings on genetic factors related to atherosclerosis as follows.

APOE4 mediates lipid transport, receptor binding and metabolism to regulate atherosclerosis

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is a protein that plays a critical role in the transport and metabolism of lipids in the body. There are several isoforms of APOE, with APOE4 being one of the three major alleles (APOE2, APOE3, and APOE4) of the APOE gene found in humans. The function of APOE4 mainly includes lipid transport、lipoprotein receptor binding、modulation of lipid metabolism, which was recognized as the key risk factor of carotid atherosclerosis. However, the relationship between APOE4 and cerebral atherosclerosis need further studies. Recent studies report no correlation between APOE4 and cerebral atherosclerosis based on population-based autopsy studies and suggest that APOE4 may play a key role in the management of lipid metabolism in the early stage of cerebral atherosclerosis development rather than the late stage [9]. However, this requires further basic and clinical experiments for verification.

Mutations in the RNF213 gene are associated with cerebral atherosclerosis

The RNF213 gene has emerged as a significant player in the pathogenesis of intracranial atherosclerosis, with recent studies elucidating its role in the disease process. Research progress indicates that genetic variants of RNF213 are implicated in the susceptibility and development of cerebral atherosclerotic lesions. Zhou H et al. analyzed data from the third Chinese National Stroke Registry, which included a total of 10,381 patients, by comparing carriers and non-carriers of the RNF213 gene p.R4810K mutation, they found that the RNF213 p.R4810K mutation was associated with large-artery atherosclerosis and anterior intracranial circulation artery stenosis in Chinese patients [10]. Okazaki S et al. discovered through following up 52 patients with intracranial artery stenosis (with a median of 10.3 years) that patients carrying the RNF213 gene p.R4810K mutation were significantly correlated with the progression time of intracranial artery stenosis and the combined endpoint time of symptomatic stroke and transient ischemic attack, and it is an important risk factor for cerebral atherosclerosis [11]. However, the mechanism of RNF213 p.R4810K mutation in cerebral atherosclerosis remains unclear.

NTNG1 gene modulates the homeostasis of blood cholesterol

The NTNG1 gene (Netrin G1), encodes a protein that is part of the netrin family. Netrins are guidance proteins that play a crucial role in the development of the nervous system. The NTNG1 protein, expressed by the NTNG1 gene, is essential in axon guidance, neuronal migration, and synapse formation. Previous research has found that the NTNG1 gene is primarily associated with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), schizophrenia, and neurodevelopmental diseases. However, recent studies analyzing 1,325 participants of European descent with cerebral atherosclerosis have identified new loci of the NTNG1 gene linked to cerebral atherosclerosis, including rs6664221 and rs10881463. These gene loci may influence the homeostasis of blood cholesterol by regulating the brain protein expression of the CNOT3 gene, thereby increasing the risk of cerebral atherosclerosis [12]. At present, there are no more reports on the role of NTNG1 gene in cerebral atherosclerosis, and larger clinical samples and basic studies are still needed to explore the role and mechanism of NTNG1 in this disease.

CYP2C19 and the regulation of drug metabolism in the treatment of stroke associated with atherosclerosis

The CYP2C19 gene is a member of the cytochrome P450 family and encodes a crucial enzyme known as cytochrome P450 2C19. This enzyme plays an important role in the liver, participating in the metabolism of various drugs, including antidepressants, antiepileptics, proton pump inhibitors, and antiplatelet agents (such as clopidogrel). A recent study by Patel PD et al. found that, for patients with intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) treated with clopidogrel following a transient ischemic attack (TIA), the loss of CYP2C19 function increased the risk of first ischemic stroke by three times [13]. However, there is currently no evidence to suggest that CYP2C19 genotype is associated with aspirin resistance. Therefore, the findings of the aforementioned study provide a genotypic basis for selecting the type of antiplatelet medication, which may improve stroke prevention in patients with ICAD following a TIA.

The genetic associations, mutation sites, mechanisms of action, and downstream gene targets have been identified for some of these genes, but further animal studies are needed to explore and validate these findings.

Advances in understanding the role of sex in cerebral atherosclerosis

Sex differences play a crucial role in the pathophysiology and progression of cerebral atherosclerosis. Epidemiological studies have consistently shown that men have a higher prevalence of atherosclerotic plaques in cerebral arteries compared to women, particularly before the age of 65 [14]. However, the incidence in women increases significantly post-menopause, suggesting that hormonal differences may significantly influence the disease process [15].

Estrogen is often believed to have protective effects against atherosclerosis. It was recognized to enhance endothelial function by promoting nitric oxide production and reducing oxidative stress, which helps maintain vascular homeostasis. Additionally, estrogen modulates lipid metabolism, decreasing LDL cholesterol levels and increasing high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels, thus mitigating atherosclerotic plaque formation. The decline in estrogen levels during menopause is associated with an increased risk of atherosclerosis in women, highlighting the hormone’s protective role. However, the latest research has yielded opposite conclusions. By comparing 701 postmenopausal women, it was found that the time of menopause is not an independent risk factor for intracranial atherosclerosis [16]. Some studies have even reported that endogenous estrogen levels are associated with endothelial function, but not with lipid levels [17]. Therefore, further studies with larger sample sizes are required to elucidate the impact of estrogen levels on the extent of intracranial atherosclerotic lesions and its association with menopause.

In contrast, testosterone’s role in atherosclerosis is more complex. Some studies suggest that testosterone may have protective effects by promoting endothelial repair and reducing inflammation such as IL-6 [18]. Similar studies exploring the relationship between serum testosterone and inflammation, as well as lipid levels, have found that reduced serum testosterone concentrations can promote the inflammatory process by decreasing the inhibition of inflammatory cytokine synthesis, but this is unrelated to lipid levels. This may contribute to aging and enhance the production of acute-phase proteins, suggesting that low serum testosterone concentrations may be an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis [19]. These studies indicate that the mechanisms by which testosterone and estrogen affect cerebral atherosclerosis are not entirely identical. In the treatment of this condition, besides considering the influence of endocrine hormone levels, it is also necessary to control lipid levels independently. A multifaceted intervention approach is the optimal choice for the prevention and treatment of cerebral atherosclerosis.

The latest research utilized high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging to explore the impact of gender and age on intracranial atherosclerotic plaques of undetermined origin, enrolling a total of 243 patients [14]. The findings revealed that in the 60–74 age group, males were associated with complex plaques, while for subjects aged ≥ 75 years, females had a significantly higher plaque burden compared to males. Additionally, the probability of vulnerable plaques increased with age, and their predictive power for intracranial plaques of undetermined origin was higher in males than in females. This study identifies the presence of age-related gender differences in intracranial atherosclerosis. Gender differences in cerebral atherosclerosis have been noted in previous studies, but the precise characteristics have not yet been fully elucidated. The latest research collected and included 573 patients with asymptomatic cerebral atherosclerotic stenosis (287 males), and a comparative analysis revealed a higher number of male patients than females, with a higher proportion of severe stenosis in males, in contrast, females had higher rates of anterior circulation artery stenosis and intracranial artery stenosis compared to males [20]. This study suggests that there are gender differences in the distribution, severity, and pattern of change with age in cerebral atherosclerotic stenosis. Therefore, specific management of cerebral atherosclerosis in patients of different genders is warranted.

Lifestyle factors, including diet, physical activity, and smoking, also interact with sex to influence cerebral atherosclerosis risk. Women may experience different protective or harmful effects from these factors compared to men due to biological differences and varying responses to environmental influences. Understanding these interactions is essential for developing tailored prevention and treatment strategies.

In summary, sex plays a significant role in the mechanisms underlying cerebral atherosclerosis, influenced by hormonal, genetic, and lifestyle factors. Recognizing and addressing these differences is crucial for improving prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies tailored to both men and women.

Advances in understanding the role of diet in cerebral atherosclerosis

Diet plays a critical role in the development and progression of cerebral atherosclerosis. Numerous studies have shown that dietary patterns significantly influence the risk of atherosclerotic disease in cerebral arteries. Diets high in saturated fats and trans fats are associated with increased levels of LDL cholesterol, which is a major risk factor for atherosclerosis. Conversely, diets rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats are linked to lower LDL cholesterol levels and a reduced risk of plaque formation.

The Mediterranean diet, characterized by high consumption of olive oil, nuts, fruits, vegetables, and fish, has been extensively studied for its cardiovascular benefits. This diet has been shown to reduce the risk of stroke and other cerebrovascular diseases by improving lipid profiles, reducing inflammation, and enhancing endothelial function [21]. Similarly, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins, has been found to lower blood pressure and reduce the risk of atherosclerosis [22]. However, a recent study involving 912 participants (with an average age of 64 ± 8 years, 59% female, 65% Hispanic, and an average Mediterranean Diet Score of 4 ± 1.5) found that an increased Mediterranean Diet Score was inversely associated with intracranial atherosclerosis, though not statistically significant [23]. Therefore, the relationship between the Mediterranean diet and intracranial atherosclerosis requires more rigorous statistical power and a larger sample size to explore its specific mechanisms of action.

Omega-3 fatty acids, commonly found in fish and flaxseeds, have anti-inflammatory properties and are known to decrease the risk of atherosclerosis by reducing triglyceride levels and pro-inflammatory eicosanoids as well as inhibit the development of atherosclerotic plaques [24, 25]. Thus, regular consumption of these fatty acids is associated with improved cerebrovascular health and a lower incidence of stroke.

Antioxidants, present in high quantities in fruits and vegetables, also play a protective role against cerebral atherosclerosis. These compounds help reduce oxidative stress, a key factor in the development of atherosclerotic plaques [26, 27]. Studies have shown that diets high in antioxidants are linked to a lower risk of cerebrovascular events.

Dietary fiber, found in whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, has been shown to reduce LDL cholesterol levels and improve overall cardiovascular health [28, 29]. High fiber intake is associated with a decreased risk of stroke and other forms of atherosclerosis.

Furthermore, excessive sodium intake is linked to hypertension, which is a significant risk factor for cerebral atherosclerosis [22]. Thus, reducing sodium consumption may help lower blood pressure and subsequently reduce the risk of atherosclerotic disease, which need further study and analysis.

In conclusion, diet plays a pivotal role in the mechanisms underlying cerebral atherosclerosis. Adopting healthy dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean and DASH diets, incorporating omega-3 fatty acids, antioxidants, and dietary fiber, while reducing saturated fats, trans fats, and sodium intake, can significantly lower the risk of cerebral atherosclerosis and improve cerebrovascular health (Fig. 2).

Role and mechanism of diet in cerebral atherosclerosis

The components in the diet can affect the levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), thereby promoting or inhibiting the process of atherosclerosis. A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy lipids can effectively reduce the content of LDL. This beneficial dietary pattern is typically represented by the Mediterranean diet and the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet. It can not only help improve the lipid profiles but also reduce the inflammatory response in the body and enhance the function of the vascular endothelium.

Advances in understanding the role of physical activity in cerebral atherosclerosis

Physical activity is widely recognized for its protective role against various cardiovascular diseases, including cerebral atherosclerosis. Regular exercise has been shown to improve endothelial function, reduce arterial stiffness, and enhance cerebral blood flow, all of which are critical factors in mitigating the progression of atherosclerosis in cerebral arteries [30, 31].

One of the primary mechanisms by which physical activity exerts its beneficial effects is through the modulation of lipid profiles. Exercise increases HDL cholesterol levels while reducing LDL and triglycerides, thus lowering the risk of atherosclerotic plaque formation [31]. Additionally, regular physical activity reduces systemic inflammation, as evidenced by decreased levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [32].

Aerobic exercise, in particular, has been extensively studied for its cardiovascular benefits. Studies have shown that engaging in moderate to vigorous aerobic activities, such as running, cycling, and swimming, can significantly reduce the incidence of stroke and other cerebrovascular events [33]. The underlying mechanisms include improved insulin sensitivity, reduced blood pressure, and enhanced autonomic regulation of the heart.

Resistance training also contributes to cardiovascular health by improving muscle strength and endurance, and inhibiting pro-inflammation factors, which in turn supports better overall physical functioning and metabolic health [34, 35]. Combining aerobic and resistance training appears to offer synergistic benefits, further reducing the risk of cerebral atherosclerosis and improving vascular health.

Moreover, physical activity positively impacts neurovascular health by promoting neurogenesis, enhancing synaptic plasticity, and improving cognitive function. These neuroprotective effects are particularly important in preventing the cognitive decline often associated with cerebral atherosclerosis [36, 37]. Exercise-induced increases in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) play a crucial role in these neuroprotective mechanisms [38].

In conclusion, regular physical activity plays a pivotal role in the prevention and management of cerebral atherosclerosis through multiple mechanisms, including improved lipid profiles, reduced inflammation, enhanced endothelial function, and neurovascular protection (Fig. 3). Incorporating both aerobic and resistance training into daily routines is recommended to maximize cardiovascular and cerebrovascular benefits.

Comorbidities and cerebral atherosclerosis: advances in research

Cerebral atherosclerosis, a leading cause of stroke and other cerebrovascular diseases, is significantly influenced by various comorbidities. Understanding the role of these underlying health conditions is essential for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Hypertension is one of the most critical comorbidities contributing to the development and progression of cerebral atherosclerosis. Chronic high blood pressure damages the endothelial lining of blood vessels, promoting atherosclerotic plaque formation and increasing the risk of cerebrovascular events and even dementia [39, 40]. Similarly, cerebral atherosclerosis can also increase the death rate of hypertension [41]. Effective management of hypertension is thus crucial in mitigating cerebral atherosclerosis.

Diabetes mellitus is another major risk factor for cerebral atherosclerosis [42]. Hyperglycemia leads to endothelial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and inflammation, all of which accelerate atherosclerotic processes in cerebral arteries [43]. Tight glycemic control and diabetes management are vital in reducing the burden of atherosclerosis. But it need further research about the correlation between diabetes mellitus and cerebral atherosclerosis.

Dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated levels of LDL cholesterol and decreased levels of HDL cholesterol, significantly contributes to atherosclerosis development [44, 45]. Abnormal lipid profiles promote lipid accumulation in arterial walls, leading to plaque formation and arterial stenosis. Lipid-lowering therapies are therefore essential in the management of cerebral atherosclerosis. However, in recent years, there has been a lack of studies on the role of lipids in intracranial atherosclerosis, with few valuable research reports available. Further studies are still needed to dissect the role and mechanisms of lipids in the formation and progression of intracranial atherosclerosis to provide more references for guiding treatment.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) also plays a significant role in cerebral atherosclerosis. The latest study included 1,762 patients with chronic kidney disease and found an association between a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and the presence of intracranial atherosclerotic plaques [46]. This indicates that a decline in renal function or increased kidney injury is related to intracranial atherosclerosis, although the specific mechanism remains unclear. Thus, CKD may be associated with increased vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation, all of which exacerbate atherosclerotic processes in cerebral arteries. Addressing CKD through appropriate treatment and management may help in reducing cerebral atherosclerosis risk.

Metabolic syndrome, a cluster of conditions including hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and central obesity, is strongly linked to the development of cerebral atherosclerosis. A recent study enrolled 704 patients with intracranial atherosclerosis. Compared to the metabolically healthy normal weight group, the metabolically unhealthy normal weight group had a higher risk of intracranial atherosclerosis, indicating a significant association between metabolically unhealthy normal weight and an increased risk of intracranial atherosclerosis [47]. Thus, the interplay of these risk factors creates a pro-atherogenic environment, highlighting the need for comprehensive management of metabolic syndrome to prevent cerebrovascular diseases.

Furthermore, systemic inflammatory conditions such as Periodontitis have been associated with an increased risk of cerebral atherosclerosis. A cross-sectional study involving 61 patients with periodontitis revealed that compared to subjects without periodontitis, those with periodontitis exhibited significantly higher pulsatility and resistance in the middle cerebral artery [48]. The relationship between periodontitis and intracranial atherosclerosis implies that patients with periodontitis may have a higher likelihood of developing ischemic stroke in the future [49]. Thus, chronic inflammation in these diseases promotes endothelial damage and plaque formation, underscoring the importance of controlling systemic inflammation in reducing cerebral atherosclerosis.

In conclusion, comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, metabolic syndrome, and systemic inflammatory conditions play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of cerebral atherosclerosis (Table 1).

Inflammation and cerebral atherosclerosis: advances in research

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of cerebral atherosclerosis, influencing disease progression and outcomes through multifaceted mechanisms. Chronic inflammation contributes significantly to the initiation and propagation of atherosclerotic plaques within the cerebral vasculature. Inflammatory processes promote endothelial dysfunction, facilitating the infiltration of leukocytes into the arterial wall and promoting the formation of foam cells, key components of atherosclerotic lesions.

Additionally, inflammation induces oxidative stress, leading to the modification of lipoproteins and promoting their uptake by macrophages, further exacerbating plaque formation and progression. Moreover, inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) stimulate vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration, contributing to the development of fibrous caps and vulnerable plaques [50, 51]. These processes collectively contribute to the instability of atherosclerotic lesions and increase the risk of thrombosis and ischemic events in the brain.

Recent studies have elucidated the role of specific inflammatory pathways in cerebral atherosclerosis, highlighting potential therapeutic targets aimed at mitigating inflammation-mediated vascular damage [52]. Strategies targeting inflammatory cytokines, such as monoclonal antibodies against IL-6 and TNF-α receptors, have shown promise in preclinical models and warrant further investigation in clinical settings. Furthermore, lifestyle modifications, including dietary interventions and exercise, have been shown to modulate inflammatory markers and reduce the risk of cerebral atherosclerosis.

In conclusion, understanding the intricate interplay between inflammation and cerebral atherosclerosis is crucial for developing effective preventive and therapeutic strategies (Fig. 4). Targeting inflammatory pathways holds significant promise for mitigating disease progression and improving outcomes in patients at risk for or affected by cerebral atherosclerosis. However, it is worth noting that there is currently relatively little research on the relationship between inflammation and intracranial atherosclerosis, with more evidence coming from the relationship between inflammation and extra-cranial artery diseases. Due to the different vascular structures of intracranial vessels compared to extra-cranial vessels, more research is needed to clarify the role of inflammation in the process of intracranial atherosclerosis.

Oxidative stress in cerebral atherosclerosis: mechanisms and the role of vascular endothelial cells​

Oxidative stress, a phenomenon caused by an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the capacity of antioxidant defense systems, is a key driver in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, including its cerebral manifestations [53]. In vascular biology, ROS such as superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radical are generated via enzymatic pathways (e.g., NADPH oxidase, xanthine oxidase) and mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly in response to pathogenic stimuli such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), hypertension, and chronic inflammation [54–56]. When ROS levels exceed the capacity of antioxidant defenses, oxidative imbalance ensues, leading to the development of oxidative stress.

Vascular endothelial cells (VECs) play a critical role in mediating the effects of oxidative stress on atherosclerotic progression. Under normal physiological conditions, VECs maintain vascular homeostasis by producing nitric oxide (NO), which exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects [57]. However, excessive ROS disrupt this balance by inhibiting NO production, inducing oxidative stress, and subsequently activating VECs. This activation up-regulates the expression of adhesion molecules (e.g., VCAM-1, ICAM-1) and chemokines (e.g., MCP-1), promoting sustained recruitment and migration of leukocytes to the intima, exacerbating vascular inflammation, and accelerating atherosclerotic plaque formation and instability [58–60]. Concurrently, oxidative stress disrupts the balance between apoptosis and proliferation of VECs by mediating their programmed cell death, further impairing vascular structure and increasing the risk of vascular diseases [61]. Studies have identified oxidative stress in VECs as an early marker of atherosclerosis, while reduced levels of antioxidant biomarkers (total antioxidant status, total thiols, and native thiols) are recognized as risk indicators for the disease [62]. Additionally, Yuan Ma et al. utilized photoacoustic imaging and urine analysis to assess early atherosclerosis based on end-stage oxidative stress metabolites (intraplaque and urinary malondialdehyde), confirming that these metabolites serve as reliable evidence for the auxiliary diagnosis of atherosclerosis [53]. Recent studies have deepened our understanding of these mechanisms. Das AA et al., using a murine model of cerebral atherosclerosis, demonstrated that ROS produced by NADPH oxidase in VECs promote macrophage uptake of ox-LDL by upregulating scavenger receptors, directly linking endothelial oxidative stress to lipid retention in the cerebral vascular intima. Targeting endothelial antioxidant pathways (e.g., Nrf2/HO-1) alleviated oxidative stress, preserved endothelial function, and reduced the burden of cerebral atherosclerotic lesions, highlighting the therapeutic potential of modulating oxidative stress in VECs for cerebral atherosclerosis [63].

In summary, oxidative stress, through its impact on the function and structure of vascular endothelial cells, emerges as a crucial therapeutic target in cerebral atherosclerosis. In the future, intervening in the oxidative stress of vascular endothelial cells may be one of the important directions for treating cerebral atherosclerosis (Fig. 5).

The role and mechanism of oxidative stress in atherosclerosis

Oxidative stress arises from excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Generated via enzymatic pathways and mitochondrial dysfunction in response to stimuli such as ox-LDL, hypertension, and inflammation, ROS inhibits nitric oxide (NO) production, activates vascular endothelial cells (VECs), and upregulates adhesion molecules and chemokines, thereby promoting plaque formation.

Proteomic landscapes and metabolic dysregulation in cerebral atherosclerosis: cross-talks and disease relevance

Proteomic landscapes and metabolic networks are increasingly recognized as pivotal in unraveling the pathogenesis of cerebral atherosclerosis. This section explores how proteomic signatures reveal shared and distinct mechanisms compared to coronary atherosclerosis, while dissecting metabolic dysregulation as a key driver, highlighting their cross-talk and clinical relevance.

Proteomic insights into cerebral atherosclerosis: shared and distinct signatures with coronary atherosclerosis

In proteomics, Wingo et al. performed proteomic sequencing on the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of 438 older adults, linking cerebral atherosclerosis to reduced synaptic signaling, altered RNA splicing, and enhanced oligodendrocyte development/myelination [64]. Overlapping proteins/modules with Alzheimer’s disease suggest shared mechanisms. Specific proteins like phosphoglycerate mutase 5 (PGAM5) are also implicated: Jia et al. found high PGAM5 expression in atherosclerotic plaque macrophages (positively correlated with lesion severity), which regulates angiopoietin-like protein 3 (ANGPTL3) to mediate intercellular communication—likely relevant to cerebral atherosclerosis progression [65].

These studies advance understanding, yet gaps remain in comprehensive proteomic comparisons with coronary atherosclerosis. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multi-omics integration to identify disease-specific signatures, aiding improved diagnostics and therapeutics.

Metabolic basis of cerebral atherosclerosis: key pathways and systemic connections

The metabolic basis of cerebral atherosclerosis is complex and intertwined with various systemic factors. Emerging research has spotlighted several key metabolic pathways that play crucial roles in its pathogenesis.

Lipid metabolism is a cornerstone in cerebral atherosclerosis development. Elevated levels of low - density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL - C), often referred to as “bad cholesterol,” are a significant contributor. ox - LDL can be taken up by macrophages in the arterial wall, transforming them into foam cells, which are characteristic of atherosclerotic plaques [66]. High - density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL - C), or “good cholesterol,” has a protective role, as it can reverse cholesterol transport back to the liver. However, in the context of cerebral atherosclerosis, the balance between LDL - C and HDL - C is disrupted. Recent studies have identified the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHHR) as a novel lipid biomarker for assessing cerebral atherosclerosis. It was found that the incidence of relevant stenosis is significantly higher in the high NHHR group. NHHR can serve as a lipid management indicator for patients with specific stenosis and an independent risk factor for symptomatic stenosis, facilitating risk stratification and treatment decision-making [67].

Metabolic syndrome, a cluster of metabolic abnormalities including central obesity, hypertension, elevated fasting blood glucose, and hyperlipidemia, has been strongly associated with an increased risk of cerebral atherosclerosis. A cross - sectional study in China involving 1988 participants aged ≥ 40 years without a history of stroke found that metabolic syndrome was positively associated with asymptomatic intracranial arterial stenosis [68]. Central obesity, elevated triglyceride levels, and elevated blood pressure, components of metabolic syndrome, were particularly linked to aicas.

Hyperhomocysteinemia, characterized by elevated levels of homocysteine in the blood, is another metabolic factor. Homocysteine can cause endothelial damage, promote inflammation, and interfere with cholesterol metabolism, all of which contribute to atherosclerotic plaque formation [69]. It is considered an independent risk factor for cerebral atherosclerosis. Recent studies have revealed that atorvastatin can effectively improve hyperhomocysteinemia and the cognitive decline induced by it, which implied its important role in rescuing cerebral atherosclerosis [70].

Moreover, recent research has explored the role of gut-microbiota-derived metabolites in cerebral atherosclerosis. For example, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), produced from dietary nutrients by gut microbiota, has been associated with increased atherosclerosis risk in animal models [71]. A review summarizing preclinical and clinical studies notes that TMAO, linked to cardiovascular events including stroke, promotes atherosclerotic plaque development via mechanisms like inflammation and oxidative stress, and post-stroke can infiltrate the brain through a disrupted blood-brain barrier (BBB) to exacerbate inflammation via NLRP3 inflammasome activation [72]. Additionally, in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) patients, serum metabolites such as indoxyl sulfate (sIS) and butenoylcarnitine (sBCA) relate to carotid atherosclerosis, while sorbitol (sSorb) associates with BBB dysfunction, further linking gut microbiota-derived metabolites to vascular and BBB impairments relevant to cerebral atherosclerosis [73]. Although the exact mechanisms in the context of cerebral atherosclerosis are still under investigation, it highlights the systemic nature of metabolic dysregulation in this disease.

In summary, the metabolic basis of cerebral atherosclerosis involves multiple pathways and systemic connections. Understanding these metabolic derangements is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to prevent and treat cerebral atherosclerosis.

Conclusion

This review summarizes recent advancements in the mechanisms underlying intracranial atherosclerosis from the perspectives of genetics, inflammation, comorbidities, diet, and exercise. It was found that controlling blood pressure, lipid, and glucose levels, as well as maintaining long-term moderate-intensity physical activity, can improve the vascular health status in this condition. Controlling the propagation of inflammation and maintaining the normal structure of cerebral vessels are significantly associated with slowing disease progression, offering potential therapeutic directions and genetic targets for the treatment and prevention of the disease. However, this review also identifies gaps in the exploration of the mechanisms of cerebral atherosclerosis. Most of the mechanisms are based on small-scale clinical studies from a few centers, primarily involving extracranial vascular atherosclerosis and plaque formation. The stability of these associations requires validation through large-scale investigations by multiple centers. Additionally, the development of basic research is limited by the challenges associated with intracranial atherosclerosis animal models, including their long duration, poor stability, and difficulty in tissue procurement. Therefore, the characteristics of intracranial atherosclerosis can be more comprehensively delineated through large-scale clinical imaging techniques. Simultaneously, there should be a proactive effort to develop specific animal models of intracranial atherosclerosis to provide more stable and reliable evidence and directions for the treatment of this disease.
