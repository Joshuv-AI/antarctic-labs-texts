Matrine alkaloids modulating DNA damage repair in chemoresistant non-small cell lung cancer cells
Authors: Fengping Wang, Jun Liu, Wenliang Liao, Lixiang Zheng, Shuai Qian, Lisi Mao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) presents a significant challenge in the medical field due to its high incidence and resistance to chemotherapy. Chemoresistance in NSCLC diminishes treatment efficacy and contributes to poor patient outcomes. Introduction

Lung cancer, being one of the most prevalent malignant tumors globally, has been a focal point of interest in the medical and public health sectors due to its high incidence and mortality rates [1–3]. Among all lung cancer cases, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes the majority, attracting significant attention from medical researchers worldwide [4–6]. Despite advancements in NSCLC treatment in recent years, chemotherapy resistance continues to pose a serious challenge in clinical practice [7]. Chemotherapy resistance not only diminishes the survival rates of tumor patients but also leads to suboptimal treatment outcomes and tumor recurrence [8–10]. Therefore, it is imperative and crucial to undertake comprehensive research to address the issue of chemotherapy resistance [11].

This study aims to investigate the mechanism by which Matrine alkaloids, as potential agents for reversing chemotherapy resistance, function in NSCLC treatment. Utilizing molecular dynamics simulation technology, we delved into the interaction between Matrine alkaloids and DNA repair-related proteins, examining their impact on lung cancer cells [12, 13]. Furthermore, through cell and animal experiments, we further validated the specific mechanism by which Matrine alkaloids sensitize NSCLC cells to chemotherapy, laying essential groundwork for future clinical translation.

Various experimental methods were employed to verify the therapeutic effects of Matrine alkaloids on NSCLC. In vitro cell experiments involved treating A549/DDP cells with Matrine alkaloids and conducting a series of cytological experiments to investigate their mechanisms of sensitizing lung cancer cells. Simultaneously, in animal model experiments [14, 15], tumor models were successfully established, and the effects of Matrine alkaloids in treating NSCLC were assessed by monitoring indicators, providing a robust experimental foundation for further clinical research.

Molecular docking studies revealed that Matrine alkaloids interact with DNA repair-related proteins, potentially affecting the growth and survival of NSCLC cells by disturbing the DNA damage repair process [16]. Cell experiments and animal models further confirmed the significant chemosensitizing effects of Matrine alkaloids on NSCLC, offering hope and potential breakthroughs in overcoming chemotherapy resistance and enhancing NSCLC treatment outcomes [17]. Summarizing the experimental results, we posit that Matrine alkaloids may enhance the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to chemotherapy by interfering with DNA damage repair processes and signaling pathways, potentially affecting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Future research will delve deeper into exploring the clinical prospects of Matrine alkaloids, contributing to the development of more effective treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of DNA repair-related factors

To obtain DNA repair-related factors, a search was conducted in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) using the keyword “DNA repair.” Additionally, the Phenolyzer database (https://phenolyzer.wglab.org/) was utilized to predict phenotypic targets associated with DNA repair. The study was conducted up to February 1, 2024. The results from both databases were sorted in descending order based on scores, and the top 100 genes were selected for further analysis. Subsequently, the Jvenn tool was employed to identify the intersection of these genes, thereby obtaining a common gene set [18, 19].

Gene functional enrichment analysis

Enrichment analysis was conducted using the R software package clusterProfiler [20] with a set P-value threshold of 0.05. We performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the gene intersections and visualized the results with bar charts and network graphs. The GO enrichment analysis encompassed three aspects: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF).

Analysis of gene expression differences and prognostic evaluation

The “Lung Cancer/TCGA/TCGA-LUADLUSC/RNAseq” cloud data from the Xiantao Academic Online website was selected for differential analysis and survival analysis of intersecting genes. This dataset comprised RNA expression data from 108 healthy control lung tissue samples and 1041 tumor tissue samples from lung cancer patients [21]. As these data were sourced from a public database, ethical committee approval was not required.

For the differential analysis, the log2(value + 1) data processing method was employed, and a statistical analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. In the survival analysis phase, proportional hazards assumption testing and survival regression fitting were performed using the survival package. For visualization of the results, the survminer and ggplot2 packages were utilized. In cases where the optimal grouping method was applied, the survminer surv_cutpoint function of the survminer package was used for the cut-off selection of optimal groups, followed by plotting the survival curves.

Molecular docking

The 2D molecular structure of the Matrine was converted to a 3D structure using ChemOffice 3D (v14.0) software, followed by energy minimization using the MM2 algorithm. The crystal structure of the receptor protein was then obtained from the Protein Data Bank, and PyMOL (v2.5) software was utilized to remove the ligand and solvent from the receptor protein structure. Next, AutoDockTools (v1.5.7) software was employed for hydrogenation, charge calculation, and conversion of compounds and receptor proteins to “pdbqt” files, with appropriate Grid Box parameters set. In cases of uncertainty regarding the binding of Matrine to the receptor protein, the Grid Box range was expanded to encompass more protein structures. Finally, molecular docking simulations were performed using Vina (v1.2.5) software to calculate docking energies, and the results were visualized using PyMOL software [22].

Cell culture

The human NSCLC cell line A549 (CL-0016, Wuhan Punuose Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was used for cell culture. These cells were cultured in DMEM medium (11965092, Gibco, USA, www.gibcobrl.com/) containing 10 µg/mL of streptomycin (85886, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and 100 U/mL of penicillin (9073-60-3, Sigma Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 (Heracell™ Vios 160i CR CO2 incubator, 51033770, Thermo Scientific™, Germany). Passaging was carried out when the cell growth reached 80–90% confluence [23]. A549/DDP cells resistant to cisplatin (CL-0519, Wuhan Punuose Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were cultured in DMEM medium containing 1000 ng/mL cisplatin (232120, Merck) [24].

The cisplatin-resistant A549 cells were divided into two groups: one group received treatment with Matrine alkaloids (Matrine group, concentration 2.0 mg/mL, 519-02-8, Sigma Aldrich), while the other served as the control group (Control group) without treatment. The treatment duration was 48 h [25].

Western blot experiment

Tumor cells and tissues were collected, digested with trypsin (T4799-5G, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and cultured. Cell lysis was performed using enhanced RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (AR0108, Wuhan Bosider Company, Wuhan, China), followed by measurement of protein concentrations using a BCA protein quantification kit (AR1189, Wuhan Bosider Company, Wuhan, China).

The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to a PVDF membrane, and blocked with 5% BSA (9048-46-8, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, primary antibodies (Table S1) were added at appropriate dilutions and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was washed three times with PBST (each for 5 min), followed by incubation with Anti-Mouse-HRP secondary antibody (7076, 1/5000; CST, USA) or Anti-Rabbit-HRP secondary antibody (7074, 1/5000; CST, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, with another three washes with PBST. After discarding PBST, an adequate amount of ECL working solution (Omt-01, Beijing Omijia Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 min. Excess ECL reagent was removed, the membrane was sealed with plastic wrap, placed in a darkroom, and exposed to X-ray film for 5–10 min for visualization and fixing. Finally, Image J analysis software was used for grayscale quantification of protein bands in the Western blot images, with GAPDH serving as an internal reference [23]. All the uncropped gel images can be found in the Supplementary file 1.

RT-qPCR experiment

Cell or tissue lysis was performed using the Trizol reagent kit (10296010, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher, USA) for total RNA extraction. Subsequently, the quality and concentration of RNA were assessed using ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry (ND-1000, Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher, USA). Reverse transcription was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT-qPCR kit (RR086A, TaKaRa, Mountain View, California, USA). Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed on the LightCycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, Pleasanton, California, USA) using the SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM (DRR820A, TaKaRa) with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Amplification was conducted with primers designed and provided by Shanghai General BioTech Co., Ltd., as listed in Table S2 for the primer sequences. The experimental group’s gene expression fold change relative to the control group was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, where ΔΔCt = ΔCt experimental group - ΔCt control group, and ΔCt represents the difference between the Ct value of the target gene and the Ct value of the reference gene [26].

Flow cytometry

Cell cycle analysis was performed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Initially, cells were fixed in pre-chilled 70% ethanol at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by staining the DNA content with PI staining solution (F10797, Thermo Fisher, USA) for cell cycle distribution analysis using flow cytometry [27, 28].

Apoptotic cell death was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (APOAF-20TST, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, employing flow cytometry to evaluate apoptosis levels. Cells were plated at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, cells were treated with Matrine at a concentration of 2.0 mg/mL for 48 h. After drug treatment, cells were incubated with 200 µL binding buffer, stained with Annexin V-FITC in the dark for 20 min, and then analyzed using the FACS Aria II flow cytometer [29]. In flow cytometry images, cells in the upper right quadrant with the phenotype Annexin V+PI+ represented late apoptotic cells; cells in the lower right quadrant with the phenotype Annexin V+PI− represented early apoptotic cells; cells in the upper left quadrant with the phenotype Annexin V−PI+ represented necrotic cells; and cells in the lower left quadrant with the phenotype Annexin V−PI− represented live cells [30].

CCK-8 experiment

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Subsequently, fresh culture medium with or without Matrine alkaloids was added to the wells, followed by the addition of 10 µL CCK-8 assay kit solution (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. The 96-well plate was then incubated in a humidified 37 °C cell culture incubator. Finally, the absorbance values at 450 nm for each well were recorded using a Microplate Reader (abx700005, Beijing Qiwei Yicheng Technology Co., Ltd.) [31, 32].

Scratch assay experiment

A scratch assay was conducted using a 6-well plate. When cell confluence reached 90%, a 200 µl pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch. After removing the cell suspension, a fresh culture medium with or without Matrine alkaloids was added. Images of the scratch were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) for the quantification of the scratch closure area [24].

Transwell experiment

Tumor cells were cultured in vitro using Transwell polycarbonate membrane inserts (CLS3422, Corning). In the Transwell system, a suspension of 2 × 104 tumor cells was added to the upper chamber in a serum-free medium, while the lower chamber had a medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were removed, and the cells that migrated through the pores were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime). Eight random fields were selected for observation of cell migration under a 20× objective of an inverted microscope (XDS-900, Caikon) [33].

Animal model establishment

Male BALB/c-nu/nu nude mice aged 4–5 weeks were obtained from our Experimental Animal Research Center. All animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines outlined in our institution’s “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” The laboratory animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment at a temperature of 22–25 °C, relative humidity of 40–60%, and under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. To establish the NSCLC nude mouse model, cisplatin-resistant A549/DDP cells (5 × 106 cells per mouse) were suspended in PBS and then injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal region of the mice.

After 7 days post-injection, the mice were randomly divided into two groups of 6 mice each. One group received Matrine alkaloids treatment (Matrine group, dose of 150 mg/kg, thrice weekly, administered by intraperitoneal injection), while the other group received an equivalent volume of saline as the control group (Control group). After 3 weeks of continuous treatment, mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane (R510-22-10, RWD), cervical dislocation was performed to euthanize the mice, tumor samples were collected, photographed, and weighed, and subsequent experimental analyses were conducted on the tumor tissues. Tumor size was measured using calipers with measurements taken every 7 days, calculating tumor volume using the formula (length × width2)/2 [25].

The animal experiments conducted in this study strictly adhered to relevant ethical standards and regulations and received approval from our Experimental Animal Research Center. The use and care of all animals complied with the regulations outlined in our institution’s “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” Throughout the experiments, efforts were made to minimize the pain and suffering of the animals and ensure they received proper care and management.

Immunofluorescence

Cells or tumor tissues were washed with cold PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P885233, Macklin, USA) for 15–30 min. Subsequently, they were treated with 0.1% Triton (L885651, Macklin, USA) for 15 min. After two washes with PBS, tissues were incubated overnight at 5 °C in PBS containing 15% FBS for 4 min. The tissues were then stained with rabbit anti-γ-H2AX antibody (MA5-33062, Thermo Fisher, USA; dilution 1:50) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with TBST (TBS with 1% Tween-20), they were incubated with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor® 488 secondary antibody (A-11008, Thermo Fisher, USA). Subsequently, DAPI counterstaining was performed, and fluorescence intensity measurement was carried out by observing selected regions under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Observer Z1, Germany). Image processing and quantitative analysis were performed using ImageJ software, and the number of positive cells was quantified [34].

Immunohistochemistry

Upon obtaining the tumor tissue, it was fixed, embedded, and sectioned. The embedded tissue sections were then deparaffinized to remove wax, rendering them hydrophilic for subsequent immunostaining procedures. Specific antibodies, including Ki67 antibody (MA5-14520, dilution 1:200, Thermo Fisher, USA), RAD51 antibody (PA5-27195, 1:200, Thermo Fisher, USA), or POLD2 antibody (PA5-55401, 1:200, Thermo Fisher, USA), were used for staining on the deparaffinized tissue sections. Subsequently, the sections were treated with an Anti-Rabbit-HRP secondary antibody (12–348, 1:1000, Sigma Aldrich, USA). DAB chromogen (ab64238, Abcam, USA) was then applied to visualize the sites where the secondary antibody bound to the primary antibody. After staining, the tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and coverslipping for observation. The stained sections were examined under a microscope to record the expression patterns. Evaluation criteria involved randomly selecting 5 lesion areas under the microscope and counting the number of positively stained cells [35].

Statistical analysis methods

Experimental data were obtained through at least three independent experiments and presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). For the comparison between two groups, the two-sample independent t-test was employed, while for comparisons involving three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized. If the ANOVA results indicated significant differences, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test was conducted to compare differences among groups. In cases of non-normal distribution or unequal variances, the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R language. A significance level of 0.05 was set, where a two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The prognostic relevance of DNA damage repair factors RAD51 and POLD2 in lung cancer patients and their high binding stability with matrine alkaloids

DNA damage repair-related factors were searched in the GeneCards and Phenolyzer databases, with both databases sorted by Score from high to low. The top 100 genes were selected from each database, and the intersection yielded 51 common genes (Fig. 1A). Further confirmation of the critical role of these 51 intersecting genes in DNA damage repair processes was obtained through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 1B-C). Subsequently, a combined analysis of the intersecting genes in the TCGA-LUAD and TCGA-LUSC datasets revealed that three genes (RAD51, XRCC5, and POLD2) were closely associated with overall survival in lung cancer patients (P < 0.05). These three genes were found to be upregulated in tumor tissues of lung cancer patients (Fig. 1D) and accurately predicted prognosis as high-risk genes (Fig. 1E-G). These results underscore the significant correlation between the DNA damage repair factors RAD51, XRCC5, and POLD2 and the prognosis of lung cancer patients.

The study indicates that the binding of small-molecule ligands with protein receptors at less than − 7.0 kcal/mol is considered stable, and the lower the binding energy, the more stable the complex conformation [22]. Subsequently, we conducted molecular docking analysis on the proteins encoded by three intersecting genes and Matrine alkaloids. Initially, we obtained the 2D structure of Matrine alkaloids from the PubChem database and converted it to a 3D structure using ChemOffice 3D software (Fig. 1H). The MM2 algorithm was employed for energy minimization. Concurrently, crystal structures of RAD51, XRCC5, and POLD2 receptor proteins were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank, and the ligands and solvents in the receptor protein structures were removed. Hydrogenation, charge calculation, and Vina software were utilized for molecular docking, and docking energy values were calculated. We observed that all three target proteins could spontaneously bind with Matrine alkaloids, with binding energies of -7.9 kcal/mol, -5.6 kcal/mol, and − 7.6 kcal/mol for RAD51, XRCC5, and POLD2, respectively (Fig. 1I). This signifies the favorable binding stability of Matrine alkaloids as small-molecule ligands with RAD51 and POLD2 protein receptors.

Inhibition of growth and DNA damage repair activity in cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cells by matrine alkaloids

To investigate the regulatory link between Matrine alkaloids and DNA damage repair proteins in NSCLC, we initially selected the cisplatin-sensitive cell line A549 and the cisplatin-resistant cell line A549/DDP. The CCK-8 cell viability assay results confirmed a notably higher resistance to cisplatin in A549/DDP cells compared to A549 cells (Fig. 2A), leading us to focus on further studying the cisplatin-resistant A549/DDP cells in relation to Matrine alkaloids. The subsequent CCK-8 assay revealed significant growth inhibition in Matrine-treated A549/DDP cells compared to the Control group (Fig. 2B). Additionally, cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry indicated a significant increase in G0/G1 phase cells and a decrease in S phase cells, with notable differences in G2/M phase cells in Matrine-treated A549/DDP cells compared to the Control group (Fig. 2C). Results from scratch wound healing and Transwell assays showed a marked decrease in migration and invasion activity in Matrine-treated A549/DDP cells compared to the Control group (Fig. 2D-E). Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in Matrine-treated A549/DDP cells compared to the Control group (Fig. 2F). Moreover, Western blotting results displayed elevated levels of the pro-apoptotic factor BAX and an increased cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio, along with a significant decrease in Bcl-2 levels in Matrine-treated A549/DDP cells compared to the Control group (Fig. 2G). These findings collectively suggest that Matrine alkaloids significantly inhibit the growth of A549/DDP cells.

Initially, we compared DNA damage repair between the cisplatin-sensitive A549 cells and the cisplatin-resistant A549/DDP cells. Inhibition of cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cell tumorigenesis and DNA damage repair activity in tumor tissue by matrine alkaloids

We further verified the regulatory relationship between Matrine alkaloids and DNA damage repair proteins in NSCLC in vivo. Initially, we established a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model in nude mice, where the Matrine group exhibited significantly decreased tumor tissue size, volume, and weight compared to the Control group (Fig. 4A-C). Subsequent qRT-PCR, Western blot, and immunohistochemistry experiments revealed markedly reduced levels of RAD51 and POLD2 in tumor tissues of mice in the Matrine group compared to the Control group, accompanied by a significant decrease in γ-H2AX levels (Fig. 4D-F). Additionally, immunofluorescence experiments demonstrated a notable decrease in γ-H2AX signal in tumor tissues of mice in the Matrine group (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry experiments indicated a significant decrease in the Ki67 cell proportion in tumor tissues of mice in the Matrine group compared to the Control group, suggesting a pronounced reduction in tumor cell proliferation capacity (Fig. 4H). Western blot analysis further revealed increased levels of pro-apoptotic factor BAX, an elevated cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio, and a significant decrease in Bcl-2 levels in tumor tissues of mice in the Matrine group compared to the Control group (Fig. 4I), indicating a marked increase in tumor cell apoptosis activity. These findings demonstrate that Matrine alkaloids inhibit tumorigenesis of cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cells and alter DNA damage repair activity in tumor tissue in vivo.

Discussion

This study employed molecular docking technology to investigate the role of Matrine alkaloids in mediating the DNA damage repair process, revealing their enhanced binding stability with RAD51 and POLD2. And this study utilized molecular docking techniques to investigate the mechanistic role of Matrine alkaloids in mediating DNA damage repair processes, revealing their interaction stability with RAD51 and POLD2. Additionally, the level of γ-H2AX content serves as a clear indicator of DNA damage extent and repair status, widely applied in DNA damage and cell apoptosis studies as an important DNA damage marker [36]. Based on this information, we can infer that Matrine alkaloids may inhibit the DNA damage repair activity by binding to RAD51 and POLD2 protein receptors, thereby suppressing the DNA damage repair capability of A549/DDP cells. Offering a fresh molecular-level interpretation compared to prior research, we suggest that Matrine alkaloids may influence cellular biological behaviors by disrupting the binding of DNA damage repair-related proteins, thus providing new insights into their potential chemo-sensitizing effects.

Cell experimental findings in this study confirm the intervention effects of Matrine alkaloids on DNA damage repair processes in A549/DDP cells, further validating their inhibitory impact on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Consistent with other studies, this emphasizes the potential value of Matrine alkaloids in suppressing lung cancer cell growth and metastasis [14, 37].

In in vivo animal model experiments, Matrine alkaloids were observed to suppress tumor growth induced by cisplatin-resistant cells effectively and inhibit DNA damage repair activity in tumor tissue. This sheds new light on exploring the mechanisms of action of Matrine alkaloids in treating chemotherapy-resistant tumors [38–40]. Future research can further explore the molecular pathways and signaling cascades involved in the repair of DNA damage by berberine. For instance, the inhibition of DNA damage repair may activate cellular transcription factors such as p53 and inhibit cyclin-dependent kinases, leading to the induction of G1/S phase arrest [41].

Comparative analysis reveals the unique chemo-sensitizing mechanisms of Matrine alkaloids in NSCLC treatment [17]. Recent studies suggest that traditional medicines with iron death-associated anticancer activity could serve as a starting point to overcome chemotherapy resistance in NSCLC by inducing iron death [42]. Additionally, pharmacological inactivation of Skp2 with current chemotherapeutic agents presents a promising approach to treat NSCLC patients with a history of treatment failure [43]. Compared to these potential drugs, berberine alkaloids may exert potent antitumor effects by interfering with DNA damage repair and signaling pathways through distinct mechanisms. This highlights the novelty and potential clinical application prospects of berberine alkaloids as potential therapeutic agents.

In addition to impacting DNA repair processes, Matrine alkaloids may influence cellular behavior by modulating signaling pathways. Notably, their role in reversing chemotherapy resistance, as observed in this study, provides insights into the molecular mechanisms and clinical translation of Matrine alkaloids, highlighting the uniqueness of this research. The translation into an academic style includes addressing several factors for the translational research of Aconitine to clinical studies, such as dosage, administration route, and potential side effects. Reports indicate that high concentrations of Aconitine (> 140 mg/L, 72 h) have been shown to reduce albumin secretion in liver cells, induce protein expression of CYP2A6, CYP2B6, and CYP3A4, decrease LDH and AST levels, lower cell viability and total protein content, and generate hepatotoxicity [44]. Moreover, as a chemosensitizer, concomitant use of Aconitine with other chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin may exacerbate liver damage, necessitating potential dosage adjustment. However, the current status of Aconitine remains in the early stages. Additionally, considering the significant clinical prospects of Aconitine, researchers have intensified formulation studies over the past few decades to improve its in vivo blood drug concentration and to alter (or control) drug tissue distribution. Various formulations have been developed, including capsules, injections, tablets, suppositories, gels, and nanoparticle formulations [45].

Taken together, Matrine alkaloids present a promising new strategy for treating NSCLC, demonstrating significant chemo-sensitizing effects and the ability to inhibit tumor growth. This offers a new perspective for overcoming chemotherapy resistance in tumors, providing vital scientific support for clinical translation and personalized treatment.

In conclusion, this study enriches and deepens our understanding of the mechanisms of action of Matrine alkaloids in treating NSCLC. However, the study has limitations, such as a small sample size and a singular experimental design. Future research should aim to expand the sample size and verify results from multiple perspectives to enhance the study’s credibility and persuasiveness. Looking ahead, further exploration of the mechanisms of action of Matrine alkaloids and the development of relevant clinical drugs aim to contribute to improving treatment outcomes and survival rates for NSCLC patients.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively investigated the molecular mechanisms of catering alkaloids from traditional Chinese medicine in sensitizing NSCLC cells to chemotherapy through a combination of network pharmacology, cell experiments, and animal studies (Fig. 5). The results indicate that Matrine alkaloids significantly disrupt the functions of DNA damage repair-related proteins, leading to the inhibition of proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of cisplatin-resistant NSCLC cells. Treatment with Matrine alkaloids in animal models markedly suppressed tumor growth, likely attributed to the inhibition of DNA damage repair pathways and promotion of apoptotic pathways. These findings provide crucial scientific evidence for the potential application of Matrine alkaloids in NSCLC treatment. The study revealed that Matrine alkaloids impact DNA damage repair and apoptotic pathways at the molecular level, enhancing the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant lung cancer cells to chemotherapy drugs. This discovery not only enhances the understanding of chemosensitizers for lung cancer but also presents new drug candidates for clinical NSCLC treatment. Although the in vitro and in vivo models demonstrated the anti-lung cancer effects of Matrine alkaloids, further validation is needed to assess their safety and efficacy in clinical applications. Future research should validate the anticancer effects of Matrine alkaloids in a broader range of lung cancer cell lines and advanced animal models, conduct preclinical studies to evaluate their safety and efficacy in humans and investigate the synergistic effects of Matrine alkaloids in combination with other chemotherapy drugs to potentially enhance the effectiveness of chemotherapy.
