All the world’s a phage
Authors: Graham F. Hatfull
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceBacteriophages play central roles in the discovery of fundamental biological mechanisms, in the development of tools for biotechnology, as powerful subjects for science education, and as promising new medicines for antibiotic resistant microbial pathogens. The vast genetic diversity of these rapidly evolving viruses presents challenges to understand their biology but also provides riches for viral discovery. An integrated approach in which curiosity-driven science, research-driven science education programs, and phage therapeutics, combine and mutually reinforce each other, offering a model for advancing microbiology in the 21st century.

From Model Systems to Biological Dark Matter

Bacteriophages are viruses that infect bacterial hosts. Although first described more than 100 B.P., interest in them as biological subjects and as potential antimicrobials has waxed and waned over the past decades (1). In the 1950s, 60s, and 70s, their relatively simple life cycles, rapid growth, propagation to high titers, and relatively small genomes provided powerful model systems for discovering the fundamentals of molecular biology, gene structure, and biological information flow (2, 3). Their therapeutic potential was of considerable early interest, but in much of the world advances in antibiotics in the 1940s rapidly superseded phage therapies, although these continued in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union (4). Today, there is a resurgence of interest in bacteriophages, arguably fueled by two key developments. First is the rapid discovery of the wealth of bacterial systems used to defend against phage attack, adding to restriction-modification (R-M) and CRISPR-Cas that provided revolutionary biotechnological tools (5). Second is the resurgence of interest in phage therapies, due to concerns about the rapidly increasing challenge of bacterial resistance to antibiotics. This interest has only been furthered by the apparent successful uses of phage to treat clinically intractable infections (4).

There are two other noteworthy contributions to the renewed interest in phages. First is the appreciation of the massive diversity of bacteriophages and bacteriophage genomes (6). The model Escherichia coli phages used to discover the fundamentals of molecular biology are terrific paradigms, but expanding our understanding to the broader phage population has revealed many new insights into viral biology. It has also clarified that phages really do represent the dark matter of the biosphere, with vast numbers of genes of unknown function (7, 8). With a predicted total phage population of 1031 particles or more, dynamically interacting with bacteria at 1023 infections per second, and all happening over perhaps as long as three billion years, this has of course produced huge diversity (9). Defining this diversity and the functions of the unknown genes remain major challenges in phage biology. Second, is the appreciation of the powerful systems that phage discovery and genomics brings to science education (10, 11). The program with arguably the greatest impact is the SEA-PHAGES program through which more than 50,000 students have participated and discovered nearly 30,000 new bacteriophages (10–12).

Elucidating the fundamentals of phage biology, science education, and translational aspects including phage-based therapies provides a fully integrated strategy in which each mission strongly enforces the other (Fig. 1). Here I will describe the choice of bacterial hosts for learning about phage diversity and evolution, how science education programs strongly fuel advances in phage biology, and how the resulting phage collection and various engineering tools have propelled the prospects for the therapeutic applications of phages to Mycobacterium infections. In turn, the clinical applications promote the roles of science education programs and impose a demand for furthering our understanding of phages and their dynamic interactions with their bacterial hosts (Fig. 1). This organizational structure for missions in curiosity-driven science, science education, and translational applications is a model that can, and arguably should be, adopted in many different areas of science.

A fully integrated approach to phage biology, science education, and therapy. Bacteriophages provide robust and plentiful subjects for biological characterization, as a focus for science education programs, and for experimental medicine. Many different aspects of phage biology warrant investigation and because the diversity is high, the large numbers of genomically characterized phages fuel the biological discoveries. The phage collections and the biological insights make key contributions to the development of phage therapeutics, and this in turn stimulates the education programs and the importance of the biological studies. The bidirectional arrows between the three main components illustrate the interdependence and positive impacts that each has on the other.

The title of this article was a favorite phrase of my late colleague and collaborator Dr. Roger Hendrix, and was used as part of the title of a paper we coauthored in 1999 (9).The sentiment was more of a prediction at that time as the nature of the phage population was just emerging. More than 25 years on, this view of life is on a firm footing and reframing Theodosius Dobzhansky (13), nothing makes sense in microbiology except in the light of phage-bacterium dynamics. Here, I describe the historical context and the progress contributing to this view in the study of phages infecting Mycobacterium hosts.

Why Mycobacterium?

Mycobacteria are a genus within the Actinobacteria phylum and include several species that are notable human pathogens. These include Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium leprae the causative agents of human tuberculosis (TB) and leprosy, respectively, and opportunistic nontuberculous Mycobacterium (NTM) including Mycobacterium avium complex and Mycobacterium abscessus strains. These impose substantial impacts on human health globally, with about one-third of the world’s population infected with M. tuberculosis and ~1.25 million deaths annually (14, 15). A six-month (“short course”) therapy with four antibiotics is a common TB treatment regimen but is challenging to complete, especially in the developing world where TB is most common, and drug-resistant strains are a rising concern (15). Opportunistic NTM infections are less prevalent than TB but commonly afflict patients with cystic fibrosis or immune disorders (16). However, they are often intrinsically nonresponsive to antibiotics as well as having acquired resistance (16). Moreover, many of the antibiotics are quite toxic and are not well tolerated by many patients. Clinical management of these infections is often a nightmare for both patients and physicians, with months or years of therapy that fail to control the infections.

Phages of Mycobacterium hosts (mycobacteriophages) were first isolated over 75 B.P. (17, 18), motivated in part by the potential for microbial diagnostics by phage typing (19, 20). Mycobacteriophage-mediated generalized transduction was demonstrated in 1970 (21) but the dearth of facile genetic tools was an impediment to advancing Mycobacterium genetics (22). Moreover, the notoriously slow growth of M. tuberculosis and some NTM strains (doubling times of 20 to 24 h) makes them challenging systems for genetic analyses, often requiring 3 to 4 wk for growth from a single cell to a bacterial colony. Development of a suitable nonpathogenic relatively fast-growing Mycobacterium strain, Mycobacterium smegmatis, advanced the field enormously, although of much less useful for studying bacterial virulence mechanisms directly (22–24). As discussed below, mycobacteriophages have been critical for advancing Mycobacterium genetics (25).

In the 1980s and 1990s research into the basic biology of phages was largely out of favor. Bill Jacobs and colleagues pioneered the use of mycobacteriophages for developing Mycobacterium genetics, and given the clinical importance of Mycobacterium pathogens, there was an excellent rationale (and funding opportunities!) for investigating the basic biology and genomics of mycobacteriophages. Some phages isolated on M. smegmatis had been described previously, and the first one to be sequenced was phage L5 (26), one of the first completely sequenced phages beyond the canonical Escherichia coli phages. The L5 sequence raised important questions about phage diversity and evolution, and over the next few years, the genomes of other previously described Mycobacterium phages—including D29 and TM4—were sequenced (27, 28). As a result, our view of phage genome diversity and relatedness expanded; we learned that even phages of disparate bacteria and separated by large swaths of evolutionary time had related genes and features. Indeed, All the World’s a Phage (9).

Education and Discovery: PHIRE and SEA-PHAGES

It became quickly apparent that our view of phage diversity and genome evolution would not change substantially without a much larger body of data—i.e. more phages and more phage genomes. Why not engage students in phage discovery and genomics to both advance these research questions and provide research opportunities for novice student scientists? Following initial successes in doing this (7), we leveraged it to develop the Phage Hunters Integrating Research and Education (PHIRE) program supported by the HHMI Professor’s initiative (29, 30). The program started in 2002 and involved Pittsburgh high school and undergraduate students isolating new M. smegmatis phages and genomically characterizing them (29, 30). The program was popular and effective in greatly expanding our view of the phage world as well as richly engaging young students in science and scientific research (29, 30). We learned some key lessons: first, that this research was accessible to these student novices; second that genome sequencing is an essential project component without which you know little about the phage or its evolutionary origins; third, that the vast phage diversity virtually guarantees that each student will isolate a different phage; fourth, that project ownership—students working on and caring about the phage they have isolated, named, and characterized—is a powerful motivator (29, 31). The power of PHIRE to rapidly expand phage biology and to deepen our understanding of viral evolution became obvious.

The PHIRE program was a great model, but the world of undiscovered phages is vast and the need for high impact educational innovations is critical. In 2008, the core concepts were used to develop the HHMI-supported Science Education Alliance Phage Hunters Advancing Genomics and Evolutionary Science (SEA-PHAGES) program, taking advantage of indivisible integration of advancing science knowledge with science education: two for the price of one (or buy one get one free, if you prefer). You can think about it as a science program in which the education advances are free; or as an education program in which the scientific advances are free. The main programmatic innovation was to centralize the program infrastructure, including the scientific directions, protocols, databases, faculty training, workshops, etc) such that almost any institution, any faculty members, and any student can participate in the program (Fig. 2); the overall structure is referred to as an inclusive research education community (iREC) (11). Establishing and maintaining an iREC such as SEA-PHAGES has considerable costs, but the structure enables it to be highly scalable, an especially challenging aspect of science education programs in general (32). As such, the cost per student becomes very affordable, and the quantifiable benefits to both science and education are highly cost-effective (11).

As of 2025, there are about 170 institutions participating in SEA-PHAGES, having run continuously for 18 y (https://seaphages.org). More than 50,000 students—primarily first year undergraduate students—have participated and more than 28,000 individual phages have been isolated, with over 5,500 completely sequenced; more than 400 publications have emerged from the SEA-PHAGES program (https://seaphages.org). The types of bacterial hosts have been expanded to include others within the phylum Actinobacteria, and the constraint to closely related bacterial hosts is deliberate to provide insights into the phage genome relatedness. This reflects the main scientific missions of the program in viral diversity and evolution. Of course, such programs could have different scientific missions, prompting different choices of bacterial hosts.

Students in the PHIRE and SEA-PHAGES programs choose the name for the phage they have isolated (with appropriate oversight). This helps to amplify the sense of project ownership, and students often think long and hard about choosing a name (“because it is important!”). Although it is a nonsystematic naming system it reflects the idea that phages have individual characteristics, and that the genomics show little evidence of sharp taxonomic boundaries. It does lead to some interesting names of which the likes of Corndog, Patience, CocoaPuff, FatCactus, and Phamished are some of the favorites.

The iREC structure is a powerful one (11). It could be implemented to address a variety of different scientific questions, although phage diversity and evolution would seem to be an especially good fit. Several other programs including Tiny Earth (33, 34) and the Genomics Education Partnership (35, 36) have similar iREC structures, but clearly there are opportunities for active development of new iREC programs. A challenge, however, is the need for commitment of funds initially to establish the structure. Federal funding agencies could play a major role in this, following the key principle that a well-structured program can effectively deliver the scientific advances, with inherent educational advances at no additional cost. Public–private partnerships such as between federal agencies and foundations or companies are especially attractive mechanisms.

Phage Diversity and Evolution: What Have We Learned From 2,600 Genomes?

Virion morphologies of the mycobacteriophages show that all are dsDNA tailed phages, and no ssDNA or RNA phages have been isolated. Moreover, only two of the three major morphotypes of dsDNA tailed phages have been identified (Fig. 3 and Table 1). Phages in Clusters C and AA have myophage virions with contractile tails, but all others are siphophages with long noncontractile tails (Fig. 3 and Table 1). Most groups have isometric (“soccerball”) capsids, but some have prolate (elongated) heads (e.g., Clusters O, I, and singleton MooMoo) (Fig. 3). However, no Mycobacterium podophages with short stubby tails have been isolated, even though these are common for other bacteria. It is plausible that the complex Mycobacterium cell wall presents a physical limitation for such phages. If phages were only studied through isolation and virion morphological analyses, then their genetic diversity would remain undiscovered, and this is why the genomics is a critical component of the educational programs (Fig. 2).

Of the 28,000 phages isolated by students in the PHIRE and SEA-PHAGES programs, over 14,000 individual phages infect M. smegmatis and over 2,600 of these are fully sequenced (https://phagesdb.org) (Table 1). These were isolated on exactly the same strain, M. smegmatis mc2155 (23), providing the greatest resolution into viral diversity of any such set of phages. Genomic comparisons show that most of these can be grouped into clusters of related phages (Cluster A, B, C, etc), using an arbitrary cutoff value of intracluster phages sharing more than 35% shared genes in pairwise analyses (38) (Table 1). Some of these clusters can be divided into subclusters, although the parameters for subdivision differ in different clusters (39, 40). Some phages (currently, there are three) have no close relatives and are “singletons” (Table 1). Several key findings about viral diversity and evolution are evident from this phage collection.

First is the heterogenous representation of the phages. For example, there are over 800 and 460 individual phages in Clusters A and B, respectively, but there are 15 clusters with half-a-dozen or fewer phages (Table 1). Second, there is substantial diversity within clusters and subclusters. For example, within Subcluster F1 some pairwise comparisons show phages with fewer than 50% shared genes. Third, the “boundaries” between different clusters and subclusters are fuzzy at best, such that different clustering parameters give you different groupings (Fig. 3). It is likely that there is an underlying continuum of diversity, albeit with uneven representation of individual phages (41), and network analysis reveals connections between some clusters (Fig. 4). Fourth, the phages have mosaic architectures generated by pervasive horizontal genetic exchange among phages mediated by illegitimate recombination events (7, 42). Last, ~70% of the phage genes are of unknown function and elucidating the roles of the many thousands of mycobacteriophage genes is both a major priority and a major challenge.

A majority of the Mycobacterium clusters contain temperate phages, with the others being lytic; 22 of the 36 clusters and all three singletons are temperate, and only 14 clusters are lytic (Table 1). However, it is relatively common for phages that genomically group in a “temperate” cluster to actually be lytic. A good example is phage D29 that is lytic and infects both M. smegmatis and M. tuberculosis, but genomically groups in Cluster A and most Cluster A phages are temperate (27). This phenomenon is explained by the emergence of clear plaque variants that may be easier to see and more attractive to pick and purify, and these phages usually have readily evident deletions of the repressor gene. Genome comparisons suggest that 10% of the 122 Cluster A2 phages are clear plaque variants.

Most of the temperate Mycobacterium phages code for an integration system including an attP attachment site and an integrase gene, including both tyrosine- and serine-integrases, and many different systems have been identified (37, 43). Most of the tyrosine-integrase systems use attB sites overlapping host tRNA genes, which is reconstructed after prophage formation, whereas the serine-integration systems use attB sites located within coding regions, leading to physiological manifestations in lysogenic cells (44). An advantage of examining nonmodel phages is the discovery of genetic novelties, especially in phage life cycle regulation. One notable example is the integration-dependent immunity systems, in which the attP site is oddly located within the repressor gene itself (45). The viral version of the repressor codes for an inactive protein because of a C-terminal signal that promotes degradation. Integration results in loss of the degradation tag and expression of a stable form of the repressor conferring superinfection immunity (45). In a second example, some phages use a tRNA-dependent lysogeny system, in which a phage-encoded tRNA is required to compensate for inactivation of the host tRNA at attB following integration (46). Interestingly, a mutant phage lacking its tRNA has a clear plaque phenotype, as stable lysogens are not viable due to loss of the host tRNA function (46).

Prophages and Viral Defense Systems

Because temperate phages of M. smegmatis are common (Table 1), many Mycobacterium strains are naturally lysogenic and carry their own prophages. There are relatively few sequenced strains of M. smegmatis, and the mc2155 strain is known to be prophage-free (47). Interestingly M. tuberculosis strains are also prophage-free, and this is not only true for lab strains but for the thousands of sequenced clinical isolates (37, 48). In contrast, many M. abscessus strains have been sequenced and they are replete with prophages. At least 75% of M. abscessus strains contain at least one prophage, and some contain six or more; the average number is about 1.5 (37). In general, these prophages are not closely related to M. smegmatis phages but are similarly diverse (37) (Fig. 3). Consolidating the Mycobacterium prophages and M. smegmatis phages into a common architecture reveals over 60 Clusters and 18 singletons (37) (Fig. 3). Little is known about the biology of these Mycobacterium prophages, but they warrant further investigation because of their potential role in determining the phage infection profiles of these clinical isolates as discussed further below.

Surprisingly, temperate phages and resident prophages are major sources of defense against lytic phage attack (49), greatly expanding the known bacterial pan-immune phage defense systems (5, 49), but having the advantage of being readily swapped between bacteria; they are thus central to the bacterium-phage arms race. These defense systems are heterotypic, defending against unrelated phages, and distinct from the repressor-mediated immunity protecting against lytic infection by the same or very closely related phages (homotypic defense) (50, 51) (Fig. 5). Such systems are lysogenically expressed and typically located near the repressor and integrase genes (Fig. 5). Phage Sbash provides an excellent example (Fig. 5), which specifically defends against phage Crossroads (51), and codes for a second ill-defined system defending generally against Cluster Q phages (51). However, there are numerous examples where many similarly located genes are also implicated in defense (Fig. 5) and await further investigation.

Phages as Toolboxes

Mycobacteriophages have not only revealed insights into viral diversity and evolution but have also provided essential molecular tools (52, 53). First, are the shuttle phasmids developed by Dr. Bill Jacobs Jr. and his colleagues, in which chimeric phages are constructed for efficient delivery of transposons, reporter genes, and allelic exchange substrates (54–57). Second are the integration-proficient plasmid vectors that enable construction of single-copy recombinant strains for complementation and other genetic analyses (58–60). Third are the recombineering systems that use mycobacteriophage-encoded recombination systems for construction of gene knockout and other types of mutants (61, 62). Some of these have been used in combinations such as the ORBIT system (63). Fourth is the use of several types of phage promoters for gene expression in Mycobacteria, including the highly active Pleft promoter of phage L5 (64, 65), and a calibrated suite of promoters from phage BPs with defined levels of promoter activity (66).

There are several other phage-derived tools that are less commonly used but may yet find widespread utility. One is a series of replication origins identified in plasmid-like replicating temperate prophages that are small, extrachromosomal, and relatively low copy number (67). A subset of temperate phages in Cluster A do not code for an attP-integrase system, and instead their prophages replicate extrachromosomally (67). At least eight distinct types have been described and use either protein-mediated or RNA-mediated origins of replication. Second is the use of temperate phage repressors as selectable markers (68). All temperate phages code for a repressor protein that is expressed from a prophage, and shuts down lytic growth, thus conferring immunity to superinfection by the same phage. A plasmid carrying the phage repressor can be introduced into a Mycobacterium strain by electroporation and transformants selected in the presence of a lytic derivative of the phage. This has been demonstrated in M. smegmatis and M. tuberculosis, using the repressor coded by phage Adephagia, and a repressor mutant of the phage (68). Relatively few genomically distinct phages infect M. tuberculosis, so the full repertoire of compatible systems may be limited, although they are numerous for M. smegmatis. If regulatory concerns impose constraints on the construction of antibiotic-resistant derivatives of virulent M. tuberculosis, repressor selection systems could find broad utility. It is also noteworthy that some tools such as the Bxb1 integration system has found broad biotechnological utility far beyond Mycobacterium genetics (69).

The engineering of lytically growing phages presents challenges as genetically selectable markers such as antibiotic resistance genes are less useful. To avoid the use of such markers, efficient recombination systems can yield recombinants at a sufficiently high frequency such that selection is not required and desirable mutants can be identified through screening (70). Bacteriophage Recombineering of Electroporated DNA (BRED) provides those capabilities by deploying the mycobacterial recombineering systems, and simple coelectroporation of phage genomic DNA and a synthetic DNA substrate (70). Primary plaques recovered by electroporation can then be screened by PCR, and mixed plaques containing both wild type and mutant alleles can be purified and rescreened by PCR. The limitations of BRED are the poor transfection of larger phage genomes, and that point mutations and insertions often require more extensive screening (70). Application of CRISPR-Cas selection against the parent phage (CRISPY-BRED) enhances the selective recovery of mutant phages and can also be used with electroporation of DNA substrates into phage-infected cells [Bacteriophage Recombineering with Infectious Particles (BRIP)] (71).

Phages as Medicines

The first therapeutic use of mycobacteriophages was to treat a young person with cystic fibrosis who had a disseminated M. abscessus infection following a bilateral lung transplant (72). The process of identifying therapeutic phages required a tour-de-force but was rewarded with favorable outcomes and greatly improved clinical status of the patient. Because discovery of phages from environmental samples did not provide many useful new phages, a subset of M. smegmatis phages chosen to represent the genomic diversity were used for screening. Three phages were identified—Muddy, BPs, and ZoeJ—as being the best therapeutic candidates, but both BPs and ZoeJ are temperate and needed to be engineered to be obligatorily lytic (72). In addition, BPs does not efficiently infect this clinical isolate (M. abscessus GD01), although a host range mutant with an amino acid substitution in the phage tail spike protein does. The three phages were prepared and administered as a cocktail at a dose of 109 plaque forming units (PFU) of each phage twice daily. The patient showed substantial improvement, and although it is unlikely that the infection was completely cleared, the patient was able to resume many normal activities; the phage administration continued for several years.

This case provides several important insights. First, that the intravenous administration of the phages was safe and no serious adverse reactions were observed (72). Second, that Mycobacterium infections likely require extended therapies with phages just as they do with antibiotics. Third, that the promise of therapeutic utilities for Mycobacterium infections warrants further investigation. What was equally striking is that so little is known about any of these types of phage therapies, including dosage, regimen, pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics, interactions between phages and antibiotics, and prospects of treatment failure due to phage resistance. It is also of note that the identification of suitable therapeutic phages likely would not have been feasible without drawing on the phage collection provided by the education programs and the insights gleaned from prior studies in mycobacteriophage biology (Fig. 1).

Screening of additional M. abscessus strains provided phages for treating a total of 20 consecutive case studies, providing insights into the prospects for broader therapeutic use (73). Of these, no data could be gleaned from five cases, and favorable outcomes were reported for 11 of the remaining 15; four cases showed no evidence of clinical or microbiological improvement (73). All of the patients were treated on a compassionate-use basis and have other clinical issues, and all continued to receive standard antibiotic treatments as well as the phages (73). Well-controlled clinical trials are needed to understand the therapeutic potential and the parameters influencing the outcomes, although patients with NTM infections are an especially difficult cohort to recruit for such trials (74). Nonetheless, the compassionate use cases show the strong safety profile and encouraging outcomes for many of these difficult cases and provide encouragement for further exploration of the therapeutic utility.

An especially critical limitation to broadening NTM phage therapies is the great variation in the phage infection profiles of M. abscessus clinical isolates (75). This requires that each isolate be empirically tested for its sensitivity to phages and to be killed efficiently by the phages in vitro (and hopefully in vivo). Broadening phage applications to more clinical isolates requires expanding the current repertoire of therapeutically useful phages (currently only 6-8 parent phages and their host range mutants) and elucidating the genetic determinants of specificity, in both the bacteria and the phages. One specific issue is the different profiles displayed by smooth and rough colony morphotypes of M. abscessus (75). The smooth colony types can be considered “wild type” and are present in environmental locations and are likely sources of many infections. Few phages both efficiently infect and efficiently kill the smooth strains, which represent 40-50% of all clinical isolates. Thus, a major initiative is needed to understand limitations to phage killing in smooth strains. Rough strains can be considered “mutants” and are defective in synthesis or export of cell wall glycopeptidolipids. At least one phage within the limited phage repertoire efficiently infects approximately 75% of the rough strains (75), and these have been the primary subjects of additional therapeutic cases.

It is noteworthy that the abundant and variable prophage content of M. abscessus clinical isolates likely contributes to the variation in their phage infection profiles, given the discovery of prophage-mediated defense systems as described above. Because M. tuberculosis isolates have limited genetic diversity and no prophages (48), there is also much less variation in the phage infection profiles (76). This raises the prospects that a small defined phage cocktail might be broadly therapeutically useful, and could pave the way toward clinical trials to determine the potential utility of phage therapies for TB (76, 77).

Looking Forward: A Vision for the Coming Decade

The next ten years promise exciting advances in phage biology and the integrated biology-education-medicine approach (Fig. 1) has much more to offer. Structural biology is expected to play a major role in understanding host recognition by the phages, and the recent cryoelectron microscopy (CryoEM) and cryoelectron tomography (CryoET) structures of phages Bxb1 and Douge show what is possible (78, 79) (Fig. 6). With such a large phage collection and the great diversity of structural proteins, many additional phage structures can be anticipated in the near future, providing structural insights into host recognition. Additional surprises are likely, including unexpected protein components and modification of virion proteins including extensive glycosylation (80). These structures will facilitate phage engineering and the design of new phages with novel specificities.

Synthetic genomics will provide great flexibility into the design and construction of phages with new specificities, and new utilities including therapy. Synthesizing mycobacteriophage DNA segments can be challenging due to the relatively high G + C% content, but newer synthesis technologies provide good solutions to this. The synthesized DNA fragments can be assembled using Golden Gate Assembly and rebooted by electroporation into M. smegmatis. Individual DNA fragments can be readily modified and used to build different phage variants, limited primarily by our imagination of what is possible. DNA synthesis of dsDNA tailed phage genomes in the 40 to 80 kbp range is still quite expensive but it will become cheaper as the technology advances and more widely used. Simple, cheap, whole genome synthesis will also enable construction of phages for which only bioinformatic data are available.

Machine learning and AI have a key role to play in functional genomics and are anticipated to illuminate the phage “dark matter” of the biosphere. In the mycobacteriophage genomes, there are more than 200,000 genes of unknown function, representing over 20,000 distinct gene phamilies (81). Many of these may influence bacterium-phage arms race, defending against phage infection in lysogeny or exclusion in lytic growth, or as antidefense systems to counter the bacterial immune system. The ability to predict protein structures using AlphaFold (82) as well as protein–protein interactions will be transformative in addressing these questions and provide a computational impetus to test specific hypotheses about gene function. Synthetic genomics and structural insights will provide experimental evidence of these predictions.

Finally, the iREC-structured science education programs will continue to enrich our knowledge of phage diversity and evolution. There are more phages in the biosphere than students, and the vast diversity shows there are many more to be discovered. Comparative genomics of phages infecting Arthrobacter, Corynebacteria, Gordonia, Streptomyces, Microbacterium, and Rhodococcus strains show similar complexities to the Mycobacterium phages, and with hundreds or thousands or other species and strains within the Actinobacteria phylum there is so much more to learn about these phages, even within this fairly narrow slice of phylogenetic space. This journey from the first sequenced mycobacteriophage through undergraduate laboratories to patient benefits illustrates how the integration of curiosity-driven science, inclusive education, and effective translational can advance microbiology in the 21st century.
