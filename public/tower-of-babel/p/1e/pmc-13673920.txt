Molecular characterization in elite rock climbers: insights from multi-omics analysis
Authors: Rongqi Liang, Jingjing Li, Long Cao, Cheng Wang, Shaoqin Zhang, Xing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveElite rock climbing is characterized by sustained upper-limb-dominant loading and complex systemic demands. However, the molecular signatures associated with elite climbing-related adaptation remain poorly defined. This study integrates physiological, functional, transcriptomic, and metabolomic profiling to characterize the potential mechanisms underlying these adaptations.DesignThis is a cross-sectional study comparing the molecular profiles of elite rock climbers and matched non-climbers.MethodsA total of 40 participants were recruited, comprising 20 elite rock climbers certified as National Master Sportsmen (athlete group) and 20 age, sex, and body mass matched non climbers (control group). Participants underwent functional assessments for vascular reactivity via flow-mediated dilation and climbing specific forearm endurance through dead hang and intermittent grip tests. Fasting venous blood samples were analyzed for biochemical markers, while integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles were established to identify molecular signatures. Data were analyzed using differential expression profiling, functional enrichment, and joint pathway analysis, with results validated by qRT-PCR.ResultsThe athlete group exhibited significantly superior functional performance characterized by enhanced vascular endothelial function and prolonged forearm endurance alongside widespread systemic adaptations, as reflected by elevated levels of cortisol, creatine kinase, blood urea nitrogen, hemoglobin, plasma volume and total hemoglobin mass (p < 0.05). RNA sequencing identified 850 differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), which were predominantly enriched in cAMP, Apelin, PPAR, TNF, and HIF-1 signaling pathways. Metabolomics detected 71 differential metabolites (VIP ≥ 1, |log₂FC| ≥1.5, FDR < 0.05), enriched in Insulin secretion, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, fatty acid degradation, and amino acid metabolism. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that elite climbers exhibited multidimensional molecular adaptation signatures associated with the specific demands of climbing, mainly characterized by enhanced metabolic flexibility, vascular regulation, and optimized metabolic homeostasis. These changes collectively optimized metabolic flexibility, redox homeostasis and controlled inflammation. Validation via qRT-PCR confirmed the RNA-Seq results.ConclusionsElite rock climbers exhibited multilayered physiological, transcriptional, and metabolic features associated with energy homeostasis, redox balance, inflammatory modulation, and neuromuscular performance. These insights establish a molecular blueprint for evidence-based training optimization and personalized sports interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13192-x.Keywords: Elite rock climbers, Transcriptomic, Metabolomic, Molecular Adaptations

Introduction

As a rapidly emerging sport worldwide, rock climbing has gained widespread popularity due to its distinctive appeal. Combining physical fitness, technical skill, and psychological demands, it is characterized by high levels of challenge, strong environmental dependency, and notable safety considerations [1]. In recent years, participation in rock climbing has increased markedly worldwide, with estimates suggesting over 50 million participants, of whom approximately 25 million are regular practitioners. However, in stark contrast to the scale of general participation, the number of high-level competitive rock climbers remains relatively limited (fewer than 1,000 elite athletes globally). This is likely due to increasingly stringent selection requirements, including younger age, lower body fat percentages, and arm spans exceeding height. Individuals with these characteristics may have a competitive advantage and are more likely to achieve high performance in competitive climbing [2].

High-level competitive rock climbers, from talent identification to daily training, are required to undertake intensive whole-body conditioning alongside core stability exercises, both of which are fundamental for optimising athletic performance. Under the cumulative load of daily high-intensity training, systematic monitoring of physiological parameters not only informs the refinement of training strategies but also underpins athletes’ ability to attain peak performance in competition, thereby maximising individual outcomes and competitive ranking [3]. Despite the global expansion of sport rock climbing, investigations into the physiological, metabolic, and molecular mechanisms underpinning elite-level performance remain comparatively scarce [4]. To date, most research has concentrated on assessments of fundamental physical capacities, psychological traits, and descriptive analyses of training methodologies, as well as injury surveillance, particularly with regard to injuries affecting the fingers, shoulders, elbows, and knees [5]. Elite rock climbers are required to meet the complex and interdependent demands of strength, endurance, and psychological stress during competition, yet their overall physiological status, metabolic features, and molecular regulatory mechanisms remain insufficiently characterized [6]. Conventional physiological markers and training assessments provide only limited insight into functional adaptations and may not fully capture the metabolic and molecular characteristics associated with long-term elite climbing training. To overcome these limitations, the application of transcriptomics and metabolomics in exercise science has demonstrated the potential of these approaches to systematically elucidate exercise-related molecular responses and energy metabolism regulation, thereby offering novel perspectives for understanding physiological and molecular responses under conditions of intensive training and competition. Given that elite climbing requires coordinated neuromuscular control, sustained force production, metabolic efficiency, and recovery capacity, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling is well suited to capture molecular and metabolic features associated with elite climbing status that may not be fully reflected by conventional physiological indicators alone. However, the application of these omics-based methods to elite climbing populations remains markedly limited. Therefore, the present study integrates physiological measurements with transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to comprehensively assess the metabolic characteristics and molecular regulatory patterns of high-level competitive climbers compared with non-athletic controls. This approach aims to provide a theoretical and methodological framework for understanding molecular features associated with elite rock climbing, supporting physiological monitoring, and optimising evidence-based training strategies.

Methods

Subjects

A total of 40 healthy participants were recruited and assigned to two groups. The athlete group comprised 20 elite rock climbers (10 males and 10 females; age 21.0 ± 2.8 years; height 166.9 ± 7.1 cm; body mass 59.7 ± 7.0 kg). All rock climbers were officially certified as National Master Sportsmen by the General Administration of Sport of China, had at least 5 years of systematic training experience (6.8 ± 2.2 years), and reported an average weekly training volume of approximately 15–20 h, verified through training logs and coach confirmation. The control group comprised 20 healthy individuals (10 males and 10 females; age 21.4 ± 2.2 years; height 167.3 ± 6.8 cm; body mass 60.2 ± 6.5 kg) who were matched to the rock climbers in terms of age, sex, height, and body mass. None of the controls engaged in regular structured exercise (less than 2 h per week during the previous year). Participants were included if they were free from acute or chronic injuries and major medical conditions; those with cardiovascular disease or recent surgery were excluded. The training histories of the rock climbers were verified by standardized questionnaires, training logs, and coach confirmation, whereas the physical activity status of controls was verified by standardized questionnaires. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of China University of Geosciences (Wuhan) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample collection

Venous blood samples were collected between 07:00 and 09:00 following an overnight fast of at least 12 h, during which participants had free access to water. Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise for 72 h and to abstain from smoking, alcohol consumption, and protein-rich meals for 24 h prior to sampling. A standardized questionnaire covering medical history, medication and dietary-supplement use, and recent physical activity was completed by all participants to assess eligibility. For female participants, sampling was scheduled between days 10 - 15 of the menstrual cycle. Cycle phase was verified using ovulation test strips to identify the follicular phase. Blood was drawn into EDTA anticoagulant tubes and serum separator tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Routine physiological indicators, including testosterone, cortisol, creatine kinase, blood urea nitrogen, uric acid, hemoglobin, plasma volume, and total hemoglobin mass, were measured. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed using blood samples collected at the standardized sampling point.

Biochemical determinations

Hemoglobin (HGB) concentration was measured using a HemoCue® Hb 201 + system (Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). Plasma volume (PV) and total hemoglobin mass (tHb-mass) were determined using the optimized carbon monoxide (CO) rebreathing method developed by Schmidt and Prommer in 2005. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatine kinase (CK), and uric acid (UA) were quantified with an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). Serum testosterone (T) and cortisol (CORT) were determined by chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) using an automated immunoassay platform (UniCel DxI 800, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA).

Assessment sequence

After blood collection, participants rested quietly before the flow-mediated dilation (FMD) assessment. FMD was performed before any exhaustive functional tests to minimize the acute effects of exercise-induced vascular responses or muscular fatigue. Functional assessments were subsequently conducted according to a standardized sequence. Because both the dead-hang test and the intermittent grip endurance test were exhaustive tasks, they were performed on separate testing days with at least 24 h of recovery between tests to minimize residual forearm fatigue.

Functional assessments

Flow-mediated dilation

Vascular reactivity was assessed using standardized FMD. Participants rested in a supine position for at least 15 min in a quiet, temperature-controlled environment prior to assessment. High-resolution ultrasound imaging (linear-array transducer ≥ 10 MHz) was used to visualize the right brachial artery, positioned 5–10 cm proximal to the antecubital fossa. Following a 60 s baseline recording of arterial diameter, a pneumatic cuff placed distal on the forearm was inflated to ≥ 200 mmHg for 5 min to induce arterial occlusion. Upon rapid cuff release, arterial diameter was continuously recorded for 3 min to capture the peak vasodilatory response. FMD% was determined as the relative change in arterial diameter from baseline to peak dilation.

Dead-hang time

Sustained forearm endurance was assessed using a dead-hang test. Participants hung bilaterally from a standardized fingerboard rung (20 mm depth) using a predefined grip position (using the same predefined grip position across participants), with both arms extended overhead and feet completely off the ground. The test commenced when both feet left the ground and was terminated when the participant could no longer maintain the prescribed grip or when finger contact was lost. Time to failure (s) was recorded as dead-hang time. This test has been widely used to assess climbing-specific forearm endurance and demonstrates good reliability and discriminative validity [7].

Intermittent grip endurance

Intermittent forearm endurance was evaluated using a standardized intermittent grip endurance protocol. Participants performed repeated hang-rest cycles on the same rung, consisting of 7 s hanging followed by 3 s rest. During hanging phases, feet were fully off the ground, whereas during rest phases, feet were allowed to lightly touch the ground while maintaining hand contact with the rung. The test continued until the participant was unable to complete the prescribed hanging duration or maintain the required grip. Total number of completed cycles and total work time (s) were recorded [8].

RNA sequencing

Peripheral venous blood samples (5 mL per subject) were collected into EDTA-coated vacutainer tubes (BD Biosciences) and processed within 2 hours to minimize ex vivo transcriptional alterations. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare): whole blood was layered over Ficoll-Paque, centrifuged at 400 × g for 30 min at room temperature with the brake off, and the PBMC interface was harvested. Cells were washed twice with PBS, and viability was confirmed by trypan blue exclusion (> 90% required). PBMC pellets were immediately lysed in TRIzol Reagent or stored at − 80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) with on-column DNase I digestion following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA 6000 Nano Kit. Samples with high-quality total RNA showed A260/280 and A260/230 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 and an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.5. RNA was stored at − 80°C prior to sequencing. Library preparation was performed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit (Illumina, Cat# RS-122-2101). mRNA was enriched from 500 ng of total RNA using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The enriched mRNA was fragmented into short sequences using fragmentation buffer at 94°C for 5 minutes and reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized using DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs, and the appropriate buffer. Double-stranded cDNA (ds-cDNA) fragments were purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The ds-cDNA was then subjected to end repair, the addition of a 3’-A overhang, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification to construct sequencing libraries. Library fragments were size-selected using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to obtain insert sizes of approximately 200–300 bp. The effective concentration of the library (> 2 nM) was quantified accurately using qPCR to ensure library quality. Quantified libraries were pooled based on their effective concentrations and the desired data output, then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads.Approximately 50 million paired-end reads were generated for each sample.

Raw sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.9). Raw sequencing data were processed to remove adapter contaminants and low-quality reads using Trimmomatic (v0.39). Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p13) using the STAR aligner (v2.7.9a). The uniquely mapped read percentage for all samples was above 90%. Gene-level counts were generated using featureCounts (v2.0.1) based on GENCODE annotation (v45). Genes with counts greater than 10 in at least three samples were considered in the downstream analysis, while all others were defined as lowly expressed genes and removed. Differential expression analysis between the two groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.34.0; https://bioconductor.org/packages/DESeq2). Raw read counts were normalized, and differential expression was assessed using a negative binomial generalized linear model. Resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap R package (v1.0.13; https://cran.r-project.org/package=pheatmap). Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package (v4.0.0; https://cran.r-project.org/package = ggplot2). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with |log2 Fold Change (FC) | ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. The transcriptomic sequence data have been deposited in the NCBI databases (accession to cite for these SRA data: PRJNA1068095).

Metabolomics analysis

Samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice and vortexed for 10 s. A 50 µL of sample and 300 µL of extraction solution (ACN: Methanol = 1:4, V/V) containing internal standards were added to a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. The sample was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Two hundred microliters of the supernatant were collected, stored at − 20 °C for 30 min, and then centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C. A 180 µL aliquot of supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis. The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD, https://sciex.com.cn/; MS, QTRAP® System, https://sciex.com/). The analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm*100 mm); column temperature, 40 °C; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; injection volume, 2µL; solvent system, water (0.1% formic acid): acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid); solvent B gradient program, 5% to 20% in 2 min, increased to 60% in the following 3 min, increased to 99% in 1 min and held for 1.5 min, then returned to 5% within 0.1 min and held for 2.4 min.

LIT and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired on a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (QTRAP), QTRAP® LC-MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: source temperature 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive), -4500 V (negative); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), curtain gas (CUR) were set at 55, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; the collision gas (CAD) was set to high. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 µmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. A specific set of MRM transitions was monitored for each period based on the metabolites eluting within that period.

The mass spectrum data were processed by Software Analyst 1.6.3. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted to identify the metabolite discrimination between the two group samples. Significantly regulated metabolites between groups were determined by Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) ≥ 1, |Log2FC| ≥1.5 and FDR < 0.05. For pathway enrichment analysis, the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) was used to find enriched metabolic signaling pathways involving differential metabolites (DEMs) between two groups.

Transcriptome and metabolomics joint-pathway analysis

Joint-pathway analysis was performed using R software. DEGs from RNA-seq and DEMs from metabolomics were uploaded as input data. Gene identifiers were converted to Entrez IDs, and metabolites were mapped using HMDB and KEGG compound IDs. The analysis was conducted for Homo sapiens using the KEGG pathway library. Over-representation analysis was used for pathway enrichment, combined with topology analysis based on relative-betweenness centrality. The background/reference set consisted of all genes and metabolites detected in the respective datasets. Pathways with p-adj < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Quantitative real-time PCR validation

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to validate the RNA sequencing results. The target genes for qRT-PCR validation were selected based primarily on their biological relevance to the key pathways and functional processes identified in the transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was conducted in biological triplicates using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNase H Plus) (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) on a real-time PCR system. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 60 s. All primers were designed and synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and their sequences are listed in Table 1. β-Actin was used as the reference gene. The relative expression levels for each target gene were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of physiological and functional data were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Before parametric testing, normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples Student’s t-tests. When data deviated from normality, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. To control the risk of Type I error from multiple biochemical and performance outcomes, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR method. For RNA-seq analysis, raw count data were normalized and low-abundance genes were filtered according to the criteria described above, followed by differential expression analysis with FDR correction. For metabolomics analysis, peak intensities were normalised, missing values and low-quality features were filtered, and differential metabolites were identified using FDR-corrected multiple comparisons as described above. Potential batch effects in both omics datasets were assessed using PCA and sample clustering, with batch correction applied where necessary. A two-sided adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman correlation analysis with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was performed between selected metabolites, genes, and functional performance indicators.

Results

Physiological and biochemical analysis

Compared with the control group, the athlete group exhibited distinct systemic physiological and biochemical features. Regarding endocrine profiles, testosterone levels did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05), whereas cortisol concentrations were significantly elevated in athletes (p < 0.01). Metabolic stress-related indicators, including creatine kinase, blood urea nitrogen, and uric acid, were markedly increased in the athlete group (p < 0.01), suggesting increased metabolic stress and alterations in muscle turnover, protein metabolism, and purine metabolism. Furthermore, athletes showed more favorable hematological and blood-volume-related profiles, with significantly higher hemoglobin concentration, plasma volume, and total hemoglobin mass (p < 0.05), indicating enhanced oxygen-carrying capacity and circulatory efficiency (Fig. 1).

Functional performance outcomes

Significant group differences were observed for all functional outcomes between the athlete group and the control group. The athlete group demonstrated significantly higher FMD% (Fig. 2A), longer dead-hang time (Fig. 2B), and greater intermittent grip endurance performance, as reflected by both the number of completed cycles and total work time (Fig. 2C, D)(all p < 0.05).

Transcriptomic analysis

To investigate transcriptional differences between the athlete group and the control group, RNA sequencing was performed on samples from both groups. Volcano plots showed DEGs between the athlete group and control group (Fig. 3A). A total of 850 DEGs were identified, including 518 upregulated and 332 downregulated genes. Hierarchical clustering, visualized in a heatmap, further confirmed these findings by revealing distinct expression patterns that clearly separate the two groups (Fig. 3B). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for the DEGs. GO enrichment analysis revealed enriched terms including response to molecule of bacterial origin, positive regulation of angiogenesis, response to lipopolysaccharide, leukocyte migration, positive regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration, and regulation of vasculature development (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified the most significantly enriched pathways associated with these DEGs as the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cAMP signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and Apelin signaling pathway (Fig. 3D). To validate the RNA-seq results, qRT-PCR was performed on 10 DEGs. As shown in Fig. 3E, the qRT-PCR results are consistent with the RNA-seq data. These findings confirmed the reliability of the RNA-seq results.

Multivariate statistical analysis and identification of differential metabolites

PCA was employed as an unsupervised multivariate analysis method to assess overall metabolic differences between groups and intra-group variability. The metabolomic profiles of the athlete group differed markedly from those of the control group (Fig. 4A). To further evaluate group differences, OPLS-DA was performed as a supervised multivariate analysis, which demonstrated clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 4B). The robustness of the OPLS-DA model was assessed using 200 permutation tests. The original model showed strong explanatory and predictive performance, with R2Y = 0.99 and Q2 = 0.804. Permutation testing confirmed that these values were significantly higher than those obtained from the permuted models, with p-values of < 0.01 for both Q2 and R2Y, indicating that the model was unlikely to be overfitted or obtained by chance. A total of 71 differential metabolites were identified, of which 54 were upregulated and 17 were downregulated (Fig. 4C). These metabolic alterations were mainly associated with pathways such as efferocytosis, regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, central carbon metabolism in cancer, insulin secretion, the cAMP signaling pathway, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Fig. 4D).

Combined analysis of transcriptome and metabolome

To explore potential upstream regulators of metabolomic changes, transcriptional profiling was performed using the same samples. Integrated metabolic pathway and gene set enrichment analyses identified 34 pathways that were significantly enriched at both the metabolomic and transcriptomic levels (Fig. 5A), including glycerolipid metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, apelin signaling pathway, and the HIF-1 signaling pathway, among others (Fig. 5B).

Correlation analysis of metabolites and genes with functional parameters

Correlation analysis showed that, at the metabolomic level, lactic acid, malic acid, S1P (d18:1), and L-glutamic acid were generally positively correlated with FMD%, dead-hang time, number of completed cycles, and total work time. Among these metabolites, lactic acid and L-glutamic acid exhibited the most consistent positive associations across functional parameters, whereas Carnitine C22:6 showed only a weak positive trend. L-arginine and arachidonic acid were negatively correlated with these functional indices, with arachidonic acid showing a more pronounced inverse association (Fig. 6A). At the transcriptomic level, SOD2, NAMPT, KLF2, DGAT2, ADM, SIRT3, MAP1LC3A, NOS3, ACSS3, ACAD11, and APLN were generally positively associated with functional performance. NAMPT, DGAT2, and ADM showed strong positive correlations with multiple functional parameters, whereas BECN1 displayed relatively weak correlations (Fig. 6B). Strong positive correlations were observed between the upregulated genes SOD2, NAMPT, KLF2, and NOS3 and the metabolites including L-arginine, lactic acid, and malic acid in elite rock climbers. Negative correlations were observed between multiple genes and Carnitine C22:6, L-glutamine, and arachidonic acid (Fig. 6C). These findings suggest that functional performance may be closely associated with coordinated changes in specific metabolites and function-related gene expression.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically integrate physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data to elucidate the molecular and metabolic characteristics of elite rock climbers compared with university students. A coordinated adaptive network involving hormonal regulation, intracellular signaling, vascular adaptation, and metabolic remodeling was identified, potentially contributing to improved energy efficiency, endothelial function, immune–inflammatory balance, neural regulation, and metabolic homeostasis. During climbing activity, elite rock climbers are repeatedly exposed to high-intensity intermittent grip contractions, sustained forearm ischemia–reperfusion, and recurrent hypoxic stress. These exercise-related physiological stimuli may contribute not only to enhanced climbing-related performance but also to greater tolerance to metabolic stress and improved resilience to training-related stress. Importantly, these molecular and metabolic alterations were accompanied by enhanced performance capacity, thereby providing functional support for the observed multi-omics adaptations.

Elite rock climbers often exhibit distinct endocrine profiles, which may be associated with the sport-specific physiological characteristics and performance demands of rock climbing. Rock climbing imposes substantial demands on energy metabolism, muscle function, and endocrine regulation, reflecting physiological features specific to elite climbers. In the present study, testosterone levels in rock climbers were slightly higher than in university students, but the difference did not reach statistical significance [9]. This finding is broadly consistent with previous studies suggesting that grip-intensive exercise is associated with elevated testosterone and growth hormone levels, which may support anabolic responses to repeated grip-intensive work, although chronic resting levels may vary depending on training history and recovery status. This lack of significant difference may be attributable to factors such as inter-individual variability, age, training history, and circadian fluctuations in testosterone levels [10]. Testosterone promotes muscle protein synthesis, increases bone density, and facilitates recovery, thereby supporting protein accretion and forearm strength essential for sustained grip and local endurance [11]. Cortisol is the primary stress hormone, regulating blood glucose and metabolism during exercise, mobilizing proteins for gluconeogenesis, and promoting fluid retention to protect joint structures [12]. Its levels are jointly regulated by exercise load and recovery status, and sustained elevations may reflect increased physiological stress or insufficient recovery, suggesting a potential imbalance between training stimulus and recovery processes that may affect overall homeostasis [13]. Elevated creatine kinase levels, commonly observed in elite rock climbers, do not necessarily indicate muscle damage but may reflect micro-injury and remodeling of forearm fibers, representing a physiological adaptation that enhances enzymatic activity, metabolic efficiency, ATP regeneration, and myofibrillar contractile performance [14]. Hemoglobin levels were slightly higher than those observed in the general population. This may contribute to oxygen transport capacity, thereby supporting both short-term explosive force and local endurance during climbing movements [15]. Consistent with this interpretation, the athlete group also demonstrated markedly prolonged dead-hang time and superior intermittent grip endurance, reflecting enhanced oxygen delivery, local metabolic efficiency, and fatigue resistance in the forearm musculature. These performance advantages align with evidence that rock climbers exhibit superior isometric fatigue resistance and forearm endurance compared with strength-trained athletes and aerobically trained individuals, linked to optimized oxygen utilization and local metabolic efficiency during climbing-specific tasks [16]. Blood urea nitrogen and uric acid levels were significantly higher in elite climbers compared with university students. These differences were associated with the purine metabolism and protein catabolism profiles identified in the multi-omics analysis [17]. Considering resting creatine kinase and other catabolic markers, these results suggest that rock climbers exhibit metabolic adaptations characterized by energy redistribution and a balance between protein synthesis and degradation, providing a mechanistic basis for metabolic and functional adjustments [18].

At the molecular level, exercise can induce rapid and pronounced changes in gene expression in vivo. This study suggests that rock climbers exhibit multilayered regulation of energy sensing, inflammatory responses, lipid metabolism, redox homeostasis, and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. ACAD11 is involved in fatty-acid β-oxidation and may be upregulated under conditions of high energy demand to accelerate lipid catabolism. It is also transcriptionally induced by p53 during glucose starvation to sustain oxidative phosphorylation [19]. Moreover, ACAD11 likely interacts synergistically with genes such as ACSS3 and DGAT2 within the lipid-metabolic network. ACSS3 furnishes substrates for energy production by catalyzing acetate metabolism and the synthesis of acetyl-CoA [20]. DGAT2 participates in triacylglycerol synthesis to regulate the balance between energy storage and utilization [21]. Within the fatty-acid metabolic pathway, ACAD11 and ACSL1 occupy central roles. ACAD11 contributes to fatty-acid breakdown and energy production, while ACSL1 catalyzes fatty-acid activation and thereby enables their entry into the β-oxidation pathway. Notably, deletion of ACSL1 in skeletal muscle markedly impairs fatty-acid oxidation and disrupts systemic glucose homeostasis [22]. The results also showed a pronounced upregulation of SOD2 in rock climbers, indicating enhanced mitochondrial antioxidant defense mediated by this manganese-dependent superoxide dismutase. SOD2 activation is observed alongside increased metabolic load and ROS production, which may mitigate oxidative stress and help preserve mitochondrial function [23]. These genes act cooperatively to maintain fatty-acid metabolic homeostasis and preserve mitochondrial function and energy supply, thereby providing a potential molecular basis for muscle function maintenance and training adaptation. HIF-1 activation reflects adaptations to hypoxia or high metabolic load through enhanced glycolysis and oxygen delivery [24]. The transcriptomic signature further underscores the potential importance of the Apelin signaling pathway in vascular adaptation and metabolic homeostasis in elite rock climbers. Activation of the apelin–APJ signaling axis may reflect a close coupling between vascular regulation and metabolic demand. During climbing, repeated gripping and localized ischemia could stimulate endothelial adaptation, whereby this pathway is potentially associated with enhanced eNOS-mediated nitric oxide production, which may improve microvascular perfusion and help alleviate oxygen delivery constraints. This pathway is likely involved in maintaining sustained grip force output and delaying fatigue under prolonged isometric loading. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant enrichment of the core ligand APLN and its receptor APLNR, alongside the coordinated upregulation of downstream effectors KLF2 and NOS3 (eNOS). This enhancement of the Apelin–APJ axis is consistent with its established role in promoting endothelial function, vasodilation, and vascular adaptation in response to exercise training, thereby supporting improved perfusion under the repeated mechanical loading and ischemia-reperfusion that are characteristic of elite rock climbing [25]. Crucially, the differential expression of autophagy-related genes BECN1 and MAP1LC3A, in conjunction with the transcription factor EGR1, reveals a potential role for Apelin signaling in maintaining proteostasis and mitochondrial quality control. These findings suggest that repeated localized ischemia-reperfusion and intermittent hypoxic stress during rock climbing are key physiological drivers of the observed pathway enrichment. Under high-intensity intermittent isometric contractions, restricted blood flow in forearm muscles leads to reduced oxygen availability and elevated metabolic stress, resulting in HIF-1-centered metabolic reprogramming. This mechanism may enhance glycolytic flux while suppressing mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, enabling dynamic energy allocation between short bursts of force production and sustained contractions under climbing-specific hypoxic conditions.

This study further indicates that ADM, NAMPT and SIRT3 may function synergistically within the antioxidant network of athletes, with ADM acting as an exercise-responsive endogenous antioxidant peptide that mitigates ischemic injury and suppresses ROS production, and engages SIRT3 to promote mitochondrial function and enhance cellular tolerance to oxidative stress [26]. NAMPT sustains cellular energy and redox homeostasis via NAD+ biogenesis, potentially contributing to mitochondrial protection and SOD2 activity through the NAD+–SIRT1–FoxO3a axis [27]. MAP1LC3A cooperates with SOD2 via mitochondrial autophagy to remove damaged mitochondria, while SIRT3 may directly activate SOD2 through deacetylation [28]. The coordinated upregulation of these factors suggests the involvement of a multidimensional ROS–NAD+/FoxO–antioxidant/mitophagy network in rock climbers, which may be associated with oxidative stress tolerance and related inflammation-and immune-regulatory pathways [29]. Alterations in these pathways, including co-activation of TNF, NF-κB, and Toll-like receptor signaling with arachidonic acid metabolism, as well as differential expression of CXCL1, CXCL8, CXCR1, and CXCR2, indicate a distinctive inflammatory adaptation. These pathways converge on NF-κB as a central hub, where TNF and Toll-like receptors activate IKK complex, leading to IκB degradation and subsequent nuclear translocation of NF-κB p65/p50 dimers. Chemokines mediate immune responses with NF-κB regulating CXCL1 and CXCL8 transcription to reinforce the CXC chemokine axis for neutrophil recruitment and vascular remodeling. CXCL8 acts via CXCR1 and CXCR2, with CXCR2 expressed on endothelial cells and involved in angiogenesis [30]. Exercise-related stimuli are associated with CXCR2 expression in muscle endothelium, which may promote local angiogenesis and exert anti-inflammatory effects on immune cells. Meanwhile, CXCR1 and CXCR2 facilitate T-lymphocyte chemotaxis, supporting immune system maintenance [31].

Metabolism serves as a central interface linking molecular regulation to organismal physiology, maintaining energy balance, cellular homeostasis, and adaptive capacity. In this study, elevated lactate, pyruvate, and TCA cycle intermediates and related metabolites, such as malic acid and L-glutamate, suggest flexible coordination among glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and oxidative phosphorylation. Lactate serves as a key connector between glycolysis and the TCA cycle and can be produced from excess pyruvate even when oxygen is sufficient, highlighting its role as an intrinsic product of glycolytic metabolism rather than a by-product of hypoxia [32]. These metabolic characteristics may support sustained exercise capacity during climbing-specific intermittent tasks by facilitating efficient mitochondrial energy turnover, optimizing glycolysis-TCA cycle coupling, and limiting the accumulation of fatigue-associated metabolites [33]. Concurrent elevation of malic acid may reflect active mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, consistent with sustained energy supply and delayed fatigue development. Notably, enrichment of the central carbon metabolism in cancer pathway should not be interpreted as evidence of cancer-related biology in rock climbers. Although annotated within a cancer-related context, this pathway reflects conserved central carbon metabolic processes, including glycolysis, pyruvate–lactate interconversion, TCA cycle activity, and glutamate-associated carbon flux, which are fundamental to exercise-induced metabolic adaptation [34]. Accordingly, this enrichment is more likely indicative of increased central carbon metabolic activity in response to exercise, whereas the disease-oriented pathway label reflects annotation overlap and should therefore be interpreted with caution [35]. The lipidomic profile of rock climbers also showed systematic remodeling, with enrichment in pathways related to fatty acid elongation, fatty acid degradation, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis. These features indicate enhanced lipid substrate utilization, potential glycogen-sparing effects, and endurance-related metabolic characteristics. Key lipid metabolism markers, including ketone bodies and carnitine, may indicate active fatty acid β-oxidation and dynamic mitochondrial regulation. Ketone bodies, products of hepatic mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, are synthesized via 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase, whereas carnitine mediates long-chain fatty acid transport, maintains mitochondrial membrane potential, modulates the acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio, activates the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, and promotes glycogen catabolism [36]. Beyond energy provision, lipid metabolism may also be linked to inflammation, vascular function, and neuromuscular coordination, illustrating multi-level coupling between metabolic and physiological characteristics in rock climbers.

Elevated levels of amino acids and related metabolites, including glutamate, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and metabolites involved in glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, reflect their dual roles as substrates for energy metabolism and precursors for neurotransmitters and vascular regulators. Glutamate replenishes TCA cycle intermediates while contributing to excitatory neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity [37]. Phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolism supports dopamine and norepinephrine synthesis, which may be associated with arousal, attention, and stress responsiveness during cognitively demanding and high-risk movements. Arginine is involved in microvascular dilation, local perfusion, oxygen delivery, and metabolic waste clearance through nitric oxide production, all of which are important for sustained muscle function in climbing [38]. However, the present study observed lower circulating arginine levels in rock climbers compared with the control population, which may be associated with altered arginine utilization or redistribution in relation to climbing-specific metabolic demands [39]. Arginine also supports protein synthesis, participates in the urea cycle for ammonia detoxification, and contributes to muscle function and repair through creatine and polyamine synthesis [40]. Collectively, these findings suggest that arginine metabolism may be preferentially involved in pathways related to energy supply, vascular regulation, nitrogen balance, and muscular function in rock climbers.

The integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in this study suggest that elite climbers display multidimensional molecular and metabolic differences compared with university student controls. These differences involve pathways related to energy supply and demand, immune–inflammatory balance, neural function, and metabolic homeostasis. Core metabolic pathways, including pyruvate metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and purine/pyrimidine metabolism, were closely aligned with key signaling axes, including AMPK, the apelin signaling pathway, the cAMP signaling pathway, and HIF-1. These signaling pathways represent coordinated regulators of energy and oxygen homeostasis. AMPK is generally considered to function as a cellular energy sensor activated under low ATP conditions and may promote glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation; HIF-1 mediates hypoxic adaptation by enhancing glycolytic gene expression; the apelin/APJ axis contributes to vascular dilation, angiogenesis, and tissue perfusion; and the cAMP pathway regulates catecholamine-driven energy mobilization and cardiovascular responses via PKA/CREB signaling. This suggests coordinated regulation may exist between transcriptional and metabolic processes in relation to climbing-specific physiological characteristics. The concerted changes in gene expression and metabolite levels reflect the plasticity of the energy network, which may support flexible switching between anaerobic bursts and aerobic endurance to meet the complex, intermittent energetic demands characteristic of rock climbing. Features related to endocrine and metabolic homeostasis were also observed, including insulin sensitivity-related signatures, and altered adipokine signaling, which may be associated with energy utilization efficiency and metabolic health. At the molecular level, AQP9 may contribute to the transmembrane transport of energetic substrates such as glycerol and lactate, while AATK-related signaling may be linked to arginine metabolism and vascular remodeling-related regulation. Specifically, AATK may be associated with the conversion of L-arginine to ornithine through arginase-2-related mechanisms in vascular endothelial cells, thereby potentially reducing nitric oxide production and contributing to vascular remodeling [41]. Notably, the multi-omics profiles highlight the apelin/APJ and cAMP signaling pathways as pivotal integrative nodes, particularly in relation to vascular regulation and metabolic flexibility in elite rock climbers. As an exercise-related myokine, apelin has been reported to respond to mechanical loading, grip-related ischemia-reperfusion, and intermittent hypoxia, which are characteristic of rock climbing. Upon binding to its G-protein-coupled receptor APJ, apelin activates intracellular signaling cascades that converge on energy-sensing and metabolic regulatory pathways, thereby supporting endothelial function, angiogenesis, and vascular remodeling. These processes may contribute to improved microvascular perfusion and substrate delivery, in parallel with the insulin sensitivity-related signatures observed in elite climbers [42].

Furthermore, enrichment of the cAMP signaling pathway is consistent with catecholamine-related responses during climbing-specific intermittent grip and dynamic movements, with potential roles in energy mobilization, synaptic plasticity, and vascular tone through PKA/CREB-mediated transcriptional regulation. Together, these pathways may serve as bridges linking transcriptional regulation, metabolite dynamics, and vascular adaptation, underscoring the physiological demands of rock climbing in this comparative analysis of elite climbers versus university student controls [43]. In rock climbers, circulating levels of lactate and pyruvate were markedly elevated, alongside higher levels of fatty acids, glycerophospholipids, various amino acids, including glutamate, phenylalanine, and tyrosine, and purine/pyrimidine metabolites. These metabolic alterations were concordant with transcriptomic pathway signatures related to energy sensing, antioxidant defense, inflammatory regulation, and neural function, suggesting a metabolism–transcription coupling pattern. Previous evidence suggests that changes in gene expression can influence metabolite levels, thereby modulating energy homeostasis and metabolic adaptation [44]. Among these alterations, the simultaneous increase in lactate levels and upregulation of ACAD11 suggests a complementary relationship between glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation. Elevated lactate levels may reflect increased ATP demand and also function as a signaling molecule through the HIF-1α and AMPK pathways, potentially regulating genes involved in fatty acid metabolism. Meanwhile, ACAD11 upregulation may indicate greater capacity for fatty acid β-oxidation, which may support sustained energy provision in elite climbers [45]. The coordinated patterns of these factors suggest a dynamic balance between rapid energy supply and endurance-related metabolism, potentially contributing to energetic flexibility under the intermittent physical demands characteristic of rock climbing.

Although the sex distribution was balanced across groups, sex-related biological variability may still influence metabolomic profiles; thus, residual sex effects cannot be fully excluded. The observed differences between elite rock climbers and controls should be interpreted as group-level associations rather than definitive sex-independent effects. In addition, as this study is based on PBMC transcriptomic data, the expression profiles may only indirectly reflect tissue-specific adaptations, and immune cell deconvolution was not performed, which may affect DEG interpretation. Furthermore, habitual dietary intake may act as a potential confounding factor influencing metabolomic measurements. The relatively small sample size, which is often unavoidable in studies involving elite athletes, should be acknowledged as a limitation. This may introduce uncertainty in effect size estimation and increase the risk of overestimation, thereby limiting statistical precision. However, the observed patterns provide preliminary insights into the metabolic and transcriptional characteristics associated with elite rock climbing performance. These findings should therefore be interpreted in an exploratory context and warrant validation in larger and independent cohorts to confirm their reproducibility.

Conclusions

Elite rock climbers exhibited physiological and multi-omics features associated with climbing-specific functional capacity. The observed differences in metabolic regulation, oxidative stress response, vascular regulation, and exercise-related functional capacity collectively reflect physiological characteristics related to high-level climbing performance. By incorporating molecular profiling alongside functional assessment, the present study provides a broader characterization of the physiological status of elite climbers and expands current understanding of sport-specific physiological characteristics in competitive climbing. These findings may also provide useful reference information for athlete monitoring and sport-specific conditioning practices.
