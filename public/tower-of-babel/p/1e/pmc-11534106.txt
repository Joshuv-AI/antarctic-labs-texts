Real-world treatment patterns, resource utilization and costs in biliary tract cancers in the USA
Authors: Liya Wang, Mukul Singhal, Adriana Valderrama, Bal Nepal, Shital Kamble, Madhulika Eluri, Usha Malhotra, Abby Siegel, Michael Grabner, Shilpi Swami, Milind Javle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: To evaluate real-world treatment patterns, survival and healthcare-resource utilization in US patients with advanced biliary tract cancers (BTC) receiving systemic therapy.Patients & methods: This study used claims data from the Healthcare Integrated Research Database (HIRD®) linked to clinical data from the Cancer Care Quality Program (January 1, 2015–September 30, 2020).Results: Of 413 patients, 84.5% received gemcitabine-based first-line (1L) treatment, 46% received second-line treatment, and 16.5% received third-line (3L) treatment. All-cause mortality was 53% and approximately 70% of patients had ≥1 inpatient visit. The total mean per-patient-per-month all-cause costs were $19,589 for 1L and $33,534 for 3L treatment.Conclusion: Results showed poor survival, significant resource use and high costs as treatment line progresses for patients with advanced BTC.

Introduction

Biliary tract cancers (BTC), which include gallbladder cancer (GBC) and cholangiocarcinoma (intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [IHC] and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [EHC]), are a group of rare, heterogeneous, aggressive malignancies with poor survival rates [1,2]. Globally, over the last two decades (1997–2017), the incidence and mortality of BTC have increased by 76% and 65% respectively, predominantly driven by a rise in IHC [2–4].

In the US, approximately 23,000 people are diagnosed with BTC each year [5]. Between 2001 and 2015, the overall incidence of cholangiocarcinoma was estimated to be 1.26 per 100,000 people per year [6]. However, this may be an underestimation as IHC and EHC epidemiologic data are often reported in conjunction with hepatocellular cancers and GBC, respectively [2]. The most recent pooled epidemiologic estimations for EHC and GBC date from 2021, with 12,000 new cases and 4,000 deaths [2].

The therapeutic options for patients with localized disease include liver resection, radiotherapy and liver transplantation, but most patients with BTC (60–70%) present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis [2,7–10]. Advanced BTC is associated with a poor prognosis, with an estimated 5-year survival of 2% [11,12].

Until recently, the standard of care (SoC) for advanced BTC has relied on systemic therapy with a combination of gemcitabine and cisplatin in the first line of therapy (1LOT) [13,14]. However, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently approved durvalumab or pembrolizumab in combination with gemcitabine and cisplatin for adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic BTC, based on the results from the Phase III TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 trials [15–18]. The combination improved overall survival (OS) compared with SoC, indicating an advancement in the treatment of BTC [15–18]. However, the median OS still remains relatively short, which emphasizes the need for further advancements in the treatment landscape [15,16].

With the limited 1LOT options and increased prevalence of BTC (specifically IHC), the economic burden of managing BTC has also increased over time [19–21]. However, there are few published studies evaluating the costs of healthcare in this disease area, particularly regarding data on costs by LOT, healthcare-resource utilization (HCRU) and treatment patterns before the advent of immunotherapy (IO) [20,21]. One study focused on costs of second-line therapies in selected patients with advanced cholangiocarcinoma who experienced failure of a first-line therapy containing either gemcitabine or fluorouracil; the total mean per-patient-per-month (PPPM) advanced cholangiocarcinoma-associated healthcare costs were $7,743 [20]. Another study noted high total all-cause healthcare costs of $20,696 PPPM for the management of BTC; however, this study did not account for disease stage or 1LOT using a validated clinical data source [21]. An understanding of advanced BTC-related costs may support decision-making to identify cost-effective current and future interventions, which could allow healthcare providers to offer optimal care.

This study was conducted to describe the real-world treatment patterns, treatment sequencing and mortality outcomes of patients receiving systemic therapy, HCRU and costs associated with advanced BTC in the US before recent approval of IO-based regimens.

Materials & methods

Study design & data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients diagnosed with advanced BTC (all stages) and treated with systemic therapies identified through medical and pharmacy claims from the Healthcare Integrated Research Database (HIRD®), linked to clinical data from the Cancer Care Quality Program (CCQP) [22]. The HIRD contains longitudinal claims and eligibility data from 14 geographically diverse states in four US regions: Northeast, South, Midwest and West; the CCQP contains clinical data submitted by providers as part of utilization management and enhanced reimbursement programs. The database included data for most patients enrolled in a health plan who receive injectable/infusion treatments.

Patients were identified during the period from 1 January 2015 to 30 September 2020. The date of 1LOT on or after BTC diagnosis during the identification period was defined as the index date. The study period extended from 1 July 2014 to 31 March 2021, which included at least 6 months of pre-index continuous enrollment (baseline) and a post-index (follow-up) period. The study design is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1.

Patient population

All patients aged ≥18 years at the index date, with commercial or Medicare Advantage plans and a diagnosis of cancer of the bile duct (extrahepatic), biliary tract (intrahepatic), or gallbladder and documented 1LOT in the CCQP, were included in the study. Other eligibility criteria included availability of patient data on documented disease stage (i.e., I, II, III, IV) at the 1LOT and ≥6 months' pre-index continuous health-plan enrollment. All patients with a record of 1LOT were included, regardless of disease stage. Some patients with early-stage BTC may not have been amenable to curative resection because of comorbidities or tumor location, resulting in absence of neo-adjuvant/adjuvant therapies in the data source. Therefore, the use of first-line systemic treatments acted as a proxy to identify patients not eligible for surgery or other treatments with curative intent. Patients with ampullary cancer, brain/central nervous system metastases, or other primary cancer before BTC diagnosis were excluded from the study.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes assessed were the identification of treatment patterns (including sequencing) and all-cause mortality (OS). The secondary outcomes included all-cause and cancer-related HCRU and cost during the follow-up period. Please refer to Table 1 for the descriptions of study outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the data, and no formal statistical hypotheses were evaluated. Mean, median and standard deviation (SD) were used to report continuous data, while numbers and percentages were reported for categorical variables. A Sankey plot was generated to visualize treatment composition across all LOTs. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to report survival outcomes and time to next treatment. HCRU and cost (reported in PPPM) were reported by LOT, in which the outcomes were reported during the time when patients were receiving systemic treatment in specific lines.

Treatment patterns & sequencing

The majority (84.5%; n = 349) of patients with advanced BTC received gemcitabine-based mono- or combination therapy in 1LOT. Gemcitabine plus cisplatin was the most frequently used 1LOT by patients at all disease stages: stage IV (n = 204; 64.8%), stage III (n = 23; 52.3%) and stage II/I (n = 36; 66.7%). The second most frequently used regimen across all disease stages was gemcitabine alone (n = 26; 6.3%) followed by gemcitabine combined with oxaliplatin (n = 24; 5.8%). In total, 46% (n = 190) of patients initiated second line of therapy (2LOT), which predominantly involved 5-fluorouracil (5FU)-based mono- or combination chemotherapy (overall, 71.1%; n = 135; stage IV: n = 96, 69.1%; stage III: n = 20, 80%; stage II/I: n = 19, 73.1%). Only a few patients (n = 68; 16.5%) received third line of therapy (3LOT) and most received 5FU-based regimens (stage IV: n = 41; 78.8%; stage III and II/I: ≤10 patients; see Figure 2A & B).

Out of 413 patients who received 1LOT, 101 (24.5%) patients were lost to follow-up and of the 190 patients who initiated 2LOT, 55 (28.9%) patients were lost to follow-up. Patients were lost to follow-up possibly due to switching health insurance plans, reaching the end of the data observation period (31 March 2021), or misclassification of a death as a loss to follow-up.

The median time on 1LOT from index date during the follow-up period was 4.6 months; this was similar for patients at all disease stages. The median time on 2LOT was 3.6 months and was longer for patients with stage III disease (4.6 months) than for patients with stage IV and stage I/II disease (3.6 months). There were fewer patients receiving 2LOT compared with 1LOT, which led to bigger variances in the data.

Overall, the median time to discontinue 1LOT from the index date was 4.6 months, which was consistent across disease stages. The median time to discontinue 2LOT from the index date was 3.6 months.

The median OS from the index date was 11.5 months (Figure 3A & B). In total, 70.1% (n = 289), 47.8% (n = 197), 34.9% (n = 144) and 29.6% (n = 122) of patients survived 6, 12, 18 and 24 months, respectively, from the index date.

OS from the date of diagnosis of BTC

The median OS time from first presumed BTC diagnosis was 13.3 months. Approximately 40% of all patients survived up to 18 months. Patients with stage II/I and stage III disease had similar survival, which was longer than for patients at stage IV (Supplementary Table S2).

Time to next treatment

The median time to next treatment from 1LOT to initiation of 2LOT (or death) was 5.5 months; this was longer for patients with stage II/I disease (7.0 months) versus those with stage III disease (6.4 months) and stage IV disease (4.6 months). Among patients who received 2LOT, median time from 2LOT to 3LOT (or death) was 4.6 months (Figure 3C–F & Supplementary Table S3).

Overall, 69.5% of patients had all-cause inpatient hospitalizations and 65.6% patients had BTC-related inpatient hospitalizations during the follow-up period. Patients stayed in hospital for a mean period of 7 days per visit. Discussion

This observational study assessed real-world treatment patterns, healthcare utilization and cost per patient by LOT using data from a commercially insured US population with advanced BTC before recent approval of IO-based regimens. Utilizing multiple integrated real-world data sources, this study builds further on the current evidence to characterize treatment patterns and high costs of BTC over time in the USA.

This study revealed that gemcitabine-based mono- or combination therapy was the most common (84.5%) 1LOT. These therapies are among the recommended 1LOT options in the US guidelines (NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology [NCCN Guidelines®]) [14]. The recommendation for gemcitabine plus cisplatin was based on the results of the ABC-02 trial, which reported the favorable outcomes of gemcitabine and cisplatin combined for patients with advanced BTC [13,14]. As treatment options advance further, the combination therapy of durvalumab or pembrolizumab with gemcitabine and cisplatin has been approved by the FDA and the European Medicines Agency, and recommended as a preferred systemic treatment for unresectable and metastatic BTC (gallbladder cancer, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma) in the updated NCCN Guidelines®. This recommendation was based on the results from the TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 Phase III trials [14–18].

In the current study, 46.0% of patients received 2LOT, which could be due to a number of reasons such as potentially high morbidity in patients who progress after 1LOT, poor performance status, or lack of effective treatment options in 2LOT. These observations are consistent with the findings from a prior multicenter retrospective analysis of 766 patients in Korea, where 44% (n = 331) of patients with advanced BTC progressed to 2LOT after the failure of 1LOT (gemcitabine + cisplatin) [23].

Overall, 5FU-based chemotherapy was the most frequently used 2LOT in our study, a finding that was mirrored in previous retrospective database studies involving similar patient populations [23–25]. The NCCN Guidelines recommend a 5FU-based regimen, FOLFOX (fluorouracil, folinic acid and oxaliplatin) as a subsequent-line systemic therapy option for patients with advanced progressive BTC (category 2A) [14].

Targeted therapies have emerged as a promising treatment option for some patients. For instance, pemigatinib has been approved for patients with fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) fusions or rearrangements, while ivosidenib targets isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) mutations, as a 2LOT for locally advanced or metastatic cholangiocarcinoma [26,27]. Additionally, trastuzumab deruxtecan has recently been approved for HER2-expressing (immunohistochemistry 3+/2+ by local or central testing) locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors [28]. The availability of these targeted therapies is likely to increase the treatment heterogeneity. Our study captured only a small number of patients using targeted therapy due to the timing of their approval; however, future studies could be planned to monitor real-world usage of these emerging treatments. Understanding the sequencing and efficacy of these targeted therapies is essential for patients with actionable genomic mutations.

The survival rates in our study population were poor (all-cause mortality: 53.0% during the follow-up period; median OS: 11.5 months from initiation of 1LOT), which indicates a high unmet need for more effective treatment options for advanced BTC. Median OS reported in the ABC-02, TOPAZ-1 and KEYNOTE-966 Phase III trials ranged from 11 to 13 months, which reinforces this need [15,16]. Previous real-world studies assessing the efficacy of fluoropyrimidine-based regimens as 2LOT for advanced BTC reported median survival from 5.2 to 7.2 months, which align with our reported survival of 4.6 months during 2LOT [23,24,29,30]. Collectively, these studies suggest that for most patients with advanced BTC, there are only limited treatment options available, which typically provide modest survival benefits in the real-world, contributing to the burden of illness in clinical practice.

In addition to the unmet treatment needs of patients with advanced BTC, the care costs pose a significant burden on patients and payers that has increased over time [20]. A previous study comparing the trend of HCRU and cost from 1997 to 2012, using a Nationwide Inpatient Sample database, found a significant increase in the frequency of cholangiocarcinoma-related inpatient hospitalizations and average hospital charges per patient (adjusted for inflation) [19]. Our results echo previous reports underscoring the substantial HCRU and economic burden associated with advanced BTC, despite differences in study design [19–21]. Chamberlain et al. reported the burden of illness in patients with advanced cholangiocarcinoma but they only included patients who experienced failure of a line of therapy containing gemcitabine or fluorouracil [20]. In the study by Healey and colleagues, treatment patterns, HCRU, costs and mortality were assessed. In contrast to previous studies, the current study used a validated clinical data source (the CCQP) to ascertain disease stage and 1LOT, and provide information on cancer type and ECOG [21]. This might have helped us to select the correct population of interest (patients starting 1LOT), to stratify the analysis by cancer stage, and to paint a richer picture of patients' baseline characteristics.

We found that total all-cause PPPM cost was $19,589 during 1LOT, increasing to $22,617 and $33,534 as the treatment advanced to 2LOT and 3LOT, respectively, with >80% of PPPM costs only related to BTC. As anticipated, both all-cause and BTC-related inpatient hospitalizations increased with each LOT. The primary driver of costs across all three LOTs was outpatient (including antineoplastic therapy visits) and inpatient utilization followed by outpatient pharmaceutical costs.

The cost burden of advanced BTC estimated in our study is higher than for many other types of cancers (such as non-small-cell lung cancer [mean $10,885 PPPM], metastatic breast cancer [mean PPPM $11,991], metastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer [median PPPM $10,292–10,916] and metastatic colorectal cancer [mean PPPM $16,469–17,653]) reported in other real-world studies using similar methodologies, which may be explained by increased resources needed for the management of biliary obstruction, septic complications and the need for multidisciplinary care [31–34]. Clinical comorbidities associated with advanced BTC (e.g., liver disease) and common complications of the disease (including kidney failure, sepsis, biliary obstruction, dehydration and abdominal pain) might also contribute to increased costs. Healthcare professionals could implement strategies to identify and prevent some of these complications at an early stage and initiate pre-emptive discussions regarding advanced treatment options and patient preferences for further management to limit excess costs.

As with prior claims-based studies, this study also has some limitations. The LOTs in the current study were identified via an algorithm informed by clinical guidelines and subject-matter expertise. Comparison against LOTs developed from other real-world data sources, such as Electronic Health Records, is recommended. The identified 1LOT reported by healthcare providers through the CCQP was cross-validated with claims data to minimize misclassification; however, this validation might have excluded patients whose treatment regimens were not typically reported through CCQP. Additionally, most of the patients in this analysis had commercial health insurance, with the remainder being covered under Medicare Advantage. Moreover, we had limited visibility into healthcare resources and treatments for patients in our study who were also enrolled in an interventional clinical trial at some point during the study period; therefore, outpatient HCRU and costs may be underreported for these patients. Generalizability of our findings to other populations, including older BTC patients with Medicare Fee-for-service coverage, may be limited. Other common limitations of administrative claims database analyses, such as potential coding errors and incomplete data, also need to be considered while interpreting the results. Despite these limitations, the current study addresses an unmet need by providing data on costs related to LOT, HCRU and treatment patterns before the introduction of IO-based regimens. This information can be used as a valuable foundation to demonstrate the positive impact of IO-based regimens for patients with BTC.

Conclusion

This study provides insights into treatment patterns, survival, HCRU and costs related to advanced BTC before recent approval of IO-based regimens. Despite the evolving treatment landscape for advanced BTC, there exists high unmet need with patients having a poor prognosis and experiencing substantial economic burdens. This emphasizes the need for effective novel treatment options for patients with advanced BTC. With recent approvals for IO-based regimens and consequent availability of these novel therapies, there is a need for further research to focus on contemporary treatment patterns post-IO approval.
