A practical, low-cost floating flux chamber for measuring greenhouse gas emissions from small urban waterbodies
Authors: Bryn E Reynolds, Cole J C Van De Ven, Evan Heyes, Jennifer Drake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSmall urban waterbodies (SUWs) play an important role in managing stormwater, treating wastewater, and building climate resiliency. Recently, SUWs have been identified as potential sources of greenhouse gases (GHG) such as carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4), although quantification methods vary. To reduce contamination during sampling and improve spatial variance testing at a lower cost, a static, closed flux chamber with real-time sensors was proposed as an alternative to traditional syringe sampling and GHG analyzers. A floating flux chamber was designed and constructed using 3D-printed components and hardware-store items for ~ $820 USD. Validation experiments showed that real-time sensors reported 3.5% (R2 = 0.9966) and 3.1% (R2 = 0.9987) more than the values measured using gas chromatography (GC) for CO2 and CH4, respectively. The system was deployed in a wastewater lagoon in Almonte, Ontario, Canada, to provide proof of concept and calculate flux in a real SUW. Large increases in concentration upon lid closure were noted before equilibrium was reached between the chamber and water. Exponential and linear fits were applied to the recorded concentrations, giving fluxes of 892 ± 61 mg m−2 min−1 and 546 ± 37 mg m−2 min−1 for CO2, and 254 ± 12 mg m−2 min−1 and 245 ± 11 mg m−2 min−1 for CH4. The system is suitable for a wide range of fluxes across all types of SUWs, advancing research on how waterbodies factor into the global climate budget.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10661-026-15463-2.Keywords: Carbon dioxide, Methane, Stormwater management pond, Wastewater treatment lagoon, NDIR sensor, Diffusive flux

Introduction

As global efforts to quantify greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions intensify, small urban waterbodies (SUWs), such as stormwater management (SWM) ponds, wastewater lagoons, and reclaimed quarries, have been identified as potential sources of carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4) (Gorsky et al., 2019; Holgerson & Raymond, 2016). Most SUWs remove particulate-bound pollutants from runoff water through sedimentation, where the organic matter then decomposes, producing CO2 and CH4. Small ponds (defined as less than 1000 m2) make up about 8.6% of the global surface area of lakes and ponds but could release up to 15% of all CO2 and 40% of all diffusive CH4 emissions from lentic freshwater sources (Holgerson & Raymond, 2016).

Closed static chambers are a common approach for measuring diffusive emissions from waterbodies (Livingston & Hutchinson, 1995). In this method, gases accumulate within an enclosure, and concentrations are recorded using manual (grab) sampling (Gorsky et al., 2019), high-precision gas analyzers (Rodríguez-García et al., 2023), or real-time sensors (Macagga et al., 2024). The rate of change in gas concentration over time is then used to calculate flux, eliminating the need for carrier gases, complex gas-exchange chemistry, and highly technical or expensive equipment.

Many of the closed chambers used in waterbody studies rely on syringe sampling at fixed time intervals (e.g., every 10 min for an hour), followed by laboratory analysis of gas samples by gas chromatography (GC) (Livingston & Hutchinson, 1995). However, syringe sampling introduces the potential for atmospheric contamination during sample collection, transportation, and processing (Livingston & Hutchinson, 1995). Furthermore, sampling at fixed time intervals (e.g., every 10 min) may fail to capture important dynamics of GHG emissions, such as high but short-lived flux rates that allow gases in the chamber to reach equilibrium before a sample was taken, as well as short-lived ebullitive events.

Gas analyzers are one approach that eliminates the need for syringe sampling and provides higher sampling frequencies (e.g., 1 Hz). However, these systems can be prohibitively expensive, costing upwards of $38,500 USD (Rodríguez-García et al., 2023). Gas analyzers also add significant weight to the flotation device and require substantial energy inputs. They are often deployed for a short duration of time (such as 10 min) and are limited to a single sample at a time, as their cost makes it unlikely that more than one analyzer is available (Rodríguez-García et al., 2023). Several studies have shown the effects of spatiotemporal variability on a variety of waterbodies, including lakes (Thanh Duc et al., 2020) and wetlands (Zhu et al., 2015). In contrast, spatiotemporal variability has rarely been examined for SUWs, as existing literature has generally focused on inter-pond variability and not intra-pond variability (Bauduin et al., 2024). Therefore, there is a need for low-cost options that allow multiple systems to be deployed simultaneously to explore intra-pond variance.

Recent advances in sensor technology, coupled with growing demand for GHG monitoring, have led to the emergence of low-cost, high-precision, and high-accuracy CO2 and CH4 sensors (e.g., Macagga et al., 2024). Well-calibrated, nondispersive infrared (NDIR) sensors have been shown to produce reasonable agreement when compared to gas analyzers (Martin et al., 2017). By integrating these sensors into a flux chamber, high-frequency sampling can be performed at a fraction of the cost compared to conventional gas analyzers.

Motivated by the need to better quantify emissions from underrepresented waterbodies in the global carbon budget, this study aimed to design and test a low-cost (i.e., less than $1000 USD), highly accessible, and easily constructable static closed floating flux chamber for CO2 and CH4 emission measurements. The objectives of this work were to (i) design and construct a low-cost floating flux chamber that could be 3D-printed and constructed using hardware store items, (ii) calibrate and validate real-time sensors to ensure they could accurately report CO2 and CH4 concentrations in a controlled laboratory environment, and (iii) demonstrate that flux could be reasonably calculated for an attenuation pond by comparing the performance of the system with the traditional method of syringe sampling and GC analysis.

Methodology

Flux calculations

The floating flux chamber was designed to float on a SUW for several hours, allowing CO2 and CH4 emitted from the water surface to accumulate inside the chamber and real-time sensors record changes in gas concentration over time. The measurement duration was informed by sensor sensitivity and flux rates reported in the literature (Ge et al., 2025; Goeckner et al., 2022; Gorsky et al., 2019). Diffusive gas fluxes were calculated from the rate of change in concentration over time, dCdt (ppm s-1), using either a linear (Dai et al., 2015; Gorsky et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2015) or exponential approach (De Mello & Hines, 1994; Xiao et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2018). Flux chamber design

The floating flux chamber (Fig. 1) consisted of a 3D-printed (polylactic acid with epoxy coating on all interior surfaces) cylindrical chamber with an open bottom, allowing gases emitted from the water surface to accumulate within. A removable lid included one septum port for syringe-based gas injection and sampling, as well as two ports with tubing and stopcocks for closed-loop operation with a gas analyzer (not applicable to this study). Chamber dimensions were selected based on reported GHG emissions rates from SUWs, sensor sensitivity and detection limits, and the desired deployment time for field data collection. The gross interior dimensions of the chamber were 20 cm in diameter by 20 cm in height. The true headspace, accounting for the thickness of the floating board and collar, was 20 cm in diameter by 13.5 cm in height and the headspace volume was 4184 cm3.

a An expanded diagram of the floating flux chamber with important aspects labeled, b modeled diagram of completed floating flux chamber with electronics box, and c a photograph of the floating flux chamber deployed in an attenuation pond

An NDIR CO2 and CH4 sensor (ExplorIR-M by Gas Sensing Solution and INIR-ME100% CH4 sensor by SGX Sensortech, respectively), a pressure sensor (SCCRDNT 1.6BAA3 silicon by Honeywell), a thermistor (DS18B20-1-wire by Adafruit Industries), and a small fan, to ensure complete mixing within the chamber headspace, were mounted on the inside of the lid (see S1 in the Supplementary Material). As condensation on optical sensors can significantly affect measured concentration, long-term deployment (such as a 24-h cycle, although sensor type and conditions likely make this a case-by-case basis) in extreme conditions (e.g., high humidity or overly hot environments) should be treated with caution and could be a limitation of these sensors. A suggestion is to add a hydrophobic membrane around the sensors if condensation is identified as a major issue. A rubber gasket placed on the chamber flange, along with latches, was used to ensure an air-tight connection between the chamber and lid during experiments. A waterproof electronics box contained a Raspberry Pi microcontroller (Raspberry Pi 5 with MCC 118 DAQ HAT), an LCD screen that displayed the real-time CO2 and CH4 concentrations, and a battery to power the system while in the field. The electronics box and chamber were placed on a 109 × 55 × 5.6 cm polyethylene foam board to float on the surface of the SUW. A hole was cut into the board to allow the chamber to extend into, creating a 6.5 cm collar to prevent lateral emissions from contributing to concentration buildup. A rope-and-anchor system was used to maintain its location during deployment. The total cost to construct the system was ~ $820 USD (Table S8). Complete installation instructions are available as an open-source package on GitHub (see S7 in the Supplementary Material).

Sensor calibration and validation

The sensors were calibrated in a controlled laboratory environment to ensure reliability and accuracy under field conditions. A known quantity of gas was injected into the chamber and concentrations reported by the CO2 and CH4 sensors were compared to gas samples collected and analyzed using a Scion 8500 GC with triple thermal conductivity detectors (see S2 in the Supplementary Material).

To simplify the laboratory setup, a 20-cm diameter, 10-cm tall closed-bottom chamber was 3D-printed to approximate the expected chamber volume during field deployment. A wet sponge was placed in the chamber to mimic the high relative humidity expected at the water surface. After 15 min of sensor warm-up, 2 mL of pure CO2 or CH4 was injected into the septum port every five minutes. Three minutes after each injection, a 3 mL sample (0.07% of the interior volume) was taken for GC analysis. GC samples were processed within 30 min. This sequence was repeated 12 times, covering an expected range for CO2 (430 to 8070 ppm) and CH4 (2 to 7640 ppm). Tests were performed in triplicate for each gas type. Raw CO2 and CH4 sensors readings (default CO2 concentration in ppm and sensor voltage for CH4) were compared with GC-derived concentrations and sensor-specific calibration equations were developed from the pooled triplicate data. Subsequently, a validation experiment was performed for each gas type. Lastly, the pressure and temperature sensors were validated against laboratory reference sensors.

Field validation experiment

Following sensor calibration and laboratory-based validation, the system was tested on August 20th, 2025, at a sewage lagoon at the Mississippi Mills Wastewater Treatment Plant in Almonte, Ontario, Canada. The lagoon had an estimated surface area of 78,800 m2 and was historically the only wastewater treatment facility in the community. However, a wastewater treatment plant was opened in 2012, and while it is currently only used for temporary storage of overflows during high-flow events, settled solids (i.e., primary sludge) are still present at the bottom of the lagoon. For the field test, a shallow, secluded area with a depth of 0.8 m was selected where wind effects were negligible. Small bubbles were noted in the area, suggesting ebullitive gas release. The flux system was turned on 15 min prior to placement in the water. An anchor on a rope was tethered to the chamber and carefully lowered to the bottom to avoid disturbing sediments. As the chamber creates a closed seal that extends only 8.5 cm above the floating board (when considering the collar and thickness of the board) and the rope-and-anchor system maintains the systems firmly in their location, any potential wind effects will be negated and its physical stability maintained. Additionally, due to the chamber’s relatively small 20-cm diameter and pond waves generally classified as ripples, surface turbulence is also not a factor in quantifying fluxes.

During deployment, sensors recorded measurements at approximately one-second intervals. A syringe was used to collect 3 mL samples every 30 min, using the same methods as in the laboratory, and the samples were processed at the end of the day (no more than 10 h from time of collection to analysis). A deadband of 30 s from when the chamber lid was closed was removed to allow for sensor stabilization. Sensor noise was smoothed using an eighth-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.01 Hz for the entire monitoring period and a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.1 Hz during flux events, because the flux events were much shorter and required less smoothing (Miller et al., 2024). Linear and exponential approaches were used to calculate flux from the recorded concentration over time, and a paired t-test was performed to determine whether there was a statistical difference between the values reported by the sensors and the GC. Results and Discussion

Sensor calibration and validation

Linear regressions of the pooled calibration results gave coefficients of determination (R2) of 0.9829 and 0.9964 for CO2 and CH4 respectively (Figure S1 in the Supplementary Material). The standard error of each sample for the sensors ranged from 0 to 88 ppm for CO2 (default sensor output) and 0.0076 to 0.0226 V for CH4 and ranged from 7 to 297 ppm for CO2 and 3 to 125 ppm for CH4 for the GC (see S3 in the Supplementary Material).

For the validation experiments, linear regressions of the comparison points for the CO2 and CH4 experiments showed slopes of 1.035 (R2 = 0.9966) and 1.031 (R2 = 0.9987), respectively, indicating that the sensors reported 3.5% and 3.1% higher than the GC-measured values (Figure S3 in the Supplementary Material). Residuals ranged from 133 to 698 ppm for CO2 and −43 to 348 ppm (Figure S3). A multiple linear regression model was fit to the validation results to determine whether the slopes reported by the GC and the sensors were statistically different. The interaction between time and measurement method had p-values of 0.1342 and 0.251 for CO2 and CH4, respectively, indicating no significant difference (p>0.05) between the slopes reported by the GC and the sensors (see S4 in the Supplementary Material). These statistics indicate that the calibrated sensors accurately reflect the high-precision GC concentrations in a controlled laboratory environment.

Field results

The floating flux chamber recorded fluxes for CO2 and CH4 over two deployment periods, 11:24 – 13:23 and 13:24 – 14:50, respectively, on August 20th, 2025. In Fig. 2, sensor concentrations were measured every second and 60-s average concentrations (rounded to the nearest recordable concentration) corresponding to when each syringe sample was taken (“Sensor Point Concentration”) are compared to GC-derived concentrations. A paired t-test on the raw data, prior to Butterworth filtering, indicated no significant difference between the sensor and GC concentrations (p-value of 0.086 for CO2 and 0.329 for CH4).

A rapid increase of 527 ppm and 1100 ppm for CO2 and CH4, respectively, was noted across the first 30 s. An exponential curve was fit to the Butterworth-smoothed data for the first 50 s (RMSE = 28 ppm) for CO2 and 46 s (RMSE = 86 ppm) for CH4, corresponding to when the concentrations began to approach an asymptote and sensor noise increased. The rate of change of the exponential curve (Eq. 1) at t0 was found to be 62 ppm s−1 for CO2 and 49 ppm s−1 for CH4. For CO2, linear regression was performed for the first 10 s, producing a value of 38 ppm s−1 (R2 = 0.9924). For CH4, linear regression from 5 to 24 s gave 48 ppm s−1 (R2 = 0.9977) (see S5 in the Supplementary Material).

The CO2 and CH4 fluxes calculated using a linear relationship were 62% and 97% of the fluxes calculated using an exponential relationship, respectively. Xiao et al. (2016) studied the use of linear regression to calculate flux from static floating chambers and found that it accounts for 55% and 36% of the true flux for 30- and 60-min experiments, respectively, concluding that exponential regression gives the most accurate results. Therefore, it is important to present the data in both forms and determine which relationship provides the best fit. Analysis of the concentration data suggests that both CO2 and CH4 exhibited exponential trends because the fluxes were high, causing the chamber concentrations to approach equilibrium with the water quickly. Note that these chamber dimensions were selected to maximize gas accumulation across a short period due to expected low fluxes from most SUWs (most others generally do not contain sludge) and the desire to run several tests throughout the day. Chamber size should be increased if extremely high emissions are expected, extending the time before equilibrium is reached between the headspace and water, allowing for more accurate rate of changes to be calculated.

Another important aspect of this flux chamber is that the use of high-frequency, real-time sensors enabled the measurement of high flux, which GC sampling would have missed at the typical 5- to 10-min sampling rate. This may have led to the incorrect assumption of a linear relationship across the entire 119 and 86 min, giving a fraction of the true flux. In addition, using well-calibrated and validated sensors reduced any error that may have arisen from the intermediate steps of syringe sampling before analysis, ensuring concentration was known.

The system was left to continue running for the remaining 119 and 86 min, respectively, to take GC samples for comparison with the sensors and to capture possible ebullitive events, although none occurred. It is important to note that while this flux system was designed to measure only diffusive fluxes, any ebullitive events that occur would also be captured by the sensors as a large spike in concentration beyond the steady increase. A suggested threshold to distinguish between typical sensor noise and a sharp ebullitive increase is four times the sensor resolution (e.g., 421.4 ppm for CO2 and 139.24 ppm for CH4) and no return to concentrations prior to the increase in less than 15 s (for a 1 Hz frequency; this could be adjusted to 15 sampling points if much smaller than 1 Hz), allowing time for the sensors to correct in the case of sensor noise or else clearly identify an ebullitive event. van Bergen et al. (2019) found that there tended to be large spatial variability in ebullitive events, with larger emissions found in the center of the pond. This highly variable spatiotemporal occurrence makes it difficult to capture all events, making ebullitive calculations beyond the scope of this technical paper (Bastviken et al., 2004). However, future laboratory experiments will be performed using a bubbler to simulate ebullitive events to determine its effectiveness at capturing and quantifying ebullitive events.

For the CO2 deployment, the initial pressure in the chamber was 0.987 atm and the temperature was 21.6°C. The flux calculated using the linear and exponential fits was 546 ± 37 mg m−2 min−1 and 892 ± 61 mg m−2 min−1, respectively (see S5 and S6 in the Supplementary Materials). For the CH4 deployment, the initial pressure in the chamber was 0.987 atm and the temperature was 27.0°C. The flux calculated using the linear and exponential fits was 245 ± 11 mg m−2 min−1 and 254 ± 12 mg m−2 min−1, respectively.

There are varying results reported for wastewater ponds by other studies. Yan et al. (2014) found that a wastewater treatment plant in Beijing, China, produced CO2 emissions ranging from 708 mg m−2 min−1 to 1137 mg m−2 min−1 and CH4 emissions from ~ 0 mg m−2 min−1 to 77 mg m−2 min−1. Similarly, Kosse et al. (2018) used a floating chamber with an NDIR-probe at a wastewater treatment plant in Freiberg, Germany, and found CO2 emissions ranging from 576 to 1037 mg m−2 min−1 across all treatment components. Other findings are much smaller, such as village ponds that receive domestic wastewater in northern China emitting 3.1 ± 6.1 mg m−2 min−1 of CO2 and 0.6 ± 1.4 mg m−2 min−1 of CH4 (Ge et al., 2025). It is also expected that emissions from other types of SUWs may be smaller due to the absence of sludge. For example, SWM ponds in Florida emitted mean values of 4.1 ± 0.6 mg m−2 min−1 of CO2 and 0.03 ± 0.01 mg m−2 min−1 of CH4 (Goeckner et al., 2022). The results from this study are comparable to emissions from similar waterbodies and can be applied to cases where the emissions are expected to be lower, such as SWM ponds.

Conclusion

A significant advantage of this floating flux chamber is that many chambers can be constructed and deployed across a SUW at the same time for a much lower cost than a single gas analyzer, giving more extensive spatial coverage. In addition, high-frequency sampling ensures all ranges of flux measurements can be captured. This study has shown that emissions can be accurately calculated for ~ $820 USD. The methods described above can be used to improve the way GHG emissions are measured from waterbodies, which are an under-researched potential source of GHG emissions.
