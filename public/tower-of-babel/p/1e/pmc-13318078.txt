Blood cadmium levels and its associated factors in middle school children in Kuwait
Authors: Sharifa Al-Kandari, Reem Jallad, Muddanna Rao, Anwar Al-Harbi, Lemia Shaban, Abdur Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cadmium (Cd), a toxic heavy metal, is associated with several adverse health consequences. In Kuwait, the presence of heavy petrochemical industry, heavy traffic burden, and high prevalence of smoking makes environmental Cd exposure more likely. Due to its cumulative nature in the body, children and adolescents are particularly vulnerable to the chronic effects of Cd. However, data on Cd exposure of adolescents is scarce. This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Cd toxicity and its associated factors in a nationally representative sample of adolescents.Methods:Adolescents (N = 1385; 673 boys and 712 girls; age, 11–16 years) were selected from middle schools from all six governorates of Kuwait using stratified multistage cluster random sampling. Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire from parents and face-to-face interviews with adolescents. Blood Cd levels were determined by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry.Results:Geometric mean (95% confidence interval) blood Cd level was 1.22 (1.15, 1.30) µg/L; 1.26 (1.14, 1.38) µg/L in boys and 1.19 (1.11, 1.29) µg/L in girls (P < 0.001). 22% of boys and 3.2% of girls had elevated blood Cd levels (≥5 µg/L; χ2 = 115.13; P < 0.001). In the multivariable logistic regression, sex, governorate, father income, and blood levels of manganese, selenium, and zinc were significant predictors of Cd levels ≥5 µg/L.Conclusion:A significant proportion of adolescents, particularly boys, in Kuwait have blood Cd levels ≥5 µg/L. Due to the long half-life of Cd in the body and chronic adverse health consequences, public health measures are needed to limit exposure to this toxic environmental pollutant.

What this study adds

This manuscript is based on the results from the first systematically collected data on the blood levels of this toxic heavy metal (cadmium) in a nationally representative sample of adolescents in Kuwait. The major finding of this study is that 22% of boys and 3.2% of girls had elevated blood Cd levels (≥5 µg/L). These results have significant public health implications and thus need to be disseminated to get the attention of concerned authorities to address this issue.

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is a toxic heavy metal that is associated with several serious adverse health consequences. Cd is toxic to the kidney, liver, bone, and cardiovascular system, among others.1–5 Cd is also a known carcinogen, classified by the Environmental Protection Agency, the International Agency for Research in Cancer, and the National Toxicology Program as a group I carcinogen,6 particularly from inhalation exposure, and has been implicated in the development of cancers affecting the kidney, lungs, pancreas, breast, prostate, and gastrointestinal (GI) tract.7,8

Cd is ubiquitous in the environment and is widely used in several industries as an anticorrosive agent, as a stabilizer in polyvinyl chloride products, in color pigments, and in Ni-Cd batteries.9 As reviewed by Genchi et al,7 major sources of environmental contamination include copper and nickel smelting and refining, fossil fuel combustion, the use of phosphate fertilizers, cigarette smoke, wear-and-tear of automobile tires, and the recycling of electronic waste. Besides environmental and occupational exposure, ingestion of contaminated food and water is another significant source of Cd contamination. Foods with high levels of Cd include seafood like oysters, cephalopods, crabs, crustaceans, mollusks, and organ meats (liver and kidney), some mushrooms, oilseeds, and cocoa beans.10–12 Plant foods grown in contaminated soil accumulate significant amounts of Cd from the soil.13 Compared with other heavy metals, Cd passes easily from soil to plants and enters the food chain.14 In plants, Cd mostly accumulates in roots and leaves, but to a lesser extent, it can be found in other parts such as the fruits and seeds. Peanuts, soybeans, and sunflower seeds are rich in Cd due to their ability to accumulate Cd, like tobacco leaves. Leafy greens such as lettuce, spinach, parsley, cabbage, and celery contain relatively high values of Cd.11,15,16 Absorption of inhaled Cd is the major source of human exposure, with 40%–50% absorption of the inhaled Cd, followed by absorption from the GI tract, with 5%–10% absorption of the ingested Cd.17 In the human body, Cd accumulates mostly in the kidney and the liver and can remain there for years, with a biological half-life of 16–30 years, resulting in a number of adverse effects18 including degenerative bone disease, kidney dysfunction, GI disorders, lung injuries, and liver damage.19,20

In Kuwait, sources of Cd pollution include coastal sediments, the petrochemical industry,21 landfill and their effect on soil and groundwater contamination,22 vehicle exhausts, and the wear-and-tear of automobile tires.23 Tobacco use, particularly smoking, is also a major source of Cd exposure in Kuwait, as approximately 60% of men and 8% of women were reported as current smokers.24 Adolescent boys in particular are a high-risk group for Cd exposure, as approximately 80% of smokers have reported starting smoking at an age younger than 21 years.25 Moreover, smoking Sheesha (also known as hubble-bubble) among youth and adults is common nowadays in Kuwait. Smoking Sheesha also increases the risk of Cd exposure.26

Thus, Cd levels are likely to be high in Kuwait, but systematic data on Cd levels are not available. As Cd can last in the body for decades due to its limited excretion from the body, children and adolescents exposed to high levels of Cd would suffer from its adverse health consequences in their adult life as well, in addition to its acute ill-health effects. Therefore, studying Cd toxicity in this earlier age group is important from the public health point of view, as preventing high levels of exposure can reduce chronic disease associated with high Cd exposure.27 This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of Cd toxicity and its associated factors in a nationally representative sample of adolescents in Kuwait.

Material and methods

Study design and protocol

This school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in public middle schools selected from all six governorates of Kuwait. Study subjects, in the age range of 11 and 16 years, were selected from 12 schools using stratified multistage cluster random sampling, with the probability proportional to the size of the schools. The study was approved by The Ethics Committee at the Ministry of Health, Kuwait (No: 2015/248) and the amended version 2022/2072) and The Ethics Committee of the Health Sciences Centre, Kuwait University (No: DR/EC/2338). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki ethical principles for medical research involving human subjects. Written informed consent from the parents and verbal assent of each participant were obtained. Study protocol, subject selection, and data collection are described in detail previously.28

Data collection

Data on various demographic characteristics and other covariates were collected through a self-administered questionnaire that was sent to the parents, and through face-to-face interviews with study subjects using a carefully developed structured questionnaire. Standing height and body weight were measured in a standardized manner, using a digital weight and height scale (Detecto, Webb City, MO).

Blood collection and biochemical analyses

Blood samples were collected over a 3-month period (February–April 2016). Five mL of venous blood was collected from each student by a highly trained pediatric nurse. Blood samples were analyzed in a major tertiary care hospital under strict quality control for complete blood count, calcium, parathyroid hormone, serum Fe, ferritin, transferrin saturation, vitamin B12, and red blood cell folate. Details of these analyses are previously provided.28

For mineral analyses, whole blood samples (0.5 mL) were digested in 5 mL of perchloric acid/nitric acid (1:5) solution. After complete digestion of the samples, the perchloric acid/nitric acid solution was evaporated, and the residue dissolved in 5 mL of 1% nitric acid. Initially, the samples were analyzed for lead, and the remaining digested samples were kept at −70 °C. After obtaining ethical approval for the amended protocol from the Ministry of Health, Kuwait, in 2022, the samples were analyzed for Cd, copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), selenium (Se), and zinc (Zn). Double-deionized water was used in all sample preparation steps to avoid contamination of the samples. Mineral analyses were performed using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS). For a quality control check, lyophilized whole blood samples of known mineral content (Clin-Check- Control, Cat. # 884042; Recipe, Munich, Germany) were included in the analyses.

Statistics

Data were double-entered into a specifically designed database using Epidata Entry. Body mass index was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Weight status was categorized into normal, overweight, and obese according to the WHO growth charts. Participants were divided into two Cd categories based on the cutoff points of 5 µg/L. Univariable logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with Cd level ≥5 µg/L. Factors that showed an association with Cd (≥5 µg/L) at less than 20% level of significance were considered in multivariable logistic regression analysis. Factors with P <0.05 were deemed to be statistically significant. For normally distributed parameters, data are expressed as a mean and standard deviation (SD). For parameters that were not normally distributed, data are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), or as geometric mean (GM) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Means were compared with one-way analysis of variance and by chi-square test, depending on the nature of the data. Data were analyzed with SPSS version 30.

Results

Data were available for 1385 adolescents, of which 673 were boys and 712 were girls. The mean (SD) age was 12.48 (0.93) years. The median (IQR) blood Cd level was 1.41 (0.46–3.41) µg/L, and the GM (95% CI) blood Cd level was 1.22 (1.15, 1.30). GM (95% CI) blood Cd levels (Table 1) in boys and girls were 1.26 (1.14, 1.38) µg/L and 1.19 (1.11, 1.29) µg/L, respectively (P < 0.001). Of the total sample, 173 (12.5%) had blood Cd levels above the CDC safety limit of 5 µg/L. Of those who had blood Cd levels above the safety limit, 150 (22% of the total sample) were boys and 23 (3.2% of the total sample) were girls (χ2 = 115.13; P < 0.001). GM (95% CI) blood Cd levels in Kuwaiti and non-Kuwaiti adolescents were 1.09 (1.01, 1.16) µg/L and 1.82 (1.60, 2.07) µg/L, respectively (P < 0.001).

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, the sociodemographic factors that were significantly associated (P <0.05) with Cd levels ≥5 µg/L were male sex, age <13 years, father and mother education of university degree or above, father or mother income <1000 KD, and living in a rented flat (Table 2). Among the nutritional and physical activity-related factors that were significantly associated with Cd levels ≥5 µg/L were consuming dinner prepared at home daily, consumption of sugary drinks two times or less per week, number of times walking to school, walking time to school of ≥5 minutes, medium and high level of physical activity, time spent outdoors of ≥30 minutes on weekdays and ≥1 hour on weekends, and being exposed to cigarette smoke at home (passive smoking). Body mass index and anemia status were not significantly associated with Cd levels of ≥5 µg/L (Table 3). Among the biochemical parameters studied, Cd levels of ≥5 µg/dL were significantly associated with higher quartiles of vitamin D or having insufficient or sufficient levels of vitamin D (compared to severely deficient levels), higher tertiles of folate and Mn, and higher quartiles of Fe, Cu, Se, and Zn. Vitamin B12 was not associated with Cd levels of ≥5 µg/L (Table 4). In the multivariable logistic regression, the factors that remained significantly associated with Cd levels ≥5 µg/L were sex, governorate, father’s income, and microminerals Mn, Se, and Zn. Housing type showed a marginal level of significance (P = 0.056) (Table 5).

Discussion

Cd is a toxic heavy metal with adverse and serious health implications, and is widely distributed in the environment and in the food chain.29 Kuwait is predominantly a petrochemical industry-based economy, with a heavy automobile burden. As such, the population is likely to be exposed to high levels of Cd, particularly from the environment through inhalation. In addition, the high levels of Cd from the environment get into the soil and become part of the food chain in locally grown fruits and vegetables. Locally grown green leafy vegetables could become a source of Cd contamination, as these vegetables are able to accumulate Cd from the soil. Although most of the food consumed in Kuwait is imported, fresh vegetables like tomato, cucumber, lettuce, and spinach are locally grown and consumed. However, systematic data from Kuwait on population exposure are scarce. In this study, we report the prevalence of Cd exposure in a nationally representative sample of adolescents. The data showed that 22% of adolescent boys and 3.2% of adolescent girls had blood Cd levels above the safety limit of 5 µg/L. Male gender, being in a location with the petrochemical industry and high automobile burden, lower income of father, and higher levels of blood Mn, Se, and Zn were associated with higher blood Cd levels.

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration, U.S.A., regards a blood Cd level of ≥ 5 µg/L as hazardous.30 Our data show that although the mean blood Cd levels (GM [95% CI] 1.22 [1.15, 1.30] µg/L; median [IQR] 1.41 [0.07, 8.97] µg/L) are below the safety limit, over 12% of adolescents have Cd levels ≥5 µg/L. The GM Cd levels in our study population were higher than the reported mean blood Cd levels of 0.237 µg/L in adolescents (aged 13–19 years) in Turkey,31 and 0.19 µg/L in adolescents aged 12–19 years who participated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles 2011–2018.32 It is also higher than the median blood Cd levels of 0.12 μg/L in Swedish adolescents in the age range of 12–18 years,33 Korean adolescents (0.32 µg/L),34 and Brazilian adolescents (0.6 µg/L).35 Wide variations have been reported in blood Cd levels based on geographical locations, environmental exposure level, age, and gender. For example, in the United States, the GM blood Cd levels in those who were ≥20 years were reported to be 0.38 µg/L, which was slightly higher than the younger age groups (0.32 µg/L).29

In our study, boys had not only higher GM blood Cd levels (1.26 µg/L) than girls (1.19 µg/L), but the proportion of those with a blood Cd level of ≥5 µg/L was also significantly higher in boys than in girls (22% in boys compared with 3% in girls). A similar pattern of higher blood Cd levels in men than in women was reported from an urban population in Southern Brazil.36 In contrast to our findings, no gender difference was observed in the blood Cd levels in adolescents in some studies.32,34 In other studies, higher blood Cd levels in female adults compared with males were reported.33 It has been suggested that females in general absorb more Cd from the GI tract than males and are at a greater risk of toxicity.37 Absorption is affected by diet composition, especially fiber and iron. Females have more iron deficiency compared with men and thus accumulate more Cd.

The lower Cd levels in girls compared with boys in our study could be explained in two ways: First, iron deficiency anemia in adolescent girls in Kuwait is less prevalent compared with other regions, as is evident from the data from this same cohort of adolescent girls (11% anemia and 6% severe anemia) published earlier.28 As such, adolescent girls in Kuwait are less likely to absorb more Cd, due to anemia, than boys. This is further supported by the lack of association between anemia status and the odds of having a blood Cd level ≥5 µg/L (P = 0.496). Second, boys in general spend more time outdoors compared with girls due to local traditions and customs, and thus are more likely to be exposed to Cd inhalation from the environment. Significant governorate-sex interaction was also found in our study. In some governorates, girls had higher blood Cd levels compared with boys, while in others the opposite was true. The GM blood Cd level in girls was almost 15 times higher than in boys in Farwaniya, whereas it was approximately 4–5 times higher in boys than in girls in Al-Ahmadi and Mubarak Al-Kabeer. This pattern also fits with the exposure time, as the governorates with high Cd levels in boys are the ones that are more likely to have high environmental Cd levels.

In our study, adolescents younger than 13 years had higher blood Cd levels compared with those who are older in the univariate regression analysis. This contrasts with some studies in which adolescents above 13 years old had higher blood Cd levels.34,38 This could be related to the extra time spent outdoors by the younger participants in our setting, where environmental exposure seems to be the major source of Cd contamination. This is supported by the fact that age did not remain significantly associated with the odds of having Cd levels ≥5 µg/L in the multivariate logistic regression model.

Higher median blood Cd levels and the proportion of adolescents with blood Cd levels ≥5 µg/L were observed in the governorates of Al-Ahmadi, Hawally, and Farwaniya. Al-Ahmadi is the governorate where the largest oil refinery and other petrochemical installations are located. The governorates of Hawally and Farwaniya are generally very congested with a heavy traffic burden. As such, environmental contamination is expected to be high in these governorates. This is supported by the relatively high Cd content (>0.08 µg/g) in soil sediment samples collected from industrial areas in Kuwait.39 Similarly, the Cd content of the atmospheric particulate matter (PM) samples collected from several urban areas in Kuwait was >40 times higher than the corresponding background soil Cd content.40 The North-Westerly winds, particularly in summer, can spread this Cd-enriched PM to the more residential areas like Hawally and Farwaniya governorates,41 which are located to the North of Al-Ahmadi.

Sociodemographic and nutritional factors linked to high Cd levels in univariable logistic regression included nationality (being non-Kuwaiti), parents’ education, parents’ income below 1000 KD, living in rented apartment, vitamin D levels, eating breakfast and dinner at home, and reduced consumption of sugary drinks. In the multivariable logistic regression, only the father’s income remained significantly associated with Cd level ≥5 µg/L.

Physical activity factors associated with Cd levels ≥5 µg/L included daily walking to school, walking time to school of at least 5 minutes, medium and high level of physical activity, and spending at least 30 minutes outdoor on weekdays and at least 1 hour on weekends. These factors suggest that longer exposure and inhalation to outdoor air is the major source of Cd exposure in Kuwait. However, none of these factors remained significant in the multivariable logistic model, likely due to the collinearity among many of these variables.

Another finding of interest is that a higher tertile of folate was significantly associated with Cd level ≥5 µg/L. Green leafy vegetables are among the major sources of folate. Leafy vegetables tend to accumulate more Cd from the soil.42 It has been reported that the uptake of Cd by plants is more pronounced from sandy soil than from clayey ones because Cd remains in soluble and exchangeable form in sandy soil.43 Thus, green leafy vegetables grown in Kuwaiti soil (a desert climate with sandy soil) might have high levels of Cd. It is, therefore, important to investigate the Cd levels in locally grown leafy vegetables and compare them to vegetables imported from other regions.

Cigarette smoke is one of the major sources of exposure to Cd.44–46 Blood Cd levels in smokers have been reported to be higher than in nonsmokers. Adolescent nonsmokers can also be exposed to Cd through exposure to secondhand smoke at home, in public places, and at school.47 In this study, we did not observe a statistically significant association between Cd levels ≥5 µg/L and cigarette smoking. This lack of association may be due to the small number of subjects who reported smoking (n = 12). This, however, seems to be a gross underreporting by the study subjects, as smoking among adolescents in Kuwait, particularly boys, is fairly common.47

A significant positive association of Cd with Mn, Se, Cu, Zn, and Fe was observed in the univariable logistic regression models, and this association remained highly significant for Mn, Se, and Zn in the multivariable logistic regression. A similar positive correlation between Cd and other divalent metal ions has been reported in various biological fluids in adolescents.48 This positive correlation between Cd and other divalent ions has been suggested to be due to common transporters shared by these metals for their intestinal absorption.27 Furthermore, Cd can displace these minerals from their binding proteins, pushing blood levels up.

To our knowledge, this study provides the first systematically obtained data on a nationally representative sample of adolescents in Kuwait. In addition, we have collected information on numerous covariates as potential factors associated with Cd exposure. This study, however, has several limitations. First, the Cd level was measured in blood samples only, which reflects recent exposure. Thus, chronic and cumulative exposure could not be deduced. It has been reported that urinary Cd levels represent cumulative exposure and body burden as Cd accumulates in the kidneys and is gradually excreted in the urine.49 However, the validity of urinary Cd levels in estimating long-term exposure has been questioned due to the fact that it is confounded by protein excretion, and blood and kidney Cd levels were suggested to be more reliable indicators of long-term Cd.50,51 As kidney Cd requires invasive procedures and is not appropriate for epidemiological studies, blood Cd levels remain a reliable and practical measure of long-term Cd exposure. Second, dietary intake data were not collected, and we cannot separate the contribution of diet from that of environmental exposure to Cd levels. Another limitation is that the data do not represent the current Cd status of adolescents, since blood samples were collected in 2016 and analyzed in 2022. However, the data presented here still provide a baseline benchmark to which any future studies could be compared.

In conclusion, our data suggest that a significant proportion of adolescents in Kuwait are exposed to environmental Cd and have blood Cd levels that are above the CDC safety limit of 5 µg/L. Given the cumulative nature of Cd due to its long half-life in the body, together with the adverse and long-term physical and neurological health consequences of Cd, public health measures are needed to limit exposure to this toxic environmental pollutant.
