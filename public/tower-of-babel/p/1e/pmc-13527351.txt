A novel grey system model with discrete Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative and its applications
Authors: Zhenguo Xu, Rui Peng, Mengyi Pang, Jianming Jiang, Rujing Zhang, Caixia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, fractional-order grey system models have shown significant advantages in time series prediction, but their core fractional-order accumulation and difference operators often rely on complex calculations, which restrict their theoretical development and practical application. To address this issue, this paper adopts the standard product-integration rectangle rule discretization of the classical Riemann–Liouville fractional integral to derive a fractional-order accumulation operator, and further constructs the Riemann–Liouville fractional grey system model (RL-FGM). By directly introducing the fractional integral theoretical framework, this model simplifies the computation process and enhances theoretical rigor. Numerical experiments show that the proposed model is simple and effective to compute. Comparisons of prediction performance on multiple typical datasets indicate that RL-FGM significantly outperforms traditional integer-order grey models and some existing fractional-order grey models in prediction accuracy, particularly showing stronger adaptability and stability in the long memory of non-stationary sequences. This study provides a theoretically sound and easy-to-implement new approach for fractional-order grey modeling and expands its potential applications in complex time series prediction.Keywords: grey system model, Riemann–Liouville fractional integral, prediction model, optimization algorithm, time series forecasting

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

Time series forecasting is a cornerstone of decision-making across diverse domains, from energy policy and environmental management to economic planning.1,2 Accurate predictions enable governments and industries to anticipate future trends, mitigate risks, and formulate effective strategies. However, real-world systems often operate under conditions of small samples and poor information, where data is scarce, incomplete, or characterized by high uncertainty. 3 Traditional statistical methods, such as ARIMA, and machine learning models, including Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, while powerful, typically require large volumes of high-quality data to achieve reliable performance.4–6 They can be prone to overfitting or fail to capture the underlying dynamics when historical observations are limited.7,8 To address these challenges, grey system theory, pioneered by Deng in 1982, offers a compelling alternative. 3 Grey prediction models are specifically designed to extract valuable information from uncertain systems with limited data, exhibiting superior performance with simple operation and high accuracy for small-sample time series.9,10 The core of these models lies in the Accumulating Generation Operator (AGO), which transforms the original, often noisy, sequence into a smoother, near-exponential one, thereby reducing randomness and revealing the system’s intrinsic evolution law.11,12 Despite their widespread application, traditional integer-order grey models, such as the GM(1,1), have inherent limitations in characterizing sequences with complex, non-linear, or long-memory features. 13 To overcome this bottleneck, the integration of fractional calculus into the grey modeling framework has emerged as a significant research direction. Fractional-order grey models, by introducing a fractional-order accumulation operator, provide greater flexibility and adaptability for capturing the dynamic complexity of real-world sequences.10,14 However, existing fractional-order grey models often rely on complex calculations or operator definitions that can hinder their theoretical development and practical implementation. 15 The computational complexity and the challenge of determining the optimal fractional order remain key obstacles. To address these issues, this paper proposes a novel and theoretically robust fractional grey system model based on the classical Riemann-Liouville fractional integral. By directly introducing a well-established fractional calculus framework, our model simplifies the computation process and enhances theoretical rigor.

2. Literature review

2.1. Fundamentals of fractional calculus

The theoretical foundation of fractional-order grey models is deeply rooted in fractional calculus, a branch of mathematical analysis that extends the concepts of differentiation and integration to arbitrary non-integer orders. Since the pioneering work of Leibniz and L’Hôpital in the late 17th century, followed by the major contributions of Liouville, Riemann, Caputo, and others, fractional calculus has grown into a rigorous discipline supported by a well-established theoretical framework. 16 Podlubny 16 provided a comprehensive exposition of fractional differential equations, formalizing the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral and its fundamental properties including linearity, semigroup property, and power-law memory kernels. Kilbas et al. 17 systematically developed the theory and applications of fractional differential equations, extending the classical calculus to fractional orders. Samko et al. 18 offered an encyclopedic treatment of fractional integrals and derivatives, establishing the rigorous mathematical foundations that underpin modern fractional calculus. For numerical implementation, Diethelm et al. 19 presented comprehensive analysis of fractional differential equations, including product-integration rules and other discretization schemes. In parallel, Lubich 20 introduced convolution quadrature methods for fractional calculus, providing efficient and accurate numerical algorithms. These theoretical advances have naturally extended to time series analysis, where Granger et al. 21 and Hosking 22 independently developed fractional differencing for modeling long-memory processes, establishing the autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average model. Caputo 23 proposed an alternative definition of fractional derivatives that accommodates classical initial conditions, further broadening the applicability of fractional calculus in real-world modeling. Despite these rich theoretical developments, the integration of classical fractional calculus into grey system modeling remains incomplete, with many existing models employing ad hoc operator definitions that deviate from established mathematical theory.13,15

Beyond the continuous theory, the numerical discretization of Riemann–Liouville fractional operators has been extensively studied in computational mathematics. The product-integration scheme, originally proposed by Young 24 and later systematically analyzed by Diethelm et al. 19 , provides a standard framework for approximating fractional integrals on uniform grids. In parallel, the emergence of discrete fractional calculus, pioneered by Atici et al. 25 and subsequently formalized by Goodrich et al. 26 , has established rigorous definitions of discrete Riemann–Liouville operators that operate directly on sequences. These discrete formulations are particularly relevant to time series analysis, as they naturally accommodate the discrete nature of sampled data and underpin the construction of fractional accumulation operators in grey system modeling. Additionally, Meerschaert et al. 27 investigated finite difference approximations for fractional advection-dispersion equations, while Li et al. 28 provided comprehensive survey of numerical methods for fractional calculus. Oldham et al. 29 offered one of the earliest systematic treatments of the fractional calculus foundations that support these numerical developments.

2.2. The evolution of grey prediction models

Since its inception, grey system theory has been widely applied and continuously developed for time series forecasting, particularly for systems characterized by small samples and scarce information. 3 The classic GM(1,1) model, established by Deng, laid the foundation for this field. 3 However, the inherent limitations of integer-order derivatives in characterizing complex, non-linear, and long-memory sequences soon became apparent. 13 To overcome this bottleneck, the introduction of fractional-order calculus into the grey modeling framework has been a pivotal advancement. 14 Wu et al. 10 formally proposed a fractional-order accumulation generation operator and established a fractional-order grey model, which addressed the insufficient reflection of the new information priority principle in traditional grey models. Building on this, Mao et al. 14 systematically constructed the FGM(1,1) model, which not only extended the traditional GM(1,1) but also integrated fractional-order accumulation with fractional-order differential equations. These early fractional-order models significantly enhanced adaptability to non-linear and non-stationary sequences by leveraging non-integer order accumulation effects. Subsequent research has focused on refining and diversifying these models. For instance, to simplify computations and enhance theoretical consistency, Ma et al. 15 defined a new accumulation operator based on the conformable fractional derivative, constructing the Conformable Fractional Grey Model (CFGM). Other scholars have explored different fractional operators and model structures. Xie et al. 2 developed a novel fractional grey system model with a non-singular exponential kernel to overcome the overweighting of early data. Xie et al. 30 also proposed a fractional Hausdorff grey system model, demonstrating its effectiveness in various applications. More recently, Ma et al. 31 proposed a fractional Bessel grey system model, using the first-kind Bessel function as the grey input to capture oscillatory and decaying characteristics in renewable energy generation. 2.3. Advancements in multivariable and system grey models

Recognizing that real-world systems are influenced by multiple factors, researchers have extended grey models from univariate to multivariable forms. The traditional GM(1,n) model, which integrates a system’s behaviour variable with n-1 associated factors, was developed for this purpose. 36 However, this model suffers from inherent shortcomings, such as treating the linear combination of driving factors as a constant grey value, which is often unrealistic. 37 To improve the model’s practicality, Tien 38 introduced the grey multivariable convolution model (GMC(1,n)), replacing the grey constant with a Laplace transformation to derive a more accurate solution. This work has inspired numerous enhancements. For example, Ding et al. 39 proposed a collaborative optimization framework combining damping accumulation and Simpson’s rule for the GMC(1,n) model. More recently, Ding et al. 40 proposed a multi-step carbon emissions forecasting framework using an interpretable data preprocessing technique (UNAGO) combined with an improved grey multivariable convolution model, demonstrating superior accuracy. Other notable advancements include the nonlinear grey Bernoulli model (NGBM(1,1)) for handling nonlinear trends, 33 and the fractional time-delayed grey model (FTDGM) for capturing lag effects in time series data. 32 Xiao et al. 41 proposed an improved seasonal rolling grey forecasting model using cycle truncation accumulated generating operation for traffic flow forecasting. Zhou et al. 42 developed a novel discrete grey seasonal model for handling seasonal time series. At a higher level of complexity, system grey models have been developed to forecast interactions within interconnected systems, such as the economy-energy-environment (3E) system. For instance, Li et al. 43 proposed a multivariable discrete grey model with terms to compensate for abrupt events and policy changes. Similarly, Ye et al. 44 developed a nonlinear interactive grey multivariable model based on dynamic compensation for forecasting the 3E system. Wang et al. 45 also proposed a non-linear systematic grey model for forecasting the industrial 3E system.

2.4. Innovations in accumulation and optimization techniques

A critical aspect of grey model enhancement lies in the development of advanced Accumulating Generation Operators (AGOs). The traditional 1-AGO assigns equal weight to all data, which contradicts the new information priority principle. To address this, various new-information-based AGOs have been proposed. These include the new information priority operator (NIPO), 46 the r-order adjacent accumulating generation operator (RAAGO), 47 and the accumulated generating operation with the new information priority principle (NAGO). 48 More advanced operators like the adaptive exponential accumulated generation operator (AEAGO) 49 and the new information priority generalized accumulation generation operator (NGAGO) 50 were subsequently developed to introduce scaling effects and more flexible weight structures. A significant breakthrough was achieved by Ding et al., 51 who proposed a unified new-information-based accumulating generation operator (UNAGO) based on feature decoupling. This operator integrates the functionalities of five existing new-information-based AGOs and the traditional 1-AGO into a single framework, offering unconstrained scaling, exponential, and equal-accumulation effects. Guo et al. 52 applied the UNAGO to a GM(1,1) model for hierarchical forecasting of wind electricity generation, demonstrating its superior adaptability and accuracy. Alongside operator innovation, the optimization of model parameters has been crucial. As fractional orders and other hyperparameters are difficult to determine analytically, heuristic intelligent algorithms have become standard. PSO is widely used due to its efficiency and ease of implementation. 53 Other algorithms such as the Genetic Algorithm (GA), 54 Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO), 34 Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA), 44 and Salp Swarm Algorithm (SSA) 31 have also been effectively employed to optimize grey model parameters, significantly enhancing predictive performance.

2.5. Hybrid grey models and emerging applications

To further improve forecasting accuracy, especially for data with complex characteristics like seasonality and non-linearity, researchers have developed hybrid models that combine grey systems with other techniques. For instance, Yuan et al. 55 proposed a hybrid framework combining a grey system model with Gaussian process regression and STL decomposition for CO2 emissions forecasting. Xu et al. 35 developed a tempered fractional neural grey system model with Hermite orthogonal polynomials, merging fractional calculus and neural networks for enhanced non-linear fitting. Xie et al. 56 also proposed a neural grey system model based on generalized conformable fractional derivatives. These hybrid approaches leverage the strengths of grey models, such as small-sample handling, and other methods, such as nonlinear mapping and pattern recognition, to tackle more challenging forecasting tasks.57,58 Zhang et al. 59 proposed a novel discrete multivariable grey model with spatial proximity effects for economic output forecasting, demonstrating the potential of integrating spatial considerations into grey modeling frameworks.

In summary, although significant progress has been made in recent years in the development of fractional-order grey models and hybrid grey models, a comprehensive review of existing research reveals that many models still rely on operator structures that are complex in form and computationally intensive, or suffer from certain theoretical weaknesses and a lack of rigorous mathematical support. Therefore, how to effectively simplify the model structure and reduce computational burden while maintaining or even further improving forecasting accuracy and model robustness remains a key challenge that urgently needs to be addressed in this field. To fill this research gap, this paper proposes the RL-FGM model, which is rooted in the classical Riemann-Liouville fractional integral. By adopting the classical product-integration rectangle rule discretization, it achieves a simpler and easier-to-implement computational process while ensuring mathematical rigor, thereby providing a new approach for complex time series forecasting tasks that combines theoretical depth with practical convenience.

5.2. Distributional robustness of RL-FGM

We next assessed whether the forecasting behaviour of RL-FGM remains stable when the data-generating process changes. Synthetic positive sequences were produced under four multiplicative perturbation laws, including normal, lognormal, gamma and Weibull noise. For each setting, the model was evaluated over fractional orders r ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.9}, and the test-stage error was measured by APE=100|xk−x^k|/max(|xk|,ϵ). Across all four perturbation regimes, RL-FGM exhibits stable central error behaviour (Figure 1). The median APE remains relatively insensitive to changes in r under normal and lognormal perturbations, while gamma and Weibull perturbations mainly increase the upper tails of the error distributions. This indicates that non-Gaussian and asymmetric fluctuations affect extreme errors more strongly than the typical forecasting accuracy. The intermediate fractional orders yield compact and stable error distributions in most cases, particularly under gamma and Weibull perturbations. These results show that the fractional accumulation operator provides a useful control of memory intensity, enabling RL-FGM to retain robust small-sample forecasting performance across different stochastic environments.

6. Validation of the RL-FGM

To systematically verify the effectiveness and robustness of the RL-FGM(1,1), the proposed RL-FGM was compared with five representative baseline models, including CFGM(1,1), 15 FHGM(1,1), 13 SVR, 62 GM(1,1), 3 and DGM(1,1). 63 The analysis focuses on evaluating each model’s fitting accuracy, forecasting performance, and stability across different datasets.

6.1. Evaluation criteria

6.2. Empirical research: Grain production in China’s provinces

Grain production refers to the total quantity of grain produced by agricultural operators within a calendar year. According to the harvest season, it includes summer-harvested grains, early rice, and autumn-harvested grains; according to crop type, it includes cereals, tubers, and legumes. Grain production is a core indicator for measuring agricultural productivity and food security, reflecting a region’s or country’s basic capacity for grain supply. In this section, we use a model to forecast grain production, with the original data sourced from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (https://www.stats.gov.cn). We selected grain production data from five major grain-producing regions, including Hebei, Shanxi, Anhui, Shandong, and Hubei, from the National Bureau of Statistics for analysis. Data from 2006 to 2020 were used as fitting data for model construction, while data from 2021 to 2024 were used as test data to examine the model’s generalization ability and predictive performance. We used eight types of errors, including MAPE, MSE, MAE, RMSE, RMSPE, SMAPE, MAAPE, and RRSE, as model evaluation metrics, and selected five types of models, including CFGM, FHGM, SVR, GM and DGM, as comparison models to verify the effectiveness of the proposed model. To comprehensively assess the model’s fitting performance and prediction accuracy, we calculated the eight types of error values for each model on both the fitting data and the test data.

Table 3 shows the forecasting errors of the models on the test datasets. It can be seen that, compared with other models, the RL-FGM achieved very small prediction errors on all five prediction datasets. For example, on the Hebei dataset, the MAPE of the RL-FGM is 0.7753%, while the MAPE of the CFGM, FHGM, SVR, GM, and DGM models are 1.2078%, 1.6008%, 5.7015%, 8.0093%, and 7.9853%, respectively, indicating that the new model has strong forecasting performance. At the same time, the new model also achieves very small prediction errors on the training datasets. As shown in Table 4, compared with previous fractional-order and integer-order prediction models, RL-FGM achieves very small prediction errors on all five datasets. For example, on the Hubei dataset, the new model’s MAPE is 2.4161%, whereas the MAPE of the CFGM, FHGM, SVR, GM, and DGM models are 2.8645%, 2.9511%, 5.6658%, 3.5641%, and 3.5739%, respectively. It is clear that the fitting performance of the new model is far superior to that of the other grey prediction models.

7. Applications and analysis

In this section, based on empirical research cases, the superiority of the RL-FGM proposed in this paper compared to other forecasting models will be discussed. This section mainly presents a comparative analysis of model prediction errors and a comparative analysis of model performance improvement.

7.1. A comparative analysis of model prediction errors

Figure 2 presents the experimental results of error comparisons between our new model, RL-FGM, and several typical forecasting models, including GM, DGM, CFGM, FHGM, and SVR. The data used in this experiment are the regional grain production (unit: 10,000 tons) from five provinces, including Hebei, Shanxi, Anhui, Shandong, and Hubei, spanning the period from 2006 to 2024. Specifically, the data from 2006 to 2020 were used for model fitting, while the data from 2021 to 2024 were employed to evaluate the predictive performance of the models. Relative error was adopted as the evaluation metric to assess the forecasting accuracy of the models.

Boxplot analysis of six different forecasting models. The horizontal axis corresponds to the GM, DGM, CFGM, FHGM, SVR, and RL-FGM, the vertical axis corresponds to the relative error.

As can be observed from the boxplot analysis, the RL-FGM demonstrates excellent predictive performance in the majority of the provinces, which is closely related to its theoretical foundation based on the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral operator. Compared to traditional integer-order models, such as GM and DGM, the RL-FGM, by introducing a fractional-order operator with long-term memory characteristics, can more accurately capture the nonlinear and dynamic evolution features within time series. Consequently, it achieves the lowest median relative errors in datasets such as Hebei, Anhui, and Hubei.

When compared with other fractional-order models like CFGM and FHGM, the RL-FGM still exhibits superior or comparable predictive performance. The CFGM, constructed based on the conformable fractional derivative, simplifies calculations in form, but its operator is weaker than the Riemann-Liouville integral operator in capturing the long-term dependencies of sequences. Meanwhile, although the FHGM introduces the Hausdorff derivative in an attempt to enhance adaptability to small-sample data, the RL-FGM, leveraging the global accumulation property of its integral kernel, demonstrates more stable fitting and extrapolation capabilities when handling non-stationary sequences. For instance, in the Hubei and Anhui datasets, the median relative errors of the RL-FGM are 0.018 and 0.010, respectively, both outperforming CFGM and FHGM. Similarly, in the Shanxi dataset, the RL-FGM still significantly outperforms the SVR model. Although the SVR possesses nonlinear mapping capability in high-dimensional feature spaces, its performance degrades substantially from the training stage to the test stage, indicating overfitting and poor generalization under small-sample conditions. In contrast, the RL-FGM maintains stable and excellent performance across both training and test sets for all five provinces, reflecting its superior robustness and broad applicability in small-sample, scarce-information scenarios.

In summary, the superior performance of the RL-FGM across multiple datasets can be attributed to its accumulation operator, which is grounded in classical fractional integral theory. This operator possesses a sound mathematical structure and memory capability, enabling it to effectively extract long-term trends and nonlinear features from sequences. This provides a solid theoretical foundation and practical advantage for its application in complex time series forecasting.

7.2. A comparative analysis of model performance improvement

Figure 3 presents the performance improvement comparison results of our new model, RL-FGM, relative to several typical forecasting models, including GM, DGM, CFGM, FHGM, and SVR, in forecasting grain production in Hebei Province. The data used in this analysis are the same as those in Figure 2. We adopt the mean error as the evaluation metric to conduct a comparative analysis of the predictive accuracy of each model.

Performance comparison of different forecasting models for Hebei province. The horizontal axis corresponds to various typical forecasting models and the RL-FGM, and the vertical axis corresponds to the mean error.

As can be observed from the results of the predictive performance comparison analysis, the RL-FGM model demonstrates significant and stable performance improvement on the Hebei dataset. This is primarily attributed to the global accumulation property of its operator based on the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral, which enables it to more accurately capture long-term trends and nonlinear evolutionary patterns when processing non-stationary sequences.

In terms of specific data performance, the RL-FGM achieves the lowest prediction error in Hebei Province. For example, the relative error of the RL-FGM is as low as 0.010%, significantly outperforming other comparison models. This is attributable to the long-term memory capability of its fractional-order accumulation operator, which effectively extracts deep dynamic features from the sequence. In contrast, traditional integer-order models, such as GM and DGM, can only reflect local variations and struggle to capture complex fluctuations, resulting in generally higher relative error, reaching 0.034%. Although other fractional-order models such as CFGM and FHGM also possess a certain degree of nonlinear adaptability, the local differential nature of CFGM, based on the conformable fractional derivative, limits its ability to characterize global dependencies. Meanwhile, the Hausdorff derivative employed by FHGM exhibits slightly reduced stability when handling sequences with significant fluctuations. Consequently, the RL-FGM maintains superior predictive performance in Hebei Province. Furthermore, the SVR model performs worse than the RL-FGM in Hebei Province, as its performance is highly dependent on parameter tuning, which limits its generalization ability. In contrast, the RL-FGM exhibits the lowest error level, highlighting its stronger robustness and broad applicability in small-sample, scarce-information scenarios.

8. Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a novel fractional-order grey system model, RL-FGM, based on the Riemann- Liouville fractional integral with its classical product-integration rectangle rule discretization. This model serves as an effective tool for time series forecasting, particularly suitable for modeling complex data under conditions of small samples and scarce information. The optimal fractional order is selected using PSO with MAPE as the fitness function. By comparing it with various existing grey forecasting models, including GM, DGM, CFGM, FHGM, and the machine learning model SVR, we observe that the new model exhibits excellent predictive accuracy. Furthermore, we apply this new model to forecast grain production in five major grain-producing regions of China. Through systematic experimental analysis, we verify that the RL-FGM achieves significant improvements in both fitting accuracy and predictive accuracy compared to traditional integer-order and fractional-order grey forecasting models. Meanwhile, the stability of the model is validated through visualization techniques such as boxplots, with results indicating that the model demonstrates good robustness across different regional datasets. Although the new model exhibits superior performance and high accuracy, there is still room for further improvement. Notably, the model may exhibit overfitting when processing certain specific types of data. Therefore, to mitigate this phenomenon in future research, we consider incorporating a regularization mechanism to enhance the model’s generalization capability.
