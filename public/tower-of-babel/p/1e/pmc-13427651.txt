Epigenetic silencing of miR-141 via core promoter methylation is associated with short-term bladder cancer progression
Authors: Katerina-Marina Pilala, Artemis-Melina Papadimitriou, Maria-Alexandra Papadimitriou, Konstantina Panoutsopoulou, Georgios-Christos Giagkos, Panagiotis Levis, Zoi Kanaki, Dido Vassilacopoulou, Konstantinos Stravodimos, Apostolos Klinakis, Andreas Scorilas, Margaritis Avgeris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alterations in DNA methylation represent one of the earliest molecular events in bladder tumorigenesis and are strongly associated with treatment efficacy and prognosis. Herein, we investigated DNA methylation landscape of MIR141 promoter and elucidated its clinical relevance in bladder cancer (BlCa) patients. miRNA-seq was performed to analyze miRNA profile in bladder tumors. Expression analysis of miR-141-3p and methylation profiling of MIR141 promoter were performed in 15 patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) mouse models, and in our screening patients’ cohort (n = 133). TCGA-BLCA (n = 412) was used as independent validation cohort. Survival analysis was performed employing tumor relapse/progression as clinical endpoints in non-muscle-invasive BlCa (NMIBC; TaT1), and mortality in muscle-invasive BlCa (MIBC; T2–T4) patients. Internal validation was conducted through bootstrap analysis, and the clinical benefit of the findings was evaluated using decision curve analysis. miRNA-seq highlighted miR-141-3p as one of the most significantly downregulated miRNAs in muscle-invasive and high-grade tumors, while PDXs, TCGA-BLCA and screening cohort revealed its robust epigenetic regulation through MIR141 core promoter methylation. Consistent with miR-141-3p loss, hypermethylation of MIR141 core promoter was associated with advanced stage and high-grade tumors, reduced survival in PDXs, and significantly higher risk of TaT1 patients for short-term progression to muscle-invasive disease. Multivariate models highlighted MIR141 promoter hypermethylation as independent predictor for NMIBC short-term progression, displaying superior clinical benefit in patients’ prognostication. Overall, MIR141 core promoter methylation results in miR-141-3p loss in bladder tumors and correlates with patients’ poor treatment outcome, offering improved risk-stratification and addressing prognostic heterogeneity beyond traditional markers.

Bladder cancer (BlCa) stands as the sixth most common cancer and the ninth leading cause of cancer-related death among men globally, with Europe displaying the highest incidence (fourth) and mortality (sixth) rates (1, 2). The most prevalent histological disease subtype is the urothelial bladder carcinoma (UBC; >90%), arising from the urothelial lining of the bladder (3). Depending on the extent of invasion into the detrusor muscle, BlCa is classified into two major groups: non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC; Tis, Ta and T1), where the tumor remains confined to the mucosa or submucosa, and muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC; T2-T4), where the tumor penetrates the muscle layer of the bladder wall and/or extends beyond it (3). Non-muscle-invasive tumors account for ∼75% of primary diagnosed cases and are distinguished by high recurrence rates (50%–70%), along with a risk of progression to muscle-invasive stages in ∼15% of patients (4). MIBC comprises roughly 25% of initial diagnoses and is associated with aggressive metastatic behavior and considerably higher mortality rates (5).

Besides the notable advancements in the clinical management of the disease, BlCa continues to impose a weighty global health burden (6, 7). This is largely attributed to the pronounced molecular and cellular heterogeneity of bladder tumors, which results in highly variable disease progression at the clinical level, even among patients with similar clinicopathological features and within the same clinical risk groups (8). Moreover, BlCa prognosis continues to rely predominantly on traditional clinicopathological parameters, including tumor stage, grade, multifocality, and the presence of carcinoma in situ (CIS), which remain insufficient for precise risk prediction and hinder the application of individualized therapeutic approaches (8, 9). As a result, the up-to-date BlCa management necessitates lifelong post-treatment surveillance, typically involving frequent invasive cystoscopies and medical imaging, deteriorating patients' quality of life (10, 11, 12). In that vein, there is a pressing need to reduce reliance on unnecessary and/or distressing interventions by improving clinical decision-making and advancing more personalized therapeutic strategies (13).

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small non-coding RNAs (20–24 nt) that serve as pivotal post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression by inhibiting translation and mediating mRNA degradation (14). By fine-tuning essential cellular processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation and metabolism, miRNAs play crucial roles in preserving cellular homeostasis (14). Disruption of miRNAs balance is strongly associated with cancer occurrence, progression, and therapy resistance, functioning either as oncogenes or tumor suppressors depending on their influence on cellular transformation (15). MicroRNA-141-3p (miR-141-3p; miR-141) has been widely studied for its pivotal role in carcinogenesis and the regulation of cancer development and progression, acting predominantly as a tumor suppressor in several malignancies, including lung, hepatocellular, colorectal, pancreatic and prostate cancers (16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21). Moreover, decreased expression of miR-141 has been associated with adverse survival outcomes in patients with colorectal, lung, and pancreatic carcinomas (17, 19, 21). In this regard, miR-141 downregulation induces epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through direct targeting ZEB1 and ZEB2 transcription factors, thereby promoting oncogenic TGF-β signaling (22, 23). Intriguingly, emerging evidence indicates that miR-141 expression is epigenetically regulated through DNA methylation of MIR141 promoter, while restoration of miR-141 expression inhibits cell proliferation in prostate cancer and suppresses inflammatory cytokine production in brain meningitis in vivo models (24, 25). However, the role and the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms of miR-141 in bladder tumors remain elusive.

Herein, to explore the impact and clinical value of the epigenetic regulation of miR-141 in BlCa, we have analyzed the methylation of MIR141 core promoter and miR-141 expression profiles in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mouse models, as well as in screening and independent validation patients’ cohorts. Our study highlighted that MIR141 promoter methylation is associated with miR-141 loss in advance stage and high-grade bladder tumors and documented its translational relevance in improving patients’ risk-stratification and predicting post-treatment outcomes, supporting personalized prognosis and treatment decisions.

Clinical and experimental baseline data

Regarding tumor stage, 73.7% (n = 98) of the patients were diagnosed and treated for primary NMIBC (TaT1), while 26.3% (n = 35) were diagnosed with MIBC (T2–T4). Additionally, according to the WHO 2004 classification system, 51.9% of the tumors were classified as high grade (HG). Clinicopathological and follow-up data of the screening cohort

Given the differing clinical behaviors of NMIBC and MIBC, survival analyses were conducted separately for each group. Tumor relapse and progression to a higher and/or invasive tumor stage were used as clinical endpoints for NMIBC, while patients’ mortality was considered for MIBC. Over a median follow-up period of 38.0 months (95% CI: 32.99–42.01), 44 (44.9%) and 23 (23.5%) NMIBC patients experienced disease relapse and progression, respectively, while 17 (48.6%) patients with MIBC succumbed to the disease. The estimated mean disease-free survival (DFS) and progression-free survival (PFS) for NMIBC were 44.3 months (95% CI: 37.6–51.0) and 58.8 months (95% CI: 53.1–64.6), respectively, while overall survival (OS) for MIBC patients reached 74.7 months (95% CI: 52.1–97.3). Thereafter, the analysis was stratified into two comparisons: (a) MIBC (T2-T4) vs. NMIBC (TaT1) and (b) high-grade (HG) vs. low-grade (LG) tumors, to examine differences in tumor invasiveness and histological grade. A total of 301 miRNAs were commonly expressed across T2-T4, T1HG, and TaLG subgroups (Fig. 1A). Using fold-change criteria (FC ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67), we identified 52 miRNAs showing monotonically up- or down-regulation across the three subgroups, of which 30 (57.7%) were upregulated and 22 (42.3%) downregulated in MIBC and HG tumors (Fig. 1, A–C).

Subsequently, in silico analysis of TCGA-BLCA data were conducted to assess the prognostic value of the differentially expressed miRNAs. A total of 33 miRNAs (21 upregulated and 12 downregulated) showed significant associations with patients’ survival outcomes (Figs. 1D, S2 and S3). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between miRNA levels and the DNA methylation status of their corresponding promoter regions to identify miRNAs regulated epigenetically. Notably, among the 33 clinically relevant miRNAs, miR-141-3p levels displayed the strongest inverse association (rs = −0.742; p < 0.001) with MIR141 core promoter methylation (Fig. 1E).

Prompted by the in silico analysis findings, we quantified miR-141-3p and MIR141 core promoter methylation (−20, −18, −13 nt upstream, and +1, +5 nt downstream of transcription start site) in our PDX (n = 15) mouse models (Fig. 1, F–H). In line with TCGA-BLCA findings, a significant inverse correlation was observed between miR-141 expression and MIR141 promoter methylation (rs = −0.550; p = 0.034; Fig. 1G). Moreover, the survival analysis of PDX models highlighted the association of MIR141 promoter hypermethylation with a trend toward worse survival (p = 0.042; Fig. 1H), prompting us to further investigate the underlying epigenetic regulation of miR-141 and clinical evaluation of MIR141 methylation profile in our screening patient’s cohort.

MIR141 core promoter methylation increases patients’ risk for disease progression

Performing expression and methylation analyses in BlCa patients, Spearman correlation analysis verified the significant inverse relationship between miR-141 expression and MIR141 promoter methylation in bladder tumors (rs = −0.396; p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Descriptive analysis (Table S4) highlighted comparable methylation levels of MIR141 promoter CpG sites examined in bladder tumors (mean: 37.8% [−20], 39.1% [−18], 38.5% [−13], 35.6% [+1], 32.8% [+5]). Focusing on MIBC patients, the survival analysis of our screening cohort did not highlight a statistically significant association (Fig. S6A). Therefore, we analyzed the TCGA-BLCA dataset as a validation cohort to further explore the prognostic value of MIR141 promoter methylation in BlCa. Specifically, MIR141 promoter hypermethylation was associated with worse post-treatment survival in TCGA-BLCA cohort, as demonstrated by Kaplan-Meier survival curves (p = 0.051; Fig. S6B), and univariate Cox regression analysis (HR: 1.338; 95% CI: 0.998–1.793; bootstrap p = 0.050). These findings on the TCGA-BLCA cohort confirmed the worse treatment prognosis of patients with hypermethylated MIR141 promoter regions and validated our findings in our screening cohort.

MIR141 promoter methylation-fitted models improve risk-stratification and prognosis of TaT1 patients

Leveraging the clinical value of MIR141 core promoter methylation for patients’ treatment outcome, we next explored its capacity to benefit the predictive value of established disease markers and to improve NMIBC patients’ risk-stratification. Tumor stage/grade and the EORTC risk model (based on tumor number, stage, grade, size, prior recurrence rate and concomitant CIS) are routinely used for NMIBC prognosis and patient’s risk-stratification to guide treatment and follow-up decisions and intensity. In this regard, incorporating MIR141 promoter methylation levels with tumor stage and grade, significantly improved risk stratification among Ta/T1LG and T1HG patients, enabling a more accurate estimation of progression risk (p = 0.012; Fig. 5A). Accordingly, combining MIR141 promoter methylation levels with EORTC risk model markedly enhanced the stratification of low/intermediate- and high-risk patients for progression to invasive disease (p = 0.013; Fig. 5B). Finally, we employed DCA, following Vickers et al., (26) to assess the clinical utility of MIR141 methylation as a prognostic adjunct. Compared to the control prognostic model, including tumor stage, grade, and EORTC risk group, the DCA revealed that the MIR141 methylation-fitted multivariate model provided superior net benefit for predicting PFS outcomes in NMIBC patients (Fig. 5C). Discussion

Although recent advances in clinical management - including improvements in imaging, surgical techniques, diagnostic modalities, and the recent incorporation of artificial intelligence tools - have substantially reduced BlCa–related mortality, the absence of fully developed precision medicine strategies and truly individualized care still limits accurate prognostication and reliable prediction of disease course (27). At the same time, the necessity for lifelong surveillance, driven by the high likelihood of recurrence and/or progression, makes BlCa the most costly malignancy to manage on a per-patient basis, posing a considerable burden on healthcare systems (28, 29). Therefore, the identification of novel molecular markers toward improving patient stratification and guiding personalized therapeutic decisions remains an unmet need that warrants continued and ongoing investigation.

The ENCODE project has demonstrated that ∼70% of the human genome is transcribed into ncRNAs (30). Among them, miRNAs have emerged as pivotal regulators of gene expression, exerting control at both post-transcriptional and epigenetic levels (31). Functioning as key orchestrators of cellular processes, miRNAs maintain homeostasis and normal physiology, whereas their deregulation is a well-recognized hallmark of tumor initiation and progression across diverse cancer types, including BlCa (15, 32). DNA methylation represents a major regulatory layer of gene transcription, typically leading to gene silencing (33, 34). Nearly 50% of human miRNA loci are located within CpG islands, and aberrant methylation has been implicated in the downregulation of several well-known tumor suppressor miRNAs (35), such as miR-34 (36), miR-125a/b (37), miR-137 (38), miR-143/145 (39), and miR-296 (40) across different malignancies. Given the well-documented tumor suppressor role of miR-141-3p, primarily through the inhibition of EMT, we endeavored to explore the epigenetic mechanisms governing miR-141 expression in bladder tumors (33, 34, 41). After all, epigenetic silencing of miR-141 via promoter hypermethylation has also been described in invasive breast, colorectal and prostate cancers (24, 42, 43). In the light of these, we analyzed the DNA methylation status of the MIR141 locus in bladder urothelium to clarify its role in epigenetic regulation and to evaluate its potential clinical significance for patient’s stratification and personalized prognosis.

Following small RNA-seq of TaLG, T1HG, and T2–T4 bladder tumors, miR-141 was identified as one of the most downregulated miRNAs in muscle-invasive and HG tumors. In silico evaluation of MIR141 gene loci revealed a high density of CpG sites within promoter region, while the analysis of the TCGA-BLCA cohort demonstrated a clear inverse association between MIR141 promoter methylation and miR-141 expression across bladder tumors. Validation in our PDX mouse models confirmed the inverse relationship between MIR141 promoter methylation and miR-141 expression and additionally showed that higher methylation was associated with poorer survival in the PDXs.

To expand on these findings, we conducted targeted methylation analysis and clinical evaluation in our screening cohort. Our analysis demonstrated a strong negative correlation between miR-141 expression and MIR141 promoter methylation, reinforcing the impact of epigenetic regulation. Additionally, elevated MIR141 core promoter methylation was significantly linked to muscle-invasive tumors, as well as advanced stage and HG disease. Moreover, the survival analyses in our screening cohort demonstrated that MIR141 promoter hypermethylation is associated with a markedly elevated likelihood of NMIBC (TaT1) patients early-relapse and progression to invasive disease. In addition, the analysis of TCGA-BLCA as validation cohort confirmed the worse post-treatment survival outcome of the patients this hypermethylation of MIR141 promoter region. Overall, multivariate analysis highlighted MIR141 promoter methylation as a robust independent prognostic marker of NMIBC, offering translation impact in clinical decision-making strategies. More precisely, Ta/T1LG patients or those classified as low- or intermediate-risk who exhibit elevated MIR141 promoter methylation and increased likelihood of disease progression could benefit from more intensive treatment approaches, typically reserved for high-risk disease. These include intravesical bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) therapy (1–3 years), intensified follow-up schedules, and improved patient counseling. In cases of BCG-refractory disease or early recurrence, such patients may warrant consideration for radical cystectomy. On the other hand, T1HG and EORTC high-risk subgroups, generally considered as a transitional stage toward more advanced disease and as clinically challenging NMIBC cases to manage, appear to exhibit a more favorable clinical course when presenting lower MIR141 promoter methylation levels. In this context, these patients might benefit from a more individualized risk-adapted therapeutic strategy with close but potentially less intensive management and standard surveillance according to current guidelines. Such an approach could contribute to improved patient stratification and optimization of treatment intensity, avoiding potential overtreatment while preserving oncological safety. Overall, MIR141 promoter methylation-fitted models improved the predictive value of the clinical used prognostic disease markers, refining risk assessment for TaT1 patients.

The observed association between the hypermethylation of MIR141 promoter, the reduced miR-141 expression, and the poor clinical outcome of BlCa patients is consistent with the established tumor-suppressive role of miR-141 (42). Interestingly, supporting our in silico observations in TCGA-BLCA cohort, miR-141 has emerged as a key regulator of EMT by directly targeting the EMT-inducing transcription factors ZEB1 and ZEB2 in colon cancer (44, 45). EMT drives the detachment of tumor cells from the primary lesion, accompanied by phenotypic changes and acquisition of invasive potential, largely through downregulation of adhesion molecules such as E-cadherin (46, 47). In this regard, loss of miR-141 has been documented to result in ZEB1 and ZEB2 accumulation and repression of CDH1 expression towards EMT and tumor invasiveness. Intriguingly, ZEB1 can also directly repress miR-141 transcription by binding to E− and/or Z-box motifs in its promoter, establishing a double-negative feedback loop (48, 49, 50).

EMT represents a critical process in bladder tumors, particularly enriched during progression from superficial (TaT1) to muscle-invasive (T2-T4) disease (51). Additionally, EMT has been linked to higher risks of recurrence, metastasis, and poor survival in human neoplasms, including BlCa (52, 53, 54). Within this context, miR-141 appears to act as a molecular switch that orchestrates EMT, and its epigenetic regulation is thus of considerable biological and clinical significance. Overall, as EMT and tumor invasion are closely linked to aberrant epigenetic remodeling, MIR141 promoter hypermethylation emerges as a promising prognostic biomarker in BlCa. Importantly, restoring miR-141 expression by targeting MIR141 promoter methylation with advanced molecular tools -including Zinc Finger Nucleases (ZFNs), TALENs, and CRISPR/dCas9 platforms-could constitute a promising therapeutic strategy.

Regarding the limitations of our study, bisulfite-based pyrosequencing, although considered a gold-standard method for quantitative DNA methylation at single-base resolution (55, 56), does not distinguish between 5-methylcytosine (5mC) and oxidized cytosine derivatives generated through TET enzyme activity, most notably 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) (57). While promoter-associated 5mC is widely recognized as a transcriptionally repressive epigenetic modification, 5hmC has been associated with transcriptionally active chromatin states and gene regulation (33, 58). Although global 5hmC levels in most somatic tissues are substantially lower than those of 5mC (∼10–15-fold lower at the genome-wide level) (57), and are often further depleted in human malignancies, approaching near-background levels (59, 60, 61), we cannot fully exclude a potential contribution of 5hmC to the methylation signal detected at the MIR141 promoter. In this regard, future studies employing modification-specific approaches, such as 5mC/5hmC immunoprecipitation-based assays or oxidative bisulfite sequencing, may further elucidate the precise epigenetic mechanisms underlying miR-141 silencing and its biological significance in BlCa progression.

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive analysis of MIR141 methylation in BlCa, demonstrating that miR-141 is epigenetically regulated in bladder tumors, with the MIR141 core promoter serving as the principal regulatory region controlling expression. MIR141 core promoter hypermethylation was strongly associated with aggressive tumor phenotypes and significantly higher risk of disease relapse and progression in NMIBC patients independently of the clinically used disease markers. Importantly, multivariate prognostic models fitted for MIR141 promoter methylation enabled superior risk-stratification of the patients, offering a robust framework to guide personalized treatment and monitoring decisions.

Experimental procedures

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mouse models

Subcutaneous implantation of primary bladder tumor specimens (up to 5 mm, depending on sample availability) was performed in 6-week-old NSG mice (stock number 005557) sourced from The Jackson Laboratory. Anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of a ketamine and xylazine mixture (90 mg/kg ketamine, 10 mg/kg xylazine) (62). Tumor growth was monitored weekly using calipers until reaching a maximum diameter of 1 cm, at which point animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Mice were also sacrificed earlier if signs of distress—such as significant weight loss or ulceration—were observed.

Tumor’s growth time varied between 1 and 7 months. Tumors that failed to grow within 8 months (∼30%) were classified as non-engrafted (“no take”) (63). Harvested tumors were either snap frozen at −80 °C for molecular analyses or preserved for future engraftments by cryopreservation in 1.5 ml of 5% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich Inc., St Louis, MO, USA) and 95% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich), gradually frozen at −80 °C overnight in isopropanol-containing containers before transfer to liquid nitrogen storage. All animal procedures took place at the Biomedical Research Foundation of the Academy of Athens (BRFAA) under specific pathogen-free conditions in individually ventilated cages and were conducted following national and international ethical standards, with approval from the BRFAA Animal Ethics Committee and the Attica Prefecture authorities. Clinicopathological features from the PDX cohort are summarized in Table S6.

Screening cohort

The screening cohort included 133 patients diagnosed with primary UBC. Fresh-frozen tumor samples were collected either via transurethral resection of bladder tumors (TURBT) in cases of NMIBC (TaT1) or through radical cystectomy in MIBC (T2-T4) at 'Laiko' General Hospital in Athens, Greece. Clinicopathological features of the participants are summarized in Table 1. All patients received adjuvant treatment in accordance with the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines, with none undergoing neoadjuvant therapy before surgery. Tissue samples were preserved in RNAlater Solution (Ambion) as per the manufacturer's protocol and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Risk-stratification for NMIBC patients was carried out using the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) scoring system (https://www.eortc.be/tools/bladdercalculator/) (64), while post-treatment surveillance followed EAU guidelines, incorporating routine cystoscopy and, for high-grade cases, urinary cytology. MIBC patients (T2–T4) underwent scheduled monitoring with renal ultrasound at 3 months and thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) every six months. Additional imaging, including bone scans and brain MRIs, was performed as clinically indicated. Recurrence (same or lower tumor stage) and progression (recurrence of higher/invasive stage) in NMIBC were histologically confirmed following positive cystoscopy findings and subsequent TURBT. For MIBC patients, recurrence was identified through follow-up CT.

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of “Laiko” General Hospital, Athens, Greece, and conducted in consonance with 1975 Declaration of Helsinki ethical standards, as revised in 2008. Informed consent was obtained by all participating patients.

Validation cohort

TCGA-BLCA cohort was employed as an external validation cohort, comprising 412 primarily diagnosed cases, including patients with UBC (n = 409), alongside single cases of papillary adenocarcinoma, epithelial carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma. Most of the tumors represented muscle-invasive stages (T2–T4; n = 406, 98.5%). DNA methylation data (n = 412 tumor) were obtained via the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 array, while mRNA (n = 407 tumors) and miRNA (n = 409 tumors) expression data were generated using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 RNA-Seq platform. Both datasets, alongside clinicopathological information, are publicly accessible through the UCSC XENA Browser (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) and cbioportal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Clinicopathological features from the TCGA-BLCA cohort are summarized in Table S7.

Small RNA-seq

Small RNA-seq libraries (n = 20) were prepared from 5 Ta low-grade (TaLG), 5 T1 high-grade (T1HG), 5 T2 and 5 T3-T4 tumors from our screening cohort. Library preparation was conducted using the MGIEasy Small RNA Library Prep Kit (MGI Tech Co., Ltd), with 500 ng of total RNA input per sample. Library quality control was assessed via capillary electrophoresis using the Qsep1 (BiOptic Inc. New Taipei City, Taiwan (R.O.C.)) and the S2 Standard Cartridge (BiOptic Inc.). Sequencing was carried out on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (MGI Tech).

In silico analysis

The NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/) and the UCSC Genome Browser gateway (https://genome.ucsc.edu/) were used to analyze CpG site distribution within gene promoter regions, exploiting GRCh37/hg19 assembly. Expression levels of mRNAs, miRNAs and the methylation levels of gene promoters of TCGA-BLCA cohort were visualized and retrieved by XENA Browser Visualization Tool (https://xenabrowser.net/).

TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) was used to predict potential targets of miR-141 (65). To minimize the prediction error rate, stringent inclusion criteria were applied, and only high-confidence targets with a weighted context++ score (CWCS) ≤ −0.4 were retained (65). The resulting gene set was subjected to functional enrichment analysis using Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) (66). The top 10 enriched Biological Process (BP) and Molecular Function (MF) terms were retained, along with six Cellular Component (CC) terms that met the selection criteria (p < 0.05 and combined enrichment score >20). Cell marker and pathway enrichment analysis was subsequently performed using CellMarker and KEGG via Enrichr (66, 67). Finally, volcano and scatter plot visualization of CellMarker and KEGG pathway enrichment results was generated using the Appyter Enrichment Analysis Visualizer (https://appyters.maayanlab.cloud/Enrichment_Analysis_Visualizer/) (68).

Genomic DNA and total RNA extraction

Total RNA and genomic DNA (gDNA) were isolated from 40 to 100 mg of pulverized fresh-frozen tissue using TRI-Reagent (Molecular Research Center), following the manufacturer's protocol. Total RNA was resuspended in RNA Storage Solution (Invitrogen), while gDNA was dissolved in 8 mM NaOH and pH-balanced with 0.1 M HEPES buffer. Both RNA and DNA samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. DNA/RNA concentrations and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically, and RNA quality was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Total RNA polyadenylation and first-strand cDNA synthesis

1 μg of total RNA was polyadenylated at the 3′-end in a 10 μl reaction containing 800 μM ATP and 1 U of E. coli poly(A) polymerase (New England Biolabs), incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. The enzyme was subsequently inactivated by heating at 65 °C for 10 min. Following polyadenylation, reverse transcription was performed using an oligo-dT adapter primer (Table S8) in a final reaction volume of 20 μl. The reaction included 50 U M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), 40 U RNaseOUT Recombinant Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Invitrogen), 500 μm dNTP mix, and 0.25 μm oligo-dT adapter primer. The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70 °C for 15 min.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantification of miR-141-3p levels was conducted using SYBR Green-based quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Specific forward primers (Table S8) for miR-141-3p, and the small nucleolar RNA SNORD48 (also known as RNU48), were designed based on their published sequences (NCBI RefSeq: NR_029682.1, and NR_002745.1, respectively) and validated through in silico analysis. Each forward primer was paired with a universal reverse primer (Table S8), complementary to the oligo-dT adapter, producing amplicons of 65 bp for miR-141-3p, and 105 bp for RNU48.

The qPCR reactions were performed using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with a 10 μl reaction volume comprising Kapa SYBR Fast Universal 2X qPCR Master Mix (Kapa Biosystems), 200 nM of each primer, and 0.2 ng of cDNA template. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial 3 min denaturation at 95 °C for enzyme activation, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s for annealing and extension. Melt curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis were used to confirm amplification specificity and exclude non-specific products or primer-dimer formation.

Relative quantification of miR-141 expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. All reactions were performed in duplicate, and the average Ct value was used for analysis. RNU48 served as the internal reference control for normalization.

Sodium bisulfite conversion of gDNA

Bisulfite conversion of unmethylated cytosine (C) residues to uracil (U) was conducted using the EpiMark Bisulfite Conversion Kit (New England Biolabs). Specifically, 1.5 μg of gDNA was treated with a sodium bisulfite solution and subjected to a series of thermal cycles involving denaturation and incubation steps: 95 °C for 5 min, 65 °C for 30 min, followed by two additional rounds of 95 °C for 5 min and 65 °C for 60 min and 90 min, respectively. Upon completion of the conversion process, desulfonation, purification, and elution of the DNA were carried out using EpiMark spin columns in line with the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting bisulfite-modified DNA was preserved at −80 °C for downstream analyses.

PCR amplification of bisulfite-converted gDNA

PCR assay was designed and optimized to target the core promoter region of the MIR141 gene, generating amplicon of 102 bp, using bisulfite-converted gDNA as the template. Primers specific for the bisulfite-treated DNA were developed using the PyroMark Assay Design 2.0 Software (Qiagen), based on the reference sequence (NCBI RefSeq: NC_000012.11; Table S8).

Each PCR reaction was performed in a 25 μl volume, comprising 1.5 μl of bisulfite-treated gDNA, 200 μm dNTPs, 400 nM forward and reverse primers, and 1 U of EpiMark Hot Start Taq DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs). Thermal cycling consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 amplification cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 59 °C for 30 s, and extension at 68 °C for 1 min, concluding with a final extension step at 68 °C for 5 min. Negative (non-template) controls were included to ensure specificity, and agarose gel electrophoresis was used to confirm successful amplification and absence of non-specific products or primer dimers.

Methylation analysis by pyrosequencing

Biotin-labeled PCR products (18 μl) were incubated with 20 μl of Binding Buffer (Qiagen) and 2 μl of streptavidin-coated sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) under agitation at 1200 rpm for 20 min. The immobilized products were then processed into single-stranded DNA using the PyroMark Q24 vacuum workstation (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Single-stranded amplicons were mixed with 0.3 μm sequencing primer (Table S8) in Qiagen’s annealing buffer, heated at 80 °C for 2 min, and allowed to cool at room temperature for 7 min to enable primer binding. Methylation profiling was performed using the PyroMark Q24 Pyrosequencer and PyroMark Gold Q24 Reagents (Qiagen), following standard protocols. Quantitative methylation percentages at CpG sites were calculated using the PyroMark Q24 Software 2.0 (Qiagen). Successful bisulfite conversion was verified by assessing the conversion of non-CpG cytosines within the amplified region.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. Due to non-normal distribution, non-parametric tests were employed: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare miR-141 expression and MIR141 methylation between tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues, while the Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to examine associations with clinicopathological features.

Survival analysis was performed by Kaplan-Meier curves using log-rank tests, along with univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Optimal cutoffs for MIR141 promoter methylation levels were defined using the X-tile algorithm. To assess the robustness of the multivariate model, internal validation was performed via bootstrap analysis with 1000 iterations. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was employed to estimate the clinical utility of MIR141 promoter methylation for prognostic purposes, following the methodology of Vickers et al., (26) using STATA version 13 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).
