Effect of oxycodone vs. morphine as first‐line opioid on new persistent opioid use after orthopaedic surgery: A prospective sequential cohort study
Authors: Eward J. Melis, Johanna E. Vriezekolk, Frédérique H. C. Bollen, Dirk E. Schrander, Arnt F. A. Schellekens, Bart J. F. van den Bemt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative opioids, intended for short‐term analgesia, contribute to new persistent opioid use in 1%–7% of patients, adversely affecting outcomes. Oxycodone may carry higher risk than morphine, though long‐term data are limited. This prospective comparative sequential cohort study assessed whether replacing from oxycodone with morphine as first‐line opioid after orthopaedic surgery affected new persistent opioid use at 6 months and maintained analgesic equivalence.MethodsPatients ≥18 years undergoing orthopaedic surgery requiring ≥1 night hospitalization were enrolled April 2023 to January 2024. Primary outcome: new persistent opioid use at 6 months, assessed by online survey. Secondary outcome: analgesic effectiveness assessed by pain at rest (NRS ≥ 4) at discharge and 6 months, and daily opioid use (oral morphine equivalents) during hospitalization.ResultsOf 3675 eligible patients, 1894 (51.5%) completed the survey and were included (mean [SD] age 62.9 [13.1] year; 62.5% female). Among these, 1021 were in the morphine first‐line opioid cohort and 873 in the oxycodone first‐line opioid cohort. The proportion of new persistent opioid use at 6 months after surgery was 2.5% (morphine) vs. 2.6% (oxycodone) (p = .90). Pain at rest (NRS ≥ 4) was reported by 9.9% vs. 12.5% at discharge, and 19.3% vs. 19.7% at 6 months (morphine vs. oxycodone). Median (IQR) daily opioid dose during hospitalization was 12.3 (0–30) and 15 mg (0–30) (morphine vs. oxycodone). No significant differences were observed.ConclusionsReplacing oxycodone with morphine as first‐line opioid after orthopaedic surgery did not appear to reduce new persistent opioid use (NPOU) and provided comparable analgesia.

INTRODUCTION

In a recent multicenter cohort study across 25 countries, opioids were prescribed following discharge to approximately 48% of orthopaedic, 33% of urologic, 29% of general and 21% of gynecologic surgery patients. 1 In the context of postoperative pain, opioids are intended for short‐term use. 2 , 3 , 4 However, opioids prescribed for postoperative pain management lead to new persistent opioid use (NPOU) in approximately 1%–7% of patients, depending on the surgical procedure. 5 , 6 , 7 Since NPOU is associated with poor health outcomes, 8 and increased healthcare, 9 preventive strategies are needed. One strategy is using evidence‐based prescribing guidelines, including preference for opioids with low abuse liability. 10 However, current guidelines do not specify which opioid carries the lowest risk of persistent use and should therefore be the first‐line in postoperative pain management. 2 , 3 , 4 , 11

NPOU may be attributable to the pharmacological properties of the opioid itself, which can induce physiological dependence and addiction. 12 Globally, oxycodone is one of the most widely used opioids for postoperative pain management, 13 , 14 , 15 however it also has been identified as a major contributor to the opioid epidemic in the United States. 16 Evidence suggests that oxycodone has a higher addictive potential and is more likely than other opioids to lead to persistent use. 12 , 13 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 This may be at least partly attributable to its unique pharmacological properties: oxycodone penetrates the brain more effectively than morphine, 12 , 20 produces higher dopamine concentration in reward pathways than morphine 17 and has a higher abuse liability than morphine. 18 Retrospective cohort studies also report that oxycodone use after hip fracture or orthopaedic surgery nearly doubles the risk of NPOU compared with morphine or tapentadol, respectively. 13 , 19 The aforementioned studies have inherent limitations—animal models, 12 , 17 , 20 short‐term outcomes 18 and retrospective databases 13 , 19 —which restrict robust conclusions about which opioid carries the lowest NPOU risk.

Prospective studies directly comparing different opioids in equipotent doses are needed to identify which opioid carries the lowest risk of NPOU. In this prospective comparative sequential cohort study, oxycodone was replaced with morphine as first‐line opioid. The objective was to compare oxycodone and morphine as first‐line opioids after orthopaedic surgery with respect to NPOU at 6 months while maintaining equivalent analgesia at discharge and at 6 months. It was hypothesized that replacing oxycodone with morphine would reduce the risk of NPOU.

METHODS

Setting, design and study population

This prospective comparative cohort study was conducted at the Department of Orthopedics, Sint Maartenskliniek, a tertiary orthopaedic centre in the Netherlands, and included two sequential cohorts. The oxycodone cohort included consecutive patients who underwent surgery between April and August 2023 under the previous postoperative pain protocol with oxycodone as first‐line opioid. The morphine cohort included consecutive patients who underwent surgery between September 2023 and January 2024 under the new postoperative pain protocol with morphine as first‐line opioid. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years, underwent any type of orthopaedic surgery and stayed at least one night. Exclusion criteria included surgery within 3 months prior to the index date, surgery more than 3 months after the index date, or declining email contact or research participation. Surgeries beyond 3 months were considered new episodes with potential opioid reinitiation, possibly affecting 6‐month opioid use, and were therefore excluded. Reoperations within 3 months were typically related to complications of the index procedure and were reported in baseline characteristics. The study was powered to detect a 2% reduction in NPOU, requiring 768 patients per cohort. Further details of the calculation of the sample size are shown in Supporting Information S1. 5 , 13 The Medical Research Ethics Committee Oost‐Nederland (MREC Oost‐Nederland), Radboudumc, Nijmegen, The Netherlands reviewed the study and determined that it was not subject to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (case number 2024‐17646). Analyses involving pseudonymized medical record data, including data from non‐responders, were conducted under the hospital's routine informed consent procedure for the use of medical record data for scientific research. The STROBE Statement checklist 21 was followed to ensure high‐quality reporting.

The intervention

The intervention investigated in this study was a modification of the postoperative pain protocol, altering the order of the prescribed opioids. Previously, oxycodone was first‐line, morphine second‐line and buprenorphine third‐line. In the new protocol, morphine was first‐line, buprenorphine second‐line and oxycodone third‐line. Second‐ or third‐line opioids were prescribed if analgesia was insufficient or adverse effects occurred. The protocol employs a multimodal analgesic approach consisting of standard prescriptions of acetaminophen, an NSAID, and a short‐acting opioid for elective orthopaedic surgeries requiring overnight hospitalization, unless contraindicated. Additional components of the multimodal regimen comprised peripheral nerve blocks (administered either as single injections or via a catheter in situ), epidural analgesia, antineuropathic agents and benzodiazepines for myogenic pain associated with spine surgery. During hospitalization, patients are treated according to the postoperative pain protocol to ensure adequate analgesia and enable discharge with continuation of the established analgesic regimen. No changes were made to this multimodal analgesic regimen during the study period. As part of routine clinical practice, efforts to reduce length of hospital stay were ongoing during this period.

The postoperative pain protocol was modified by the Drug Committee of the Sint Maartenskliniek, which comprises an internist, orthopaedic surgeon, anesthesiologist, clinical pharmacologist, pharmacist and other clinicians. Based on available literature, the committee concluded that (1) oxycodone and morphine have comparable analgesic efficacy and adverse effects at equivalent doses, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 (2) both are approved for postoperative pain and included in guidelines 2 , 3 , 4 , 11 and (3) the risk of NPOU appears lower with morphine. 12 , 13 , 17 , 18

The new postoperative pain protocol was implemented on 4 September 2023, for all newly admitted patients, with morphine as first‐line opioid. Patients already hospitalized on the transition day remained on the previous protocol with oxycodone as first‐line opioid. After 4 September 2023, any remaining oxycodone orders were converted to morphine by pharmacy technicians unless contraindicated. Staff were informed via internal communication channels.

Study procedure

Eligible patients received a personalized email via Castor EDC© to complete a web‐based survey 6 months after surgery. The survey included an information letter, informed consent, questionnaires on current opioid use and opioid‐related side effects and a pain assessment. The questionnaire used a forced‐response design with adaptive questioning, which reduced complexity and allowed respondents to review, modify and track their responses; only complete‐case responses were included in the analysis. Three researchers (E. M., J. V. and M.S.) assessed system usability and technical performance. The information letter outlined study aims, data handling and contact details. Informed consent was required to complete the survey. Non‐respondents received an automatic reminder after 4 weeks via Castor EDC©. No incentives were offered.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the proportion of patients with NPOU 6 months after surgery. NPOU was defined as no opioid use preceding surgery (i.e., opioid‐naive patients) and any opioid use 6 months after surgery. The overall proportion of patients with persistent opioid use 6 months after surgery was reported as a secondary outcome; this included both patients with NPOU and those who were using opioids prior to surgery and continued use 6 months after surgery. Additional secondary outcomes, at hospital discharge, included daily opioid dose during hospitalization and pain intensity scores. Six months after surgery, secondary outcomes included daily opioid dose, pain intensity scores and opioid‐related side effects.

Measures

Persistent opioid use was assessed using an online survey. Before assessing current opioid use in the survey, participants were asked two preliminary questions: ‘Are you still using one or more pain medications since your surgery 6 months ago?’ (if no, the survey ended) and ‘Are you using these medications for pain in the area where you had surgery?’. If the opioid was not used for pain in the surgical area, it was nevertheless classified as persistent opioid use. Respondents could then choose from a comprehensive list of pain medications available in the Netherlands, specifying which pain medications they were currently using, along with the dosage and frequency.

Daily opioid dose during hospitalization was obtained from administration records in the electronic medical record. All opioids administered on the orthopaedic ward were summed and divided by the length of hospitalization (rounded to full days) to calculate the average daily dose, which was subsequently converted to an oral morphine equivalent daily dose (OMEDD). The conversion factors used are presented in Supporting Information S2.

Daily opioid dose 6 months after surgery was determined from self‐reported opioid use in the online survey, where participants specified the frequency of use for each opioid by selecting from predefined response categories. The reported doses of all opioids were then summed and converted to an OMEDD using the conversion factors presented in Supporting Information S2.

Pain scores at discharge and 6 months after surgery were measured using a numeric rating scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). Patients were asked to rate the intensity of pain at rest in the affected surgical body region. The pain score was dichotomized into two categories: <4 and ≥4. In clinical practice, a pain score of ≥4 is considered unacceptable and typically prompts intervention.

Opioid‐related side effects 6 months after surgery were assessed through the online survey. For each opioid used, patients were asked whether they experienced any side effects; if so, they were prompted to specify them from 0 predefined categories (see Supporting Information S3).

Patient and clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic medical record: age, sex, affected surgical body region (foot/ankle, hip, upper extremity, knee and spine), length of hospitalization (days), opioid use prior to admission and at discharge (type, dose and frequency) and reoperation within 3 months (yes/no). Opioid use prior to admission was routinely assessed from each patient during preoperative screening, approximately 4 weeks before hospitalization, and subsequently documented in the medical record.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses of baseline characteristics were performed. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as proportions. Comparisons were made using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney U tests or t‐tests, as appropriate for continuous variables. A non‐respondent analysis was performed to assess potential differences in baseline characteristics between survey respondents and non‐respondents. The proportion of patients with NPOU 6 months after surgery in both cohorts was analysed using intention‐to‐treat analysis with a chi‐square test. Subsequently, a per‐protocol analysis (based on discharge opioid) was performed adjusting for adherence differences to the postoperative pain protocol between cohorts. A post hoc sensitivity analysis using logistic regression was performed to assess NPOU 6 months after surgery; the model included cohort, baseline characteristics (Table 1) and calendar period to account for potential historical cohort effects; inverse probability‐of‐response weighting based on variables associated with response was applied to account for differential follow‐up response between cohorts (see Table S4). Cohort comparison regarding the overall proportion of patients with persistent opioid use 6 months after surgery, dichotomized pain scores at discharge and after 6 months and opioid‐related side effects after 6 months was performed using chi‐square tests. The daily opioid dose during hospitalization, the pain score at rest at discharge, the daily opioid dose 6 months after surgery and the pain scores 6 months after surgery were compared with Mann–Whitney U‐tests or t‐test, as appropriate. Significance level was set at p value < .05. All analyses were performed using STATA version 17.

RESULTS

A total of 4187 patients underwent orthopaedic surgery between April 2023 and January 2024. Of these, 512 were excluded: surgery within 3 months prior to the index date (n = 234), surgery more than 3 months after the index date (n = 107) or declined email contact or research participation (n = 171). This resulted in 3675 eligible patients, of whom 1894 (51.5%) completed the survey and were included; mean (SD) age was 62.9 (±13.1) years, and 62.5% were female. Enrolment for both cohorts is shown in Figure 1. Baseline eligibility was similar between cohorts, with comparable exclusion rates. Sample characteristics did not differ significantly, except for shorter hospitalization, lower buprenorphine use at discharge and fewer opioid‐free patients at discharge in the morphine cohort (Table 1). The response rate was higher in the oxycodone cohort (53.8% vs. 49.7%; p = .014). Non‐respondent analysis showed that survey respondents were older, more often female, and had fewer upper extremity procedures and less preoperative opioid use compared with non‐respondents (Supporting Information S4). In both cohorts, the types of opioids used at discharge among opioid‐naïve patients were comparable to those in the total study population (Table 1), except that opioid‐naïve patients used tramadol less frequently (morphine cohort: 0.7% vs. 7.6%; oxycodone cohort: 1.5% vs. 9.1%).

Primary outcome: Proportion of patients with NPOU 6 months after surgery in the morphine and oxycodone cohorts

The proportions of patients with NPOU 6 months after surgery in the morphine and oxycodone cohort are shown in Table 2; no significant differences were found between the cohorts. Six months after surgery, the median (IQR) daily opioid dose (OMEDD) among patients with NPOU was 10.2 mg (2.9–30.0) in the morphine cohort and 8.6 mg (2.9–15.0) in the oxycodone cohort (p = .12). In the post hoc sensitivity analysis, the odds ratio for NPOU 6 months after surgery comparing the oxycodone and morphine cohorts was 0.40 (95% CI 0.03–5.68; p = .50) after adjustment for baseline characteristics (Table 1) and calendar period and application of inverse probability‐of‐response weighting to account for differential follow‐up response (see Section 2 and Table S4).

Secondary outcomes: Overall proportion of persistent opioid use 6 months after surgery and the occurrence of opioid‐related side effects in the morphine and oxycodone cohorts

DISCUSSION

This prospective cohort study compared morphine and oxycodone as first‐line opioids in patients undergoing orthopaedic surgery with respect NPOU. The proportion of patients with NPOU 6 months after surgery did not differ between morphine (2.5%) and oxycodone (2.6%). Analgesic effectiveness was comparable, with similar pain severity at discharge and 6 months, and similar opioid consumption during hospitalization (OMEDD) in both cohorts.

Although previous studies suggest otherwise, this study found no difference between morphine and oxycodone regarding NPOU after orthopaedic surgery. Earlier nonclinical and laboratory research suggested that morphine has a lower addictive potential 12 , 17 , 18 , 20 and a reduced risk of NPOU 13 compared to oxycodone, providing a rationale for preferring morphine over oxycodone in postoperative pain management. This lower addictive potential was based on pharmacological in vitro models, animal studies 12 , 17 , 20 and likability studies involving limited opioid doses. 18 However, such studies provide limited insight into long‐term clinical outcomes. The only long‐term study showed that NPOU was lower with initial morphine (9%) than oxycodone (14%) after hip fracture—a 1.6‐fold difference. 13 As a retrospective database study, it lacked information on opioid selection rationale, introducing potential indication bias and limiting interpretation. In the current study, the proportion of patients with NPOU was 2.6%, which aligns with previous research reporting average rates of 2–4%. 5 , 6 , 7 Although clinically very relevant, this prevalence was so low that the ability to detect meaningful differences between treatment strategies may have been limited, potentially due to a floor effect. Moreover, NPOU is a complex and multifactorial phenomenon, 7 which may explain why a potentially lower addictive potential of morphine compared to oxycodone does not translate into a clinically meaningful difference in NPOU risk.

The response rate was lower in the morphine cohort, possibly because surveys were partly distributed during the holiday period (July–August). Baseline differences between survey respondents and non‐respondents were minor and are unlikely to have influenced the primary outcome. Overall, patient characteristics were comparable between cohorts. Adherence to the postoperative pain protocol was higher in the morphine cohort (75.2% vs. 62.5%), likely reflecting renewed prescriber awareness after the protocol change. Fewer patients were opioid‐free at discharge in the morphine cohort, possibly due to shorter hospital stays—consistent with trends of decreasing hospitalization 30 —resulting in greater analgesic need at discharge. Nevertheless, median inpatient opioid doses and NPOU risk were similar across cohorts, with consistent results in both intention‐to‐treat and per‐protocol analyses adjusting for baseline opioid use.

In a 2021 study from our hospital, we reported an overall proportion of persistent opioid use after surgery of 12.5%. 5 The lower proportion observed in the current study may reflect increased awareness of opioid‐related risks, suggesting a cohort effect. This may also partly account for the slight decline at 6 months between the oxycodone (8.8%) and morphine (7.1%) cohorts, although the difference was not statistically significant. The median daily opioid dose (OMEDD) during hospitalization, as well as pain scores at discharge and 6 months after surgery, were comparable between the morphine and oxycodone cohorts. About one‐third of patients with persistent opioid use experienced side effects, with similar rates in both cohorts, consistent with previous reports of comparable analgesic effectiveness and side effect rates for morphine and oxycodone. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the inclusion of a large prospective cohort, enabling meaningful comparisons of the risk of NPOU after orthopaedic surgery between morphine and oxycodone. An additional strength is that the study utilizes real‐world data, which enhances the external validity of the results and makes them highly applicable to clinical practice. One limitation of the study is its non‐randomized design with two sequential cohorts, introducing the potential for historical cohort effects, such as increased awareness of the risks of persistent opioid use over time. Additionally, minor differences in response rate and baseline opioid use differences, between the cohorts could affect their comparability. However, sensitivity analyses adjusting for potential historical bias and differential response rates between cohorts did not materially alter the results. In the sample size calculation, a moderate effect size was assumed; although a smaller effect size—requiring a larger sample—might have been more appropriate based on previous literature, the nearly identical rates of NPOU (2.5% vs. 2.6%) suggest that increasing the sample size would be unlikely to change the conclusion. NPOU in this study was estimated solely by patient self‐report, which would have been more robust if supported by pharmacy dispensing records; however, such data were not available. Finally, as this is a single‐centre study including only orthopaedic patients, the results may not be easily generalizable to other patient populations.

Clinical implications

Replacing oxycodone with morphine as the first‐line opioid following orthopaedic surgery does not reduce the risk of NPOU. NPOU is a complex, multifactorial phenomenon, with chronic pain and psychiatric comorbidities—particularly mood and substance use disorders—consistently associated with increased postoperative risk. 7 A recent study found that arthroplasty patients using psychopharmacological treatment have nearly twofold higher risk of developing NPOU. 31 Given this complexity, effectively reducing the risk of NPOU may require a comprehensive, multimodal strategy that extends beyond simply substituting one opioid for another. 32 Such a multimodal approach may include preoperative risk assessment, 32 patient and caregiver education, 32 the setting of realistic pain expectations, 33 shared decision‐making regarding analgesia 33 and continuous evaluation of pain management throughout the perioperative period. 33 In addition, feedback interventions to surgical clinicians using peer comparisons and patient‐reported data, may further support appropriate opioid prescribing. 34 Although several risk factors for NPOU have been identified, future research should focus on elucidating predictive factors to better identify and target patients at increased risk. Nonetheless, there may be other reasons to designate morphine as the first‐line opioid, including patient preferences 35 and the fact that morphine, unlike oxycodone, tramadol, hydrocodone, fentanyl and methadone, is associated with significantly fewer drug–drug interactions, particularly with strong CYP3A4 modulators 12 , 36 , 37 such as rifampicin and carbamazepine. Finally, morphine and oxycodone demonstrate comparable analgesia and side effect profiles.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings of this study indicate that replacing oxycodone with morphine as a first‐line opioid for postoperative pain management in orthopaedic patients may not reduce the risk of NPOU and results in comparable analgesia with a similar side‐effect profile at equivalent doses.
