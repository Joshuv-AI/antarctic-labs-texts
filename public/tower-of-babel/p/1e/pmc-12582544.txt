Development of a novel web-based platform to support culturally and medically tailored comprehensive nutrition assessments: SPICE-Healthcare
Authors: Minakshi Raj, Haeley Peters, Jemimah Bakare, Fangyu Zhou, Varsha Chikkamagaluru Lakshmikanth, Jackson Vaughan, Lisa Gatzke, Matthew Berry, Naiman Khan, Margarita Teran-Garcia, Ian Brooks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesA point-of-care resource is required to support Registered Dietitian Nutritionists (RDNs) in providing comprehensive culturally-tailored nutrition services. This study aims to describe the iterative development and testing of a comprehensive nutrition assessment tool as part of a novel web-based platform to Support Personalized and Inclusive Cuisines in Environments for Healthcare (SPICE-Healthcare).Materials and MethodsLeveraging co-design approaches, we iteratively developed and tested a nutrition assessment tool with RDNs across the U.S. We conducted qualitative interviews, a usability testing session, and follow-up survey and interviews to evaluate the usability, acceptability, ease of use, and likelihood of using the tool among practicing RDNs.ResultsOur development of the assessment tool involved four phases (n = 40 total): (1) conceptualizing its structure and vision, (2) testing a low-fidelity prototype and designing optimized features (n = 10); (3) testing a high-fidelity prototype with optimized (culturally personalized) features (n = 10); and (4) testing a fully functioning, programmed tool (n = 20). Findings suggest above average usability of the tool (Phase 4 average System Usability Scale score = 80 out of 100) and acceptability (Phase 4 average Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 score = 26.5 out of 32).DiscussionOur study suggests potential for future implementation testing of the nutrition assessment tool within SPICE-Healthcare. The use of co-design approaches enabled the collaborative generation and evaluation of the assessment tool with expert RDN end-users.ConclusionThe comprehensive nutrition assessment tool within SPICE-Healthcare shows potential for facilitating culturally and medically tailored nutrition assessments at the point-of-care to support adults with diet-related health conditions.

Introduction

Recognition of the relationship between food and health status is steadily growing in the U.S. with rising prevalence of diet-related chronic health conditions such as Type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and dementia alongside rising costs associated with the long-term management of such conditions.1–6 For example, many Americans eat less than the recommended amounts of fruits and vegetables, whole grains, beans, and legumes and more than suggested amounts of meat, sodium, saturated fats, and added sugars per the 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans7–10 These trends highlight the critical role of food and nutrition interventions in improving health status within the broader context of health and healthcare services.11

Registered Dietitian Nutritionists (RDNs) have an important role in supporting nutrition education and counseling for individuals with diet-related health conditions. RDNs are nationally credentialed food and nutrition experts with specific academic and clinical training requirements; they are licensed to provide nutrition services via Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT), an effective treatment plan to manage and treat diet-related health conditions such as overweight, obesity, malnutrition, cancer, diabetes, heart disease, and kidney disease.12–16 MNT applies the Nutrition Care Process (NCP), a systematic approach to providing high quality nutrition services from assessment through diagnosis, intervention, monitoring and evaluation.17 During the nutrition assessment (hereon, assessment)—which is usually conducted by an RDN18—the clinician collects information about a patient to understand their medical history, anthropometric measurements, food and nutrition-related history, and dietary preferences, requirements, and restrictions.17 The assessment, which may be repeated, is crucial for identifying the problem, symptom, and etiology of diet-related issues, and subsequently, for developing an effective and individualized care plan.17

Various tools support nutrition screening (ie, risk factor identification) and assessment (ie, compilation of in-depth information towards a diagnosis) and are used across populations (eg, children versus adults), geographic contexts (eg, US vs Taiwan), and/or clinical setting (eg, hospital versus outpatient).19 These tools have been validated in different populations; for instance, the Nutritional Risk Index and Mini Nutritional Assessment have been developed specifically for older adults in the hospital setting with an emphasis on screening for malnutrition and the Subjective Global Assessment is used in the surgical population and in contexts such as hemodialysis.19 However, for adult nutrition services, the most common types of information collected in the assessment include usual body weight, current weight, weight history, height, body mass index, fat loss, muscle loss, chewing and swallowing assessment, gastrointestinal function, and nutrition intake.18 Nutrition intake often involves self-reported approaches such as a 24-hour dietary recall or food frequency questionnaire of consumption over a short time-period.20 Consumer-facing monitoring devices (eg, continuous glucose monitor) and smartphone and web-based applications (eg, MyFitnessPal) can also collect information (eg, daily steps, macronutrient intake, physical activity) that clinicians can view.21

The modality of conducting assessments also varies—for example, in the hospital setting, RDNs may leverage the Electronic Health Record (EHR) for patient health information including lab results, anthropometric measurements, and medical history.18 Across settings (eg, hospitals, long-term care, outpatient, private practice) there is tremendous variation in the types of tools used for facilitating the various aspects of assessments including gathering, compiling, and storing patient information.18,22 For instance, some RDNs use specific sections within the EHR to gather and document assessment information, while others use a template Word document that they modify or customize as needed (ie, an “in-house tool”), and others document entirely on paper without specific guidance or prompts.18,22

In addition to variation in the types and modalities of assessments within the adult population, there are also significant gaps in the types of information collected presenting barriers to the personalization of nutrition services.23,24 In particular, existing assessments—including those conducted in a traditional clinical setting and those collecting information remotely—lack cultural relevance, linguistic accessibility, and may not be accurate representations of the nutritional quality of culturally diverse foods and eating patterns which limits the relevance and effectiveness to multicultural populations who experience an amplified risk of diet-related health conditions.21,25–28 One significant barrier to cultural personalization of nutrition services is the limited knowledge and awareness of culturally diverse dietary norms and traditions.29 A clinician-facing tool is required to support assessments in a way that enhances medical and cultural personalization that additionally functions as an educational, point-of-care resource for RDNs.30 The purpose of this article is to describe the iterative development and testing of a comprehensive nutrition assessment feature within SPICE-Healthcare, a tool to Support Personalized and Inclusive Cuisines in Environments for Healthcare.

Methods

Study overview

This mixed methods study was conducted between May 2024 and April 2025. We used an iterative co-design approach whereby stakeholders (eg, RDNs) who are expected to be the primary user of the tool or product are deeply involved in the development, design, implementation, and evaluation of the tool in an environment that fosters trust and open communication between the stakeholders, researchers, designers, and software developers in order to improve design outcomes and enhance inclusion.31 The study was granted permission with exemption by the [blinded for review] Institutional Review Board.

Platform design and features

Our development of the assessment tool comprised four phases: (1) conceptualizing the structure and vision of the assessment tool; (2) testing a low-fidelity prototype of the tool and designing optimized features; and (3) testing a high-fidelity prototype of the tool with optimized (culturally personalized) features; and (4) testing a fully functioning, programmed tool.

We used Figma Design (Figma) for prototyping and testing the assessment function. Figma is a collaborative web-based application for interface design enabling collaborative creation, sharing, and testing of designs for websites and other digital products.32,33 Our use of Figma streamlined the development process by providing clear, reusable design patterns that could be efficiently translated into code. Moreover, for subsequent testing, Figma was ideal for allowing real-time edits on the interface design (eg, fixing minor bugs or issues) during usability testing sessions without interfering with user participants’ actions or compromising their workflow. Although Figma requires use of pre-filled open-text fields, meaning that participants could not enter new data into fields during Phase 2 and 3 testing, the interface was still considered superior given that the purpose of our initial testing was to evaluate the content and “look-and-feel” of the prototype, rather than any specific responses provided by participants.

Participants

Participants included RDNs practicing in U.S. healthcare settings who were recruited to participate in of usability testing to evaluate the content and delivery of information within the nutrition assessment function of SPICE-Healthcare. We recruited participants through the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics member interest groups, informational QR codes displayed at the Academy’s 2024 Annual Meeting, and snowball sampling (ie, participants referred subsequent participants to our study). Since some recruitment information was viewed by an indeterminable number of people (eg, displayed QR code at conference), we are unable to calculate a response rate.

Procedures

After our initial conceptualization of the prototype (Phase 1), we conducted three rounds of usability testing (Phases 2-4) from May 2024-April 2025 over Zoom (Figure 1). Sessions were audio-recorded, transcribed via Zoom live-captioning, and edited for analysis by a member of the study team. One member of the study team (facilitator) led all testing sessions while at least one other member either took notes during the session or while reviewing the recording. These testing sessions ranged from 1-1.5 hours and involved four components: (1) qualitative interview, (2) click-through usability testing, (3) quantitative survey, and (4) qualitative follow-up interview.

Summary of phased testing.

Qualitative interview

We conducted a semi-structured qualitative interview to learn about participants’ professional experience (eg, current and previous roles, time in current role, patient population, practice setting), assessment tools typically used in practice (including formal tools used in addition to types of information collected in customized forms), and perceived advantages and limitations of current assessment tools. Participants completing more than one testing session were only interviewed during their first instance of participation.

Click-through usability testing

The facilitator led a click-through demonstration of the platform. In Phase 2 testing, participants were provided a link to the Figma prototype and were asked to share their screen. Participants were reminded that all open-text prompts were pre-filled and that they could click on selection boxes or text boxes to populate information. They were then instructed to interact with each page while verbalizing their thoughts on content and functionality. The moderator instructed participants to first indicate with a “yes” or “no” whether each prompt provided on the assessment was one that they would typically see on their own assessment, and then, to complete each prompt. If the participant indicated “yes,” they were instructed to move to the next prompt. If they indicated “no,” the moderator asked them to verbalize and describe their perceived value of that prompt. At the end of each section, participants were asked to discuss any content areas that were missing, redundant, or unnecessary. In Phase 3 testing, based on findings from qualitative descriptions of navigability and ease of use of the platform, and due to some technical issues with screen sharing on workplace computers, the moderator shared their screen and led the click through demonstration. In both rounds, participants were asked to prioritize the content of the assessment function rather than the delivery of information. In Phase 4, the tool was fully functional, and participants were asked to share their screen and complete the assessment while verbalizing their thoughts related to content, and their actions related to functionality, with minimal intervention from the research team. For this phase, participants were asked to conduct a full initial assessment just as they would with a new patient using the fully programmed tool. One study team member acted as the patient using scripted responses to assessment questions. Another study team member observed and took notes on participant feedback.

Quantitative measures

Following the click-through demonstration, participants were provided a link to a brief online survey via REDCap. The survey included the validated ten-item System Usability Scale [SUS], the eight-item Client Satisfaction Questionnaire [CSQ-8], and a set of questions used to contextualize the participant’s professional role (eg, specialty areas, practice or interest groups within the Academy, tenure in RDN role), workplace information (eg, type of facility and whether it is public or private), and sociodemographic information (eg, age, gender, race and ethnicity).34,35 Participants completed the survey during the session; one participant completed the survey after the session due to time constraints.

Follow-up qualitative interview

After the survey, we conducted a brief semi-structured interview to explore usability, usefulness, and acceptability of the platform in greater depth. We asked participants to describe (1) how easy or difficult it was to use the tool, (2) how likely they would be to use it in practice; and (3) other potential additions to the tool. We also asked participants to reflect on additional types of information they would want to gather through an assessment to support personalized, person-centered, and culturally tailored nutrition services.

Data analysis

We analyzed qualitative interviews using a content analysis, whereby we evaluated similarities and differences in (a) types of assessment tools used, (b) information obtained through assessment tools, and (c) perceived limitations or gaps in current assessment tools.

During or after the session, a member of the research team used a “tool testing tracker” on Microsoft Excel that we developed internally for each phase of click-through testing. The “tool testing tracker” included information on every prompt of the assessment tool, response options, and an area to document positive feedback and suggestions for each item. After recording notes for individual prompts, this information was given to the designer and/or software developer who evaluated all interviews within each phase to identify commonalities in suggestions or concerns expressed by participants. Suggestions or issues discussed by multiple participants were prioritized for discussion and modification; other suggestions were presented to subsequent participants and implemented if there were no perceived concerns about the addition (eg, utility of question relative to length of assessment).

We summarized frequencies and proportions of quantitative measures and scored the validated scales (SUS, CSQ-8) per validated methodology for each distinct phase. To analyze the follow-up qualitative interview, we identified patterns in the types of information participants expressed as facilitators and barriers to usability and the types of information they desired in subsequent phases. The qualitative follow-up interview in combination with the click-through testing also functioned as a generative co-design process, whereby we prompted participants to brainstorm ideas for the content and delivery of new prompts—including those to capture cultural context and preferences—that we could implement in subsequent design phases.

Results

Platform design and progression

Phase 1: Conceptualizing the structure and vision of the assessment tool

We created a concept map of key aspects and components of the assessment guided by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics’ (hereon, Academy) NCP (Table 1).17 The Academy recommends six domains of a nutrition assessment: (1) Food/Nutrition-related history; (2) Anthropometric measurements; (3) Biochemical data, medical tests, and procedures; (4) Nutrition-focused physical findings; (5) Client history; (6) Assessment, monitoring, and evaluation tools.

Phase 3: Testing a high-fidelity prototype of the tool with optimized (culturally personalized) features and designing any additional features

In Phase 3, we optimized our prototype based on content and delivery needs and preferences identified in Phase 2 testing, including content that enhanced the capacity for cultural tailoring and personalization (Table 2).

Phase 4: Testing a fully functioning, programmed tool

In Phase 4, we optimized the prototype based on any additional ideas generated in Phase 3 testing and programmed the prototype as a fully functional platform for testing. Some additional content generated through Phase 3 testing and implemented in Phase 4 included a prompt about how cultural norms, traditions, and beliefs influence food and nutrition patterns, the inclusion of a problem, etiology, and signs and symptoms (PES) statement, information about referring clinicians, and a prompt asking caregivers completing the assessment on behalf of the patient whether they want nutrition-focused caregiver training to support them in preparing meals for the patient. This builds on the NCP and includes a specific set of questions about caregivers (Table 2).

The final tool included ten pages covering NCP domains: (1) Basic information [16 prompts]; (2) Health and Medical section [25 prompts]; (3) Allergies and Sensitivities [9 prompts], (4) Special Diets [2 prompts], (5) Food preferences, (6) Meal visualization, (7) Lifestyle [8 prompts], (8) 24 hour timeline, (9) Food security [5 prompts], and (10) Other concerns and PES statement.

Overall qualitative feedback

We sought qualitative feedback on (1) ease of use and (2) how likely participants would be to use it in their practice.

Ease of use

Participants consistently reported the platform as easy to use. They appreciated the simplicity of clicking through responses and found the types of responses (eg, text box or radio button) to be appropriate and relevant for the prompts shown. Several participants highlighted that the tool would be especially beneficial for co-filling assessments with patients, fostering collaborative visits.

Likelihood of using the tool in practice

Many participants noted an interest in utilizing the tool in their practice, particularly in long-term care and hybrid settings. Some participants were concerned about its length and duplication of documentation, suggesting that integration with existing EHR systems (eg, auto-population of lab results) would enhance tool integration in the practice setting. Several participants were enthusiastic that the fully programmed tool covered a wide range of relevant assessment domains and components and expressed interest in incorporating the tool into their daily practice, particularly appreciating its organized flow and potential for improving patient interactions and engagement.

Discussion

This study reports findings from our iterative development and usability testing of a novel web-based platform to support RDNs in conducting culturally tailored comprehensive nutrition assessments in healthcare settings. We used co-design approaches including interviews, surveys, and a combination of generative and evaluative usability testing to identify areas for refining the assessment to meet the needs of RDNs.

Our findings demonstrate strong usability and acceptability of the assessment tool among RDNs across diverse healthcare settings. Usability scores remained high across all testing phases, and participants consistently reported that the platform was easy to navigate and well designed. Qualitative feedback reinforced this, with participants highlighting the ease of interaction and the logical flow of assessments. The high SUS scores observed across phases suggest that the tool is suitable for use in different clinical settings. The SUS score is comparable to usability of other web-based nutrition and dietary assessment tools tested globally (eg, myfood24 in Norway, NutriDiary in Germany) suggesting its alignment with global indicators of usability for assessment tools.38,39 Notably, most assessment tools used in U.S. healthcare settings (eg, the Mini Nutritional Assessment) have been tested for validity and reliability as screeners (eg, for malnutrition), but not for usability—likely because these screeners have not necessarily been developed as part of a broader web-based tool.40 The high satisfaction rating also demonstrated acceptability of the platform and interviews reflected a high likelihood of actual use in practice. Participants viewed our tool as facilitating an assessment that cohesively integrated a variety of screening tools and information gathering systems that are often fragmented in current nutrition practice due to the lack of a comprehensive tool that captures all aspects of an individual’s nutrition-related experiences, challenges, and needs.

SPICE-Healthcare’s nutrition assessment tool is novel in three ways. First, it is uniquely comprehensive in its integration of several components that our participants recognized as both novel and necessary for improving nutrition care delivery. Tailored prompts (eg, language preference, dietary preferences or requirements for religious, ethical, or cultural reasons, and caregiver involvement), aligned closely with recommendations from the NCP, while advancing its application for more diverse populations and patient circumstances.17 Although some participants expressed concerns about the length of the assessment, future refinement of the tool will include adaptations for various settings (ie, distinguishing prompts for hospital versus outpatient settings).

Second, ours is the first web-based tool to embed cultural personalization in nutrition services at the point-of-care. There is growing evidence of the efficacy of culturally tailored nutrition education and dietary interventions in supporting diet-related health management among diverse populations.41–43 This underscores the need for culturally tailored nutrition assessments occurring at the point-of-care to ensure effective delivery of these promising interventions. Nuanced prompts (eg, evaluating cultural factors influencing dietary patterns and norms) and components (eg, the 24-hour timeline) enable RDNs to understand cultural factors that may motivate, facilitate, or limit dietary modifications in the context of individual patients’ medical history, symptoms, and goals. Cultural personalization remains a key element of personalized nutrition that has largely gone under the radar in development point-of-care interventions.44–46

Third, the use of co-design actively engaged RDNs across all phases of the tool development and testing, and our iterative approach ensured that the tool was evidence-based and also shaped by the daily workflows, preferences and cultural considerations of end users.31,47,48 The use of co-design approaches is limited in the context of nutrition services, including in technology innovation—particularly in a way that engages RDNs. Engaging with a subset of RDNs more than once enabled us to garner insights into how effectively we had interpreted and implemented feedback and suggestions. Even though our tool’s primary intended function is to enable comprehensive nutrition assessments, it is also possible that RDNs may leverage our tool as an educational resource over time. For example, by collecting information about cultural norms and influences over the course of 24 hours, RDNs may learn more about the cultural groups represented in their patient population over time, thereby sustaining a long-term learning process.

Our study has some limitations—first, the limited sample size may not fully capture the diversity of experiences across practice settings and geographic regions. Second, there may be some selection bias in the RDNs who agreed to participate in our study; since our recruitment materials specified that we aimed to test a culturally tailored web-based tool, it is possible that participants with an interest in dietary cultural inclusivity were more likely to enroll in the study. Third, we did not include a baseline measure of digital comfort or proficiency, and since a subset of RDNs conduct assessments on paper, our sample may have been skewed towards having higher digital proficiency than the general population of RDNs.49 Fourth, there is a risk of social desirability bias in usability testing, whereby participants may over-report positive aspects (or underreport negative aspects) of the service or product being tested.50 However, our session facilitators and observers reported that most participants were thoughtful, candid, and constructive in their feedback. This was especially salient among RDNs who participated in multiple phases and may have developed rapport and trust with the study team.51

There is a need to further refine SPICE-Healthcare’s nutrition assessment for adaptability across settings (ie, to distinguish prompts based on setting and population) and test its feasibility in practice settings. For example, SPICE-Healthcare could be adapted further for use by other professionals such as nurses, social workers, or speech pathologists who could leverage the tool for referrals to RDNs. The nutrition assessment is only one step of the NCP; education and counseling, including the development of a culturally relevant, personalized care plan is a necessary next step. Future research must also seek input from patients and caregivers—while they may not be direct end-users of the assessment, which is typically a clinician driven process, their insights into the experience of receiving a nutrition assessment via SPICE-Healthcare can provide important insights into feasibility and effectiveness of the assessment tool. Given our tool’s emphasis on cultural inclusivity, it is important to identify any additional prompts or types of information patients wish to provide to their RDN about their cultural context and its role in their nutrition and dietary patterns. With greater emphasis on transparency of clinical information, development of patient-facing summary pages and form completion options could streamline workflow for RDNs while also providing patients with access to comprehensive reports about their nutritional status.52 Components of the tool, such as those gathering information on patient food preferences, could be completed by patients in advance of appointments to streamline RDN workflow. Future testing with both patients and caregivers could enable identification of which components can be completed by patients in advance. In addition, further enhancements of SPICE-Healthcare could benefit from additional measures of user acceptability. Our study measured acceptability quantitatively using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire which measures satisfaction with a product, and qualitatively by gathering insights about ease of use and likelihood of using the tool in practice.35,53 However, additional measures such as voluntariness of use, or whether RDNs choose to use the tool in practice (versus being required to use it by their organization), and willingness to pay among patient and clinician users could examined in future testing.54 Even if such a tool is made accessible and available at a minimal cost, end users’ willingness to pay can provide information on the perceived value of the tool and opportunities for policymakers and organizations to incentivize its use.55 In addition to examining patient behavioral and health outcomes and RDN knowledge, self-efficacy, and quality of care outcomes associated with use of SPICE-Healthcare, future effectiveness studies could also evaluate the impact of using the tool on costs. Comparative effectiveness studies could facilitate an understanding of how use of SPICE-Healthcare compares to standard care and costs associated with diet-related health conditions that may disproportionately impact culturally diverse patients.56

Conclusion

Our development and testing of the usability and acceptability of SPICE-Healthcare suggests potential for implementation in clinical nutrition services to support culturally- and medically tailored comprehensive nutrition assessments. SPICE-Healthcare can be enhanced and leveraged in the future to support additional components of the Nutrition Care Process as well as patient- and caregiver-facing diet management and modification.
