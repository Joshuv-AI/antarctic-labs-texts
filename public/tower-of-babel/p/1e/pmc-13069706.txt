The effects of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression on online deviant behavior: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Danping Wang, Qihui Hou, Jingkai Li, Chen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined how parental psychological control influences online deviant behavior and explored the mediating roles of Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression. A total of 876 adults (mean age = 23.04 ± 8.49 years; 616 women) participated in the study. Latent profile analysis identified subgroups of individuals based on perceptions of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and emotional distress. Three distinct profiles emerged: a low negative-factor subgroup (31.8%), a medium subgroup (44.8%), and a high subgroup (23.5%). The three profiles differed significantly in levels of online deviant behavior, with higher negative factors associated with more deviant behavior. Males and only-child participants were more likely to belong to the high negative-factor subgroup compared with females and non–only-child participants. Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression jointly mediated the association between parental psychological control and online deviant behavior. In the low negative-factor subgroup, both direct and indirect effects were significant. In contrast, direct effects were non-significant in the medium and high subgroups, although indirect effects via Internet addiction and emotional distress remained significant. Conclusion Parental psychological control presents in three distinct forms, each showing different patterns of association with online deviant behavior. Internet addiction and emotional distress were differentially related to these profiles and to online deviant behavior, suggesting that they may represent important correlates linking psychologically controlling parenting to maladaptive online behaviors. These findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of heterogeneity in parental psychological control and its potential associations with adolescents’ online maladjustment.Keywords: Online deviant behavior, Parental psychological control, Internet addiction, Anxiety and depression, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

Online deviant behavior refers to actions undertaken by individuals during internet use that violate or disrupt established norms of cyber conduct [1]. Such behaviors not only impose negative consequences on the perpetrators themselves but also pose significant risks to the physical and mental well-being of others. In addition, online deviance may contribute to societal norms, particularly within the digital ecosystem.

According to DataReportal’s Digital 2022 April Global Statshot report, the number of global internet users surpassed 5 billion as of January 2021. Since 2013, the average daily time spent online among users aged 16 to 64 has steadily increased, indicating rising dependence on digital connectivity. Parallel to this surge in global internet engagement, incidents of online deviant behavior have also grown more frequent. Recent studies have shown that engaging in online deviant behaviors can transfer to offline deviance through mechanisms such as social contagion, thereby posing significant societal risks [2].

Online deviant behavior comprises a range of maladaptive online actions that may adversely affects other’s physical and mental health [3]. Its main manifestations include cyber overactivity, cyber pornography, and visual aggression [4]. Current classification systems for online deviant behavior primarily consider the level of intentionality, the scope of influence, and the modality through which the deviance occurs. Such classification frameworks provide valuable guidance for identifying, categorizing, and analyzing the causes, patterns, and potential consequences of online deviant behaviors.

Influences on online deviant behavior

The influencing factors of online deviant behavior mainly involve individual and environmental factors. While environmental aspects mostly pertain to family, peers, school, and the Internet environment, individual components include personality qualities, self-control, self-awareness, etc [5].

Parental psychological control (PPC) refers to intrusive and manipulative parenting style that pressure children to think, feel, or behave in parent-prescribed ways. Barber conceptualized PPC as a distinct form of intrusive parenting characterized by strategies such as guilt induction, love withdrawal, and authority assertion, all of which intrude upon the child’s inner world and constrain the development of autonomy [6, 7]. Importantly, Barber emphasized that these strategies do not merely vary in intensity but can appear in different configurations, forming qualitatively different psychological control styles. Building on this, Soenens and Vansteenkiste provided a theoretical upgrade by situating PPC within the framework of self-determination theory (SDT), proposing that psychological control frustrates children’s basic psychological needs—particularly autonomy—thereby heightening vulnerability to maladaptive emotional and behavioral outcomes [8]. According to SDT, when parents impose their own will and demands on their children, they constrain the child’s ability to make autonomous choices, which not only fosters a negative family climate but also erodes the quality of the parent–child relationship. Therefore, excessive psychological control may lead to difficulties in mental health and adjustment [9], undermines children’s need for autonomy [10], and exert a profound influence on their psychological development and behavioral patterns [11, 12]. Importantly, inappropriate psychological control may also elevate adolescents’ likelihood of engaging in online deviant behaviors, possibly through its impact on emotional distress and compensatory Internet use.

Combining these insights with contemporary models of technology-related behaviors, PPC may influence online deviant behavior through a cascade of emotional and behavioral processes. Although few studies have directly examined the association between parental psychological control and online deviant behavior. Existing empirical evidence suggests several plausible pathways. Excessive parental psychological control has been linked to adolescents’ aggressive behavior, Internet addiction, and other undesirable Internet usage behaviors [13]. It has also been associated with increased engagement in smoking, drinking, aggressive behaviors and criminal activities [14]. And positively predicts aggressive behavioral patterns more broadly [15]. Furthermore, parental psychological control has been found to negatively affect adolescents’ interpersonal functioning [16]. This finding may be explained by the fact that parental psychological control psychologically controlling parenting fosters hostile or aggressive interaction styles, which adolescents may generalize to their broader social relationships. These maladaptive interaction patterns may become even more salient in online environments, where anonymity and reduced social cues can amplify the expression of aggression and other deviant behaviors.

Relationship between Internet addiction and online deviant behavior

Goldberg defined Internet addiction disorder (IAD) as a type of impulse control disorder that occurs in the absence of psychoactive substances, characterized by excessive or maladaptive Internet use [17]. Individuals with Internet addiction typically exhibit a strong psychological dependence on online activities, spend increasing amounts of time on the Internet, expect emotional gratification from online engagement, and often experience negative affect such as irritability or depression when unable to go online. Excessive Internet use lead individuals to neglect interpersonal activities in real-life, such as studying, working and daily life, etc [18]. Among adolescents, Internet addiction has been shown to exert detrimental effects on physical and mental health, personality development, academic performance, interpersonal relationships, and overall family functioning [19].

Previous studies examining the link between Internet addiction and online deviant behavior have mainly focused on online game addiction and its association with aggressive behavior [20]. Lim et al. demonstrated that aggression, particularly intentional aggression directed toward others—is closely associatesd with Internet addiction [21]. Building on this, subsequent studies have shown that the Internet addiction significantly and positively predicts online aggression, and that this relationship displays notable gender differences: male tend to show higher levels of Internet addiction and are more likely to engage in online aggressive behaviors compared with females [22].

However, online deviant behavior encompasses a broader spectrum of maladaptive online activities and cannot be fully equated with aggressive behavior alone. Thus, although prior findings highlight a meaningful association between Internet addiction and online aggression, the pathways linking Internet addiction to the wider range of online deviant behaviors remain insufficiently understood. Further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms through which Internet addiction contributes to online deviance.

Relationship of anxiety and depression with online deviant behavior

Depression is typically characterized by persistent low mood, whereas anxiety reflects tension and fear arising from perceived threats or failures [23, 24]. Prior research shows that anxiety and depression play key roles in aggressive and other externalizing problem behaviors [25–28], and the two often co-occur within the same individuals [29]. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to such difficulties due to rapid developmental changes, a pattern supported by neurostructural evidence indicating immature emotion-regulation systems during this period [30–32].

Current studies have confirmed the significant correlation between Internet addiction and mental health problems (e.g., anxiety, depression, etc.) [33]. In addition, psychological control, as a negative family environmental factor, can negatively affect adolescents’ Internet use and mental health. According to the Compensatory Internet Use Model, individuals may turn to the Internet as a coping strategy to respond psychological distress [34]. Previous studies further show that parental psychological control significantly and positively predicts adolescents’ smartphone addiction through the mediation of psychological need satisfaction [35], and increases externalizing behaviors such as aggression through the inhibition of expression [7]. The I-PACE framework similarly posits that predisposing conditions (e.g., family context) promote problematic/ addictive Internet use [36]. These findings suggest that Internet addiction may serve as an important mechanism linking parental psychological control with both online deviant behavior and emotional difficulties [22].

From a self-determination perspective, persistent parental psychological control can generate chronic stress and negative affect in adolescents, motivating them to seek coping resources outside the family. Internet addiction has been conceptualized as a proximal behavioral response to maladaptive coping and impaired self-regulation in response to psychosocial stressors [34]. Adolescents who rely on the Internet excessively may gradually develop problems in regulating emotions and controlling behavior, which can increase the risk of developing anxiety and depressive symptoms. Longitudinal studies have shown that pathological Internet use predicts subsequent increases in depressive and anxiety symptoms over time [37]. In addition, Chen et al. found that Internet addiction influences depression through the mediating effect of anxiety [38]. These findings suggest that Internet addiction can serve as a behavioral pathway through which PPC heightens adolescents’ vulnerability to emotional problems, ultimately increasing the likelihood of online deviant behaviors. Based on these theoretical and empirical insights, the present study models Internet addiction as the first mediator between PPC and later emotional difficulties, aligning the mediation sequence with developmental and longitudinal evidence. Based on this, the present study conceptualizes Internet addiction as a proximal behavioral pathway through which parental psychological control elevates adolescents’ risk for anxiety and depression, thereby increasing the likelihood of online deviant behavior. Examining these sequential pathways may help clarify the mechanisms linking family context to both emotional problems and maladaptive online behaviors, and inform more targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Despite this evidence, most studies examining online deviant behavior have used variable-centered approaches, which overlook individual heterogeneity and obscure differences in risk profiles. Latent profile analysis (LPA) offers a person-centered alternative that can identify distinct configurations of parental psychological control and clarify how these subgroups relate to Internet addiction, emotional problems, and online deviance.

Guided by Self-Determination Theory [8], the Compensatory Internet Use Model [34], and the I-PACE framework [36], this study examines how parental psychological control contributes to online deviant behavior through addictive (Internet addiction) and emotional (anxiety, depression) pathways.

To capture heterogeneity in perceived parental psychological control, we adopted a person-centered latent profile analysis to identify distinct configurations of parental control and compare their associations with online deviant behavior and mental-health outcomes. Subsequently, a chained (serial) mediation model was developed, treating parental psychological control as the independent variable, Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression as mediators, and online deviant behavior as the dependent variable.

Based on prior research, we hypothesized that:

H1: Distinct parental psychological control profiles will emerge.

H1: Distinct parental psychological control profiles will emerge.

H2: Higher-risk profiles will be associated with online deviant behavior.

H2: Higher-risk profiles will be associated with online deviant behavior.

H3: Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression will jointly and sequentially mediate the relationship between parental psychological control and online deviant behavior.

H3: Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression will jointly and sequentially mediate the relationship between parental psychological control and online deviant behavior.

This integrated person- and variable-centered approach aims heterogeneous parental control patterns and psychological pathways through which family context shapes adolescents’ maladaptive online behaviors.

Research methodology

Subjects

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design, conducted between June and July 2023. A convenience cluster sampling method was utilized to select primary and secondary school students as well as college students as the subjects of the research. Data collection was carried out through online (the Brain Island Research Platform) and offline methods. All offline data collection was conducted uniformly in classrooms or school computer labs during the regular academic hours of the students’ respective schools.

Using GPower 3.1, a total sample size of at least 252 subjects was predicted to reach the 95% level of statistical power at the significance level α = 0.05 with a medium effect (f = 0.4). The subjects and protocol of this study were pre-registered (https://osf.io/5mqsb). A random sample of 876 students (M ± SD = 23.04 ± 8.49; females: 616) participants were selected online from the Brain Island Research Platform and offline.

All participants signed the informed consent form prior to their participation. After the questionnaires were completed, appropriate compensation was given to the them.

Measures

Parental psychological control scale (PPCS)

Developed by Wang et al., the original questionnaire comprises two dimensions: behavioral control and psychological control [39]. The psychological control dimension was chosen to be measured in this study. The 18 items for psychological control measure parents’ behaviors in guilt induction, love withdrawal and authority assertion. A 5-point scale was used, ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, with higher scores indicating higher levels of psychological control. The questionnaire contains no reverse-scored items. The two questionnaires have demonstrated measurement invariance in cross-cultural comparisons between Chinese and American adolescents [39]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the total scale score and the three dimensions of triggering child guilt, withdrawal of love, and entitlement were 0.926, 0.907, 0.918, and 0.845, respectively.

Internet addiction scale (IAS)

The 10-item Internet Addiction Diagnostic Questionnaire developed by Young [40] and translated by Li et al., [41] was used to assess adolescents’ Internet addiction. The questionnaire was scored on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = completely incorrect to 6 = completely correct. The higher the score, the higher the degree of internet addiction. The questionnaire contains no reverse-scored items. The Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.919 in this study.

Anxiety and depression scales

The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21) was developed by Lovibond et al. [42], The Chinese version of the DASS-21 was revised by Xu et al. [43] and contains 21 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with a score of 0 = did not apply to me at all to 3 = applied to me very much or most of the time for each of the three subscales of depression, anxiety, and stress. The higher the score, the more severe the symptoms are. The questionnaire contains no reverse-scored items. The depression and anxiety subscales were used in this study. The Cronbach’s α for the total scale, anxiety, and depression dimensions in this study were 0.915, 0.854, and 0.890, respectively.

Online deviant behavior scale

The Online Deviant Behavior questionnaire developed by Zhang [44] was used to assess individuals’ deviant behaviors across four dimensions: interaction, speech, pornography, and Internet usage. It comprises a total of 19 items. A 5-point scale ranging from 1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree was utilized. The higher the score, the more instances of online deviant behavior there are. The questionnaire contains no reverse-scored items. The Cronbach’s α for the total score and the four aspects of Internet contact deviance, Internet speech deviance, Internet pornography, and Internet use deviance were 0.914, 0.875, 0.935, 0.906, and 0.940, respectively.

Data processing

First, all continuous variables were mean-centered. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted in Mplus 8.3 using six indicators: the three dimensions of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and anxiety and depression. After identifying the optimal profile solution, the three-step approach was applied to account for classification uncertainty [45]. To reduce potential confounding, sex (1 = male, 2 = female) and only-child status (1 = yes, 2 = no) were included as covariates.

Within the three-step framework, covariates were incorporated using R3STEP to predict profile membership, while BCH was used to compare distal outcome means across profiles. Distal outcome patterns were further cross-validated using a profile-informed SEM adjusting for the same covariates. In the mediation and serial-mediation SEM models, Internet addiction, anxiety, depression, and online deviant behavior were each regressed on the covariates. Indirect effects were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals.

Potential profile analysis

Potential profiling of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and anxiety and depression

The three factors of parental psychological control (parents’ behaviors of triggering children’s guilt, withdrawal of affection, and arbitrary use of power) and Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression were used as the six exogenous indicators to establish a latent profile model. The number of latent categories of the model was then sequentially increased to conduct the model fit estimation of the latent profile analysis. AIC, BIC, aBIC, Entropy index, LMR, and BLRT were used as the indicators of the model’s fitness. Results indicate that Category 3 had a high Entropy value close to 1, and both BLRT and LMR indexes were significant, suggesting that the model for Category 3 was a better fit than the others (Table 2). Accordingly, the three-class model was selected as optimal. Figure 1 illustrates the profile patterns of the three-class solution based on this model.

Discussion

Potential profiling analysis of factors influencing online deviant behavior

In this study, the questionnaire method was conducted to assess individuals’ online deviant behaviors and the influence of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and anxiety and depression. A person-centered latent profile analysis was subsequently employed to classify participants into three subgroups based on these dimensions. Consistent with Hypothesis 1, three distinct profiles emerged. BCH analyses further revealed significant group differences in online deviant behavior, with the high negative-factor subgroup exhibiting the highest levels of deviance, followed by the medium subgroup and the low subgroup. This pattern suggests that exposure to greater psychological control, higher Internet addiction, and more severe emotional distress is associated with increasingly maladaptive behavioral outcomes [34], thereby supporting Hypothesis 2.

In addition, the results of R3STEP showed that males were significantly more likely than females to belong to both the high and medium negative-factor profiles relative to the low-profile group, consistent with prior findings [46]. Previous research indicates that boys are are more likely to engage in cyberbullying [47, 48]. From an evolutionary psychology perspective, these tendencies may be explained by the fact that aggression offers males greater adaptive value in terms of competition and status acquisition, thereby increasing their likelihood of displaying externalizing behaviors under stress. The findings of the present study also indicated that only-child status significantly predicted membership in the high and medium negative-factor subgroups. This suggests that, within the current sample, only children were more likely to report elevated levels of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, anxiety, and depression. These results are consistent with the empirical findings of Hao and Feng [49], who noted that only children may face greater challenges in certain dimensions of social adaptation, possibly due to their unique developmental context as the primary focus of family resources and expectations. Such concentrated parental attention may increase both psychological pressure and parental involvement intensity, which may in turn elevate adolescents’ vulnerability to negative emotional and behavioral outcomes. Together with gender differences, these patterns highlight how demographic and family-contextual factors may shape adolescents’ exposure to different psychological control profiles.

Beyond demographic influences, the qualitative differences among the latent profiles further reflect the distinct configurations of psychological control strategies proposed by Barber, as discussed below.

The qualitative distinctions among the three latent profiles are also consistent with Barber’s conceptualization of psychological control as comprising multiple intrusive strategies that parents deploy in different combinations [6]. In the current study, the profiles reflected precisely such configurations: some parents relied more heavily on love withdrawal, others on guilt induction, while still others showed a more evenly distributed pattern across strategies. These findings underscore the value of latent profile analysis in capturing distinct psychological control styles that would be masked by a single aggregated score. Moreover, they offer a theoretically grounded explanation for the observed subgroup differences in emotional distress, Internet addiction, and online deviant behavior.

Profile-based model of the mediating role of online deviant behavior

This study found that parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and anxiety and depression were all positively correlated with online deviant behavior. Internet addiction as well as anxiety and depression jointly mediated the effect of parental psychological control on online deviance, suggesting that psychologically controlling parenting may heighten adolescents’ emotional distress and problematic Internet use, which in turn increases the likelihood of maladaptive online conduct. The findings are consistent with Self-Determination Theory [8] and the Compensatory Internet Use Model [34], both of which suggest that intrusive parenting undermines adolescents’ basic psychological needs, prompting compensatory Internet use as a maladaptive coping strategy. This maladaptive Internet use may then elicit negative emotional responses such as anxiety and depression, ultimately contributing to problematic online behaviors. This integrated perspective helps explain how family-based psychological control contributes to maladaptive digital behavior.

First, parental psychological control positively predicts Internet addiction, consistent with the previous research results [12]. High levels of parental psychological control behaviors may be related to a long-term state of tension and stress in the children, making online engagement an avoidance-based coping strategy, as described in the “compensatory use” mechanism of Internet addiction [34, 50]. Second, Internet addiction significantly predicted online deviant behavior, consistent with Hasanova’s findings [51]. Individuals with higher levels of Internet addiction tend to disengage from real-life responsibilities and regulate emotions online, which increases the likelihood of deviant behavior. Third, Internet addiction predicted higher levels of anxiety and depression, echoing previous work by Koc [52] and others [38, 53]. Such comorbidity not only exacerbates the complexity of clinical intervention through a bidirectional mechanism, but also may cause cumulative physical and mental harm to teenagers, forming a vicious cycle of addiction and emotional imbalance, causing great distress to the physical and mental growth of teenagers. Fourth, Parental psychological control positively predicts anxiety and depression, consistent with Lin’s findings [54]. This result indicate that Intrusive control disrupts autonomy, creates a tense family climate, and increases parent–child conflict, which elevates emotional distress [55]. Fifth, anxiety and depression predicted online deviant behavior. Negative emotional states arising from stress or adverse events can trigger maladaptive behaviors [56], especially when non-adaptive coping strategies strengthen the link between stress and deviance [57]. According to Davis’s “Cognitive-Behavioral Model“ [58], irrational cognition enhance negative affect, and these mutually reinforcing processes can escalate into online deviant behaviors—a pattern supported by the present findings.

Furthermore, the path from parental psychological control to anxiety and depression was non-significant in the low negative-factor subgroup. This result is consistent with the conclusion drawn by Rogers et al., [59] from their 4-wave tracking. Researchers used latent variable growth curves and found that only when the parents’ psychological control T score was ≥ 45, the intercept factors of depression and anxiety significantly increased; the low control group (T < 45) did not show any difference in fitting with the zero growth model. That’s possibly due to the lower degree of parental psychological control in this group, and a low-intensity parental psychological control sorely is less likely to lead to anxiety and depression. Additionally, the direct effect of parental psychological control on online deviant behavior was non-significant in the medium and high negative-factor subgroups. This may be partly explained by cultural factors: in Chinese families, parental authority and filial expectations are culturally sanctioned. Chao’s concept of “training-style” parenting suggests that certain forms of control may not necessarily undermine adjustment and may even be weakly or negatively related to internalizing problems [60]. Adolescents socialized in such contexts may show stronger behavioral self-regulation, which reduces the direct impact of parental control on deviant behaviors. It is also possible that other contextual variables—such as school environment, peer relations, or social networks—moderate these associations [61]. Taken together, these findings indicate that Hypothesis 3 was partially supported.

Practical implication

Online deviant behavior refers to maladaptive online actions that deviate from social norms and pose risks to adolescents, others, and society. Such behaviors are shaped by multiple factors, including individual traits, family and school environments, and features of the online setting. Existing studies often examine isolated predictors and rely primarily on variable-centered approaches, which limit ecological validity and overlook individual heterogeneity.

By employing latent profile analysis, this study identifies subgroups of adolescents characterized by different levels of parental psychological control, Internet addiction, and emotional distress, and examines how these configurations contribute to online deviant behavior. These findings deepen understanding of the mechanisms underlying adolescents’ online maladjustment and enhance the ecological validity of research on online risk-taking. From a family education perspective, parents of adolescents in high and medium negative-factor subgroups—especially male and only-child adolescents—should prioritize reducing intrusive psychological control. At the same time, parents should monitor Internet use without imposing excessive restrictions, by setting reasonable daily online time limits, understanding adolescents’ online activities, and encouraging engagement in offline activities (e.g., sports, hobbies, family interactions) to reduce compensatory internet use.

From a school perspective, the study highlights the need for targeted interventions. Schools should implement mental health programs that promote healthy Internet habits and emotional regulation, focusing on students with co-occurring Internet addiction and emotional dysregulation. This can improve offline social skills and reduce maladaptive online behaviors.

At the policy level, educational authorities could consider incorporating family psychosocial environment assessments into routine student mental health screenings, and providing parent-child communication training resources for high-risk families to reduce the incidence of online deviance at its source.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal ordering or causality among parental psychological control, Internet addiction, emotional distress, and online deviant behavior. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify potential developmental pathways.

Second, all variables were assessed through self-report measures, which may introduce common method bias and social desirability effects. Future research could incorporate multi-informant reports (e.g., parents or teachers) and behavioral or objective indicators to enhance validity.

Third, the sample was drawn from a specific sociocultural context. In Chinese cultural settings, family dynamics are often shaped by norms emphasizing parental authority and filial piety, which may influence both parenting practices and adolescents’ behavioral responses. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings to other cultural contexts remains uncertain. Cross-cultural comparative studies are warranted.

Finally, online deviant behavior may be linked to other important developmental domains—such as physical health, academic functioning, and peer relationships—that were not examined in the present study. Including these broader adjustment indicators would provide a more comprehensive understanding of adolescents’ online maladjustment.
