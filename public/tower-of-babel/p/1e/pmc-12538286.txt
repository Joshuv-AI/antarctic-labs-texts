Effect of healthy lifestyle on renal dysfunction risk: interactions with genetic risk
Authors: Masato Takase, Naoki Nakaya, Mana Kogure, Rieko Hatanaka, Kumi Nakaya, Ippei Chiba, Sayuri Tokioka, Kotaro Nochioka, Tomohiro Nakamura, Naho Tsuchiya, Takumi Hirata, Ikumi Kanno, Akira Narita, Taku Obara, Mami Ishikuro, Hisashi Ohseto, Ippei Takahashi, Akira Uruno, Tomoko Kobayashi, Eiichi N Kodama, Yohei Hamanaka, Masatsugu Orui, Soichi Ogishima, Satoshi Nagaie, Nobuo Fuse, Junichi Sugawara, Shinichi Kuriyama, Koichi Matsuda, Yoko Izumi, Kengo Kinoshita, Gen Tamiya, Atsushi Hozawa, Masayuki Yamamoto

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhether adherence to a healthy lifestyle can mitigate genetic risk for renal dysfunction remains unclear.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 12 680 adults aged ≥20 years, free from chronic kidney disease at baseline, enrolled in the Tohoku Medical Megabank Community-based Cohort study. A healthy lifestyle score—based on normal weight, never smoking, never drinking, regular physical activity, and a low urinary sodium-to-potassium ratio—classified participants into ideal, intermediate, or poor lifestyle groups. A polygenic risk score (PRS) was constructed using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data from a previous multi-ancestry genome-wide association meta-analysis. The primary outcome was renal dysfunction, defined as eGFR <60.0 ml/min/1.73 m2.ResultsAmong the 12 680 adults, 123 participants (0.9%) developed renal dysfunction during a mean follow-up of 4.4 ± 0.8 years. Poor lifestyle was consistently associated with higher risk of renal dysfunction across most PRS categories. Participants with intermediate genetic risk had elevated risk only when also exhibiting an intermediate lifestyle. Formal testing for effect modification by PRS provided modest evidence that the association between high genetic risk and increased risk of renal dysfunction was stronger among individuals with an intermediate lifestyle. Although the addition of the PRS to the model included the C-statistic, this improvement was not statistically significant.ConclusionsMaintaining a healthy lifestyle is associated with lower risk of renal dysfunction, regardless of genetic risk. Combining PRSs with lifestyle information may enhance risk stratification, although further studies are needed to improve predictive accuracy.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, lifestyle factor, polygenic risk score

INTRODUCTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects 10% of adults globally, substantially affecting non-communicable disease morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3]; its incidence is rising, with mortality increasing from 0.9 million to 1.2 million annually between 2005 and 2017 [2], highlighting its public health significance and the imperative for prevention.

CKD arises from genetic and lifestyle factors [4, 5]. Modifiable lifestyle factors, such as maintaining a healthy weight, increasing potassium intake, reducing salt consumption, quitting smoking, and regular physical activity, play a crucial role in CKD prevention [6–10, 11]. Combining these behaviors can effectively prevent CKD incidence [12, 13]. Genetic factors also contribute, with family studies estimating CKD heritability at 35%–75% [5, 14, 15, 16]. Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified >400 estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)-related loci [17–19, 20]. A polygenic risk score (PRS) has been constructed from eGFR-related single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified via GWAS to predict new-onset CKD [21–23, 24]. This approach shows promise in risk-stratifying populations, enhancing screening, and informing diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment strategies [4].

Precision medicine research, incorporating genetic information, has investigated how PRS and lifestyle affect various diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), dementia, and depression [25–30, 31]. These studies suggest that adopting a healthy lifestyle can mitigate genetic risks and that PRS and lifestyle factors aid in categorizing disease risk [25–30, 31]. However, several unresolved issues persist. Most CKD-PRS studies focused on European populations [21, 22, 23], limiting evidence of its relevance to non-European groups, potentially exacerbating health disparities and complicating clinical application [4, 32]. Additionally, to the best of our knowledge, no study has examined whether adhering to a healthy lifestyle reduces genetic risk for impaired renal function. Whether PRS can integrate lifestyle factors to stratify renal dysfunction risk remains unclear. Elucidating the influence of genetic and lifestyle factors on renal function may aid in individual risk assessment, early detection, and personalized prevention strategies.

Although genetic and lifestyle factors are associated with renal function, it remains unclear whether lifestyle can modify the effect of genetic risk. We hypothesized that adherence to a healthy lifestyle may mitigate the impact of polygenic susceptibility on renal dysfunction. Testing this hypothesis could clarify the interplay between genetic predisposition and modifiable behaviors, and support personalized prevention strategies.

This study aimed to examine the independent and joint associations of polygenic risk and modifiable lifestyle factors with the risk of renal dysfunction >10 000 Japanese participants in the Tohoku Medical Megabank Community-based Cohort (TMM CommCohort) Study, a comprehensive general population-based prospective cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

TMM CommCohort Study participants

The TMM CommCohort Study, a prospective population-based cohort study, enrolled participants aged ≥20 years from Miyagi Prefecture, Northeastern Japan. Additional details of the study were previously described [33, 34]. Briefly, it commenced in May 2013 and recruited >50 000 participants until March 2016 using various approaches: a type 1 survey (n = 40 433 participants) was performed at specific municipal health check-up sites; an additional type 1 survey (n = 664 participants) was conducted on dates different from those of the specific health check-up sites; and a type 2 survey (n = 13 855) was performed in assessment centers [35]. Participants completed a self-reported questionnaire regarding information on lifestyle and other potentially health-related aspects and provided blood and urine samples. All participants provided written informed consent.

Between June 2017 and March 2021, we invited respondents from the baseline survey to participate in a repeat assessment center-based survey (follow-up survey). This survey, during the second period, collected basic information similar to that obtained during the baseline survey [34]. The Institutional Review Board of Tohoku Medical Megabank Organization approved this study (approval number 2022-4-047; approval date: 30 June 2022).

Initially, type 2 participants (n = 9833) with genotype and eGFR data from the baseline survey were selected to determine the best-fit PRS for eGFR (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of study participants.

Subsequently, we established a cohort comprising type 1 survey participants (n = 40 433) to examine the association between genetics, lifestyle score, and CKD incidence. We excluded participants who withdrew from the study by 13 July 2021 (n = 770), those with a history of kidney disease and proteinuria, defined as dipstick urine test score of 1+ or more (n = 2 708), those lacking cystatin C information (n = 23), those diagnosed with CKD at the baseline survey (n = 497), those with a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio of 30.0 mg/gCr or higher (n = 2 635), and those lacking genetic information genotyped using the Affymetrix Axiom Japonica Array (v.2) (n = 6 391). Furthermore, we excluded participants with missing information on cystatin C during the follow-up survey (n = 14 723) and those with a ≥6 standard deviation (SD) from the mean of each genetic principal component (n = 6). Finally, data from 12 680 participants were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Modifiable lifestyle factors

Previous studies have revealed that obesity, current and former smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, low potassium intake, and high salt intake are modifiable factors that can predict CKD incidence [6–10, 11]. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that higher dietary and urinary sodium-to-potassium (Na/K) ratios are associated with a higher risk of CKD incidence [35, 36]. Thus, five modifiable risk factors were used and scored: never smoked, never drinker, non-obese, regular physical activity, and low urinary Na/K ratio [6–10, 11, 36, 37]. Height and weight data were collected during municipal health check-ups, with obesity defined as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥25.0 kg/m2, following the Western Pacific Region criteria of the World Health Organization for the Japanese population [38]. Never-smokers were defined as participants who had smoked <100 cigarettes during their lifetime [39]. Regular physical activity was defined as engaging in at least 150 min of moderate activity per week or 75 min of vigorous activity per week [29, 40]. A low Na/K ratio was defined as below 4.0. Never drinkers were defined as participants who consumed little or no alcohol or were constitutionally incapable of alcohol consumption. The details of the measurement and definition of physical activity and Na/K ratio are shown in the Supplementary Methods. In accordance with approaches used in previous epidemiological studies, overall lifestyle was subsequently categorized as ideal (having at least four ideal lifestyle factors), intermediate (having two or three ideal lifestyle factors), or poor (having none or only one ideal lifestyle factor) [40, 41].

Other measurements

In the baseline survey, blood pressure was measured at municipal health check-up sites. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg or self-reported treatment for hypertension. Blood samples were collected at the venues of the municipal health check-up sites. Plasma glucose concentrations and glycated hemoglobin levels were measured using an enzymatic method. Diabetes was defined as plasma glucose ≥200 mg/dl or glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5% or self-reported treatment for diabetes. Education status was collected through a self-reported questionnaire and categorized into three categories: below high school, vocational school or junior college or technical college, and university or graduate school. We used the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, which was available from spot urine samples collected during municipal health check-ups. Information on medication use—including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, other antihypertensive agents, and antidiabetic medications—was obtained through a self-reported questionnaire.

Assessment of kidney function and ascertainment of renal dysfunction

The eGFR was calculated using serum cystatin C, as it is less affected by sex, age, body size and composition, and nutritional status than serum creatinine [42, 43]. Serum cystatin C levels were measured using the latex agglutination turbidimetry method. For the baseline survey, although we commissioned two companies (LSI Medience Corporation and BML, Inc.) to measure serum cystatin C, the measured values differed slightly depending on the testing company. Therefore, the serum cystatin C values measured by BML, Inc. were calibrated using the formula: calibrated cystatin C = measured cystatin C × 1.00634 + −0.05572. This calibration formula was calculated using data from 50 individuals measured by two testing companies. For the follow-up survey, serum cystatin C was measured by BML, Inc. using the latex agglutination turbidimetry method. The eGFR was calculated based on serum cystatin C, age, and sex using the Japanese equation, which has been used in many previous studies [44]. The renal dysfunction was defined as eGFR <60.0 ml/min/1.73 m2 in the following survey.

PRS derived from multi-ancestry genome-wide association meta-analysis

Genotyping and imputation are described in Supplementary Information. We constructed the PRS for eGFR based on cystatin C using publicly available GWAS summary statistics from the large-scale meta-analysis [19]. This meta-analysis was conducted by the CKDGen Consortium and UK Biobank, with a total sample size of 460,826 individuals from multiple ancestries: predominantly European, as well as 165,726 East Asians from Biobank Japan. The PRS was constructed from these multi-ethnic, predominantly European results and therefore was not ancestry-matched to the Japanese study population. Our participants were independent from the GWAS population. All SNPs on the X and Y chromosomes were removed to eliminate the possibility of non-autosomal sex effects. The PRS was calculated using PRSice-2, software specialized in PRS analysis [45]. P value thresholds of 6000 between 5.0 × 10−8 and 0.5 were tested with distance thresholds of 250, 500, and 1000 kb and linkage disequilibrium (LD) R2 thresholds of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3. Among various PRSs with different numbers of SNP, we chose the list of variants that demonstrated the best fit (determined by the variance explained using the PRS) to the eGFR at the baseline of the type 2 survey. The settings [the number of SNPs (P thresholds, distance, LD)] for the best-fit PRS for eGFR in the type 2 survey was 16,140 (P < .0147501, 500 kb, and LD R2 0.3). Using the best-fit PRS setting, we generated PRS in the type 1 survey and categorized it into three groups for the main analysis: low (lowest), intermediate (second), and high (highest tertiles).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means (SD) or medians (interquartile range) for continuous variables, and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using a t-test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical variables.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between lifestyle score and renal dysfunction incidence. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Similarly, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between PRS and renal dysfunction risk. To assess linear trends, the lifestyle score and PRS were additionally treated as continuous variables in separate models.

The PRS tertiles and lifestyle categories were then combined and classified into nine groups. The association between combined genetic and lifestyle factors and renal dysfunction risk was examined. To estimate adjusted absolute risk differences between genetic risk groups within each lifestyle category, a multivariable logistic regression model including an interaction term between genetic risk and lifestyle category was fitted. All models were adjusted for age, sex, educational attainment, use of ACE inhibitors, ARB, SGLT2 inhibitors, other antihypertensive agents, antidiabetic medications, and the first 10 genetic principal components. From this model, adjusted probability of renal dysfunction for each combination of genetic risk and lifestyle group were estimated by marginal standardization. Absolute risk differences were calculated by subtracting the adjusted probability in the low-risk group from that in the intermediate- and high-risk groups within each lifestyle category. Robust standard errors were used to construct 95% CIs based on the delta method.

The multiplicative interaction between lifestyle and genetic risk was investigated using a likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without an interaction term between PRS and lifestyle score. Moreover, additive interaction was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI). PRS and lifestyle score were categorized into three groups (low, intermediate, high for PRS; ideal, intermediate, poor for lifestyle). A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted, including main effects and their interaction terms, with the low PRS and ideal lifestyle group set as the reference category. Although RERI is conventionally calculated using relative risks, we used adjusted ORs as an approximation because the outcome was rare in our study population. The 95% CIs for RERI were estimated using the delta method.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and 95% CI were calculated to assess whether adding PRS to the statistical model, including lifestyle risk factors, increased the predictive ability for renal dysfunction incidence. AUROC was compared via the DeLong test.

Missing data were imputed using random forest imputation via the missRanger R package. Inverse probability weighting was applied in all analyses to mitigate selection bias from including only participants who completed the baseline and follow-up surveys.

Several sensitive analyses were performed to confirm the robustness of the study. First, we assessed multicollinearity between BMI and physical activity using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The VIF values were 1.08 for BMI and 1.02 for physical activity, indicating no significant multicollinearity. However, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding BMI from the lifestyle score. Second, the distribution of CKD risk factors and renal dysfunction risk vary by sex. Thus, we repeated the main analysis, stratifying by sex. Third, hypertension and diabetes are well-known risk factors for CKD, and lifestyle changes may affect renal function. Thus, we limited participants to those without hypertension, diabetes, and CVD history at the baseline survey. Finally, we examined a composite outcome defined as either an eGFR <60.0 ml/min/1.73 m2 or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30.0 mg/gCr.

Statistical significance was set at two-sided P < .05. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Participants’ characteristics

This study included 12 680 participants [mean (SD) age, 60.3 (10.8) years, 8192 women (64.6%)]. During a mean follow-up period of 4.4 (0.8) years between the baseline and follow-up assessments, 123 participants (0.9%) developed renal dysfunction. The median (interquartile range) value for eGFR at the baseline survey was 99.5 [88.3, 112.7 ml/min/1.73 m2]. Additionally, 75.6% of participants were non-obese, 54.5% met recommended physical activity levels by the World Health Organization, and 64.4% were never-smokers. In total, 4025 participants (31.7%) met at least three ideal lifestyle factors (Table 1). Despite a healthy lifestyle, individuals with higher genetic risk had lower eGFR values. Conversely, those with poor lifestyles were more likely to have hypertension, diabetes, and CVD history regardless of genetic risk. Women were more likely to have an ideal lifestyle regardless of genetic risk.

Association of lifestyle factors with renal dysfunction risk

We observed that a poor lifestyle was significantly associated with a higher risk of renal dysfunction. The multivariate ORs were 1.00 (reference), 1.27 (95%CI, 1.08–1.50), and 1.59 (95%CI, 1.28–1.98) for ideal, intermediate, and poor lifestyles, respectively (P for linear trend <.001).

Associations of PRS with renal dysfunction risk

A higher PRS was associated with a higher risk of renal dysfunction. The multivariate ORs were 1.00 (reference), 1.35 (95%CI, 1.16–1.57), and 1.11 (95%CI, 0.95–1.31) for low, intermediate, and high genetic risk, respectively (P for linear trend =.19).

Associations of combined lifestyle and genetic risk with renal dysfunction risk

Even among individuals with an ideal lifestyle, those with intermediate genetic risk had a higher risk of renal dysfunction compared to those with low genetic risk (OR 2.06, 95% CI: 1.11–3.95) (Table 2). Individuals with high genetic risk and an intermediate lifestyle had a higher risk of renal dysfunction (OR 2.36, 95%CI, 1.39–4.27). A multiplicative interaction was observed between PRS and lifestyle score for renal dysfunction (P < .001). The joint exposure of high genetic risk and intermediate lifestyle was more strongly associated with higher CKD risk than the simple sum of the two exposures alone (RERI = 1.23, 95%CI = 0.37, 2.08) (Table 2).

Similarly, across all genetic risk groups, the adjusted absolute risk of renal dysfunction in intermediate and poor lifestyle groups was negligible, compared with ideal lifestyle groups.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded BMI from the lifestyle score and reanalyzed the data. The results remained broadly similar (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Second, after stratifying by sex, the association between genetics and lifestyle factors with renal dysfunction risk remained consistent (Supplementary Tables S5–S7). Third, similar associations were observed among participants without hypertension, diabetes, and CVD history at baseline (Supplementary Tables S8 and S9). Finally, we examined a composite outcome defined as either an eGFR <60.0 ml/min/1.73 m2 or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30.0 mg/gCr. Among individuals with intermediate or low PRS, the risk of renal dysfunction increased with a less favorable lifestyle profile. Similarly, among individuals with intermediate or poor lifestyle, higher PRS was associated with increased risk of renal dysfunction (Supplementary Tables S10 and S11).

DISCUSSION

This study showed that in a Japanese sample of adults aged >20 years, individuals with a poor lifestyle consistently had a higher risk of renal dysfunction across most PRS strata. The adverse association was particularly pronounced among participants with an intermediate PRS. Because the number of CKD events was limited, the CIs were wide and the statistical power to detect effect modification was low; nevertheless, the observed positive effect modification may be noteworthy.

Recently, large-scale GWASs have identified numerous genetic loci, enabling the construction of PRS from these SNPs [17–19, 20]. Previous epidemiological studies have shown an association between PRS and CKD incidence and prevalence [21–23, 24]. However, most studies focused on the European population [21, 22, 23], limiting investigations into PRS performance for CKD across other ethnicities. This study represents the first prospective assessment of the association between PRS derived from multi-ethnic GWAS data and renal dysfunction in Japanese populations. Our findings indicate that PRS is associated with an increased risk of renal dysfunction, consistent with previous studies [21–23, 24].

Previous studies highlighted modifiable lifestyle factors such as non-obesity, non-smoking, non-drinking, higher potassium intake, lower sodium intake, and regular physical activity as preventive measures against CKD progression [6–10, 11, 36, 37]. However, whether maintaining a healthy lifestyle can mitigate renal function decline regardless of genetic risk remains unclear. Our study indicates that adhering to a healthy lifestyle is associated with reduced renal dysfunction risk across most genetic risk profiles, consistent with and surpassing previous findings [6, 12, 13]. Notably, even within the high-PRS group, participants who have an ideal lifestyle showed numerically lower absolute risk than their counterparts with a poor lifestyle, indicating that genetic predisposition does not necessarily determine disease onset and that lifestyle intervention remain a powerful and broadly applicable preventive strategy.

Overall, the findings suggest that while adherence to a healthy lifestyle may be universally beneficial regardless of one’s genetic risk, individuals with high genetic risk and intermediate lifestyle may be at increased risk of renal dysfunction. The underlying mechanism remains unclear. One possible explanation is that individuals in the intermediate lifestyle group may exhibit only one or two unhealthy behaviors, but in various combinations—such as smoking without obesity, or physical inactivity with moderate alcohol consumption. This behavioral heterogeneity may obscure consistent associations and complicate the interpretation of how genetic risk interacts with lifestyle factors. Furthermore, as PRSs are sensitive to the choice of GWAS and the methods used for score construction [46], future studies using alternative PRS approaches and independent cohorts are needed to validate and generalize these findings.

Prior studies have demonstrated that integrating PRS into models with clinical risk factors could slightly improve CKD prediction accuracy [23, 24, 47, 48]. Similarly, our study found that incorporating PRS into a model with a healthy lifestyle slightly improved renal dysfunction prediction, although not statistically significant, aligning with previous research [23, 24, 47, 48]. However, the improvement in discriminatory ability was marginal. Given the considerable cost, technical requirements, and limited accessibility of genetic testing, our findings highlight the practical importance of promoting healthy lifestyles as a primary and cost-effective preventive strategy for renal dysfunction.

Interestingly, we observed that the ROC values increased when individuals with hypertension, diabetes, or CVD at baseline were excluded. Although the exact mechanism underlying this finding is unclear, one possible explanation is that participants with existing chronic conditions may represent a more heterogeneous group in terms of clinical status, medical treatment, and disease progression. These factors could obscure the relationship between baseline predictors and future risk. In contrast, among individuals without such conditions, the contribution of genetic and lifestyle factors to risk prediction may be more prominent and consistent. Further investigation is needed to better understand why model performance improves in apparently lower-risk populations. Although genetic information may be useful for risk stratification in specific settings, lifestyle modification remains the most feasible and effective approach at the population level.

The strengths of this study include its population-based, longitudinal design and the novel finding that the adverse effect of genetic risk may be mitigated by a healthy lifestyle. However, this study has some limitations. First, although our lifestyle score was based on well-established modifiable risk factors for CKD identified in previous studies [6–10, 11, 36, 37], it has not been formally validated for renal outcomes. The categorization of the lifestyle score was adapted from approaches used in previous large-scale epidemiological studies that evaluated four lifestyle factors [40, 41]. While this method enhances comparability across studies and avoids potential overfitting by using an unweighted score, it assumes equal contributions of each lifestyle factor to renal dysfunction risk, which may not fully reflect their relative impact. Further research is warranted to explore alternative weighting schemes and to validate this classification specifically for renal outcomes. Second, although we incorporated the urinary Na/K ratio, other important dietary components such as intake of ultra-processed foods, saturated fats, and added sugars were not available in our dataset. These omissions may have limited our ability to fully capture the influence of diet on CKD risk. Future studies using more comprehensive dietary assessments are warranted to better elucidate the role of lifestyle in CKD prevention. Third, our study was limited to Japanese participants, and the frequency and impact of risk alleles and healthy lifestyle factors vary by ethnicity [33, 49]. The PRS for eGFR was constructed from summary statistics of a predominantly European, multi-ethnic GWAS meta-analysis. Although the meta-analysis included a substantial East Asian subset from Biobank Japan, differences in LD patterns, allele frequencies, and effect sizes between populations may reduce its predictive accuracy in the Japanese population. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies in other ethnic groups are warranted to validate the generalizability of our results. Fourth, PRS does not include rare variants that strongly influence renal function. Including rare variants may clarify genetics and lifestyle influences on renal dysfunction, improving risk stratification accuracy. Fifth, our use of cystatin C to assess eGFR may overestimate the impact of smoking, as smokers tend to have higher cystatin C levels independent of creatinine clearance than never-smokers [50]. Furthermore, factors such as thyroid function, corticosteroid use, and inflammation affect serum cystatin C levels [42, 43], which were not accounted for in our analysis. Finally, the number of renal dysfunction events was relatively small, especially in subgroup analyses. This may have led to unstable or imprecise estimates in certain combinations of genetic risk and lifestyle. A longer follow-up period and larger number of events would be needed to validate these subgroup findings.

CONCLUSION

In a large community-based Japanese prospective cohort study, a healthy lifestyle was associated with a decreased risk of renal dysfunction, regardless of individual genetic risk, whereas the additional risk associated with a higher PRS was modest. These findings suggest that adopting a healthier lifestyle may mitigate the adverse impact of genetic predisposition on renal dysfunction risk. Although genetic and lifestyle information can be used to stratify renal dysfunction risk, the predictive ability did not improve significantly. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine the clinical utility of PRS in risk prediction.
