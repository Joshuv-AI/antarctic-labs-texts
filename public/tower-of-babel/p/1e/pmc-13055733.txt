Discontinuation of Heart Failure Pharmacotherapy in Older Adults With Frailty
Authors: Ryo Nakamaru, Shiori Nishimura, Hiraku Kumamaru, Kyohei Daigo, Kayo M. Walsh, Shun Kohsaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGuideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) improves outcomes in heart failure irrespective of frailty status. Nevertheless, the magnitude of frailty‐associated discontinuation of GDMT remains insufficiently studied. We aimed to quantify 12‐month discontinuation rates of GDMT across frailty strata.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used a nationwide insurer‐based database. We identified patients aged ≥65 years who were hospitalized for heart failure between 2014 and 2022 and newly initiated on at least 1 GDMT at discharge, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors, β‐blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, or SGLT2is (sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors). Discontinuation was defined as a gap of 60 days without a prescription following the expected completion of the dispensed supply. The cumulative incidence of GDMT discontinuation within 12 months was estimated according to Hospital Frailty Risk Score, with death treated as a competing risk.ResultsWe analyzed 33 155 patients (mean age: 83.2 years, women: 53.0%). The initiating rates were as follows: angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors 48.3% (N=16 025), β‐blockers 54.9% (N=18 197), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists 44.1% (N=14 608), and SGLT2is 5.9% (N=1948). The Hospital Frailty Risk Score categories were as follows: low (33.2%), intermediate (47.1%), and severe (19.7%). Discontinuation rates increased with frailty severity: angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin II receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors 27.5%, 39.0%, 45.5% (for low, moderate, and high Hospital Frailty Risk Score, respectively, P<0.001); β‐blockers 22.0%, 28.4%, 33.6%, respectively (P<0.001); and SGLT2is, 32.3%, 34.7%, 40.2%, respectively, (P=0.008), but not for mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists 42.2%, 42.5%, and 42.3%, respectively, (P=0.22).ConclusionsIn older adults with heart failure, approximately one‐third discontinued GDMT within 12 months, and discontinuation appeared disproportionately common among those with frailty.

Heart failure (HF) is a growing public health concern that predominantly affects older adults, 1 and represents a global pandemic affecting >64 million individuals worldwide. 2 , 3 , 4 Advances over the past decades, particularly the development of highly effective guideline‐directed medical therapies (GDMTs), have significantly improved mortality and quality of life, as demonstrated in large‐scale randomized clinical trials. 5 , 6 Importantly, these benefits depend on the continued use of these agents. The unwarranted cessation of guideline‐directed agents can precipitate rapid decompensation and worsen prognosis. 7

Despite clinical guidelines recommending the ongoing use of GDMT unless contraindicated, 8 , 9 , 10 discontinuation remains common, particularly in older adults. 11 However, chronological age alone may not adequately capture the heterogeneity within older adults. 12 In particular, frailty, a multidimensional syndrome characterized by decreased physiologic reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, 13 affects an estimated 40% to 50% of older adults with HF. 14 , 15 , 16 Frailty, often accompanied by adverse drug reactions, complex polypharmacy, and functional or logistical barriers to adherence, 10 may be overemphasized in clinical decision‐making. Although such concerns may lead to discontinuation of GDMT, post hoc analyses of landmark randomized trials have consistently shown that the therapeutic benefits of GDMT are preserved across frailty strata. Nonetheless, the influence of frailty on persistence with GDMT has not been fully characterized.

Accordingly, we sought to clarify the relationship between frailty and persistence with GDMT in a nationwide cohort of older adults hospitalized for HF. We hypothesized that frailty, independent of chronological age, serves as a significant barrier to the sustained use of GDMT, and discontinuation occurs disproportionately in these patients who stand to benefit substantially from continued therapy. Specifically, we quantified 12‐month discontinuation rates for individual drug classes across strata of frailty severity.

METHODS

Based on the confidentiality agreement related to the data, we are unable to make the analytical data accessible to readers.

Data Sources

The insurance system in Japan operates as a universal, mandatory framework that guarantees equitable, affordable, and comprehensive health care access to all residents, regardless of their income level, employment status, or social background. Established in 1961, it is characterized by a combination of public and private insurance schemes, primarily divided into Employee Health Insurance, which covers employed working‐age individuals, and National Health Insurance and Advanced Elderly Medical Service System, which cover the self‐employed and older population. 17

This study used claims data provided by DeSC Healthcare (Tokyo, Japan). The DeSC database contains anonymized information from approximately 12.5 million insurance subscribers, based on data provided by Employee Health Insurance, National Health Insurance, and Advanced Elderly Medical Service System. Claims data include medical and dental diagnoses recorded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), along with corresponding Japanese claims codes. Additionally, the database contains information on prescribed medications, which can be mapped to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system. The number of days prescribed for each medication is also documented. Comprehensive details about this health insurance claims data set are provided elsewhere. 18

This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, it was conducted in conformity with the ethics guidelines of the University of Tokyo (approval number: 2023181NIe). The requirement for informed consent was waived because all data included in this data set were anonymized.

Study Population

In this study, we included patients aged ≥65 years who were hospitalized for HF between April 2014 and October 2022 and were newly prescribed at least 1 of the following GDMTs at discharge: angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), β‐blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), or SGLT2is (sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors) (see Table S1 for full ATC code list). Participants were identified based on ICD‐10 codes related to HF, including I500, I501, I509, and other HF‐related diagnoses (Table S2 for full code list). The date of the new prescription for any GDMT at hospital discharge was defined as the index date. We then excluded the following participants: (1) those who had not been continuously enrolled in the insurance database for at least 12 months before the index date, (2) those without a 1‐year washout period, (3) those who were dispensed GDMT during the 365‐day period before the index admission, and (4) those for whom mortality data were unavailable (Figure 1). We obtained the demographic characteristics of each patient from the insurance subscriber list, and baseline comorbidities were identified using diagnostic codes (Table S2) and dispensation claims during the 12 months before the index date.

Frailty Measurements

We assessed frailty using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), 19 a method for frailty risk stratification using ICD‐10 diagnosis codes obtained from administrative claims data in hospital settings. Given the absence of specific diagnostic categories, such as fall‐related codes, in the Japanese diagnosis coding system, we calculated a modified HFRS using 92 ICD‐10 codes and dispensation records from the 12 months preceding the index date (Table S3). We previously evaluated this modified algorithm using Japanese administrative claims data, demonstrating acceptable discriminatory power for all‐cause mortality. 20 Eligible participants were then categorized into 3 subgroups according to their HFRS: low risk (HFRS <5), intermediate risk (HFRS 5–15), and high risk (HFRS >15) based on the original study. 19

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were described separately for each GDMT class, including ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs, β‐blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2is. For each medication group, patient characteristics at the time of drug initiation (index date) were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median with interquartile range, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. To assess potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between the final analysis cohort and patients excluded solely due to missing mortality data.

The primary outcome was drug discontinuation within 12 months after initiation, defined as a ≥60‐day gap in prescription refills. Discontinuation was defined as a nonprescription period of 60 days following the dispensed days of each medication. The follow‐up period was defined as the time from the index date to the date of death. Patients were censored at death, disenrollment, or at the end of the 12‐month follow‐up period, whichever occurred first. To account for the potential influence of death as a competing event, we estimated the cumulative incidence of GDMT discontinuation within 12 months for each GDMT class, treating death as a competing risk. Group differences in cumulative incidence across frailty strata were assessed using the Gray test. The Fine‐Gray subdistribution hazard model was used to explore the associations between frailty and discontinuation of each GDMT. Because patients were frequently prescribed multiple GDMTs, the analysis cohorts were not mutually exclusive; separate independent models were constructed for each drug class. Thus, a single patient was included in the specific analysis for each drug they were prescribed at baseline. The models were adjusted for the following variables: age, sex, frailty severity (ie, HFRS risk strata), comorbidities (including atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy, chronic coronary syndrome, a history of myocardial infarction, and peripheral artery disease), and medications (including antithrombotic agents, loop diuretics, antiarrhythmic drugs, and other GDMT classes). Potential multicollinearity among these covariates was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (Table S4), which indicated no significant multicollinearity across the models. Additionally, the proportional hazards assumption for frailty categories was assessed by visual inspection of the cumulative incidence function curves, confirming no major violations such as crossing curves. To further characterize discontinuation patterns, specifically for SGLT2is given their relatively recent approval, we performed a temporal analysis stratifying cumulative incidence according to fiscal year of initiation (2020, 2021, and 2022).

Finally, we performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings. First, to evaluate the robustness under a stricter criterion, we repeated the analyses for GDMT discontinuation using a definition of a 30‐day prescription gap. Second, to further investigate potential selection bias and confounding for MRAs, we constructed an extended model to account for renal and electrolyte risks; in this model, the general chronic kidney disease covariate was replaced with chronic kidney disease (stage ≥3, defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2), and history of hyperkalemia was added as an independent variable.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Overall, this study included 33 155 older adults (mean age: 83.2 years; 53.0% women) who were admitted for HF and newly initiated on ≥1 of the GDMTs at discharge (Figure 1). To ensure we examined a representative cohort of patients, we analyzed differences in baseline characteristics between included patients and those excluded due to missing mortality data (Table S5). Although the mean HFRS was similar between the groups (standardized mean difference <0.1), the excluded patients were younger (standardized mean difference =0.33).

The participants were categorized by HFRS as low (33.2%), intermediate (47.1%), or severe (19.7%). The most prevalent comorbidity was hypertension (82.1%), followed by diabetes (43.6%), dyslipidemia (36.4%), and atrial fibrillation (34.2%) (Table S5). The proportions of dementia and history of fracture were 26.2% and 18.2%, respectively. The cumulative incidence of all‐cause death at 12 months increased with frailty severity, ranging from 8.1% in patients with low HFRS to 18.4% and 31.1% in those with intermediate and high HFRS, respectively.

The percentage of patients who initiated each GDMT was as follows: ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs 48.3% (N=16 025), β‐blockers 54.9% (N=18 197), MRAs 44.1% (N=14 608), and SGLT2is 5.9% (N=1948) (Table 1). Among patients initiating any GDMT, MRA recipients were the oldest, were more likely to be women, and had the highest mean HFRS. Conversely, SGLT2i recipients were the youngest, predominantly men, and had the lowest mean HFRS. The SGLT2i group was also characterized by a markedly higher prevalence of diabetes but a lower prevalence of dementia compared with other GDMT recipients (Table 1).

GDMT Discontinuation

Among patients who initiated ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs, 35.1% (95% CI, 34.3%–35.9%) discontinued treatment by 12 months after discharge. For those initiating β‐blockers, MRAs, and SGLT2is, the discontinuation rates within the same period were 26.6% (25.9%–27.2%), 42.4% (41.5%–43.2%), and 34.3% (31.8%–36.8%), respectively.

The proportion of patients who discontinued therapy increased with frailty severity: ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs (27.5%, 39.0%, 45.5% for low, moderate, high HFRS, respectively; Gray test P<0.001; Figure 2); β‐blockers (22.0%, 28.4%, 33.6%, respectively; P<0.001; Figure 3); and SGLT2is (32.3%, 34.7%, 40.2%, respectively; P=0.008; Figure 4), but not for MRAs (42.2%, 42.5%, 42.3%, respectively; P=0.22; Figure 5). Over half of all discontinuations occurred within the first 3 months across GDMT classes and frailty strata (Table S6).

In regard to SGLT2is, a temporal analysis stratified according to initiation year showed a decline in overall discontinuation rates from 2020 to 2022 (Gray test P<0.001; Figure S1). When stratified according to frailty within each year, no clear trend in discontinuation relative to frailty was observed in 2020 or 2021. In 2022, patients with high frailty risk exhibited numerically higher discontinuation rates compared with those with low or intermediate risk (Figure S2).

Factors Associated With GDMT Discontinuation

For patients initiating ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs and β‐blockers, greater frailty was associated with a higher risk of discontinuation. This was observed for ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs (intermediate versus low: subdistributional hazard ratio (sHR), 1.34 [95% CI, 1.25–1.43]; high versus low: sHR, 1.55 [95% CI, 1.42–1.70]) and for β‐blockers (intermediate versus low: sHR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.15–1.33]; high versus low: sHR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.31–1.60]; Tables 2 and 3). In addition, for SGLT2i initiators, there was a trend toward a higher discontinuation risk in patients with greater frailty (intermediate versus low: sHR, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.90–1.34]; high versus low: sHR, 1.33 [95% CI, 0.98–1.80]; Table 4). Conversely, no meaningful association was found between frailty and the discontinuation of MRAs (Table 5). In addition, details on other clinical characteristics associated with discontinuation, including age, comorbidities, and concomitant medications, are presented in Tables 2 through 5.

Sensitivity Analyses

In a sensitivity analysis defining discontinuation as a 30‐day prescription gap, absolute discontinuation rates were higher across all classes. The association between frailty severity and discontinuation was maintained for ACEis/ARBs/ARNIs (33.8%, 47.7%, 55.6% for low, moderate, high HFRS, respectively; Gray test P<0.001; Figure S3), β‐blockers (29.8%, 40.4%, 48.6%, respectively; P<0.001; Figure S4), and SGLT2is (37.7%, 45.0%, 56.4%, respectively; P<0.001; Figure S5). For MRAs, the 30‐day definition revealed a difference in discontinuation rates across frailty categories (48.8%%, 52.3%, 53.0%, respectively; P<0.001; Figure S6); however, the magnitude of the difference across frailty categories remained modest compared with other drug classes.

Finally, among MRA initiators, the prevalence of chronic kidney disease (stage ≥3) and history of hyperkalemia increased with frailty severity (Table S7). Furthermore, in an extended multivariable model explicitly adjusting for these factors, the association between frailty and MRA discontinuation remained close to the null, with results consistent with little influence of baseline renal and electrolyte‐related risks on the observed association. DISCUSSION

In this nationwide claims‐based cohort conducted within Japan's universal health care system, we provide a comprehensive description of real‐world prescribing patterns and subsequent discontinuation of GDMT for HF, including the contemporary class of SGLT2is. Almost one‐third to one‐half of older adults discontinued at least 1 newly initiated drug within 12 months, with more than half of all discontinuations occurring during the first 90 days after discharge. The likelihood of discontinuation increased progressively with greater frailty severity across all GDMT classes except for MRAs. These findings suggest that frailty continues to strongly influence treatment persistence, although it is not considered an independent contraindication to GDMT in contemporary clinical practice. 8 , 9 , 10 , 21

Although previous research has identified considerable gaps in GDMT persistence among older adults, particularly in patients with comorbidities such as low blood pressure, anemia, dementia, or chronic kidney disease, 22 , 23 these studies primarily focused on clinical characteristics without accounting for patients' overall physiological vulnerability. Our study demonstrated that frailty, independent of chronological age, was associated with GDMT discontinuation across GDMT classes. The clinical rationale for discontinuing the GDMTs in the context of frailty is, however, uncertain. Post hoc analyses of pivotal trials have revealed that the cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2is are not only preserved but also are associated with the greatest absolute risk reduction in patients with advanced frailty. 24 , 25 This consistency of benefit across the spectrum of frailty has also been reported for other GDMTs, 14 , 26 indicating that there is no evidence to support discontinuing or withholding the life‐saving medications based on frailty alone. 9

Despite this evidence, discontinuation remains common in real‐world practice. Although administrative data cannot discern specific reasons, this clinical paradox likely involves 2 distinct mechanisms. First, in regard to clinician‐driven factors, physicians may overemphasize frailty, leading them to consider that rigid adherence to GDMTs in this population could be detrimental. To address this issue, future clinical trials should deliberately include older adults with varying degrees of frailty and evaluate outcomes beyond traditional end points, such as functional capacity, quality of life, and treatment burden. In the interim, individualized assessment tools, such as the Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment, should be routinely used in clinical practice to guide nuanced decision‐making. 27 , 28 Second, in regard to patient‐driven factors, frailty is intrinsically linked to functional and cognitive decline. Qualitative studies indicate that the treatment burden of polypharmacy is a major barrier for older adults. 29 Physical limitations and cognitive impairment may lead to unintentional nonadherence.

Distinct from other GDMTs, MRAs demonstrated consistently high discontinuation rates across the entire spectrum of frailty. This may be partially explained by the higher underlying baseline risk of the MRA‐recipient cohort. However, a more critical barrier for MRAs may lie not in treatment persistence, but in their initial underuse. The well‐documented underprescription of MRAs 22 , 23 is largely driven by clinician concerns over hyperkalemia, renal impairment, and hypotension. 30 Consequently, the confluence of cautious initiation and frequent discontinuation despite their well‐established therapeutic benefits suggests a complex interplay between patient vulnerability and physician hesitancy, underscoring the urgent need to clarify the risk–benefit profile of MRAs in this population.

We observed that GDMT discontinuation frequently occurred during the early postdischarge period, with over half of all discontinuations occurring within the first 3 months across all GDMT classes and frailty strata. These findings highlight heightened vulnerability during care transitions, underscoring the need to integrate proactive frailty screening into discharge planning and outpatient HF management. 21 , 31 Additionally, older adults with frailty often face practical barriers to adherence, including low health literacy, cognitive impairment, and insufficient social support, each independently associated with poor self‐care and worse clinical outcomes. 8 , 32 To address these complex challenges, care strategies must evolve beyond standard management. Integrated cardio‐geriatric care models, involving close collaboration between HF specialists and geriatricians, are warranted to optimize physical function and simplify medication regimens. 33 Furthermore, evidence suggests that transitional care interventions, such as structured telemonitoring programs and nurse‐led home visits, can effectively bridge the gap between hospital and home, enhancing self‐care confidence and adherence. 34 Incorporating these personalized and multidisciplinary strategies into outpatient care pathways is essential to reduce discontinuation rates and improve prognosis among older adults with HF and frailty. Tailored interventions such as nurse‐led home visits coupled with telephone follow‐ups may enhance self‐care confidence and medication adherence. 34 Incorporating these personalized strategies into outpatient care pathways may reduce discontinuation rates and improve prognosis among frail patients with HF. With the aging and increasing clinical complexity of the HF population, comprehensive approaches are essential to bridge the gap between guideline recommendations and real‐world clinical practice for this vulnerable population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis used administrative claims data, which generally lacks detailed clinical information such as laboratory values and echocardiographic parameters. Specifically, we could not determine the left ventricular ejection fraction, blood pressure, renal function trajectories, and patient preferences. Furthermore, we could not differentiate between newly diagnosed HF and acute decompensation of chronic HF, nor could we ascertain the precise cause of cardiomyopathy beyond recorded comorbidities. However, the observed prevalence of previous myocardial infarction (16.3%) is consistent with Japanese HF registries, 16 suggesting that our cohort likely comprises a higher proportion of nonischemic causes compared with Western populations. 22 Moreover, the underlying causes of GDMT discontinuation were unavailable; consequently, we could not differentiate between physician‐directed deprescribing, patient nonadherence, adverse events, or other patient‐centered reasons. Similarly, due to the inherent nature of claims data, actual medication adherence could not be precisely determined, because prescription records might not reflect true patient adherence. Second, frailty was assessed using the HFRS, a validated claims‐based measure predominantly reflecting hospital‐based diagnoses. Although the HFRS reflects the accumulation of physiological deficits and correlates with physical performance measures central to the Fried Frailty Phenotype (eg, gait speed, grip strength) 35 and the Clinical Frailty Scale, 36 , 37 it is not a direct measure of functional performance. Consequently, this approach may inadequately capture key domains such as gait speed, grip strength, and social frailty. Furthermore, although HFRS includes diagnoses of dementia, it may lack sensitivity for cognitive frailty defined as the co‐occurrence of physical frailty and nondementia cognitive impairment. These unmeasured factors could directly drive GDMT discontinuation; for instance, cognitive impairment may lead to unintentional nonadherence due to forgetfulness, whereas reduced physical mobility could create logistical barriers to visiting pharmacies. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as describing the association between claims‐based frailty risk and GDMT discontinuation, and residual confounding by unmeasured functional or social markers likely remains. Third, the number of patients initiating ARNIs was limited in our cohort, particularly among those with severe frailty. Specifically, ARNIs were approved in Japan (June 2020) substantially later than in Western countries, whereas SGLT2is were approved for HF in late 2020. Consequently, the observation window for both agents was limited within our study period. For ARNIs, given the lower proportion of patients with high HFRS scores in the ARNI cohort, these patients may have represented a selectively chosen subgroup compared with those treated with other GDMT classes. This potential selection bias could partly explain the lower discontinuation rates observed for ARNIs in our study. For SGLT2is, the limited sample size likely resulted in insufficient statistical power. Although we observed a trend toward higher discontinuation in patients with severe frailty, the confidence interval crossed the null value, suggesting a potential type II error. Therefore, our findings on these newer agents should be interpreted as preliminary, and future research is warranted to verify these patterns as their use becomes more widespread among patients with varying degrees of frailty. Fourth, unmeasured confounders and potential selection bias should be acknowledged. Our claims data did not capture several important determinants of medication persistence, including caregiver support or detailed information on out‐of‐pocket medication costs. These factors are likely to be intertwined with both frailty and the risk of GDMT discontinuation. 38 , 39 Because these constructs were not measurable in our data set, residual confounding in either direction cannot be excluded, and the observed associations between frailty and GDMT discontinuation should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, in regard to the exclusion of patients with unavailable mortality data, our comparison revealed that the excluded group was younger compared with the analytic cohort, although the frailty burden was similar. This age discrepancy indicates a potential selection bias driven by data availability. Consequently, our findings may be less generalizable and should be interpreted with this limitation. Finally, the generalizability of our findings should be interpreted within the context of the Japanese health care system. Japan uses a universal health insurance system with relatively low out‐of‐pocket costs for older adults, which minimizes financial barriers to medication access. 17 In health care settings with higher financial burden, 40 discontinuation rates might be even higher due to cost‐related nonadherence. 41 Nevertheless, the fundamental association between frailty and discontinuation observed in our study likely reflects universal challenges related to physical and cognitive vulnerability, suggesting that our findings remain relevant to aging HF populations globally, regardless of the specific health care infrastructure.

CONCLUSIONS

We found that approximately one‐third of patients discontinued GDMT within 12 months, with higher discontinuation rates observed among those with greater frailty severity. Frailty should be recognized not only as a prognostic marker but also as a key factor influencing the feasibility of sustained pharmacologic treatment. Strategies that align HF care with the unique vulnerabilities of older adults with frailty, through personalized and multidisciplinary care models, may help bridge the gap in real‐world clinical practice.
