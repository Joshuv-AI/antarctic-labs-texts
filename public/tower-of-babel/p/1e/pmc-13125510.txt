Machine learning–driven design of engineered cilia enables hybrid operations in acoustic microrobots
Authors: Yun Ling, Yujing Lu, Joseph Rich, Mingyuan Liu, Xianchen Xu, Ty Naquin, Ying Chen, Shanglin Li, Ruoyu Zhong, Kaichun Yang, Shuaiguo Zhao, Qian Wu, Ke Jin, Tony Jun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microrobotic systems offer significant potential for precision medicine by enabling minimally invasive interventions in complex fluidic environments. However, effective operation in these settings requires actuators capable of more than simple linear or rotational motion, often necessitating programmable changes in both direction and shape. This remains a major challenge due to fundamental constraints in the design and control of microscale actuators, particularly in acoustic systems. Here, we introduce engineered cilia for hybrid operations microrobots, a class of acoustic microrobots that use geometry-tuned cilia and resonance-induced forces to execute complex motions such as bidirectional bending, controllable rotation, and adaptive morphing. The microrobots design is driven by a self-augmenting machine learning framework integrated with finite element analysis, enabling rapid prediction and optimization of geometry-resonance relationships across design space. This approach achieves >10⁵-fold reduction in prediction time and over 20-fold in memory savings, while maintaining >90% accuracy in peak amplitude and >98% in resonance frequency. Compliant mechanism strategies further expand the mechanical versatility of the microrobots, enabling programmable shape transformations tailored to specific tasks. These advances establish acoustic-driven microrobots as a scalable and efficient platform for intelligent microrobotic actuation in biomedical and microfluidic applications.

Introduction

Microrobotics has made progress in recent years, enabling diverse applications across multiple disciplines1–4. Examples range from biomedical uses such as targeted therapy and minimally invasive procedures5–7 to environmental remediation8–11, microassembly12,13, and microscale inspection14,15. In the biomedical field, microrobotic systems enable highly precise interventions at scales where conventional surgical techniques cannot reach, thereby minimizing physiological disruption and improving therapeutic outcomes4,7,16–20. Although recent progress has greatly advanced microrobotic technologies, achieving consistent and precise control in dynamic environments continue to be a primary hurdle to real-world translation. Researchers have explored a broad spectrum of actuation mechanisms, each developed to overcome specific physical, chemical, or biological constraints3–5,21. Photothermal actuation, for example, allows fine and localized manipulation through laser heating of asymmetric Janus microstructures. In practice, however, its utility is limited by shallow optical penetration, which makes operation in deep tissue difficult7,22,23. Magnetic actuation allows for remote, wireless control and has been widely adopted, but it requires strong magnetic fields, and spatial precision deteriorates with increasing distance due to magnetic field decay24–28. Chemical actuation provides self-sustained mobility through catalytic reactions or electrokinetic propulsion, but it depends on highly specific biochemical conditions, limiting adaptability across biological environments29,30. Biohybrid microrobots offer biocompatibility and autonomous motion by integrating living cells with synthetic materials, yet their reliance on complex biological processes makes precise and responsive control more difficult in complex environments31,32.

Acoustic actuation, which utilizes sound-induced vibrations and streaming to generate controlled forces, has enabled precise manipulation of microrobots in confined fluidic environments33–48. Current acoustic actuation methods include bubble-based actuation and non-bubble-based microstructure oscillations. Bubble-based acoustic actuation exploits the primary and secondary Bjerknes acoustic forces to achieve controlled fluidic transport and object manipulation33–35,49–53. Non-bubble-based acoustic actuation primarily utilizes cilia-inspired microstructures, where high-frequency acoustic fields induce asymmetric vibratory motion, driving directed locomotion54–57. However, existing acoustic microrobots are constrained by simple geometry and limited control, restricting them to linear or rotational motion and preventing the complex shape transformations that would significantly enhance their functional adaptability in biomedical settings35,49–51. Recent works, such as SonoTransformers, have enabled acoustically actuated shape transformations using resonance-tuned soft-hinge joints40. This design, however, relies on closed-loop connections between rigid and soft segments, which limits certain key deformations such as asymmetric folding or reconfigurable shapes.

In recent years, researchers have begun to incorporate machine learning into acoustic microrobotics, particularly in control-oriented tasks such as navigation and swarm coordination. Examples include reinforcement learning strategies for ultrasound-guided microswimmers and data-driven approaches for modeling the collective behavior of microrobotic swarms20,58,59. Although recent studies highlight the growing role of machine learning in acoustic microsystems, most efforts have focused on swimming control, with relatively little attention to AI-assisted structural design or fine-grained deformation control. Designing acoustic microrobots to meet prescribed resonance and deformation targets typically relies on extensive finite element simulations with iterative experimental testing. This slow and costly workflow limits systematic exploration of high-dimensional design spaces and makes nonlinear geometric effects hard to resolve. Cilia-based structures are a natural fit: they are simple to fabricate, inherently liquid compatible, and their vibrating tips generate strong acoustic streaming for propulsion and surface self-cleaning, providing a large design space for exploration across geometry, acoustic parameters, and collective behavior. Combining AI-driven design with cilia-based actuation leverages these advantages to accelerate development and expand the functional versatility of microrobotic systems in biomedical applications60.

Here, we present Engineered Cilia for Hybrid Operations microrobots (ECHObots), a class of acoustic microrobots designed to address the limited actuation versatility of current microscale systems. ECHObots utilize geometry-tuned cilia that harness resonance-induced forces to achieve diverse deformation behaviors, including directional bending, controlled rotation, and morphing of complex geometries in a single design. They can perform diverse actions such as bending, rotation, opening, and closing. The design process is guided by a self-augmenting machine-learning framework integrated with finite element analysis, which predicts and optimizes resonance across the geometric design space, significantly accelerating the iterative design process61–65. Compared with finite element analysis alone, this approach reduces computation time by more than 105-fold and memory usage by over 20-fold, while maintaining high predictive accuracy (98% for frequency and 90% for amplitude). To further enhance functional adaptability, we incorporate compliant mechanism strategies that support diverse and reversible morphologies, such as soft robotic arms opening, closing, and multi-segmented soft actuation66,67. With these capabilities, ECHObots are no longer limited to straight-line or rotational motion, instead providing a programmable and scalable actuation platform tailored to a wide range of tasks. This versatility positions them for potential use not only in biomedical contexts but also in microscale manufacturing and environmental monitoring.

Results

Machine learning accelerated computing enables controlled shape deformation

The ECHObots utilize acoustic induced deformation to achieve dynamically tunable and responsive soft robotic motion. Figure 1a presents an optical image of the device positioned next to a coin to highlight its minimal physical dimensions, measuring less than 1 mm and nearly invisible to the naked eye. Despite their microscopic scale, ECHObots can be driven by acoustic fields, allowing deformation to be tuned and actuation to be controlled. Reversible deformation is a key metric for advanced soft robotics24,25,53,68. As shown in Fig. 1e, we evaluated ECHObots under repeated actuation. The devices sustained more than 200 deformation cycles with consistent recovery, indicating high controllability with strong reversibility and repeatability. The soft robotic structure was fabricated using UV-photopolymerized polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA), as described in Supplementary Fig. 2. PEGDA exhibits great mechanical stability in aqueous environments. The Young’s modulus of ECHObots can be tuned by adjusting the UV exposure time and PEGDA700/PEGDA250 ratio (Supplementary Fig. 3). Longer UV exposure or higher fraction of short-chain PEGDA increases the modulus, but excessive exposure leads to light scattering and trapezoidal profiles in height direction (along the z-axis). Furthermore, excessive PEGDA250 results in immiscible suspensions (Supplementary Fig. 4). Since a higher modulus hinders deformation and a lower modulus reduces restoring force, we selected an optimized composition and exposure time that balance deformability with structural recovery. Due to PEGDA’s elastic deformation properties, the robotic arm bends under an acoustic field and generates an equal restoring force upon field removal. In most cases, the micro-robot rapidly returns to its original shape; for instance, the flexible robotic arm bends up to 60° and fully recovers. In rare cases, viscous drag from the surrounding fluid or minor plastic deformation results in incomplete or slower recovery.

With material flexibility and stability defined, we further tailor the ECHObots geometry to achieve resonance-tuned, controllable deformation. Conventional microrobotic optimization typically combines FEA with empirical parameter tweaking, which constrains controllability and prevents systematic tuning of geometric parameters. This issue is especially acute for ECHObots, where cilia geometry governs the resonant frequencies and the peak amplitudes. Running FEA across the full geometric design space is computationally expensive, so comprehensive structural optimization is not practical. To overcome this, we employed a self-augmenting machine learning framework to accelerate the computational process. The methodology and workflow are illustrated in Fig. 1f. We first fixed external factors such as solution parameters and material modulus, focusing solely on optimizing the geometric parameters: aspect ratio and cilia length. Our workflow starts with a sparse sweep of FEA runs over a gently varying parameter grid. For each run, we extract the peak frequencies and amplitudes through signal processing, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 5. Using this curated set, a Random Forest model learns the underlying trends from geometry changes to the resonance features and predicts the responses in unsampled regions of the parameter space. As shown in Fig. 1g, memory per prediction dropped from about 1.2 GB for FEA to about 50 MB for the Random Forest, a reduction of more than twenty-fold measured across ten independent geometric configurations. This smaller footprint supports deployment on portable devices and on platforms with limited computational resources.

Self-augmenting machine learning predicts resonance peak efficiently and accurately

To support the accurate prediction and optimization of geometric-actuation relationships, we now elaborate on the design and implementation of the self-augmenting machine learning method. Unlike linear models or kernel-based approaches like Gaussian Process Regression, Random Forest does not assume any predefined functional kernel between input and output and can effectively capture both gradual trends and sharp local variations. By constructing an ensemble of decision trees and partitioning the input space through data-driven splits, it models complex nonlinear resonance behaviors with high fidelity61.

To ensure an accurate ground truth dataset, resonance peak features are extracted from FEA simulations, as shown in Fig. 2a. Starting from a specific geometry, we measure the resonance spectrum and use FEA insets to show how the stress is distributed and what the cilia look like at selected modes. When we sweep aspect ratio and cilia length, the peak frequencies and amplitudes do not change linearly; instead, both quantities change in nonlinear ways. This is illustrated in Fig. 2b, where varying the aspect ratio at a fixed length markedly shifts the resonance peaks. A complementary sweep, in which the aspect ratio is held constant while the length is varied, is shown in Supplementary Fig. 6. Taken together, the two sweeps demonstrate that each parameter modulates the peak response nonlinearly. Other geometric parameters, such as cilium-attachment positions, different supporting framework lengths, and structure heights, also affect resonance peaks. Here we focus on aspect ratio and length of the cilia, because FEA shows the attachment position has only a secondary effect on peak amplitude and frequency (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Different supporting frameworks lengths can also shift the resonance frequencies (Supplementary Fig. 7b). In practice, however, the framework is dictated by application needs, for example the target arm length and the intended bending direction are usually treated as fixed rather than modified. The structural height of ECHObots is constrained by the microchannel depth. At low heights, the cross-section of ECHObots remains uniform, whereas deeper channels cause light attenuation during UV curing, producing a tapered profile with a wider base and narrower top that complicates characterization and modeling69. However, a very small height makes the rods overly compliant, increasing their susceptibility to random bending or fracture. With these trade-offs in mind, we kept the height at 50 μm across all experiments and simulations. Resonance is also sensitive to the material modulus (Supplementary Fig. 8b). When selecting the materials modulus, we balanced reversibility of deformation and fabrication complexity. Reversibility of deformation requires a sufficiently high modulus, as overly soft structures fail to recover under viscous drag, while excessively stiff ones hinder deformation. Furthermore, fabrication constraints limit the realization of devices with higher moduli, since UV exposure time leads to wider structures due to light scattering. Accordingly, we fixed the material modulus to 590 kPa.

The relatively high values of MI between cilia length and both resonance frequency and amplitude indicate strong nonlinear relationships. Because the data contains both linear trends and sharp nonlinearities, standard regression is not suitable to analyze this data. This is because standard regression tends to fit either straight lines or one globally smooth curve. Sweeping the entire geometry space with FEA is likewise impractical. Random Forests addresses these issues. Because the Random Forests model interactions and nonlinear effects without choosing a kernel in advance, it can be trained on a sparse set of FEA runs and accurately predict responses for unsampled designs. The result is high predictive accuracy with a substantially smaller computational footprint than using FEA alone.

Another key advantage of the Random Forest method lies in its computational efficiency. The prediction process operates with a fixed complexity of O(m⋅d), where m = 100 is the number of trees and d = 10 is the tree depth, is over six orders of magnitude lower than the complexity of FEA simulations in our system, as illustrated in Fig. 2d (left). Consequently, the prediction runtime is drastically reduced, from 1620 ± 176 s per geometry for FEA to just 8.1 ± 0.18 ms using Random Forest, as shown in Fig. 2d (right). In addition, memory usage is reduced by more than 20-fold (Fig. 1g), enabling deployment on miniaturized or resource-constrained hardware platforms. Full complexity derivations and implementation details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Despite the computational advantage of machine learning prediction, the training process still relies on a sufficiently rich set of FEA-derived samples. Dense global sampling is unnecessary and inefficient, since large nonlinear shifts in peak features are confined to pockets of the design space. We therefore use localized refinement to balance cost and accuracy. A self-augmenting learning loop expands the training set where the model errors the most. This is done by mapping the residuals across the parameter space after an initial FEA-based pass. The high-residual regions are then resampled, and the model is retrained. Figure 1f and Fig. 2e show that this adaptive strategy markedly reduces frequency prediction error. After five iterations, the global residual stabilizes, with the average frequency prediction error falling below 2% and the amplitude prediction residual around 10%, as shown in Fig. 2f. The small mismatch comes from the 10 Hz resolution in the FEA sweep. If the actual resonance falls between sample points, the peak is not captured exactly and its amplitude is underestimated. The effect is modest and stays within experimental tolerance. In Fig. 2g, predictions for L = 80 µm and 200 µm show peaks that are clearly separated across geometries, which allows selective actuation without interference. Further implementation details are given in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). By contrast, other machine learning models, such as XGBoost and a multilayer perceptron model, performed worse under sparse sampling, largely due to noise sensitivity and overfitting (Supplementary Text and Supplementary Table 1).

Refined design of ECHObots enables versatile manipulation in microrobotic systems

Using the self-augmenting machine learning pipeline, we select geometries that produce the desired resonance and, in turn, the required deformation. As shown in Fig. 3a, modest changes in cilia shape shift both the frequency and amplitude of the resonance peaks. The FEA insets visualize the corresponding stress patterns, and the colored arrows mark the parameter sets. Since the resonance features is tunable, the robotic arm can be driven in a controlled, predictable way, allowing motion to be customized. A concrete example is shown in Fig. 3b, where two cilia of different sizes are placed on opposite sides of a flexible arm. At 5 kHz, the larger cilium resonates and drives a rightward bend, while the smaller cilium has little influence on the deformation. At 7.2 kHz, the roles reverse, and the arm bends to the left. The spacing minimizes coupling, keeping the peaks separate and allowing bidirectional actuation from a single acoustic driver. The underlying mechanism can be understood as geometry–frequency coupling: at different excitation frequencies, one cilium resonates more strongly than the other, creating unbalanced hydrodynamic forces that drive the structure to bend in the corresponding direction. Intuitively, the system behaves like a lever under opposing forces, where the side experiencing stronger resonance acts as the dominant force that tilts the balance of bending. These results indicate the ability to predictably select distinct deformation modes by tuning excitation frequency according to the designed cilia geometry. To further investigate the structural and fluidic responses under different excitation frequencies, FEA simulations in Fig. 3c visualize the corresponding stress distribution and acoustic streaming fields. We next examined how material properties and the surrounding fluid influence the resonance spectrum and mode shapes of the same flexible rod-cilia system. FEA results (Supplementary Fig. 11) indicate that even small increases in material relaxation time broaden and flatten the peaks, underscoring the central role of viscoelastic damping. Changing the fluid viscosity and density similarly alters the resonance spectrum, as shown in simulations (Supplementary Fig. 12) of biomedical-relevant environments, such as water, ethanol, blood, and phosphate-buffered saline.

We also varied the incidence angle of the acoustic wave, with 0° defined as propagation perpendicular to the cilium (Fig. 3d). Spectra at 0°, 20°, and 40° show large changes in peak amplitude but little shift in peak frequency, confirming that frequency is set primarily by the material and fluid rather than the incidence angle of the acoustic wave. Leveraging this property, we designed a rotating flower-shaped structure, as shown in Fig. 3e. When an acoustic signal is applied, it rotates, with low-voltage excitation resulting in slow rotation (16 rpm) and negligible structural deformation, as shown in Supplementary Movie 2. As the driving voltage increases, the flexible arms contract inward, reducing the overall structure size and leading to a marked increase in rotational speed (110 rpm) as reflected in Supplementary Movie 3. The rotational acceleration is primarily driven by the reaction force from the vibrating cilia, and it is aided by two secondary effects: drag is reduced, and the effective moment of inertia decreases as the structure deforms. Paired with bidirectional bending, this controlled rotation shows how ECHObots behavior can be altered by applying varying acoustic amplitudes. Therefore, a single acoustic driver can support a broad set of hybrid operations. To verify the coupling between the drive and cilia dynamics, we performed FEA on the same geometry (Fig. 3f). The measured resonances closely track the predictions, within approximately 2% (≈200 Hz). Rotating the cilia orientation produces clearly different mode shapes, in line with experiment (Supplementary Fig. 13). The right panel of Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 14 add a circular “wall,” where the driven flow generates particle paths with localized swirl. Particles circulate along the wall in closed loops, much like a tiny mill that repeatedly sweeps the boundary. These simulations match the experimental observations in Fig. 3e and point to cilia-generated reactive forces as the driver of both rotation and deformation. As shown in Fig. 3g, the rotation is voltage dependent, with speed rising in a controlled, systematic way as the drive increases. Together, the frequency-selective and the amplitude-dependent deformation tuning demonstrate the potential of ECHObots as a programmable platform for microscale soft robotics.

Structural diversity in ECHObots enabled by compliant mechanisms

Diverse deformation modes are central to the versatility of microrobots. In complex, unstructured environments, the ability to change shape passively and adaptively allows microrobots to move effectively and interact with their surroundings70. To realize this, we incorporate soft mechanisms in a controlled manner, enabling precise tuning of both geometry and motion. Figure 4a shows two approaches. Type-I relies on modulus modulation, where patterned exposure produces softer segments that bend preferentially. This reduces local stress and restoring force, enhancing durability and minimizing the risk of plastic deformation. However, material and fabrication constraints limit the achievable modulus reduction, so the decrease in actuation force is modest. Type-II, in contrast, modifies the geometric layout: narrowed joints act as hinges, substantially lowering the required actuation force and enabling large bending at relatively low power. The trade-off is that stress concentrates at the hinges, and recovery after acoustic excitation is less effective, potentially shortening device lifetime. To quantitatively assess the mechanical response of these compliant mechanisms, we measured the maximum stress and corresponding recovery force under a 45° bending deformation of the upper segment (relative to the x-axis). As shown in Fig. 4b, the two designs present distinct differences in stress distribution and restoring forces. Those differences explain the balance of each design between flexibility, robustness, and power efficiency. Building on this insight, we extended the approach to a set of ECHObot configurations, shown in Fig. 4c. The designs include Closure-type and Open-type-I configurations derived from the original structure; Open-type-II, which incorporates Type-I compliant mechanisms to improve adaptability (see Supplementary Movie 4); and Type-II connection-based designs such as the Curved Hemisphere and Multi-Segmented Arm. In the Curved Hemisphere design, cilia at the bases of the small triangular units generate upward reactive forces under acoustic excitation. With the leftmost large triangle held fixed and the smaller triangles connected by flexible hinges, the net forces pull the small triangles inwards, contracting the array into a hemisphere-like dome.

The Multi-Segmented Arm design (Supplementary Movie 5) further demonstrates the potential for complex morphing and diverse transformation modes. Under repeated actuation at 20 V, the multi-segment arms exhibit slightly larger deformation after ~20 cycles compared to the initial response, while still recovering to their original shape. The minor shift likely reflects localized plastic deformation at the hinge regions, consistent with the stress concentrations in Fig. 4b. For each architecture, we measured recovery time at its designated driving frequency (as labeled in the figures) under 20 V. The recovery times are as follows: closure-type, 1.5 s; open-type-I, 1.0 s; open-type-II, 3.5 s; curved hemisphere, 6.0 s; multi-segment arms, 1.0 s. The spread underscores the dependence of recovery on geometry and drive conditions. In addition, the recovery times for these structures provide a quantitative measure of controllability, showing that different morphologies return to their original configurations within predictable timescales. To demonstrate the capabilities of the robots, we pair experiments with FEA stress fields and simulations of the streaming flow. Together, they reveal the variety of deformations that can occur and how recovery and drive conditions impact controlled motion, and on-demand shape change. By integrating Type-I and Type-II compliant mechanisms, ECHObots strike an elegant balance between flexibility and control, achieving tunable, energy-efficient, and mechanically robust deformations. By selecting the appropriate bending mechanism, we can program the system to favor either fine, precise actuation or large, multi-state transformations tailored to diverse tasks. These programmable architectures unlock structural diversity and complex deformation in ECHObots, enabling soft robotic arms that open and close with lifelike motion and multi-segmented structures that morph dynamically to meet the challenge of their environment.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and optimized acoustic-driven ECHObots, integrating self-augmenting machine learning with FEA simulations to achieve precise and controllable shape transformations. The frequency and amplitude of the applied acoustics drive cilia oscillation, enabling ECHObots to undergo tunable and reversible shape change. To efficiently explore geometric design space, we implement a self-augmenting machine learning pipeline that identifies resonance peaks from a limited set of FEA simulations and iteratively refines itself by sampling regions where prediction errors are highest. Residual maps guide denser sampling in complex regions and sparser sampling elsewhere. Using a Random Forest model, the system captures the nonlinear relationship between geometry and resonance, achieving accurate predictions at over 105-fold lower computational cost than FEA alone, making data-driven microscale design both practical and scalable.

Altogether, we demonstrated precise and controllable actuation of ECHObots. Mode selection is realized through frequency- and amplitude-resolved deformation behaviors, such as bidirectional bending and controllable rotation. There is only 2% mismatch in resonance frequency between the FEA predictions and the experimental results. Under a 20 V driven voltage, the devices return to their original shape within 1–6 s, and the simplest design could operate more than 200 cycles. These results establish reliable, predictable, and reversible deformation control at the microscale. Furthermore, the actuation exhibits high spatial precision, with bending angles controlled to within a few degrees (Figs. 1d and 3b), and temporal precision, with recovery times of 1–6 s, providing clear quantitative benchmarks for both spatial and temporal control.

Guided by the optimized model, we can set the drive frequency and amplitude to make a single ECHObot bend in prescribed direction or rotate in a controlled way. The self-augmenting machine learning approach learns the resonance behavior well enough to deliver reliable control and fine functional precision, yielding predictable and tunable morphing suited to microscale and biomedical tasks. To make the structures themselves more adaptable, we employ two forms of compliance, Type-I (modulus modulation) and Type-II (spatial architecture). By integrating these two forms of compliance, we can control the force, flexibility, and recovery and enable multi-segment geometries and soft actuators, further widening the range of achievable deformations.

Looking ahead, we aim to improve structural stability, tighten system integration, and test performance in complex fluids, ultimately in vivo. We also expect the design workflow to evolve: machine learning tools that began as surrogates can mature into full platforms that suggest more structures within fabrication and stress limits. With FEA and experimental results as training data, the models can optimize resonance, deformation, and actuation efficiency simultaneously and drive a closed-loop cycle of geometry creation, simulation, testing, and continual improvement.

While most previous microrobotic studies have focused on directional locomotion, our work takes a deformation-centric approach, exploring an underexamined aspect of acoustic microrobotic functionality. Programmable, frequency-selective morphing offers a powerful complement to locomotion-focused designs, and integrating propulsion strategies, such as cilia arrays or bubble-based thrust within this deformation framework could enable microrobots that combine precise, directed movement with versatile shape changes. By enabling programmable shape change and frequency-addressed control, these systems open possibilities for future microscale manipulation strategies, including particle handling and other dynamic tasks. With in vivo validation, these principles could transform microrobot behavior to smart, adaptive, acoustically actuated manipulators capable of navigating and operating in complex biological and technological environments with precision and versatility.

Methods

Materials and reagents

Polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA700 and PEGDA250), photo initiator(2-Hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone), TE buffer (pH 8.0), Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, Mw 13,000-23,000, 87-89% hydrolyzed), 2-propanol (IPA, 99.5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. PDMS (Sylgard 148) was purchased from Dow Inc. Transducer (7BB-27-4C) was purchased from Murata.

Fabrication of ECHObots

ECHObots were fabricated using a UV photolithography technique. The PEGDA precursor solution was prepared by mixing 40% (v/v) PEGDA700, 30% (v/v) PEGDA250, 15% (v/v) photoinitiator, and 15% (v/v) TE buffer. PEGDA-based resin was injected into a 50 μm-high microfluidic channel, whose substrate had been pre-coated with PDMS to prevent the polymer from adhering. Assisted with an inverted microscope setup, a high-resolution photomask (ArtnetPro Inc.) was employed which was positioned within the field stop of a Nikon Eclipse Ti inverted microscope. A mercury lamp (Intensilight C-HGFI, Nikon Inc.) was mounted onto the microscope, delivering UV light through the photomask. A 20× objective lens was used to focus the UV light. The polymerization process was finely controlled by an electric shutter system (Smart Shutter, Sutter Instrument), which regulated the UV exposure to a precise duration of 500 ms at full light intensity, ensuring complete polymerization of the PEGDA. After UV exposure, the device was washed with isopropyl alcohol to remove any residual PEGDA. The microchannel was then carefully covered with a glass slide, the isopropyl alcohol was replaced with deionized water, and the device was transferred under a microscope for further observation. The heights and geometric parameters of the ECHObots were determined using optical microscopy, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 15.

ECHObot triggering and testing

We performed all tests on an inverted microscope with the devices mounted on glass slides. After gluing the transducer in place, the device was transferred to the slide, sandwiched with cover slips, and filled with deionized water to a depth of roughly 100 µm. When particle tracking was required, the medium consisted of a 10:1 volume mixture of deionized water and 5.7 µm tracer particles from Polysciences. A Tektronix AFG 3102C, driving a Krohn-Hite 7620, provided the acoustic signal, and both frequency and voltage were adjusted for each experiment. PEGDA stiffness was measured by nanoindentation using an Optics11 Piuma.

FEA simulation

We computed the acoustic flow in COMSOL Multiphysics (v6.1) by coupling the Pressure Acoustics, Thermoviscous Acoustics, and Laminar Flow interfaces. The acoustic source was prescribed with a 1 µm boundary displacement, consistent with related studies60. First, the Pressure Acoustics interface provided the time-harmonic, first-order field. This solution then served as the forcing for the time-averaged Navier–Stokes equations to obtain the steady streaming velocity. Reaction forces on the cilia were evaluated by applying the acoustic–structure boundary condition and integrating the traction on the cilium. Further details are given in the Supplementary Information.

Machine learning analysis

All analyses were performed in Python 3.11. We first trained a Random Forest model as an initial predictor. A sparse sweep of the geometric space (aspect ratio and cilia length) was run with FEA to extract resonance peak frequency and amplitude. To simplify training, we modeled only peak frequency and amplitude, and grouped the resonances into four principal sets to clearly show the trending of the peaks. Prediction residuals were then used to guide adaptive sampling: regions with high residuals received denser sampling, the new data points were added, and the model was retrained. This loop continued until residuals reached a target value, such as under 5% for frequency prediction, or reach a plateau for amplitude prediction. This approach yields a practical balance between computational cost and accuracy. Full details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Characterizations and measurements

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was performed on an Apreo S (Thermo Fisher Scientific). UV–vis spectra were recorded with a Shimadzu UV-3600i, and optical micrographs were acquired using an Elikliv EM4K digital microscope. Temperature was monitored with an HT-19 thermal camera, and PEGDA moduli were measured with a Piuma nanoindenter (Optics11).
