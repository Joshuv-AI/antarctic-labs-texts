Biochemical Upcycling of PET via Glycolysis and Engineered Microbial Consortia
Authors: Francisco J Molpeceres‐García, Alejandro García‐Miró, Alicia Prieto, David Sanz, Jorge Barriuso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolyethylene terephthalate (PET) waste remains a major environmental challenge due to its recalcitrance and low economic value. Here, we present an integrated biochemical approach that couples glycolysis with a synthetic microbial consortium to upcycle PET into polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs). Glycolysis efficiently depolymerized post‐consumer PET into bis(2‐hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (BHET) in 2 h, circumventing the limitations of in vivo PET degradation. We engineered a two‐species microbial consortium composed of Comamonas testosteroni RW31, able to metabolize terephthalic acid, and Pseudomonas putida JM37, able to consume ethylene glycol, each modified for the extracellular secretion of PET‐ and MHET‐hydrolases, employing different plasmid architectures. This division of labour creates a metabolic co‐dependency, enabling rapid BHET hydrolysis and the subsequent upcycling of the released monomers into PHAs. The combination of the different strains allowed us to select C. testosteroni pSEVA354‐MHETase and P. putida pSEVA234‐PETase as the best consortium combination, based on growth and PHAs content. Overall, this work proposes a strategy for PET waste depolymerisation and valorisation, highlighting the potential of mixed chemical and biological approaches and the use of non‐conventional microbial chassis within engineered consortia.Keywords: Comamonas testosteroni , glycolysis, microbial consortia, plastic waste upcycling, Pseudomonas putida

Introduction

The accumulation of plastic waste in the environment is currently an enormous challenge that requires the development of efficient strategies to be solved. Biotechnological approaches to this issue aim to employ bioprocesses to recycle or upcycle plastic waste, meaning to use it as feedstock to obtain valuable products which provide economic incentives for its proper management (Carniel et al. 2024; Ellis et al. 2021). Within this research field, the valorisation of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) has experienced the greatest development. There is a number of described enzymes and microorganisms able to depolymerize PET's chemical structure (Choi et al. 2023; Yao et al. 2024), although they are not effective against non‐amorphous material, which limits their applicability for degrading post‐consumer PET (pcPET) (Amalia et al. 2024). On the other hand, chemical depolymerization can easily degrade pcPET through a variety of strategies, including hydrolysis, aminolysis and alcoholysis (Cao et al. 2022). These methodologies lead to full or partial PET degradation, yielding its monomers ethylene glycol (EG) and terephthalic acid (TPA), intermediates such as bis‐(2‐hydroxyethyl)‐terephthalate (BHET) or PET oligomers (Barnard et al. 2021). Glycolysis, a type of alcoholysis, is the most widely applied of these methods. It uses EG to solubilize and break down PET into BHET, which facilitates an efficient repolymerization to produce high‐quality PET (Feng et al. 2025). However, these procedures often require high temperatures and pressures or hazardous catalysts, reducing their sustainability. Current research in this field aims to develop milder processes, using green catalysts to reduce the energy costs and environmental impact of industrial recycling (Barnard et al. 2021).

Within this framework, microorganisms can play an essential role, since some of them can assimilate TPA and/or EG and transform them into a variety of valuable compounds (Carniel et al. 2024). Thus, PET monomers can be used as feedstock for biotransformations. To date, most of the published studies employ single‐strain cultures. An example is Pseudomonas umsongensis GO16, which can assimilate both TPA and EG and produce polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) and hydroxyalkanoyloxy‐alkanoates (Tiso et al. 2021), together with Rhodococcus jostii RPET, which is also able to assimilate both monomers and produce a variety of compounds, such as lycopene, succinic acid or lipids (Diao et al. 2025).

On the other hand, the use of microbial consortia offers potential advantages over single strains, as their wider genetic repertoire can expand the bioprocess' possibilities. Some examples are the combination of metabolic pathways not expressed in mono‐cultures (Netzker et al. 2018), the increased production of target molecules or enzymes (Hays et al. 2017), or the enhanced robustness against environmental stress (Brenner and Arnold 2011). In addition, the main advantage of consortia is the division of labour, through which tasks are divided between the members of the community. Coupled with synthetic biology strategies, division of labour allows the distribution of the metabolic burden associated with heterologous protein expression, potentially increasing process efficiency (McCarty and Ledesma‐Amaro 2019). In the case of PET, the availability of two different carbon sources (TPA and EG) facilitates the design of consortia, as it avoids competition for carbon (McCarty and Ledesma‐Amaro 2019).

In previous studies, we have characterized two bacteria as synthetic biology chassis in the context of PET upcycling: Comamonas testosteroni RW31, which can use TPA to accumulate polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025), and Pseudomonas putida JM37, which can grow on EG and accumulate medium chain length polyhydroxyalkanoates (mcl‐PHA) (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026). In this work, we have designed a microbial consortium in which these bacteria have been modified to secrete two enzymes that work synergistically to degrade PET, the FAST‐PETase (Lu et al. 2022) and the MHETase from Ideonella sakaiensis (IsMHETase) (Yoshida et al. 2016). Furthermore, we have distributed the expression of the two enzymes between the two bacterial chassis using different plasmid architectures, establishing a metabolic co‐dependency to evaluate its effect on the consortium population dynamics. To demonstrate its applicability, we propose a chemo‐biological approach for PET upcycling to PHB and mcl‐PHA, combining glycolysis of pcPET with BHET valorisation by the selected microbial consortium.

TPA and EG Tolerance

C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 were grown in MC minimal medium with 10 mM TPA (Sigma‐Aldrich, Ref 185,361) or EG (Sigma‐Aldrich, Ref 102,466), respectively, as carbon sources. To assess C. testosteroni RW31 tolerance to EG, the bacterium was grown with 0 mM, 10 mM, 25 mM and 50 mM EG. In the case of P. putida JM37, TPA was tested at the same concentrations. C. testosteroni RW31 tolerance to TPA and P. putida JM37 tolerance to EG have been evaluated in previous works (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025, 2026). Cultures were conducted in 96 well plates using a Victor Nivo Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer) at 30°C and 200 rpm. Growth was measured as OD600. Bacteria were inoculated at 0.1 OD600.

Plasmid Design and Transformation

Codon‐optimized FAST‐PETase and IsMHETase with the signal peptide SPpstu from Pseudomonas stutzeri MO‐19 (Fujita et al. 1989) were cloned into pSEVA334, pSEVA354, pSEVA234 and pSEVA254 using the restriction sites EcoRI and HindIII (Durante‐Rodríguez et al. 2014). KmR plasmids were transformed into P. putida JM37, whereas CrR plasmids were employed for C. testosteroni RW31. The resulting strains were named P. putida 3‐FP, P. putida 5‐FP, P. putida 3‐MH, P. putida 5‐MH, C. testosteroni 3‐FP, C. testosteroni 5‐FP, C. testosteroni 3‐MH and C. testosteroni 5‐MH (Table 1). Plasmids were transformed by electroporation into electrocompetent cells as explained in Molpeceres‐García et al. (2026).

Esterase Activity Assay for PETase

The FAST‐PETase activity in P. putida 3‐FP and 5‐FP strains was reported in a previous work (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026). C. testosteroni 3‐FP and 5‐FP esterase activity was measured similarly. Briefly, the pre‐cultured strains were inoculated in LB at 0.1 OD600, adding 1 mM IPTG and Cr for plasmid induction and maintenance. The wild‐type and non‐induced recombinant strains were employed as negative controls. After 48 h of culture, 100 μL of supernatant were used. The extracellular esterase activity was assessed following the hydrolysis of 1.5 mM p‐nitrophenyl‐butyrate (pNPB) (Sigma‐Aldrich, Ref N9876), measuring absorbance at 410 nm. For the reactions, a phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) was used. One activity unit corresponds to the amount of enzyme necessary for releasing 1 μmol of p‐nitrophenol (ε410 = 15,200 M−1 cm−1) per minute.

MHETase Activity Assay

As MHETase activity cannot be determined following pNP‐aliphatic esters hydrolysis (Yoshida et al. 2016), in vivo assays were performed. P. putida 5‐FP was co‐inoculated with either P. putida 3‐MH or P. putida 5‐MH, each at 0.05 OD600, in MC minimal medium with 0.5 g/L yeast extract as nitrogen source and 10 mM EG as carbon source, adding 5 mM of BHET (Sigma‐Aldrich, CAS 959–26‐2) and 1 mM IPTG. A similar assay was performed for co‐cultures of C. testosteroni 5‐FP with C. testosteroni 3‐MH or C. testosteroni 5‐MH, using 10 mM TPA as carbon source. The resulting monomers were measured by HPLC, as shown in the following section, to determine the complete degradation of BHET to TPA and EG. Mono‐cultures of each strain were also analysed as negative controls.

PET Monomers Quantification by HPLC

EG, BHET, MHET and TPA were quantified employing an Agilent 1200 series HPLC. EG concentration was measured using an RID detector (Agilent), a BioRad Aminex HPX‐87H column (300 × 7.8 mm), and H2SO4 2 mM as the mobile phase, under isocratic conditions at 0.5 mL/min over 25 min. The column was maintained at 60°C and RID at 35°C. EG retention time was 19.5 min. For BHET, MHET and TPA analysis, samples were first diluted 1:1 with acetonitrile to ensure proper dissolution of BHET and MHET. Then, they were separated on a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm) maintained at 30°C and detected at 260 nm using a DAD detector. Solvent A was 0.1% acetic acid in Milli Q water and solvent B 0.1% acetic acid in acetonitrile. 95% A and 5% B were mixed for column equilibration, and a gradient from 95% to 30% A over 25 min was used for elution, with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. Retention times were 10.2 min for TPA, 11.4 min for MHET and 12.1 min for BHET.

Post‐Consumer PET Glycolysis

Glycolysis was carried out at 200°C in a 250 mL flask containing 75 mL of EG and 6 g of post‐consumer PET (pcPET) obtained from a commercial water bottle (Nestlé Aquarel). Zn(CH3CO2)2 2H2O was added as catalyst (1% w/v). pcPET was cut into ~1 × 1 cm pieces and added to the flask once the EG reached the proper temperature. The reaction was carried out for 2.5 h, sampling every 30 min to monitor BHET formation, and allowed to cool before post‐consumer BHET (pcBHET) recovery by crystallization. To do so, Milli‐Q water was added in 1:10 proportion and the solution stored at 4°C overnight. pcBHET crystals deposited at the bottom of the flask were recovered after filtered through filter paper, washed with Milli‐Q water and dried at 50°C. Consortia Cultivation and Growth Monitoring

Experiments were carried out in 250 mL flasks with 50 mL of MC medium supplemented with 5 mM TPA and 5 mM EG for 48 h, at 30°C and 180 rpm. Each strain was inoculated at 0.05 OD600. The tested consortia are listed in Table 3. Either BHET or pcBHET was used at a final concentration of 5 mM to test the conversion efficiency of the consortia. Samples were taken at 0, 6, 24 and 48 h for the quantification of PET monomers, PHAs content analysis and growth assessment. To monitor C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 growth separately, the plate counting method was used. For that, three 10 μL drops were plated on two MC‐agar Petri dishes, one supplemented with 10 mM TPA and the other with 10 mM EG as the sole carbon source. Plates were incubated 24 h at 30°C, and then growth was measured as colony forming units per mL (CFU/mL). As C. testosteroni RW31 cannot assimilate EG and P. putida JM37 cannot grow on TPA (Figure S3), this method allowed the distinction between populations.

PHAs Analysis and Quantification by GC–MS

PHAs accumulation was visually confirmed after Nile Red staining (Zuriani et al. 2013), as explained in Molpeceres‐García et al. (2026). The consortium with the highest PHA content was selected for monomer identification and quantification.

Total PHA content and composition were analysed and quantified after methanolysis by GC–MS. Two technical replicates with 2–5 mg of freeze‐dried biomass were employed per biological sample. Samples were subjected to two different treatments, in order to properly extract PHB and mcl‐PHA. Briefly, pellets were freeze‐dried and resuspended in 2 mL of methanol with sulfuric acid 3% for PHB and 15% for mcl‐PHA. Then, 2 mL of internal standard (0.5 mg/mL of 3‐methylbenzoic acid in chloroform) were added to every vial. Samples were incubated at 100°C for 4 h in the case of PHB, and 5 h in the case of mcl‐PHA, placed on ice to stop the reaction, and washed with 2 mL Milli‐Q water twice. The aqueous phase and the cellular debris were removed after centrifugation. For mcl‐PHA samples, the residual sulfuric acid was eliminated with an additional washing using 2 mL of a saturated solution of Na2CO3. Traces of residual water were removed by adding a small amount of Na2SO4 to the organic phase.

Methanolized samples were analysed in an Agilent INTUVO 9000 GC System gas chromatography system (Santa Clara, California, USA), with a HP‐5MS UI column (30 m × 0.25 mm x 0.25 μm film thickness), coupled with a 5977C GC/MSD detector (EI, 70 eV). The samples were analysed using two different temperature programmes. For PHB, the starting temperature was 60°C, with an increase of 5°C/min until 65°C and 15°C/min until 125°C, maintaining this temperature for 3.5 min. The retention time for the methyl ester of the C4 monomer was 2.4 min, and for the internal standard 7.7 min. For mcl‐PHA, the starting temperature was 80°C and it was increased 15°C/min up to 175°C, holding 20 min at this temperature. The methyl ester monomers' retention times were: 3.5 min (C6), 5.2 min (C8), 7.0 min (C10), 9.1 min (C12:1) and 9.4 min (C12). Internal standard was detected at 4.9 min.

Results and Discussion

C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 Are Suitable for a Consortium

In order to develop a PET‐upcycling consortium, we first assessed TPA and EG toxicity in P. putida JM37 and C. testosteroni RW31, respectively. With 50 mM EG, C. testosteroni showed a delay of 18 h to reach the stationary phase and a decrease of ~7% in its maximum OD600, whereas with 10 and 25 mM almost no differences compared to the positive control without inhibitor were detected (Figure 1A). In the case of P. putida JM37, the effect of TPA was more marked, with a decrease in the maximum OD600 of ~18% with 10 and 25 mM of TPA and ~22% with 50 mM (Figure 1A). Regarding the growth rates at the exponential phase, the effect of TPA in P. putida JM37 and EG in C. testosteroni RW31 was more pronounced at 50 mM (Table 2). In the case of P. putida JM37 cultures, a slightly longer lag phase was detected with 50 mM EG (Table 2). Hence, both microorganisms were able to tolerate high concentrations of TPA and EG, although P. putida JM37 seemed to be more sensitive to TPA. Growth inhibition by TPA has been observed in a modified strain of P. putida KT2440 able to assimilate EG, in which increasing concentrations of TPA led to a decrease in EG consumption and bacterial growth (Bao et al. 2023). The same study proved that the formation of a consortium with a second P. putida strain, modified to assimilate TPA, relieved the inhibitory effect. In our case, the co‐culture of P. putida JM37 with the TPA‐consumer C. testosteroni RW31 is expected to have a similar impact.

Furthermore, C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 demonstrated to grow together in MC minimal medium with 10 mM of TPA and EG as carbon sources. This consortium achieved a cellular density of 1.75 × 109 CFU/mL in 24 h, containing 64.5% of C. testosteroni and a 35.4% of P. putida (Figure 1B). The prevalence of C. testosteroni over P. putida could be explained by the imbalance between carbon sources. In this 24 h experiment, P. putida fully consumed the EG from the culture medium, whereas C. testosteroni assimilated ~71% of the available TPA (Figure 1B), suggesting a carbon excess in TPA and a deficit in EG. An optimization of the culture conditions for consortia experiments would probably enhance TPA depletion. Moreover, the metabolism of TPA and EG may also play a role in this carbon imbalance. In previous works, we showed that C. testosteroni RW31 likely assimilates TPA through protocatechuic acid by the 4,5‐meta pathway, which yields pyruvate and oxaloacetate, meaning that 7 carbons are used from each TPA molecule assimilated (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025; Wilkes et al. 2023). However, P. putida JM37 assimilates EG through the Gcl pathway, which yields only one pyruvate per two EG molecules, meaning that each EG would provide 1.5 carbons for biomass production (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026; Mückschel et al. 2012). Thus, C. testosteroni RW31 would have 4.67‐fold more potentially available carbon than P. putida JM37. This suggests that the C. testosteroni RW31—P. putida JM37 proportion could be balanced by adding more EG or a co‐substrate to the media. However, we decided to use equimolar concentrations of TPA and EG considering the use of hydrolysated PET as substrate in future experiments, which would yield TPA and EG in 1:1 proportion.

Expression of PET‐Hydrolases in C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37

Following our biological approach, we developed a BHET‐degrading consortium cloning the FAST‐PETase and the IsMHETase in C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37. Both enzymes were cloned in plasmids with pBBR1 or RSF1010 replication origins (Table 1) to assess which one was better for protein expression and consortium fitness, as they differ in copy number (Cook et al. 2018). The extracellular esterase activity against pNPB of C. testosteroni 3‐FP and 5‐FP strains was 0.12 ± 0.007 and 0.28 ± 0.007 U/mL, respectively (Figure 2). The activity of P. putida 3‐FP and 5‐FP strains was reported in a previous work (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026), resulting in 0.36 and 0.41 U/mL against pNPB, respectively. In both bacteria, RSF1010 plasmids resulted in an improved esterase activity, probably due to their higher copy number. The activity was 3‐fold higher in P. putida 3‐FP compared to C. testosteroni 3‐FP and 1.46‐fold higher in P. putida 5‐FP compared to C. testosteroni 5‐FP.

In the case of the MHETase‐carrying strains, enzymatic activity was tested indirectly in consortia with a PETase‐carrying strain to measure the joint degradation of BHET. Co‐cultures of C. testosteroni 5‐FP with C. testosteroni 3‐MH or C. testosteroni 5‐MH degraded 41.2% and 44.4% of BHET into EG and TPA in 6 h, respectively, and completely in 24 h. MHET was barely detectable during the experiment, indicating that it was quickly degraded. In the case of P. putida , the strain 5‐FP in consortia with P. putida 3‐MH or 5‐MH degraded BHET completely in 6 h and MHET in 24 h (Figure 3).

In all the monocultures of MHETase‐producing strains, a partial degradation of BHET was observed (Figure 3B,C), especially in C. testosteroni RW31 strains. Since IsMHETase activity against BHET is very low (Palm et al. 2019), we tested the degradative effect on BHET of the wild‐type C. testosteroni RW31, which has some unspecific activity (data not shown). This suggests that, rather than a non‐specific PETase activity of IsMHETase, this bacterium may secrete an enzyme with low activity against BHET. The faster BHET degradation by P. putida 5‐FP cultures compared to C. testosteroni 5‐FP could be explained by its higher PETase activity. Regarding MHET, all the MHETase‐carrying strains showed high activity.

Recombinant C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 Strains Can Degrade BHET in Consortium

Once the activity of all recombinant strains was demonstrated, several consortia combining the two species (Table 3) were evaluated for their capacity to grow in MC minimal medium containing BHET as substrate. In addition, previous results showed that both C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 can produce PHAs in this medium using TPA and EG as substrate, respectively. Hence, microbial growth (CFU/mL, Figure 4), accumulation of PHAs (Nile Red staining, Figure S1) and BHET, MHET, TPA and EG degradation and consumption (Figure S2) were evaluated in the consortia listed in Table 3 using commercial BHET as substrate. In all cases, growth was higher for C. testosteroni RW31 strains than for P. putida JM37 strains, which is consistent with the results obtained with the wild‐type consortium (Figure 1). However, expressing heterologous enzymes did have an effect on bacterial fitness. While population proportions were more balanced in Con3, Con5 and Con7, transformation with pSEVA254‐IsMHETase (Con4, Con8) produced a strong detrimental effect on P. putida JM37 growth (Figure 4). This may be explained by the higher copy number associated with the RSF1010 origin of replication and the larger molecular weight of IsMHETase (65 kDa) compared to FAST‐PETase (29 kDa) (Palm et al. 2019; Seo et al. 2019), which may represent a metabolic burden for P. putida JM37. These results suggest that plasmid architecture strongly influences the growth dynamics of C. testosteroni RW31 and P. putida JM37 recombinant strains. It is worth mentioning that the expression of FP in P. putida would enable limited growth, as the enzyme can release a single molecule of EG from BHET. In contrast, expression of MHETase alone would not support growth. Conversely, C. testosteroni would be unable to grow on BHET when expressing only FP or only MHETase, since TPA would not be liberated. Consequently, the degradation of BHET establishes a metabolic interdependence between the two species, requiring their combined activities to fully release both monomers. Considering substrates degradation, BHET was fully depleted in 24 h in all the consortia tested but Con1, in which 0.5 mM remained at this time. Similarly, MHET was barely present during the experiments, although in Con4 some was accumulated at 6 h, although it was depleted at 24 h. TPA was not fully consumed in any condition, while EG disappeared after 48 h (Figure S2).

These differences will be useful for a better design of future consortia, as population proportions have been proven important to obtain an optimal performance in synthetic consortia (Bao et al. 2023; Mehta et al. 2025; Qi et al. 2021). To conduct the following experiments, Con6 ( C. testosteroni 5‐MH + P. putida 3‐FP) was selected for its high growth (1.12 × 109 CFU/mL, with 78.8% of C. testosteroni 5‐MH and 21.2% of P. putida 3‐FP) and PHAs accumulation at 24 h (Figure S1).

Con6 completely degraded 5 mM commercial BHET in 24 h, while MHET was barely present during the experiment (Figure 5A), demonstrating an effective expression of both FAST‐PETase and IsMHETase. While EG was fully consumed, only 59.5% of the available TPA was assimilated in 48 h. The relative abundance of each population and the TPA/EG consumption of Con6 was similar to the wild‐type strains (Figure 1), although growth was slower, as it peaked 24 h later. Despite there being 15 mM EG potentially available (5 mM in the medium +10 mM from the BHET hydrolysis) and all of it being consumed, the proportion of P. putida was not larger than in the wild‐type consortium. These growth differences could be explained by the metabolic burden of expressing the FAST‐PETase. Moreover, in a previous work in which FAST‐PETase and IsMHETase were produced in C. testosteroni RW31, we observed that increasing the amount of BHET from 5 to 10 mM in the medium inhibited bacterial growth (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025). Again, as in the wild‐type consortium (Figure 1), TPA was not completely depleted, which could probably be solved by optimizing the culture conditions. As controls, monocultures of C. testosteroni 5‐MH and P. putida 3‐FP were grown in the same medium as the consortium (Figure S3). Here, P. putida 3‐FP grew similarly to the Con6 experiment, while C. testosteroni 5‐MH exhibited lower growth. As expected, no MHET accumulated in the C. testosteroni 5‐MH monoculture and EG concentration increased with time, while TPA was partially consumed. On the other hand, MHET accumulated in P. putida 3‐FP culture, while EG was consumed and TPA accumulated.

In vivo degradation of PET or BHET by microbial consortia has been studied mainly from a top‐down perspective. In most reports, workflows involve the isolation of microbial communities from contaminated areas, followed by its characterization and assessment of enzymatic activities and plastic degradation (Gao and Sun 2021; Maheswaran et al. 2023; Meyer‐Cifuentes et al. 2020; Roberts et al. 2020). Alternatively, the bottom‐up approach, in which a synthetic consortium is designed for an specific objective, allows to choose suitable organisms with desirable capacities, as well as their improvement by synthetic biology tools (Jia et al. 2016; Stephens and Bentley 2020). For instance, Qi et al. (2021) designed a multi‐species consortium in which Bacillus subtilis 168 was able to express either IsPETase or IsMHETase. As TPA was inhibitory for B. subtilis , Rhodococcus jostii RHA1 was added for detoxification. This consortium degraded 7.9 mM of BHET dissolved in a 50% γ‐cyclodextrin solution in 20 h and ~10.5 mg of an amorphous PET film in 7 days, growing in LB medium. Also, the addition of a modified P. putida KT2440 allowed the assimilation of the released EG. Moreover, the authors reported that the inoculum ratio and medium composition played an important role in the behaviour of the consortium. In our case, the use of TPA‐ and EG‐assimilating organisms as chassis makes the process simpler, as substrate inhibitory effects should not be a concern. Furthermore, the fact that each microorganism produces a single degrading enzyme imposes a cooperation between the consortium members. While P. putida 3‐FP is needed for the degradation of BHET to MHET, C. testosteroni 5‐MH degrades MHET, obtaining TPA for its own consumption, providing an extra EG molecule to P. putida and eliminating the MHET, which is known to be a PETase inhibitor (Zhang et al. 2023).

Post‐Consumer PET Glycolysis

To obtain pcBHET, 6 g of pcPET were glycolyzed to produce 940 mM of pcBHET in 2 h (Figure S4A), with no presence of MHET and TPA in the glycolysis solution. pcBHET was crystallized by adding water to the reaction mixture, obtaining 4.7 g of a solid fraction (59.2% pcBHET molar yield) and a diluted EG solution. After glycolysis, usually part of the PET is not fully converted to BHET but remains as oligomers. Furthermore, some BHET can be lost in the crystallization step, for example because it is dissolved in the wash water (López‐Fonseca et al. 2010). Also, it is worth mentioning that an undetermined fraction of the pcPET weight can correspond to additives and glues, which could interfere with molar yield calculations and microorganisms' growth. This conversion yield could be improved by optimization of parameters such as reaction time, PET:EG molar ratio and the type and concentration of catalyst added (López‐Fonseca et al. 2010), or by the addition of cosolvents (Huang et al. 2023). Although residual EG is obtained in the process, it can be purified by distillation and reused for subsequent PET glycolysis. Aguado et al. (2023) performed glycolysis of 200 g PET, reusing the EG up to 10 times, observing a decrease of 6.6%–7.4% of pcBHET conversion in the last reaction respect to fresh EG. As an alternative for EG reutilization, we tested whether the residual EG can serve as feedstock to produce PHAs in pure cultures of P. putida JM37. With 200 mM of the residual EG in MC minimal medium, JM37 grew up to 1.67 ± 0.03 OD600 in 48 h and accumulated PHAs, as confirmed by Nile Red staining (Figure S4B,C). In previous studies, we observed that P. putida JM37 reached ~2 OD600 employing 200 mM of commercial EG in the same medium (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026). This suggests that residual EG has an inhibitory effect on the bacterium, perhaps caused by the presence of the glycolysis catalyst, Zn(CH3CO2)2, which is soluble in EG and can be toxic for bacteria (Alhasawi et al. 2014; Carné Sánchez and Collinson 2011). Moreover, this strain can use EG as a carbon source to produce violacein, a highly valuable compound (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026). Hence, the utilization of the EG recovered after glycolysis can be part of a biochemical strategy for PET upcycling.

Chemo‐Biological Upcycling of Post‐Consumer PET to PHAs

To demonstrate the feasibility of this approach, we tested the pcBHET obtained by glycolysis as carbon source for Con6. As with commercial BHET, 5 mM pcBHET was hydrolysed in 24 h, and MHET was not detected during the experiment (Figure 5B). In the co‐cultures, 68.5% of TPA and 100% of EG from pcBHET was consumed in 48 h, obtaining 1.36 × 109 CFU/mL in 48 h in the growth assays. Surprisingly, in this case the C. testosteroni 5MH— P. putida 3‐FP proportion was nearly 50%–50% across the incubation period. Although 9% more TPA was assimilated in these conditions, a 1.55‐fold decrease in C. testosteroni 5‐MH maximum growth was observed compared with cultures with commercial BHET, while the growth curve of P. putida 3‐FP was similar in both cases. The presence of undetermined additives and glues into the pcPET used could explain this detrimental effect on C. testosteroni 5‐MH growth.

Con6 cultures grown with both BHET and pcBHET were methanolized to quantify PHAs. To decide when to harvest the cells, we performed Nile Red staining of the samples at 6 h, 24 h and 48 h (data not shown). Based on these qualitative observations, we harvested the cells at 24 h, as they seemed to have a higher PHAs amount. Previous works showed that C. testosteroni RW31 accumulates PHB from TPA, while P. putida JM37 produces a mixture of mcl‐PHA, dominated by the C10 monomer, when growing on EG (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025, 2026). In this work, Con6 produced predominantly PHB, amounting to 24.7% ± 1.58% relative to the total cell dry weight of the consortium (%PHB/CDW) in the medium with BHET, and 29.5% ± 0.87 %PHB/CDW when using pcBHET (Table S1). These values show a notable improvement compared with mono‐culture of C. testosteroni RW31 expressing both FAST‐PETase and Is MHETase accumulated 12.03%PHB/CDW at 24 h in cultures with 5 mM of BHET (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2025). It is known that PHAs accumulation can be influenced by nutritional imbalances (Mitra et al. 2022; Montiel‐Jarillo et al. 2017). Therefore, these differences could be due to a different ratio C/N for both microorganisms in the media, as in Con6 the nitrogen source is shared between the two populations. Nonetheless, the PHB accumulation regulation mechanisms in C. testosteroni RW31 are not well understood yet. These questions should be assessed with further experimental data. Regarding mcl‐PHA, we found small amounts with a profile (Table S1) marked by the predominance of C10 (~70%), followed by C12 (~16%) and C8 (~11%), which slightly differs from that determined for P. putida JM37 growing on EG (60% C10; 19% C12; 12% C8; 2% C6) (Molpeceres‐García et al. 2026).

In vitro depolymerized PET has been upcycled to a number of valuable compounds, usually with monocultures (Diao et al. 2023, 2025; Kim et al. 2019; Rebocho et al. 2025). Werner et al. (2021) used glycolysis to depolymerize PET into BHET, which was upcycled to β‐ketoadipic acid by a single P. putida KT2440 strain modified for EG and TPA assimilation as well as for IsPETase and IsMHETase secretion. However, glucose was needed as a supplementary carbon source to avoid a lag in MHETase activity. In the case of microbial consortia, Bao et al. (2023) designed a P. putida KT2440 strain able to assimilate TPA and EG, and compared it with two KT2440 strains, each of them specialized for assimilating either TPA or EG. Employing a PET hydrolysate with 75 mM of both TPA and EG, the authors showed that the consortium outperformed the monoculture at upcycling the monomers to mcl‐PHA (637 mg/L, 93% increase) and muconic acid (4.73 g/L, 2.84‐fold increase). These results reinforce the concept of applying division of labour within microbial consortia for upcycling.

Conclusions

This study presents a chemo‐biological strategy for the upcycling of post‐consumer PET by integrating chemical depolymerization with a synthetic engineered microbial consortium composed of two novel bacterial chassis. Glycolysis effectively converted PET into BHET, overcoming the limitations of in vivo degradation. We designed consortia with C. testosteroni RW31— P. putida JM37, which can respectively assimilate TPA and EG. These bacteria were modified to secrete either a heterologous MHETase or a PETase, allowing the consortium to collaboratively degrade BHET and produce PHAs from its monomers. Moreover, various plasmid architectures were tested, producing notable differences in population proportions, which could be interesting to design future consortia. We selected a consortium formed by C. testosteroni 5‐MH and P. putida 3‐FP strains (Con6), based on growth and PHAs production, which could degrade pcBHET and accumulate PHAs, mainly PHB. Our work provides a proof of concept for the applicability of multi‐species microbial consortia in the design of biotechnological strategies, while highlighting the potential of two non‐conventional chassis for PET waste valorisation. Further studies on microbial interactions within this consortium will lead to a better understanding of PET upcycling, enabling the optimization of this process.
