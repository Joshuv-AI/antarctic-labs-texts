Smoking‐Related Clinical and Molecular Abnormalities in Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis—Associated Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Iryna Zhyvylo, Namita Sood, Janelle Vu Pugashetti, Angela L Linderholm, Ssu‐Wei Hsu, Justin Oldham, Ching‐Hsien Chen, Elena A Goncharova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a frequent complication of interstitial lung diseases (ILD) that worsens morbidity and mortality. Cigarette smoking has a detrimental effect on transplant‐free survival of patients with ILD, including patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). However, smoking‐related molecular alterations in patients with ILD‐PH are not well understood. We performed analysis of 226 patients with ILD with and without PH (59.3% smokers, 40.3% with IPF) from the University of California, Davis cohort. Supporting previous findings, smokers with ILD had significantly shorter transplant‐free survival than ILD non‐smokers. Using a multivariable Cox regression model, we identified cigarette smoking as a significant independent predictor of mortality of patients with ILD. Smokers with IPF‐PH have significantly lower diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) compared to non‐smokers. Transcriptomic analysis of whole blood of IPF‐PH patients identified 616 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in smokers compared to non‐smokers with most deregulated fat cell differentiation and protein ubiquitination pathways. Three DEGs belonging to these pathways (BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1) showed moderate negative correlation with mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) in patients with IPF‐PH. These data show that, in patients with IPF‐PH, smoking is associated with reduced DLCO, and suggest that BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 could be considered as potential biomarkers of PH severity in smokers with IPF‐PH.Keywords: interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, RNAseq, smoking, transcriptome

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive and fatal disease characterized by unresolved increase in pulmonary artery (PA) pressure (PAP) caused by vasoconstriction and remodeling of muscularized PAs [1, 2]. Group 3 PH due to chronic lung disease and/or hypoxia is the second most common form of PH [3]. Specifically, PH is a frequent and underrecognized complication of interstitial lung disease (ILD) that significantly worsens patients’ morbidity and mortality [4, 5, 6, 7, 8].

Cigarette smoking is widely accepted as a primary cause of respiratory bronchiolitis ILD, desquamative interstitial pneumonitis, and pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and is a major risk factor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Smoking has a detrimental effect on transplant‐free survival of patients with progressive fibrosing ILD and significantly impacts overall patients’ survival [9, 10, 11]. Pre‐clinical studies demonstrate that cigarette smoke directly contributes to the development of experimental PH by increasing cell senescence, vascular tone alterations, and proliferation of PA smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) [12, 13]. Pulmonary vascular remodeling has been detected in the lungs of cigarette smokers with and without chronic obstructive lung disease (COPD) [14]. It was also shown that smoking‐dependent PA muscularization correlates with PAP, providing the link between cigarette smoking and group 3 PH. However, despite the well‐established links between smoking and ILD, the impact of smoking on the patients with ILD‐PH remains to be determined.

In this study, we aimed to determine whether smoking is associated with ILD‐PH severity and to evaluate potential smoking‐associated changes in the whole blood transcriptome of patients with ILD‐PH.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

The UC Davis cohort was comprised of 226 subjects who were diagnosed with fibrotic ILD between July 2016 and November 2020 (Figure 1A, Table 1). The ILD was diagnosed using a multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) approach based on complete analysis of clinical, radiological, and serological test results in accordance with the American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) guidelines [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Patients with an MDD diagnosis of combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE) were excluded. PH was diagnosed by right heart catheterization (RHC) (43 patients) and was defined as an elevation of resting mean PAP (mPAP) > 20 mmHg with a PA wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) > 2 Wood Units (WU) [20] or based on echocardiographic findings, including the presence of all of findings: dilated right ventricle (RV), estimated RV systolic pressure (RVSP) greater than 50 mmHg, and interventricular septal bowing. Echocardiography was performed according to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines using the modified Bernoulli equation [21]. The peripheral blood samples were obtained from the patients after written informed consent and biobanked as a part of ILD biorepository under the protocol approved by University of California, Davis Institutional Review Board. For each patient, demographics were collected, including age at enrollment, ethnicity, sex, and smoking history comprised current or past smoking status, and number of packs smoked per day. The pack year (PY) was calculated as a [(cigarettes per day/pack size) × years] [22]. Clinical data, including PAP, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), high‐resolution computed tomography (CT) of the chest, and complete pulmonary function test included forced vital capacity (FVC), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO), as well as survival status, lung transplantation or death were retrieved from medical records [23, 24].

Sample Collection, RNA Sequencing, and Pathway Analysis

Peripheral whole blood was collected in PAXgene blood RNA tubes from consenting patients by simple venipuncture under aseptic conditions. Upon acquisition, samples were kept at room temperature for 1 h, then at −20°C for 24 h, and then transferred at −80°C without thawing. PAXgene blood RNA extraction, RNA library preparation, and next generation sequencing were performed by Gatehouse Bio. Gene expression analysis to identify and compare the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between smokers and non‐smokers was performed using DESeq 2. The default setting of DESeq 2 for DEGs identification was used. Volcano plots were created using ggplot2 using default settings. A heatmap was generated using normalized expression data to visualize gene expression patterns across samples. Functional interactome analysis was performed using the STRING database. Pathway analysis was conducted using DAVID.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons between two groups were performed using an independent t‐test or Mann‐Whitney U test for datasets with normal and abnormal distribution, respectively. The normality of distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis of the time from the first visit to lung transplantation or death was performed using the Kaplan‐Meier method with the log‐rank test. Survival time was censored on August 5th, 2023. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were used to determine prognostic markers of all‐cause mortality while accounting for potential confounding. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess global gene expression variability based on normalized expression data. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA).

Results

Smoking Is a Significant Predictor of Mortality and Is Associated With Worse Survival in Patients With ILD

The ILD cohort comprised of patients with IPF (40.3%), connective tissue disease (CTD) (23.5%), chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (CHP) (10.2%), idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIP) (25.7%) and others unclassifiable ILD (0.3%). Baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table. The median age of ILD population at diagnosis was 71.1 ± 9.8 years (range: 35.8–90.8) and 54.4% were male. Of the whole ILD group, 134 subjects (59.3%) were active smokers or had a history of smoking, most of them were men (62.7%) with average PY of 24.4. Smokers were significantly older, predominantly males, and emphysema among smokers was diagnosed significantly more often than among non‐smokers (Table 2). We observed no significant differences in DLCO and FVC between smoker and non‐smoker ILD subjects (Table 2). In the overall ILD cohort, DLCO showed a moderate positive correlation with FVC (r = 0.557, 95% CI 0.456 to 0.644, p < 0.0001), while no significant correlations were observed with RVSP, mPAP, cardiac index (CI), and PVR.

The emphysematous changes identified on CT were generally mild and radiologically reported and did not represent classical CPFE. To further evaluate the potential impact of CT‐defined emphysema, we performed additional subgroup analyses within the ILD smokers’ group. PVR was significantly higher in patients with emphysema compared to those without emphysema (4.87 vs. 2.49 WU, p = 0.0476), while no significant differences were observed in hemodynamic or echocardiographic parameters.

Antifibrotic therapy was present in a very small proportion of the ILD cohort, with only 7 patients (3%) receiving treatment (1 patient on nintedanib and 6 patients on pirfenidone), and none of the patients with PH were receiving inhaled prostanoids at the time when blood samples were collected.

On average, the follow‐up of our ILD patients was 40 months, with a maximum follow‐up of 7 years. A retrospective evaluation conducted in August 2023 showed that between May 2016 and August 2023, in the smoker group 51 patients (38.1%) died and 9 (6.7%) underwent bilateral lung transplantation (LT), which was worse than in non‐smoker group (23.9% deaths and 5.4% bilateral LT, respectively). A median transplant‐free survival was significantly higher in non‐smokers compared to smokers in ILD cohort (p < 0.0353) (Figure 1B). Because smokers were significantly older and differed in sex distribution from non‐smokers (Table 2), multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to adjust for these potential confounders. Supporting our observations, our analysis identified cigarette smoking as a significant independent predictor of mortality after adjustment for age, sex, and presence of PH in the ILD cohort (Figure 1C). Taken together, this data demonstrate that, in UC Davis cohort, smoking is associated with worse transplant‐free survival in patients with ILD, which is in good agreement with previously published studies [10, 25], and is an independent predictor of all‐cause mortality.

Smoking Is Associated With Lower DLCO in Patients With IPF‐PH

In total, 89 patients in ILD cohort had PH (39.4%). Smokers with ILD‐PH trended to worsening transplant‐free survival (a median transplant‐free survival was 3.6 years); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1‐A). In the ILD‐PH cohort, DLCO showed a moderate positive correlation with FVC (r = 0.470, 95% CI 0.284 to 0.623, p < 0.0001) (Figure S1‐B), and in the multivariate analysis, FVC remained the only independent predictor of DLCO (β = 0.292, 95% CI 0.016–0.568, p = 0.039). We detected no significant differences in the RVSP (assessed by ECHO), mPAP, DLCO, mPAP, PAWP, cardiac output (CO), CI, or PVR between smokers and non‐smokers with ILD‐PH (Table 3). Analysis of the less heterogeneous IPF‐PH group revealed that smokers with IPF‐PH have significantly lower DLCO compared to non‐smokers (Figure 1D, Table 4). In the whole IPF‐PH cohort, DLCO was associated with both lung function and pulmonary vascular parameters, demonstrating moderate positive correlation with FVC (r = 0.551, 95% CI 0.246 to 0.756, p = 0.0009) (Figure S2‐A) and moderate‐to‐strong inverse correlation with PVR (r = −0.642, 95% CI −0.901 to −0.049, p = 0.039) (Figure S2‐B).

Smoking in Patients With IPF‐PH Is Associated With Molecular Changes in Whole Blood Transcriptome

To test whether smoking was associated with biological changes, we next performed whole blood RNA sequencing analysis. Transcriptomic analysis of the ILD cohort identified 170 DEGs in smokers compared to non‐smokers (adjusted p < 0.05, FC ≥ 1.5). Of these, 89 genes were up‐regulated, and 81 were down‐regulated (Figure S3). In the ILD‐PH group, 82 DEGs were significantly up‐ and 123 were significantly down‐regulated in smokers compared to non‐smokers (Figure S4). Interestingly, pathway analysis did not identify any significantly dysregulated molecular pathways/biological processes between smokers and non‐smokers with ILD or ILD‐PH. We reasoned that this may be attributed, at least in part, to the high patients heterogeneity within these groups. To overcome this limitation, we next evaluated IPF‐PH as the most homogenous group. Transcriptomic analysis of whole blood of IPF‐PH cohort identified 616 DEGs with 352 genes up‐ and 264 down‐regulated in smokers compared to non‐smokers (Figure 1E). The top 10 up‐ and down‐regulated DEGs are presented in Figure S5. Pathway analysis revealed three significantly dysregulated biological functions and two significantly dysregulated molecular functions in smokers with IPF‐PH (Figure 1F). Additionally, we attempted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis; however, no significantly enriched pathways were identified. Also, PCA analysis did not demonstrate clear separation between smokers and non‐smokers with IPF‐PH (Figure S6). To determine whether correlation exists between identified DEGs and clinical parameters, we next performed correlation analysis. We found moderate negative correlation between the levels of BBS1 (Bardet‐Biedl syndrome 1), BARD1 (BRCA1‐associated RING domain 1), and SMURF1 (Smad ubiquitination regulatory factor 1) and mPAP (r = −0.61, p = 0.046; r = −0.66, p = 0.028; r = −0.62, p = 0.041, respectively) (Figure 1G‐I). These data show that smoking is associated with changes in whole‐blood transcriptome of patients with IPF‐PH and suggest potential correlation between BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 levels and mPAP in IPF‐PH. We next conducted STRING‐based interactome analysis focusing on three DEGs of interest, BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 (Figure S7). Although no direct protein‐protein interactions were identified among these three candidates, indirect network connections were observed. Specifically, PTCH1 (Patched 1) was connected with both BBS1 and SMURF1, while UBE2D3 (Ubiquitin Conjugating Enzyme E2 D3) was connected with both SMURF1 and BARD1. These findings suggest that the candidate genes may be linked through broader regulatory networks rather than direct protein‐protein interactions.

Discussion

PH is a chronic progressive disorder with high mortality rates and limited treatment options [26]. Cigarette smoking is a risk factor for males with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [27] and promotes the development of experimental PH in mice [12]. Cigarette smoking is a well‐established factor contributing to the development and progression of several lung diseases, including several forms of ILD [28], and is associated with worse ILD outcomes [29]. However, smoking‐related changes in ILD‐PH are not well understood, and its clinical significance remains to be determined. In this study, we report that, in the UC Davis ILD cohort, smoking is an independent predictor of mortality. We also show that smoking is associated with decrease in DLCO in patients with IPF‐PH and identify dysregulated genes in blood of smokers with IPF‐PH, expression of which correlates with mPAP, suggesting its potential prognostic value in IPF‐PH cohort.

Compelling evidence links cigarette smoking with ILD and IPF, the most common and severe form of idiopathic ILD [28]. In agreement with previously published studies [10, 25], we found that, in the UC Davis ILD cohort, smoking is an independent predictor of all‐cause mortality that is associated with worse transplant‐free survival. Interestingly, we did not find significant clinical differences between smokers and non‐smokers with ILD‐PH. However, in the less heterogeneous IPF‐PH group, smoking was associated with significant reduction in DLCO. To note, decline in DLCO is associated with a higher risk of mortality and higher pulmonary vascular volume in patients with IPF [30, 31], and DLCO is an independent predictor of transplant‐free survival in patients with PH [32, 33]. Although further studies are needed, our findings link smoking with lower DLCO in patients with IPF‐PH and suggest that smoking could be considered as an independent contributing factor to the severity of IPF‐PH.

Cigarette smoking induces whole blood transcriptomic changes linked with dysregulated inflammatory, proliferative, and vascular responses [34, 35]. Changes in whole‐blood transcriptome in patients with PAH were associated with disease severity [36, 37, 38]. By using whole‐blood transcriptomic analysis of the UC Davis ILD cohort, we identified BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 as clinically relevant DEGs, up‐regulation of which in smokers with IPF‐PH negatively correlates with mPAP levels. Interestingly, BBS1 and SMURF1 act as negative regulator of inflammatory responses [39, 40, 41], and BARD1 is a growth suppressor that inhibits progression of various cancers [42]. Thus, up‐regulation of these DEGs in blood of patients with IPF‐PH could represent compensatory responses to smoking and can be considered for further studies as a biomarker of favorable prognosis in patients with IPF‐PH.

BBS1 has not been extensively studied in pulmonary vascular or fibrotic lung disease; however, it is a core component of the BBSome complex involved in ciliogenesis and cilia‐dependent signaling. Disruption of ciliary function may affect epithelial repair and signaling pathways relevant to tissue remodeling, including Hedgehog signaling, which has been implicated in fibrotic responses [43, 44]. BARD1, through its interaction with BRCA1 (Breast Cancer gene 1), participates in DNA damage responses and apoptosis. These processes are relevant to the smoking‐injured lung microenvironment, where cigarette smoke can induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, epithelial injury, and downstream remodeling responses [45, 46]. SMURF1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, regulates various signaling pathways, including Transforming Growth Factor‐beta (TGF‐β) and Bone Morphogenetic Protein (BMP) signaling, both of which are highly relevant to pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary vascular remodeling. Dysregulated TGF‐β signaling is associated with fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix deposition, and vascular remodeling in fibrotic lung disease and PH [47, 48].

Although the current dataset does not establish direct mechanistic interactions among BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1, the indirect STRING network connections suggest potential convergence on pathways related to epithelial dysfunction, stress responses, ubiquitin‐mediated protein regulation, and profibrotic signaling. For example, PTCH1, a key component of Hedgehog signaling, has been associated with airway epithelial remodeling and abnormal epithelial‐mesenchymal signaling [49]. In addition, ubiquitination pathways involving enzymes such as UBE2D3 may contribute to regulation of protein turnover and cellular stress responses [50]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the identified candidate DEGs may be biologically relevant to smoking‐associated ILD‐PH through pathways involving epithelial injury, DNA damage responses, cellular stress regulation, and dysregulated remodeling signals.

There are several limitations in our study. The first limitation is the limited number of patients with ILD‐PH included in this study. Next, it should be noted that whole‐blood bulk RNAseq approach derives transcripts from a mixed population of cells, and extended studies to dissect the roles of BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 in different immune cells from smokers and non‐smokers with IPF‐PH would be beneficial. In addition, careful analysis of identified DEGs in immune and somatic cells could be performed. As an example, overaccumulation of Smurf1 in pulmonary vasculature of rodents with monocrotaline‐ and hypoxia‐induced PH leads to down‐regulation of BMP receptors, which may induce proliferative response and pulmonary vascular remodeling [51], suggestive of its dual role in regulating mPAP in PH.

In conclusion, our study identifies cigarette smoking as an independent predictor of mortality in the UC Davis ILD cohort and suggests BBS1, BARD1, and SMURF1 as potential biomarkers of disease severity in patients with IPF‐PH. Further studies are required to elucidate the potential mechanistic links between cigarette consumption, immune cell gene expression changes, and the development or progression of ILD‐PH.
