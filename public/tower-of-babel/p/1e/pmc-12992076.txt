Apoptotic body-encapsulated zinc-doped Salvia miltiorrhiza carbon dots trigger PANoptosis for targeted therapy of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yi-qian Feng, Bi-jia Cui, Bing Shi, Xin-xiu Shi, Ang Li, Wei-long Zhong, Hui-juan Liu, Tao Sun, Heng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) stands as one of the most lethal cancers globally, with its treatment constrained by limitations of conventional therapies. In this study, we developed zinc-doped Salvia miltiorrhiza-derived carbon dots (Zn-SaCDs) and encapsulated them within apoptotic bodies (Zn-SaCDs@Ap) to induce targeted, broad-spectrum cell death in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In vitro experiments reveal that Zn-SaCDs@Ap effectively inhibits stemness properties and invasive potential while triggering robust apoptosis in HCC cells. Furthermore, studies on orthotopic liver tumors and subcutaneous tumors in mice showed that Zn-SaCDs@Ap can effectively inhibit tumor growth. It is noteworthy that Zn-SaCDs@Ap can induce various types of cell death, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis. Transcriptome analysis reveals that Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment markedly activates the expression of the C2H2 zinc finger family transcription factors (KLF7, SALL4, SNAI2, ZNF516), which enhance the expression of key proteins involved in PANoptosis regulation, such as AIM2 and NAIP. In conclusion, Zn-SaCDs@Ap can inhibit the progression of HCC though inducing PANoptosis of tumor cells. Zn-SaCDs@Ap demonstrates potential as a novel therapeutic agent for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, is widely occurring and resistant to conventional interventions. Exploring novel therapeutic agents capable of triggering broad apoptotic mechanisms is an important direction in the development of anticancer drugs [1]. In the development of therapeutic strategies targeting HCC, inducing PANoptosis in tumor cells offers a new perspective. PANoptosis is a form of inflammatory, multifaceted programmed cell death mediated by the PANoptosome complex [2]. PANoptosis not only involves traditional apoptotic processes but also encompasses various cell death pathways such as pyroptosis, necroptosis, collectively constituting a multimodal assault on tumor cells [3,4]. To date, multiple PANoptosome complexes [5] have been identified, including ZBP1 [6], AIM2 [6,7], and RIPK1 [8]. The shift in the mode of cell death represents a potent approach to effectively inhibit tumors [9]. A promising therapeutic approach involves using nanomaterials to induce pyroptosis of cancer cells, which showed the potential to overcome the limitations associated with traditional treatments [10].

In recent years, carbon dots have been recognized as novel carbon nanomaterials with excellent bioactivity and biocompatibility, which can be modified to impart unique tumor selectivity and intrinsic biological activity [11]. Demonstrating significant potential in cancer treatment, they encompass a variety of applications, including drug delivery, photothermal therapy, and tumor imaging. These offer new strategies for the targeted therapy of tumors [12,13].

The main active components of Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge have been reported to promote apoptosis of tumor cells [14], with recent data suggesting that its lipophilic compounds, especially Tanshinone, exhibit potent anticancer activity both in vitro and in vivo. The anticancer effects of hydrophilic phenolic acids have also been reported. Additionally, Tanshinone provides a structural framework for chemical modification, enabling a series of derivatives to be chemically modified [15]. There is currently limited research on the preparation of nanomaterials using active ingredients from Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge. Metal ions play fundamental roles in the essential components of most protein functions, participating in nearly all biological activities. Intracellular zinc (Zn), referred to as the “zinc wave", has the ability to maintain the stability of critical regulatory proteins in neurofibromas [16]. Furthermore, Zn plays a crucial role in many physiological processes, and its deprivation or supplementation can affect the regulation of cellular protection and apoptosis [17]. In recent years, the lysosomal zinc-induced cell death mechanism has garnered significant attention. Studies have shown that, in rapidly proliferating cancer cells, lysosomal function is often upregulated to meet their increased energy demands. The lysosomal membrane protein TRPML1 is a cation channel with dual permeability to both calcium (Ca2+) and zinc (Zn2+) ions, regulating the ion concentrations within the lysosome. While Zn2+ is an indispensable trace element in cellular physiology, its excessive release can trigger a series of lethal effects. The accumulation of Zn2+ in cells disrupts mitochondrial function, leading to the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP synthesis, and ultimately, cellular energy depletion, which triggers cell death [18]. These findings suggest that modulating the zinc ion content in the tumor microenvironment may play a beneficial role in cancer therapy.

This work developed apoptotic body-encapsulated zinc-doped Salvia miltiorrhiza carbon dots (Zn-SaCDs@Ap), which can induce tumor cell PANoptosis, thereby providing a promising therapeutic strategy for HCC treatment. The utilization of Salvianolic acid-riched Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge extract [19,20] facilitates the creation of zinc doped carbon dots (Zn-SaCDs) through chelation with zinc. As a natural encapsulant for nanomedicines, apoptotic bodies from liver cells possess inherent targeting effects on tumor cells, thus avoiding the common issue of damage to normal healthy cells associated with traditional cancer treatments. Interestingly, we found that Zn-SaCDs@Ap can induce the PANoptosis of HCC cells. Zn-SaCDs@Ap demonstrates the ability to initiate a comprehensive death signaling network within cells, activating the expression of C2H2 family zinc finger transcription factors. ChIP-seq analysis revealed that, Zn-SaCDs@Ap was found to activate downstream transcription factors of the C2H2 family, including KLF7, SALL4, and SNAI2, thereby regulating key PANoptosis-related proteins such as AIM2, MEFV, and NLRP1. This study showed the potential of Zn-SaCDs@Ap to induce PANoptosis of HCC cells.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of apoptotic body-encapsulated zinc-doped Salvia miltiorrhiza carbon dots

We firstly synthesized the metal ion-doped Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge based carbon dots (SaCDs) (Fig. 1A). Various metal ions, such as iron, copper, zinc, and magnesium, have been shown to possess the ability to induce cell death. Copper ions have been demonstrated to induce “cuproptosis" by catalyzing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disrupting the function of iron-sulfur cluster proteins [21]. Similarly, iron ions can trigger “ferroptosis" by promoting lipid peroxidation and iron-dependent apoptotic pathways [22]. Zinc ions are known to regulate lysosomal function, and excessive zinc can induce lysosome-associated cell death (lysosomal cell death) [18]. Magnesium ions, on the other hand, exhibit potential in modulating cell growth in the tumor microenvironment by influencing cellular metabolism. Considering the rich content of active polyphenolic compounds such as Salvianolic acid in Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge extract [23], we hypothesized that functional groups would enhance the anticancer activity of the SaCDs through chelation with metal ions [24]. When Salvianolic acid-rich compounds encounter metal ions, the oxygen atoms of its hydroxyl group can provide lone pair electrons to form coordination bonds with the empty orbitals of the metal ions, stabilizing the metal ions and forming larger-membered chelation complexes [25]. The affinity of Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge extract with different metal ions and the antitumor activity of SaCDs doped with different metals varies. Based on this, we synthesized zinc doped carbon dots (Zn-SaCDs), iron doped carbon dots (Fe-SaCDs), copper doped carbon dots (Cu-SaCDs), and magnesium doped carbon dots (Mg-SaCDs). Following the assessment of cell viability, Zn-SaCDs demonstrated the most potent cytotoxic effect on Hep3B hepatocellular carcinoma cells (Fig. 1B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals that Zn-SaCDs exhibits a spherical shape with a uniform size distribution (Fig. 1C), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) indicates a diameter of approximately 10 nm for Zn-SaCDs (Fig. 1D). The structure and lattice of the carbon dots were revealed by Field-Emission High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (Fig. 1E), proving that the carbon dot is a homogeneous spherical structure within 10 nm. The image shows the lattice structure of the nanoparticles, with clearly visible lattice spacing, indicating that these particles possess high crystallinity. And the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) experiment shows the elemental composition (Fig. 1F) on the surface of the carbon dot. The Zn 2p peak typically appears within the binding energy range of 1020-1040 eV, with the Zn (2p3/2) and Zn (2p1/2) peaks corresponding to different chemical states of zinc. Based on this information, it can be determined that the surface of Zn-SaCDs mainly contains Zn, C, O, and possibly a small amount of N. To assess the stability of Zn-SaCDs, we placed it in PBS at 37 °C, for 24 h, the particle size remained uniform and stable (Fig. 1G). The UV absorbance of Zn-SaCDs indicates strong absorption across the 200–600 nm wavelength range (Fig. 1H). Infrared spectroscopy (IR) reveals significant changes in multiple functional groups following the formation of carbon dots (Fig. 1I and J). The IR spectra highlight not only the coordination of primary and secondary alcohols with Zn2+ but also a reduction in hydroxyl groups and an increase in aldehyde groups, further corroborating the successful formation of carbon dots.

To enhance the targeting specificity of Zn-SaCDs, we synthesized Zn-SaCDs@Ap coated with apoptotic body membrane. First, apoptotic bodies were prepared by inducing apoptosis in normal liver cells (THLE-2) using staurosporine for 4 h, followed by drug withdrawal and subsequent addition of Zn-SaCDs [26]. After 12 h of incubation, the supernatant containing apoptotic bodies was collected (Fig. 1K). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that apoptotic bodies encapsulated Zn-SaCDs (Zn-SaCDs@Ap) exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with even distribution (Fig. 1L). The particle size of Zn-SaCDs@Ap reached approximately 500 nm after encapsulation within apoptotic bodies (Fig. 1M), consistent with the reported size of apoptotic bodies. To evaluate the encapsulation of Zn-SaCDs within apoptotic bodies (Zn-SaCDs@Ap), staining of apoptotic body membranes and zinc ions was performed. The results showed a high degree of colocalization between apoptotic bodies and zinc ions, further confirming that Zn-SaCDs were effectively encapsulated within the apoptotic bodies (Fig. 1N). Through atomic absorption spectroscopy analysis (AAS), we detected that the proportion of Zn ions in Zn-SaCDs was approximately 1.036%. Then,Zn-SaCDs were added to 1.3 × 107 cells to induce the formation of apoptotic bodies, yielding approximately 3 × 107 apoptotic bodies. After collecting the apoptotic bodies, they were resuspended in 1 mL of solution and sonicated. The Zn concentration was determined using AAS. Using non-drug-induced apoptotic bodies as the baseline control, the Zn content derived from Zn-SaCDs was calculated to be 0.1932 μg/mL. Moreover, Zn-SaCDs@Ap and Zn-SaCDs were used to treat Hep3B cells, and their targeting specificity was compared. Live cell imaging experiments showed that Zn-SaCDs@Ap significantly accumulated in liver cancer cells after 12 h (Fig. 1O), whereas Zn-SaCDs without encapsulation in apoptotic bodies did not exhibit significant intracellular aggregation. This indicates that encapsulation within apoptotic body membranes enhances the targeting uptake of Zn-SaCDs@Ap. The intracellular uptake of Zn-SaCDs@Ap reached 73.2% (Fig. 1P). Further, using the CCK8 assay, we demonstrated that Zn-SaCDs@Ap caused minor damage to normal liver cells (THLE-2) (Fig. S1A).

Zn-SaCDs@Ap suppresses malignant behavior and induces apoptosis of HCC cells

To evaluate the impact of Zn-SaCDs@Ap on HCC cell invasion, migration, and stemness, we employed Hep3B cells as the principal model system. All findings were validated in Hepa1-6 cells to ensure generalizability. Zn-SaCDs@Ap inhibited the invasive and migration capability of both Hep3B and Hepa1-6 cells, as shown by a gelatin-based invasion assay (Fig. 2A) and wound-healing assay (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2A). Inducing cellular apoptosis is considered a crucial mechanism to inhibit tumor growth. Annexin V-PI staining was used to evaluate the effect of Zn-SaCDs@Ap on cell apoptosis (Fig. 2D), and the results showed that Zn-SaCDs@Ap significantly increased the overall apoptosis rate in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E). To further validate its pro-apoptotic effect, we performed the TUNEL assay and Caspase-3 activity detection. The results showed that Zn-SaCDs@Ap dose-dependently increased the number of TUNEL-positive cells (Fig. 2F) and elevated Caspase-3 activity (Fig. 2G). Together with the flow cytometry data, these findings collectively demonstrate that Zn-SaCDs@Ap can induce apoptosis of HCC cells. In addition, in the key in vitro experiments, we included a separate treatment group with an equimolar concentration of zinc ions, where the zinc content was exactly matched to the total zinc content present in the high-dose Zn-SaCDs@Ap group (62 ng/mL). The results showed that, compared with the Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment group, the equivalent dose of zinc ions alone did not exhibit a cell-inhibitory trend, nor did it induce a comparable level of apoptosis or significantly suppress cell migration and invasion. This control experiment strongly demonstrates that the pronounced antitumor activity of Zn-SaCDs@Ap is primarily attributable to its integrated nanostructure and specificity, rather than being driven solely by zinc ion release.

Notably, scanning electron microscopy revealed various forms of cell death in tumor cells after Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment (Fig. 2H). Some images show apoptotic phenomena, while others display evident necrotic cell death. However, the specific mechanism of cell death and how the drug induces this process require a more detailed molecular elucidation. We speculate that this acute death of tumor cells may induce an innate immune defense response, leading to the generation of inflammasomes. Upon the addition of apoptotic body-encapsulated Zn-SaCDs, we observed a significant increase in caspase-1 expression in Hep3B (Fig. S2B), along with enhanced release of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. S2C and D), which resulted in tumor cell death (Fig. S2E). This phenomenon aligns with the characteristics of pyroptosis [23], suggesting that Zn-SaCDs@Ap induces pyroptosis in tumor cells. Further staining the cells with propidium iodide (PI), we found that most apoptotic tumor cells exhibit significant aggregation of zinc ions (Fig. 2I). The experimental results indicate that Zn-SaCD@Ap accumulates in dead cells, which may be a major factor in inducing tumor cell death. This further confirms the therapeutic potential of apoptotic body-encapsulated nanodrugs for tumor treatment.

To assess the stability of apoptotic body-encapsulated nanomedicine in peripheral blood, we co-incubated Zn-SaCDs@Ap with red blood cells at 37 °C. Observation of red blood cell morphology at 30 min and 90 min revealed no apparent damage (Fig. S2F), maintaining a discoid shape. This suggests that Zn-SaCDs@Ap causes minimal damage to red blood cell (Fig. 2J). The concentration of Zn in the serum co-culture solution remained consistently stable, indicating the stable encapsulation of apoptotic bodies (Fig. 2K), with no leakage of nanoparticles. Upon re-separation from the serum, Zn-SaCDs@Ap particles maintained uniform size and distribution, as observed under scanning transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. S2F). Additionally, we also evaluated the stability of the nanomaterial under FBS conditions at 0 h, 12 h, and 24 h (Fig. S2G). Zn-SaCDs@Ap was incubated in FBS and sampled at 0, 12, and 24 h for dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis of particle size and polydispersity index (PDI). Particle size changed minimally over time, and the PDI remained between 0.15 and 0.21, indicating good stability in FBS (Fig. S2H). Furthermore, we reintroduced the separated Zn-SaCDs@Ap into tumor cells to verify its activity, and the experimental results showed that the reintroduced Zn-SaCDs@Ap still induced tumor cells death (Fig. 2L).

The above experiments indicated that Zn-SaCDs@Ap exhibits a significant cytotoxic effect on tumor cells and maintains good stability in peripheral blood. Additionally, the observation of various forms of apoptosis in tumor cells after Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment suggests that Zn-SaCDs@Ap may inhibit the malignant progression of HCC by inducing multiple cell death modes.

Zn-SaCDs@Ap inhibits tumor growth and promotes necroptosis in tumor tissues

In this study, transplanted tumor model and in situ tumor model in BALb/c mice were used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of Zn-SaCDs@Ap (Fig. 3A). After Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment, live animal imaging was performed to monitor tumor progression in transplanted tumor model (Fig. 3B) and in situ tumor model (Fig. 3C). Zn-SaCDs@Ap significantly inhibited tumor progression compared with Ctrl, as evidenced by slower tumor volume growth in the transplanted tumor model (Fig. S3A) and a time-dependent decrease in in vivo fluorescence intensity in in situ tumor model (Fig. S3D). Fluorescence intensity analysis showed that, after Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment, the fluorescence signal from the tumor cells in both transplanted tumors (Fig. 3D) and liver in situ tumors (Fig. 3E) significantly decreased. In addition, further examination of the lung and liver tissues from the treated mice was conducted. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining provided a histological perspective on the therapeutic effects of Zn-SaCDs@Ap. Compared to the control group, mice treated with Zn-SaCDs@Ap showed a significant reduction in cell density, decreased cell atypia, and weakened tumor invasiveness in liver in situ tumors. These changes were characterized by normalized nuclear size and shape and clearer cell boundaries. In liver tissues, treated mice maintained intact hepatic lobule structures, with more organized hepatocyte arrangement and clear sinusoidal structures. GPC3 and AFP staining further confirmed that Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment significantly reduced the expression of these tumor markers, indicating a correlation with the reduced malignancy of the tumors. These changes suggest that Zn-SaCDs@Ap may help alleviate tumor-induced liver pathological alterations (Fig. 3G).

Additionally, in the H&E staining of transplanted tumors, we observed an increase in necrotic and apoptotic areas, suggesting enhanced apoptosis and necrosis activity in Zn-SaCDs@Ap (Fig. 3H). For liver, kidney, and lung tissues, H&E staining showed no significant pathological changes (Fig. S3B and S3C), indicating that Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment did not cause significant pathological changes in organs. An increase in necrotic areas was also observed in in situ tumors (Fig. 3I). H&E staining of lung and kidney tissues showed no significant pathological changes. However, liver injury in mice treated with Zn-SaCDs@Ap was significantly reduced compared to the control group (Fig. S3E and S3F). This suggests that Zn-SaCDs@Ap is capable of effectively treating in situ tumors in mice to some extent.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of tumor tissues showed a significant increase in necrotic areas, along with upregulation of necrosis-apoptosis-related proteins, MLKL and RIPK3, after treatment with the Zn-SaCDs@Ap (Fig. 3J). Moreover, Immunofluorescence staining of tissues revealed a significant reduction in the proliferative marker Ki67 expression, while cleaved Caspase-8, an apoptosis-related marker, was significantly increased (Fig. 3K and L). These findings provide evidence for the significant anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects of Zn-SaCDs@Ap in vivo.

Zn-SaCDs@Ap suppresses malignancy of HCC by inducing PANoptosis

Previous studies suggest that Zn-SaCDs@Ap may have the ability to induce pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis in tumor cells. Recently, the concept of PANoptosis has emerged, highlighting extensive crosstalk among these pathways and establishing a new paradigm where multiple cell death mechanisms are simultaneously activated. We further validated the activation of pyroptosis-related proteins in tumor cells treated with Zn-SaCDs@Ap (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis revealed a significant activation of Caspase-1 in Hep3B cells, with increased expression of its cleaved form, subsequently leading to the activation of downstream effector molecules such as GSDMD and GSDME (Fig. 4B). IHC results demonstrated that Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment markedly upregulated the overall expression levels of pyroptosis-related proteins, including Caspase-1, GSDMD, and GSDME in the tumor tissues (Fig. 4C and Fig. S4A). Through Western blot analysis of three distinct tumor tissue samples, we validated the cleavage and activation of these proteins, confirming the role of Zn-SaCDs@Ap in inducing pyroptosis within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 4D). Discussion

In the past several years, nanomaterials have achieved remarkable progress in cancer-targeted therapy. In the context of tumor immunotherapy, apolipoprotein E (ApoE)-modified nanoparticles have been shown to deliver immunomodulators with high specificity, thereby enhancing local drug accumulation and potentiating antitumor immune responses [27]. Similarly, L-arginine-loaded CaP-stabilized iodized oil Pickering emulsions can neutralize the acidic tumor microenvironment, leading to strengthened immune responses [28]. In addition, calcium-based thermosensitive agents (CBTs) have shown considerable potential within the tumor microenvironment; under acidic conditions, these materials release CO2 bubbles, generating a sustained cavitation effect that enhances the thermal efficacy of radiofrequency ablation (RFA) [29]. Moreover, iron single-atom nanozymes have been reported to remodel macrophage epigenetics, thereby overcoming tumor-induced immunosuppression and augmenting antitumor immunity [30]. Zinc (Zn), as an essential trace element, also plays a crucial role in cellular physiology. Inspired by the above studies, we selected zinc as the functional core to construct the Zn-SaCDs@Ap delivery system. Experimental results demonstrate that Zn-SaCDs@Ap achieves efficient targeting of tumor cells and effectively inhibits the proliferation and invasion of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). While free Zn2+ at an equivalent concentration remains within a physiologically safe range and fails to induce cytotoxicity through passive diffusion, our delivery system leverages the active targeting and endocytic pathways of apoptotic bodies to forcibly enrich the therapeutic cargo within the confined intracellular environment. This design—characterized by low systemic toxicity and high intracellular responsiveness—achieves a localized concentration explosion that synergizes with Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge-derived carbon dots to trigger PANoptosis. Consequently, this platform provides a novel and precise therapeutic strategy for HCC treatment by achieving potent anti-tumor efficacy at an ultra-low dosage.

Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge, a traditional Chinese medicinal plant, is known for its broad pharmacological effects, with active components such as tanshinone and salvianolic acids. Studies have shown that Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge exhibits multiple pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, and anti-thrombotic effects. It is widely used in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, liver diseases, and tumors. Through mechanisms such as improving blood circulation, protecting liver function, inhibiting tumor cell proliferation, and promoting apoptosis, Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge demonstrates therapeutic potential in various diseases. In this study, we prepared carbon dots based on Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge, chelated with zinc ions, and enhanced their tumor-targeting capability by encapsulating them with apoptotic bodies. Apoptotic bodies, as natural cell membrane encapsulation structures, possess excellent biocompatibility and targeted delivery capabilities. This research also demonstrates that apoptotic bodies, as natural cell membrane encapsulation structures, possess excellent biocompatibility and targeted delivery capabilities.

The most remarkable aspect is the role of Zn-SaCDs@Ap on the PANoptosis pathway. PANoptosis is a comprehensive cell death mechanism involving various types of cell death, including known and yet-to-be-defined pathways [4]. The action of Zn-SaCDs@Ap involves the synergistic activation of these diverse death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, which are crucial for overcoming cancer cell tolerance to specific death pathways [25,31]. This discovery provides a new perspective on the role of Zn-SaCDs@Ap in overcoming drug resistance in HCC treatment.

Furthermore, our study also reveals the potential effects of Zn-SaCDs@Ap-induced cell death on triggering inflammatory responses. Research suggests that PANoptosis is a unique innate immune-mediated inflammatory pathway. PANoptosis may be implicated in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) by modulating IFN and JAK-STAT pathways in neutrophils, memory B cells, CD8+ T cells, etc. [32]. The upregulation of inflammatory markers after Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment may reflect the activation of anti-tumor immune responses [33,34], which holds significant implications in cancer therapy. Therefore, the immunomodulatory potential of Zn-SaCDs@Ap is worth further exploration, especially in conjunction with immunotherapeutic strategies. The induction of various forms of cell death by Zn-SaCDs@Ap, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, aligns with the emerging concept of PANoptosis. The induction of this broad-spectrum cell death is particularly promising, considering the resistance of cancer cells to apoptosis. It also indicates the potential of Zn-SaCDs@Ap to overcome drug resistance, a significant barrier to effective HCC treatment.

However, while our findings are promising, they also underscore the need for a deeper understanding of the complex molecular mechanisms behind the multi-modal actions of Zn-SaCDs@Ap. Additionally, given the complexity of HCC and cancer, future research should also focus on the potential non-target effects and safety of this nanomedicine in long-term treatment scenarios.

In conclusion, this work indicated that Zn-SaCDs@Ap is capable of inhibiting tumor progression by inducing PANoptosis of liver cancer cells, which may be a promising candidate for HCC treatment.

Materials and methods

Preparation of various kinds of CDs

First, add 3 g of Salvia miltiorrhiza bunge dissolved in 10 mL of PEG-200 to a 50 mL beaker. To the mixed solution, add 1 mL of ZnCl2 (1 mol/L). Stir well and then add 10 mL of NaOH (1 mol/L), followed by 10 mL of HNO3 (1 mol/L). Stir until no gas is produced, and the solution turns into a bright reddish-brown color, indicating successful synthesis. Place the mixture and the beaker into a high-temperature reaction vessel and heat at 150 °C for 12 h. This process yields a dark brown carbon dot liquid. Filter the obtained carbon dot liquid through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove microorganisms. Zn-SaCDs is then purified by dialysis (MWCO = 1000) against ultrapure water. The synthesis of SaCDs@Mn, SaCDs@Fe, or SaCDs@Cu follows similar procedures. For the synthesis of SaCDs doped with Mn, Fe, or Cu, ZnCl2 was replaced with MnCl2 (1 mol/L), FeCl3 (1 mol/L), or CuSO4 (1 mol/L), respectively.

Characterization of SaCDs

The particle hydrodynamic diameter of Zn-SaCDs and Zn-SaCDs@Ap was determined using the Zetasizer Nano ZS system at 25 °C (Malvern Instruments, Marvin City, UK). The morphology and size of Zn-SaCDs and Zn-SaCDs@Ap were studied using TEM (Alos F200C, UK). SEM (S-3500N, HITACHI, Japanese) was employed to investigate the morphology and size of apoptotic bodies encapsulating Zn-SaCDs@Ap. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum of Zn-SaCDs was recorded on a Nicolet iS5 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectrum was acquired using a UV-3600i Plus spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan).

Cell culture

The Hepa1-6, H22-luc, THLE-2 and Hep3B human HCC cell lines were procured from Nanjing Keygen Biotech. H22-luc cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Keygen Biotech), THLE-2 cells were maintained in BEGM kit medium (Lonza Clonetics), while Hep3B and Hepa1-6 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Keygen Biotech), both supplemented with 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Mycoplasma testing was conducted on the cell lines prior to use, and comprehensive cell identification was provided by Cellcook or KeyGen Biotech.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as follows. Cells were washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore). After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Caspase-1 (Santa Cruz, USA), GSDMD (Zenbio, China), GSDME (Zenbio, China), Caspase-8 (Santa Cruz), Caspase-3 (Santa Cruz), Caspase-7 (Santa Cruz, USA), pMLKL (Zenbio, China), tMLKL (Zenbio, China), pRIPK3 (Zenbio, China), and tRIPK3 (Zenbio, China). Following this, the membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (Zenbio, China).

Colony formation assay

Hep3B and Hepa1-6 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were trypsinized, counted, and seeded at a density of 1000 cells per well in 6-well plates (Corning). The cells were cultured under standard conditions for 14 days, with the medium being refreshed every 3 days. After the incubation period, the medium was aspirated. The cells were then washed gently three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 30 min at room temperature. Following staining, the plates were rinsed thoroughly under running tap water to remove excess dye and air-dried overnight. Colonies were photographed using a digital scanner. The number of colonies in each well was counted manually or by using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). Experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated three times independently.

Flow cytometry

To assess apoptosis of Hep3B and Hepa1-6 cells treated with Zn-SaCDs@Ap, a fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) double-staining analysis were conducted using the Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Following the respective treatments, the cells were harvested and counted using a Petroff-Hausser cell counting chamber. A total of 1 × 105 cells were suspended in binding buffer provided by the commercial kit. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and PI (5 μL) were added to the cell suspension, followed by a 15 min incubation at room temperature. Flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, Moflo XDP, USA) was employed to measure the apoptosis ratio. Cells (3 × 103) with similar side scatter and forward scatter levels were analyzed using Flowing Software2 (developed by Perttu Terho, Turku, Finland). Annexin V-negative and PI-negative cells were classified as viable.

Caspase-3 activity assay

Caspase-3 activity was measured using a colorimetric assay kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Following treatment, cells were lysed and the protein concentration of the supernatant was quantified. Equal amounts of protein were incubated with the Caspase-3 substrate Ac-DEVD-pNA at 37 °C for 2 h. Measure the absorbance at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis was detected in situ using a Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) assay kit. After treatment, cells grown on coverslips were fixed, permeabilized, and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture to label DNA strand breaks. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using fluorescence microscopy, and the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells were calculated from multiple random fields.

Gelatin degradation assay

Porcine skin gelatin (Thermo Fisher, Cat#G13187) was diluted in 2 % sucrose in PBS, resulting in a final gelatin concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. The working solution, safeguarded from light, was heated to 60 °C to ensure uniform coverage on the slides. After drying, 1 mL of pre-cooled glutaraldehyde solution was applied, and the solution was incubated on ice for 15 min. Coverslips were then washed three times with PBS at room temperature. Subsequently, 1 mL of freshly prepared sodium borohydride solution was added, and the solution was incubated at room temperature for 3 min. Following cell inoculation and a 24 h culture period, the cells were fixed and stained for F-Actin using YF 633-Phalloidin (US Everbright, Cat#YP0053S). Finally, the slides were mounted with DAPI and analyzed using a laser confocal microscope (LSM 800 with Airyscan).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and tissue immunofluorescence staining

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was conducted using a two-step protocol, following established procedures. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated in graded ethanol, treated with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citric acid at pH 6.0. Subsequently, the tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Caspase-1, GSDMD, GSDME, Caspase-8, Caspase-3, Caspase-7, MLKL, and RIPK3 (Zenbio, China). This was followed by labeling with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (rabbit) (Thermo Scientific, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were developed using DAB solution (Gene Tech, Shanghai, China) under microscopic observation and counterstained with hematoxylin.

ELISA assay

The concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α in the cell supernatant and the serum IL-6 and TNF-α levels in mice were quantified employing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits sourced (Proteintech, China). The measurements were carried out following the provided manufacturer's protocols, allowing for a comprehensive assessment of the inflammatory cytokine profiles both in vitro and in vivo.

In vivo subcutaneous transplantation model and orthotopic implantation model

In the subcutaneous transplantation tumor model, 4-6 weeks old female BALB/c mice were used to establish a liver tumor model and observe tumor metastasis. H22-luc cells were suspended in 100 μL of PBS and injected into the subcutis of mice.

The in vivo orthotopic implantation model, female BALB/c mice aged 4-6 weeks were utilized to establish a liver tumor model and observe tumor metastasis. The H22-Luc cells, fluorescently labeled, were suspended and orthotopically injected into the left liver lobe of mice. Bioluminescence imaging studies in mice were conducted using the IVIS imaging system (Perkin Elmer). D-Luciferin potassium (150 μg/g, Meilunstar, Cat#115144-359) was intraperitoneally injected, and mice were anesthetized when the peak fluorescein uptake was reached 10 min after injection. Subsequently, imaging was performed, and bioluminescent intensity was calculated using Living Image software. For the Zn-SaCDs@Ap treatment experiment, the mice with orthotopically implanted liver tumors were randomly assigned to three groups: the control group, the Zn-SaCDs@Ap-L group, and the Zn-SaCDs@Ap-H group. The treatment was administered continuously for 2 weeks, and concomitantly, tumor growth in mice was monitored using the in vivo imaging system. Finally, the livers of mice in each group were collected for subsequent IHC experiments.

PDX mouse model

Eight freshly resected tumor tissues (F0, HCC) from 8 patients undergoing surgery at Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital was collected immediately after the operation, with written informed consent obtained from each patient. In a sterile environment, the tumor tissue was sectioned into pieces and subcutaneously implanted in the left axilla of NOD/SCID mice. These mice, at the transplantation stage, were designated as F1 mice. Tumor nodules were identified by palpating the skin at the tumor site, and tumor volumes were measured upon reaching 100-200 mm3. Once the F1 tumor reached the specified volume, it was dissected into smaller fragments for in vivo passage to establish F2 and F3 tumors. Subsequently, when the F3 PDX model reached a tumor volume of 100-200 mm3, mice harboring various tumor types were randomly allocated into control, Zn-SaCDs@Ap-L, and Zn-SaCDs@Ap-H groups, each consisting of 6 mice. Following a two-week treatment period, the tumors of mice in each group were harvested for histopathological (HE) and immunohistochemical (IHC) experiments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing GraphPad Prism version 8 for Windows or R 3.6.0. Significance testing involved Student's t-test, one-way ANOVA, Pearson's correlation, and Kaplan–Meier as applicable. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.
