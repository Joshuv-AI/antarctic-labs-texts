Microglial CLEC7A restrains amyloid beta plaque pathology in a mouse model of Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Aman Mangalmurti, Kristine E. Zengeler, Ava Hollis, Emily Golden, Gabrielle Riddlemoser, John R. Lukens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCLEC7A is a surface receptor that is highly upregulated on microglia in many Alzheimer's disease (AD) models. Little is known about the role that microglial CLEC7A signaling plays in AD‐related pathogenesis.METHODSWe utilized an inducible, central nervous system (CNS) macrophage‐specific knockout of Clec7a to evaluate the role of CLEC7A in the 5xFAD mouse model of AD at 5 months of age. We used immunofluorescence microscopy, single‐nuclei RNA sequencing, along with biochemical assays, to evaluate plaque burden, microglial activity, and neuronal health.RESULTSCNS macrophage‐targeted deletion of CLEC7A in 5xFAD mice led to a twofold increase in plaque burden, exacerbated neuritic dystrophy, and altered the expression of neuronal health genes, but did not appreciably impact microglial activation, plaque engulfment, or disease‐associated microglia acquisition.DISCUSSIONThese findings identify protective roles for CLEC7A in AD‐related amyloidosis and suggest that CLEC7A‐targeting therapeutics may offer promising strategies for treatment of AD.Highlights Conditional loss of CLEC7A in central nervous system (CNS) macrophages of 5xFAD mice results in increased amyloid beta deposition.Loss of CLEC7A does not alter the disease‐associated microglia transcriptional program or affect the recruitment of microglia to plaque surfaces.Exacerbation of amyloid deposition with loss of CNS‐macrophage CLEC7A is associated with worsened neuronal health highlighted by increased neuritic dystrophy.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative condition characterized by progressive memory loss and the hallmark pathologies of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaque accumulation and tau protein inclusions. AD is the most common cause of dementia in the United States, affecting 7.2 million individuals 65 years of age or older. The prevalence of AD in the United States is expected to nearly double by 2060, leading to increased strain on health care systems and caregivers. 1 The limited efficacy of current AD therapies necessitates further research into mechanisms of disease to generate new treatments.

Microglia, which are tissue resident, parenchymal macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS), are strongly implicated in the pathogenesis of AD and related dementias. Microglia actively surveille the brain parenchyma and respond to neurodegeneration through the recognition of misfolded proteins, phagocytosis of debris, and the orchestration of immune responses via chemokine/cytokine release. 2 , 3 Microglia are thought to play a protective role early in the response to AD‐related amyloidosis 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 through Aβ phagocytosis, plaque recognition, and the formation of barrier surfaces around plaques that protect neighboring cells. 9 Microglia also play a part in plaque seeding and formation through compaction and deposition of Aβ. 10 , 11

When responding to Aβ plaques, microglia adopt a transcriptional program termed the disease‐associated microglia (DAM) phenotype, which involves alterations in the expression of innate immune and metabolic genes. 12 , 13 Aspects of this program have been shown to be protective in AD models. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The transition to DAM follows a stereotyped, progressive pattern of gene expression: from homeostatic microglia to an intermediate DAM 1 stage, and finally to the DAM 2 stage, which is dependent on the cell‐surface receptor triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2). Features of the DAM transcriptional phenotype have also been exhibited by microglia in models of tauopathy, 18 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), 19 Parkinson's disease, 20 and multiple sclerosis. 21 This pattern points to a conserved transcriptional response of microglia to dyshomeostasis and neuropathology in the CNS. Among the most highly upregulated DAM genes is Clec7a, which has been used as a marker for this transcriptional transition. 21 Despite its recognition as a core DAM signature gene, the precise role that Clec7a plays in coordinating the microglial response to AD‐related neuropathology remains poorly characterized.

Clec7a is highly expressed by myeloid cells and encodes for the cell surface receptor Dectin‐1/C‐type lectin domain family 7 member A (referred to hereafter as CLEC7A). Extensive work has identified CLEC7A as playing a key role in the defense against fungal pathogens. 22 , 23 Upon recognition of fungal β‐glucans, homodimers of CLEC7A are phosphorylated at hemi‐immunoreceptor tyrosine activation motifs (ITAMs), allowing for the recruitment of spleen tyrosinase kinase (SYK) and downstream signaling leading to reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, phagocytosis, and cytokine expression. 24 , 25 Our lab 26 , 27 and others 28 , 29 have shown that loss of signaling molecules downstream of ITAM‐associated receptors (i.e., SYK, CARD9) blunts microglial responses and leads to exacerbated AD‐related amyloidosis. Moreover, loss of function of the ITAM‐associated receptor TREM2 similarly exacerbates disease in models of Aβ pathology and has been linked to risk for late‐onset AD in humans. 30 , 31 How targeted deletion of CLEC7A in brain macrophages impacts amyloidosis has not been investigated in detail to date.

Thus, we sought to clarify the role of microglial CLEC7A in the response to Aβ pathology. To this end, we generated Clec7a fl/fl mice to enable inducible deletion of CLEC7A in CNS‐resident macrophages. We then crossed these mice to the 5xFAD model of AD‐related amyloidosis to interrogate the contribution of microglial CLEC7A signaling in the context of Aβ‐driven pathology. We found that loss of Clec7a in CNS macrophages leads to worsened Aβ disease, resulting in increased plaque burden and compromised neuronal health. These findings highlight the critical impact of CLEC7A signaling on AD‐related amyloidosis.

METHODS

Mice

All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the University of Virginia and approved by the University of Virginia Animal Care and Use Committee. 5xFAD (Strain #034848‐JAX) 32 and Cx3cr1 CreERT2 knock‐in/knock‐out (Strain #021160) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Clec7a fl/fl mice were generated in collaboration with Cyagen Biosciences. In short, a guide RNA (gRNA) to the Clec7a gene, a donor vector containing loxP sites flanking exons 3 and 4 of the Clec7a gene, and Cas9 messenger RNA (mRNA) were co‐injected into a fertilized mouse egg to generate targeted conditional knockout offspring. F0 found animals were bred to C57Bl/6J mice to test for germline transmission and to generate F1 animals. 5xFAD, Cx3cr1 CreERT2, and Clec7a fl/fl animals were crossed to generate 5xFAD‐/‐ Clec7a fl/fl Cx3cr1 +/+ (denoted Clec7aWT), 5xFAD‐/‐ Clec7a fl/fl Cx3cr1 CreERT2/+ (denoted Clec7aΔMG), 5xFAD+/‐ Clec7a fl/fl Cx3cr1 +/+ (denoted 5xClec7aWT), and 5xFAD+/‐ Clec7a fl/fl Cx3cr1 CreERT2/+ (denoted 5xClec7aΔMG). Recent evidence showed that the parental origin of the 5xFAD transgene impacts Aβ burden in offspring, 33 and thus all mice inherited the 5xFAD transgene from the male parent. Female mice were used in all studies. To induce Cre recombinase–mediated excision of loxP‐flanked DNA sequences, mice were fed tamoxifen chow from 4–6 weeks of age following weaning. Mice were then returned to standard chow for the remainder of the study. Mice were housed with food and water available ad libitum under specific pathogen‐free conditions with controlled temperature (21 ± 1.5°C) and humidity (50 ± 10%) and subjected to the standard 12‐h light/dark cycle.

Brain sample preparation

Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation, followed by cervical dislocation, and then were transcardially perfused with 20 mL of 1x Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline without calcium and magnesium (1x DPBS; Gibco #14190144). The brain was bisected and the right hemisphere was snap frozen and stored at −80°C, whereas the left hemisphere was drop fixed for 24 h in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Electron Microscopy Sciences #15714‐S) at 4°C and then switched to 30% sucrose in 1x DPBS to dehydrate for 48 h. Brains were stored in 30% sucrose until sectioning. Dehydrated brains were submerged in Tissue‐Tek OCT compound (Sakura #4583) within disposable embedding molds (Polysciences #18646A‐1). Tissue blocks were frozen on dry ice followed by sagittal sectioning at 40 µm using a cryostat (Leica #CM1950). Free floating sections were placed in 0.05% sodium azide in 1x DPBS (Ricca Chemical #71448‐16) and stored at 4°C. Snap‐frozen brain tissue was thawed on ice and homogenized using an Omni Tissue Homogenizer (Revvity #TH115) in 500 µL Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (T‐PER; Thermo Fisher #78510) supplemented with PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche #C764L25) and cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche #11‐697‐498‐001).

Protein extraction

Half‐brain homogenates were centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to yield a supernatant and pellet containing soluble and insoluble proteins, respectively. To isolate proteins from the insoluble material within pelleted homogenate, 25 µL of the pellets were incubated in 5 M guanidine‐hydrochloride (Gu‐HCl; Thermo Fisher #24115), 50 mM Tris base (pH = 8.0) solution for 3 h at room temperature (RT), with vigorous agitation on a laboratory rocker (Labnet #S203‐DE). The Gu‐HCl and pellet mixtures were diluted 1:8 in 1x DPBS with PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma‐Aldrich #4906845001) and cOmplete protease inhibitors (Sigma‐Aldrich #11697498001), and centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Supernatants were collected to recover insoluble proteins. Total soluble and insoluble protein were quantified using the Protein660 reagent (Thermo Fisher #22660) according to manufacturer's instructions and stored at −80°C for later use.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: Publicly available databases of peer‐reviewed sources (e.g., Pubmed) reveal numerous studies that cite upregulation of CLEC7A as a marker for disease‐associated microglia (DAM) in the innate immune response to neurodegenerative disease pathologies. No studies thus far have identified a causal role of CLEC7A on central nervous system (CNS) macrophages in a model of Alzheimer's disease (AD)–related amyloidosis. Interpretation: Our results indicate a protective role for the DAM marker, CLEC7A, on CNS macrophages in mitigating the accumulation of amyloid beta plaque in the 5xFAD model of AD‐related amyloidosis. Loss of CLEC7A in CNS macrophages altered plaque composition and led to an exacerbation of neuronal damage around plaque surfaces. The transcriptional shift of microglia to the DAM phenotype was unaffected by loss of CLEC7A, thus differentiating it from the immunoreceptor tyrosine activation motifs (ITAM) surface receptor triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2), which is necessary for aspects of the DAM transition. Future directions: What role CLEC7A plays in the microglial response to other neurodegenerative pathologies is unknown and should be investigated, for example, in models of tauopathy. Further work is needed to validate the therapeutic potential of CLEC7A modulation for the treatment of AD and other neurodegenerative diseases.

Aβ ELISA

Soluble and insoluble protein lysates were diluted in 1x DPBS at 1:10 and 1:1000, respectively. Aβ42 and Aβ40 levels were measured by sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Aβ42: Invitrogen #KMB3441; Aβ40: Invitrogen #KMB3481) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, samples and standards were incubated with detector antibody solution in the 96‐well ELISA plate provided. Wells were washed and then immunoglobulin G (IgG) horseradish peroxide (HRP) solution was added. Wash steps were repeated, and stabilized chromogen solution was added to the wells. Stop solution was added and plates were read at 450 nm using a BioTek Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (Agilent). Aβ42 or Aβ40 concentrations were determined using a standard curve and normalized to total protein levels.

Magnetic‐activated cell sorting isolation of microglia

Mice were euthanized with CO2 and the diaphragms cut before being immediately transcardially perfused with 20 mL 1x DPBS. Brains were collected and their meninges and choroid plexus were removed before using a previously described magnetic‐activated cell sorting (MACS) protocol to isolate microglia. 34 Brains were homogenized in 5 mL Buffer A (Hanks Basic Salt Solution (HBSS) + Ca + Mg with 4 U/mL, and 50 U/mL DNAse I) by gentle trituration with a serologic pipette. Samples were then incubated for 15 min at 37°C and then triturated again using a serologic pipette. Samples were returned to 37°C for a second incubation and then triturated again using a serologic pipette. Samples were then returned to 37°C for a third and final incubation and then triturated finally using a 1 mL pipette to yield a smooth single cell suspension. Cells were filtered using a 70 µm cell strainer and washed with 20 mL Buffer B (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F12 with 10 % Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 0.1 % GlutaMAX, and 0.1 % antibiotic antimycotic) and then cells were spun at 300 g for 10 min at 4°C (fast acceleration, slow brake). Supernatants were aspirated and the cell pellets were resuspended in 180 µL 1x MACS buffer (Miltenyi Biotec, 130‐0910376) and 90 µL of CD11b magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130‐093‐634). Samples were incubated at 4°C for 10 min and then spun down at 300 g for 10 min at 4°C (fast acceleration, fast brake). Microglial cells were then purified utilizing LS columns and a QuadroMACS magnet (Miltenyi, 130‐042‐401 and 130‐091‐051) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Column‐bound cells were resuspended in TRIzol (Life Technologies #15596018) for downstream quantitative PCR (qPCR).

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, qPCR

RNA was isolated from MACS‐isolated microglia using a standard TRIzol extraction method as described previously. 35 Briefly, samples were thawed on ice and vortexed; then 100 µL chloroform was added. Samples were vortexed thoroughly, incubated at RT for 5 min, and then spun down at 18,845 g (14,000 rpm) for 15 min at 4°C. The clear top layer was carefully collected, an equal volume of isopropanol was added, and then samples were vortexed thoroughly. Samples were incubated at RT for 10 min and then spun down at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Resulting RNA pellets were washed twice with 70% ethanol (EtOH) and spun down at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C between washes. RNA pellets were dried at RT and then resuspended in 20 µL DNase‐ RNAse‐free water.

Sample quality and quantity for RNA samples were evaluated using NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). The RNA was then converted to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a Sensifast cDNA Synthesis kit (Bioline, BIO‐65054). Levels of Clec7a (Mm01183349_m1) and Gapdh (Mm99999915_g1) mRNA were determined using Taqman Gene Expression Assay primer/probe mix (Thermo Fisher), Sensifast Probe No‐ROX kit (Bioline, BIO‐86005), and a CFX384 Real‐Time PCR System (BioRad, 1‐855‐484). All reagents were used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Immunofluorescence

Floating brain sections (Methods Section 2.2 Brain sample preparation) were placed in blocking buffer (2% donkey serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.1% Triton‐X, and 0.05% Tween‐20 in 1x DPBS) for 1 h at RT while rocking. Sections were then transferred to blocking buffer containing primary antibody overnight at 4°C with rocking. (Primary Antibodies used: Iba1 [Abcam #ab5076; 1:300], D54D2 [Cell Signaling Technologies #38424SF; 1:300], 6E10 [BioLegend #803014; 1:1000], ATG9a [Abcam #ab108338; 1:500], P2RY12 [Abcam #ab300140; 1:500], AXL [R&D Systems #AF854; 1:50], TREM2 [R&D System #AF1729; 1:300], MHCII [Invitrogen #14‐5321‐82; 1:100], AND CD3 [ThermoFisher #14‐0032‐82; 1:200]). Sections were then washed 3x for 10 min in 0.05% Tween‐20 in 1x DPBS with rocking at RT. Sections were then transferred to blocking buffer containing secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT while rocking (Antibodies conjugated to Alexa fluor fluorophores and raised in donkey were used for all secondaries, e.g., Invitrogen #A‐31571; 1:1000). Sections were washed three times in wash buffer for 10 min each at RT with rocking, and then sections were counterstained with Thioflavin S (ThioS) or 4',6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI). For ThioS staining, sections were incubated in 0.2 mg/mL ThioS in 50% EtOH while rocking for 8 min at RT, washed three times in 50% EtOH for 2 min each, and then transferred to 1x DPBS. For DAPI staining, sections were incubated in 20 µg/mL DAPI in 1x DPBS while rocking for 10 min at RT and transferred to 1x DPBS. Counterstained sections were then mounted using ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen #P36930) onto charged glass microscope slides (Fisherbrand #12‐550‐15) and under cover glass (Fisherbrand #12‐544C). Mounted slides were stored at 4°C until imaging on a Stellaris 8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) using the Leica Application Suite (LAS) X microscope software (Leica Microsystems, v3.5.5.19976). Quantification of images were performed using Fiji software or Imaris software (9.5.1).

Single‐nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq)

Brains were collected as described previously (2.2 Brain sample preparation). Half‐brain samples were microdissected to remove the brainstem, cerebellum, olfactory bulb, and midbrain, and then the remaining brain was stored at −80°C. Pooled microdissected brain samples from three mice per group were submitted to GENEWIZ (Azenta Life Sciences) for processing and RNA sequencing. We targeted 10,000 cells per sample at a read depth of 50,000 reads per cell. Nuclei were isolated to generate single‐nuclei 3′ RNA libraries using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit (10X Genomics). Libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq 4000 (Illumina), and raw FASTQ files were processed with the Cell Ranger (v7.0.1, 10X Genomics) pipeline using the mm10 mouse reference genome. Raw feature matrices containing the barcodes, features, and matrix files for each sample were used for further analysis in Python and RStudio (4.2.1).

Initially AnnData files were created in Python. SCVI tools were used to generate doublet scores using Solo. The Seurat package (5.1.0) in R was used for downstream analyses. 36 Doublet scores generated using SCVI tools (Solo) were imported into R and merged with the metadata from Seurat objects. For quality control, nuclei containing a low number of reads (<500 unique molecular identifiers (UMI)) and genes (<500 genes) and >3% mitochondrial reads were removed. Doublet/multiplet nuclei were further filtered out by removing nuclei containing a high number of genes (>5000 genes). Nuclei with doublet scores (generated from SCVI Tools, Solo) above 0.7 were excluded. In addition, nuclei containing a low complexity score (<0.8 log10GenesPerUMI) were removed. After filtering, 17,610 and 13,677 nuclei remained from 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG samples, respectively.

Data integration, normalization, variable feature identification, scaling, and principal component analysis (PCA) were run using commands from the Seurat package. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) reduction were conducted using the top 20 principal components (PCs) based off ElbowPlot. Clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, which use the k‐nearest neighbors (KNN) graph model and Louvain algorithm. A resolution of 0.03 was chosen for downstream clustering of the main analysis. Cell populations were assigned manually using published literature of population‐enriched markers. Following clustering, nuclei populations were arranged in 12 distinct clusters including neuron, myeloid cell, astrocyte, oligodendrocyte, oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC), and endothelial clusters. Nuclei from the myeloid cells and neuron clusters were isolated to further analyze specific subpopulations. Myeloid cells and neurons were re‐integrated and then analyzed using the steps outlined. The top 17 PCs and a resolution of 0.25 were chosen for conducting UMAP for neuronal analysis, whereas the top 12 PCs and a resolution of 0.1 were chosen for conducting CNS myeloid cell UMAP analysis. When performing sub‐clustering of CNS myeloid cells we found four distinct populations. We found that most myeloid cells in our dataset belonged to a Hexb+, Cx3cr1+, Spi1+, P2rx7+ cluster that we identified as microglia. The other myeloid subclusters included a Ttr+ cluster of putative choroid plexus macrophages, as well as clusters containing mixed astrocytes and oligodendrocytes. Mixed clusters were removed from our analysis. Finally, a sub‐subcluster analysis of microglia was performed using the top 12 PCs and using a resolution of 0.4 to enable discovery of microglial subpopulations. Differential gene expression analysis was performed for all cells and within each cell cluster using the ZINB‐WaVE (v1.24.0) and DESeq2 (v1.42.0) packages. Significantly downregulated and upregulated genes were assayed using GProfiler 37 to generate Gene Ontology (GO) terms. Module scores used the following gene lists: Inflammation Module [Il6, Il1b, Il18, Il1r1, Il33, Nfkb1, C1qa, C1qb, C1qc, Ccl2, Ccl6, Ifngr1, Ifngr2, Clu, Ctsb, Saa3] and Interferon Response Module [Ifnar1, Ifnar2, Stat1, Ifitm3, Stat3, Irf1, Irf2, Irf3, Irf8, Oas2, Rnasel]. Analyses were performed and plots were generated in RStudio using the following packages: BiocManager, Seurat, dplyr, tidyverse, ggplot2, Matrix, scales, cowplot, RCurl, ggridges, magrittr, patchwork, ggraph, clustree, metap, limma, DESeq2, EnhancedVolcano, RColorBrewer, sctransform, openxlsx, zinbwave, and scran.

Multiplex cytokine assay

A multiplex cytokine assay was conducted on undiluted samples as described previously. 38 All reagents were used according to the manufacturer's instructions: Bio‐Plex Pro Reagent Kit III (Bio‐Rad, 171304090 M), Bio‐Plex Pro Coupled Magnetic Beads (from Mouse Cytokine 23‐plex Assay, Bio‐Rad, M60009RDPD), and Group I Cytokine standards (Bio‐Rad, 171I50001). The plate was read on a Bio‐Plex 200 System with HTS (Bio‐Plex, 171000205).

Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay

Lipid peroxidation was measured using the malondialdehyde (MDA; Abcam #118970) assay according to the manufacturer's instructions. Whole‐brain soluble protein lysates were diluted 1:10 in 1x PBS prior to the assay. Samples and standards were incubated with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) for 60 min at 95°C and cooled in an ice bath for 10 min. MDA concentrations were determined using a standard curve and normalized to total protein levels.

Western blot analysis

Protein expression levels were analyzed by western blot. Briefly, soluble fractions of brain homogenate were obtained as described. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce 660 Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #22660) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and equal amounts of protein were denatured in Laemmli buffer, separated by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate‐Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), and transferred to Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (BioRad #1704157). Membranes were blocked in SuperBlock Blocking Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific #37535) and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: Synapsin1/2 antibody (Synaptic Systems, 106 006), Homer1 antibody (Synaptic Systems, 160 003), and Vinculin antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #4650) as a loading control. After washing in Tris‐buffered Saline with Tween (TBS‐T), membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were developed with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific #34580) and imaged on a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio‐Rad). Densitometric quantification was performed using ImageJ software and target protein levels were normalized to vinculin.

RESULTS

Clec7a is expressed by plaque‐associated microglia

To investigate the role of CLEC7A in the microglial response to Aβ pathology, we first evaluated its expression by microglia in wild‐type (Clec7aWT) mice and in the 5xFAD mouse model of AD‐related amyloidosis (5xClec7aWT; Figure 1A). We observed little to no expression of CLEC7A in Clec7aWT animals at 5 months of age (Figure 1A, B); however, in 5xClec7aWT mice of the same age we saw that microglia in proximity to Aβ plaques highly upregulated CLEC7A expression (Figure 1A, C, Figure S1A–D). It appeared that CLEC7A expression was limited to plaque‐associated microglia (PAM), as microglia far from plaques in 5xClec7aWT mice were not found to express CLEC7A (Figure 1D), with the exception that microglia located in white matter regions also expressed CLEC7A, even in the absence of nearby plaque structures (Figure S1E, F).

Given the proximity of CLEC7A expression to plaques, we sought to understand the impact of CLEC7A at the microglial–plaque interface. To this end, we generated 5xFAD+/– Clec7a fl/fl Cx3cr1 CreERT2/+ animals (hereafter referred to as 5xClec7aΔMG mice; Figure S2A, B). We provided female, 5xClec7aΔMG mice and Cre‐negative littermate controls (referred to as 5xClec7aWT mice) tamoxifen chow from 4–6 weeks of age to induce Clec7a excision in Cx3cr1‐expressing, Cre+ cells, which are predominantly tissue and bone marrow–derived macrophages. We then returned animals to standard chow to allow for the turnover and repopulation of peripheral macrophages by Clec7a‐competent, bone marrow–derived cells. This model leads to selective loss of Clec7a expression in long‐lived and self‐renewing Cx3cr1‐expressing cells, which in the CNS includes microglia and border‐associated macrophages. 39 , 40 , 41

To validate the specificity of our model, we sorted CD11b+ cells, which are predominantly microglia, from the brains of 5xClec7aΔMG and 5xClec7aWT mice and evaluated relative Clec7a expression. In samples from 5xClec7aΔMG mice, we observed loss of Clec7a expression in the CD11b+, but not the CD11b‐, fraction (Figure S2C), indicative of macrophage‐specific Clec7a deficiency. We also observed abrogated expression of CLEC7A protein by immunofluorescence (IF) in PAM in 5xClec7aΔMG mice relative to 5xClec7aWT controls (Figure S2D–F). In summary, these findings indicate that CLEC7A is highly upregulated by PAM in 5xFAD mice and establishes a novel genetic tool to explore how CLEC7A signaling in CNS‐resident macrophages influences AD‐associated amyloidosis.

Deletion of CLEC7A in CNS‐resident macrophages leads to exacerbated Aβ deposition

Given the role of microglia in compaction, formation, and seeding of Aβ, 10 , 42 we began interrogating our 5xClec7aΔMG mouse model by evaluating Aβ plaque burden. We stained for pan‐Aβ using the D54D2 antibody and observed an increase in total plaque area in both the cortex and hippocampus of 5‐month‐old 5xClec7aΔMG mice when compared to 5xClec7aWT littermate controls (Figure 2A–C). ThioS staining (marking β‐sheet structures found in the dense core of plaques) also revealed an increase in total plaque volume and the number of plaques present in fields of view (FOVs) in the cortex of 5xClec7aΔMG compared to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure 2D–F).

We next investigated plaque composition and structure given previous studies pointing to a role for microglia in the compaction of Aβ into plaque structures. 10 , 42 We evaluated two common Aβ peptides (Aβ42 and Aβ40) in brain homogenates from 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG mice. Aβ42 is necessary for insoluble plaque formation in mice, 43 and has been shown to exhibit greater pathogenic properties than Aβ40. 44 , 45 In line with our immunostaining findings, we found increased concentrations of insoluble Aβ42 in 5xClec7aΔMG brain lysates when compared to brain lysates isolated from 5xClec7aWT littermate controls (Figure 2G). We also observed an increased ratio of insoluble Aβ42 to Aβ40 (Figure S3A), which has been associated with exacerbated neurodegeneration. 46 In contrast, we detected no appreciable differences in either soluble Aβ42 or Aβ40 concentrations between brain lysates from 5xClec7aΔMG and 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure S3B). We expanded upon these findings using an IF approach in which we utilized the 6E10 antibody to stain diffuse plaque structures, which are enriched for Aβ42 and are associated with greater neurotoxicity to surrounding structures. 9 , 10 , 42 We observed an increase in total 6E10 staining (Figure 2H,I) that corresponded to greater ratio of diffuse to dense core ThioS+ plaques in 5xClec7aΔMG mice relative to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure 2H, J). Taken together these findings suggest that loss of Clec7a expression in CNS‐resident macrophages leads to impaired control of amyloid plaque accumulation.

Loss of CLEC7A does not impact microglial recruitment to Aβ plaques

Given the location of CLEC7A expression near Aβ plaques in 5xClec7aWT mice, as well as the increased plaque burden observed in 5xClec7aΔMG mice, we next sought to evaluate microglia‐plaque interactions. To address this, we first assessed microglial coverage utilizing the myeloid marker IBA1. Here we observed increased IBA1 relative coverage in the hippocampus of 5xClec7aΔMG compared to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure 3A–C). We next assessed the microglia recruitment to plaques by quantifying the number of PAM within 15 and 30 µm of Aβ plaques. Compared to 5xClec7aWT littermate controls, 5xClec7aΔMG mice showed an increase in the total number of microglia in each FOV that were within 30 µm from plaque surfaces; however, no difference was observed in the number of the most closely associated microglia within 15 µm of plaques (Figure 3D, E). Furthermore, when normalizing the number of microglia within 30 µm to the total number of plaques per FOV, we found no difference in microglia numbers between 5xClec7aΔMG and 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure 3F). These findings suggest that although Clec7a deletion in CNS‐resident macrophages leads to an increase in IBA1 staining in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice, it had no appreciable effects on PAM when corrected for Aβ load.

Microglia have been shown to proliferate locally at plaque surfaces. 47 To test whether the number of microglia surrounding Aβ plaques may be influenced by local proliferation we stained for the cell proliferation marker Ki67. However, we observed no differences in the number of Ki67+ microglia in plaque‐containing regions of the cortex when comparing 5xClec7aΔMG mice to 5xClec7aWT controls (Figure S4A–C).

Because CLEC7A protein appears upregulated predominantly in PAM, we next evaluated Aβ encapsulation at the plaque surface. To this end we quantified the volume of plaque within IBA1+ brain macrophages using three‐dimensional reconstructions. These studies revealed a trend toward an increase in the total volume of plaque encapsulated within IBA1+ macrophages in 5xClec7aΔMG mice compared to 5xClec7aWT animals (Figure 3G, H). However, we found no difference in this encapsulated plaque volume following normalization to macrophage volume per FOV (Figure 3G, I). We also evaluated macrophage encapsulation of diffuse Aβ marked using the 6E10 antibody and found an increased encapsulation of diffuse Aβ when normalized to macrophage volume (Figure S5A–C). These findings altogether suggest that there is no impact on microglial engagement of dense core Aβ plaques with genetic ablation of CLEC7A in CNS‐resident macrophages; however, CLEC7A‐deficient CNS macrophages do exhibit increased interaction with diffuse plaque structures.

Transcriptomics reveals alterations to complement deposition and a preserved DAM phenotype in Clec7a‐deficient microglia

To further probe the impact of microglial Clec7a deficiency in the brains of 5xFAD mice in an unbiased fashion, we next performed single‐nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq). After pre‐processing and quality control filtering, we analyzed 17,610 and 13,677 nuclei from 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG samples, respectively, which were derived from pooled brain tissue (n = 3 brains per group). We identified cell‐type clusters across both groups corresponding to CNS myeloid cells, astrocytes, neurons, OPCs, oligodendrocytes, and endothelial cells (Figure 4A; Figure S6A–D). We found differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in all cell types (Figure S7A–F). Infiltrating T cells have recently been recognized to modulate microglial activity and plaque pathology in 5xFAD mice. 48 Although we did not capture T cells in our sequencing data, we evaluated CD3+ T cell numbers in the subiculum of 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG and found no differences in T cell numbers (Figure S8A,B).

We performed further clustering of our CNS myeloid cells population and identified a sub‐population of Hexb+, Cx3cr1+, Spi1+, P2rx7+ that we identified as microglia (see Section 2.8 Methods Single‐nuclei RNA sequencing and Figure S9A). When comparing DEGs within the microglia cluster from 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG samples we found 310 DEGs (Figure 4B). These DEGs included genes related to microglial activation and lysosomal activity (Apoe, Cst3, Ctsb, and Ctsd), which were upregulated in 5xClec7aΔMG microglia. We also observed increased expression of several complement related genes (C1qa, C1qb, and C1qc) within 5xClec7aΔMG microglia. GO pathway analysis of DEGs revealed increased biological processes related to cell activation (e.g., “leukocyte activation, ” “leukocyte mediated immunity”) and cell fate (e.g., “regulation of cell population proliferation, ” “programmed cell death”). Decreased biological processes were related to maintenance of neurons (e.g., “synapse organization” and “nervous system development”) in 5xClec7aΔMG microglia when compared to 5xClec7aWT microglia (Figure 4C).

To further interrogate the impact of CLEC7A on microglial transcriptional state, we further subclustered microglial nuclei. We found three populations of microglia that aligned closely with previously characterized homeostatic DAM1 and DAM2 gene profiles 13 (Figure 4D and Figure S9B–G). We observed limited changes in the frequencies of homeostatic or DAM populations between groups (Figure 4D, E). These findings suggest that CLEC7A signaling does not influence the transition of microglia from a homeostatic to a DAM transcriptional state. To further investigate microglial transition into the DAM state on the protein level, we co‐stained for several markers related to DAM gene profiles and microglia activity, including P2RY12 and AXL (Figure 4F–K) as well as MHCII and TREM2 (Figure S10A–E). Consistent with our snRNA‐seq findings (Figure S9G), we found that the absence of microglial CLEC7A signaling in the 5xFAD mouse model does not appreciably impact the protein expression levels of canonical markers of homeostatic microglia (P2RY12; Figure 4F–H) or DAM (AXL, MHCII, and TREM2; Figure 4I–K, Figure S10A–E). In summary, we find that the loss of Clec7a in brain‐resident macrophages leads to increased expression of lysosomal and complement genes in the microglia of 5xFAD mice but does not alter the expression of proteins associated with transition of microglia into the DAM phenotype.

Loss of microglial Clec7a leads to widespread transcriptional alterations in CNS‐lineage cells in 5xFAD mice

We next sought to leverage our snRNA‐seq dataset to investigate the impact of CLEC7A loss from CNS macrophages on broader CNS cell function in 5xFAD mice. We first analyzed transcripts in neurons, which represented the greatest proportion of nuclei in our dataset. We re‐clustered neuronal nuclei (Rbfox3+ clusters; Figure 4A, Figure S6A–C) and identified 17 subclusters of cells, which we then grouped as either excitatory (Slc17a7+, Satb2+) or inhibitory (Gad1+, Slc6a1+) neurons (Figure 5A and Figure S11A–C). Neuron subclusters that highly expressed glial cell markers or mixed excitatory and inhibitory markers were identified as independent populations (Figure S11A–C and Figure 5A). We observed an increase in the frequency of excitatory neurons and a decrease in the frequency of inhibitory neurons when comparing 5xClec7aΔMG to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure 5B). When assessing DEGs in inhibitory neurons, we found 3765 DEGs, with the vast majority (3,687) being downregulated in 5xClec7aΔMG samples (Figure 5C). GO analysis of biological processes revealed downregulated pathways related to neuronal function, including “synapse organization” and “cognition” (Figure 5D). Downregulation of these pathways suggests neuronal impairment in 5xClec7aΔMG samples. In excitatory neurons, we similarly found a majority of DEGs downregulated in 5xClec7aΔMG nuclei as compared to 5xClec7aWT nuclei (Figure S11D). Relevant biological pathways determined by GO analysis of downregulated genes in excitatory neurons were related to neuron formation and included “neurogenesis,” “neuron differentiation,” and “synapse assembly” (Figure S11E).

Other cell types identified in our snRNA‐seq also revealed transcriptional changes. Of the glial cells, OPCs exhibited the greatest number of DEGs, with the majority being downregulated in 5xClec7aΔMG samples when compared to 5xClec7aWT samples (Figure S7A,B). GO analysis of downregulated genes in OPCs suggested a reduction in biological processes related to myelination. Similar analysis of downregulated genes within oligodendrocytes also revealed pathways related to myelination, including “central nervous system myelination” and “axon ensheathment in the central nervous system” (Figure S7C,D). In astrocytes, we observed increased expression of disease‐associated astrocyte (DAA)–related genes such as Cst3, Apoe, and Clu 49 (Figure S7E). GO pathway terms related to biological processes of upregulated genes by astrocytes in 5xClec7aΔMG mice compared to 5xClec7aWT mice included Aβ‐related processes, including “negative regulation of amyloid beta formation,” driven by the upregulation of the genes Clu and Apoe, as well as “regulation of cell motility.” Downregulated processes in 5xClec7aΔMG astrocytes were related to neuronal function and included “neurogenesis” and “synapse assembly” (Figure S7E,F). These transcriptional findings revealed broad gene expression changes following CNS macrophage deletion of Clec7a in 5xFAD mice.

Aβ plaque has been shown to drive inflammatory cytokine and chemokine expression in the CNS of 5xFAD mice. 50 Given the increased plaque burden exhibited in 5xClec7aΔMG as compared to 5xClec7aWT mice, we next leveraged our transcriptomic data to evaluate the impact of CNS macrophage deletion of CLEC7A on inflammatory markers by calculating gene expression module scores for common markers of CNS inflammation in glial cell populations and neurons. Inflammatory module scores were constituted by inflammatory cytokines (Il6, Il1b, Il18, Il1r1, Il33, Ifngr1, Ifngr2, and Nfkb1), synapse pruning molecules (C1qa, C1qb, and C1qc), chemokines (Ccl2 and Ccl6), and markers of activated glia (Clu, Ctsb, and Saa3). We found that microglia, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and neurons exhibited an increase in inflammatory gene module scores in 5xClec7aΔMG compared to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure S12A). We next performed a BioPlex ELISA to evaluate inflammatory cytokines in soluble, brain homogenate from 5xClec7aWT and 5xClec7aΔMG mice. We found an increased concentration of interleukin 6 (IL‐6) in 5xClec7aΔMG compared to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure S12B). Type I interferon responses have been implicated specifically in neurodegeneration in the 5xFAD mouse model. 51 , 52 Therefore, we also evaluated interferon response genes using module scores, including interferon‐stimulated genes 53 and interferon receptor signaling (Ifnar1, Ifnar2, Stat1, Ifitm3, Stat3, Irf1, Irf2, Irf3, Irf8, Oas2, and Rnasel), and we found a greater interferon signature in microglia, astrocytes, and neurons of 5xClec7aΔMG when compared to 5xClec7aWT mice (Figure S12C).

CLEC7A deficiency in microglia exacerbates neuronal dystrophy in 5xFAD mice

Given the increase in plaque burden observed in 5xClec7aΔMG mice paired with the differences in gene expression related to maintenance of neurons in our snRNA‐seq dataset, we next sought to evaluate neuronal health. To this end we measured the presence of dystrophic neurites (DNs), which are axonal swellings that result from damaging interactions between axons and plaque surfaces. 9 , 54 These structures accumulate endolysosomal proteins such as the autophagy‐related protein ATG9a. 55 , 56 We observed an approximately twofold increased volume of ATG9a+ DN in cortical regions of 5xClec7aΔMG mice when compared to 5xClec7aWT littermate controls (Figure 5E, F). In addition, we found that individual plaques had a greater ratio of DN to plaque volume, suggesting increased axonal damage surrounding individual plaques (Figure 5E, G). We next measured synaptic proteins in brain lysates and found a reduction in the post‐synaptic marker Homer‐1 in 5xClec7aΔMG mice when compared to 5xClec7aWT. We found no differences, however, in the pre‐synaptic protein synapsin (Figure S13A–C). We also evaluated the oxidized lipid breakdown product, malondialdehyde (or MDA), in lysates and found a greater concentration of MDA in samples from 5xClec7aΔMG brains as compared to 5xClec7aWT samples (Figure S13D). This result suggests increased oxidative stress in 5xClec7aΔMG brains, which has been linked to ferroptotic cell death. 57 , 58 Taken together, we observe markers of declining neuronal cell health including greater dystrophic neurites, reduced synaptic proteins, and increased markers of oxidative stress that suggest increased neuronal damage in 5xFAD mice lacking microglial Clec7a signaling.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that microglial CLEC7A signaling in CNS resident macrophages plays a protective role in AD‐related amyloid disease. We observed limited surface expression of CLEC7A by microglia under steady‐state conditions in adult mice but noted that CLEC7A expression was markedly upregulated on CNS macrophages near Aβ plaques. We found that conditional loss of CLEC7A in brain macrophages resulted in a nearly twofold increase in plaque coverage in 5xFAD mice, and that this effect was driven by an increase in the number of plaques. Our findings suggest a failure of CLEC7A‐deficient microglia to restrain plaque accumulation.

We also observed increased damage to neurons. Specifically, we found an increase in the volume of ATG9a+ axonal swellings (i.e., DNs) surrounding plaques in the cortex of 5xClec7aΔMG mice as compared to 5xClec7aWT mice. This was not driven solely by a greater number of plaques, as we found a greater volume of DNs on a per plaque basis. Microglia have been observed to establish barrier structures surrounding plaques and to limit the interaction of neurotoxic Aβ with adjacent neuronal structures. 9 Moreover, microglia mediate neuronal protection from plaque structures by compacting plaques and reducing the surface area of the more toxic oligomeric species of Aβ. 9 Our observed increase in DNs in 5xClec7aΔMG mice suggests an impairment in the function of CLEC7A‐deficient microglia in barrier function or plaque compaction. Although we did not observe differences in the recruitment of microglia to plaque surfaces or the amount of plaque contained within microglia, we did detect increased fibrillar plaque species labeled by the anti‐Aβ antibody, 6E10, which suggests an impairment in the compaction of Aβ.

Our observation of worsened axonal dystrophy matched our snRNA‐seq findings showing reduced expression of genes related to neuronal activity within inhibitory neurons in 5xClec7aΔMG mice, including Ube3a (ubiquitin protein ligase E3A [UBE3A]) and Ntrk2 (tyrosine kinase receptor B [TrkB]). UBE3A is a ubiquitin ligase important for maintaining synaptic proteins and whose transcription is induced downstream of neuronal activity and neurotrophin signaling. 59 Reductions in TrkB, the receptor for brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), may also indicate compromised neuronal functioning, as TrkB is crucial to neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. 60

Unlike studies on the loss of TREM2 function in AD‐related amyloidosis, 12 we did not observe alterations in the frequency of DAM populations. This might have been expected given that Clec7a is not upregulated until microglia reach the DAM 2 state, which itself has been shown to be dependent on TREM2. 12 We did, however, find increased expression of transcripts related to lysosomal activity (Cst3, Ctsb, Ctsd; encoding for cystatin C, cathepsin B, and cathepsin D, respectively) and complement fixation (C1qa, C1qb, C1qc) in 5xClec7aΔMG mice when compared to 5xClec7aWT mice. Complement has been shown to play roles mediating neurodegeneration in models of AD 61 , 62 and is associated with neuroinflammatory changes in the cerebrospinal fluid of individuals diagnosed with AD. 63

Together, this work demonstrates a beneficial role for CNS‐resident macrophage CLEC7A signaling in amyloid‐related disease. This finding builds on previous work from our lab and others that have identified CLEC7A as a potential therapeutic target for neurodegenerative disease. Namely, we previously showed that injection of the CLEC7A agonist, pustulan, into the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice led to increased phagocytosis of plaque and reduced plaque burden. 26 This effect was dependent on the adaptor molecule CARD9, which signals downstream of SYK and promotes microglial activation. 26 Similarly, separate studies by others showed that short‐term delivery of an anti‐CLEC7A antibody led to microglial activation and reorganization of plaques into more inert and less filamentous structures. 28 Treatment with the fungal ligand curdlan was also found to promote axon regeneration in a model of optic nerve crush injury. 64 These examples highlight the ability to modulate CLEC7A signaling in the treatment of neurological disease and specifically suggest an adaptive role for CLEC7A in AD‐related amyloidosis and nerve injury. However, antagonism of CLEC7A has also been shown to mitigate synaptic loss in a model of tauopathy, 65 indicating that more work is needed to fully understand the context‐dependent role of CLEC7A in neurodegenerative disease.

CONCLUSION

Recent work has broadened our understanding of the role of CLEC7A/Dectin‐1 from a receptor primarily involved in the recognition and defense of fungal pathogens to a multi‐faceted pattern recognition receptor with roles in tumor immunity and CNS injury. 66 Despite a recent focus on Clec7a expression by microglia in neurodevelopment and neurodegenerative diseases, the role of CLEC7A in these contexts has remained unclear. Here we show that loss of CLEC7A in CNS‐resident macrophages responding to Aβ plaques leads to exacerbated disease, including increased plaque burden and the deterioration of neuronal health. CLEC7A upregulation is a common feature of microglia responding to neurodegenerative disease pathology (e.g., amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, tauopathy), and this study opens the door for investigating CLEC7A in these contexts.
