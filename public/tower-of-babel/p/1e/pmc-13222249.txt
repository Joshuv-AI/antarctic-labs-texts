Non-factor Therapies in Hemophilia: Mechanisms, Clinical Evidence, Patient Management, and Future Perspectives
Authors: Yuto Nakajima, Keiji Nogami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHemophilia is an inherited bleeding disorder caused by deficiencies in coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) or factor IX (FIX), leading to impaired thrombin generation and recurrent bleeding episodes. For decades, prophylactic factor replacement therapy has been the mainstay of hemophilia management; however, its limitations—including treatment burden, inhibitor development, and incomplete bleeding control—have required the development of alternative therapeutic strategies. Non-factor therapies represent a major paradigm shift in hemophilia care by restoring hemostatic balance through mechanisms independent of FVIII or FIX replacement. These approaches include bispecific antibodies that mimic FVIII cofactor activity and rebalancing strategies that enhance thrombin generation by inhibiting anticoagulant pathways such as tissue factor pathway inhibitor or antithrombin. Clinical studies have demonstrated that non-factor therapies provide stable and predictable prophylactic efficacy, significantly reduce bleeding rates, and improve quality of life in people with hemophilia A or B, irrespective of inhibitor status. Despite these advances, important challenges remain. Non-factor therapies modify physiological coagulation pathways and may be associated with thrombotic events due to imbalance between procoagulant and anticoagulant mechanisms, particularly for rebalancing agents. In addition, the lack of standardized laboratory assays for assessing global coagulation potential, uncertainties regarding combination therapy during breakthrough bleeding or surgery, and concerns about long-term safety and cost burden could limit optimal implementation. This review summarizes the mechanisms of action, clinical evidence, and safety considerations of currently available and emerging non-factor therapies for hemophilia. We also discuss unmet clinical needs, patient management considerations, and future perspectives aimed at optimizing the safe and sustainable use of non-factor therapies into routine hemophilia care.Keywords: Hemophilia, Non-factor therapy, Coagulation rebalancing, Bispecific antibody, Tissue factor pathway inhibitor

Introduction

Definition of Hemophilia and Conventional Treatment Strategies

Hemophilia is an inherited X-linked recessive bleeding disorder caused by quantitative or qualitative deficiency of coagulation factor (F)VIII (hemophilia A) or FIX (hemophilia B). These deficiencies impair the intrinsic pathway of coagulation, leading to insufficient thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation. As a result, people with hemophilia often experience spontaneous or trauma-related breakthrough bleeding. Typical bleeding manifestations include hemarthroses, intramuscular hemorrhage, and soft tissue bleeding, including joints such as the knees, ankles, and elbows. These repeated bleeding episodes result in hemophilic arthropathy, characterized by chronic pain, limited range of motion, and functional disability [1].

The clinical severity of hemophilia is traditionally classified according to residual circulating factor activity levels. Severe hemophilia is defined by factor activity levels of less than 1 IU/dL, and is characterized by frequent spontaneous bleeding. Moderate hemophilia is defined by factor activity levels of 1–5 IU/dL, with spontaneous or trauma-related bleeding episodes. Mild hemophilia, defined by factor activity levels of 5–40 IU/dL, usually manifests with traumatic bleeding and may remain undiagnosed until adolescence or adulthood because of reduced bleeding frequency [2].

Replacement therapies with plasma-derived or recombinant clotting factor concentrates have been developed and used for hemostatic management of hemophilia for several decades [3]. These products are administered intravenously prophylactically to maintain trough factor levels above 1–2 IU/dL for effective hemostasis [3]. In addition, significant advances have been made to improve the pharmacokinetic properties of factor concentrates. In particular, extended half-life (EHL) FVIII or FIX products have been developed through molecular modifications including Fc fusion, albumin fusion, or PEGylation [4–6]. These strategies prolong the circulating half-life of FVIII or FIX by reducing clearance and utilizing endogenous recycling pathways. These EHL products enable less frequent intravenous infusions while maintaining adequate trough levels. These changes reduce infusion-related burden (time, venous access difficulties, and treatment-related stress) and can decrease bleeding episodes and bleeding-related anxiety, leading to improved daily functioning, participation in school/work, and overall quality of life [7].

Demographics and Burden of the Disease

Hemophilia affects approximately 1 in 5000 male births worldwide for hemophilia A and approximately 1 in 30,000 male births for hemophilia B. The disease predominantly affects males, although some female carriers may exhibit bleeding symptoms with various severity [3]. Advances in prophylactic treatment have markedly improved life expectancy, resulting in an increasing number of patients in older ages [8]. Consequently, the demographic profile of people with hemophilia has shifted toward an aging population that faces accumulating comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and chronic joint disease, which further complicate long-term disease management [8].

Although the development of replacement therapy has significantly improved clinical outcomes, this therapeutic approach has several limitations. Conventional and EHL-factor concentrates require repeated intravenous administration, imposing a significant treatment burden, particularly in children and those with poor venous access [3]. Furthermore, some people with hemophilia continue to experience breakthrough bleeding and progressive joint damage despite receiving regular prophylaxis.

One of the most serious complications of factor replacement therapy is the development of neutralizing antibodies (inhibitors) against infused clotting factors. Inhibitors develop in approximately 20–30% of people with severe hemophilia A and in a smaller proportion of people with hemophilia B [9]. The presence of inhibitors markedly reduces the efficacy of factor replacement therapy, leading to increased bleeding risk, morbidity, and mortality rates [10]. The hemostatic treatment of people with hemophilia and inhibitors primarily depends on bypassing agents [11], such as recombinant activated FVII (FVIIa), activated prothrombin complex concentrate (aPCC). In addition to hemostatic treatment to prevent or control bleeding, immune tolerance induction (ITI) is an inhibitor-eradication strategy [12]. ITI aims to eliminate FVIII/FIX inhibitors and restore the effectiveness of conventional factor replacement therapy. Bypassing agents are characterized, however, by short half-lives, variable clinical responses, limited laboratory monitoring tools, and potential thrombotic concerns [13, 14], while ITI requires prolonged treatment and is not universally successful [15]. These challenges emphasize the unmet clinical need for novel therapeutic strategies that can provide stable and predictable hemostatic control with reduced treatment burden.

Summary of What Will Be Covered in the Review

This review provides an overview of recent advances in therapeutic strategies for hemophilia. In particular, it focuses on novel non-factor replacement therapies designed to overcome the limitations of conventional factor replacement therapy. Following a summary of the pathophysiology, clinical characteristics, and disease burden of hemophilia, the unmet clinical needs associated with current treatment, including treatment burden and inhibitor development are discussed.

The mechanisms of action, clinical efficacy, and safety profiles of non–factor therapies, including bispecific antibodies that mimic FVIII cofactor activity and rebalancing strategies targeting anticoagulant pathways such as tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) and antithrombin are reviewed. Finally, remaining challenges, safety considerations, and future perspectives are focused on the evolving therapeutic landscape of hemophilia management.

What Is Non-factor Therapy and How Does It Work?

Paradigm Shift: From Factor Replacement to Coagulation Rebalancing

Non-factor therapies are designed to restore hemostatic balance by enhancing thrombin generation through two mechanisms: mimicking the cofactor of FVIII and reducing the anticoagulant activity [16]. By attenuating natural anticoagulant pathways or mimicking procoagulant functions, these non-factor therapies aim to compensate for clotting factor deficiency and achieve sufficient thrombin generation.

Importantly, this rebalancing strategy may provide a more stable and continuous hemostatic effect than conventional factor replacement therapy. The therapeutic effectiveness of non-factor therapy is largely unaffected by circulating FVIII or FIX levels and is maintained in patients with hemophilia, irrespective of the presence of inhibitors.

Major Mechanistic Categories

Bispecific Antibodies

Bispecific antibodies that mimic the cofactor function of FVIIIa represent a major advance in non-factor replacement therapy for hemophilia A. These agents substitute for FVIIIa cofactor activity and restore effective thrombin generation by bridging FIX/FIXa and FX/FXa [17]. The mechanism exerts FVIII-independent coagulation activity and provides effective hemostatic control, irrespective of inhibitor status [18]. FVIIIa-mimetic bispecific antibodies represent a paradigm shift in hemophilia A treatment, offering sustained and effective hemostatic control without additional intravenous clotting factor infusion.

Blocking of Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI)

TFPI is a key physiological inhibitor of the tissue factor (TF)/FVIIa complex and FXa, acting as a critical regulator of initiation-phase coagulation [19]. TFPI plays an essential role in maintaining the balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant pathways under normal physiological conditions. In people with hemophilia, however, this regulatory mechanism may further restrict thrombin generation under intrinsic pathway deficiency. TFPIα, featuring three Kunitz (K) domains, plays a regulatory role in coagulation by inhibiting key enzymes in the process. In particular, its K2 domain inhibits FXa, whereas the K1 domain blocks the TF/FVIIa complex. As a result, TFPIα limits thrombin generation during the coagulation process.

Inhibition of TFPI enhances early thrombin generation by suppressing the function of the TF/FVIIa complex and FXa, thereby amplifying downstream coagulation reactions. This rebalancing effect partially compensates for clotting factor deficiencies in the intrinsic pathway and promotes more effective propagation of thrombin generation independent of FVIII or FIX. Blocking TFPI has emerged, therefore, as a promising non-factor replacement strategy for improving hemostasis in hemophilia.

Antithrombin Suppression

Antithrombin is a major natural anticoagulant that inhibits thrombin and FXa, playing a central role in regulating coagulation and preventing excessive clot formation under physiological conditions [20]. In hemophilia, the coexistence of AT deficiency is associated with a milder hemorrhagic phenotype, suggesting that partial modulation of natural anticoagulant pathways may enhance their hemostatic potential [21].

The RNA interference-mediated therapeutic inhibition of antithrombin reduces plasma antithrombin concentrations, leading to an increase in thrombin generation and enhancing overall hemostatic potential [22]. Lowering antithrombin levels enhances thrombin generation downstream of both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Importantly, this mechanism could be applicable to both hemophilia A and hemophilia B and remains effective regardless of inhibitor status.

Examples of Non-factor Therapies

Emicizumab

Emicizumab represents the first bispecific antibody approved for clinical use in hemophilia A. Subcutaneous administration of emicizumab provides stable prophylactic efficacy in patients with or without FVIII inhibitors. Clinical trials and real-world data have demonstrated a substantial reduction in bleeding rates, improved quality of life, and simplified treatment regimens compared with conventional factor replacement therapy.

The clinical efficacy and safety of emicizumab were established in a series of pivotal phase 3 clinical trials, HAVEN 1 and HAVEN 2, which demonstrated marked reductions in annualized bleeding rates (ABR) in adult and pediatric patients with inhibitors [18, 23], while HAVEN 3 and HAVEN 4 extended these findings to patients with or without FVIII inhibitors using weekly to extended-interval dosing schedules. Subsequent clinical studies, including HAVEN 5–7, further confirmed the long-term efficacy, safety, and durability of emicizumab prophylaxis in real-world clinical practice [24–28].

Emicizumab prophylaxis in these trials resulted in marked reductions in ABR compared with bypassing-agent prophylaxis or conventional replacement therapy. In addition to bleeding outcomes, emicizumab demonstrated favorable effects on patient-reported outcomes, including reduced treatment burden, improved physical function, and enhanced health-related quality of life [29]. However, emicizumab-driven coagulation is mechanistically different from native FVIII activity, and its maximal hemostatic effect may not fully reach physiological levels. A previous study suggested that in vitro coagulation potential and bleeding patterns in people with hemophilia A receiving emicizumab were similar to those in people with mild hemophilia A [30]. These observations have highlighted the unmet need for improved bispecific antibodies with higher cofactor activity, designed to more closely approximate physiological FVIIIa cofactor function.

Mim8

Based on the success of emicizumab, next-generation FVIIIa-mimetic bispecific antibodies have been developed to further enhance coagulation potential. Mim8 is an engineered bispecific antibody with optimized binding affinities for FIXa, enabling more potent and efficient FX activation compared with emicizumab [31, 32]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that Mim8 exhibits thrombin generation potency by approximately 15-fold greater than emicizumab, potentially approaching non-hemophilic coagulation levels, while maintaining subcutaneous administration and favorable pharmacokinetic properties [33–35].

The clinical development of Mim8 is being evaluated in the FRONTIER clinical trial program. FRONTIER1 was designed as a first-in-human phase 1 study [33], while FRONTIER2 represents a phase 2 trial assessing the efficacy and safety of Mim8 prophylaxis in people with hemophilia A with or without inhibitors [35]. Results from these FRONTIER studies have demonstrated robust prophylactic efficacy and low ABR using different dosing regimens. Importantly, Mim8 has shown a generally favorable safety profile to date, supporting its potential as a next-generation non-factor replacement therapy. Ongoing and future analyses from the FRONTIER program will further define the optimal dosing strategies, long-term safety, and clinical role of Mim8 in the future hemophilia treatment landscape.

NXT007

NXT007 is an advanced FVIIIa-mimetic bispecific antibody derived from emicizumab, engineered to increase FX binding affinity and overall cofactor activity. Preclinical studies demonstrated that NXT007 exhibits enhanced FX activation by FIXa compared with emicizumab, resulting in augmented thrombin generation under FVIII-deficient conditions. These studies demonstrated that optimization of binding affinity for FX enabled NXT007 to achieve coagulation potential closer to the normal range [36]. Further investigations evaluated the global coagulation profiles of NXT007 using comprehensive coagulation assays [37]. These studies demonstrated that NXT007 exerts greater and more sustained thrombin generation than emicizumab, approaching non-hemophilic levels. Importantly, experimental evidence provided by these findings confirmed that next-generation FVIIIa-mimetic antibodies can overcome some limitations of emicizumab.

Consistent with these preclinical observations, subsequent phase ½ clinical studies reported at the ISTH 2025 congress, have shown that NXT007 exerts robust thrombin generation and global coagulation profiles closer to the normal range compared with emicizumab. Ongoing clinical studies will further clarify long-term safety profile and clinical benefits of NXT007 for the management of hemophilia A.

Concizumab

Concizumab is a subcutaneously administered monoclonal antibody targeting TFPI and developed as a rebalancing strategy to enhance the initiation-phase thrombin generation in hemophilia. Its clinical development has progressed through the Explorer program (Explorer 1–7). Explorer 1 [38], was a first-in-human study primarily assessing safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), and pharmacodynamics (PD), and was followed by early dose-escalation studies such as Explorer 3 that further characterized the PK/PD profile and TFPI inhibition-associated increases in thrombin generation [39]. The phase 2 Explorer 4 and 5 trials evaluated the prophylactic efficacy of once-daily subcutaneous administration in patients with hemophilia with inhibitors (Explorer 4) and without inhibitors (Explorer 5). These studies demonstrated clinically meaningful reductions in bleeding outcomes [40].

The pivotal phase 3 trial, Explorer 7, evaluated concizumab prophylaxis in patients with hemophilia A and B with inhibitors, and showed substantial reductions in ABR compared with no prophylaxis, while highlighting the importance of careful dose optimization and safety monitoring [41]. Notably, the Explorer program was paused temporarily due to non-fatal thrombotic events, indicating the need to define an appropriate degree of TFPI inhibition. Finally, patient-reported outcome data from the phase 3 Explorer 8 study, which evaluated the efficacy and safety of once-daily subcutaneous concizumab prophylaxis, demonstrated the clinical benefits of concizumab prophylaxis compared with no prophylaxis in people with hemophilia [42].

Marstacimab

Marstacimab is a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting TFPI, and developed as a subcutaneously administered rebalancing therapy to enhance initiation-phase thrombin generation in hemophilia [43]. This antibody neutralizes TFPI activity by reducing the suppression of the TF/FVIIa complex and FXa, consequently promoting early thrombin generation and enhancing downstream coagulation reactions independent of FVIII or FIX.

The clinical development of marstacimab has been evaluated through a series of clinical studies, including phase 1 and phase 2 trials that characterized its safety profile, PK, PD, and maintained long-term efficacy. These early studies showed that marstacimab induced dose-dependent increases in thrombin generation and significantly reduced ABR, supporting the conclusion that marstacimab was safe and well tolerated [44, 45].

Subsequent phase 3 clinical trials within the BASIS program evaluated the effectiveness of marstacimab for once-weekly subcutaneous prophylaxis in hemophilia A and B [46]. Results from these studies demonstrated significant reductions in ABR compared with on-demand treatment, along with sustained bleeding control during long-term follow-up. Marstacimab was generally well tolerated; careful dose selection and safety monitoring were emphasized, however, to mitigate thrombotic risk. Taken together, current clinical evidence indicates that marstacimab represents a promising TFPI-targeted therapeutic option for non-factor replacement therapies.

Fitusiran

Fitusiran, an RNA interference-based therapeutic agent, reduces hepatic synthesis of antithrombin, leading to sustained increases in thrombin generation [47]. Its clinical development has been evaluated in the ATLAS clinical trial program, including a phase 1 study that characterized PK, PD and dose-dependent reductions in antithrombin levels [48, 49]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that fitusiran prophylaxis was associated with marked reductions in ABR in hemophilia A and B, including those with inhibitors. Subsequent phase 3 trials within the ATLAS program (e.g., ATLAS-INH and ATLAS-A/B) further confirmed significant reductions in ABR compared with on-demand treatment [50–52]. Subcutaneous administration of fitusiran demonstrated practical advantages by reducing treatment burden and improving adherence.

Nevertheless, thrombotic events reported during the clinical development of fitusiran have highlighted the delicate balance between efficacy and safety associated with antithrombin suppression [52]. These events led to a temporary pause of clinical trials and subsequent protocol modifications, including dose adjustments and enhanced safety monitoring, emphasizing the importance of precise control of antithrombin reduction. Long-term outcome data are still needed to fully characterize the safety profile of antithrombin-targeting therapies and to determine optimal patient selection criteria.

Other Serpin Inhibitors

Serpin PC has emerged as a potential strategy to restore hemostatic balance in hemophilia through inhibition of activated protein C (APC). Data from phase ½ and phase 2a clinical studies indicate that subcutaneous administration was both effective and well tolerated, resulting in an approximately 90% reduction in ABR in people with severe hemophilia without thromboembolic complications [53]. However, the clinical development of Serpin PC was discontinued by the sponsor due to business and strategic considerations [54]. Another investigational approach involves SR604, a chimeric monoclonal antibody directed against APC. In preclinical hemophilia A and B models, SR604 markedly reduced bleeding and demonstrated favorable subcutaneous bioavailability, supporting its potential use as a prophylactic therapy.

Also, modulation of protein S (PS), an endogenous anticoagulant cofactor, represents an additional therapeutic concept aimed at rebalancing coagulation. VGA039, a monoclonal antibody targeting PS, has been shown to enhance thrombin generation across several models of congenital coagulation factor deficiencies and is currently undergoing early-phase clinical evaluation [55].

Benefits of Non-factor Therapies

Non-factor therapies have fundamentally transformed the management of hemophilia by addressing several key limitations of conventional factor replacement therapy. One of the most significant advantages is providing stable and continuous hemostatic control independent of circulating FVIII or FIX levels. By rebalancing coagulation through FVIIIa-mimetic activity or suppression of natural anticoagulant pathways, these therapies achieve sustained thrombin generation with reduced variability compared with conventional factor replacement therapy.

Another major benefit is the subcutaneous route of administration, which markedly reduces treatment burden and improves adherence, particularly in pediatric patients and individuals with poor venous access. The extended dosing intervals associated with non-factor therapies further enhance convenience and quality of life, facilitating long-term prophylaxis and supporting preventive treatment strategies.

Importantly, non-factor therapies retain efficacy in patients with hemophilia and inhibitors, a population historically associated with limited treatment options and high disease burden. FVIIIa-mimetic bispecific antibodies and rebalancing therapies offer effective prophylactic hemostatic control regardless of inhibitor status, consequently reducing the need for bypassing agents that have short half-lives and inconsistent clinical efficacy.

Beyond bleeding control, accumulating evidence suggests that improved prophylactic efficacy with non-factor therapies may contribute to better joint outcomes and long-term functional preservation. These agents have the potential to mitigate the progression of hemophilic arthropathy by minimizing breakthrough bleeding and maintaining more consistent coagulation potential, Overall, these benefits highlight the paradigm shift introduced by non-factor therapies, positioning them as a mainstay of hemophilia management.

Limitations, Unmet Needs, and Cost Burden

Despite their clinical benefits, non-factor therapies are associated with important challenges that must be carefully considered in clinical practice. Thrombotic risk remains a central concern, particularly when non-factor agents are used in combination with bypassing agents or additional factor concentrates for the management of breakthrough bleeding or surgical procedures. Reports during clinical development [18, 52] have emphasized the danger of thrombotic events associated with excessive coagulation rebalancing and have highlighted the need for cautious dosing, patient selection, and safety monitoring.

Laboratory monitoring represents another major challenge in the era of non-factor therapy. Conventional coagulation assays, such as activated partial thromboplastin time, are often unreliable in people with hemophilia receiving non-factor agents and may not accurately reflect overall hemostatic potential [56]. Although global coagulation assays such as thrombin generation assays have been proposed as potential monitoring tools, standardized methodologies and clinically meaningful thresholds have not been established [56]. This lack of validated monitoring strategies complicates clinical decision-making, particularly in situations requiring dose adjustment or assessment of thrombotic risk. A recent report suggested that a modified ROTEM-based approach has been evaluated as a potential tool to assess coagulation potential in the setting of concizumab prophylaxis [57]. Such advances in practical monitoring tools could lead to the safe and effective use of non-factor therapies in routine clinical practice.

In addition, the high cost of non-factor therapies may pose a significant barrier to widespread adoption. While these agents may reduce long-term costs associated with bleeding complications and hospitalizations, the high costs remain a major concern. Addressing these unmet needs will require not only continued clinical research but also coordinated efforts involving healthcare systems and industry.

Patient Management and Quality of Life

The introduction of non-factor therapies has substantially changed strategies for patient management in hemophilia, and extended benefits beyond bleeding control to broader aspects of daily life and long-term care. Stable prophylactic efficacy in coagulation potential enables more predictable hemostatic control, which simplifies clinical decision-making and facilitates individualized treatment planning.

The subcutaneous administration and improved dosing intervals associated with non-factor therapies markedly reduce treatment burden, particularly in children and individuals with difficult venous access. These features reduce the physical and psychological stress associated with frequent intravenous infusions, and contribute to improved treatment adherence. Enhanced adherence is expected to translate into more consistent prophylaxis and improved long-term outcomes.

Reduced bleeding frequency and additional treatment convenience have been consistently associated with better quality of life, including physical functioning, emotional well-being, and social participation [58]. Furthermore, sustained bleeding prevention may help preserve joint health and functional independence over time. Overall, these advances highlight the growing importance of integrating clinical efficacy, safety, and patient-centered outcomes when selecting and optimizing non-factor therapies in routine hemophilia care.

In addition to the general improvements in treatment convenience and quality of life, practical patient management should be considered “who” should receive each non-factor therapy and “how” these agents should be integrated into prophylaxis strategies. In routine care, patient selection may further be guided by individual factors such as venous access, age (including pediatric patients), treatment burden and adherence challenges, bleeding profile (including breakthrough bleeds under prophylaxis), planned surgeries/invasive procedures, and concomitant thrombotic risk factors. These considerations become particularly important for “rebalancing” approaches that modify natural anticoagulant pathways. Furthermore, additional hemostatic therapy during major bleeding episodes or perioperative management be required and should be carefully individualized.

Conclusion

Non-factor therapies represent a major paradigm shift in hemophilia management by providing effective prophylactic hemostasis through mechanisms beyond conventional factor replacement. Mimicking FVIII cofactor activity or rebalancing natural anticoagulant pathways, offers therapies to achieve stable and sufficient coagulation potential independent of FVIII or FIX levels, and remain effective in patients with inhibitors.

Clinical evidence demonstrates that non-factor therapies substantially reduce bleeding rates, lessen treatment burden, and improve quality of life. Nevertheless, challenges remain, including thrombotic risk, the lack of standardized laboratory monitoring techniques, and concerns regarding long-term safety and cost. Ongoing research, real-world data, and individualized treatment approaches will be essential for the optimization of safe and sustainable use of non-factor therapies and to further improve outcomes for individuals with hemophilia.
