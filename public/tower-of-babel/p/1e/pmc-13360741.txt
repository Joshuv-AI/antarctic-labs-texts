Prevalence and Clinical Correlates of Intraspinal and Vertebral Anomalies in Congenital, Adolescent Idiopathic, and New-Onset Scoliosis: A Comparative Study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Faisal M Alkhunein, Mishari N Alanezi, Nwaf A Alshahir, Abdulkarim S Alsulimi, Abdulrahman H Salman, Habib Ullah Chaudhary, Abdulmajeed A Alzakri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To explore the prevalence of and clinical associations between intraspinal and vertebral abnormalities in patients with congenital scoliosis (CS), adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), and early-onset scoliosis in Saudi Arabia.Methods:This retrospective cohort investigation included patients aged 18 years or younger diagnosed with CS, AIS, or new-onset idiopathic scoliosis who underwent full-spine magnetic resonance imaging between 2015 and 2025 at a tertiary center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Clinical and radiological data were collected, and intraspinal and vertebral anomalies were classified. Statistical analyses included Chi-square, Fisher's exact test, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with significance set at p < 0.05.Results:A total of 127 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria; idiopathic scoliosis accounted for 80.3% and CS for 19.7%. Intraspinal anomalies were identified in 7.9% of patients, with a higher prevalence in CS (24%) than in idiopathic scoliosis (3.9%). Low conus was the most common anomaly (40%). Vertebral defects in CS were mainly failures of formation (76%), most commonly hemivertebrae, predominantly affecting the thoracic spine. Congenital scoliosis was significantly associated with a higher incidence of intraspinal anomalies (p = 0.004).Conclusion:Intraspinal anomalies were substantially more common in CS than in idiopathic forms in this Saudi cohort, underscoring the importance of routine preoperative magnetic resonance imaging to guide risk assessment and surgical planning.

Introduction

Scoliosis is a 3-dimensional malformation of the spine, characterized by sagittal and coronal deformities and axial vertebral rotation, commonly measured using the Cobb angle [1]. Congenital scoliosis (CS) arises from abnormal vertebral formation or segmentation that occurs during the first few weeks of gestation [2]. In comparison, adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a spinal curvature of at least 10° that develops between the ages of 10 and 18 years without any identifiable cause, and it represents the most common type of scoliosis [3]. Early-onset scoliosis (EOS), defined as a curvature of 10° or greater before the age of 10 years, without a clearly identifiable etiology, represents a distinct clinical entity, owing to its potential impact on thoracic cage development and long-term pulmonary function [4].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) identifies associated intraspinal anomalies, including syringomyelia, Chiari malformation, tethered cord, and vertebral segmentation defects, which may be clinically silent, yet have significant implications for deformity progression and overall management. A recent meta-analysis reported that approximately 37% of patients with CS demonstrated such abnormalities [5].

These neural axis anomalies can influence surgical planning and increase the risk of neurological injury when not recognized before corrective procedures. Recent evidence supports the use of selective preoperative MRI in patients with certain characteristics, such as atypical curve patterns, neurological findings, or early-onset deformities [6].

Although the international literature continues to expand, the prevalence and clinical significance of intraspinal anomalies in scoliosis remain poorly studied in Middle Eastern populations, particularly in Saudi Arabia. Regional epidemiology may differ owing to genetic, environmental, and healthcare-related factors [7].

This study aimed to explore the prevalence of and clinical associations between intraspinal and vertebral abnormalities in patients with CS, AIS, and EOS in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

This retrospective cohort analysis included patients aged 18 years or younger who were diagnosed with CS, AIS, or new-onset idiopathic scoliosis; all patients underwent full-spine MRI at the time of presentation. Patients were excluded if they had neuromuscular scoliosis, inadequate MRI quality, incomplete spinal coverage, scoliosis secondary to spinal tumors or infections, or incomplete core clinical data. Clinical and radiological information was retrieved from electronic medical records and imaging archives for all patients evaluated or surgically treated for scoliosis over a 10-year period (2015–2025) in a tertiary center in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The research team performed data collection.

A total of 127 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria: 102 had idiopathic scoliosis, including 97 with AIS and 5 with new-onset scoliosis, and 25 had CS. The collected variables included health variables (age at admission, sex, body mass index [BMI]) and imaging variables (thoracic and lumbar Cobb angles, curve pattern, and presence of spinal or vertebral anomalies). Intraspinal anomalies were classified as diastematomyelia, syringomyelia, tethered cord, low conus, and Arnold–Chiari type I malformation. Vertebral anomalies were classified according to the Hedequist and Emans [8] criteria as follows: failure of formation, including hemivertebra and wedge vertebra; failure of segmentation, including unsegmented bar and block vertebra; or mixed type involving both formation and segmentation defects.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Medicine, (Research Project E-25-10096). The research team ensured that the study adhered to established ethical guidelines.

Statistical data analysis

Data analysis was performed using RStudio (version 2024.9.1.394; Boston, MA, USA) with R version 4.4.2. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR) because of non-normal distribution. Between-group comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using Pearson's Chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare medians between groups. Univariate comparisons were performed to analyze scoliosis subtypes and their associations with intraspinal lesions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics by scoliosis type

Among the 127 patients, idiopathic scoliosis was more common (n = 102, 80.3%) than CS (n = 25, 19.7%). The median age at admission was 15 years (IQR = 13.0 to 17.0), and the majority were female (69.3%). The median BMI was 18.9 kg/m2 (IQR = 16.2 to 22.0). Regarding curve patterns, 64.6% had double curves, followed by the thoracic (20.5%) and lumbar (15%) curves. Most patients had right thoracic curves (79.5%), whereas 20.5% had left thoracic curves. The median thoracic Cobb angle was 44.8° (IQR = 33.2 to 58.3), and the median lumbar Cobb angle was 35.6° (IQR = 27.6 to 45.0; Table 1).

Based on scoliosis type, the median age at admission was significantly lower in CS (10.0 years, IQR = 7.0 to 14.0) than in idiopathic scoliosis (15.0 years, IQR = 14.0 to 17.0, p < 0.001). Curve laterality differed significantly between the groups, with left thoracic curves being more common in CS (48%) and right thoracic curves being more frequent in idiopathic scoliosis (86.3%, p < 0.001). The lumbar Cobb angle was significantly higher in CS (median = 44.6°, IQR = 33.2 to 48) than in idiopathic scoliosis (median = 34.4°, IQR = 27.0 to 41.0, p = 0.040). No significant differences were observed regarding sex distribution, BMI, curve pattern, or thoracic Cobb angle (Table 1).

Prevalence and characteristics of intraspinal anomalies

Intraspinal anomalies were present in 7.9% of patients (10 of 127). The prevalence of intraspinal anomalies was 24% (6 of 25) among patients with CS, whereas it was 3.9% (4 of 102) among those with idiopathic scoliosis.

A low conus was observed in 40% of the patients, Chiari I in 30%, tethered cord in 30%, and syringomyelia in 30%. Diastematomyelia occurred in 10% of the patients. The anomalies were mainly located in the lumbar (35.8%) and cervical (28.6%) regions (Table 2).

Prevalence and characteristics of vertebral anomalies in CS

Among the vertebral anomalies, 76% had failure of formation, 16% had failure of segmentation, and 8% had mixed anomalies. The most common specific findings included a single hemivertebra in 52%, multiple hemivertebrae in 32%, and block vertebrae in 24%. Regarding anomaly location, most were in the lower thoracic region (41.4%), 20.7% were in the middle thoracic region, and 13.8% were in both the cervical and lumbar regions (Table 3).

Discussion

This study outlines the prevalence of intraspinal and vertebral anomalies and their association in patients with congenital and idiopathic scoliosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate these associations in the Saudi population. In our study, the prevalence of intraspinal anomalies in patients with CS was 24.0%. This rate is lower than those reported in several populations, including the Indian (35%) [9], Turkish (53.7%) [10], and Chinese populations (29.4% and 30.3%) [11,12]. However, it is slightly higher than the prevalence reported by Khan et al. [13] in the Pakistani population (20.5%). The prevalence of intraspinal anomalies in idiopathic scoliosis was 3.9%, which is considerably lower than that observed in CS, consistent with previous studies [14,15], and likely reflecting the typically lower association between idiopathic scoliosis and congenital neural axis abnormalities compared with congenital forms of scoliosis.

Low conus (40%) was the most common intraspinal anomaly identified in our cohort, differing from the findings of previous studies [9,10,16]. This discrepancy may be attributed mainly to the relatively small sample size in our study. The lumbar region was mostly affected site (35.7%), lower than that reported by Ghandhari et al. [17]. This finding is consistent with the predominance of low conus anomalies, as these typically occur in the lumbar region.

In our cohort of patients with CS, the vertebral defects included failure of formation (76.0%), failure of segmentation (16%), and a mixed pattern (8%), consistent with previous reports [15,18], which similarly described formation defects as the predominant structural abnormalities in CS. This predominance is attributable to the high frequency of disruption in vertebral embryonic development during the early stages.

Hemivertebra was the most frequent vertebral defect in this series, occurring as a single lesion in 52% of affected patients and as multiple lesions in 32%, followed by block vertebra in 24%. The predominance of hemivertebrae in our cohort aligns with previous studies [16,19], reflecting the fact that asymmetric failure of formation is one of the most common developmental anomalies of the vertebral column.

In the present study, vertebral defects were most frequently located in the middle-to-lower thoracic region. This distribution is consistent with previous studies [17,19], which likewise identified the thoracic spine as the most commonly affected area. The predominance of thoracic involvement may be related to the complexity of vertebral formation during embryonic development.

In the present study, intraspinal anomalies were markedly more frequent in patients with CS than in those without congenital vertebral malformations (60% vs. 16.2%), a finding consistent with previous study comparing these 2 groups [9]. Although the existing literature directly comparing congenital and non-CS remains limited, both our results and the available evidence suggest a substantially higher prevalence of neural axis abnormalities in patients with congenital deformities. This gap in the literature, together with the observed differences, underscores the importance of routine preoperative MRI in individuals with CS to ensure early detection and appropriate management of associated intraspinal anomalies.

Previous studies suggested several potential predictors of intraspinal neural axis abnormalities in patients with idiopathic scoliosis and CS, including female sex and left thoracic curves. For example, Zhang et al. [20] reported a predominance of neural axis abnormalities among patients with left thoracic scoliosis. Similarly, Wu et al. [12] suggested an association between female sex and intraspinal deformities, although contrasting findings have also been reported, such as those of Ghandhari et al. [17], who found no correlation between sex and the presence of intraspinal anomalies.

In contrast to these earlier reports, the present study did not identify any significant associations between sex, curve laterality, or curve pattern, and the presence of intraspinal neural axis abnormalities. None of these variables demonstrated predictive value within our cohort.

However, this study has several limitations. It was conduct at a single center with a relatively small sample size—particularly for the CS subgroup (n = 25)—which limits the generalizability and precision of the prevalence estimates. Despite these limitations, the findings highlight an essential clinical implication: Patients with CS should undergo routine preoperative MRI because they demonstrate a substantially higher prevalence of intraspinal anomalies that may influence management and surgical planning.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that intraspinal anomalies are considerably more prevalent in patients with CS than in those with idiopathic scoliosis. Additionally, vertebral anomalies—particularly formation defects such as hemivertebrae—also occur predominantly in the congenital group. No clinical or radiological factors, including sex, curve pattern, or curve laterality, were identified as predictors of intraspinal anomalies. These results highlight the need for routine preoperative MRI in patients with CS to detect clinically silent abnormalities that may affect management and surgical planning.
