A novel synbiotic formulation prevents calcium oxalate stones by restoring gut microbiota homeostasis
Authors: Xian-Miao Li, Yirixiatijiang Amier, Wen-Long Wan, Yong-Qi Wang, Guo-Rui-Yu Lyu, Jun-Lin Lu, Jing-Yi Rao, Yuan-Yuan Yang, Yang Xun, Xiao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium oxalate (CaOx) nephrolithiasis, a highly prevalent renal disorder, underscores an urgent demand for novel therapeutic strategies to prevent stone formation. Accumulating evidence has elucidated a robust link between renal CaOx stone formation and gut microbiota dysbiosis, suggesting microbial modulation as a potential therapeutic target. Recently, microbiome-targeted interventions, particularly synbiotic formulations, have demonstrated promising therapeutic efficacy in mitigating hyperoxaluria and preventing renal stone formation.ResultsWe developed a novel synbiotic formulation containing Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Lacticaseibacillus casei, Bifidobacterium breve, and prebiotic galactooligosaccharides (GOS). Subsequently, the synbiotic or its individual components (multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS) were administered to ethylene glycol (EG)-induced hyperoxaluric rats via daily oral gavage for 28 days. We found that the synbiotic formulation exhibited superior anti-nephrolithic activity compared to the multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS alone, attributable to the synergistic effects of probiotic-prebiotic combinations. Remarkably, synbiotic supplementation not only significantly reversed the EG-induced reductions in gut microbiota richness and evenness, but also restored the microbiota architecture. Additionally, a significant increase was observed in the relative abundance of multiple beneficial genera implicated in short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) production, including Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, UCG_009, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 and Phascolarctobacterium. Furthermore, synbiotic supplementation reduced systemic oxalate overload by alleviating intestinal barrier damage and modulating mucosal oxalate transporter expression.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that synbiotic intervention effectively reverses EG-induced hyperoxaluria by reversing gut microbiota dysbiosis to modulate oxalate homeostasis. Thus, this study not only proposes a synbiotic formulation for nephrolithiasis prevention but also elucidates its mechanistic basis, offering potential therapeutic advancements in disease management.Keywords: Calcium oxalate, Synbiotic, Gut microbiota, Intestinal barrier, Oxalate homeostasis

Background

Nephrolithiasis, one of the most prevalent urological disorders worldwide, affects an estimated 10% of the global population [1]. With its high recurrent nature (exhibiting 50% 5-year and 60%–80% lifetime recurrence rates), renal stones impose a substantial burden on public health systems [2]. Calcium oxalate (CaOx) accounts for the most prevalent subtype of renal stones, representing approximately 80% of all cases [3]. Owing to its intricate pathophysiology and multifactorial nature, the underlying mechanisms of nephrolithogenesis remain incompletely understood. Hyperoxaluria is recognized as a major risk factor for nephrolithiasis, and urinary supersaturation coupled with low pH serves as the driving force for CaOx crystal formation [4].

The trillions of bacteria colonizing the human gut are intricately linked to host health and the pathogenesis of diverse diseases. Notably, accumulating research indicates that the gut microbiota plays a vital role in kidney stone formation. On one hand, epidemiological investigations have demonstrated a significant association between antibiotic exposure and increased prevalence of nephrolithiasis, potentially mediated through gut microbiome disruption [5, 6]. On the other hand, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing analyses have confirmed distinct gut microbial disparities in kidney stone patients compared to healthy individuals [7].

Previous studies on nephrolithiasis have demonstrated that gut microbiota can regulate oxalate homeostasis, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for hyperoxaluria. Through intrinsic metabolic pathways, certain microbial species directly utilize oxalate as an energy source, thereby reducing intestinal oxalate absorption [8]. In addition, the gut microbiome is crucial for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity; compromise of this barrier function enhances paracellular oxalate absorption, ultimately contributing to systemic oxalate overload [9–13]. Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota-derived metabolites modulate intestinal barrier function and enter systemic circulation to reach the kidney, thereby remotely regulating kidney function via the gut-kidney axis [14, 15]. In healthy individuals, 20–50% of systemic oxalate derives from dietary absorption. Despite the abundance of oxalate in foods, nearly 90% of oxalate is metabolized by gut microbiota [8]. Previous research on oxalate-metabolizing gut microbiota has primarily focused on Oxalobacter formigenes that exhibits the highest oxalate-degrading capacity. While, administration of O. formigenes fails to consistently demonstrate significant efficacy in reducing urinary oxalate levels. Emerging research indicates that oxalate-metabolizing activity may be distributed across diverse taxa (including Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Bacillus) via complex metabolic networks, rather than limited to specific strains [16]. For clarity, throughout this manuscript, the term “Lactobacillus” refers broadly to lactic acid bacteria historically classified under the genus Lactobacillus [17].

Recently, microbiome-directed interventions have emerged as a promising strategy for renal disease management [18]. Among these available therapeutic strategies, synbiotics—combinations of probiotics and prebiotics—demonstrate superior modulatory effects on the gut microbiota, thereby conferring significant health benefits to the host [19]. Beyond their individual microbiota-modulating capacity, prebiotics in synbiotic formulations selectively enhance probiotic colonization and metabolic functionality, creating a synergistic effect for improved gut microbiota modulation. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains are the most widely utilized probiotics, demonstrating a high safety profile for oral administration in humans. These two classes of probiotics not only effectively degrade oxalates but also prevent renal stone formation through the secretion of beneficial metabolites [20, 21]. Notably, recent studies have identified that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (formerly Lactobacillus plantarum) and Lacticaseibacillus casei (formerly Lactobacillus casei) exhibit renal-protective and anti-nephrolithic properties [21–24]. However, current investigations into synbiotic applications for kidney stone prevention are still remarkably scarce. Although these studies have consistently observed the anti-nephrolithic effects of synbiotics, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated [25–27].

Here, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of a novel synbiotic formulation containing Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, Bifidobacterium breve, and prebiotic galactooligosaccharides (GOS) in alleviating ethylene glycol (EG)-induced hyperoxaluria, renal injury, and CaOx crystal deposition, using a validated rat model of secondary hyperoxaluria. Through an in-depth investigation of its anti-nephrolithic mechanisms, we found that these therapeutic effects were strongly associated with the restoration of gut microbiota dysbiosis and modulation of oxalate homeostasis. These findings underscore the critical role of gut microbiota in stone formation and reveal a potential therapeutic strategy for nephrolithiasis prevention.

Methods

Bacterial strain and cultivation

The strains used in this study were Lp. plantarum ATCC 8014, Lc. casei ATCC 393, and B. breve ATCC 15,700. All strains were commercially sourced from Beijing Baio Bowei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). As type strains of non-human origin, they were obtained from well-established international culture collections. All strains were preserved in 25% (v/v) glycerol for long-term storage at − 80 °C. For experimental preparation, Lp. plantarum and Lc. casei were cultured in DeMan, Rogosa and Sharpe medium (MRS) (HB0384-1, Haibo, Qingdao, China), while B. breve was grown anaerobically in DSMZ Medium 58 (Bifidobacterium Medium, hereafter referred to as DSMZ 58) (HB8527-2, Haibo, Qingdao, China). After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, all cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 5 min. The bacterial pellets were washed twice with sterile saline, resuspended in sterile saline, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL for further experiments.

Probiotic proliferation and oxalate-degrading tests

The growth of Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, and B. breve in the presence of GOS was assessed. The GOS (BioDuly, Nanjing, China) used in this study was a freeze-dried powder comprising oligosaccharides with a degree of polymerization (DP) of 2–8 (predominantly DP 2–5), which corresponds to molecular weights of ~ 342–1314 Da. Each bacterial strain was individually inoculated into MRS or DSMZ 58 medium containing 1% (w/v) GOS to achieve an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of approximately 0.1. Control cultures were prepared in the same medium without GOS. All cultures were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 36 h, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the OD600 at 3-hour intervals.

Each bacterial strain was inoculated (initial OD600 = 0.1) into two separate conditions: (1) medium with 1% (w/v) GOS and sodium oxalate, and (2) medium with sodium oxalate only (without GOS). Animals

Thirty-six male Sprague-Dawley rats (6 weeks old, weighing 210–240 g) were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animals Co., Ltd. (Changsha, Hunan, China). All rats were housed in standard cages within a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The rats were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (a 12-hour light/dark cycle, 22 °C constant temperature, and low-noise environment) with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and sterile water. At the experimental endpoint, rats were fasted for 4–6 h with free access to water prior to anesthesia induction via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, dissolved in sterile saline). Anesthesia depth was rigorously monitored through loss of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes. Terminal blood collection was performed via inferior vena cava puncture under maintained anesthesia, followed by exsanguination as the primary euthanasia method in accordance with AVMA guidelines. Death was confirmed by cessation of vital signs (absent heartbeat, respiration, and corneal reflexes) for > 3 min. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved and supervised by the Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center of Tongji Hospital (approval no. TJH-202409003).

Experimental design

After one week of acclimatization, rats were randomly divided into six groups (n = 6 each): control group, model group, multi-strain probiotic group (MP), prebiotic group (GOS), low-dose GOS synbiotic group (LG-Synbiotic), and high-dose GOS synbiotic group (HG-Synbiotic). The control group received oral gavage of sterile saline and had ad libitum access to sterile water. In addition to sterile saline gavage, the rats in the model group had ad libitum access to sterile water with 1% (v/v) EG. The MP group received daily oral gavage of a mixed multi-strain probiotic saline suspension containing Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, B. breve (1:1:1 ratio, 3 × 108 CFU/rat/day). The GOS group was given daily oral gavage of GOS saline suspension (2 g/kg). the LG-Synbiotic group received daily oral gavage of a saline suspension containing both multi-strain probiotic and GOS (2 g/kg), while the HG-Synbiotic group received the same preparation at 4 g/kg. All groups except the control group had ad libitum access to drinking water containing 1% EG. After 28 days, all rats were euthanized, and fecal samples, urine, blood, kidneys, and intestinal tissues were collected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 1).

Histological analysis and renal injury evaluation

Kidney and intestinal tissues were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Kidney Sects. (3–5 μm) were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Von Kossa (VK). Duodenal and ileal sections were similarly processed for H&E staining. The stained sections were examined and imaged using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci-L, Japan) for histological analysis. Renal crystal deposition was quantified using a standardized scoring system, and ImageJ software (v1.8.0.112) was employed for the analysis of VK-stained sections. Renal tubular damage was evaluated based on histopathological features in H&E staining, including lumen dilatation, epithelial necrosis, cast formation, and loss of brush border. Two senior pathologists, blinded to the study design and experimental groups, independently scored the H&E-stained sections. For each sample, five randomly selected fields of view (200× magnification) were examined, and tubular injury was semiquantitatively graded on a 0–4 scale according to the percentage of affected tubules: 0 (no injury), 1 (< 25%), 2 (25–50%), 3 (50–75%), or 4 (> 75% injury) [28].

TUNEL staining

Paraffin-embedded kidney tissues Sect. (4 µm) were deparaffinized in xylene twice for 15 min each and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. The sections were then immersed in 3% H2O2 for 30 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and incubated with TdT reaction mix (60 min, 37 °C) in the dark. Subsequently, they were washed three times with PBS and counterstained with 4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min. Finally, the stained sections were examined by a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI3000B, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry staining (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded intestinal tissue Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene twice for 15 min each and rehydrated through graded ethanol. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Subsequently, Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂, followed by incubation with 3% BSA to prevent nonspecific binding. Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for about 24 h, followed by incubation with HRP-polymer secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and DAB solution for 15 min. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-ZO-1 (1:200 dilution, Abcam), anti- occludin (1:200 dilution, Abcam), anti-SLC26A3 (1:200 dilution, Santa Cruz), and anti-SLC26A6 (1:200 dilution, Santa Cruz). Finally, the nuclei of intestinal tissue were counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. The protein expressions were evaluated using a light microscope. ImageJ software was used to quantitatively assess the stained area and optical density, and the integrated optical density value of each IHC image. The average optical density (AOD) of the digital images was determined as the representative protein staining intensity (reflecting relative proteins expression levels). The signal densities from five randomly selected tissue fields per sample were analyzed in a blinded fashion and subsequently subjected to statistical analysis.

Oxalate measurement of 24-hour urine and medium

After 28 days of experimental treatment, all rats were transferred to metabolic cages for 24-hour urine collection. The samples were centrifuged at 6000 g for 30 min, and the resulting supernatant was carefully collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Urinary oxalate concentrations were quantified using LC-MS/MS, with the identical analytical method applied for oxalate detection in bacterial culture medium.

Renal function

The fresh blood collected from rats using serum separation tubes was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min. The clear supernatant serum was then carefully aspirated and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis. The serum concentrations of blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine were quantitatively determined using an enzymatic assay performed on an automated clinical chemistry analyzer (Roche cobas® 8000 e 801, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in rat kidney and colon tissues were measured using commercially available ELISA kits (IL-1β: BP00120; IL-6: BP00158; TNF-α: BP00196; Baipeng Bio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing

Total microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the HiPure Stool DNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341 F (5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). PCR cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Danvers, MA, USA), quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel. The resulting PCR amplicons were subjected to sequencing using an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), yielding 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads. The raw MiSeq sequencing reads were processed by merging paired-end reads based on overlap regions, followed by quality filtering to remove low-quality sequences. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were then clustered at 97% similarity threshold for taxonomic classification.

Bioinformatic analysis

The raw sequencing data were processed using the DADA2 package (v1.26) within R (v4.2.1) for quality filtering, denoising, paired-end merging, and chimera removal, resulting in a table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the SILVA reference database (release 138, SSURef_NR99). Unclassified taxa were retained in the analysis as distinct units to avoid loss of biological information. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon) were calculated using QIIME2 (v2021.2.0). Statistical differences in alpha diversity indices among the six groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was assessed based on weighted UniFrac distances and visualized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), with statistical significance tested by PERMANOVA. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was employed to identify differentially abundant bacterial taxa (LDA score > 2, p < 0.05). PICRUSt2 (version 2.4.1) was used to predict KEGG functional profiles based on the ASV table, and the resulting predictions were aligned against the KEGG database to identify potential genes involved in oxalate degradation within the gut microbiota. Group comparisons of the predicted functional abundances were performed using the wilcox_test function from the rstatix package in R, and results were visualized with the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.2).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v9.5.1). Data for 24-hour urine oxalate and histological results were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. As the assumptions were met, differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, denoted as * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001, and **** P < 0.0001.

Results

Prebiotic GOS promoted the proliferation and oxalate-degrading activity of three probiotic strains in vitro

The present study initially selected three prevalent probiotic strains known for their oxalate-degrading capacities—Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, B. breve—as probiotic components of the synbiotic formulation to investigate their potential to reduce urinary oxalate levels and prevent the formation of CaOx nephrolithiasis [20, 29]. Subsequently, we focused on the well-known prebiotic GOS, which exhibit significant health-promoting effects on gut microbiota, particularly by enhancing the proliferation of probiotic species such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium [30, 31]. To investigate the biological effects of GOS on three probiotic strains, we conducted in vitro experiments to evaluate the impact of GOS supplementation in culture media on both microbial growth kinetics and oxalate catabolic activity. The results demonstrated that prebiotic GOS significantly enhanced both bacterial proliferation and oxalate degradation efficiency across all three probiotic strains (Fig. 2A, B).

Synbiotic supplementation attenuated CaOx crystal deposition and EG-induced renal injury

To further explore the effects of the novel synbiotic formulation containing Lp. plantarum, Lc. Casei, B. breve, and GOS on renal CaOx crystal deposition, we orally administered the synbiotic formulation and its individual components (multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS alone) to hyperoxaluric rats induced by 1% (vol/vol) EG in drinking water. After 4-week administration, rat kidneys and 24-hour urine were collected for analysis. VK staining revealed significantly elevated renal CaOx crystal depositions and VK staining values (quantified as relative area percentage, RA%) in the EG-induced model group compared to controls. Notably, synbiotic administration (regardless of low- or high-dose GOS dosage) substantially alleviated these pathological manifestations, with significantly superior efficacy compared to individual treatments with multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS alone, demonstrating a distinct synergistic effect between the probiotic and prebiotic components (Fig. 3A, B). Further urinary analyses demonstrated significantly elevated oxalate concentrations in the model group compared to controls. Notably, synbiotic supplementation not only effectively reduced urinary oxalate levels but also outperformed the individual treatments of either the multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS alone. (Fig. 3C). Collectively, these findings suggest that the synbiotic formulation containing Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, B. breve, and GOS confers therapeutic protection against EG-induced hyperoxaluria and CaOx nephrolithiasis, attributable to the synergistic effects of probiotic-prebiotic combinations.

Given that CaOx crystal deposition is a known contributor to renal tubular injury, we next examined whether synbiotic supplementation could attenuate EG-induced kidney damage [32]. As observed in H&E-stained sections, the kidneys of model group rats exhibited extensive intratubular crystal formation, along with interstitial fibrosis accompanied by dense inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, severe tubular injury, evaluated by tubular necrosis, dilation, intratubular casts, and brush border loss, was significantly ameliorated by synbiotic supplementation (Fig. 4B). Moreover, serum urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Scr), well-established biomarkers of renal dysfunction [33], were significantly elevated in model rats but markedly reduced in synbiotic-treated rats (Fig. 4C, D).

Additionally, accumulating evidence indicates that the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhanced apoptotic activity in renal tissue, caused by CaOx crystal adhesion, mechanistically contribute to the formation of Randall’s plaques [34, 35]. To investigate these pathological alterations, we quantitatively measured renal inflammatory cytokines using ELISA and evaluated apoptotic activity by TUNEL assay (Fig. 4E, F,G). The results demonstrate that the synbiotic formulation exerts protective effects against CaOx crystal adhesion-induced renal injury, thereby indirectly suppressing Randall’s plaque formation. Therefore, these findings highlight the therapeutic promise of this synbiotic formulation in alleviating EG-induced renal injury in preclinical models.

Synbiotic supplementation ameliorated gut microbiota dysbiosis in EG-induced hyperoxaluric rats

Fecal 16 S rRNA gene sequencing on day 28 of synbiotic intervention evaluated its effects on EG-induced gut microbial dysbiosis in rats with hyperoxaluria. We assessed alpha-diversity using the Shannon and Chao1 indices, which showed a decreasing trend in EG-induced hyperoxaluria rats compared with the control rats. Strikingly, synbiotic supplementation not only significantly increased both the Shannon and Chao1 indices compared to the model group but also showed a tendency to be higher than those of the control group, indicating a healthier and more resilient gut microbiota (Fig. 5A). Moreover, compared to multi-strain probiotic mixture alone, the administration of GOS also increased microbial alpha diversity to some extent, suggesting that the prebiotic contributes to overall gut microbiota improvement. Beta-diversity, a measure of compositional dissimilarity, was analyzed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on weighted Unifrac distance. The results revealed a significant overall difference in gut microbial community structure among the six subgroups. Furthermore, visual inspection of the PCoA plot showed that the communities of the four intervention groups were closer in composition to the control group than to the model group, suggesting a partial restoration of a healthy microbiota composition following treatment (Fig. 5B).

Permutational multivariate analysis of variance with Adonis was employed to test for significant differences between sample groups. Furthermore, bacterial community composition and relative abundance across groups were characterized at both the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 5C, D). At the phylum level, ten bacterial phyla had relatively higher abundances, with only Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Actinobacteriota collectively accounting for over 90% of the gut microbiota across all groups (Fig. 5C). At the genus level, Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Turicibacter, and Romboutsia were the absolute dominant genera in the control group, each exhibiting a relative abundance of 5%–10%. In the model group, the relative abundances of Akkermansia and Allobaculum were elevated compared to the control group, while Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Turicibacter, and Prevotella showed reduced levels. Relative to the model group, both synbiotic groups exhibited increased Prevotellaceae_Ga6A_1group and Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group abundances but decreased Romboutsia, Allobaculum, and Akkermansia levels (Fig. 5D).

We further employed LEfSe (linear discriminant analysis effect size) to identify differentially abundant taxa at the genus level among the six groups, revealing 28 discriminant genera (LDA score ≥ 2) with significant differences in relative abundance (Fig. 5E, F). Notably, the control group exhibited significant enrichment of seven bacterial genera: Anaerostipes, Lachnospira, Monoglobus, 28_4, Weissella, Anaerosporobacter, and Streptococcus. In contrast, four other genera—Bacteroides, Faecalibaculum, Lachnoclostridium, and Sellimonas—were significantly enriched in the model group. Notably, previous meta-analysis revealed significantly elevated Bacteroides abundance in kidney stone patients, with its levels showing inverse correlations with all short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [7]. In the two synbiotic groups, the low-dose GOS synbiotic group was enriched with Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, UCG_009, Desulfovibrio, whereas the high-dose GOS synbiotic group exhibited enrichment of five genera: Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, Phascolarctobacterium, UCG_003, Bilophila, and Klebsiella. Comparative analysis of the two synbiotic formulations revealed statistically significant but biologically marginal differences in microbial taxa, with all differentially abundant genera exhibiting exceptionally low relative abundances (∼0.001%) (Fig. 5G). This suggests that the two GOS-dose variants exerted nearly identical modulatory effects on core gut microbiota, while demonstrating only minimal specificity toward peripheral community members. Therefore, we hypothesize that preventive synbiotic supplementation may effectively enhance gut microbiota diversity and modulate microbial community structure in rats.

Synbiotic supplementation modulated oxalate homeostasis by alleviating gut barrier damage and modulating oxalate transporters

The gut microbial regulation of oxalate homeostasis primarily involves two key aspects: microbiota-mediated oxalate catabolism and the indirect modulation of intestinal mucosa-dependent oxalate absorption. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying synbiotic-driven gut microbiota remodeling in suppression of renal CaOx crystal, we first predicted the relative abundance of genes encoding oxalate-metabolizing enzymes using PICRUSt2 analysis of 16 S rRNA data [36]. Unexpectedly, we observed no significant differential expression of four key oxalate-metabolizing enzymes among all experimental groups: CoA: oxalate CoA-transferase, oxalate decarboxylase, formate dehydrogenase, and formyl-CoA transferase (Fig. 6A). These findings indicate that renoprotective effects of the synbiotic formulation in hyperoxaluria-associated CaOx nephrolithiasis may be mechanistically distinct from gut microbiota-mediated oxalate catabolism.

Additionally, the gut microbiota plays an essential role in maintaining intestinal barrier function [9–11]. Disruption of intestinal barrier integrity enhances paracellular oxalate absorption, leading to systemic oxalate overload, and consequently increasing the risk of hyperoxaluria and CaOx nephrolithiasis [12, 13]. Consequently, we performed H&E staining of rat duodenal and ileal tissues to examine villus length, crypt depth, and inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 6B). The results demonstrated that EG-induced hyperoxaluric rats exhibited villus atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and significantly reduced villus-to-crypt (V/C) ratios, indicative of intestinal barrier damage. Notably, these pathological alterations were effectively reversed by synbiotic supplementation (Fig. 6C). In colon, the elevated inflammatory cytokine levels accompanying intestinal barrier damage were significantly alleviated by synbiotic supplementation (Fig. 6D). Additionally, tight junctions represent crucial intercellular connections between adjacent intestinal epithelial cells, where dysregulation of tight junction proteins, particularly ZO-1 and occludin, results in compromised barrier function and subsequent intestinal hyperpermeability [37, 38]. Synbiotic supplementation significantly attenuated the EG-induced downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin expression in both duodenal and ileal mucosa, suggesting restoration of intestinal epithelial tight junctions (Fig. 6E, F,G).

Furthermore, the intestine facilitates oxalate transport through apical membrane oxalate transporters, facilitating transcellular absorption and excretion of oxalate [8]. Specifically, SLC26A6 mediates oxalate excretion in the ileum [39, 40], while SLC26A3 promotes oxalate absorption in the colon [41, 42]. Emerging studies demonstrate that gut microbiota modulates oxalate homeostasis by regulating the expression of oxalate transporters [29, 43–45]. Therefore, we examined the expression of transporters SLC26A6 and SLC26A3 in the apical membrane of intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 7A). Notably, synbiotic supplementation significantly upregulated SLC26A6 expression while downregulating SLC26A3 (Fig. 7B, C). Taken together, our findings demonstrate that synbiotic supplementation modulates oxalate homeostasis by alleviating intestinal barrier damage and bidirectionally regulating intestinal oxalate transporters.

Discussion

Nephrolithiasis is a highly prevalent disorder worldwide, with CaOx stones accounting for approximately 80% of all cases, in which hyperoxaluria serves as a key pathogenic driver [3, 4, 46]. In recent years, the link between gut microbiota dysbiosis and oxalate homeostasis dysregulation has garnered increasing attention [29]. Notably, synbiotic interventions—combining probiotics and prebiotics—have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for nephrolithiasis and other renal disorders [25, 47, 48]. In the present study, we established an EG-induced hyperoxaluric rat model to investigate the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of a synbiotic formulation (comprising Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, B. breve, and GOS) for preventing CaOx nephrolithiasis. Our findings demonstrate that the synbiotic intervention effectively restores oxalate homeostasis by repairing intestinal barrier integrity and modulating oxalate transporter expression, thereby significantly ameliorating EG-induced hyperoxaluria, renal injury, and CaOx crystal deposition (Fig. 8). These therapeutic effects were strongly associated with the restoration of EG-induced gut microbiota dysbiosis.

Schematic of the synbiotic-mediated prevention of renal CaOx stones

Given the role of gut microbiota in oxalate degradation, previous research on gut microbiota in nephrolithiasis has primarily focused on O. formigenes which exhibits the highest oxalate-degrading capacity. Unfortunately, the utility of O. formigenes in nephrolithiasis prevention remains clinically limited, due to challenges in commercial-scale production and controversial therapeutic efficacy [20]. These limitations underscore the need for probiotic interventions to shift focus toward alternative strains with oxalate-degrading capabilities, particularly via combinatorial multi-probiotic administration. In this study, we formulated a multi-strain probiotic preparation consisting of three well-characterized oxalate-degrading strains (Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, and B. breve) and quantitatively demonstrated their oxalate-degrading capacity through in vitro assays. Following combination with the prebiotic GOS to form a synbiotic, we observed enhanced pro-proliferative and oxalate-degrading capacities. Following a 4-week intervention in rats, the synbiotic formulation showed superior efficacy in both reducing urinary oxalate excretion and suppressing renal CaOx crystal formation compared to individual treatments with multi-strain probiotic mixture or GOS alone, indicating that its therapeutic effects are mediated through probiotic-prebiotic synergistic interactions. In addition, the comparable therapeutic effects observed with both low- and high-dose GOS in the synbiotic formulation suggest that the beneficial action may depend more on the presence of critical prebiotic-probiotic interactions than on the absolute dosage of GOS components.

Analogously, an animal study demonstrated that exogenous supplementation with a bacterial mixture comprising Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, L. acidophilus, and B. longum significantly reduced urinary oxalate levels in rats [49]. In contrast, two clinical studies revealed that oral administration of a Lactobacillus-Bifidobacterium probiotic mixture reduced urinary oxalate levels in only a subset of enrolled patients [50, 51]. Another clinical trial conducted by Tavasoli et al. also found no significant effect of a probiotic supplement containing L. acidophilus and B. lactis on urinary oxalate levels, potentially due to the insufficient bacterial colonization [52]. Given the inconsistent efficacy observed with standalone probiotic mixtures, further research remains necessary to identify superior probiotic strains and incorporate efficacy-enhancing adjuvants.

The concept of ‘synbiotics’ was first introduced in 1995 to describe the synergistic effects of probiotic-prebiotic combinations [53]. Beyond improving probiotic viability, synbiotics also contribute to the growth of specific native bacterial strains resident in the host gut. Mechanistically, synbiotics exert pleiotropic effects encompassing regulation of gut microbiota metabolic activity, improvement of intestinal barrier function, selective enrichment of beneficial microbiota, and competitive exclusion of enteric pathogens [19]. Although the therapeutic potential of synbiotics has been preliminarily investigated in gastrointestinal disorders, obesity, diabetes, and cancer, their role in nephrolithiasis prevention remains underexplored. In an in vitro study, the prebiotic inulin enhanced the growth and oxalate-degrading capacity of Lactobacillus spp. strains. When co-cultured with inulin, these multi-strain probiotic mixture demonstrated a significantly higher oxalate degradation rate, along with a synergistic inhibitory effect against Escherichia coli [26]. In another animal study, the synbiotic formulation—comprising four Lactobacillus strains, two Bifidobacterium strains, two Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (formerly Lactobacillus paracasei) strains, and herbal extracts—significantly attenuated EG-induced hyperoxaluria, renal injury, and calcium oxalate crystal deposition [27]. Recently, the commercially available synbiotic have also been shown to restore the oxalate-degrading capacity in the feces of ceftriaxone-treated rats and significantly reduce urinary oxalate excretion [25]. Based on the published literature cited above, all evaluated synbiotics contained probiotic strains primarily from the genera Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, known for their oxalate-degrading potential. Although these studies collectively suggest the potential of synbiotics in oxalate degradation and kidney stone prevention, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. Given that synbiotics act as modulators of gut microbiota and intestinal function, investigating post-treatment alterations in gut microbial composition and intestinal function in rat models would help elucidate the anti-nephrolithic mechanisms of synbiotics.

Accumulating evidence reveals distinct gut microbiota profiles between kidney stone patients and healthy controls, suggesting that the entire gut microbial community, rather than individual bacterial species, plays a significant role in nephrolithiasis formation. Therefore, we initially compared gut microbiota composition across the six experimental groups, identifying significant dysbiosis in EG-induced hyperoxaluric rats characterized by reduced α-diversity and altered microbiota architecture. These alterations mirror the dysbiosis patterns found in nephrolithiasis patients, demonstrating that the EG-induced model effectively recapitulates the gut microbial changes associated with human stone formers [16, 54, 55]. Remarkably, synbiotic supplementation not only significantly reversed the EG-induced reductions in both richness and evenness of the gut microbiota, but also restored the microbiota architecture. Although we observed that synbiotic supplementation did not significantly enhance the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium at the genus level, this finding aligns with previous studies in various disease models demonstrating comparable genus-level abundances following administration of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium strains [56, 57]. This phenomenon may be explained by (i) the limited taxonomic resolution of genus-wide effects (where increases in specific species do not necessarily elevate the entire genus) and (ii) the competitive proliferation of native bacterial strains under synbiotic conditions. Additionally, we found equivalent anti-nephrolithic effects between high- and low-dose GOS groups, suggesting a potential threshold effect in GOS-mediated microbiome modulation and nephrolithiasis prevention. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that the bifidogenic effect of GOS reaches a plateau at a relatively low dose (5 g), with no significant additional benefits observed when the dose was increased to 10 g [58]. Further analysis via 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed limited disparities in bacterial abundances between the two groups. Although statistically significant in a few taxa, these differences were minimal and deemed biologically irrelevant, supporting the conclusion that beyond a certain dosage, increased GOS intake does not induce substantial structural changes in the gut microbiota.

The oxalate-degrading gut microbiota plays a crucial role in reducing dietary oxalate absorption and alleviating systemic oxalate load. To elucidate the impact of the tested synbiotic on intestinal oxalate homeostasis, Using PICRUSt2-based functional prediction, we estimated the relative abundance of genes encoding oxalate-metabolizing enzymes in the gut microbiota, but observed no significant alterations in their predicted abundance following synbiotic treatment. This finding prompted us to investigate whether the impact of the synbiotic on oxalate homeostasis is associated with intestinal barrier function. As a dynamic interface separating luminal contents from systemic circulation, the intestinal mucosal barrier actively regulates oxalate absorption and excretion. The intestinal mucosa mediates oxalate absorption through two principal mechanisms: the predominant paracellular pathway regulated by tight junctions, and the transcellular pathway mediated by specialized oxalate transporters, such as SLC26A3 [8]. In the present study, EG-induced hyperoxaluric rats exhibited characteristic intestinal barrier damage, accompanied by reduced expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and occludin). However, synbiotic supplementation significantly attenuated EG-induced impairments in intestinal barrier integrity and restored ZO-1/occludin expression levels in both duodenal and ileal mucosa.

Microbial homeostasis critically sustains gut barrier function, with disrupted intestinal permeability now recognized as a key factor in the pathophysiology of numerous diseases [59]. In both synbiotic groups, we observed a significant increase in the relative abundance of several beneficial bacterial genera, including two Prevotellaceae members, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, UCG_009, Phascolarctobacterium, UCG_003, and Bilophila. Similarly, Zhang et al. demonstrated that beneficial gut bacteria, including UCG_003 and Bilophila, can improve the intestinal barrier function by producing beneficial metabolites [60]. UCG_003 from the Oscillospiraceae family has been found to be significantly reduced in both Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis [61]. Moreover, members of the Prevotellaceae family, including Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group and Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, along with UCG_009 from Butyricicoccaceae family, are recognized as probiotic strains capable of producing SCFAs [54, 62]. Candidatus_Saccharimonas showed a positive correlation with SCFA levels in both fecal and serum samples, suggesting a potential role in ameliorating diabetic renal injury [63]. As another major producer of SCFAs, Phascolarctobacterium has been demonstrated to reduce the risk of nephrolithiasis by enhancing the biosynthesis of all SCFAs, particularly butyrate [64, 65]. Mechanistically, SCFAs generated by gut microbiota during dietary fiber fermentation enhance intestinal barrier function through dual mechanisms: direct reinforcement of tight junction protein expression, and multifaceted regulation of epithelial homeostasis via anti-inflammatory effects, oxidative stress modulation, and goblet cell-derived mucus production, collectively ensuring optimal barrier integrity [66]. Furthermore, the synbiotic significantly downregulated the expression of the apical membrane oxalate transporter SLC26A3 in colonic epithelial cells, and upregulated SLC26A6, which is primarily localized at the apical membrane of ileal epithelial cells and facilitates systemic oxalate secretion into the intestinal lumen. Given established roles of SCFAs in modulating SLC26A3/A6 expression, we propose that the synbiotic’s bidirectional regulation of these transporters is mediated through gut microbiota-derived SCFAs [43].

While this study provides compelling evidence for synbiotic efficacy, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the study was conducted exclusively on EG-induced hyperoxaluria rats, and the potential preventive effects of the synbiotic formulation against human renal stones require further validation through clinical trials. Second, while a synergistic anti-nephrolithic effect was observed between the multi-strain probiotic mixture and GOS, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Future studies should employ multi-omics approaches (e.g., metagenomics and metabolomics) to elucidate the specific interactions between these components. Third, while our PICRUSt2 analysis provides initial insights into oxalate catabolism potential, it infers functions solely from 16 S rRNA-based taxonomy, which cannot resolve strain-level variations in oxalate catabolism. Future studies should integrate shotgun metagenomics to directly quantify oxalate-degrading genes, complemented by metatranscriptomics to assess their expression. Moreover, while our results highlight promising synbiotic candidates, their ideal compositional ratios, temporal administration windows, and inter-individual response variations warrant systematic exploration in future trials.

Conclusions

Collectively, the study indicates that gut microbiota-mediated regulation of intestinal epithelial function critically modulates oxalate homeostasis in the pathogenesis of secondary hyperoxaluria. The synthetic formulation (containing Lp. plantarum, Lc. casei, B. breve, and GOS) restores oxalate homeostasis by educating dysbiotic gut microbiota, effectively reducing urinary oxalate excretion and inhibiting CaOx nephrolithiasis formation. Compared to previously reported bio‑formulations, our synbiotic offers distinct advantages: it exhibits a robust safety profile by utilizing well-documented probiotic strains and the prebiotic GOS, both of which have been extensively validated for safe human consumption. Furthermore, unlike many peer formulations that rely solely on theoretical synergy, we provide empirical evidence confirming the superior anti-nephrolithic efficacy of our synbiotic compared to its individual components, underscoring its proven synergistic benefits. These findings advance our understanding of microbiota-mediated nephrolithiasis prevention and may inform future therapeutic strategies. The continued development and refinement of this synbiotic formulation shows considerable promise for translation into a clinically viable adjunctive therapy for recurrent CaOx nephrolithiasis.

Looking ahead, the unique clinical value proposition of this synbiotic lies in its targeted mechanism, evidence-based synergy, and favorable safety profile—positioning it as a practical adjunctive therapy for recurrent calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis. Unlike conventional approaches that focus primarily on dietary modification or urinary citrate supplementation, our formulation addresses the gut–kidney axis directly by modulating microbial communities to restore endogenous oxalate homeostasis. This pathophysiology-driven strategy may benefit patients with hyperoxaluria who respond inadequately to existing treatments. Moreover, the use of well-established probiotics and GOS facilitates regulatory approval and accelerates clinical adoption. Future efforts will focus on validating these outcomes in larger patient cohorts and exploring companion biomarkers for patient stratification, ultimately paving the way for personalized microbiome-directed interventions to reduce stone recurrence.
