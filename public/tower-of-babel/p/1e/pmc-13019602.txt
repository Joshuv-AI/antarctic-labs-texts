Catalytic degradation of 4-nitrophenol and methylene blue using ceria-coated gold nanoparticles as a dual-functional catalyst
Authors: Yifan Cui, Junqi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA structurally tunable and morphologically homogeneous core/shell nanocatalysts of cerium dioxide-coated gold nanoparticles (AuNPs@CeO2) were successfully prepared. By investigating AuNPs@CeO2 with different CeO2 shell layer thicknesses on the hydroreduction of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP), as well as probing the optimal photocatalysis of AuNPs@CeO2 at different irradiances and methylene blue (MB) concentrations, the optimal thicknesses of AuNP-covered CeO2 layers were identified as 16.43 ± 1.17 nm. AuNPs@CeO2 exhibited excellent catalytic performance for both heterogeneous catalytic degradation of 4-NP and photocatalytic degradation of MB. With the addition of 50 μL of AuNPs@CeO2, the normalized apparent rate constant reached 3.145 × 104 min−1 mmol−1, and the optimal degradation rate of methylene blue (MB) reached 99.2% with the addition of 0.5 mL of AuNPs@CeO2. This not only provides a novel nanocatalyst for the efficient degradation of organic pollutants, but also provides a new research idea and theoretical basis for the design and development of composite functional materials.Subject areas: Electrochemical materials science, Electrochemistry

Introduction

Core-shell structured nanomaterials composited with precious metals and oxides have received much attention due to their unique physicochemical properties. Such composite structures usually consist of precious metals (e.g., platinum, palladium, and gold) as the core or shell layer and oxides (e.g., titanium dioxide and cerium dioxide) as the corresponding part together. Significant optimization of the functionality of composite nanostructures can be achieved by precisely modulating the composition, size, and interfacial interactions between the core and shell.1,2,3,4,5,6

Numerous attempts have been made to enhance the catalytic activity of composite nanostructures of noble metals and cerium dioxide.7,8,9,10,11 For example, Li et al.12 embedded gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) into cerium oxide nanoparticles (Au/CeO2) with enhanced antioxidant activity and used them to scavenge excess reactive oxygen species produced by oxides for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease. However, the structure fabrication process is cumbersome and requires the introduction of hyaluronic acid for additional modification. Bastús et al.13 synthesized citrate-stabilized gold@cerium dioxide (Au@CeO2) hybrid nanocrystals by a one-pot hydrothermal method. This method is able to prepare flower-like Au@CeO2 hybrid nanocrystals by simply adjusting the ratio of Au3+ and Ce3+ ions precursors, but there is no study on how to control the uniform thickness of the cerium dioxide layer and analyze the photocatalytic performance. Li et al.14 obtained a versatile approach for coating CeO2 on monometallic Au and bimetallic Au@Pt, Au@Pd nanocrystals by heterogeneous nucleation and growth. However, more exploration is still needed to expand the application of this structure in pollutant control. In some cases, the CeO2 shell layer may grow unevenly, which affects the performance of the nanostructures. Jiang et al.15 synthesized (platinum metal yolks)/(porous cerium dioxide shells) nanostructures and applied them to the selective oxidation of benzyl alcohol under visible light, but avoiding the collapse of porous structures and improving the stability and reusability of the structures need to be particularly considered. López et al.16 fabricated a highly active CO oxidation catalyst by loading Au onto CeO2 through a nanocasting route. In addition, Primo et al.17 prepared nanocomposite structures of CeO2 loaded with trace amounts of gold by the biopolymer template method and used them for the photocatalytic water splitting under visible light. Tanaka et al.18 prepared Au/CeO2 structures with a strong surface plasmon resonance and achieved the selective oxidation of alcohols to aldehydes or ketones under green light irradiation. However, these structures were based on the random adhesion of Au nanoparticles on cerium dioxide and could not satisfy the plasma metal nanocrystals bound to cerium dioxide in well-defined geometries for better light absorption, charge carrier excitation, and facilitation of chemical reactions to proceed. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) are particularly attractive as a catalytic core due to their excellent chemical stability, tunable localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), and efficient electron-donating capability under irradiation, which are highly beneficial for both chemical reduction and photocatalytic oxidation processes. Recent advances have focused on immobilizing AuNPs onto solid substrates (e.g., polythiourea-modified filter paper or lithium-ion battery separators) to enhance their recyclability and operational stability.19,20 While these strategies successfully address catalyst recovery, they often compromise the precise structural control at the nanoscale and the synergistic effects arising from intimate core-shell interactions.

4-Nitrophenol (4-NP) is one of the highly toxic organic pollutants among the substances bearing a nitro group.21,22,23 It is recognized as a priority hazardous pollutant by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) due to its poisonous and volatile nature.24 4-NP is used in several industries, namely the manufacturing of drugs (e.g., acetaminophen), pesticides (e.g., ethyl and methyl parathion), pH indicators, and dyes.25 4-NP enters the aqueous environment through the accumulation of waste products from numerous industries. Meanwhile, the water pollution caused by the mixture of common dyes and methyl blue dyes poses a potential threat to the maintenance of water quality.26,27,28,29 Therefore, developing an efficient and reusable catalyst to remove 4-NP and the textile organic dye methylene blue from water is an important priority task.30,31,32 Moorthy et al.33 synthesized rutile-type TiO2 (R-TiO2) nanocrystalline materials with polyhedral structure by the sol-gel method and optimized their MB degradation rate through high-temperature treatment. However, under 6 h of light exposure, the degradation rate could only reach 90%. Naveen et al.34 utilized the extract of spent coffee grounds as a reducing agent through in situ green synthesis method. The pyridinic N anchored Ag2O/Au co-modified porous silica hybrid material was synthesized, and the high-efficiency degradation of 4-NP was achieved. However, the Ag-based catalyst still lags behind the Au-based catalyst in terms of stability. While the pursuit of high-performance nanocatalysts is intensifying, growing attention is being paid to their potential environmental and health impacts.35,36 The persistence, mobility, and (eco)toxicity of nanomaterials upon release into the environment are critical concerns for their sustainable application.37 Therefore, alongside developing efficient materials, understanding their environmental footprint is becoming an integral part of catalysis research.

In the present research, the core-shell structure of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) coated with a thin shell layer of cerium dioxide (AuNPs@CeO2) was prepared, and the nanostructures were applied as catalysts for heterogeneous catalyzation of 4-NP and photocatalytic degradation of MB. Through the analysis of the preparation process and the study of the catalytic performance, not only was the controllable preparation method of the shell layer of AuNPs@CeO2 obtained, but also the optimal CeO2 cladding thickness of 16.43 ± 1.17 nm was obtained, and the tunable characteristics of localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of the metal/semiconductor composite nanoparticles were realized. This kind of uniformly morphological core-shell nanomaterial not only exhibits excellent catalytic performance, but also enhances the utilization efficiency of precious metals and avoids the secondary environmental pollution caused by precious metals. Moreover, this study significantly enhanced the heterogeneous catalytic degradation performance of the material for 4-NP, achieving an apparent pseudo-first-order rate constant (Kapp) of 3.145 × 104 min−1 mmol−1, while the degradation rate for methylene blue (MB) reached 99.2%, it was proved that the composite nanostructures could exhibit the best performance for both the heterogeneous catalysis and the photocatalysis.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of AuNPs@CeO2

Figure 1A shows a TEM image of the synthesized AuNPs. It can be observed that the as-prepared grown AuNPs exhibit homogeneous morphology characteristics with uniform size distribution (72.52 ± 3.55 nm, Figure 1B) and excellent monodispersity. Figure 1C exhibits the TEM images of the AuNPs@CeO2 composites with a size of 105.54 ± 5.08 nm (Figure 1D). By successively introducing appropriate amounts of cerium nitrate (Ce(NO3)3) and EDTA-NH3, a cerium dioxide shell layer was successfully and uniformly encapsulated on the surface of the grown AuNPs, resulting in the construction of core-shell-structured nanomaterials with AuNPs as the core and cerium dioxide as the shell. Figures 1E and 1F show the SEM images of AuNPs and AuNPs@CeO2, respectively. AuNPs have good electrical conductivity, which can show the spherical morphology characteristics more clearly in SEM (Figure 1E), and when the surface is coated with semiconductor characteristics of cerium dioxide, the SEM images of composited nanospheres become obviously rather blurry under the same testing conditions, which indirectly indicates that the AuNPs are coated with a semiconductor cerium dioxide layer (Figure 1F). Figure 1G shows UV-vis absorption spectra of AuNPs and AuNPs@CeO2 colloidal solutions. A single resonance absorption peak of AuNPs was clearly observed at 539 nm, indicating that the AuNPs were spherical in structure, whereas the resonance absorption peaks were red-shifted by about 23 nm after the successful coating of the cerium dioxide layer on the surface, and this spectroscopic red-shift was a remarkable feature of larger nanoparticles and the formation of a new coating layer on the nanoparticles surface. This spectral characterization result matches quite well with the above TEM images and can indicate the successful preparation of the core-shell structure. Figure 1H shows XRD pattern of AuNPs@CeO2, and according to JCPDS No. 04–0784, it can be seen that the positions of the diffraction peaks at 2θ = 38.54°, 44.88°, 65.04°, and 77.78° correspond to (111), (200), (220), and (311), respectively, of the face-centered cubic (fcc) structure of Au,38 and the diffraction peak positions at 2θ = 27.86°, 31.41°, 45.84°, and 69.04° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (400) crystal faces of CeO2 in the fluorite-type cubic structure, respectively, according to JCPDS No.34-0394.39 XRD analysis indicates that the synthesized AuNPs@CeO2 structure has high purity and good crystallinity.

We regulated the thickness of the shell layer to prepare AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanomaterials with different sizes and shell layer thicknesses. In the experiments, the precise regulation of the thickness of the shell layer of CeO2 grown on the surface of AuNPs was realized by modulating the additions of Ce(NO3)3 and EDTA-NH3. Specifically, the volume ratio of Ce(NO3)3 solution (0.1 mol/L) to EDTA-NH3 solution (0.01 mol/L) was strictly maintained at 1:10, and a total volume of 110 μL, 330 μL, 550 μL, and 770 μL of the mixed solution of Ce(NO3)3 and EDTA-NH3 was added to prepare different shell layer thicknesses of the AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterials. The obtained core-shell nanoparticles with different ceria shell thicknesses are accordingly denoted as AuNPs@CeO2(10), AuNPs@CeO2(30), AuNPs@ CeO2(50), and AuNPs@CeO2(70), where the number in parentheses represents the volume (in μL) of the Ce(NO3)3 precursor added. As shown in Figures 2A–2D, with the gradual increase in the addition of Ce(NO3)3 and EDTA-NH3 mixed solution, the CeO2 shell layer on the surface of the AuNPs was gradually transformed from the initial discrete island-like structure (Figure 2A) to the homogeneous thin layer (Figure 2B) to the denser and thicker shell layer (Figure 2C), and a large area of sticking phenomenon appeared in case of over-addition (Figure 2D). Figure 2E shows a statistical plot of the thickness of the uniformly thin CeO2 shell layer in Figure 2C, where it can be seen that the thickness of the CeO2 cladding layer is 16.43 ± 1.17 nm. The cladding layer resulting from the other conditions was not statistically analyzed due to the non-uniformity of the thickness. Figure 2F illustrates the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) images of the AuNPs@CeO2, in which continuous diffraction rings were observed, illustrating the polycrystalline nature of the nanoparticles. In addition, in the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images (Figures 2G and 2H), a crystalline spacing of 0.236 nm corresponds to the gold (111) crystalline plane, while a crystalline spacing of 0.270 nm corresponds to the cerium dioxide (200) crystalline plane. These results provide direct evidence for the crystal structure and elemental distribution characterization of the AuNPs@CeO2 composites. Compared to AuNPs, the characteristic absorption peaks of the UV-vis of the AuNPs@CeO2 showed a gradient redshift and gradual broadening with the increase in the amount of the mixture solution (Figure 2I). This indicates that by regulating the amount of the capping agent addition, the different thicknesses of the CeO2 layer can be capped on the surface of the AuNPs in order to form the different surface plasmonic characteristics of the AuNPs@CeO2. It is noteworthy that variations in shell thickness not only alter the physical dimension but may also profoundly influence key catalytic parameters of ceria. As the shell thickness increases: (i) the specific surface area may change, consequently affecting the adsorption capacity of reactants; (ii) the oxygen vacancy concentration (crucial for oxygen storage capacity, OSC) may be modulated, wherein thinner shells potentially exhibit higher defect density due to electronic interactions with the gold core, while thicker shells may behave more like bulk ceria; and (iii) furthermore, the shell thickness may tune the band gap of the composite material, thereby influencing its photocatalytic activity (if photocatalysis is involved). Consequently, the observed changes in catalytic activity represent a complex interplay of these factors, with shell thickness serving as an intuitive, comprehensive indicator.40,41 Moreover, EDS and quantitative data in Figure 2J also illustrate the successful preparation of AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanomaterials.42,43,44

The prepared AuNPs@CeO2 nanoparticles were subjected to detailed elemental analysis and characterization using a HAADF-STEM, and the results are shown in Figure 3. Figure 3A clearly shows the HAADF of the nanostructure and the mapping of mainly composed elements, such as gold (Au), cerium (Ce), and oxygen (O). Since the elements Ce and O are mainly enriched in the peripheral region of AuNPs, it further confirms that the material is a core-shell structure with AuNPs as the core and cerium dioxide as the shell. In addition, by linear scanning analysis of the surface of individual nanostructures (Figure 3B), it can also be observed that AuNPs@CeO2 contain the elements of gold (Au), cerium (Ce), and oxygen (O). It is noteworthy that the content of Ce and O elements is significantly lower than that of Au elements, a phenomenon that reinforces the existence of a CeO2 thin-shell cladding layer in the nanomaterials, which coincides with the original intention of this study aimed at realizing a semiconductor oxide thin-shell layer cladding precious metal nanostructure.

To further investigate the effect of Ce(NO3)3 precursor dosage on the CeO2 shell structure in the AuNPs@CeO2 composites and to gain deeper insight into the fundamental properties of the material, a series of characterizations was performed. Firstly, the XRD patterns (Figure 4A) clearly demonstrate the controllability of the shell thickness. Characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to both face-centered cubic Au and fluorite-structured CeO2 were simultaneously observed in all samples, confirming the successful formation of the Au-CeO2 composite across different precursor volumes. We observed that the sample with a precursor volume of 50 μL exhibited the highest intensity of the CeO2 diffraction peaks, which is consistent with the TEM results (Figure 2B–2D). Concurrently, the intensity of the Au diffraction peaks relatively weakened, aligning with the expectation of the core being encapsulated by a denser CeO2 shell. To further probe the chemical state and microstructure of the composite, a representative sample (AuNPs@CeO2(50)) was selected for XPS and Raman analysis. As shown in Figure 4B, pure CeO2 exhibits a sharp characteristic Raman peak at approximately 454.3 cm−1, belonging to the F2g vibrational mode of the fluorite structure of CeO2. However, in the Raman spectrum of the AuNPs@CeO2 composite, this characteristic peak displays a significant redshift to a lower wavenumber (∼450.4 cm−1).45,46 This phenomenon is a direct signature of an increased concentration of oxygen vacancies within the CeO2 lattice, leading to decreased lattice symmetry and weakened chemical bonds. The XPS survey spectrum (Figure 4C) confirms that the material is composed of Au, Ce, and O elements. In the Au 4f region (Figure 4D), the positive binding energy shift indicates electron transfer from Au to CeO2.47 The high-resolution Ce 3d spectrum (Figure 4E) shows that the intensity of the Ce4+ fingerprint peak (u''') at ∼916.8 eV is relatively weak, whereas the characteristic peaks for Ce3+ (v0 and u0) at ∼880.5 eV and ∼899.0 eV are significantly enhanced.48 This phenomenon can be attributed to the unique nature of this core-shell structure. XPS, as a surface-sensitive technique, primarily probes the surface region of the ultra-thin CeO2 shell. In the nanoscale CeO2 shell, the high proportion of surface atoms readily facilitates the formation of oxygen vacancies to reduce surface energy, consequently leading to the reduction of a substantial amount of Ce4+ to Ce3+.49 Therefore, the pronounced Ce3+ signal indicates the successful preparation of a CeO2 shell rich in oxygen vacancies, which are crucial for enhancing catalytic performance as active sites. Meanwhile, in the corresponding O 1s spectrum (Figure 4F), the enhanced intensity of the defect oxygen peak at ∼531.5 eV further confirms the formation of oxygen vacancies. The O 1s spectrum can be deconvoluted to three peaks at binding energies of 533 eV, 531.5 eV, and 530 eV, which can be ascribed to adsorbed oxygen (OAds), surface active oxygen (OSur), and lattice oxygen (OLatt), respectively. More importantly, this core-shell structure induces a strong metal-support interaction, resulting in electron transfer (based on XPS) and the generation of abundant oxygen vacancies (based on XPS and Raman). These characteristics are key to endowing this material with exceptional catalytic performance.

Catalytic properties of AuNPs@CeO2

Heterogeneous catalytic degradation of 4-NP

The prepared AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanomaterials were used as catalysts in catalytic experiments for heterogeneous hydrogen reduction of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) and photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) to analyze their catalytic activities. First, experiments using the hydrogen reduction of 4-NP to 4-aminophenol (4-AP) were used to evaluate the heterogeneous catalytic performance of AuNPs@CeO2. In the specific experimental procedure, the characteristic absorption peak of 4-NP at 317 nm originated from the chromophore group in its benzene ring structure, and after the addition of NaBH4, the absorption peak red-shifted to 400 nm, and the solution changed from light to bright yellow, indicating the conversion of 4-NP to 4-nitrophenol anion. Subsequently, AuNPs@CeO2 catalyst was introduced to initiate the reaction, and the reaction process was monitored in real time by UV-Vis, and a gradual decrease in the intensity of the absorption peak at 400 nm could be observed, along with the appearance of a new peak at 300 nm, which indicated that the 4-NP was gradually reduced to 4-AP. Meanwhile, the catalytic reaction exhibited excellent selectivity toward 4-aminophenol (4-AP), as confirmed by the disappearance of the characteristic peak of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) at 400 nm and the simultaneous emergence of the characteristic peak of 4-AP at 300 nm in the UV-Vis spectra. No discernible peaks corresponding to by-products were observed throughout the process (Figure 5A). After the completion of the reaction, the solution became colorless, and the absorption peak at 400 nm disappeared completely, confirming that 4-NP had been completely converted to 4-AP. Figure 5B shows the intensity change of the 4-NP absorption peak at 400 nm at the same time intervals, which indicates that 4-NP was catalytically reduced and its concentration was decreased. The duration of the catalytic reduction of 4-NP to 4-AP varied with the catalyst dosage, as shown in Figure 5C. Since the amount of NaBH4 was significantly higher than that of 4-NP, the reaction was considered to be a proposed first-order reaction with the kinetic equation ln(Ct/C0) = -t⋅ Kapp (where Kapp denotes the apparent rate constant in s−1, t is the time, and C0 is the initial concentration of the 4-NP solution, which is proportional to the initial absorption at 400 nm, Ct is the corresponding 4-NP concentration at time t, also proportional to the absorption at 400 nm). According to the slope of the linear fit of the kinetic equation, the rate constants corresponding to the amounts of AuNPs@CeO2 catalyst of 25, 50, 75, and 100 μL were 0.43206 min−1, 0.95805 min−1, 1.51306 min−1, and 2.24834 min−1, respectively.

We also experimentally compared the catalytic degradation performance of AuNPs, CeO2, and AuNPs@CeO2 with different shell thicknesses for 4-NP (Figure 5D). The results showed that the Kapp was only 0.00642 min−1 when catalyzed by AuNPs alone. The catalytic effect of CeO2 was also not satisfactory, with a Kapp of only 0.0642 min−1. In contrast, when AuNPs@CeO2 with different shell layer thicknesses were introduced into the system, the decrease of the characteristic absorption peak of 4-NP within the same reaction time increased significantly, which indicated that the thicknesses of both the gold nanocores and the CeO2 shell layer had a significant effect on the catalytic activity. Specifically, the thicker the CeO2 shell layer (i.e., the more Ce(NO3)3 was added), the higher the catalytic efficiency. In particular, the uniform-surface AuNPs@CeO2 structure prepared with the addition of 50 μL Ce(NO3)3 has the maximum catalytic reaction rate, which suggests that this optimized thickness of the shell layer (16.43 ± 1.17 nm) is able to exert the best catalytic performance. However, when Ce(NO3)3 was added up to 70 μL (Kapp = 0.93762 min−1), the Kapp of AuNPs@CeO2 was slightly lower than that of the case of adding 50 μL Ce(NO3)3 (Kapp = 0.95805 min−1). This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that the excess Ce(NO3)3 led to a large adhesion of the CeO2 shell layer in the outer layer, which impeded the effective contact between the inner gold nuclei and the catalytic environment, weakened the synergistic catalytic effect between AuNPs and CeO2, and ultimately led to the decrease of the catalytic efficiency. Essentially, the synergistic catalytic effect of AuNPs and CeO2 is related to the size of their work functions. This is because it is well known that AuNPs have a high work function (5.65 eV) and CeO2, as a semiconductor, has a low work function (4.64 eV).50,51,52 When AuNPs are in contact with CeO2, Fermi energy level alignment occurs, resulting in the transfer of electrons from AuNPs to CeO2, creating an electron-rich region. This electron transfer makes CeO2 more receptive to electrons, which promotes the reduction of 4-NP. Meanwhile, after the electron transfer from AuNPs to CeO2, the oxygen vacancy defects (because of the presence of Ce3+) in CeO2 further facilitated the electron transfer, which made the 4-NP molecules more susceptible to accepting electrons and converting them to 4-AP. This synergistic effect would significantly increase the rate of the catalytic reaction.

The normalized rate constants (Knor, ratio of Kapp to catalyst metal content) of AuNPs@CeO2 showed a clear dose effect (Figure 5E), which is similar to previously reported noble metal catalysts.53,54 Additionally, AuNPs@CeO2 showed excellent catalytic activity compared to other composite gold nanostructures (see Table 1). The Knor of this nanostructure is about 10 times higher than that of other materials (Table 1). In addition, the reusability of the catalyst is an important factor in practical applications. Based on the catalytic rate (Kapp), the catalytic rate of AuNPs@CeO2 for 4-NP reduction was close to 84% after 5 cycles (Figure 5F), proving the good stability and reusability for potential large-scale applications.

Photocatalytic degradation of MB

In addition, we analyzed the photocatalytic degradation ability of AuNPs@CeO2 for MB under a white light source. Kapp is equal to the linear coefficient obtained by comparing the logarithm of the ratio of concentration change at time t with time t; the above calculations can be used as an index to evaluate the catalytic activity of the catalyst.

We conducted a detailed study on the light conditions, the concentration of reactants, and the type of catalyst. Figure 6A demonstrates the UV spectra of AuNPs@CeO2 (adding 50 μL of Ce(NO3)3) for the degradation of MB at 250 μW/cm2 irradiance light source. Meanwhile, the intensity of the absorption peak of MB at 665 nm varied with time, as shown in Figure 6B. The photocatalytic performance of AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterials for MB was obviously different under different light irradiance conditions. The catalytic time was significantly shortened from 45 min to 15 min as the light source irradiance increased from 50 μW/cm2 to 650 μW/cm2. This indicates that the photocatalytic process has a significant dependence on the light conditions. The calculated Kapp increased from 0.05678 min−1 to 0.27163 min−1, which increased the photocatalytic efficiency by more than five times, and the degradation conversion rates all reached more than 95%, showing excellent degradation effects (Figure 6C). The most important point is that, under the maximum light irradiance (650 μW/cm2), the degradation rate of MB reached 99.2%. The time required for the photocatalytic degradation of MB was gradually contracted from 33 min to 18 min in the experiments with different MB dosages. As the amount of the catalyst increased from 120 to 360 μL, it was calculated that Kapp increased from the initial 0.08974 min−1 to 0.23643 min−1(Figure 6D). Additionally, AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterials have obvious photocatalytic performance advantages over AuNPs and pure CeO2. Figure 6E shows the photocatalytic process of AuNPs@CeO2, AuNPs, and CeO2 as catalysts for MB under the irradiation of a white light source of 250 μW/cm2. The results showed that the noble metal AuNPs had a poor photocatalytic effect on MB, and the intensity of the characteristic absorption peaks of MB only decreased by about 59.3% with a Kapp of 0.00381 min−1 after a reaction of up to 3 h. Pure CeO2 had a certain photocatalytic effect on MB, and the whole catalytic process took 30 min, with a conversion efficiency of about 83.8% and a Kapp of 0.032285 min−1. In contrast, the AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterials basically completed the catalytic process in 21 min, and their conversion efficiency was as high as 93.5%. This indicates that AuNPs significantly enhanced the photocatalytic degradation of MB with cerium dioxide. The main reason is that in photocatalysis, the AuNPs@CeO2 catalyst typically generates electron-hole pairs after absorbing light energy. These photogenerated carriers undergo redox reactions with reactant molecules on the catalyst surface to achieve degradation. AuNPs can act as electron trapping centers to promote the transfer of electrons from the CeO2 conduction band to the surface of AuNPs, thus inhibiting the complexation of electron-hole pairs and improving the separation efficiency of photogenerated carriers. In addition, AuNPs have a surface plasmon resonance effect, which enhances the absorption and scattering of light, thus increasing the utilization of light and further improving the photocatalytic efficiency.59

Meanwhile, we carried out a comparison of the catalytic effect with that of other previous similarly structured nanomaterials for the photocatalytic degradation of MB (Table 2). The photocatalytic efficiency of AuNPs@CeO2 was significantly higher than that of other previous similarly structured nanomaterials when the catalytic time, the degradation rate, and the Kapp were considered together. Moreover, the AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanomaterials had high reproducibility, and the catalytic rate could still reach 96% after five cycles of photocatalytic experiments (Figure 6F). Although the degradation intermediates were not directly identified in this study, it is well-documented in the literature that in analogous catalytic oxidation systems, MB typically undergoes a stepwise demethylation and aromatic ring cleavage pathway, ultimately mineralizing into CO2, H2O, NO3−, and SO42−. The bio-toxicity of these intermediate products is significantly diminished compared to the parent MB molecule, thus implying the potential of this catalytic system for environmental remediation applications.64,65 These results fully demonstrated the high efficiency and superiority of AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanomaterials in the photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants such as MB.

AuNPs@CeO2 confirmation of heterogeneous catalysis and the role of adsorption

To further investigate the potential contribution from adsorption to the reactant removal during the initial stage and unequivocally confirm the heterogeneous nature of the catalysis (i.e., to rule out homogeneous catalysis leached metal species), the following control experiments were conducted. To quantify the contribution of adsorption, control experiments were performed in the absence of reductant (NaBH4, for 4-NP reduction) and in the absence of NaOH and under dark conditions (for MB degradation), respectively. As shown in Figure 7A, only approximately 2.814% of 4-NP was adsorbed onto the catalyst within 4 min. Similarly, Figure 7B demonstrates an MB adsorption ratio of merely 9.691% over 30 min. The removal efficiencies via adsorption for both pollutants were significantly lower than their total removal efficiencies achieved in the catalytic reactions, indicating that adsorption played a limited role and catalytic degradation was the dominant process.

Heterogeneous catalytic nature

A filtration experiment was performed to verify the heterogeneous catalytic nature. As depicted in Figure 7C, when the catalyst was removed from the MB reduction system at 6 min (conversion ∼70%), the reaction rate decreased abruptly. Although the reaction did not cease instantaneously due to the inevitable trace residual catalyst, the concentration of the remaining reactant showed no significant decrease over the subsequent 12 min. This result strongly indicates that the leaching of Au or Ce species was negligible, and the catalytic reaction was primarily driven by the solid catalyst surface. Therefore, the observed high catalytic activity originates predominantly from the efficient inherent heterogeneous catalytic capability of the AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterial itself.

Mechanistic investigation via scavenger tests and Raman spectroscopy

To elucidate the mechanism of the photocatalytic degradation of MB, scavenger experiments were conducted to identify the dominant active species (Figure 8A). In the experiment of capturing photogenerated electrons, we originally planned to use silver nitrate as the scavenger. However, due to the spontaneous galvanic displacement reaction between the gold nucleus and Ag+ ions in the AuNPs@CeO2 catalyst, this reaction alters the surface structure and chemical composition of the catalyst, thereby interfering with and even masking the real photocatalytic process. Meanwhile, the generation of superoxide free radicals itself requires the participation of photogenerated electrons (O2 + e− → ⋅O2−). The addition of 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ) completely inhibited the reaction, which in itself constitutes the most powerful evidence that photogenerated electrons play a core role in the reaction. The introduction of BQ, a scavenger for superoxide radicals (⋅O2−), led to an almost complete suppression of the degradation process. In stark contrast, the addition of isopropanol (IPA, a ⋅OH scavenger) and EDTA-2Na (a h+ scavenger) resulted in only a minor inhibition of the photocatalytic efficiency.

Investigation of the photocatalytic degradation mechanism of methylene blue

(A) The corresponding pseudo-first-order kinetic fittings in the presence of different scavengers. The complete inhibition of the reaction by benzoquinone indicates that superoxide radicals are the predominant active species.

(B) Raman spectra of the samples during the degradation process.

This stark contrast unequivocally identifies the superoxide radical (⋅O2−) as the primary active species responsible for the degradation of MB over the AuNPs@ CeO2 catalyst. The generation of ⋅O2− can be attributed to the efficient transfer of photogenerated electrons from the conduction band of CeO2 (or from the surface plasmon resonance of AuNPs) to adsorbed oxygen molecules (O2). Meanwhile, the negligible impact of IPA and EDTA-2Na suggests that hydroxyl radicals and photogenerated holes play secondary, non-dominant roles in this specific reaction system. This proposed pathway is consistent with the band structure of the composite and the efficient charge separation facilitated by the Au core.

To track the degradation of methylene blue (MB) at the molecular level during the photocatalytic process, Raman spectroscopy was used to monitor the solid residues after different irradiation durations. As shown in Figure 8B, the pristine, undegraded MB sample displays its intense characteristic Raman peaks at 1620 cm−1 (aromatic C=C stretching), 1395 cm−1 (aromatic C-N stretching), and around 450 cm−1 (attributed to molecular skeletal deformation or C-S-C bending modes). As the irradiation time increases, the intensity of all these characteristic peaks across different wavenumber ranges decreases dramatically and continuously. After just 21 min of reaction, these fingerprint peaks belonging to the MB molecular skeleton become very weak or virtually disappear. This result provides direct spectroscopic evidence that the conjugated aromatic ring structure and the molecular skeleton of MB are effectively and rapidly disrupted and cleaved during the photocatalytic process, strongly supporting its degradation pathway via aromatic ring opening leading to eventual mineralization.

Proposed catalytic mechanism and reaction pathways

The principle of heterogeneous catalysis is similar to the above description (Figure 9A). The Au core is the main active center for the catalytic reduction of 4-NP, responsible for activating BH4− and generating active hydrogen species; while the CeO2 shell enhances the overall catalytic activity and stability through its adsorption and enrichment effect, the interface electronic effect with Au, and possibly the hydrogen spillover effect. Due to the higher work function of Au nanoparticles (5.65 eV) compared to that of CeO2 as a semiconductor (4.64 eV), when Au nanoparticles come into contact with CeO2, Fermi level alignment occurs, leading to electron transfer from Au nanoparticles to CeO2, forming an electron-rich region. This electron transfer makes CeO2 more prone to accepting electrons, thereby promoting the reduction of 4-NP. At the same time, after electrons transfer from Au to CeO2, the oxygen vacancy defects (the presence of Ce3+) in CeO2 further facilitate electron transfer, making 4-NP molecules more likely to accept electrons and convert to 4-AP. This synergistic effect significantly increases the rate of the catalytic reaction.50,51,52

Catalytic mechanism diagram

(A) Heterogeneous catalytic mechanism of 4-NP reduction to 4-AP on the surface of AuNPs@ CeO2 catalyst in the presence of NaBH4.

(B) Photocatalytic mechanism of MB degradation on AuNPs@CeO2 catalyst under visible light irradiation.

The root cause of the enhanced photocatalytic activity lies in the efficient charge separation guided by the Schottky junction formed between AuNPs and CeO2. Considering the energy band structure shown in Figure 9B, CeO2, as a typical n-type semiconductor, has a Fermi level higher than that of the metal Au. When they come into contact, a potential barrier known as a “Schottky junction” forms at the interface until the Fermi levels of both align.66,67 Under visible light excitation, electrons in the valence band of CeO2 transition to the conduction band, generating electron-hole pairs. Due to the lower Fermi level of Au compared to the conduction band bottom of CeO2 and the direction of the built-in electric field of the Schottky junction pointing from CeO2 to Au, photogenerated electrons spontaneously and unidirectionally transfer and accumulate on the Au nanoparticle core. The Au core thus acts as an efficient “electron trap,” significantly suppressing the recombination of electron-hole pairs. Meanwhile, the photogenerated holes remaining in the valence band of CeO2 are blocked by the potential barrier of the Schottky junction and cannot migrate back to the Au core, thus being effectively confined in the CeO2 shell. The high-density electrons accumulated on the Au core are effectively injected into adsorbed oxygen molecules, reducing them to superoxide radicals, which are the main active species in the degradation reaction, and the process can be represented as: O2 + e− → ⋅O2−. The holes confined in the CeO2 shell can either react with adsorbed water molecules or hydroxyl groups to generate hydroxyl radicals or directly oxidize the adsorbed methylene blue molecules on the catalyst surface. Additionally, the abundant oxygen vacancies in the CeO2 shell (confirmed by XPS and Raman spectroscopy) act as effective electron capture centers, further assisting in charge separation and optimizing the adsorption and activation pathways of O2.

Conclusion

In this study, monodisperse AuNPs@CeO2 core-shell structured composite nanomaterials were successfully coated with a CeO2 shell layer of controllable thickness on the surface of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) using a surface modification strategy with the chelator EDTA-NH3 with Ce(NO3)3. This method obtained the optimal catalytic activity by precisely regulating the CeO2 shell layer thickness. The experimental results show that when the shell thickness of AuNPs@CeO2 is prepared with 50 μL of Ce(NO3)3 is 16.43 ± 1.17 nm. The optimized structure has a Kapp = 0.95805 min−1 and a Knor up to 2.413×104 min−1⋅mmol−1 in the catalytic reaction of 4-NP; the photocatalytic activity for MB also showed remarkable photocatalytic activity under visible light irradiation, and the degradation conversion rate was up to more than 99%, with Kapp up to 0.272 min−1. Thus, the AuNPs@CeO2 nanomaterials exhibit excellent heterogeneous catalytic and photocatalytic properties with plasmon resonance absorption regions covering the visible to near-infrared regions. The successful construction of the Au@CeO2 core-shell nanostructure for dual-mode catalytic reduction of 4-NP and MB, along with performance optimization through precise control of shell thickness, provides a robust foundation for the development of high-performance and environmentally benign nanocatalysts.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this study reported representative AuNPs@CeO2 core-shell composite nanoparticles and applied them for catalytic research. The different sizes of gold nanoparticles and different thicknesses of cerium dioxide layers have not been systematically optimized. Additionally, expanding the catalytic effects on other substances can further illustrate the dual-function catalytic effect of these AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanoparticles.

Method details

Experimental reagents

Sodium citrate, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), tris(hydroxymethyl) aminomethane (Tris base, TB), chloroauric acid (HAuCl4⋅3H2O), cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3⋅6H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethylenediamine tetra acetic acid (EDTA, 99.5%), 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) and methylene blue (MB) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co. Ltd., (China). All chemicals were of analytical grade and used directly without further treatment. Ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18.25 MΩ⋅cm was used in all experiments.

Preparation of AuNPs

Based on previous studies,68,69 48 mL of ultrapure water was heated to boiling (∼95 °C) and 0.5 mL of 25 mM chloroauric acid was added. The solution was cooled to 90 °C and further heated for 10 min. Then, 1.5 mL of 1% (w/v) sodium citrate solution was quickly added, and the reaction lasted for 30 minutes to obtain a stable colloidal solution of gold seeds.

Subsequently, 47 mL of ultrapure water was heated to 90 °C, and then 2 mL of TB solution with a concentration of 0.1 M was added, followed by 0.5 mL of the above prepared gold seed solution and 0.5 mL of 25 mM chloroauric acid solution. Condensation and reflux for 30 min were used to prepare the grown AuNPs. The total volume of the reaction solution was controlled to be 50 mL, and the amount of gold seeds and chloroauric acid could be adjusted to obtain different sizes of grown AuNPs.

Preparation of AuNPs@CeO2 nanostructures

Take 10 mL of the above grown AuNPs and wash them by centrifugation at 5000 r/min for 2 times to remove the excess TB solution in the supernatant. After concentrating the cleaned AuNPs to 2 mL, 1 mL of 0.2 M CTAB was added and stirred it at 200 r/min for 30 min at 30 °C to make the CTAB molecules adsorbed well on the surface of AuNPs. Then, appropriate amounts of 0.01 M EDTA-NH3 and 0.1 M Ce(NO3)3 were added sequentially (keeping the volume ratio of EDTA-NH3 and Ce(NO3)3 at 10:1). The solution was thoroughly mixed under stirring at 300 r/min. Finally, the mixed solution was put into a reactor and kept it at 90 °C for 5 h to obtain AuNPs@CeO2 composite nanostructures with different shell layer thicknesses. In this synthetic route, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was employed as a surfactant. It serves a dual function: firstly, it forms micelles that act as a soft template, guiding the nucleation and growth of the nanoparticles, thereby controlling their size and dispersity. Secondly, its positively charged head groups adsorb onto the particle surfaces via electrostatic interactions, which effectively prevents agglomeration during the synthesis.70,71

Catalytic activity of AuNPs@CeO2 nanostructures

Heterogeneous catalytic performance on 4-NP

The amount and concentration of 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) with sodium borohydride (NaBH4) were consistent with our previous study.72,73,74,75 After the addition of a certain amount of AuNPs@CeO2 nanocatalyst into the reaction system, the reaction process was monitored by an ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (UV-vis) in the wavelength range of 250-500 nm at a scanning rate of 0.5 nm/s, and the catalytic activity of the catalyst was evaluated by the kinetic analysis of the intensity changes of the characteristic absorption peak (λmax) of the reactants. To evaluate the heterogeneous catalytic cyclic stability of AuNPs@CeO2 nanocatalysts, five consecutive catalytic experiments were performed under the same reaction conditions. Briefly, after the completion of the first catalytic reaction, 0.04 mL of 0.01 mol/L 4-NP solution and 0.16 mL of 0.5 mol/L NaBH4 solution were supplemented to the reaction system, and the molar ratio of the reactants (C4-NP: CNaBH4 = 1:200) was maintained to restart the catalytic reaction. The reusability performance and stability of the catalysts were analyzed by comparing the Kapp values of each cycle.

Photocatalytic degradation test for MB

Firstly, 240 μL of 1.5 mM methylene blue (MB) solution was mixed with 60 μL of 3% (w/v) NaOH solution and 4.5 mL of ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ⋅cm) (CMB:CNaOH=1:125), followed by the addition of 200 μL of AuNPs@CeO2 colloidal solution to form the reaction system. The photocatalytic reaction was carried out under the irradiation of a xenon (Xe) lamp with 250 μW/cm2 irradiance, maintained at a constant temperature of 25±0.5 °C and under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm. The photocatalytic performance of the catalysts was evaluated by kinetic analysis with 150 μL of reaction solution taken at 3 min intervals, and the intensity change of the MB characteristic absorption peak at 665 nm was detected by UV-visible spectrophotometer (scanning range, 300-900 nm, rate, 0.5 nm/s). Five consecutive catalytic experiments were performed under the same reaction conditions to evaluate the photocatalytic cycle stability of the AuNPs@CeO2 nanocatalysts. That is, after completing the first catalytic reaction, 240 μL of 1.5 mM MB solution and 60 μL of 3% (w/v) NaOH solution were supplemented to the reaction system to restart the catalytic reaction (CMB:CNaOH=1:125). The reusability and stability of AuNPs@CeO2 photocatalysts were obtained by comparing the Kapp values of each cycle.

Photocatalytic active species capture experiment

To identify the primary active species involved in the photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB), active species trapping experiments were conducted. Three different scavengers were selected for this purpose: 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ) as a scavenger for superoxide radicals (⋅O2-), isopropanol (IPA) as a scavenger for hydroxyl radicals (⋅OH), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na) as a scavenger for photogenerated holes (h+).76 All scavengers were pre-prepared as 10 mM aqueous stock solutions, with BQ solution being stored in the dark to prevent decomposition. The experiments were performed under conditions identical to the standard photocatalytic degradation procedure. Specifically, a series of reaction tubes containing an MB solution (1.5 mM) and the catalyst (0.5 mL) were prepared. Prior to light irradiation, 0.5 mL of one of the aforementioned scavenger stock solutions was added to each tube. Deionized water was then added to bring the total reaction volume to 5 mL, resulting in a final concentration of 1 mM for each scavenger in the reaction system. All reaction tubes were magnetically stirred in the dark for 30 minutes to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium. Subsequently, the Xe lamp was activated as the visible light source to initiate the reaction, and timing was started. Aliquots of 150 μL were sampled at 3-minute intervals. The absorbance of each sample was immediately measured at 664 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer to calculate the degradation efficiency of MB. Characterization

The morphology, structure, composition and surface properties of the composites of AuNPs and cerium dioxide (CeO2) were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), UV-vis, X-ray diffraction (XRD) and high angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). Prior to the measurements, the samples were dried at room temperature. TEM was obtained directly on a JEM-1400 microscope at 200 kV. SEM was carried out on a FEI Quanta 450 FEG SEM operated at 30 kV. All UV-vis spectra were recorded on a Cary 5000 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Petaling Jaya, Malaysia). HAADF-STEM measurements and elemental mapping analysis were obtained on a FEI Talos F200S transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were recorded on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS system with a standard Al-Kα source (1486.6 eV). Raman spectra tests were conducted on a LabRAM Odyssey (France) confocal micro-Raman spectrometer from Horiba with 532 nm laser sources.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data in this study were derived from three independent experimental repetitions (n = 3) and presented in the form of mean ± standard deviation (SD). The exact n value (the number of independent experiments) for each data set is explained in the corresponding legend.

All data plotting and statistical analysis were completed using OriginPro and Microsoft Office software. No other complex statistical methods or software were used in this study.
