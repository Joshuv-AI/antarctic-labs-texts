Impact of trace metals in fish waste-based organic fertilizer on growth promotion and nutritional components of spinach plant (Spinacia oleracea L.)
Authors: Parveen, Zubia Masood, Huma Batool, Afroz Riaz, Wajid Ali, Wali Khan, Muhammad Kabir, Muhammad Aslam, Mourad Ben Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fermented fish waste is increasingly utilized as a nutrient-rich organic fertilizer to enhance soil fertility and sustainable crop production. However, limited data exist on how trace metals in fish waste–based fertilizers affect plant growth and food safety. Therefore, our current study addresses this gap by evaluating the effects of liquid organic fertilizers (LOFs) derived from fermented carp fish waste on the growth and nutrient composition of spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.). This experimental study investigated varying LOF concentrations (10–50 ml) compared to water, NPK, and control treatments over a 60-day growth period. The overall results demonstrated that LOF applications, particularly at 40 and 50 ml, significantly improved leaf growth, photosynthetic pigments, and essential mineral accumulation while maintaining trace metal levels within safe consumption limits. These findings suggest fish waste–based LOFs can effectively promote spinach growth and nutritional quality, offering an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers that supports soil health and reduces environmental impact. Furthermore, our study provides a novel and comprehensive evaluation of the agronomic benefits and potential health risks associated with trace metals in fish waste-derived fertilizers. This aspect has rarely been explored in previous research.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of fish processing industries has led to the generation of substantial amounts of waste, such as heads, bones, viscera, scales, and wastewater. If not properly managed, these wastes can cause environmental issues including soil and water contamination, unpleasant odors, and greenhouse gas emissions1,2. Nevertheless, recent studies have identified fish waste as a valuable resource for producing nutrient-rich organic fertilizers and soil amendments, owing to its high content of essential macro- and micronutrients (e.g., N, P, Ca, Mg, Fe, Zn, Cu, Mn) that support plant growth, enhance microbial activity, and improve soil fertility3–5.

Valorization of fish waste through composting and fermentation techniques, such as Gunapaselam and Bakasang, has been shown to enhance nutrient availability and microbial activity while reducing phytotoxicity6–8. Consequently, fish waste–based liquid organic fertilizers (LOFs) are increasingly recognized as sustainable alternatives or supplements to chemical fertilizers, improving soil properties and crop productivity and aligning with circular economy principles and sustainable agriculture9,10.

Several studies have reported positive effects from the application of fish waste–derived fertilizers, which improve soil fertility and nutrient uptake by enhancing nutrient availability and microbial activity. This leads to increased growth and yield in various crops, including rice, maize, cucumber, lettuce, chilli, strawberry, onion, and legumes12–15. Fermented fish waste products such as Gunapaselam and liquid organic fertilizers (LOFs) can further improve nutrient uptake and biochemical characteristics of plants by increasing enzymatic activity and soil organic matter6,8,16.

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.), a nutrient-rich leafy green from the Amaranthaceae family, is widely cultivated as a winter vegetable in Pakistan. It is an excellent source of carotenoids, vitamins, and minerals such as phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, and iron, making it a valuable dietary component. However, spinach is also known for its high capacity to absorb and accumulate trace and heavy metals from soil and fertilizers, making it particularly sensitive to metal contamination17,18. Several studies have documented elevated levels of lead, cadmium, chromium, nickel, and arsenic in spinach grown in contaminated soils or under wastewater irrigation, raising concerns over food safety and human health risks19–22. Prolonged consumption of contaminated spinach may pose health risks, including neurological and muscular degeneration (similar to symptoms observed in Alzheimer’s disease)18.

Fish waste–based fertilizers inherently contain trace elements derived from aquatic environments and fish metabolism. Trace metals such as Fe, Zn, Cu, and Mn are essential for plant metabolic processes, including enzyme activation and photosynthesis; however, excessive accumulation of these metals may disrupt plant physiology and compromise food safety23–25. The concentrations of trace metals in fish waste fertilizers can vary depending on fish species, habitat, processing methods, and fermentation duration3,4.

Previous studies directly examining the effects of fish waste fertilizers on spinach growth and nutritional quality are still limited. The integrated use of fish waste-based fertilizers with inorganic nitrogen has been shown to improve spinach growth and leaf nutrient composition26,27. However, other evidence suggests that spinach can bioaccumulate metals from organic amendments, potentially transferring them into the human food chain28,29.

Despite the recognized agronomic benefits of fish waste–based liquid organic fertilizers, limited information is available on their influence on trace metal accumulation and the nutritional quality of leafy vegetables, particularly spinach. Most existing studies focus either on crop growth responses or on heavy metal contamination under wastewater or polluted soil conditions, with few integrating growth performance, nutrient enhancement, and trace metal bioaccumulation under fish waste fertilizer application30–32. Therefore, a systematic evaluation of trace metal accumulation, bioavailability, and food safety implications in spinach grown with fish waste–based fertilizers remains insufficient and warrants further investigation.

The present study aims to evaluate the effects of fish waste–based liquid organic fertilizer derived from two different carp species, on the growth performance, nutritional composition, and trace metal accumulation in spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) in comparison to traditional chemical fertilizers. This study specifically assesses changes in growth parameters, essential mineral content, and the uptake of selected trace metals in spinach cultivated under fish waste fertilizer application. This research seeks to promote sustainable farming by encouraging the recycling of fish waste, supporting eco-friendly agricultural practices, and reducing dependence on chemical fertilizers, thereby lowering input costs, improving vegetable yields, and enhancing food security.

Materials and methods

Samples collection and preparation

Collection of spinach seeds, clay pots and soil

Spinach seeds and clay pots were purchased from a local supermarket in Quetta. Soil was collected from a nearby nursery in Quetta.

Collection and characterization of fish wastes

Fish waste from major carps, including Catla catla and Labeo rohita comprising heads, tails, scales, viscera, skin, gills, and fins was collected from the Fish Market on Joint Road in Quetta, Balochistan, and stored in clean polythene bags (Fig. 1). The fish wastes were refrigerated at 4 °C in the Zoology Department laboratory of SBKWU for later use. To remove contaminants, the collected wastes were cleaned and rinsed with warm water. After cleaning, samples were weighed (1 kg each of Labeo rohita, Catla catla, and a mixed fish waste sample), placed in plastic bags, and stored at 4 °C using established preservation methods33,34.

Seed germination procedure

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea) seeds were planted from early spring through late July 2024 at two-week intervals (Fig. 2A). Following the recommendation of sowing 12 to 15 seeds per square foot, 6 to 7 seeds were planted per pot. Three replicates were maintained for each treatment, and a single plant was taken as an experimental unit6. Seeds were sown in these experimental pots: one irrigated with water only (control), one treated with NPK fertilizer (positive control), and the remaining pots treated with three fermented fish waste-based organic liquid fertilizers (LOFs) at varying concentrations of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 ml, as and illustrated in (Fig. 2B).

Seed germination of Spinacia oleracea in experimental pots (A) and preparation of three fermented fish waste-based organic fertilizers (LOFs) (B).

Preparation of fish waste based organic liquid fertilizers (LOFs)

Five-liter clean plastic pots were filled with 2.5 L of distilled water, 1 kg of powdered jaggery (Gur), and 1 kg of fish waste (Labeo rohita, Catla catla, and mixed fish waste). A thoroughly blended banana was also added. The pots were placed in a well-ventilated area with their mouths covered and stirred daily. After 21 days, the mixture was filtered to produce an organic liquid fertilizer. The filtrate was diluted by mixing 10 ml of LOFs with 1 L of distilled water (Fig. 2B) by following a slightly modified methodology of Thiripurasundari35. Using a slightly modified method from Ellyzatul et al.11, filtered extracts were applied directly to the topsoil of experimental pots in volumes of 10, 20, 40, and 50 ml (Table 1). Physical properties of the liquid fertilizer, including color, odor, pH, electrical conductivity, and dissolved oxygen, were analyzed7.

Experimental design

This experimental study examined the effects of varying dosages of fish waste-based liquid organic fertilizers (LOF) on the growth of Spinacia oleracea by using methods of Ellyzatul et al.11. A completely randomized design (CRD) was employed for this experimental study, with treatments applied at seven-day intervals over a 60-day trial period, however the more details can be seen in Table 1.

Measuring plant growth parameters using LOFs

After the seedlings stage, the plants were allowed to grow for up to 10 days following the method of Ekinci et al.26, with moisture content maintained by daily water spraying. The total trial period lasted 60 days and involved three types of liquid organic fertilizers LOFs (LOFCC= Catla catla-based Liquid Organic fertilizer; LOFLR = Labeo rohita-based Liquid Organic fertilizer; LOFM= Mixed species-based Liquid Organic fertilizer) were sprayed. Water, NPK, and diluted LOFs (every other day) were given to the treatment groups for 60 days, depending on their needs. After 60 days of seeding, the plants along with their roots and leaves were harvested. At harvest, the spinach’s shoots, roots, and leaves were measured. Growth parameters namely, the increase in the number of leaves per plant, leaf length and width, and shoot length were measured at 14-day intervals to assess the effects of varying doses of fermented fish waste fertilizer (FWF) on spinach growth, following the methods of Hassan et al.22, and Hussain et al.37. After maturity, the vegetables were harvested, and the fresh and dry weights (g) of the whole plants and leaves were recorded. Both plant and leaf samples were oven-dried at 105 °C for 48 h, and their dry weights (g) were measured (Table 2).

Nutrient analysis of liquid organic fertilizers (LOFs)

The amount of nutrients was measured by taking 100 g of liquid organic fertilizer (LOF) sample of Catla catla, Labeo rohita, and mixing, adding 20 ml of concentrated HNO3 heated for 10 min at 60 °C, filtering, and preparing it for further analysis of the metals using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) followed by Tiwow et al.7.

Analysis of macronutrients and micronutrients in plant leaves

Ten vegetable leaf samples were taken from each potted plant. After washing the vegetable samples with distilled water to get rid of airborne impurities, they were sun-dried and then oven-dried for 48 h at 68 °C then stored in sterile plastic bags for additional examination. For one hour, two grams of vegetable leaf samples (from each tested pot) were digested on a hot plate using a 2:1 ratio of concentrated H2O2 and H2SO4. The transparent solution that was left over after digestion was filtered. 50 ml of distilled water was used to dilute this filtrate. The atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) was used to measure the amounts of nutrients like Zn, Mn, Cu, Co, Ni, Fe, Cl, K, Ca, Mg, N, and P in vegetable leaf samples using the method of Ahmed et al.37.

Analysis of metal levels in soil samples

At the end of this experiment, two grams of soil samples were taken from each potted plant. These soil samples were digested for 60 min in a hot plate with concentrated H2O2 and H2SO4. The resulting filtrate was then diluted with 50 ml of distilled water. To measure the concentrations of metals and metalloids in the digested soil samples, the method described by37 was employed, utilizing an atomic absorption spectrophotometer.

Statistical analysis of data

All data analyzed in this study are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (S.D.). Statistical analysis of the data was performed using the GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software package. Differences in mean values among the seven treatment groups were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test40. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physicochemical properties of three fermented Liquid Organic Fertilizers (LOFs)

Key parameters during fermentation

The physicochemical properties of LOFCC, LOFLR, and LOFM were monitored over 21 days (Fig. 3A and D). Fermentation temperatures remained within the optimal 25–45 °C range, supporting stable microbial activity (Fig. 3A). The pH values (3.54–4.80) were slightly below the recommended 4.4–7.0 range, likely due to organic acid accumulation during fermentation (Fig. 3B). Electrical conductivity (1212–1635 µS cm⁻¹) indicated moderate ionic strength favorable for nutrient availability to plants, though below the optimal 2000–4500 µS cm⁻¹ (Fig. 3C). Dissolved oxygen was very low (0.10–0.20 mg L⁻¹), confirming anaerobic fermentation conditions (Fig. 3D). These physicochemical conditions indicate effective fermentation and the potential suitability and effective use of these LOFs for agricultural production.

Nutrient composition

The LOFs contained high organic matter (72.9–77.9%; ideal value 20–60%) and organic carbon (40.5–43.3%; ideal value 20–40%), reflecting elevated nutrient levels (Fig. 4A and B). Nitrogen content (2–5%) was within the optimal range for plant growth (2–5%), whereas phosphorus (0.246–0.342%; ideal value 0.5–3%) and potassium (0.315–0.342%; ideal value 2–5%) were slightly below recommended levels but adequate for root development and plant health (Fig. 4C and D). Among these LOF fertilizers, the mixed formulation (LOFM) exhibited marginally higher organic matter and carbon (Fig. 4E). Despite of some parameters did not fully meet ideal range; the LOFs still remained suitable for effective use as organic fertilizers.

Effects of LOFs on spinach growth

Seed germination

Germination rates ranged from 30 to 40%. Treatments T5, T10, T15, and the control achieved the highest rate (40%) and 100% relative seed germination (Fig. 5).

Shoot and root growth

The longest shoots (11.1 ± 5.6 cm) were produced by T10, followed by T5 and NPK; T2 yielded the shortest shoots (3.1 ± 1.1 cm). Root length was greatest under NPK, control, and T10 treatments. Statistical analysis (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test) showed no significant differences (P > 0.05) for most growth parameters (shoot length, leaf number, leaf length & width) across treatments. Significant differences (P < 0.05) in shoot length were observed only for T2 vs. NPK and T2 vs. T10 (Fig. 6A).

Leaf development

Treatments T5, T10, and T15 produced the highest total leaf count (48 each) as compared to NPK and control group (Fig. 6B). NPK was most effective for leaf length (7.4 ± 5.5 cm), with T14 showing significantly reduced leaf length versus the control (Fig. 6C). For leaf width, T5 and T10 reached 5.3 cm by day 60 (Fig. 6C), though no significant differences (P > 0.05) were found among treatments (Fig. 6B and D).

Plant biomass

Both NPK and control treatments showed high fresh and dry weights for both plants and leaves (Fig. 7). The overall results indicate that LOF treatments, especially T5, T10 and T15, produced comparable seed germination, shoot and root length, leaf number, and leaf dimensions relative to other treatments (Fig. 6A and D). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons revealed that most pairwise comparisons among treatments were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) for shoot length, leaf number, leaf length, and width. However, significant differences were observed in specific cases, such as T2 vs. NPK and T10 vs. T2, highlighting the variable impact of LOF concentrations.

Effect of fertilizers (LOF and NPK) treatments on trace metal contents of spinach plant leaves

The trace metal content (mg kg⁻¹) in spinach leaves varied under different NPK and LOF treatments (Fig. 10). Most treatments maintained mineral concentrations within optimal ranges for vegetable nutrition. Exceptions included excessive potassium (T14, T15), sulfur (T14), phosphorus (T11), and iron (T4, T14), while calcium was deficient in T11. Highly significant differences (P < 0.000001) were observed for both potassium and calcium relative to the other minerals (Fig. 10). No significant differences (P > 0.05) in mineral composition were found among LOFs, NPK and Control groups (Fig. 11). No significant differences (P > 0.05) were found in mineral composition were found among the three LOFs treatments groups (Fig. 12). These results highlight the importance of careful fertilizer management to maintain balanced nutrient levels and support healthy spinach growth.

Effect of fertilizers (LOF and NPK) treatments on the trace metal content of rhizosphere soil

The trace metal composition of soil collected from the rhizomes of 17 experimental spinach plants revealed distinct variances depending on the type of fertilizer applied, including NPK, Liquid Organic Fertilizers (LOFs), and untreated controls (Fig. 13). Soil potassium (2.0–3.3 mg kg⁻¹) and calcium (25.9–38.6 mg kg⁻¹) were well below optimal ranges for spinach growth. Sulfur and phosphorus were undetectable, whereas iron levels were within the optimal range. Manganese, zinc, and copper were low, while silicon (36–50.5 mg kg⁻¹) was within the beneficial range. Aluminum concentrations were highly variable, peaking in T14 (104 mg kg⁻¹). NPK application increased K, Ca, Fe, and Si, whereas LOF treatments produced variable effects on soil mineral content. No significant differences (P > 0.05) in mineral composition were found among LOFs, NPK and Control groups (Fig. 14). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test revealed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) among certain minerals, including Ca, Si, Al, Fe and K in soil under different LOF treatments (Fig. 15). Non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks in spinach under LOFs

The health risk assessment indicated no significant potential health risks for consumers. All Target Hazard Quotient (THQ) values were < 1, and Hazard Index (HI) values were well below 1 (one), indicating no appreciable non-carcinogenic risk (Table 3). These findings highlight the need for continued monitoring and evaluation of metals in fertilizers (LOFs and NPK) contributing to soil contamination (Fig. 18).

Soil microbial analysis

The microbial analysis of plant soil revealed significant variations among the control, NPK, and organic fertilizer treatments (LOFs), highlighting the influence of different fertilizers on soil microbial activity. LOFM soil supported the highest microbial population (292 CFU/ml), followed by LOFCC and the control. NPK soil consistently showed the lowest microbial counts (48 CFU/ml at 10⁻² dilution), indicating limited microbial stimulation by synthetic fertilizer. Organic fertilizers sustained higher microbial abundance and diversity across dilution levels (Fig. 20). Overall, these findings suggest that liquid organic fertilizers, particularly fish-based ones, provide a more favorable environment for microbial proliferation and may play a critical role in improving soil fertility and plant growth.

Discussion

Effects of LOFs treatments in promoting the spinach plant growth and its mineral contents

In the present study, among the three liquid organic fish fertilizer (LOF) treatments, T5, T10, and T15 demonstrated favorable plant growth in terms of plant height and leaf development, comparable to the NPK treatment. Notably, the control group (T0) also showed good growth performance, suggesting that the initial soil conditions were favorable for plant development. Similar results have been reported in previous studies. For example, Arifin and Pratiwi14 observed enhanced root length, fresh and dry weight, plant height, and leaf number in green lettuce treated with fish waste-based LOF, with 50 ml identified as the most effective concentration. Rosadi and Catharina41 found that strawberry plants treated with increasing doses of fish waste-based LOF (up to 3.5 ml) exhibited improved growth parameters, including leaf number, plant height, and earlier harvest. Significant improvements in plant height and leaf number of shallot were also observed with higher doses (67.5–90 ml) of LOF derived from Kampar River fish waste. Additionally, Aranganathan and Rajasree42 reported that foliar application of 5% and 10% LOF produced from marine trash fish significantly increased tomato plant height and leaf development compared to chemical fertilizer treatments. Fahlivi et al.3 found that a 16-day fermentation period of milkfish viscera waste-based fertilizer produced the most favorable results for purple eggplant, with improved nutrient content (pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and C-organic) and enhanced plant growth parameters such as plant height, root length, leaf size, and stem diameter. Similarly, Zhao et al.5 showed that substituting part of conventional fertilizer with fish amino acid fertilizer significantly boosted bamboo shoot yield, enhanced root structure, and improved soil quality by increasing microbial biomass, nutrient availability, and enzyme activity. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the substantial benefits of fish-based liquid organic fertilizers on both plant growth and soil health, highlighting their potential to enhance plant development through improved nutrient profiles and by promoting beneficial soil microbial and enzymatic activity.

Numerous studies have highlighted the rich nutrient composition of fish-based liquid organic fertilizers (LOFs), supporting their effectiveness in promoting plant growth. In our current study, potassium (K) was identified as the most abundant element in all three LOF types (LOFCC, LOFLR, and LOFM), followed by sulfur (S), calcium (Ca), chlorine (Cl), and phosphorus (P), while trace elements such as iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and titanium (Ti) were present at lower concentrations. This nutrient profile aligns with findings from previous studies, which reported similar elemental compositions in fish-based fertilizers and their positive influence on plant development4. For example, Khairul et al.4 reported high levels of essential macronutrients, such as phosphorus and calcium, in fish bone meals, which support key physiological functions like root development and cell wall formation in plants. Similarly, Muscolo et al.10 demonstrated that organic fertilizer derived from anchovy fish wastes is rich in proteins, potassium, and other key nutrients vital for plant health. Fahlivi et al.3 also confirmed that liquid fertilizers made from fish viscera contain essential elements such as potassium, phosphorus, and nitrogen, further validating the nutrient-rich nature of fish-based organic fertilizers and their potential to support sustainable agricultural practices.

Various elements play critical roles in the growth and development of plants. The optimal mineral concentration ranges essential for healthy plant growth include: Potassium (K), required in the range of 1.5–3.0 mg/g, which is vital for water regulation and enzymatic activity; Calcium (Ca), with an optimal range of 20–30 mg/g, essential for cell wall integrity, root and leaf development, and cell division; Sulfur (S), a key component of proteins, vitamins, and amino acids, which also enhances seed oil production; and Phosphorus (P), needed in smaller amounts (0.03–0.07 mg/g), crucial for energy transfer through ATP, supporting photosynthesis and root development23. Iron (Fe), required at a range of 4.5–10 mg/g, plays a central role in chlorophyll synthesis and the electron transport chain during photosynthesis, while manganese (Mn), with an optimal concentration of 0.1–0.2 mg/g, aids nitrogen metabolism and activates enzymes necessary for chlorophyll synthesis37. Titanium (Ti) is not an essential nutrient for plants but may contribute to enhanced stress tolerance and growth30. Both copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn) are required in trace amounts (0.01–0.05 mg/g) and function as essential cofactors in enzymatic reactions, with Cu also involved in lignin formation and Zn playing a key role in protein synthesis and growth hormone regulation25,37. Elements such as ruthenium (Ru), strontium (Sr), and bromine (Br) are not essential for plants but may influence growth and development under specific conditions32,43,44. Meanwhile, silicon (Si), required in relatively high amounts (20–50 mg/g), enhances structural integrity and increases plant resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses; chromium (Cr) can improve seed germination and nitrogen metabolism; and vanadium (V) can enhance chlorophyll synthesis and enzymatic activity24. Platinum (Pt) and aluminum (Al) are generally non-essential and may be toxic to plants, with Al interfering with nutrient uptake and inhibiting root growth31. Zirconium (Zr) and yttrium (Y) have negligible roles in plant growth, although they may accumulate in plant tissues; rubidium (Rb) can mimic potassium but has minimal biological function; and nickel (Ni) is essential in small amounts, playing a role in urease activity and nitrogen metabolism24. Collectively, all these minerals support various biochemical and physiological processes in plants, making them indispensable for optimal growth, health, and productivity.

Effects of minerals found in LOFs treated spinach plants on human health

The NPK treatment resulted in the highest DIM values for essential metals such as Ca and Si, indicating its effectiveness in supplying these nutrients. Fe and Cr were present at levels similar to those observed in LOFM. No significant accumulation of many other metals, particularly micronutrients, was detected in this study. The control group (T0) showed minimal nutrient uptake across all metals and served as a baseline, demonstrating that both organic and inorganic fertilization can significantly enhance metal and nutrient contents in spinach plants. Therefore, both organic (LOFM) and inorganic (NPK) fertilizers improve the uptake of essential nutrients compared to the control. These findings can guide agricultural practices aimed at enhancing the nutritional quality of spinach (Table 4).

In humans, calcium (Ca) is necessary for strong bones, nerve transmission, and muscle contraction; its deficiency leads to cramps and osteoporosis; Potassium (K) maintains fluid balance and heart function, with deficiency causing arrhythmia and weakness; Sulfur (S) is crucial for detoxification and protein metabolism; while Phosphorus (P) supports bone health and energy metabolism; its deficiency may result in bone pain and muscle weakness23. Iron (Fe) is fundamental for hemoglobin function; lack of iron causes anemia and fatigue, and Manganese (Mn) aids metabolism and bone formation, though deficiency is rare7. Titanium (Ti) may influence bone strength, but its effects are not well established39. Copper (Cu) supports red blood cell production and immune function; deficiency leads to anemia and connective tissue issues7,25. Zinc (Zn) is essential for immune health, growth, and wound healing; deficiency results in stunted growth and hair loss7. Ruthenium (Ru) may play catalytic roles in medicine but lacks biological necessity42. Strontium (Sr) aids bone health in small amounts but may weaken bones in excess43. Bromine (Br) may help in immune regulation but can be toxic at high levels43. Silicon (Si) strengthens bones and skin but may affect kidney health in excess, and Chromium (Cr) regulates glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity; excess intake is harmful to kidneys and liver44. Platinum (Pt) is used in chemotherapy drugs but is toxic at high doses31. Aluminum (Al) has no beneficial role and is linked to neurotoxicity, including Alzheimer’s disease; Zirconium (Zr) is biologically inert but may be toxic with chronic exposure; Vanadium (V) might help in diabetes treatment, though it is toxic in high doses; Rubidium (Rb) may interfere with potassium levels, impacting nerve function; Nickel (Ni) is required for enzyme function, but overexposure may harm organs; and Yttrium (Y) is not essential and can cause respiratory problems if inhaled in large quantities24.

Estimated cost for the production of fish waste based Liquid Organic Fertilizers (LOF)

Table 5 presents the estimated production cost for preparing 5.0 L of three types of liquid organic fertilizers (LOFCC, LOFLR, and LOFM) using fish waste and jaggery in Pakistan. For each fertilizer type, 2.0 kg of powdered jaggery was used at a rate of PKR 300 per kg, totaling PKR 600. Similarly, 2.0 kg of fish waste—sourced from Cyprinus carpio (for LOFCC), Labeo rohita (for LOFLR), or mixed fish species (for LOFM)—was utilized, also costing PKR 600. In addition to these material costs, an estimated labor expense of PKR 4,500 was included, calculated based on 7 h of work at PKR 500 per hour. Altogether, the total production cost for 5 L of any of these LOFs amounts to PKR 5,700.

The cost analysis in Table 6 further estimates the expense of spinach cultivation per acre using fish-based liquid organic fertilizers (LOFCC, LOFLR, or LOFM). Each type of LOF yields 5 L (5,000 ml) at a total production cost of PKR 5,700, equivalent to PKR 1.14 per ml. A standard 10% LOF solution is prepared by mixing 10 ml of LOF stock with 1,000 ml of distilled water. Over seven foliar spray applications, each spinach plant receives a total of 70 ml of the diluted solution, corresponding to 0.7 ml of LOF stock. At a unit cost of PKR 1.14 per ml, the cost per plant for seven sprays is approximately PKR 0.798. Given a planting density of three spinach plants per square foot—resulting in about 130,680 plants per acre—the total estimated cost of LOF application for spinach production is PKR 91,476 per acre. This calculation provides a detailed cost framework for evaluating the economic feasibility of adopting fish-based LOFs in commercial spinach cultivation (Tables 5 and 6).

Conclusions

Thus, in conclusion, our findings demonstrated that the experimental soil of this study was deficient in several essential macro- and micronutrients even after NPK fertilization, which may limit spinach growth and uptake of essential nutrients. Application of fish waste–based liquid organic fertilizers (LOFCC, LOFLR, and LOFM) showed potential to enhance spinach growth and essential mineral composition compared with the control, although responses varied among growth parameters and treatments. Higher application rates (40–50 ml LOF) generally promoted improved vegetative growth, while selected treatments (notably T5 and T10) contributed to enhanced mineral content, though differences were not always statistically significant relative to NPK fertilizer. Though NPK fertilizer performed well in promoting specific growth traits, such as leaf length but it did not consistently outperform fish waste–based fertilizers in terms of nutrient enrichment or sustainability. These findings highlight the potential of fish waste–derived fertilizers (LOFs) as sustainable nutrient sources, particularly in regions such as Balochistan, Pakistan, where soil degradation, groundwater depletion, and excessive use of synthetic fertilizers threaten agricultural productivity. Future studies should investigate long-term field applications, crop-specific growth response, trace metal dynamics, and food safety implications to support the safe and effective integration of fish waste–based organic fertilizers into sustainable agricultural practices.
