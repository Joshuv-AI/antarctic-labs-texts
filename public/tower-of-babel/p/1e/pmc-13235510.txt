Endoscopic resection for cervical and thoracic spinal osteoid osteoma: A case series
Authors: Mazda Farshad, Florian Wanivenhaus, Christoph J Laux, José M Spirig, Chantal Pauli, Lukas Zingg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoid osteomas (OOs) are benign but painful bone tumors characterized by a vascularized nidus of osteoblastic cells surrounded by sclerotic bone. They affect the spine in 6%–20% of cases, most commonly in the posterior elements. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) often provide initial relief, but surgery is required when symptoms persist. Complete nidus excision is critical to prevent recurrence. Minimally invasive techniques such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA) have gained popularity, but proximity to neural structures limits their use. Full endoscopic resection has emerged as a promising alternative, offering direct visualization, histologic confirmation, and minimal tissue disruption. Introduction

Osteoid osteoma (OO) is a benign osteoblastic bone tumor of unknown etiology that typically affects individuals under 30 years old, with a marked male preponderance (sex ratio 2–4:1) [1]. First characterized by Jaffe in 1935 [2], it consists of a small, highly vascularized osteoid producing nidus surrounded by sclerotic bone [1,3]. It is distinguished from osteoblastoma by its smaller nidus, typically less than 2 cm in diameter. OO most commonly arises in the cortex of long bones [[3], [4], [5]], but 6%–20% of cases involve the spine, predominantly the posterior elements [1,6]. Spinal OO typically presents with localized back pain that worsens at night, caused by prostaglandin-mediated stimulation of peripheral pain receptors [7,8]. Due to the small size of the lesion, neurological impairment in OO is rare [4,9]. Diagnosis is based on radiographs, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Initial treatment is conservative with salicylates or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), as spontaneous regression may occur [10,11]. Surgery is recommended when conservative treatment fails. The goal is complete nidus excision while preserving stability. Traditional open approaches (en bloc resection or curettage) are effective but may require extensive muscle dissection and bone removal, increasing the risk of instability and the potential need for spinal fusion [9,12,13]. Minimally invasive techniques such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA), laser ablation, and cryoablation are less invasive and cost-effective [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], but carry the risk of harming adjacent neural structures and lack histological confirmation. Additionally, there is a potential risk of recurrence due to incomplete nidus ablation [19].

Recently, a number of cases of endoscopic resection of OO have been reported [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. This technique allows minimal incisions, reduced tissue damage, continuous visualization for complete tumor removal, and histological confirmation [20,21,23,25].

In this study, we present 4 cases of minimally invasive full endoscopic resection of cervical and thoracic OO, highlighting the safety and effectiveness of this approach.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the latest revision of the Declaration of Helsinki [26] and was approved by the Cantonal ethics Committee of canton Zurich. All patients provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the procedure, associated risks, potential complications, and postoperative care.

This retrospective, consecutive, single-center case series included four patients who underwent full endoscopic resection of cervical or thoracic OO at a single academic spine center between December 2023 and April 2025. Patients were referred by general practitioners or orthopedic surgeons. Diagnosis was based on clinical presentation, radiological imaging, and histological confirmation via biopsy in one case. Clinically, patients presented with nocturnal pain responsive to aspirin or other NSAIDs. Preoperative imaging included anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs, CT, and MRI. MRI demonstrated bone marrow and osseous edema with inflammatory changes, while CT showed a well-demarcated lucent nidus surrounded by reactive sclerosis. Treatment options were discussed with each patient following multidisciplinary consultations involving spine surgeons and radiologists.

Patient characteristics

The cohort included 2 women and 2 men (sex ratio 1:1) with a mean age 30.5 years (range: 22–41 years). Clinical data for all patients is summarized in Table 1. Lesions were located in the T10 (2 cases), T1 (1 case) and C7 (1 case) vertebra (Fig. 1A–P). All patients presented with localized back or neck pain, which was nocturnal in 2 cases and responded to NSAIDs in 3 cases. One patient did not take NSAIDs. Symptoms duration prior to surgery ranged from 3 months to over 1 year (Table 1).

Surgical procedure

All surgical resections were performed by the senior author (M.F.). Procedures were carried out under general anesthesia with patients positioned prone on a radiolucent table. Fluoroscopy was used to localize the lesion (Fig. 2A, Video 1). A 1.2 mm skin incision was made, followed by insertion of a dilatation canula, working tube, and endoscope. The Vertebris endoscopic stenosis system (10 mm outside diameter, 5.6 mm working channel; RIWOspine gmbH, Knittlingen, Germany) was used in all cases. Continuous irrigation with epinephrine saline solution was maintained during the surgery.

After cleaning soft tissues from the bony structures, the lesion was visually identified and the working tube position over the nidus was confirmed via fluoroscopy.

A 5.5 mm diamond burr was used to grind down the bone overlying the lesion and expose the nidus cavity (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Video 1). The opening was further enlarged using a 2 mm Kerrison punch (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Video 1). The nidus was mobilized with a dissector (Fig. 2D) and extracted en bloc using an endoscopic rongeur (Fig. 2E, F), then sent for histopathological confirmation. The cavity walls were burred to ensure complete excision under direct endoscopic visualization. The wound was closed with a subcuticular suture after removal of the instruments.

Operative time and estimated blood loss were recorded. Postoperative NSAIDs were administered as needed for pain control.

Results

The median operative time was 31 minutes (range: 20–40 minutes), with estimated blood loss under 50 mL. Hospital stays ranged from 24 to 36 hours, and full mobilization was allowed from the first postoperative day with no restrictions. All patients experienced rapid and definitive pain relief within 24 hours after surgery. No intraoperative or postoperative complications occurred, and the postoperative course was uneventful in all cases. Treatment success was confirmed clinically and by postoperative CT or MRI. All patients had a minimum follow-up of six-months, with one patient followed up to 15 months. In all cases, imaging revealed progressive ossification at the resection site with no evidence of recurrence (Fig. 3).

Histology

Excised samples were sent for pathological evaluation. In all cases, the histomorphology, in correlation with radiological findings, was compatible with the diagnosis of OO (Fig. 4 A–D). In patient 4, a prominent spindle cell proliferation prompted additional immunohistochemical analysis. Staining was negative for H3 histone family member 3A (H3F3A), H3 histone family member 3B (H3F3B), and FBJ murine osteosarcoma viral oncogene homolog (c-FOS), with focal positivity for FosB proto-oncogene, AP-1 transcription factor subunit (FOSB). Correlation with the radiological features confirmed the diagnosis of OO.

Discussion

This case series highlights the successful use of full endoscopic resection for cervical and thoracic spinal OO. All patients experienced immediate postoperative pain relief, early mobilization, and no residual tumor on follow-up imaging.

OO ranks as the third most common benign bone tumor after osteochondroma and enchondroma. Although it typically affects long bones, spinal involvement occurs in 6%–20% of cases. Initial management involves NSAIDs or aspirin; however, as pain intensifies and becomes less responsive to medication, surgical intervention is indicated [5,27].

En bloc resection or curettage has long been standard treatment for OO, offering high success rate and minimal recurrence. However, despite the lesion’s small size, resection may require extensive surgical exposure and excision of surrounding sclerotic bone, which may cause postoperative instability and necessitate bone grafting or spinal fusion [9,28].

In recent years, several minimally invasive techniques have been introduced for spinal tumors, including cryoablation, percutaneous RFA, coagulation, microwave ablation, and MR-guided focused ultrasound, among others [4,[14], [15], [16], [17], [18],29,30]. These procedures have demonstrated high success rate with low complications rate. Among them, RFA has become widely used due to its minimal tissue damage, low morbidity, and high success rates—achieving symptoms remission in 78%–93% of cases [31,32]. However, RFA has important limitations. The risk of thermal damage to adjacent neurovascular structures restrict its use to lesions located at least 1 cm away from neural or vascular structures [16,33]. It also does not provide tissue for histological confirmation. Additionally, concerns persist regarding recurrence and potential long-term degenerative thermal changes in surrounding bone [19,32,34].

In our cases, RFA was deemed inappropriate due to the proximity of the lesions to critical neuronal structures. Therefore, we agreed on a full endoscopic resection as the treatment approach.

Full endoscopic spine surgery is a promising technique for OO. While technically demanding, it has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes with low complication rates [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24],[35], [36], [37], [38], [39]]. Advantages include minimal surgical morbidity, shorter hospital stays, quicker rehabilitation, and the possibility to obtain tissue for histological confirmation. It also preserves facet and pedicle integrity, reducing the risk of postoperative instability [21,23]. Technical challenges include navigating deeper tumors, and controlling potential bleeding from the nidus, which can impair visualization and prolong surgery. At our institution, we minimize intraoperative bleeding through blood pressure control, intravenous tranexamic acid before skin incision, and epinephrine-infused irrigation.

In our cohort, full endoscopic resection of cervical and thoracic OO was successful, with an excellent postoperative course. All patients reported immediate pain relief and were allowed full mobilization from the first postoperative day. To our knowledge, this is the first case series describing full endoscopic resection of cervical and thoracic spinal OO.

Postoperative imaging confirmed complete nidus resection in all cases. However, long-term studies are warranted to assess the technique’s efficacy in preventing recurrence.

This study is limited by its small sample size, short follow-up, single-center retrospective design, and lack of a comparison group, which may affect generalizability. Additionally, the technique requires specialized equipment and surgical expertise, potentially limiting its applicability. Despite these limitations, our findings provide early evidence supporting the safety and efficacy of full endoscopic resection for spinal OO and may help inform future clinical decision-making and surgical practice.

Conclusion

In this case series of cervical or thoracic OO, we performed full endoscopic resection due to the vicinity of lesions to neural structures. Our findings demonstrate that, when performed by experienced endoscopic spine surgeons, this procedure is safe, minimizes soft tissue and bone disruption, and allows complete nidus removal with immediate remission of symptoms. Full endoscopic resection should be considered a viable and effective treatment option for spinal OO.
