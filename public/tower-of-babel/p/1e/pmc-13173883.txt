Microbiota-gut-muscle axis shapes fish muscle texture by regulating collagen synthesis
Authors: Zhe Wang, Lu-Kuan Li, Nan-Nan Zhou, Tong Wang, Yue-Xin Wang, Fang Qiao, Zhen-Yu Du, Mei-Ling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncreasing studies have emphasized the communication network between the gut microbiome and host organs, revealing that such interactions significantly influence host physiological performances. However, whether a gut-muscle axis exists to regulate muscle quality in animal production is unknown.ResultsIn two independent cohorts, the muscle hardness of tilapia subjected to a long-term faba bean diet exhibited significant inter-individual variation. RNA-Seq analyses of the high-hardness (H) and low-hardness (L) groups pointed to collagen-based extracellular matrix as a possible factor driving muscle hardness development. Transplantation of gut microbiota from the H donor resulted in enhanced collagen synthesis in gnotobiotic zebrafish. Muscular collagen deposition was featured with an increased abundance of gut Cetobacterium. Gnotobiotic models colonized with live C. somerae exhibited enhanced collagen synthesis. Integrated analyses of microbiome function, bacterial genome, and metabolic profiles identified acetate as a key effector of C. somerae. Acetate incubation upregulated collagen I expression in TGF-β-activated fibroblasts in an acetylation-dependent manner. Mechanistically, acetate promoted the acetylation of SMAD2/3, enhancing its nuclear transport and stability, which ultimately increased collagen expression. An acetate-supplemented feeding experiment corroborated these findings.ConclusionThe comprehensive results provided evidences that gut microbes regulated tilapia muscle texture through SMAD2/3 acetylation-driven collagen synthesis. This study expands our understanding of the multifaceted role of the gut-muscle axis in muscle physiology. Furthermore, our findings highlight that targeting gut microbiota and the downstream collagen synthesis pathway could be promising for manipulating muscle quality in animal production. Introduction

The interaction between the gut microbiota and host organs is one of the focuses in contemporary microbiology research [1]. Over the past decade, numerous studies have unequivocally established the existence of the gut–muscle axis [2, 3]. The emerging role of gut microbiome in regulating muscle physiology has garnered considerable attention from researchers across various scientific disciplines. For instance, gut bacterium Bifidobacterium adolescentis-derived nicotinic acid enhanced skeletal muscle mitochondrial function in sarcopenic mice [4]. The increased temperature fluctuation exacerbated muscular atrophy through interactions with gut microbiota and circulating aminoadipic acid [5]. Beyond these laboratory models, accumulating evidence in farm animals has also shed light on the link between gut microbiota and muscle quality, with a comprehensive review summarizing major findings in this area [6]. While existing evidence remains preliminary, it provides valuable clues about how the gut microbiota and its metabolites may influence muscle quality by shaping muscular components such as muscle fibers and intramuscular fat. Nonetheless, the causal relationships and the mechanisms underlying this cross-talk remain poorly defined, especially in non-mammalian systems.

Fish serve as a crucial source of dietary protein and essential nutrients worldwide, and with rising living standards, consumer preference for high-quality fish products has become increasingly prominent [7]. Among quality attributes, texture is a key sensory determinant that directly affects mastication and overall palatability, thereby influencing consumer acceptance and market value of fish products [8]. Despite its importance, the biological basis of fish muscle texture remains poorly understood, and strategies for reliably modulating this trait are still lacking. More importantly, the potential of gut microbes and their associated metabolites as viable targets for regulating fish muscle texture remains underexplored.

Aquaculturists and scientists are actively engaged in efforts to enhance the muscle texture quality of farmed fish, accumulating valuable practical experiences. A notable example is China’s development of “crisp” fish, a practice initiated in the 1970 s [9–11]. By incorporating a diet enriched with faba bean (FB) for aquaculture species such as grass carp and tilapia, the typically soft and mushy muscle is transformed into a firmer and crisper texture, thereby improving the overall masticatory experience. This discovery quickly gained popularity and has since been industrialized. However, the efficiency of “crisp” fish farming remains unstable, with significant inter-individual variations in muscle crispness. This issue is considered as major challenge for “crisp” fish industry, while the underlying causes remain unclear.

To overcome these limitations, we hypothesized that gut microbiota might contribute to host texture phenotype. To test this hypothesis, we simulated “crisp” tilapia farming in two independent cohorts, under outdoor pond condition and controlled laboratory setting. Specifically, we (I) performed texture profile analysis, gut microbiome, and muscle transcriptome on tilapia; (II) generated and colonized gnotobiotic zebrafish (Danio rerio) model; and (III) investigated the mechanistic role in vivo and in vitro. Our aim was to verify whether the gut microbiota influences the muscle texture properties of fish and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Methods

Animals and sampling

A preliminary feeding experiment was conducted to simulate actual “crisp” tilapia farming in an outdoor pond (4 × 4 × 1 m). A cohort of 74 adult genetically improved farmed tilapia (GIFT, Oreochromis niloticus) with uniform body weights was enrolled. A formulated diet based on FB, designed to mimic specialized commercial feeds for “crisp” tilapia farming, was developed (Table S1). The experimental diets were prepared as follows: Feed ingredients were ground, sieved through a 300-µm mesh, and homogenized thoroughly. Lipid sources and water were then added separately and mixed evenly to form a dough. The dough was transferred to a twin-screw extruder, extruded through a perforated iron plate (1.5 or 3 mm diameter), and cut into pellets with a rotating blade. Pellets were dried at 50 °C for 8 h to achieve a moisture content below 10%, and stored at − 20 °C until use. The fish were fed to satiation by administering excess feed twice daily. On day 70, the fish were anesthetized using 0.02% tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222). Subsequently, skeletal dorsal muscle samples were collected from all fish for texture profile analysis. The values of muscle hardness, ranging from 398 to 1582 g, followed a normal distribution (Fig. S1A). To compare the robustness of this result, the feeding experiment was replicated with another cohort of tilapia possessing a homogeneous genetic background under controlled laboratory conditions. This population was derived through artificial insemination of a full-sibling family at a tilapia genetic breeding facility in Guangdong, China. Upon completion of the larval stage, only male specimens were retained to avoid the potential influence of sexual dimorphism on the experimental results. Subsequently, fifty-four healthy fish (average body weight: 6.1 ± 0.05 g) were randomly selected and placed in three aquarium tanks connected to a recirculating water system. The water temperature was maintained between 29 and 31 °C, with a dissolved oxygen concentration of 6.0 mg/L and pH levels ranging from 7.1 to 7.3. Throughout the 70-day feeding trial, the fish were hand-fed the FB diet (Table S1) twice daily until satiation. Upon completion of the feeding trial, the fish were anesthetized with 0.02% MS-222, and the skeletal dorsal muscle was dissected for texture profile analysis. The muscle samples were categorized based on hardness. The individuals ranking in the top six and bottom six were assigned to the high-hardness (H) group and the low-hardness (L) group, respectively. The remaining individuals were categorized as the medium-hardness (M) group. Additionally, the intestinal contents of each fish were collected for DNA extraction. Tissues from the intestine and muscle as well as blood samples were collected for RNA extraction and biochemical analysis.

Muscle texture profile

Muscle specimens were sectioned into 1 cm-thick segments and analyzed for texture profile using a CT3–1500 surface analyzer (CNS Farnell, UK), in accordance with our established protocols [12]. The specimens underwent dual compression at a crosshead velocity of 0.50 mm/s utilizing a cylindrical probe with a 6 mm diameter. The target compression depth was established at 0.5 cm.

Collagen content

Muscular collagen content was analyzed spectrophotometrically with standard test kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China), adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol. This method is based on the principle that hydroxyproline oxidation products react with dimethylaminobenzaldehyde to produce a purple-red chromophore. The concentration of collagen is determined by measuring the intensity of the color through a colorimetric assay.

Acetyl-CoA quantification

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed to determine muscle acetyl-CoA concentration, in accordance with our established methodology [13]. Briefly, the mobile phase comprised 70 mmol/L sodium acetate and 100 mmol/L monosodium phosphate, with the pH adjusted to 4.6 using phosphoric acid. Acetonitrile was incorporated into the prepared mobile phase at a ratio of 6:94. Prior to HPLC analysis, the freshly prepared mobile phase was subjected to filtration and degassing. Ultraviolet detection was conducted at a wavelength of 259 nm.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from muscle and cell samples utilizing FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co.,Ltd, China). cDNA synthesis and qPCR were conducted using commercial kits (Cat. No. AG11705 and AG11701; Accurate biotechnology (Hunan) Co., Ltd., Changsha, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each 10 µL qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 5 µL of 2 × SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers, and 4 µL of template cDNA. The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final melting curve stage of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 1 s. The sequences of the primers were provided in Table S2, and all primer pairs were validated for amplification efficiency. The relative expression levels of mRNA for each gene were determined using the 2^ΔΔCT method as described by Schmittgen and Livak [14].

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Muscle RNA extraction, library construction and sequencing were conducted at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The library underwent quantification and sequencing using the NovaSeq X Plus platform. Subsequently, the clean data were aligned to the tilapia reference genome (GCF_001858045.2) and assembled. The raw paired-end reads were subjected to trimming and quality control via the fastp tool. Gene expression levels for each sample were quantified using the transcripts per million reads method. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with screening thresholds set at a P-value < 0.05 and |fold change|≥ 1.5. Distinct patterns of gene expression between groups were discerned and visualized with a principal component analysis (PCA) plot. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using Python software, with default parameters applied for any unspecified settings.

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

The DNA was extracted from gut contents utilizing the Tiangen DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). The DNA quality and concentration were assessed via 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The hypervariable V3-V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified. The purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar concentrations and sequencing on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Quality filtering of the raw sequence files was performed using fastp. Subsequently, the optimized sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence similarity threshold. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Euclidean distance were performed at OTU level. Predicted pathways were inferred using an exact amplicon sequence variant approach with the PICRUSt2 software and annotated via the MetaCyc database [15]. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was performed to identify biomarkers among the different groups (LDA score > 3, P < 0.05) [16]. The co-occurrence networks were developed to investigate the internal community relationships among the samples. A correlation between two nodes was deemed statistically robust (|Kendall coefficient|> 0.5, P < 0.05).

Isolation of Cetobacterium somerae and genome sequencing

The gut contents of tilapia were collected and diluted in PBS before being inoculated onto commercial Fusobacteriota-selective medium (FM agar, modified; Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) in anaerobic environment at 28℃ for 20 h. A single clone was isolated from the predominant colonies based on morphological characteristics. This single colony served as the template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and sequencing. The candidate strain (C. somerae ZNN-1) was used for whole-genome sequencing following standard protocol at Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Gnotobiotic zebrafish

Germ-free zebrafish were generated based on the methodology described by Rawls et al. [17]. Briefly, fertilized eggs were subjected to a triple rinse with sterile water and antibiotic water before being cultured for five hours in gnotobiotic zebrafish medium (GZM). Subsequently, the zebrafish eggs were immersed in a 0.4 g/L polyvinylpyrrolidone solution for one minute and subsequently washed three additional times with GZM. The eggs were then treated with 0.05 g/L sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by another three washes with GZM. The processed eggs were placed in sterile dishes. The medium was semi-quantitatively replaced on a daily basis, and 100 µL samples from each dish were inoculated onto agar plates to evaluate sterility.

Bacterial transplantation

The gut contents from every two fish within the same group were pooled for intestinal microbiota transplantation (IMT) in accordance with our established protocols [18]. In short, 1 g of gut content was dissolved in PBS and thoroughly mixed. The suspension was filtered sequentially through two layers of gauze, followed by three layers. Subsequently, a counting test was conducted using a hemocytometer to verify that the concentration exceeded 10^6 CFU per mL. At 3 days post-fertilization (dpf), the larvae were aseptically transferred to sterile 6-well plates, with 20 larvae per well. Gnotobiotic zebrafish colonized with gut bacteria from the L and H groups were designated as IMT-L and IMT-H, respectively. The larvae were sampled seven days following bacterial transplantation. C. somerae was cultured and expanded in Gifu Anaerobic Medium (GAM) broth until reaching the mid-logarithmic growth phase. Subsequently, a total of 1 mL (1 × 10^8 CFU) of bacterial cells were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in PBS. A comparable concentration of bacterial pellets was pasteurized at 85 °C for 15 min. To generate mono-associated gnotobiotic zebrafish, 100 µL of either live or pasteurized bacterial pellets (1 × 10^8 CFU/mL) were resuspended in PBS and inoculated into GZM at 3 dpf. PBS administration was served as a vehicle control. The larvae were sampled seven days following bacterial transplantation.

Short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) determination

The samples of serum were extracted using 100 µL of 15% phosphoric acid, supplemented with 20 µL of a 75 µg/mL 4-methylvaleric acid solution and 280 µL of ether. Following extraction, the samples underwent centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min at a speed of 13,000 rpm. The resulting supernatant was then transferred into vials for subsequent analysis using a Race 1300 gas chromatograph coupled with an ISQ 7000 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), in accordance with our established protocols [19].

Cell culture and treatment

ZF4 (ATCC® CRL-2050™), a fibroblast-like cell line derived from 1-day-old zebrafish embryos, was maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM)/F-12 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 28 °C. The NOR10 skeletal muscle fibroblast cell line (ATCC® CCL-197™) was established from the abdominal wall muscle of a mouse. The cells were maintained in high glucose DMEM containing 10% FBS under controlled conditions. To stimulate the cells, the medium was first removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS before being resuspended in medium. Subsequently, TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL), sodium acetate (0.5–1 mM), 4-cmtb (1 µM), AR420626 (10 µM), GLPG0974 (1 µM), ACSS2i (10 µM), and acetyl-CoA (4 µM) were selectively added as interventions. Cytoplasmic or nuclear extracts were extracted using standard kit (Cat. No. 20126ES, Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the analysis of protein stability, cells were pretreated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 and 1 mM sodium acetate, followed by exposure to 40 µM cycloheximide (CHX) for durations of 0, 4, and 8 h, respectively. For the ubiquitination assays, cells were treated with 20 µM MG132 for 6 h prior to harvesting.

Western blotting

Cell and tissue protein samples were extracted utilizing a total protein extraction kit (Beijing Boxbio Science & Technology Co.,Ltd, China). Approximately 40 µg of protein were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were then blocked and underwent sequential immunoblotting with primary and secondary antibodies. The antibody information was detailed in Table S3. Protein bands were visualized and quantified using the Odyssey CLx Imager (Licor, USA).

Co-immunoprecipitation

Following treatment, the cells were lysed in a lysis buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Suzhou, China) for 30 min. Subsequently, the cell lysates were collected and incubated with anti-SMAD2/3 on a rocking platform at 4 °C overnight. The antibody-protein mixture was incubated with protein A/G beads (Cat. No. L1204, biolinkedin, Shanghai, China) for 4 h. The beads underwent three washes with PBS and were resuspended in loading buffer. Following denaturation at 100 °C for 6 min, the bound proteins were subjected to western blotting.

Histology

Muscle samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at a thickness of 4 µm for Sirius red and H&E staining (Cat. No. G472. Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China).

For the immunohistochemical (IHC) staining procedure, muscle tissue sections were initially deparaffinized, followed by antigen retrieval. Subsequently, the sections were treated with a peroxidase block and incubated with an anti-COL1A2 for 12 h. The tissue sections were then washed and incubated with a reaction-enhancing solution for 20 min. Following four washes with PBS, the sections were treated with a goat anti-rabbit IgG polymerase for 20 min. The staining was developed using a 3,3′ diaminobenzidine solution, counterstained with hematoxylin, and finally mounted with a mounting medium.

For the immunofluorescence (IF) staining procedure, muscle tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, followed by antigen retrieval and peroxidase blocking. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight with mouse anti-COL1A2 antibodies. Post-incubation, the sections were washed and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. To prevent fluorescence quenching, the sections were treated with an anti-fade reagent containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. In addition, ZF4 cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with either anti- acetylated-lysine or anti-SMAD2/3 antibodies and processed for IF staining as described above.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v8.0 software and are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of the data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, while the homogeneity of variance was verified through Levene’s test. Unpaired t test was employed to assess the differences between treatments. Microbiome data were compared using Wilcoxon test. Statistical significance was indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and n.s. denoted not significant.

Results

The heterogeneity of muscle texture was linked to altered collagen deposition

In alignment with observations from outdoor pond cultures (Fig. S1A), after a 70-day feeding period on a FB diet under controlled laboratory conditions, the muscle hardness of tilapia exhibited significant inter-individual variation, ranging from 580 to 1412 g, and followed a normal distribution (Fig. 1B). To disclose the intrinsic reasons behind these observations, the tilapia reared under laboratory conditions was categorized according to muscle hardness. Specimens with muscle hardness values below 800 g and above 1200 g were classified as the L group (n = 6) and H group (n = 6), respectively (Fig. 1A). No difference in mean body weight was observed between groups (Fig. S2A). The H group exhibited significantly higher muscle hardness and other textural attributes, including springiness, gumminess, and chewiness, compared to the L group (Fig. 1C). Nonetheless, no significant variations in muscle hyperplasia and cellularity were detected under light microscopy (Fig. S2B, C). To explore the mechanism underpinning texture heterogeneity, RNA-seq was performed. The PCA model identified distinct clusters between the L and H groups (Fig. 1D). A total of 8505 co-occurring genes were found between the groups, of which 1373 genes were differentially expressed (Fig. 1E). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the top 20 pathways enriched by the differentially expressed genes (Fig. 1F). Notably, compared to the L group, the H group showed significant upregulation of genes within the extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction pathway, which included numerous collagen genes (Fig. 1G). This observation was further substantiated by gene expression analysis (Fig. 1H). Additionally, the H group showed a significantly higher content of muscular collagen in comparison to the L group, as confirmed by Sirius red staining and COL1A2 IF staining (Fig. 1I, J). We conducted a detailed analysis of the muscle hardness and collagen content in all specimens. A significant positive correlation was observed between muscle hardness and collagen content (Fig. 1K). As the most abundant component of the ECM, collagen provides tensile strength and structural support, conferring mechanical properties to muscle tissues [20]. Consequently, altered muscle texture profiles might be correlated with the development of muscular collagen. Distinct gut microbiota profiles associated with muscle collagen development

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing was employed upon all fish to analyze the gut microbiota among groups. A PCoA plot distinguished cluster H from cluster L and most dots of cluster M, indicating differences in microbial community composition across groups (Fig. 2A). The dominant phyla in the L group were Bacillota (41.55%), followed by Actinobacteria (19.08%) and Fusobacteria (11.34%) (Fig. 2B). However, in the H group, the order of top three phyla were Fusobacteria (68.98%), Bacillota (13.43%), and Actinobacteria (6.04%). Furthermore, the community heatmap and hierarchical clustering confirmed a distinct separation between the L and H groups (Fig. 2C, D). To investigate the causal relationship between gut microbiota and host phenotype, we generated and colonized gnotobiotic zebrafish (Fig. 2E). The IMT-H receivers exhibited significantly higher expression of collagen I and muscular collagen contents compared with the IMT-L receivers (Fig. 2F, G). C. somerae-derived acetate involved in host collagen development

The 16S rRNA gene-based MetaCyc pathway profiling was inferred by PICRUSt2. The heatmap revealed the top 20 differential pathways between the L and H groups, organized by average fold change and P-values (Fig. 3A). Notably, the “L-lysine fermentation to acetate and butanoate” pathway exhibited the highest fold change among them. Furthermore, KEGG Ortholog (KO) annotation indicated that most of the enzymes involved in the “L-lysine fermentation to acetate and butanoate” pathway exhibited significantly higher relative abundance in the H group compared to the L group (Fig. 3B). These results suggested a potential enrichment of acetate-producing bacteria within the high-hardness population. LEfSe analysis was conducted to identify potential biomarkers. Cetobacterium, which was enriched in the H group, exhibited the highest LDA score (> 5) among the various microbial taxa (Fig. 3C). In addition, we constructed a correlation network to examine microbe-phenotype interactions. Cetobacterium was identified as the sole genus exhibiting a significant positive correlation with both hardness and collagen (Fig. 3D). This finding was further corroborated by linear regression analyses (Fig. 3E, F). To solidify the laboratory findings, we examined an adult tilapia population (n = 74) that was subjected to a FB diet under outdoor pond conditions, as we previously described. Consistent with the laboratory results, the high-hardness sub-population exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance of Cetobacterium, along with increased muscle collagen content, compared to the low-hardness sub-population (Fig. S1B–D). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis indicated a significant positive correlation between Cetobacterium abundance and muscular collagen content (Fig. S1E). These findings indicated that gut Cetobacterium might play a role in muscle collagen development in fish across diverse environmental conditions. To further consolidate this idea, we isolated an indigenous strain of C. somerae from the gut of tilapia and developed a mono-colonized gnotobiotic model in zebrafish (Fig. 4A). Colonization with live C. somerae led to increased collagen deposition, as evidenced by elevated COL1A1/2 expression and collagen contents, whereas pasteurized C. somerae did not yield similar effects (Fig. 4B, C). These observations from microbiota and single-strain analyses prompted an investigation into bacterial metabolites. We observed a high concentration of acetate in the C. somerae supernatant (Fig. 4D). Through whole-genome sequencing, the genes pta and ackA, which are responsible for the final two steps of acetate synthesis in both the “L-lysine fermentation to acetate and butanoate” and “Wood-Ljungdahl” pathways [21, 22], are annotated in the C. somerae genome (Fig. 4E). We further analyzed the SCFAs profile in gut contents and serum samples. The concentrations of acetate in both fecal and serum were significantly higher in the H group compared with the L group (Fig. 4F, G). More importantly, there was a significant positive correlation between serum acetate concentration and muscle collagen content (Fig. 4H). These findings suggested that gut commensal C. somerae contributed to acetate accumulation in the H group, potentially influencing muscle collagen development. Acetate enhanced collagen expression in activated fibroblast in an acetylation-dependent manner

Fibroblasts are the primary producers of ECM proteins across all organs [23]. In this study, ZF4 (a zebrafish embryonic fibroblast) and NOR10 (a mouse skeletal muscle fibroblast) cells were chosen to explore the role of acetate in vitro. Under standard conditions, acetate incubation for 24 h had minimal impact on the expression of collagen type I in both ZF4 and NOR10 cells (Figs. 5A and S3A). Interesting, in the presence of TGF-β stimulation, acetate dose-dependently increased the expression of COL1A1 and COL1A2. Given that TGF-β is key mediator of fibroblast activation and ECM formation under various physiological conditions [24], the present results suggested that acetate magnified the effect of TGF-β on ZF4 and NOR10 cells. To elucidate this action mechanism, agonist and antagonist assays were conducted targeting two acetate-bound free fatty acid receptors (FFAR2/3). We found that GLPG0974 (a FFAR2 antagonist) incorporation did not impede the upregulation of Col1a1 by acetate (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, neither 4-CMTB (an agonist o FFAR2) nor AR420626 (an agonist of FFAR3), was able to replicate the effects of acetate. These results indicated that the role of acetate in regulating collagen expression is independent of FFARs signaling. Acetate can be converted to acetyl-CoA via the action of acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2), thereby contributing to the global acetylation progression [25]. In this study, ACSS2 inhibition was found to block the acetate-induced upregulation of COL1A1 and COL1A2, while this effect was completely reversed upon the subsequent addition of external acetyl-CoA (Fig. 5C, D). Further, acetate significantly increased global protein acetylation in ZF4 cells (Fig. 4E, F). These findings revealed an acetylation-dependent role for acetate in regulating collagen expression in fibroblasts. Acetate enhanced SMAD2/3 acetylation, nuclear transport, and protein stability

The transcriptional regulation of collagen genes is influenced by complex pathway components, particularly the TGF-β/SMADs signaling pathway [26]. This seemingly straightforward pathway is subject to intricate regulation, much of which occurs at the level of post-translational modifications [27]. Initially, we assessed the acetylation status of SMAD2/3 in tilapia muscle. SMAD2/3 was immunoprecipitated from muscle lysates, and acetylated SMAD2/3 (Ac-SMAD2/3) was analyzed via western blotting. Results showed that SMAD2/3 acetylation level was significantly enhanced in the H group compared to the L group (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, acetate incubation significantly increased SMAD2/3 acetylation in both ZF4 and NOR10 cells in the presence of TGF-β (Figs. 6B and S3B). The nuclear transport of SMAD complexes is essential for their functionality, a process affected by their modification status and dependent on specific cell types [27]. The impact of SMAD2/3 acetylation on its localization and activity in fibroblasts has not been documented. Cytosolic and nuclear proteins of ZF4 and NOR10 cells were extracted for western blotting. Results showed that acetate facilitated the nuclear transport of SMAD2/3 in the presence of TGF-β (Figs. 6C, D and S3C), a finding corroborated by IF analysis (Fig. 6E). We further investigated the influence of SMAD2/3 acetylation on protein stability. SMAD2/3 protein levels significantly decreased in ZF4 cells treated with CHX (a protein synthesis inhibitor), while this reduction was mitigated by MG132 (a proteasome inhibitor), suggesting that SMAD2/3 underwent rapid degradation through the proteasomal pathway (Fig. 6F). Similarly, acetate treatment inhibited the degradation of SMAD2/3 in response to CHX (Fig. 6G). To further explore the underlying mechanism, SMAD2/3 was immunoprecipitated from ZF4 cells, and its acetylation and ubiquitination were simultaneously assessed. Western blot analysis revealed that acetate increased SMAD2/3 acetylation while concurrently reducing its ubiquitination, leading to enhanced protein accumulation (Fig. 6H). Discussion

Skeletal muscle exhibits high plasticity. Numerous studies have emphasized the crucial role of the gut microbiome and its associated molecules in influencing various muscular components, including muscle fibers, connective tissue, and intramuscular fat [28]. This suggests the potential for enhancing muscle quality in animal production through gut microbiota intervention. Our study, for the first time, showed that collagen-based ECM deposition underlay muscle texture heterogeneity observed in “crisp” tilapia farming. Furthermore, we identified altered muscular collagen deposition with an increased abundance of gut Cetobacterium in two independent cohorts. Multi-levels and multi-omics analyses revealed that gut commensal C. somerae produced acetate to promote host collagen synthesis. Furthermore, we presented the novel evidence that acetate enhanced SMAD2/3 acetylation in TGF-β-activated fibroblasts, a key regulatory mechanism that promoted nuclear transport and stability through decreased ubiquitin–proteasome degradation (Fig. 8). These results revealed a previously unrecognized role of gut microbes in regulating muscle texture trait and offer promising prospects for improving muscle quality in animal production through gut microbiota manipulation.Fig. 8Collagen-based extracellular matrix deposition underlies the muscle texture heterogeneity observed in “crisp” tilapia farming. Individual responses to specific external stressors frequently differ among individuals, and such heterogeneous responses can significantly affect animal production phenotypes, including weight gain of fish [29, 30], feed utilization efficiency of chickens [31], and milk protein yield of dairy cows [32]. The muscle hardness is generally considered stable, but it varies considerably among individuals in “Crisp” fish farming for poorly understood reasons. The present study provided evidence that collagen-based ECM deposition underlies the heterogeneity of muscle hardness in “Crisp” tilapia. Connective tissue and its ECM component confer mechanical properties and structural integrity to tissue [25, 26]. Indeed, the contribution of collagen to meat texture has long been recognized in food science, with a particular focus on post-slaughter beef and pork [27, 28]. Several studies have documented that increased muscle hardness was linked to enhanced muscular collagen content in harvested fish [29, 30], but these evidences are quite preliminary. The present study offers novel insights into the relationship between muscular collagen components and texture traits, which potentially aids in developing strategies to improve muscle quality of farmed animals.

Variation in the gut microbiome is a critical factor influencing host physiological phenotype. A prior study investigated gut microbial and host genetic data in chickens, identifying a class of microorganisms capable of interacting with host genotypes and associated with feed efficiency [31]. Additionally, Xue et al. [32] employed a linear mixed-effects model to estimate the contributions of rumen microbiome to the milk protein yield of dairy cows. Furthermore, the fecal microbiota of dairy cows, due to its glutathione synthesis capability, contributing to host postpartum oxidative stress [33]. These observations suggested that variations in the gut microbiota might exacerbate inter-individual phenotypic differences. In this study, we observed a distinct gut microbial communities between the L and H groups. Cetobacterium emerged as the predominant genus in the H group. Cetobacterium has been identified as a core genus in the gut of freshwater fish, while its abundance is quietly susceptible to internal and external factors [34–36]. The physiological function of C. somerae has been linked to antioxidant [37], anti-pathogen infections [38], and metabolic regulation [39, 40] in various fish species, with the primary target organs being gut and liver. In this study, we employed gnotobiotic zebrafish models to uncover a novel cross-organ function of the core gut microbe in regulating the development of muscle collagen and texture attributes in fish.

The interaction between the gut and distal organs is largely dependent on microbial metabolites. In this study, we integrated microbiome function prediction, C. somerae whole-genome sequencing, and SCFA profiles analysis to identify acetate as a key effector molecule of gut microbiota. Furthermore, external acetate promoted collagen expression in TGF-β-activated fibroblasts, a process dependent on the production of acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA served as the primary donor for acetylation reactions, underlying the subsequent acetylation of SMAD2/3. SMAD signaling is intricately regulated to mediate diverse TGF-β-driven biological responses through post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and acetylation [27]. The acetylation of SMAD2/3 displays variable functions across different organs and cell types. p300 acetyltransferase-mediated SMAD2/3 acetylation enhanced the activation of hepatic stellate cells in response to TGF‐β stimulation [41]. In contrast, HDAC6 inhibition increased acetylation of SMAD3 while suppressed TGF-β signaling in C2C12 myotubes [42]. In this study, utilizing fish and mammal fibroblasts, we found that the acetylation of SMAD2/3 facilitated its nuclear translocation and enhanced protein stability by diminishing ubiquitin degradation. These findings were further validated in vivo through an acetate-supplemented feeding experiment. Previous studies reported that the fish fed the FB-enriched diets exhibited sustained activation of TGF-β signaling [43, 44]. FB might therefore serve as an exogenous activator of TGF-β signaling in vivo. Accordingly, we hypothesized that FB feeding provided a permissive signaling context for acetate function, while acetate amplified TGF-β downstream signaling by enhancing SMAD2/3 acetylation, promoting collagen gene transcription and texture development in tilapia.

Overall, our study provided evidences that gut commensal C. somerae regulated muscle texture attributes through SMAD2/3 acetylation-mediated collagen synthesis. Our findings expand our mechanistic understanding of muscle texture development. In light of our findings, targeting specific gut microbiota and collagen pathways holds promise to manipulate muscle quality in fish and other farmed animals.
