Dynamic sensor selection for biomarker discovery
Authors: Joshua Pickard, Cooper Stansbury, Amit Surana, Lindsey Muir, Anthony Bloch, Indika Rajapakse
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThere is an outstanding need to discover and improve biomarkers that monitor biological systems in research and clinical settings. We leverage observability theory to identify the optimal time-dependent biomarkers from experimental data. We demonstrate the technique on biomedical and agricultural genomics, electroencephalogram, and endomicroscopy data. Our approach accelerates biomarker discovery and improves the monitoring of biological systems by aligning the mathematical theory for sensor selection with practical applications.

Monitoring the state of a cell or tissue is experimentally and computationally challenging. Recently developed on-demand sequencing technologies, including live single-cell sequencing and adaptive sampling, are increasing the accessibility of high-dimensional, high-frequency time series genomics data (1–3). These technologies are shifting the bottleneck in monitoring biological systems from the acquisition to the synthesis of data—posing a challenge for the selection of biomarkers that represent a specific biological state in clinical and research settings (4–6). Observability theory—an engineering framework for sensor selection—provides a framework to uncover biomarkers and offers an approach to analyze and interpret vast biological datasets.

Systems theory models the genome as a dynamical system, where temporal changes of gene expression and chromatin structure are described by the differential equation:[1]dx(t)dt=f(x(t),θ,t).

The cell’s state is described by a vector x(t)∈Rn and the function f(·) models the dynamics with parameters θ. Eq. 1 models a cell’s state as autonomous, with no control inputs or external perturbations considered. Observability involves an additional measurement function g(·) that maps the system state x(t) to data y(t)∈Rp with the equation:[2]y(t)=g(x(t),t).

Here, p is the number of measurements collected at each time point, which is often significantly smaller than the dimension n of the system state. Informally, the system governed by the pair of functions (f,g)—describing the dynamics and measurement equations—is said to be observable when data y collected up until time t enables one to reconstruct or estimate the initial state of the system x(0). There are a variety of formal observability criteria (7–9); for mathematically precise details and definitions, see ref. 10. From the standpoint of observability, biomarker discovery aims to design the measurement function g(·) to collect data y(0), y(1), and so on, in order to accurately determine the state x(t) of a biological system. For example, accuracy can be characterized using mean squared error.

Biomarker discovery traditionally operates within siloed experimental modalities—such as wearable sensors or mass spectrometry—yet lacks a unified methodological framework (SI Appendix, section 1.4) (11, 12). Unlike engineered systems with well-defined dynamics and known components, biological systems contain millions of proteins and other elements, operating at a scale far beyond the typical applications of systems and control theory including jet engines or communications networks (13). Longitudinal studies, however, are becoming increasingly utilized across data modalities, which may improve the modeling of biological dynamics (14–16).

Despite thorough theoretical characterization, practical limitations of biological data—such as noise, high dimensionality, and sparsity—have slowed the adoption of observability-guided sensor selection to facilitate biomarker discovery (17–20). Metabolic and gene regulatory networks have been analyzed using structural observability, an approach that prioritizes scalability (n) over precision (θ) (21–23). Using time series transcriptomics, Hasnain et al. employed data-driven modeling and observability optimization to learn dynamics (f) and design biomarkers (g) for pesticide detection (24). Still, many practicalities of biological systems and data remain unaddressed by observability theory.

Biomarker discovery has previously relied on: 1) a wealth of domain knowledge that exists independently of mathematical models (f and g), and 2) an increasing array of high-dimensional, multimodal data, and experimental techniques. Unlike observability, these two approaches struggle to scale and generalize across biological systems and data modalities. To bridge the gap between observability theory and biomarker discovery, we integrate observability into the biomarker detection problem as follows (Fig. 1):

Guided by the capabilities and limitations of current biotechnologies, our study adopts two core assumptions. First, following prior work on observability, we overinstrument the system to learn its dynamics before performing sensor selection. This approach ensures that the resulting model and selected biomarkers generalize across replicates and future experimental trials. Second, because the biological systems we consider are uncontrolled, we consider observability only through the perspective of autonomous systems, though the framework is extensible to nonautonomous systems.

Our work provides insights into the relationship between observability and the monitoring of biological systems, alongside the development of methods for DSS. We introduce approaches for DSS and propose strategies to integrate gene expression (RNA-seq) and chromosome structure (Hi-C) data within the observability framework. We demonstrate the utility and versatility of our framework across multiple datasets from genomics and beyond.

Preliminaries

The rank of the observability matrix O(x) is the traditional criterion used to assess observability. A system is observable when rank(O(x))=n. For the uncontrolled pair (f,g) the observability matrix is[3]O(x)=Lfg(x)Lf2g(x)Lf3g(x)⋮.

Here, Lfg denotes the Lie derivative (28). When (f,g) are nonlinear, the matrix derivatives are taken at x, and the rank of O(x) determines a local, weak observability criterion at the state of the system x (10). When the system is linear time-invariant (LTI), i.e. f(x)=Ax and g(x)=Cx, where A and C are matrices, the observability matrix is O=C⊤(CA)⊤(CA2)⊤⋯⊤, and the rank criterion is the famous Kalman condition, which establishes a global observability criterion for all x (29, 30). While local observability criteria indicate using data y one can distinguish the system state at x from neighboring states, global criteria indicate that with data y one can distinguish x from all possible states; see ref. 31 and references therein for mathematically precise definitions.

The application of rank-based criteria is impractical for high-dimensional systems with imperfect models of dynamics, as is often the case with biological systems. Take, for example, the DNA replication model in fission yeast proposed by B. Novak and J. J. Tyson (Fig. 1A) (32). Their differential equation models the mass of a cell and the concentration of the products expressed by twelve genes, forming a system that becomes observable if any gene is monitored (SI Appendix, Eq. S1) (22). Yet, in practice, due to poor conditioning the observability matrix O(x) is approximately low rank. To demonstrate this, we used synthetic data to construct O(x) for one thousand randomly selected state vectors x, testing each configuration where a single variable serves as the sensor. Although O(x) is full rank when using symbolic calculations, the singular values σ1,⋯,σ13 of O(x) reveal that these matrices are only effectively low rank across all thousand simulated data points (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The simulated data show that poor conditioning of the observability matrix O(x)–characterized by the ratio σk/σ1 being extremely small for k=2,⋯,13–gives the appearance that rank(O(x))≠n, as if the system is locally unobservable at all sampled state vectors when only one sensor is utilized (Fig. 1B). To address the practical concerns of rank based observability tests, a range of graded observability measures have been developed.

Observability Metrics.

Beyond the Kalman condition, several metrics M to quantify observability have been proposed (33). The Kalman condition, for instance, can be relaxed to measure the rank of the observability matrix:M1=rank(O(x))(directions).

When the system is not fully observable, i.e. M1<n, the number of directions or principal components of the system that can be observed is given by M1 (7).

The observability Gramian Go is an alternative matrix to quantify observability. For LTI systems, the observability Gramian Go is defined as the solution to the Lyapunov equations:A⊤Go+GoA=−C⊤C(continuous time),A⊤GoA−Go=−C⊤C(discrete time).

This matrix facilitates the calculation of two additional observability metrics:M2=x(0)⊤Gox(0)(energy)M3=trace(Go)(visibility).

The energy metric M2 reflects the amplitude of the measured data y(t), and the visibility metric M3 is like an average measure of observability for each direction in the state space (26).

The ability to compute the observability Gramian Go and the observability matrix O using various algorithms makes M1, M2, and M3 robust metrics well-suited for DSS. Additional observability metrics include: 1) structural observability, which is favored for its scalability (21), 2) algebraic observability, which is applicable to nonlinear systems (8, 22), and 3) additional Gramian based metrics (26, 33) (Table 1).

Motivating Problem

Sensor Selection Problem.

In order to maximize observability, the biomarker or sensor selection problem is formulated as[4]maxsensorsMsubject to experimental constraints.

Common constraints include a budget or inability to measure certain variables. Since each variable may or may not be measured, there are 2n candidate solutions to the sensor selection problem making the optimization challenging for high dimensional systems where n is large.

To optimize observability of the Novak and Tyson model, we applied a greedy sensor selection algorithm (SI Appendix, Algorithm S1). The greedy approach selects sensors iteratively, at each step selecting the candidate sensor that maximizes M1, averaged across all instances in the synthetic dataset. G1R was chosen first because it provided the highest rank of O(x) (Fig. 1A). With G1R selected, the algorithm then evaluated the observability M1 when monitoring G1R and each remaining state variable, selecting G2R as the next sensor (Fig. 1C). This process was repeated until M1 is maximized at 13, when monitoring the five variables: G1R, G2R, PG2R, UbE, and mass.

When there is no limit on how much data can be measured in a simulation or experiment, a greedy algorithm finds an optimal solution to the sensor selection problem of Eq. 4. As the greedy algorithm iteratively selects sensors, the system’s observability improves with each step. This is reflected in both the ratio between singular values σk/σ1 (Fig. 1B) and the average effective rank of M1 (Fig. 1D). However, the marginal utility of each additional sensor decreases as fewer unobserved directions remain, reflecting diminishing returns in observability provided by each additional sensor. The poor conditioning of O(x) and the diminishing return of additional sensors leads to our first result: Practical application requires consideration of data quality, local system states, and resource constraints to ensure the effective use of biomarkers that theoretically make a system observable.

Biomarker Observability Depends on Biological State.

Discovering new biomarkers traditionally relies on domain expertise (5). For example, the PIP-FUCCI system, a live cell microscopy approach for determining cell cycle stages (G1, S, G2, M), was developed based on knowledge of how certain genes vary throughout the cell cycle (34–36). Imaged expression of three genes reveals cell cycle stages and transitions: CDT1 (G1), PCNA (S), and GEM (S/G2/M). The position within the cell cycle (G1/S/G2/M) determines the relevance of each of these three marker genes.

As cells progress through the cell cycle, they may stall in the G1 phase, often referred to as G0, entering a state known as quiescence (Fig. 2A). During quiescence, the cell ceases to divide, much like a system reaching a stable equilibrium. Quiescent cancer cells are linked to high cancer recurrence, as these cells can reenter the cell cycle, and the reduced cell cycle activity diminishes the effectiveness of chemo- and immunotherapies (37–40). The proliferation-quiescence bifurcation is described mathematically as a transition between stability and periodicity in dynamical systems and coincides with a shift in observability (41). Smale’s two-cell system illustrates this concept, exhibiting stable solutions that correspond to quiescence and periodic solutions that resemble progression through the cell cycle (42). As a special case of Turing’s equations of morphogenesis, these dynamics have been characterized as “mathematically dead” when stable and “mathematically alive” when oscillatory (43–45).

Systems that are mathematically alive exhibit heightened observability. The Hopf bifurcation, the archetypal example of the transition between stable and periodic dynamics (47), is observed in the Andronov–Hopf oscillator:[5]dx1dt=αx1−x2−x1x12+x22dx2dt=x1+αx2−x2x12+x22.

As the parameter α transitions from negative to positive values (Fig. 2B), the system shifts from being mathematically dead to alive! To assess how observability transitions between the mathematically dead and alive states, we select x1 as a sensor and measure the output with y=x1. With the dynamics of the Andronov–Hopf oscillator and fixed sensor, empirical observability Gramians were constructed from simulated data with both dead and alive choices of α (48). Empirical observability Gramians Go are constructed from simulations and perturbations of the system Eq. 5, as opposed to solving the Lyapunov equations or other approximation techniques (SI Appendix, section 1.2). From the observability Gramian for each simulation, M3 measured the observability of the system (Fig. 2C). The degree of observability of this system is primarily controlled by the bifurcation parameter α and modulated by the choice of initial condition for the simulation. Variance in observability diminishes for α>0 because simulated trajectories are driven toward similar limit cycles or cyclic patterns. In contrast, when the system is stable and α<0, observability varies significantly between simulations with different initial conditions. This occurs because the starting point becomes the key differentiator for how quickly trajectories converge to the stable point. In other words, mathematically alive systems are more observable because the periodic behavior causes the system state cover a wider range of the state space, which increases the measurable information in the sensor data. This is consistent with the mathematically dual results that bifurcations and time-varying systems offer greater controllability (49–51).

The variation in observability governed by α serves as a mathematical analog to insights from PIP-FUCCI biomarkers: the degree of observability provided by sensors changes with the system’s intrinsic properties, such as α or the stage of the cell cycle, that are not represented in x. Although the gene CDT1 is a biomarker for the G1 phase, its contribution to observability diminishes during other cell cycle phases, necessitating the use of PCNA and GEM to delineate each phase. Similarly, as α increases, the contribution of the sensor x1 to system observability increases by a factor of 103. The highest variability in observability occurs near the bifurcation point during the transition between periodic and stable behaviors, where α is 0. Thus, the utility of measuring y=x1 is least certain at the point where the system is nearest to transitioning between stable and periodic behaviors (52). This observation leads us to our second main result: The dependence of biomarkers on biological state requires time-dependent biomarker selection.

Results

Dynamic Sensor Selection (DSS).

To address the need to identify and allocate biomarkers over shifts in the underlying dynamics, we developed DSS to identify time varying biomarkers based upon models of dynamics learned from time series data genomics. Due to the high dimensionality and relatively few time points found in genomics data, this approach is tailored to discrete time LTI and linear time-varying (LTV) models of dynamics that can be learned from time series transcriptomics data (SI Appendix, section 1.3). After learning a model of dynamics x(t+1)=A(t)x(t), DSS adapts the sensor selection problem (Eq. 4) to select sensors for each point in time:[6]maxsensors(t)Msubject to constraints at timet.

The selected sensors are placed in a measurement matrix C(t) that varies with time. While time-dependent systems have been studied in the context of controllability and robustness (53, 54), sensor selection for time-dependent systems has only received limited attention in the literature.

To optimize Eq. 6 for M1, M2, and M3, the time-dependent observability Gramian is required. The observability Gramian of a LTV system from time t0 to time t is[7]Go(t0,t)=∑k=t0tΦ(t0,k)⊤C(k)⊤C(k)Φ(t0,k),

where Φ(t0,t) is the time-dependent state transition matrix from t0 to t, given byΦ(t0,t)=A(t)⋯A(t0+1)A(t0).

Maximization of M2 is achieved by solving the eigenvalue problem:[8]K(t,t0)C(t)⊤=C(t)⊤D,

where K(t,t0) is a Gram matrix learned from data asK(t,t0)=Φ(t,t0)x(t0)x(t0)⊤Φ(t,t0)⊤.

Like the observability Gramian, the Gram matrix K(t,t0) describes the pairwise relationship between states of the system from time t0 until time t (SI Appendix, section 2.1) (24, 55). The columns of C(t) in Eq. 8 correspond to optimal sensor placement at time t and the contribution to observability is weighted by the eigenvalues found in D.

Measure M3 can be maximized with a linear program. The LTV observability Gramian in Eq. 7 can be expressed equivalently as[9]Go(t0,t)=∑i=t0t∑j=1nδijWij,

where Wij=Φ(t0,i)⊤(cij)⊤(cij)Φ(t0,i), δij indicates 1 if variable j is measured at time i and 0 otherwise, and cij is a row vector with the jth entry as 1 and 0 otherwise. Because the matrix trace is linear, when δij is relaxed to a continuous value, i.e. 0≤δij≤1, a linear program can solve the optimization:[10]maxδM3where0≤δij≤1.

Constraints can be incorporated into this optimization, such as restricting the number of sensors p at time t with the constraint: ∑j=1nδij≤p(t). See SI Appendix, section 2 for precise details on these optimizations. In the following sections, we demonstrate a range of applications for the DSS methodology in biological systems.

Estimating Unmeasured Genes.

To evaluate DSS, we identified genes critical to observing the dynamics of Pseudomonas fluorescens SBW25, a bacterium used for insect control, from data collected by Hasnain et al. (24). The dataset comprises time series spanning nine time points and 600 genes, representing a high-dimensional system that presents challenges for observation. Gene regulation in the bacteria or other cells is represented by Eq. 1, where x(t)i denotes the expression of the ith gene, and A(t) is the gene regulatory network at time t. The matrix element A(t)ij specifies the influence of gene j on gene i at time point t. We selected two sets of sensors that 1) optimize M2 over time with DSS and 2) optimize M2 with fixed sensors throughout the experiment.

We found that DSS improves state estimation relative to using fixed sensors. To test estimation capabilities, first, dynamics A(t) are learned and biomarkers are selected using data from one replicate of an experiment, and then, data from another test replicate of the experiment are divided into selected biomarkers and remaining unobserved nonbiomarkers. Estimating the nonbiomarker gene expression can then be formulated as solving the following least squares problem:[11]minx^‖Y−Ox^‖,

where x^ is an efficient estimator of x(0) (56). The biomarker data y(t) is assembled in a matrix Y=y(0)⊤y(1)⊤⋯⊤. In Eq. 11, O is constructed from the data used to learn the dynamics and select biomarkers, and Y is observed data from the replicate used for validating biomarker efficacy. The estimation of unmeasured genes in Eq. 11 has the solution x^=O†Y, where † denotes the pseudoinverse (57). To measure the quality of the estimation of x^(0), it is compared to the true data of x(0), which comes from the same replicate as the test data Y. This error can be measured using several metrics, e.g. ‖x^−x(0)‖, but the component-wise error is most relevant to assessing the error of estimating individual genes. DSS consistently improved the median estimation error for each of the 600 genes, regardless of the number of sensors used (Fig. 3A). The median estimation error measures the ability to estimate the expression values of individual genes. In spite of the biomarkers and system not satisfying the Kalman rank condition or the Popov–Belevitch–Hautus test, the incorporation of time-varying dynamics and sensors enables unmeasured genes to be estimated with an error within 50%, a level of accuracy that is practically useful for many real-world applications.

Functional Observers for Cellular Reprogramming.

While DSS enhances state estimation of unmeasured missing gene expression values, many biomedical applications rely on biomarkers to indicate phenotypes or cell types. Early detection of cellular reprogramming, a process that transforms cell type and induces a shift in the dynamics of the cell’s transcriptional program, is an important and unresolved challenge in biomanufacturing (58). This task falls under the framework of functional observability, where the goal is to select biomarkers or sensors that enable the estimation of specific modes of the unmeasured states—such as phenotype—without reconstruction of all unmeasured state variables or genes (59).

A system is functionally observable for the modes defined by the rows of the matrix F if[12]rankOF=rank(O).

Here, F is a matrix where each row represents a functionally observable mode or direction of the system. When F is sparse, with only one nonzero entry per row, individual states can be estimated—a property known as targeted observability (9, 60). In contrast, when the rows of F are dense, each row can represent a cell type, and each element within a row can correspond to the expression of a particular gene for that cell type, enabling the system to be functionally observable for a biological state.

A system is always functionally observable for the principal components (or right singular vectors) of O, which are utilized in the estimation framework of Eq. 11 (7). By applying the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to the observability matrix O=UΣV⊤, the pseudoinverse O† is expressed as O†=VΣ−1U⊤. Consequently, the state estimation of x^ is computed as[13]x^=O†Y=VΣ−1U⊤Y.

This equation demonstrates that the state estimate x^ is a linear combination of the right singular vectors (V) weighted by the contributions of the nonzero singular values (Σ−1) and the data (U⊤Y). Since the rows of V⊤ associated with nonzero singular values are in the row space of O, the system is functionally observable for the modes described by F=V⊤.

With time series gene expression data from a recreation of Weintraub’s seminal 1989 reprogramming experiment, we built a functional observer for the reprogramming of fibroblasts to skeletal muscle (61). To monitor the progression of myogenic reprogramming initiated by the introduction of the transcription factor MYOD, bulk RNA-seq data were collected at 8-h intervals (62). Cellular reprogramming remains characterized by partial reprogramming and low efficiency, which result in weak and noisy signals. To address this, we developed two LTV models of gene expression dynamics to amplify the reprogramming signal and facilitate observer construction. The first model (Model 1) encompassed all 19,235 genes measured during the experiment, while the second model (Model 2) focused on 406 genes involved in cell cycle regulation and myogenic lineages, aiming to enhance the weak reprogramming signal. To identify biomarkers, we optimized M2 on Model 2 to identify which reprogramming genes most strongly contribute to system observability. The top ranked reprogramming biomarkers identified by Model 2 were used to monitor Model 1.

To identify the functionally observable modes of Model 1 with the selected biomarkers, we constructed the observability matrix O of Model 1, performed the SVD O=UΣV⊤, and considered the rows of V⊤ associated with nonzero singular values. Each row of V⊤ is a functionally observable mode, and the entries of a row in V⊤ indicate the expression of different genes. We performed cell type and functional enrichment to determine the biological processes that could be observed associated with each mode. This revealed cell types such as fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, and myoblasts, all known to be involved in myogenic reprogramming (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Similarly, there is a strong preference for myogenic and cell cycle genes, both involved in reprogramming, to be heavily weighted in the functionally observable modes. Further enrichment analysis highlights cellular activities like the defense response to viruses (GO:0051607), aligning with the expected response due to Lentiviral reprogramming, and regulation of the cell cycle (GO:0051726) and smooth muscle cell proliferation (GO:0048661), consistent with the cell division and differentiation that occurred in these data (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The enrichment of functionally observable modes corresponding to biological states and processes consistent with the experiment suggests that, despite the low reprogramming signal and substantial noise, biomarkers identified using DSS are well suited for monitoring cellular reprogramming.

Chromatin Informed Biomarkers.

Integrating biological insights or domain knowledge that is not captured in the system model f(·) or state space x into the observability-guided biomarker selection framework helps align the sensor selection problem with practical biological considerations. For instance, monitoring multiple biomarkers within a transcription factory—a group of genes that is colocalized in the nucleus where the genes are often coregulated—may provide redundant information (63, 64). Modifying the sensor selection problem to limit the number of biomarkers per transcription factory or satisfy other requirements is achieved by modifying the constraints of Eq. 6.

To select biomarkers in the context of transcription factors, we use Hi-C data, which provides information on genome structure, to constrain Eq. 4. With Hi-C data from the study by Liu et al. (62), we generated gene-by-gene Hi-C matrices representing observed contact frequencies between genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). From the gene-centric Hi-C matrix, we performed hierarchical clustering and optimized the silhouette score to construct gene clusters c1,⋯,cl, where each cluster represents a group of genes that are likely proximal to one another (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Then, we constrained Eq. 4 to prevent the simultaneous selection of multiple genes found within the same cluster: maxsensors(t)Msuch that at most one gene is measuredper cluster at each timet.

To maximize M2, we applied a greedy heuristic by first solving the unconstrained maximization, then selecting the top ranked sensors that meet the constraints. For M3, these constraints from Hi-C data can directly be incorporated into the linear program used to maximize Eq. 10.

Although this constrained optimization yields a lower utility of the objective function M, the selected biomarkers have two practical advantages. First, they are distributed across the genome in a pattern that mirrors natural gene placement across chromosomes (Fig. 3B). Second, their performance is comparable to that of biomarkers selected from the unconstrained dataset. Integrating chromatin-informed constraints into the biomarker selection process, ensures observability maximization in the context of and consistent with prior biological knowledge.

Beyond the Genome.

To demonstrate the utility of observability guided biomarkers beyond genomics, we applied DSS to in vivo single-cell endomicroscopic signals collected by Sweeney et al. (65). We constructed a LTI model for each experimental phase—feeding, fasting, and refeeding—and identified the contribution to observability (Fig. 3C). Our results revealed substantial shifts in monitoring utility across neurons. Notably, the neuron with the lowest initial output energy (M2) exhibited the highest contribution to observability during refeeding (Fig. 3D). A similar pattern is confirmed for M3. The varying contributions to observability-where some neurons consistently act as good sensors, others are transiently effective only during feeding states, and some gain significant observability contributions after fasting-underscore the importance of dynamically selecting sensors.

At a larger scale, we applied DSS to assess the observability of electroencephalogram (EEG) signals (66). Brain activity, sampled from 64 EEG leads at 160 Hz across a cohort of over 100 patients, provides high-quality time series data well suited for modeling and observability analysis (67, 68). We evaluated the observability contribution of each of the 64 EEG leads as participants transitioned through various states, including open-eye and closed-eye conditions and four tasks involving hand and foot movements (Fig. 3E). Notably, sensor performance varied significantly, particularly between the open-eye and closed-eye conditions (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). These findings highlight the applicability of DSS and observability-guided biomarkers to a range of critical applications and data modalities.

Discussion

In this work, we extended the tools of observability theory to identify biomarkers, accounting for the practicalities and constraints of experimental biological data. Our key findings include 1) that the contribution to observability of biomarkers depends on the biological state. This work establishes a connection between the state-dependent utility of biomarkers and the concepts of local and time-dependent observability. Moreover, 2) we developed DSS as a mechanism to optimize observability as the dynamics of the underlying system change. The development of DSS provides a computational approach for sensor selection in time-varying systems, a concept long recognized in observability theory but only recently made feasible for genomic biomarkers due to advances in measurement technologies. Compared to traditional methods like Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs), our observability approach selects biomarkers with improved monitoring and estimation capabilities, along with clearer mechanistic interpretation (SI Appendix, section 1.4.2) (24).

Our application to real biomedical data demonstrates both the versatility of this approach and highlights practicalities of observability that are often overlooked in theoretical discussions and the absence of real data. In particular, this work relaxes the need for mathematical modeling of biological systems by leveraging data-guided modeling, enabling the detection of biomarkers through observability analysis in any biomedical time series data. This also highlights the relevance of ensuring theoretical criteria can be validated numerically, within real or synthetic data.

This work also raises several research directions worthy of future pursuit. First, controllability—a concern in treatment and prevention—is mathematically dual to observability in LTI models. Like observability, the controllability of time-varying systems has been analyzed within similar mathematical frameworks-for example, to identify a minimal set of driver nodes (27, 53). As biotechnology advances to enable closed-loop control at the transcriptomic level, incorporating temporal dynamics and optimizing actuator selection could provide biological systems with benefits analogous to those achieved in engineered and networked systems (51). However, current biological control strategies at the scale considered in this study are typically precomputed, open-loop interventions that operate independently of sensor feedback (69). Consequently, the observability framework developed here assumes no dynamic input. However, it is well known that for nonlinear systems, observability can depend critically on the applied input signal. Therefore, if time-dependent inputs were introduced during an experiment, Eqs. 1 and 2 would need to be generalized to include input terms, and a more comprehensive observability analysis that accounts for input-dependent dynamics would be required.

Second, while measured data can determine the unmeasured states of an observable system, there are many ways and algorithms to perform such an estimation. Here, we have used the most basic approach, performing a least squares estimation; this leaves open several avenues for the design and construction of observers tailored to the unique noise, sparsity, and destructive nature of transcriptomics assays. The development of observers is important for realizing the utility of observability-guided biomarkers with emerging sequencing technologies.

Third, the state space representation of a cell is crucial for determining observability criteria (70). In this work, we adopted the raw data as the state space, but this may not be the most optimal approach. Future research could explore enhanced representations of a cell that integrate both genomic structure and function by incorporating additional data modalities.

Finally, theoretical investigations into bifurcation observability, particularly in the context of Smale’s two-cell system, are closely related to the bifurcation control problem and warrant further attention (49, 71). These areas, and others, must be further developed to leverage the rapid development of emerging experimental and computational biotechnologies.
