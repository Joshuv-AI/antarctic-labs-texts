Relevance in question: A rare case of Actinomyces radicidentis bacteremia
Authors: Hagen Frickmann, Frauke Theis, Philipp Warnke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActinomyces spp.-associated bloodstream infections have been infrequently described and repeatedly but not exclusively associated with intra-oral infections. For Actinomyces radicidentis, which has been shown to cause biofilm-forming endodontic infections, bacteremia has only once been reported so far. To elaborate on the scarcely available information on potential etiological relevance of A. radicidentis as a cause of bloodstream infection, we describe another case of A. radicidentis bacteremia.Case reportA. radicidentis confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing was detected in the bloodstream of an 81-year-old male multimorbid patient with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma of non-germinal center type under chemotherapy as the most critical medical condition. A specific focal infection was not recorded. A concomitantly diagnosed Pseudomonas aeruginosa-associated urinary tract infection was treated with piperacillin/tazobactam for 1 day and meropenem for additional 9 days. While the course of the infection was considered as favorable by the clinicians in charge, an unfavorable course of the underlying malignant disease let to demission of the patients for palliative care at home.DiscussionAlthough A. radicidentis bacteremia was not specifically addressed, negative effects on the clinical course were not confirmed.ConclusionsThe etiological relevance of A. radicidentis bacteremia in patients without specific infectious foci remains uncertain.

Introduction

Acintomyces radicidentis is a coccoid bacterium from the phylum Actinomycetota. It was described in 2000 associated with infected root canals of teeth [1] and later repeatedly reconfirmed at this location [2]. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Resistance Testing (EUCAST) does not report breakpoints according to the S (susceptible) – I (susceptible at increased dosage) – R (resistant) classification. However, as recently reported, expected minimum inhibitory concentrations of 90% of isolates (MIC90) are 0.5 mg L−1 for benzylpenicillin, cefuroxime and vancomycin as well as 8 mg mL−1 for ciprofloxacin and colistin applying E-tests (bioMérieux, Nürtingen, Germany) [2].

A. radicidentis' potential of forming biofilms is considered as a relevant virulence factor mediating the species' facultative pathogenicity for human individuals. Large bacterial aggregates embedded in extracellular matrix even within vital host tissues form a protective biofilm-environment. This evolutionary strategy helps circumventing immunological host responses like phagocytosis [3].

Nevertheless, the potential role of A. radicidentis as a pathogen even at typical isolation sites like root canal samples of teeth with endodontic infections, chronic peri-radicular lesions or acute apical periodontitis has to be considered as uncertain. The reason is its comparably rare detection compared to other likely causative agents like, e.g., Propionibacterium proprionicus [4]. In contrast, individual case reports on A. radicidentis-associated failure of endodontic therapy have been convincingly described [5]. Consequently, anti-A. radicidentis effects of endodontic sealers used in case of endodontic interventions were comparatively assessed [6] as well as anti-A. radicidentis-containing biofilm effects of ultrasonically activated trypsin with or without chlorhexidine and of sodium hypochlorite [7–9]. A potential association of A. radicidentis and dental caries has been investigated but could not be confirmed [10].

Systemic infections associated with A. radicidentis have even less frequently been reported in international medical literature. In 2016, an individual case of A. radicidentis bacteremia in a pneumonia patient was presented as a clinical picture format [11]. Apart from this single described case report, Actinomyces-induced pulmonary lesions are usually associated with allergic reaction to inhalation of thermophilic actinomycetes. This medical condition is called “farmer's lung” and usually not associated with bacteremia [12]. Anyway, even beyond the species level, Actinomyces spp. bacteremia remains a rare event, which is often but not exclusively associated with infections in the oral cavity [13–17]. Adding to the scarcely available literature, we describe another case of A. radicidentis-associated bacteremia to elaborate on its controversial role in case of diagnostic detection in the human bloodstream.

Case report

In the here-presented case, A. radicidentis was detected in a peripheral blood culture taken at admission of an 81-year-old male patient with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (NOS) of non-germinal center type at a German university hospital. At the time of admission, the patient's malignant disease was under chemotherapy including rituximab, doxorubicin, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, and prednisolone (R-mini CHOP scheme, 4th cycle). The medical history of the multimorbid patient comprised chronical heart and kidney insufficiency, two-vessel coronary heart disease, arterial hypertonus, persistent atrial fibrillation, multi-nodal thyroid enlargement, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), type-2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, obesity, as well as a history of appendectomy, encephalomyelitis and nicotine abuse. Due to benign prostate hyperplasia, residual urine formation had facilitated a urinary tract infection due to Enterococcus faecium six weeks prior to the present admission, resulting in antimicrobial treatment with linezolid for 7 days and the placement of a suprapubic catheter. Prior to admission, the patient had lived with his wife in an assisted living facility for elderly individuals. He had formerly worked as a blacksmith.

At the time of hospital admission resulting in the detection of A. radicidentis in peripheral blood, the patient presented in a reduced general condition with dyspnea and intermittent chills but without fever, cough or nausea. Increased values of C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin and increased lymphocyte counts (Table 1) pointed towards a likely infection event. Increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 455 U/L (reference: 135–255 U/L) was in line both with the patient's malignant disease and cell destruction due to an infection event.

Physical examination was inconspicuous with the exemption of the known cardiac arrhythmia and wheezing over both lungs in line with the patient's COPD. In particular, examination of the oral cavity did not reveal more than a slight redness. A redness was also observed at the entry site of the recently placed suprapubic catheter.

X-ray of the thorax indicated peri-bronchial compaction in the left lower lung field which could be interpreted either as ventilation disturbance or as pneumonic infiltration. Echocardiography showed organizing residuals of a previously known hemorrhagic pericardiac effusion without hemodynamic relevance and a reduced left-ventricular ejection fraction of about 45%–50%.

Microbiological work-up indicated >105 colony-forming units (cfu) per µL of a wild-type Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 500 cfu/µL Candida parapsilosis in supra-pubic catheter urine. Workup of the slightly turbid urine at the clinical chemistry department indicated nitrite as well as increased values for leukocytes at 75/µL (reference <25/µL), erythrocytes at 10/µL (reference <10/µL) and protein at 1,000 mg L−1 (reference <300 mg L−1).

From a single set of positive blood culture bottles taken at the day of admission, A. radicidentis was isolated, while another set taken at the same day remained negative. Gram-staining from incubated blood culture material, colony morphology on agar as well as Gram-staining from brain heart infusion broth and Kinyoun staining from agar culture material are visualized in Figs 1–4. Of note, the coccoid micromorphology combined with positive Gram staining and negative Kinyoun staining results, along with the macromorphology of the colonies on Columbia agar, may lead investigators to confuse it with staphylococci, as Actinomyces spp. are typically associated with a rod-shaped morphology.

As matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (Vitek MS IVD V2, database MS-CE version CLI 2.0.0; bioMérieux Deutschland GmbH, Nürtingen, Germany) assessment proved to be insufficient for a reliable diagnosis at species level, the isolate was subjected to 16S rRNA sequence analysis using the pan-bacterial forward primer 16S8_27 (AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG) and reverse primer 16S519 (GWATTACCGCGGCKGCTG) as well as a PCR run protocol as described elsewhere [18, 19]. The run conditions comprised initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 30 cycles at 94 °C (denaturation), 50 °C (annealing) and 72 °C (amplification) for one minute each with a subsequent final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min on a Biometra TProfessional Basis cycler (Biometra, Göttingen, Germany). Agarose gel electrophoresis using 1.2% agarose gels was applied to purify the obtained bacterial amplicons. Afterwards, the amplicons were shipped for commercial Sanger sequencing to Seqlab (Microsynth AG, Göttingen, Germany). Quality-control of the obtained sequence-files was ensured using the Finch Trace Viewer software (Geospiza Inc., Seattle, WA, USA). Subsequently, the sequence was identified with the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) provided by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). A 100% matching with the A. radicidentis strain CCUG 36733 (GenBank accession number CP014228.1) over 489 base-pairs was confirmed, the sequence with removed primer-binding sites is provided as Appendix 1. The next closely related species was the Gram-positive coccoid bacterium Actinomyces haliotis. However, with only 97.96% sequence identity, the matching was beyond the acceptable range of <1.5% sequence identity which has been recommended for 16S rRNA-gene sequence based identification of Actinomyces spp. on species level [20]. As sequencing was conducted for diagnostic purposes only, no sequence upload of the newly obtained sequence to GenBank was conducted. In deviation from EUCAST (European Committee for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) recommendations advising broth microdilution to obtain MIC (minimum inhibitory concentrations) values for aerobic bacteria with lacking EUCAST reference values, E-tests from Liofilchem (Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) were applied. Measured MIC values were 0.047 mg L−1 for amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, 0.008 mg L−1 for meropenem, and 0.75 mg L−1 for linezolid.

Clinically, suspicion of a P. aeruginosa-associated urinary tract infection led to an antibiotic therapy for altogether 10 days. An initial treatment with piperacillin (4 g)/tazobactam (0.5 g) 1-1-1 started at day 1 directly after admission was switched to meropenem (1g) 1-1-1 at day 2. At the time of demission, leukocyte count had normalized (Table 1), while persisting high CRP values were interpreted as likely tumor-associated. No specific focus was associated with the A. radicidentis detection, although it was clinically considered as co-treated keeping the measured low MIC value for meropenem in mind, even in spite of lacking reference values for susceptibility assessment. In addition to antimicrobial therapy, the suprapubic catheter was changed.

While the initially suspected infectious focus was clinically considered as adequately addressed, the underlying malignant disease drastically deteriorated during the patient's hospital stay. Respecting the patient's wishes, chemotherapy was aborted and the patient was retransferred for palliative care to his assisted living facility. No further hospital stays were recorded, suggesting a fatal course in line with the palliative situation.

Discussion

The case once more demonstrates the challenging interpretation of A. radicidentis detections in the human bloodstream. In the so far only published historic case [11] of such a finding in a 47-year-old male with pneumonia responsive to combination therapy with vancomycin and piperacillin/tazobactam for 3 days followed by another 7 days of oral amoxicillin/clavulanic acid therapy, it remained uncertain whether A. radicidentis was associated with the pneumonic infiltrates or whether it just represented a transient bacteremia associated with the patient's poor dental status.

In the here-presented case, the managing clinicians considered the P. aeruginosa detection in the patient's urine to be the more likely source of the patient's presumed infection, while the A. radicidentis detection was considered as a side finding. Nevertheless, the recorded low MIC of A. radicidentis for meropenem was another reason for the therapeutic switch from piperacillin/tazobactam to meropenem. Notably, there are no therapeutic standards for the antimicrobial therapy of systemic A. radicidentis infections and no interpretation standards for E-test-based MIC values of A. radicidentis isolates. Insofar, it can only be speculated whether or not the chosen antimicrobial therapy was really associated with the observed clinical course.

Next to the antimicrobial substance of choice, the treatment duration in case of infections caused by systemic A. radicidentis isolates is also uncertain. The 10 days of antimicrobial treatment chosen for the here-presented case of A. radicidentis bacteremia perfectly match the previously described case [11]. Further, the treatment duration is well in line with the up-to-date evidence-guided recommendation of short antibiotic courses about 7 days for cases of bloodstream infections apart from special situations [21].

Most reported human A. radicidentis infections have been associated with oral foci [1–5, 10] and even in the other reported case of A. radicidentis in the human bloodstream [11], a poor dental status had been reported. Interestingly, such an infectious focus in the oral cavity was not observed in the here-presented case and the patient had documented complete dentures in the upper and lower jaw. In case of historic pharyngeal or laryngeal infections with Actinomyces spp., requirement for long antibiotic therapies between 1 month and up-to 2.5 years have been reported [22]. Surgical management of topical foci is commonly necessary as well. This is particularly true if biofilms have to be considered [3, 5], easily resulting in failure of non-surgical approaches [5].

The lack of a detected lesion in the oral cavity might therefore explain the good therapeutic success of beta-lactam antibiotics-based treatment over just 10 days. On the other hand, it challenges potential assignment of etiological relevance to the blood culture isolate. Hypothetically, a transient bacteremia event, e.g., associated with mild trauma affecting the mucous membranes during oral hygiene, might have led to the single case of a positively tested peripheral blood culture with A. radicidentis as well.

Focusing on the diagnosis P. aeruginosa-associated urinary tract infection as favored by the clinicians in charge, some uncertainties remain, too. First of all, both P. aeruginosa and Candida spp. can occur as mere colonizers in mixed biofilms on urinary tract catheters [23]. So, the sole abundance, even in significant quantities, does not prove infection by itself. Second, leukocyturia and erythrocyturia do not necessarily indicate urinary tract infection in case of indwelling urinary catheters [24, 25]. Third, although positive nitrite tests have indeed been infrequently reported in case of P. aeruginosa-associated urinary tract infections [26], measurable nitrate reduction in urine samples is not typically associated with this microorganism.

Considering the unfavorable general course of the patient's disease, the questions of successful anti-infectious therapy and of potential contributions of an infection event to the observed clinical course are difficult to answer anyway. While initial leukocytosis was regredient in the course of antimicrobial therapy, CRP values remained increased and their interpretation stayed challenging due to the concomitant malignant disease. And considering the patient's final demission for palliative care, not even intermediate-term survival could be used as a criterium to verify or falsify a favorable therapeutic outcome of the chosen infectious disease management.

Focusing on the bacterial agent itself, the Gram staining of the blood culture material and the broth culture as well as colony morphology on blood agar again confirmed likely confusion with staphylococcal bloodstream infection as reported elsewhere [11]. Sequencing of the 16S rRNA genes finally allowed a conclusive diagnosis, while traditional diagnostic assays like the applied MALDI-TOF-MS approach had provided non-conclusive results only. However, depending on the quality of the used database, MALDI-TOF-MS-based identification of A. radicidentis has been described as feasible as well [11].

Conclusions

The here-presented case confirms that the assignment of etiological relevance to A. radicidentis detections in the human bloodstream remains challenging, especially if no pathogen-specific infectious focus can be identified. This case report shows that non-consideration of potential systemic A. radicidentis infection during antimicrobial therapy at least did not lead to proven therapeutic failure. Challenges regarding the discrimination of A. radicidentis when applying routine diagnostic standard approaches in the infectious disease laboratory can be successfully overcome applying 16S rRNA gene sequencing.
