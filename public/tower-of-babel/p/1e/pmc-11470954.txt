Association between brain resting-state functional activities and migraine: a bidirectional mendelian randomization study
Authors: Qian Lu, Zhenyu Jia, Hanqing Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Researchers have conducted extensive research on the correlation between brain resting-state functional activities (RSFA) and migraine. However, we still do not fully understand the exact nature of the causal relationship between these RSFA and migraine. We conducted a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate the causal association between migraine and RSFA. We gathered summary statistics from genome-wide association studies for 191 resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging phenotypes. We employed various analytical methods for bidirectional two-sample MR analyses. This included inverse variance weighted, weighted median, MR Egger, and the constrained maximum likelihood approaches. We also conducted pleiotropy and heterogeneity analyses to evaluate the robustness and reliability. We found the functional connectivity between the default mode and the central executive network (OR = 1.39, p = 4.77 × 10−4, FDR corrected p value = 0.040) and the intensity of spontaneous brain activity in the calcarine or lingual gyrus within the visual network (OR = 0.74, p = 5.94 × 10−4, FDR corrected p value = 0.040) having a causal effect on the risk of migraine. Our MR analysis provided genetic support for these networks, which may play an important role in influencing migraine susceptibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74745-2.

Introduction

Migraine is a common episodic disorder characterized by debilitating headaches, often accompanied by nausea and/or sensitivity to light and sound1. From 1990 to 2019, the global prevalence of migraine was 14–15% according to epidemiological studies. Migraine is a prominent cause of population ill health, accounting for 4.9% of global population ill health as measured in years lived with disability2. Nevertheless, the pathophysiological mechanisms of this disorder remain unclear. Given its high prevalence, significant health burden, and wide-ranging impact on individuals and society, the study of the pathophysiological mechanisms of migraine is important for reducing the health burden of the population and improving individual health.

Lakhan et al.3 have elucidated key changes in brain regions associated with migraine in a review covering three decades of research, supporting the view that migraine is a complex central nervous system disorder primarily linked to abnormal central nervous system functioning. A positron emission tomography (PET) using [15O]H2O found significant hypothalamic activation during the premonitory phase of a triggered migraine attack4. Similarly, increased activity in the occipital cortex was observed in PET scans with [15O]H2O during the prodromal phase of migraine5. These findings suggested that migraine is associated with abnormal brain functional activity in cortical and subcortical areas. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) measures the synchronization of spontaneous fluctuations in blood oxygenation level-dependent signals in the absence of a task or stimulus. This technique provides an indirect measure of brain resting-state functional activities (RSFA) and has proven effective in exploring the pathophysiological mechanism of migraine6.

The majority of studies have employed rs-fMRI to explore brain RSFA abnormalities in migraine patients using seed point-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis and independent component analysis7. For example, the default mode network (DMN) and its associated brain regions have been proposed to have a robust correlation with migraine7,8. Tessitore et al.9 observed a reduction in the FC between the prefrontal and temporal regions within the DMN in migraine patients. Zou et al.10 observed a reduction in FC between the left superior frontal gyrus and the left precuneus of the DMN. Another study reported an increase in FC between left precuneus and the posterior cingulate cortex11. Furthermore, a study of migraine with aura (MwA) demonstrated that MwA exhibited stronger FC in lingual gyrus within visual network compared to migraine without aura (MwoA)12. In contrast, Hougaard et al.13 did not identify this abnormality in a relatively large sample of MwA patients. Due to the limitations of current observational studies, such as small sample sizes, potential uncontrolled confounding factors, and unclear causality, further research is needed to explore and validate the causal relationship between brain RSFA and migraine.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a widely employed methodology in epidemiological studies, whereby causal inferences are drawn from observational data by introducing genetic variation as a tool14,15. Given that genetic variation is an intrinsic and largely autonomous phenomenon, largely independent of self-selected behaviors, MR serves to mitigate the issue of reverse causation and confounding variables within observational data16,17. The large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that target brain RSFA and neuropsychiatric disorders afford the opportunity to explore the causal relationships between exposures (such as brain functional networks) and outcomes (such as diseases)18,19. Recently, two MR studies have investigated the causal relationship between brain imaging phenotypes and migraine. A study examining the causal effects of subcortical areas and intracranial volume on migraine20, while the other study explored the causal relationship between the microstructure of brain white matter and migraine21. However, the causal relationship between brain RSFA and migraine remains unclear.

In this study, we used a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal effect of brain RSFA on migraine and the reverse causality, based on the genetic variants of the GWASs summary statistics of migraine and 191 brain rs-fMRI phenotypes. Based on the reported association between brain RSFA and migraine, we hypothesized that the abnormality in brain RSFA is causally associated with an increased risk of migraine. By exploring this potential relationship, we hope to offer insights into the neurophysiological changes underlying migraine, which may have significant implications for therapeutic and preventive strategies.

Methods

Study design

We applied a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between brain RSFA and migraine, using genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs). Three key assumptions must be satisfied for the selected single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to be accepted as IVs: (1) they must be consistently linked to the exposure; (2) they must be linked to the outcome exclusively through the exposure; and (3) they must not be linked to other variables that influence the outcome.

Data sources

The genetic data for 191 brain rs-fMRI phenotypes (N = 34,691 cases) were obtained from a large GWAS study of intrinsic brain function18. This work investigated the association in the UK Biobank dataset between 9,026,427 common variations and 1777 intrinsic brain activity phenotypes. Considering the relatively limited impact of genetics on brain RSFA compared to brain structure22–24, they used a filter to identify the phenotypes that were significantly influenced by genetic variations from a total of 1777 characteristics, using a significance threshold of p < 5 × 10−8. Ultimately, 191 phenotypes were selected as GWAS, including 75 for node amplitude features, 111 for pairwise functional connectivity and 5 for global functional connectivity.

The GWAS summary statistics for migraine were obtained from FinnGen (ID: G6_MIGRAINE) and included 20,908 cases and 312,803 controls of European ancestry (http://www.finngen.fi/en). 82.7% of the patients were female. At the first migraine episode, the average age was 40.47 years. The Finnish Hospital Discharge Register’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD) criteria were used to diagnose each case. ICD-10: G43 was used to diagnose migraine patients.

Instrumental variables selection

To fulfil the three key assumptions, we filtered the IVs prior to conducting MR analyses. Initially, selected were only SNPs that had a highly significant association with exposure (p < 5 × 10 −8). Linkage disequilibrium clump was used to ensure ensuring minimal correlation between selected SNPs with the default parameters: r2 = 0.001, a window size of 10,000 kb, and European population. Subsequently, we harmonized the exposure and outcome data by eliminating SNPs with alleles that are not compatible or SNPs with intermediate frequencies that form palindromic sequences. Following this, we performed Steiger filtering to preclude the potential influence of reverse causality on our findings. To mitigate bias from weak IVs, we selected SNPs with F-statistic values exceeding 10. Lastly, we eliminated the SNPs linked to confounding variables. Previous research suggests that drinking and smoking may have an impact on migraines25,26. We eliminated the SNPs that have a strong correlation with these two traits in forward MR analysis.

Bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses

We conducted bidirectional two-sample MR analyses to investigate the causality between brain rs-fMRI phenotypes and migraine. Brain rs-fMRI phenotypes were used as the exposure in forward MR studies, with migraine as the outcome. Reverse MR analyses were conducted with migraine as the exposure and brain rs-fMRI phenotypes as the outcome. The main method employed for causal inference in the present investigation was the Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) approach. IVW approach has the highest statistical power, but can bias results if the pleiotropy is different from zero27,28. In order to enhance the dependability of the MR estimates, we therefore applied three additional MR methods. The MR-Egger technique implies that the instrument’s strength is not influenced by the direct influence, and the estimated causality impact aligns with the slope coefficient derived from the Egger regression29,30. The Weighted median method determines the causal impact by calculating the median of the weighted empirical distribution of each SNP effect estimation. This method accounts for horizontal pleiotropy in a maximum of 50% of genetic variations31. The constrained maximum likelihood (cML) approach approach was used to get rid of bias caused by both related and unrelated pleiotropy, thereby offering robust inferences for MR analysis involving numerous weak instruments32. For the sake of robustness of MR results, a causation between exposure and outcome can only be assumed if: at minimum, four SNPs are considered IVs, all causal effect assessments are in the same sense. To mitigate the risk of an increased overall false positive rate due to multiple comparisons, we employed the Benjamini-Hochberg method for false discovery rate (FDR) correction. IVW results with an FDR-corrected p value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

The validity of the MR analyses findings was confirmed by a number of sensitivity studies. MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO Global test were used to examine the existence of horizontal pleiotropy29,33. The heterogeneity test of the impact between SNPs was conducted using Cochran’s Q statistic. A leave-one-out (LOO) analysis was conducted to see whether the relationship between variables was primarily influenced by a single SNP with significant horizontal pleiotropy28.

Statistical analysis

The MR-PRESSO package (1.0) and TwoSampleMR (version 0.6.0) in R Software 4.3.3 (https://www.R-project.org) were used for all analyses. As stated in Table S5 (in supplementary information), our research adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using MR Statement34. Online resources were used to calculate power for the MR investigation (https://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/). The metrics computed for the binary outcomes were the sample size, α (Type-I error rate, with an α threshold of 0.05), percentage of cases, odds ratio (OR), and R2 (the percentage of variation in the exposure variable explained by SNPs). The forward MR analysis estimated the OR for migraine risk with each standard deviation (SD) rise in brain RSFA. An OR of 1 indicates an equal risk of migraine; an OR < 1 indicates a decreased risk of migraine and vice versa. Then, we used genetic IVs in migraine GWAS data to figure out the β value, which shows the number of standardized coefficients of change in brain RSFA attributable to migraine.

Results

The causal effects of brain resting-state functional activities on migraine

After removing the IVs with linkage disequilibrium and the SNPs directly associated with migraine, as well as potential confounding factors, a total of 439 SNPs were ultimately included in the forward MR analysis (shown in Table S1 in Supplementary information). All the forward results of genetic correlations are shown in Table S2 (in Supplementary information). Two sample MR analyses identified two brain RSFA (phenotype 1296 and 29) that exhibited significant causal effects on migraine in the forward MR analysis (FDR corrected p value < 0.05) (Fig. 1). For phenotype 1296, 4 SNPs were ultimately included in the analysis, with a total exposure variance of 0.005. Specifically, the intensity of spontaneous neural activity in the precuneus, angular or cingulate and temporal at resting state was positively associated with the risk of migraine, and 1 SD increase in the FC between the DMN and the central executive network (CEN) increased the migraine risk by 39% (IVW: OR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.16–1.67, p = 4.77 × 10−4, FDR corrected p value = 0.040; Weighted median: OR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.03–1.63, p = 0.029; MR-Egger: OR = 1.26, 95% CI 0.81–1.97, p = 0.418; cML: OR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.13–1.71, p = 0.002) (Fig. 1b). We calculated the statistical power for this result using a sample size of 333,711, a case-to-control ratio of 0.066, an exposure variance (R2) of 0.005 for the genetic variants, and an OR of 1.39. This result had 90% statistical power.

For phenotype 29, 6 SNPs were ultimately included in the analysis, with a total exposure variance of 0.007. In the visual network, a decrease of 1 SD in the amplitude trait of the calcarine or lingual gyrus in visual network was associated with a 26% lower risk of migraine (IVW: OR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.63–0.88, p = 5.94 × 10−4, FDR corrected p value = 0.040; Weighted median: OR = 0.77, 95% CI 0.62–0.96, p = 0.019; MR-Egger: OR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.23–2.03, p = 0.535; cML: OR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.62–0.91, p = 0.003) (Fig. 1b). We calculated the statistical power for this result using a sample size of 333,711, a case-to-control ratio of 0.066, an exposure variance (R2) of 0.007 for the genetic variants, and an OR of 0.74. This result had 93.9% statistical power. The effect direction estimated by the other three supplementary MR methods were consistent with the primary IVW analyses.

We observed that the MR-Egger analysis results for both outcomes differed from the other three MR analytical methods. The primary purpose of MR-Egger analysis is to detect and correct for bias introduced by horizontal pleiotropy in the IVs, offering a more robust causal inference compared to traditional MR analysis. However, when the number of IVs is limited, MR-Egger may introduce substantial uncertainty in the estimation of causal effects. For phenotype 1296 and 29, four and six IVs were included, respectively, and neither phenotype exhibited horizontal pleiotropy. Therefore, the conservativeness of MR-Egger analysis might lead to an underestimation of the causal relationship.

The causal effects of migraine on brain resting-state functional activities

In the reverse MR analysis, a total of 3 SNPs linked to migraine were included after quality control to analyze the causal effect of migraine on RSFA (shown in Table S3 in Supplementary information). The result showed that there was no statistically significant causal effect of migraine on brain RSFA with or without FDR corrected. The detailed results of genetic correlations are shown in Table S4 (in Supplementary information).

Discussion

This study investigated the causal relationships between 191 brain rs-fMRI phenotypes and migraine based on bidirectional two-sample MR. Our results showed two brain RSFA had the causal effect on migraine, but no reverse causalities. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the causal relationships between brain RSFA and migraine. Our findings may provide insights into the pathophysiology of the disease and suggest potential new targets for non-invasive neuromodulation techniques on migraine.

The current study showed that connectivity within DMN and CEN was disturbed in migraine patients35,36. DMN is a neural network that exhibits significant activity during rest and is linked to an individual’s perception of stress or pain, emotional processing, and their ability to adapt to a challenging situation (e.g., stress)37,38. The DMN includes the medial prefrontal cortex, temporo-parietal junction, precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex, and malfunctioning of the DMN may explain the abnormalities in stress and pain perception in migraine39. The CEN includes the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the parietal cortex. Disruption of this network may be the cause of subclinical impairment of complex executive functions in migraine patients36. Building on previous research, we found that increased in the FC of the DMN and CEN with an increasing risk of migraine. This may indicate that barriers between pain management and coping with cognitive tasks increase migraine susceptibility. The results emphasize the important role of DMN and CEN in migraine.

FC analyses based on seed points can explore the connectivity between brain regions and depict complex functional coupling patterns40. This provides further insight into the relationship between DMN or CEN spatial pattern abnormalities and migraine. In our forward MR analysis, increased FC between the precuneus, angular gyrus, or cingulate cortex, and temporal regions resulted in an increasing risk of migraine. This may be attributed to the central sensitization (CS) induced by the abnormal Functional in the precuneus36, angular gyrus41, cingulate cortex36, and temporal regions42. CS is one of the pathophysiological mechanisms of migraine, leading to a decrease in threshold to painful stimuli and an increase in response to painful stimuli, making the brain more sensitive to pain perception43. The temporal lobe integrates multiple sensory inputs, including auditory, somatosensory, olfactory, and visual stimuli44. The precuneus is linked to pain sensitivity45, while the cingulate gyrus and angular gyrus are central to pain perception and involved in pain processing46. Enhanced functional connectivity between the precuneus, cingulate gyrus, or angular gyrus and the temporal lobe may indicate abnormalities in the brain’s perception and processing of pain, promoting CS and exacerbating of migraine symptoms10. This is consistent with previous reports that migraine patients exhibit lower discomfort thresholds to somatosensory, visual, auditory, and olfactory stimuli47–49.

In additional, the anterior cingulate cortex is involved in the emotional response to pain-related unpleasant experiences50. Previous studies have shown that migraine patients exhibit increased spontaneous activity in the cingulate gyrus which associated with an increased emotional response51,52. Therefore, the FC between the cingulate gyrus and the temporal lobe cortex may contribute to an increased susceptibility to pain-induced negative emotions, exacerbating headaches, leading to chronic conditions and increasing risk of comorbid mental health disorders53.

On the other hand, our study found that a decreased amplitude trait of the calcarine or lingual gyrus within visual network was associated with a lower risk of migraine. Cortical spreading depression (CSD) is another key pathological hypothesis in migraine and is thought to underlie visual aura in migraine54,55. CSD is the slow depolarization of neurons and glial cells, followed by a sustained suppression of spontaneous neuronal activity54. Previous studies have shown that cortical neurons in the visual cortex are abnormally activated during visual aura episodes, leading to CSD, and triggering migraine55. The calcarine and lingual gyrus are the main visual network representative areas in the occipital lobe. Several studies have confirmed the abnormal activated with the visual network in migraine patients. For example, Tedeschi et al.12 found that abnormal FC of resting-state visual network connectivity centered on the lingual gyrus in MwA patients. Datta et al.56 found visual discomfort increase during migraine attacks with or without aura, but only people with migraine with aura experience increased activation of the visual cortex when exposed to visual stimuli. Therefore, reduced activity in the calcarine and lingual gyrus within the visual network potentially reduces migraine susceptibility by decreasing the occurrence of CSD. These findings may provide valuable targets for the treatment and evaluation of migraine patients with aura, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation.

In this study, we conducted an inverse MR analysis to investigate the causal effect of migraine on RSFA. Our findings revealed no significant reverse causal relationship between migraine and these neural networks. Migraine is a complex neurological disorder characterized by significant heterogeneity, including different subtypes such as migraine with and without aura, each having distinct genetic underpinnings57. Using untyped migraine samples may obscure or confound the associations between specific SNPs and particular migraine subtypes, as well as their effects on RSFA. Additionally, migraine may have temporally dynamic effects on brain function. An fMRI study has revealed that migraine patients exhibit different activation patterns in the brain at various time stages58. Using dynamic brain network analysis (graph theory) methods, it has also been found that the brain networks of migraine patients show dynamic changes in connectivity at different time points, and these changes are related to the duration of the migraine59. However, MR analyses typically assume a stable relationship between the exposure and outcome. The complex temporal dynamics involved in migraine could potentially lead to non-significant results. Therefore, more refined analytical methods and larger-scale studies are needed in the future to further explore the causality between migraine and brain function.

The following limitations of this study need to be considered. In the initial stage of the study, all samples of the selected GWAS were of European ancestry. Although it reduced the bias induced by population stratification to some extent, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Secondly, the limited number of significant SNP IVs in forward Mendelian randomization analysis may lead to insufficient study power due to the restricted proportion of explained total variance. To overcome the issue of insufficient power for weak associations, we hope that future studies will conduct larger sample size GWAS research on rs-fMRI phenotypes. Thirdly, this study did not explore the causal relationship between brain RSFA and different migraine subtypes (e.g., MwA, MwoA, episodic, or chronic migraines). Future research should further analyze the causal relationship between brain RSFA and different migraine subtypes to gain a deeper understanding of the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these subtypes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using bidirectional two-sample MR analyses, we identified the two brain RSFA having a causal relationship on migraine: the FC between the precuneus, cingulate gyrus, or angular gyrus and the temporal lobe in the DMN and CEN, and the intensity of neural activity of the calcarine or lingual gyrus in the visual network. The findings enhance our comprehension of the neuro-pathophysiology that underlying the occurrence of migraine, highlighting potential regions for more accurate and effective interventions.
