Molecular characterization of a naturally occurring isolate of Begomovirus solanumkarnatakaense causing breakdown of Ty-3-mediated resistance in tomato plants with increased disease severity under elevated temperature
Authors: Firoz Mondal, Shipra Saxena, Mehulee Sarkar, Anik Majumdar, Prantik Mazumder, Zakir Hussain, Bikash Mandal, Kajal Kumar Biswas, Anirban Roy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTomato leaf curl disease, caused by rapidly evolving begomoviruses, poses a major threat to tomato production worldwide. Although resistance genes like Ty-1/Ty-3 have effectively managed key viruses such as tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV), emerging new viral variants continuously challenge this control.ResultsIn this study, we identified and characterized a resistance-breaking isolate of tomato leaf curl Karnataka virus (ToLCKV, MZ578458, species: Begomovirussolanumkarnatakaense) from symptomatic tomato genotypes carrying the Ty-gene(s). Agro-infectious construct of this new isolate of ToLCKV was found to be capable of overcoming homozygous Ty-3 mediated resistance in tomato cultivars, which otherwise showed resistance when inoculated with agro-infectious constructs of another predominant begomovirus, tomato leaf curl New Delhi virus (ToLCNDV). Compared with other ToLCKV isolates, key genetic variations were detected in ORF C1, the 5′ region of C2, and the intergenic region, while the viral suppressors of RNA silencing and other pathogenicity-associated genes showed evidence of adaptive evolution. Host range assays confirmed systemic infectivity in Solanaceae hosts and asymptomatic replication of the virus in multiple Cucurbitaceae hosts. Elevated temperature (35 °C) significantly enhanced viral accumulation and hastened symptom development in both susceptible (cv. Pusa Ruby) and Ty-3-based resistant cultivars (cv. Kashi Adarsh, cv. Kashi Aman), compared to ambient conditions (25 °C). Notably, no direct correlation between symptom progression and viral titres was found, suggesting that resistance breakdown under heat stress involves complex host–virus dynamics beyond viral load alone.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first report documenting the breakdown of Ty-3-mediated resistance by a begomovirus, together with evidence that elevated temperature exacerbates this breakdown under controlled environmental conditions. The findings of our study highlight the synergistic threat of viral evolution and climate change and emphasize the need for future research into the molecular basis of temperature-mediated resistance failure to guide the development of climate-resilient tomato cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08978-w.

Background

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) is the second most important vegetable crop with 238.17 million tons of global production in 2023 (FAOSTAT, https://www.fao.org/faostat/en/#data/QCL). Its nutritional, economic, and industrial significance stems from its richness in essential vitamins (A and C), antioxidants such as lycopene, and various minerals, making it a key component of a routine healthy diet. Cultivated across tropical, subtropical, and temperate climates, the crop is most extensively grown in China, India, Turkey, and the United States.

Despite its global importance, tomato productivity is frequently threatened by tomato leaf curl disease (ToLCD). ToLCD a severe viral disease that manifests as leaf curling, vein clearing, yellow mottling, growth retardation, and, in severe cases, total crop failure [1–3]. This disease is caused by begomoviruses (family Geminiviridae), a group of whitefly-transmitted DNA viruses, with over 100 tomato-infecting species reported worldwide (ICTV, https://ictv.global/report/chapter/geminiviridae/geminiviridae/begomovirus).

The genetic material of a begomovirus is circular single-stranded DNA. It can exist as either monopartrite (DNA-A, ~ 2.7 kb) or bipartite (DNA-A and DNA-B, each about 2.7 kb), depending on the number of genomic segments. In the case of a bipartite genome, each segment is encapsidated separately. DNA-A codes for six to nine primary open reading frames (ORFs). ORF AV1 in bipartite begomoviruses and the homologous ORF, V1 of monopartite begomoviruses, encodes the coat protein (CP), required for the virus assembly [4, 5]. The pre-coat protein of ORF AV2/V2 functions as a viral suppressor of RNA silencing (VSR) [6–8]. Moreover, the pre-coat protein is involved in both replication and movement of the virus [9, 10]. The replication initiator protein (Rep) encoded by ORF AC1/C1, along with replication enhancer protein (REn) encoded by ORF AC3/C3, are essential for the rolling-circle replication of the viral genomic DNA [10]. ORF AC2 encodes the transcription activator protein (TrAP) that modulates the expression of AV1/V1 and AV2/V2, and also acts as a VSR [8, 11]. The protein encoded by ORF AC4/C4 has a multifunctional role, mainly focused as a pathogenicity determinant and silencing suppressor [9, 12]. Although no documented function has been reported for the protein encoded by ORF AC6/C6 [13], the AC5/C5 and AC7 /C7-encoded proteins have been identified as virulence factors involved in RNAi suppression [14–17]. In bipartite begomoviruses, the DNA-B component features two ORFs. ORF BV1 encodes the nuclear shuttle protein (NSP), and ORF BC1 encodes the movement protein (MP). The movement protein works in conjunction with the NSP to facilitate the short-distance transfer of viral nucleic acids from cell to cell [18, 19].

Among tomato-infecting begomoviruses, TYLCVs are the most widespread globally. However, in the Indian context, TYLCV is notably absent. Instead, ToLCNDV dominates, along with at least 13 other begomovirus species, causing ToLCD in various regions [20, 21]. Besides tomato, ToLCNDV also infects several other hosts like brinjal, chilli, cucurbits, and pulses [22–25]. The management of these viruses is complicated due to their broad host range, rapid mutation rates, and efficient vector transmission. Consequently, the deployment of genetically resistant cultivars has been the most sustainable and eco-friendly strategy to manage ToLCD. To date, six resistance loci (Ty-1 to Ty-6) have been identified in different wild Solanum sp. against the leaf curl causing begomoviruses of tomato [26–31]. Additionally, the Sw5a gene, encoding a CC-NBS-LRR protein, has been implicated in resistance [32]. Notably, Ty-1 and Ty-3, later shown to be allelic, encode an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) with a conserved DFDGD motif characteristic of the RDRγ class [33]. Among these, the Ty-1 allele is widely incorporated into global tomato breeding programs due to its broad-spectrum resistance. In India, however, the allelic variant Ty-3 has proven especially effective, either individually or in combination with other Ty genes, for imparting resistance against major tomato-infecting begomoviruses occurring in India [34–38]. Ty-3 is also found effective against tomato mottle virus (ToMoV), indicating its better potential in breeding programs [26].

However, emerging evidence indicates that Ty-1-mediated resistance may be compromised. Multiple factors, including the emergence of recombinant TYLCV strains [21, 39–42], synergistic interactions with betasatellites or other viruses like tomato chlorosis virus [43, 44], and environmental stresses such as elevated temperatures [45] have been found responsible for this resistance breakdown.

Prompted by field observations of ToLCD symptoms in Ty-3-containing lines [46], this study was undertaken to explore two key hypotheses: (i) the possible emergence of a novel or previously minor begomovirus variant overcoming Ty-3-mediated resistance, and (ii) the influence of temperature on symptom expression and virus accumulation.

In this study, we report the molecular characterization and infectivity analysis of an isolate of Begomovirus solanumkarnatakaense (ToLCKV), consistently associated with Ty-3-containing tomato lines. Furthermore, we demonstrate that elevated temperatures significantly compromise Ty-3-mediated resistance, leading to enhanced susceptibility and symptom development. This study provides the first evidence of ToLCKV-mediated resistance breakdown in Ty-3 cultivars and underscores the critical need to reassess the durability of resistance genes under changing climatic and epidemiological conditions.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

For the initial screening of begomoviruses, seventeen different tomato genotypes with Ty-genes and a susceptible cultivar, Pusa Ruby, were used. The Ty-gene(s) containing genotypes are ToLCD Tol-1 (Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 2 (Ty-2), ToLCD Tol − 3 (Ty-1), ToLCD Tol − 4 (Ty-2 + Ty-3a), ToLCD Tol − 5 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 6 (Ty-5 + Ty-6), ToLCD Tol − 7 (Ty-3a), ToLCD Tol − 8 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 9 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 10 (ty-5), ToLCD Tol − 11 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 12 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 13 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 14 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 15 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 16 (Ty-2 + Ty-3), ToLCD Tol − 17 (Ty-2 + Ty-3) [47]. The details of these genotypes are mentioned in our previous study [47]. In short, they are the genetic stocks of tomato, used as sources of Ty-genes in resistance breeding programs against begomoviruses. They were grown in the experimental plot at ICAR- Indian Agricultural Research Institute (ICAR-IARI), New Delhi, India. For the infectivity and host range study of the newly identified begomovirus isolate, along with the tomato cv. Pusa Ruby, three other solanaceous hosts, viz., Nicotiana benthamiana, Nicotiana glutinosa, Nicotiana tabacum, and four cucurbits, viz., cucumber cv. Swarna Sheetal, muskmelon cv. F1 Hybrid Bond, sponge gourd cv. Dhanshree, and pumpkin cv. Desi Round, were used. Five plants of each host genotype were used for agroinoculation. Breakdown of Ty-3-conferred resistance was confirmed in two tomato cultivars, Kashi Adarsh and Kashi Aman. These cultivars were used for the resistance-breakdown study because, unlike the earlier used ToLCD Tol-1, cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman are commercially released varieties and cultivated widely in India. The seeds of these cultivars were sourced from ICAR-Indian Institute of Vegetable Research, Varanasi, India (seed lot no. 2024–78055 and 2024–15387, respectively). The experiment was conducted in two sequential phases. In the initial experimental phase, agroinoculation was performed using the agro-infectious construct of the new begomovirus isolate under study on one resistant cultivar (cv. Kashi Adarsh) and one susceptible cultivar (cv. Pusa Ruby), with 10 plants per cultivar. To verify these findings, an independent follow-up experiment was conducted that included two resistant cultivars (cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman), with five plants per cultivar, and compared the resistance-breaking nature of the new isolate under study with a non-resistance-breaking begomovirus (ToLCNDV). For this study, plants were grown at two temperatures, i.e., 25 °C and 35 °C, in an environment-controlled growth chamber. For all other studies, only a 25 °C temperature was maintained along with 70% RH and 16 h light (~ 11000 lx)/8 h dark cycles.

Sample collection, Total Nucleic Acid (TNA) isolation, and detection of begomovirus using PCR

Five individual tomato plants were used to collect the leaf samples for each genotype. The samples were collected from the field during the month of December 2020. Total nucleic acids (TNA) were isolated following the standard CTAB method [48]. The CTAB DNA isolation buffer was prepared using 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 2% CTAB, 1.4 M NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, and 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol in autoclaved distilled water. A Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to check the quality and quantity of isolated TNAs. A pooled TNA sample was prepared for each genotype by mixing equal quantities from the five TNAs of that genotype. The representative 18 pooled TNAs were used as templates in PCR-based detection of major tomato-infecting begomoviruses in India including ToLCKV. A begomovirus universal primer was used for initial detection, followed by the species-specific primers (Additional file: Table S1). The 10 µl of final PCR reaction mixture had ~ 300 ng of template TNA, 10 mM dNTPs mix, 10 µM of each primer, 0.125 U DreamTaq DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and 1X DreamTaq DNA polymerase buffer. For the ToLCKV-specific amplification in PCR, 3 min of initial denaturation at 95 °C was followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 57 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The PCR amplified products were separated using 1% agarose gel visualized using the Vilber Bio-print Gel Imaging System (Vilber, France). Two representative PCR amplified products were used for sequencing using Sanger's di-deoxy method to confirm the identity of the begomovirus.

Cloning of the viral genome

The TNA from genotype ToLCD Tol-1 was used for the amplification of the complete genome (DNA-A) of ToLCKV following the rolling circle amplification (RCA) method. for RCA. The RCA reaction mixture contained ~300 ng of TNA, 25 µM exo-resistant random primers, 1U phi-29 DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), 1 mM dNTPs, 1U inorganic pyrophosphatase, and 1X phi-29 DNA polymerase buffer. Both the RCA product and pUC18 cloning vector were digested using BamHI and ligated using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and following the manufacturer’s protocol. 10 µl ligation mixture was used to transform 50 µl chemically competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells following heat-shock (90s at 42 °C) method [49]. Colony PCR was done using pUC18-specific M13 forward and reverse primers to confirm the transformed colonies. Recombinant plasmids were also confirmed through BamHI+BglI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) restriction endonuclease digestion.

Sequence analysis

Three confirmed recombinant plasmids were Sanger sequenced (Central Instrumentation Facilities, University of Delhi South Campus, New Delhi). ORFfinder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/) was used to detect the putative ORFs in the nucleotide sequence. The genome map of the virus was constructed in SnapGene software (www.snapgene.com). An NCBI-BLASTn search was done to retrieve the genome sequences of the closely related begomoviruses. Multiple sequence alignment with other closely related begomovirus sequences was performed using MUSCLE implemented in the sequence demarcation tool (SDT) version 1.3 [50]. For phylogenetic analysis, the Maximum-Likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates was used in MEGA-X version 10.2.3 [51]. Recombination detection program (RDP) 4.101 was used with RDP, GENECONV, Bootscan, MaxChi, Chimera, SiScan, and 3Seq tools, to check for evidence of genetic recombination in the viral genomic sequence [52]. The sequences retrieved from NCBI-BLASTn search were used in recombination detection. SimPlot 3.5.1 [53] was employed to measure the regional nucleotide similarity map of the viral genome sequence, taking the current isolate sequence as the query. The parameters used in SimPlot were- Window: 100 bp, Step: 10 bp, GapStrip: On, Kimura (2-parameter), T/t: 2.0. The overall nucleotide diversity and Tajima’s D analysis were performed in DnaSP6 [54]. Codon-based Z-test for selection was performed using default parameters in MEGA-X [51]. The FUBAR tool in the Datamonkey server (https://www.datamonkey.org/fubar) was used to detect per-site selection pressure in the individual gene sequences of the virus. The presence of nuclear localization signals, subcellular localization, and different functional motifs in the amino acid sequences of different begomoviral proteins was predicted using cNLS mapper (https://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp/cgi-bin/NLS_Mapper_form.cgi), Virus-mPloc (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/virus-multi/), and Motif Scan (https://myhits.sib.swiss/cgi-bin/motif_scan) tools, respectively. For the tertiary structures of the proteins, firstly, the AlphaFold 2 tool in the Galaxy server (https://usegalaxy.org/) was used to predict the models, and later, the models were refined using the Galaxy Refine web server (https://galaxy.seoklab.org/refine). The predicted models were further validated by PROCHECK and ERRAT, implemented in the SAVES server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). For the identification of intrinsically disordered amino acid residues and to predict the ligand binding sites in the virus proteins PrankWeb server (https://prankweb.cz/) was used, respectively.

Preparation of the infectious construct of the virus

For the infectivity analysis of the newly characterized ToLCKV isolate, a partial tandem repeat (PTR) construct of the begomovirus was developed (Additional file: Fig. S1). In the first step, a partial fragment (586 bp) of viral genome containing the IR was released from the cloned viral genome using BamHI+SacI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) digestion and ligated with the same enzyme digested pCAMBIA2300 binary vector using T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), as described earlier [21]. The ligation product was used to transform E. coli DH5α cells, and the recombinant pCambia2300 (0.2-mer partial construct) was confirmed by restriction endonuclease digestion of the isolated plasmid using the same enzymes used for cloning. In the second step, the complete genome of the virus (~2.7 kb) was cloned at the BamHI site of the 0.2-mer partial construct in a tandem orientation, to generate a 1.2-mer PTR construct of the virus (Additional file: Fig. S2A). SacI digestion confirmed the tandem orientation of the construct.

Agroinoculation of begomoviruses

For testing the infectivity of the ToLCKV PTR construct, chemically competent Agrobacterium tumefaciens (strain GV3101) cells were transformed following the freeze-thaw method [55]. The transformed A. tumefaciens cells were used to inoculate the test plants. Leaf infiltration of the bacterial culture was followed for all the test host plants except tomato. In the case of cucurbits, cotyledonary leaves were chosen for infiltration. To prepare the bacterial culture, a single transformed colony was picked and grown overnight in liquid Luria Bertani broth supplemented with antibiotics (Rifampicin @50 µg/ml, Kanamycin @50 µg/ml), in a shaker-incubator (28 °C, 200 rpm). Next day, the bacterial cells were collected via centrifugation (4100 rpm) for 3 min at room temperature and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl2, and 150 µM Acetosyringone) till OD600 reaches 0.6. This bacterial suspension (OD600- 0.6) was used for infiltration after 2.5 h of incubation at 28 °C with gentle shaking. Equal amount of bacterial suspension was used for infiltrating three leaves of each plant.

For tomato plants, the pin-prick method of agroinoculation was followed. For that, 5–6 sublethal injuries were created below the apical meristem of the seedlings using a sterile needle and bacterial slurry, prepared in Gamborg’s B5 media supplemented with 150 µM Acetosyringone, was pasted on the injuries using sterile pipette tips.

15–20 days old seedlings were used for agroinoculation studies. The infectious constructs for ToLCNDV DNA-A (GenBank ID: PQ468836) and DNA-B (GenBank ID: PQ468837) were included as non-resistance-breaking control (positive control for confirming Ty-3 effectiveness) [56]. Plasmid maps of ToLCNDV PTR-constructs are shown in additional file: Fig. S2B and S2C. For all the agroinoculation studies, Agrobacterium cells transformed with the empty backbone of pCAMBIA2300 vector were used as a negative control (mock).

Ty-3-RDR gene-specific marker analysis of tomato genotypes

The presence of the Ty-3-RDR gene in the tomato genotypes was confirmed through a derived cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence (dCAPS) marker [45] (Additional file: Table S1) analysis. NcoI restriction endonuclease digestion of the PCR amplified DNA is expected to yield a Ty-3 allele-specific 113 bp amplicon. Ty-1, ty-1, and ty-3 yield a 96 bp amplicon. The restriction-digested PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 3% MetaPhor agarose (Lonza Bioscience, Switzerland).

Assessment of disease severity and virus quantification using quantitative real-time PCR

To estimate the leaf curl disease severity in tomato plants, each plant was assigned a severity score (SS, 0-100) based on its combined score for the three major symptoms, leaf curl, leaf smalling, and stunting (Additional file: Table S3). The average SS of a genotype was then converted to a severity grade (SG) of 0 to 5. Here, SG0: SS = 0, SG1: 0 < SS ≤ 20, SG2: 20 < SS ≤ 40, SG3: 40 < SS ≤ 60, SG4: 60 < SS ≤ 80, SG5: 80 < SS ≤ 100. The disease severity data were recorded at 7-, 15-, 30-, and 60-days post inoculation (dpi).

For the absolute quantification of viral DNAs, firstly, standard curves were generated by plotting known quantities of a plasmid containing the target amplicon against their respective Ct-values. Slope, efficiency and R² values of the standard curves were − 3.153, 107.57%, 0.981 for ToLCKV and − 3.00, 115.44%, 0.986 for ToLCNDV, respectively. The lower limits of quantification (LLOQ) were 38018 and 45621 copies per reaction for ToLCKV and ToLCNDV, respectively. Later, the standard curve was used to deduce the concentration of viral DNA in the total DNA isolated from inoculated plants. Total DNAs were isolated at 7, 30, and 60 dpi, and qPCR was performed using 1X SYBR green qPCR MasterMix (G-Biosciences, USA) and virus-specific primers (0.5 µM each, Additional file: Table S1) in a CFX Opus Real-Time PCR (BioRad, USA). 100 ng of total DNA was used as the template for each 10 µl qPCR reaction. The temperature profile for the qPCR was as follows: initial denaturation of DNA at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, primer annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Results

ToLCKV was predominantly associated with the leaf curl disease in tomato genotypes with different Ty-genes

During a routine field survey conducted at the ICAR–Indian Agricultural Research Institute (ICAR-IARI), New Delhi, in December 2020, characteristic symptoms of ToLCD, including leaf curling, chlorosis, and growth stunting, were observed in multiple tomato genotypes known to carry one or more Ty resistance alleles (Fig. 1A). PCR-based diagnostics using ToLCKV-specific primers (Additional file: Table S1) successfully amplified a ~ 474 bp fragment in 12 out of 18 genotypes tested, indicating a widespread association of ToLCKV among the affected lines (Fig. 1B). Subsequent Sanger sequencing of a representative PCR amplicon (OP120776) confirmed the presence of ToLCKV, showing 100% nucleotide identity with a previously characterized isolate (MH255790). Interestingly, while mixed infections involving multiple begomoviruses along with ToLCKV were common across most genotypes carrying Ty-3, one genotype, ToLCD Tol-1, carrying the Ty-3 resistance gene, showed exclusive infection by ToLCKV. Notably, no betasatellite or DNA-B components were detected in this genotype.

Genomic analysis of ToLCKV revealed high conservation across hosts, absence of recombination, and divergence in the Rep–TrAP genomic region

To confirm the identity and genomic architecture of the begomovirus isolate associated with symptomatic Ty-3-carrying tomato plants, the complete viral genome was amplified using rolling circle amplification (RCA). Digestion of the RCA product with BamHI produced a ~2.7 kb fragment, consistent with the expected size of a begomovirus genome (Fig. 1C). Cloning and Sanger sequencing of the viral DNA yielded a full-length genome of 2760 nucleotides (GenBank accession no. MZ578458).

Annotation of the genome identified six canonical open reading frames (ORFs) typical of monopartite begomoviruses: V1 (304–1074 nt; 771 bp), V2 (144–500 nt; 357 bp), and complementary sense ORFs C1 (1523–2608 nt; 1086 bp), C2 (1216–1620 nt; 405 bp), C3 (1071–1475 nt; 405 bp), and C4 (2158–2451 nt; 294 bp) (Fig. 2A). The intergenic region (IR), spanning 295 bp, featured the characteristic geminiviral stem-loop structure with the conserved nonanucleotide motif (TAATATT↓AC), Rep-binding iterons (GGTGT, GGAGT), and cis-regulatory elements such as the TATA-box and CAAT-box (Fig. 2B).

Comparative sequence analysis revealed that MZ578458 shares the highest nucleotide identity (99.6%) with a ToLCKV isolate infecting pigeon pea (GenBank ID: KX246859), followed closely by isolates from radish (99.57%, MH255790) and parthenium (99.42%, MN839537) (Fig. 2C, Additional file: Table S4). ORF-specific nucleotide and amino acid comparisons further confirmed > 99% identity for all coding regions, supporting the classification of this isolate as ToLCKV (Additional file: Table S4).

Phylogenetic analysis using complete genome sequences of major tomato-infecting begomoviruses in India placed MZ578458 within a well-supported clade of diverse ToLCKV isolates infecting hosts from different botanical families, including Solanaceae, Leguminosae, Cruciferae, Asteraceae, Amaranthaceae, Rosaceae, and Cucurbitaceae (Fig. 2D). To explore genomic variability across ToLCKV isolates, similarity profiling of all 52 full-length ToLCKV genomes available in the NCBI database was performed using SimPlot, with MZ578458 as the query (Additional file: Table S5). The analysis revealed a prominent region of nucleotide divergence encompassing the 3′ terminus of ORF C1 and the 5′ terminus of ORF C2, followed by the intergenic region (Fig. 2E). This pattern of divergence was also consistent in MZ578458 when compared with other tomato-infecting isolates, analyzed under even more stringent parameters (window size: 10 bp, step: 1 bp) (Fig. 2F). However, recombination analysis did not identify any recombination events within the MZ578458 genome.

ToLCKV genes coding for the RNAi suppressors were under weaker purifying selection pressure than the CP, Rep, and REn

To study the evolution of the current isolate of ToLCKV (MZ578458), the nucleotide sequences of the complete genome of the virus and individual ORFs of all 52 ToLCKV isolates were subjected to analysis for overall nucleotide diversity (π), Tajima’s D, codon-based Z-test for selection, and codon-specific selection pressure. The IR was found to have the highest per-site nucleotide diversity (0.089), followed by the ORF V1 (0.084). Complete genome sequences (π = 0.066) and ORF C1 (π = 0.067) showed a moderate diversity, compared to V2 (π = 0.046), C2 (π = 0.044), C3 (π = 0.045), and C4 (π = 0.042) (Table 1). Though the complete genome as well as all the individual ORFs had negative values of Tajima’s D, suggesting negative or purifying selection, only the value for C3 (D= -2.1) was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 1). However, in the two-tailed codon-based Z-test for selection (H0: dN = dS), which shows the difference between the rate of nonsynonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) amino acid substitution per site, all the other genes except C4 showed statistically significant (p < 0.05) evidence of negative or purifying selection. The coat-protein gene (V1) was found to be under the strongest negative selection (Z=-13.49), followed by Rep (C1, Z=-8.87) and REn (C3, Z=-4.06). The ubiquitous begomovirus RNAi-suppressor protein coding genes V2, C2, and C4 had relatively higher Z-stat of -3.48, -3.22, and − 1.11, respectively (Table 1).

When the individual ORF sequences were subjected to analysis for identifying the codons under pervasive positive/negative selection, again, the RNAi-suppressor protein coding genes had the greatest number of codons under positive or diversifying selection, 6.7% of V2, 6.6% of C2, and 4.1% of C4. On the contrary, 54% and 27.6% codons of the CP gene (V1) and Rep (C1), respectively, were under pervasive purifying/negative selection. It was 15% for V2 and 14% for both C2 and C3. The multifunctional C4 protein had only 6.1% codons under negative selection (Table 1).

Differential disorder and structural organization reflect functional divergence in TOLCKV proteins

To study the structural features of the MZ578458 genome-encoded proteins and to get an idea about their involvement in the biological functions of the proteins, the amino acid sequences of individual genes were used to identify different functional motifs, subcellular localization signals, tertiary structures, potential active sites in protein structure, and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). The nuclear localization signals (NLS) were observed in the N-terminal region of CP (V1), Rep (C1), and TrAP (C2) with the cNLS Mapper prediction scores of 9.2, 6.4, and 3.7, respectively. The pre-coat protein (V2), REn (C3), and C4 protein were predicted to localize in the cytoplasmic membrane. Additionally, both V2 and C3 were expected to localize in the host cytoplasm. The endoplasmic reticulum was another potential site of localization for the C3 protein. The most abundant functional motifs found in all six proteins were different kinase motifs. Other important motifs included the ATP/GTP-binding motif of C1, N-glycosylation motifs of V1 and C1, WCCH motif of V2, etc. (Additional file: Table S6).

In the prediction of 3D structures for individual proteins, only V1, C1, and C3 displayed comparatively well-organized and stable conformations (Additional file: Fig. S3A). These proteins comprised 55.3%, 54.6%, and 73.9% of their amino acid residues in α-helices and β-sheets, respectively. In contrast, V2, C2, and C4 exhibited significantly lower percentages, with V2 showing 49.2%, C2 35.1%, and C4 34% of their residues forming α-helices and β-sheets. In the search for intrinsically disordered regions within these proteins, it was found that nearly the entire C4 protein, consisting of 78.4% of its amino acids, and over half of the C2 protein’s amino acids (55.2%), were disordered. For V2, 26.3% of its residues at the C-terminus are disordered. In contrast, V1, C1, and C3, despite some overlap with V2, C4, and C2, exhibited only a small percentage of disordered residues: 2% for V1, 5.8% for C1, and 0% for C3 (Table 1). Moreover, the potential active sites on the surface of these proteins for protein-protein or protein-ligand interaction were also predicted (Additional file: Fig. S3B). The amino acid residues involved in the top three sites with the highest prediction scores are listed in Table 1.

PTR construct of the new ToLCKV isolate can infect tomato, tobacco, and cucurbits

To confirm the infectivity of the developed PTR construct of ToLCKV, the Agrobacterium tumefaciens cells transformed with the construct were used to inoculate the plants of its natural host tomato (cv. Pusa Ruby) and three other experimental hosts of the same family: Nicotiana benthamiana, N. glutinosa, and N. tabacum. The typical leaf curl symptoms started appearing in the newly emerged leaves of these inoculated plants at 6–7 dpi, 6 dpi, 11 dpi, and 14 dpi, respectively, confirming systemic infection by the virus. Over time, the symptoms became severe, and the most striking symptom exhibited by all the inoculated plants except N. tabacum was severe stunting of the entire plant (Fig. 3A and D). Other major symptoms observed in the inoculated tomato plants are vein thickening, leaf curl, leaf smalling, puckering, shortening of internodes and petioles, etc. N. benthamiana, N. glutinosa plants showed symptoms like vein thickening, downward curling, and marginal rolling of leaves. (Figure 3B and C). N. tabacum plants had vein thickening and moderate downward bending of leaf lamina without significant stunting of the plants (Fig. 3D). PCR analysis using the ToLCKV-specific primers (Additional file: Table S1) and the isolated DNA from the representative inoculated plants from each host confirmed the association of the virus with the symptomatic plants (Fig. 3J).

dCAPS marker analysis confirmed the presence of the Ty-3 allele in a homozygous state in the resistant cultivars

A derived cleaved amplified polymorphic sequence (dCAPS) marker assay was employed to target the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RDR) gene within the Ty-3 locus (Additional file: Table S1) to confirm the presence of the Ty-3 allele in the tomato cultivars. This assay yielded an amplicon of approximately 113 bp in cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman, which aligns with the expected fragment size for homozygous Ty-3/Ty-3 genotypes (Fig. 4). In contrast, the susceptible cultivar Pusa Ruby produced a ~ 96 bp fragment, indicating a homozygous ty-3/ty-3 genotype. These findings confirm the presence of the homozygous Ty-3-resistant allele in both cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman, while it is absent in cv. Pusa Ruby.

Susceptible cultivar Pusa Ruby exhibited more severe symptoms with ToLCKV compared to ToLCNDV

In the susceptible cultivar Pusa Ruby, the pattern of symptom development, following ToLCKV infection, was consistent in both the experimental phases (Additional file: Table S2 and Table 2). In both phases, ToLCKV-inoculated plants developed visible leaf curl symptoms at 6–8 dpi [Fig. 5A(i), B(i)]. By 15 dpi, ToLCKV infection resulted in severe downward curling, puckering, pronounced leaf smalling, and stunting at both 25 °C and 35 °C [Fig. 5C(i), D(i)], with chlorosis being more prominent at 35 °C. These symptoms persisted through 60 dpi, accompanied by sustained growth reduction and strong symptom expression [Fig. 5H(i)].

In the second experimental phase, plants inoculated with ToLCNDV exhibited comparatively milder symptoms, including upward curling, mild smalling, and marginal yellowing, which were more evident at 25 °C than at 35 °C [Fig. 5C(ii), D(ii)]. Symptom severity declined over time at 35 °C, with partial recovery of plant vigor by 60 dpi [Fig. 5H(ii)]. Thus, while ToLCNDV produced a moderate and temperature-modulated response, ToLCKV consistently induced more severe and persistent symptoms in the susceptible cultivar Pusa Ruby.

Cultivar Kashi Adarsh showed temperature-dependent susceptibility to ToLCKV and strong immunity to ToLCNDV

Like cv. Pusa Ruby, in cv. Kashi Adarsh, a Ty-3-resistant cultivar, the response to ToLCKV in the second experimental phase was similar to the trend obtained in the initial experiment (Additional file: Table S2 and Table 2). Following ToLCKV inoculation at 25 °C, plants remained symptomless until 27–32 dpi, only mild leaf smalling and moderate stunting appeared [Fig. 5E(iii)]. Under elevated temperature (35 °C), early upward curling appeared at 13–16 dpi [Fig. 5D(iii)], progressing to puckering, downward curling, shortened petioles, and pronounced stunting by 30 dpi [Fig. 5F(iii)], with disease progression continuing up to 60 dpi [Fig. 5H(iii)]. This reflects a clear temperature-dependent manifestation of susceptibility to ToLCKV.

In the second experimental phase, ToLCNDV-inoculated cv. Kashi Adarsh plants remained asymptomatic at both temperatures, with only transient puckering in two plants at 35 °C that resolved by 30 dpi [Fig. 5A(iv)–H(iv)], indicating effective resistance to ToLCNDV.

Cultivar Kashi Aman displayed compromised resistance to ToLCKV and effective tolerance to ToLCNDV

Cultivar Kashi Aman, also harboring the Ty-3 resistance allele, exhibited a distinct response to ToLCKV. Visible symptoms such as leaf puckering, smalling, and reduced petiole length appeared by 10–12 dpi at both 25 °C and 35 °C [Fig. 5C(v), D(v)]. By 30 dpi, pronounced stunting was evident under both temperature regimes, with more severe symptoms at 35 °C [Fig. 5E(v), F(v)]. At 60 dpi, disease progression was exacerbated at 35 °C [Fig. 5H(v)], whereas plants maintained at 25 °C showed signs of recovery, with newly emerging leaves free from visible symptoms [Fig. 5G(v)]. Upon ToLCNDV inoculation, cv. Kashi Aman plants remained asymptomatic throughout the 60-day observation period at both temperatures. Only minor transient stunting was observed during early stages at 25 °C, which resolved in later phases, suggesting a robust resistance response similar to that observed in cv. Kashi Adarsh.

Temperature-dependent viral accumulation dynamics of ToLCKV and ToLCNDV in tomato cultivars with varying resistance levels

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was employed to determine the absolute quantity of ToLCKV and DNA-A of ToLCNDV in inoculated susceptible and Ty-3-containing genotypes of tomato plants across two temperature conditions (25 °C and 35 °C).

Factorial ANOVA revealed significant effects of cultivar (p < 0.0001), temperature (p < 0.001), and time (p < 0.001) on viral accumulation, whereas the virus species effect alone was not significant (p > 0.05). Several higher-order interactions were significant, including virus × cultivar (p < 0.0001), virus × temperature (p ≈ 0.01), temperature × time (p < 0.01), and the virus × temperature × time interaction (p < 0.001). A significant four-way interaction (virus × cultivar × temperature × time; p < 0.01) indicated that virus accumulation patterns were context-dependent across treatment combinations.

ToLCKV accumulation patterns

Across all three cultivars inoculated with ToLCKV, two consistent trends in viral accumulation were observed. First, at both temperatures, the susceptible cultivar Pusa Ruby exhibited significantly higher (p < 0.05) ToLCKV DNA concentration compared to the Ty-3-containing genotypes, cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman. Second, regardless of disease severity, viral DNA accumulation increased from 7 dpi to 30 dpi, followed by a decline at 60 dpi. Overall, plants maintained at 35 °C accumulated more ToLCKV DNA than those at 25 °C (Fig. 6A).

In cv. Pusa Ruby, disease severity increased from severity grade 1 (SG1) at 7 dpi to SG5 at 30 dpi at 25 °C, with a corresponding 11.8-fold rise in viral DNA accumulation. However, a 4.1-fold decrease in viral load was observed at 60 dpi, despite no reduction in severity. A similar trend was noted at 35 °C, where an 11.5-fold increase in viral DNA from 7 to 30 dpi was accompanied by severity progression from SG1 to SG5. At 60 dpi, viral load decreased 1.4-fold, yet severity remained unchanged. Across all time points, ToLCKV DNA accumulation was consistently at least 2.25-fold higher in plants at 35 °C than at 25 °C.

In cv. Kashi Adarsh, no significant difference in severity (SG0) or viral accumulation (p > 0.1) was detected between the two temperatures at 7 dpi. By 30 dpi, plants at 35 °C showed increased severity (SG4) compared to those at 25 °C (SG1), along with a 3.19-fold higher viral DNA concentration at 35 °C. By 60 dpi, severity remained unchanged (SG4) in plants at 35 °C, whereas severity at 25 °C progressed to SG2, even though both showed reduced viral loads (1.5-fold at 35 °C; 3.6-fold at 25 °C).

For cv. Kashi Aman, plants at 35 °C consistently accumulated at least 6.5-fold more ToLCKV DNA across all time points, although no significant temporal variation in viral concentration was observed. Disease severity at 35 °C gradually increased from SG1 at 15 dpi to SG5 at 60 dpi. In contrast, at 25 °C, severity peaked at SG2 at 30 dpi and later declined to SG1 by 60 dpi (Fig. 6A and B).

ToLCNDV accumulation patterns

In ToLCNDV-inoculated plants at 25 °C, all three cultivars demonstrated a rise in viral DNA concentration from 7 to 30 dpi, followed by a reduction at 60 dpi (Fig. 6C). However, at 35 °C, virus accumulation was initially high at 7 dpi, but sharply declined at 30dpi and remained unchanged thereafter.

In cv. Pusa Ruby, similar severity scores were observed at both temperatures at 7 dpi (SG1) and 30 dpi (SG3). However, viral DNA accumulation differed substantially: plants at 35 °C harbored 111-fold more viral DNA than those at 25 °C at 7 dpi. At 30 dpi, viral concentration increased 328-fold at 25 °C, while a 3.6-fold reduction occurred at 35 °C. By 60 dpi, viral DNA decreased 3.7-fold at 25 °C with a concomitant increase in severity to SG4, whereas severity in 35 °C plants declined to SG2 with no significant change in viral concentration.

In cv. Kashi Adarsh, plants at 35 °C accumulated 70-fold more viral DNA than those at 25 °C at 7 dpi, despite showing no symptoms (SG0). By 15 dpi, mild symptoms (SG1) developed in two of five plants at 35 °C, but full recovery occurred by 30 dpi (SG0), coinciding with a 4.7-fold decline in viral DNA. At 30 dpi, viral DNA in 25 °C plants increased 75-fold, yet no symptoms were observed (SG0). By 60 dpi, viral accumulation remained stable at 35 °C but decreased 2.7-fold at 25 °C, with no changes in severity.

In cv. Kashi Aman, ToLCNDV accumulation remained low at both temperatures compared to the other cultivars (p < 0.05). Mild symptoms (SG1) were observed in two plants at 25 °C at 15 dpi, but they subsequently recovered. No significant temporal or temperature-related changes in viral DNA levels were detected throughout the experimental period (p > 0.1) (Fig. 6C and D).

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to identify and characterize the begomovirus isolate responsible for leaf curl disease in Ty-3-carrying tomato cultivars and to investigate how temperature influences virus accumulation, resistance response, and disease severity. Our investigation revealed the emergence of a resistance-breaking isolate of ToLCKV, capable of infecting cultivars previously considered resistant, thereby posing a significant threat to tomato production.

This newly characterized isolate (MZ578458) was identified in symptomatic plants harbouring the Ty-3 gene. The occurrence of severe leaf curl symptoms in these genotypes indicated a potential breach of resistance, which was confirmed through molecular identification and full-genome characterization of the virus. ToLCKV has been predominantly found in southern India, with a few reports from northern part of the country [37, 57–66]. Detection of ToLCKV in majority of the Ty-3 containing genotypes suggests a selective advantage of this isolate within resistant genetic backgrounds over the most predominant ToLCNDV, against which the Ty-3 resistant gene was found effective. The emergence of new begomovirus species or isolates often confers adaptive advantages, challenging existing resistance mechanisms. This phenomenon is well-documented in begomoviruses, which are known for their high genetic plasticity and rapid evolution, allowing them to circumvent host defense systems [39–41, 43, 45].

Analysis of MZ578458 genome sequence revealed that, unlike many begomoviruses, genetic variability in ToLCKV was concentrated in the non-overlapping C1 region, the 5′ terminal of C2, and the intergenic region (IR) [67–70]. The IR, in particular, exhibited the highest nucleotide diversity (π), while still preserving key cis-regulatory elements such as the iterons, TATA-box, and CAAT-box, a hallmark of begomovirus evolution driven by mutation and recombination [71–73]. Negative values of Tajima’s D and Z-stat across all genomic regions indicate that purifying selection is acting to eliminate deleterious mutations and preserve the functional integrity of viral proteins. The strongest purifying selection in the CP and Rep genes is consistent with their essential structural and enzymatic roles [74–78]. In contrast, signatures of weaker purifying selection and higher diversifying selection on VSRs and pathogenicity determinants indicate adaptive evolution under host immune pressure, a pattern frequently observed in plant-virus interactions [79, 80]. Furthermore, presence of large intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) in the VSRs, which enhance their functional plasticity and capacity to interact with multiple host targets, are likely to facilitate immune evasion and multifunctionality [79, 81, 82]. However, the effect of the genetic changes identified in the virus genome needs to be examined in the future to determine whether such changes contribute to resistance breakdown or influence temperature sensitivity. The information on tertiary structures and active sites of the MZ578458-encoded proteins may further help in designing or selecting potential inhibitors of these proteins through molecular docking and dynamic simulation studies [83–85].

Although cucurbit hosts remained asymptomatic upon MZ578458 inoculation, it underscores the potential for cross-species transmission and further evolution. However, detection of ToLCKV from cucurbits under field conditions, vector transmission including a back inoculation experiment from the asymptomatic cucurbits to tomato may strengthened this hypothesis in future.

Ty-3 was found to be effective against multiple begomoviruses including ToLCNDV, infecting tomato crops in India [34, 36, 86]. The current findings corroborate these observations, as no persistent symptoms were observed in ToLCNDV-inoculated Ty-3-homozygous tomato cultivars Kashi Adarsh and Kashi Aman. A recent study has shown breakdown of Ty-1 mediated resistance by TYLCV at high temperature (35 °C) [45]. Thus, our objective was to check for similar phenomenon of resistance breakdown in the case of Ty-3, the allelic variant of Ty-1, at higher temperature (35 °C). Accordingly, the temperature treatments applied in this study were conducted under constant growth-chamber conditions (25 °C and 35 °C), which enabled controlled assessment of temperature effects on Ty-3–mediated resistance, without the influence of other environmental fluctuations. Both the homozygous Ty-3 cultivars exhibited clear and severe symptom development when challenged with ToLCKV, especially under elevated temperature condition (35 °C). However, field environments are characterized by diurnal and seasonal temperature fluctuations, and resistance outcomes under fluctuating thermal regimes may differ from those observed under constant high temperature. Future experiments incorporating intermediate or diurnally varying temperature cycles will be essential to determine the stability and ecological relevance of Ty-3–mediated resistance under realistic field conditions.

Temperature also significantly influenced begomovirus accumulation. Previous research has shown increased TYLCV titers at higher temperatures [45, 87, 88]. Consistent with these findings, both susceptible (cv. Pusa Ruby) and resistant (cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman) cultivars infected with the ToLCKV isolate displayed enhanced viral accumulation at 35 °C. However, this pattern was not replicated for ToLCNDV. Decline in ToLCNDV titer at 35 °C further replicated the previous reports for Sri Lankan cassava mosaic virus and cotton leaf crumple virus under elevated temperatures [89, 90]. Such differential response of the resistant plants against two viruses may be the result of the host-virus interaction dynamics. Future studies should investigate how these plants respond to heat and infection at the molecular and physiological levels. Further, expanding this study with a larger number of resistant cultivars may determine whether the resistance-breaking phenomenon by this virus isolate has a broader impact.

While a positive correlation between viral load and disease severity has been reported in various pathosystems [91–93], only a partial correlation was observed in this study. In both susceptible and resistant cultivars infected with ToLCKV, symptom severity increased alongside viral accumulation until 30 dpi. However, after this point, viral titers either plateaued or declined, whereas disease symptoms continued to progress, particularly in cv. Pusa Ruby and cv. Kashi Adarsh. In cv. Kashi Aman, viral DNA levels remained relatively stable over time, despite an increase in disease severity. The results thus suggest that symptom progression is not solely determined by viral load and may involve additional complex host–virus interactions. Similarly, no consistent correlation between viral DNA levels and symptom expression was observed in ToLCNDV-infected plants, aligning with previous findings in cotton leaf curl disease and tomato leaf curl disease [94, 95]. Despite having same resistance gene (Ty-3) and similar resistance response to ToLCNDV, cv. Kashi Adarsh and cv. Kashi Aman showed a huge difference in virus accumulation. The reason behind this observation was not clear. The difference in the genetic background in these varieties could be a probable reason.

Although high temperatures appeared to enhance viral replication and disease severity, the development of symptoms under ambient temperature conditions suggests that viral genetic factors may also contribute significantly to the evasion of Ty-3-mediated resistance. It is therefore likely that specific interactions between viral effectors and host immune components, potentially modulated by temperature, facilitated the resistance breakdown observed with this ToLCKV isolate. Further studies are required to elucidate the functional significance of the observed genetic alterations in the MZ578458 genome. Approaches such as constructing chimeric viruses by swapping genomic regions between resistance-breaking and non-breaking isolates may help identify key determinants of resistance evasion [96, 97]. Since Ty-3 resistance is associated with transcriptional gene silencing (TGS) [98], and begomoviruses encode multiple RNAi suppressors [7, 99, 100], functional assays involving overexpression of suppressors from MZ578458 could provide insights into their roles in resistance breakdown. Additionally, transcriptomic and small RNA profiling approaches, as employed in the context of Ty-1 resistance evasion [101], could be adapted to dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying Ty-3 resistance failure. The influence of temperature-driven resistance breakdown in natural environments, where temperatures fluctuate, can be examined to assess the effects of climate change on viral pathogenesis.

Conclusion

In this study, we have characterized a resistance-breaking isolate of ToLCKV, with key adaptive changes in RNAi suppressor proteins. This isolate is capable of infecting Ty-3-mediated resistant cultivars under controlled experimental conditions. Temperature appeared to influence virus accumulation and symptom expression, indicating that environmental conditions could modulate the extent of resistance breakdown. However, at present, it remains unresolved whether elevated temperature primarily enhances viral fitness, suppresses host immune responses, or acts through a combination of both mechanisms. Future studies employing functional assays of viral suppressors, development of chimeric viral constructs, and integrative transcriptomic and small RNA analyses will be essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying Ty-3 resistance breakdown and to guide the breeding of tomato cultivars with more durable resistance. This study thus highlights emergence of a new isolate of ToLCKV with a potential risk to the durability of Ty-3-based resistance.
