Construction and validation of a nomogram model for predicting different sites of ankle pain in runners with chronic ankle instability
Authors: Zeyi Zhang, Jian Song, Hanlin Shi, Shengmeng Wei, Youping Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to establish a risk prediction nomogram model for anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral ankle pain in runners with chronic ankle instability (CAI) and analyse the potential risk factors for pain at different ankle sites. Thirty recreational runners with CAI who reported ankle pain in the anterolateral, mediolateral, or posterolateral regions were recruited for this study. Kinematic, kinetic, and electromyographic data during running were collected using motion capture system, 3-D force platform, and surface electromyography system. These data were used to generate a dynamic nomogram. The results showed that anterolateral ankle pain in runners with CAI may be caused by insufficient gastrocnemius muscle strength (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.73–0.97), excessive ground reaction force (GRF, OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.25–6.22), and an increased percentage of ankle energy absorption (OR 9.11, 95% CI 1.50–77.79). Mediolateral ankle pain might be contributed by greater ankle inversion angle (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.00) and GRF (OR 2.13, 95% CI 1.17–4.31). Moreover, posterolateral ankle pain was predicted by increased ankle adduction angle (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.00–1.12), increased GRF (OR 2.16, 95% CI 1.07–4.80), and decreased dynamic stability (OR 0.20, 95% CI 0.05–0.68). To prevent ankle pain, runners with CAI should be encouraged to focus on improving the neuroreceptor sensitivity of the gastrocnemius muscles, and retraining their energy absorption patterns.

Introduction

Running is a highly popular sport due to its accessibility. It requires no equipment, playgrounds, or time constraints and has minimal technical requirements. Therefore, it naturally becomes the preferred exercise option for sports enthusiasts. The 2022 Research on National Fitness Behaviour and Consumption, released by the China Sporting Goods Federation, revealed that running had the highest participation rate among sports, accounting for 61.0% of participants. Nonetheless, similar to other forms of physical exercise, participation in running poses considerable risks of musculoskeletal injuries. A survey showed that approximately 40–50% of runners suffered from running-related injuries, with lateral ankle sprains being the most common1. Additionally, forty percent of runners with lateral ankle sprains would develop CAI2. This population commonly experiences repetitive ankle injuries, proprioceptive deficits, instances of “giving way”, and ankle pain during exercise, which severely affects their quality of life and sports participation2.

Ankle pain is the most common symptom in runners with CAI. According to a systematic review, approximately 60.1% of runners with CAI experienced severe lateral ankle pain, with 47.7% of cases occurring during moderate- and high-intensity physical activity3. Indeed, ankle pain could profoundly disrupt a runner’s movement patterns, leading to numerous compensatory injuries. First, to maintain a stable movement pattern under external stimuli, humans must process complex somatosensory information while running4. However, sensations of ankle pain might project to various areas of the brain, including the somatic cortex, limbic system, and frontal cortex. This could potentially affect proprioception at multiple levels in the central nervous system, may be detrimental to maintaining running stability, reduces the effectiveness of exercise, and trigger compensatory injuries4. Furthermore, recent research indicated that up to 55% of cases of knee osteoarthritis are associated with lateral ankle pain5. The underlying mechanism for this association is that CAI runners with lateral ankle pain tend to exhibit restricted ankle function, diminished position sense, and frequently demonstrate augmented ankle adduction during functional activities, which can subsequently result in tibial internal rotation6. Levinger et al.7 revealed that tibial internal rotation markedly elevates medial knee loading, thereby increasing the risk of knee osteoarthritis.

Due to the severe negative impact of lateral ankle pain, the implementation of early intervention training has been recommended for populations that are predisposed to ankle pain, especially individuals with CAI. The identification of biomechanical risk factors is an indispensable prerequisite for targeted intervention training. However, the biomechanical risk factors for ankle pain in runners with CAI remain unknown. Concurrently, the absence of relevant biomechanical studies has precluded clinicians from developing effective and targeted prevention strategies. Furthermore, several studies have highlighted that ankle pain could occur at different sites, including the anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral parts of the ankle, and that the biomechanical risk factors associated with ankle pain exhibit potential modification contingent upon the precise anatomical site where the pain is localized8–10. Therefore, to further our current understanding of the potential biomechanical risk factors associated with ankle pain at different sites, it was necessary to develop biomechanical risk prediction models based on different sites of ankle pain. Recently, risk predictive models, such as logistic regression, nomograms, and machine learning, have been increasingly used for diagnostic and clinical prognosis of specific diseases11. The risk prediction nomogram model was instrumental in preventing and rehabilitating gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, and even COVID-19 after its predictive efficacy was confirmed by scientific methods. This method also offers new ideas for establishing risk prediction models for ankle pain11.

This study aimed to investigate the potential biomechanical risk factors associated with ankle pain at different locations and to develop a risk prediction nomogram model for anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral ankle pain in runners with CAI to provide a theoretical basis for the prevention and early management of ankle pain. Based on previous studies, this research hypothesized that anterolateral and mediolateral ankle pain experienced by runners with CAI might be associated with inadequate gastrocnemius muscle strength, excessive ankle inversion angle, higher ankle energy absorption, and GRF. In addition, greater ankle adduction angle, GRF, and poorer dynamic stability might be risk factors for posterolateral ankle pain.

Methods

Participants

This study analysed recreational male runners with CAI who ran at least 10 km weekly. The diagnosis of CAI was based on the criteria of the International Ankle Society. Furthermore, the study’s inclusion criteria included the following: (1) Participants that had a history of at least one ankle sprain that caused a 1-day interruption in physical activity. The first ankle sprain must have occurred more than 1 year prior to the experiment, and the most recent ankle sprain must have occurred more than 3 months prior to the experiment. (2) Runners should have experienced two or more instances of severe 'giving way' in the 6 months prior to the test, as well as having had two or more ankle sprains during that period. Additionally, the Cumberland Ankle Instability Questionnaire (CAIT) score of the included participants had to be less than 24. (3) A Foot and Ankle Ability Measure (FAAM) of less than 80% and a Foot and Ankle Outcome Score (FAOS) of less than 75% in three or more categories. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Runners who had undergone bone, nerves, or joint surgery; (2) Runners with a history of lower extremity fractures; (3) Runners who experienced acute lower extremity injuries within 3 months prior to the experiment, which altered the integrity and function of the joint and interrupted physical activity for > 1 day12.

The Gpower software was employed to ascertain the requisite sample size, with an effect size of 0.4, a statistical power of 0.95, an alpha level of 0.05, and a group size of 4 groups. This resulted in a minimum sample size of 112 individuals, and the study initially recruited 123 CAI runners into this study. During the pre-test survey, three participants were excluded due to knee surgery experience, leaving 120 participants in the experiment. The participants were divided into four groups: the anterolateral ankle joint pain, mediolateral pain, posterolateral pain, and no pain groups. Furthermore, we referred to the work of Gogate et al.13 and adopted the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) as the instrument for measuring ankle pain in this study. This scale ranges from 0, indicating no pain, to 10, representing the worst possible pain. Pain severity is categorized such that scores of 1–3 represent mild pain, 4–6 indicate moderate pain, and 7–10 denote severe pain14. Our study specifies that participants in the no pain group must achieve an NRS score of 0, while those in the anterolateral ankle pain, mediolateral ankle pain, and posterolateral ankle pain groups must have NRS scores exceeding 3, thus reaching the threshold for moderate pain. All participants were required to carefully read the experiment protocol and sign informed consent forms. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of East China Normal University. Before the formal experiment, the participants underwent basic anthropometric tests, including height, weight, and CAIT score. The results are shown in Table 1. Equity, diversity, and inclusion statement

Our study included all identified cases of ankle pain in college student-athletes inclusive of all race/ethnicities, socioeconomic levels, and occurrence in a marginalized community. Our author team consisted of one woman and four men from different disciplines (athletic training, biomechanics), including two authors considered junior scholars. The data collection was similar in all cases and we did not alter methods based on regional, educational, or socioeconomic differences of the community in which the case occurred.

Test apparatus

Motion capture system

In this study, 12 Vicon Vero2.2 (Vicon Industries, Inc., USA) infrared cameras and a motion capture system with a 14 mm infrared marker ball were used to record running manoeuvres. The cameras were positioned at a height of 2.5 m with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz.

Three-dimensional force plate

In this study, two 40 cm × 60 cm Kistler 3D force plates (Kistler Inc., Switzerland) were used to record the GRF of the participants during running with a sampling frequency of 1 kHz.

Surface EMG testing system

The surface EMG equipment used in this study was the Telemyo 2400DTS sEMG wireless telemetry system (Noraxon Company, USA), which contains a 32-channel signal collector, a USB data transfer interface, and a wireless receiver card and could be connected to a computer either wired or wirelessly. For surface EMG testing, it is necessary to utilize the device in conjunction with electrodes and preamplifiers.

Experimental design and testing procedures

The study was conducted on October 15, 2023, at the key laboratory of adolescent health assessment and exercise intervention of Ministry of Education, East China Normal University. Prior to the test, all participants were provided with running shoes and were asked to jog on a treadmill at a speed of 3.5 m/s for 10 min to warm up. The testers gave the participants detailed explanations of the test movements and requirements. Following this, the participants were affixed with reflective markers and electrodes. The markers were meticulously affixed to the relevant bony landmarks based on the Plug In Gait-full body model, while the electrodes were securely positioned on the muscle bellies of the peroneus longus, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior muscles. Before the formal experiment, the participants underwent static calibration, which involved standing naturally with a straight torso, arms abducted, elbows flexed at 90°, and palms pronated while looking straight ahead. Simultaneously, the maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) of the muscles, including those of the peroneus longus, gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior muscles, were measured as the basis for the standardization of EMG signals. At the beginning of the experiment, the participants were asked to run on a 15 × 2 m treadmill at a comfortable speed with a movement pattern similar to that of daily exercise, as shown in Fig. 1. Two three-dimensional force platforms were embedded in the middle of the treadmill. The participants were instructed to accurately measure their step lengths to ensure that the affected leg could strike the force platform accurately while running. Overall, five valid sets of data were collected, with a 3-min interval between each test.Fig. 1Static calibration and running manoeuvres.

Data extraction and preparation

Kinematics

The kinematic data were calculated using Visual 3D software (C-Motion Inc., USA). The marker’s trajectory was filtered using a 10 Hz, four-step Butterworth filter. Afterwards, the joint angles in the sagittal, frontal, and horizontal planes of the ankle joint were calculated at the moment of initial contact. The calculations included building a transformation matrix, calculating the joint centre, and establishing a local coordinate system for the human body segment. The kinematic parameters analysed in this study focused on the following: (1) Ankle plantarflexion and dorsiflexion angle: in the present study, the angle was defined as 0° when the foot was vertical to the shank, and on that basis, the dorsiflexion angle was positive and the plantarflexion angle was negative; (2) ankle inversion and eversion angle, which was set at 0° in the neutral position of the ankle joint, positive for inversion and negative for eversion; and (3) adduction and abduction angle of the ankle: the angle was determined as 0° when the ankle was in a neutral position in the horizontal plane. It was positive during adduction and negative during abduction, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Schematic diagram of the calculation method for the joint angle.

Schematic diagram of the calculation method for the joint angle.

Kinetics

In this study, we focused on analysing kinetic data during the stance phase of the affected leg in runners with CAI. Raw GRF data were subjected to filtration utilizing a four-step Butterworth low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 50 Hz. In this research investigation, the running stance period was defined upon the evaluation of the GRF. Specifically, if the GRF exceeded 20 N, it unequivocally indicated that the participants were undergoing the moment of initial contact. Conversely, if the GRF registered below the threshold of 20 N, it reliably indicated that the participant was engaging in the pivotal action of taking off. The core indicators analysed in this study include vertical GRF, time to peak GRF (TTP), dynamic stability, and ankle energy absorption. Muscle force

OpenSim modellingThe Marks trajectory and GRF data in c3d format files were imported into Matlab 2016b for conversion. The Marks trajectory data was written into trc files, and the GRF data was written into mot files. OpenSim was used to load the trc and mot files. First, to ensure the mass and length of the human body segment in the model corresponded to real-life values, the data was scaled using the Scaling Model (SM) module. Second, inverse dynamics were used to calculate “the optimal match point” between the model and reality by solving the optimization problem of weighted least squares that minimized the marking error. Model assumptions, kinematic system errors, and noise occasionally caused dynamic inconsistencies in the model. To address this, OpenSim used the Reduce Residuals Algorithm (RRA) module to minimize the difference between the experimental results and the model simulation results (Fresidual). Finally, the Calculate Muscle Control (CMC) module was then utilized to simulate lower extremity muscle force, as shown in Fig. 3.Fig. 3OpenSim modelling process. Note In this context, the term “exp” denotes experimentally measured data, “ik” is an abbreviation for inverse kinematics. Notably, “rra” is an acronym for the “Residual Reduction Algorithm”.Model validationThe model validation process included three aspects: kinematic validation, kinetic validation, and electromyographic validation. To validate kinematics, we ensured that the disparity between the experimentally measured peak joint angle and the peak joint angle simulated by OpenSim did not exceed 5°. For kinetics, a model was considered to adequately fit the actual movement if the difference between the experimentally measured peak pelvic moments (or residual forces) and the results of the OpenSim simulation was less than 75 Nm and 20 N, respectively. The pelvic moments were calculated as follows. To begin, we established a pelvic coordinate system by analyzing the marker points' coordinates for the left anterior superior iliac spine, right anterior superior iliac spine, left posterior superior iliac spine, and right posterior superior iliac spine of the subjects. The coordinate origin was defined as “Pelvis.Origin”. The pelvic angle is then obtained by rotating the pelvic coordinate system relative to the global coordinate system15. Subsequently, the peak 3D moment of the pelvis is calculated using the inverse dynamics method, based on the 3D angle of the pelvis15. As for electromyography, we compared the experimental results with the electromyographic curves obtained from modelling. If the results were similar, we considered the results of RRA and CMC to be acceptable.

Statistics

Statistical analysis and the risk prediction nomogram model were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA) and R Studio 4.3.1 (R studio, USA). The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the biomechanical characteristics of different ankle pain locations (no pain, anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral). The results are presented as means ± SD. Lasso regression was used to select potential risk factors associated with ankle pain, build logistic regression models, and generate a dynamic nomogram for risk prediction in R Studio. The predictive efficacy, specificity, computational sensitivity, and predictive gain of the model were verified by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). All statistical tests in this study were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

The results of one-way ANOVA (Fig. 4) showed that, compared to CAI runners without pain, CAI runners who had anterolateral ankle pain had lower gastrocnemius muscle force during running (F[1,118] = 6.198, P = 0.001). Additionally, CAI runners who had mediolateral ankle pain exhibited a significantly greater ankle inversion angle during running compared to CAI runners without pain (F[1,118] = 4.772, P = 0.001).Fig. 4Muscle force and kinematic characteristics in CAI runners with different ankle pain locations.

CAI runners who had anterolateral ankle pain exhibited a greater GRF during running than did CAI runners without pain (F[1,118] = 7.347, P = 0.002). Additionally, ankle energy absorption was significantly greater in CAI runners who had anterolateral ankle pain compared to CAI runners without pain (F[1,118] = 3.451, P = 0.002), as shown in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Kinetic characteristics of CAI runners with different ankle pain locations.

As illustrated in Fig. 6, the study employed LASSO regression to select potential risk factors, which were then used as independent variables in logistic regression. The study identified four potential risk factors associated with anterolateral ankle pain: gastrocnemius muscle force, ankle adduction angle, GRF, and ankle energy absorption. The correlation coefficients for these factors were − 0.106, − 0.001, 0.652, and 1.308, respectively. Additionally, in the context of mediolateral ankle pain, our investigation identified three potential risk factors: peroneus longus muscle force, ankle adduction angle, and GRF. The correlation coefficients for these variables were found to be − 0.003, − 0.030, and − 0.359, respectively. The risk factors associated with posterolateral ankle pain may include peroneus longus muscle force, gastrocnemius muscle force, ankle adduction angle, GRF, dynamic stability, and ankle energy absorption. The correlation coefficients for these potential risk factors were − 0.022, − 0.025, − 0.033, − 0.395, − 0.819, and − 0.235, respectively.Fig. 6Predictor variables in the LASSO regression selection sub-risk prediction model.

Predictor variables in the LASSO regression selection sub-risk prediction model.

As indicated in Table 2, the variables that passed the screening process were used in the logistic regression model. The results also indicated that gastrocnemius muscle force (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.73–0.97), GRF (OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.25–6.22), and ankle energy absorption (OR 9.11, 95% CI 1.50–77.79) were independent predictors of anterolateral ankle pain. Discussion

The gastrocnemius muscle—one of the ankle plantar flexors—is connected to the foot through the Achilles tendon. During physical activity, the dynamic contraction of the gastrocnemius muscle plays a crucial role in generating an ankle plantarflexion moment. This enhances pedal efficiency and stretching capabilities, and mitigates the angular velocity of ankle dorsiflexion. Consequently, it significantly reduces the sagittal plane load exerted on the ankle joint. Moreover, this mechanism further bolsters ankle stability during the stance phase16. Runners with CAI often have reduced gastrocnemius muscle force during running due to multiple ankle sprains, which could lead to insufficient ankle muscle proprioception and inadequate neural control17. The present study identified gastrocnemius muscle force insufficiency as a potential risk factor for anterolateral ankle pain in runners with CAI, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies18. Anderson et al.18 found that gastrocnemius muscle force insufficiency was present in approximately 88% of runners with anterolateral ankle pain. Fourchet et al.19 found that after inducing gastrocnemius fatigue, runners with inadequate gastrocnemius muscle force often displayed longer forefoot stance time. The intrinsic mechanism was that gastrocnemius muscle weakness reduced the pedal and stretch force during running, and runners strategically extended the duration of forefoot support in order to maintain the impulse generated during the swing phase19. However, prolonged stance time could lead to arch collapse, which in the long term manifests as anterolateral ankle pain19. Therefore, runners with CAI may benefit from performing plyometric training to improve the sensitivity of gastrocnemius neuroreceptors (e.g., muscle spindle, Golgi apparatus, and tendon organs), which may improve neuromuscular control around the gastrocnemius muscle and increase gastrocnemius strength20.

Runners with CAI often exhibit a higher ankle inversion angle during movement due to impaired ankle inversion proprioception21. The findings of this study suggest that a greater ankle inversion angle is associated with mediolateral ankle pain. This might be because repeated ankle inversion could accelerate the formation of scar tissue and alter collagen types, water content, and proteoglycan abundance in the lateral ankle ligaments. These changes reduce the ligament's viscoelasticity and often lead to pain during ankle inversion22. Furthermore, Park et al.23 highlighted that repeated excessive ankle inversion might lead to frequent collisions between the talus and distal tibia, which could result in ankle impingement syndrome. Ankle impingement syndrome has also been associated with mediolateral ankle pain. Moreover, repeated collisions between the talus and tibia could result in insufficient blood supply to the talus, leading to inadequate healing of cartilage contusions and necrosis of cartilage debris, which could ultimately cause mediolateral ankle pain. Therefore, runners with CAI should strengthen their ankle evertors to provide sufficient eversion moments during the stance phase. Rehabilitative attention should also be given to proprioceptive training of the ankle joint to maintain a neutral position at the initial contact moment and reduce mediolateral ankle pain.

Research has shown that runners with CAI often have an excessive ankle adduction angle during running24. This could be attributed to the longer activation time of the ankle evertors compared to healthy individuals, which limits the ability of the muscle groups to decrease the ankle adduction angle25. Furthermore, runners with CAI are accustomed to placing the COP on the lateral side of the foot, which would lead to a higher ankle adduction angle26. According to the finding of Wu et al.27, an ankle adduction moment of up to 10 Nm could cause ankle pain and discomfort, moments of 23 Nm may result in a grade I ankle sprain and those of 40–45 Nm or more would lead to an ankle fracture. Kobayashi et al.24 further discovered that excessive ankle adduction, ranging from 5° to 7°, had the potential to induce an increase in anterior–posterior displacement of the talocrural joint, stress on the posterior talofibular ligament, and may lead to ankle pain.

This study's findings are consistent with those of previous research, as the prediction model indicates that the ankle adduction angle is one of the predictors of posterolateral ankle pain. Therefore, the study’s findings indicate that runners with CAI may benefit from engaging in jump stabilization training, which increases the strength of the ankle evertors, to enhance body balance. Moreover, runners with CAI could focus on optimizing the COP position to reduce excessive ankle adduction angle. Ultimately, these interventions would prevent the manifestation of posterolateral ankle pain28.

Runners with CAI tend to display greater GRF during running. This is caused by the disruption of the structure of the anterior talofibular ligament due to grade II or III ankle sprains, which could lead to the formation of scar tissue during the ligament-remodelling phase. The scar tissue in the damaged ligament is less mobile than the intact ligament, and may lead to anterior movement of the talus, which reduces the range of motion (ROM) during ankle dorsiflexion29. However, studies have consistently shown that the ankle dorsiflexion ROM during running should be at least 20°–30°29. Restricted ankle dorsiflexion has been demonstrated to strongly correlate with the occurrence of excessive GRF within the ankle joint during the stance phase. The study's prediction model found that greater GRF during the stance phase may be a risk factor for anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral ankle pain. A higher GRF cannot be adequately absorbed by ankle sagittal plane motion alone; therefore, ankle frontal plane motion is required to increase the displacement distances in the medial–lateral direction of the COP during running. This energy absorption strategy aggravated the lateral load on the ankle joint. Furthermore, an inappropriate positioning of the COP could lead to the impingement of the distal tibia on the talus. Persistent impingement irritation would consequently lead to the formation of soft tissue scarring and the development of bone capillaries. Subsequently, ankle swelling and pain could ensue as a result of these processes29. The findings of this study and those of previous research indicate that runners with CAI should focus on retraining their running posture. The forefoot strike strategy might also be effective in cushioning GRF and preventing lateral ankle pain30.

Runners with CAI frequently exhibit inadequate dynamic stability while running. This was due to ankle instability that affects not only the activation pattern of muscles around the ankle joint but also the neuromuscular control of the hip joint, which is mainly characterized by a decrease in hip extensor muscle strength, excessive displacement range of centre of mass (COM), and poorer dynamic stability31. Furthermore, an in vivo study revealed that during running, talus displacement was greater in patients with CAI than in healthy individuals due to ligament laxity, which may also result in a loss of dynamic stability32. Predictive modelling results indicate that poorer dynamic stability in runners with CAI was associated with posterolateral ankle pain. Furthermore, insufficient dynamic stability increased the total ankle displacement in runners with CAI, which repeatedly eroded and squeezed soft tissues and ligaments around the ankle, leading to local inflammation and ultimately ankle pain33. To improve dynamic stability, ankle braces are commonly used to provide mechanical support and restore the motion pattern to pre-injury states in runners with CAI34. Meanwhile, strength training of the hip extensors would improve core control during running and indirectly decrease the risk of ankle pain.

During the stance phase of running, the human body must absorb energy, primarily using the ankle plantar flexors, knee extensors, and hip extensors. Delahunt et al.35 reported that the peak of vertical and anterior–posterior GRF occurred in the early stage of the stance phase. The musculoskeletal structures of the lower extremity ensure complete energy absorption within a short time. However, due to the presence of sensory-motor deficits in runners with CAI, they cannot adequately allocate the proportion of joint energy absorption36, and this exposes the ankle joint to heightened energy absorption load36. The prediction model in this study revealed that excessive energy absorption in the ankle joint could cause anterolateral ankle pain. This may be because the ankle joint requires significant plantarflexion and dorsiflexion activity for complete energy absorption. Furthermore, during ankle plantarflexion, there was an augmented burden on the anterior talofibular ligament, leading to amplified stress and strain. Conversely, dorsiflexion of the ankle joint intensifies the stress and strain on the calcaneofibular ligament. Repeated overstretching could damage the ligament’s capillaries, leading to haemorrhage into the surrounding joint capsule and pain. Consequently, if a lateral force was applied to the ankle joint, the level of pain experienced by these individuals might increase significantly37. Therefore, individuals with CAI should focus on retraining their energy absorption strategies to prevent lateral ankle pain.

Clinical implications

This study, utilizing the risk prediction nomogram model, has unveiled the potential biomechanical risk factors associated with ankle pain in various regions. Individuals with anterolateral ankle pain should focus on strengthening the gastrocnemius muscle through heel raise exercises and adjusting their lower extremity cushioning method while running to decrease ankle energy absorption. For those with mediolateral pain related to CAI, enhancing the strength of ankle evertor muscles, like the peroneus longus muscle, is crucial to prevent excessive ankle inversion during running. Runners experiencing posterolateral pain should prioritize improving proprioception and ankle joint control to prevent adduction during running. It is worth noting that CAI runners across all pain locations exhibit higher ground reaction forces during running, underscoring the importance of modifying cushioning patterns during running to land with the lower limbs in a more flexed position (Supplementary Information 1).

Limitations

In this study, a risk prediction nomogram model was used to analyse the potential risk factors for anterolateral, mediolateral, and posterolateral ankle pain; however, the study has some limitations. First, this study was unable to distinguish the degree of ankle pain due to the limited sample size. Future studies could explore the biomechanical risk factors of different degrees of ankle pain using the VAS. Second, this study solely analysed the contribution of ankle biomechanical characteristics to ankle pain. Future studies could consider including relevant indicators of the proximal joint.

Conclusion

This study showed that anterolateral ankle pain in runners with CAI is caused by insufficient gastrocnemius muscle strength, greater GRF, and an increased percentage of energy absorption in the ankle joint during running. Furthermore, mediolateral ankle pain was found to be associated with a higher ankle inversion angle and GRF, while posterolateral ankle pain was predicted by an elevated ankle adduction angle, increased GRF, and decreased dynamic stability. To prevent ankle pain, runners with CAI should focus on improving the neuroreceptor sensitivity of the gastrocnemius muscles, supplementing proprioceptive training of ankle evertor, balance training, and retraining their energy absorption strategy.
