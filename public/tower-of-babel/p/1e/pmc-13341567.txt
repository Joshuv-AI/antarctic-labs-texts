Metabolic health and cardiovascular outcomes in treated hypertension: a secondary analysis of SPRINT
Authors: Patrick Cheon, Uttsav Sandesara, Saeid Mirzai, Elsayed Z Soliman, Michael D Shapiro, Richard Kazibwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic abnormalities frequently coexist with hypertension and independently contribute to cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. Whether metabolic dysfunction predicts incident CVD events and/or modifies the efficacy of intensive blood pressure control in primary prevention remains uncertain.MethodsData from Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial participants without baseline CVD or diabetes were analyzed. Metabolic health was classified based on triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women, fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL, and body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2. Participants with ≤ 1 abnormality were classified as metabolically healthy (MH) and those with ≥ 2 as metabolically unhealthy (MUH). Cox proportional hazards models assessed the association of metabolic health status with outcomes and tested for effect modification by treatment arm. The primary outcome was a composite of myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death.ResultsA total of 7,299 participants (mean age, 67.5 years; 37.9% female) were included, of whom 2,852 (39.1%) were classified as MUH. Over a median follow-up of 3.2 years, 440 primary outcome events occurred. The incidence of primary outcome events was higher in the MUH compared with the MH group (6.4% vs. 5.8%). After multivariable adjustment, MUH participants had a significantly higher risk of the primary outcome compared with MH participants (hazard ratio, 1.27; 95% confidence interval, 1.03–1.57; P = 0.024).ConclusionsAmong hypertensive adults free of diabetes and CVD, MUH status was associated with higher cardiovascular risk; however, intensive systolic blood pressure lowering was protective across levels of metabolic burden.Keywords: Hypertension, Metabolic health, Intensive blood pressure control, Cardiovascular outcomes

BACKGROUND

Hypertension is a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), and intensive blood pressure lowering has emerged as an effective strategy to reduce cardiovascular events [1]. In the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial (SPRINT), intensive systolic blood pressure (SBP) lowering to a target of < 120 mmHg reduced major cardiovascular events by 25% and all-cause mortality by 27% compared with standard treatment targeting < 140 mmHg [2]. These findings informed contemporary guidelines for lower SBP targets in high-risk patients [3]. However, hypertension rarely occurs in isolation; the majority of individuals with hypertension also have coexisting metabolic abnormalities that independently contribute to CVD risk [4,5,6].

Although metabolic health is a well-established determinant of cardiovascular risk, uncertainty remains regarding the benefit of intensive SBP lowering in hypertensive individuals with low metabolic burden. In clinical practice, it is unclear whether hypertensive patients without overt cardiometabolic abnormalities derive comparable benefit from more intensive SBP targets or whether metabolic health should inform SBP treatment intensity. Addressing this question is clinically relevant for extending intensive SBP-lowering strategies in primary prevention. Accordingly, we evaluated SPRINT participants without diabetes or baseline CVD to assess whether metabolic health is independently associated with cardiovascular risk and whether it modifies the effect of intensive SBP lowering.

METHODS

Data source and study population

Data for this analysis were obtained from the SPRINT dataset, publicly available through the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center. The rationale, design, and primary results of SPRINT have been published previously [2,7]. Briefly, SPRINT was a randomized, controlled, open-label trial that enrolled 9361 non-diabetic adults aged ≥ 50 years with hypertension (SBP, 130–180 mmHg) and increased cardiovascular risk, defined by one or more of the following: clinical or subclinical CVD other than stroke, chronic kidney disease (CKD) with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 20 to < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, 10-year CVD risk ≥ 15% based on the Framingham risk score, or age ≥ 75 years [8]. Participants were randomized to intensive treatment (target SBP < 120 mmHg) or standard treatment (target SBP < 140 mmHg). The trial was terminated early after a median follow-up of 3.22 years due to lower rates of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality in the intensive treatment group [2]. All participants provided written informed consent, and the trial was approved by the institutional review board at each participating site. For the current analysis, we excluded participants with baseline CVD to focus on a primary prevention population. Participants missing data on metabolic health components or model covariates were further excluded.

Metabolic health classification

Metabolic health was defined using modified metabolic syndrome criteria based on 4 components: triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 40 mg/dL for men or < 50 mg/dL for women, fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL, and body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 [9]. Blood pressure was not included as a classifying component because all SPRINT participants had hypertension at enrollment (SBP, 130–180 mmHg). BMI was used in place of waist circumference due to data availability. Although BMI and waist circumference are highly correlated (r = 0.8–0.9) and both predict cardiovascular risk, BMI may misclassify individuals with normal-weight central obesity [10,11].

Participants with ≤ 1 metabolic abnormality were classified as metabolically healthy (MH) and those with ≥ 2 abnormalities were classified as metabolically unhealthy (MUH). This threshold was selected because the conventional metabolic syndrome definition requires ≥ 3 of 5 components; however, because all SPRINT participants had hypertension and individuals with diabetes were excluded, blood pressure and the full glucose criterion could not serve as discriminating components in this population. Of the 4 remaining assessable criteria, a threshold of ≥ 2 abnormalities is proportionally analogous to the standard ≥ 3 of 5 definition. Sensitivity analyses using a stricter threshold of ≥ 3 abnormalities yielded a strengthened association with the primary outcome (hazard ratio [HR], 1.58; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.22–2.05), consistent with a dose-response relationship and supporting the validity of the chosen cutpoint. Because SPRINT excluded individuals with diabetes, the fasting glucose criterion in this analysis captures impaired fasting glucose (prediabetes) rather than the full spectrum of glucose dysregulation. Consequently, the MUH group predominantly represents individuals with subclinical metabolic dysfunction rather than fully manifest metabolic syndrome as conventionally defined.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the SPRINT primary efficacy endpoint: a composite of myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stroke, acute decompensated heart failure, or death from cardiovascular causes. Secondary outcomes included each component of the primary outcome and all-cause mortality. All outcomes were centrally adjudicated by investigators blinded to treatment assignment [2,7].

Covariates

Baseline sociodemographic data, comorbid conditions, and medication use were ascertained by trained study personnel during screening or randomization visits. Fasting blood and urine samples were collected at baseline. Serum creatinine was measured using an enzymatic procedure, and eGFR was calculated using the CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation [12]. All assays were performed at a single SPRINT central laboratory.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared according to metabolic health status. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using t-tests. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using χ2 tests.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between metabolic health status and cardiovascular outcomes, with MH as the reference group. Three sequential models were constructed: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and health insurance status; model 3 further adjusted for treatment arm, 10-year CVD risk score, SBP, eGFR, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, statin use, aspirin use, and smoking status. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals; the global test was not significant (P = 0.30), indicating the assumption was satisfied (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To assess whether the effect of intensive blood pressure lowering differed by metabolic health status, we performed stratified analyses within MH and MUH groups and tested for multiplicative interaction by including a cross-product term (metabolic health × treatment arm) in fully adjusted models. Absolute risk reduction (ARR) and number needed to treat (NNT) were calculated from observed event rates. Additional subgroup analyses were performed by age (< 75 vs. ≥ 75 years), sex, baseline CKD (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2), and BMI (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m2). Because MUH participants were significantly younger than MH participants and age is a strong cardiovascular risk factor, we performed age-stratified analyses to evaluate whether age acted as a negative confounder masking the MUH–CVD association (Supplementary Table 1). To evaluate the robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative definitions of metabolic health: a stricter threshold requiring ≥ 3 metabolic abnormalities and alternative BMI cut points of ≥ 25 and ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 (Supplementary Table 2). To explore which metabolic components were most strongly associated with incident stroke, we modeled each component individually and then simultaneously in mutually adjusted models (Supplementary Table 3). A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Study population

After excluding participants with prevalent CVD (n = 1,869) and those with missing covariate data (n = 155), 7,299 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 4,447 (60.9%) were classified as MH and 2,852 (39.1%) as MUH. Baseline characteristics according to metabolic health status are shown in Table 1. MUH participants were younger (65.4 vs. 68.9 years; P < 0.001), had higher BMI (33.2 vs. 27.8 kg/m2; P < 0.001), higher fasting glucose (105.5 vs. 94.2 mg/dL; P < 0.001), higher triglycerides (159.3 vs. 97.0 mg/dL; P < 0.001), and lower HDL-C (45.8 vs. 58.7 mg/dL; P < 0.001) compared with MH participants. Treatment assignment was well-balanced within both groups (50.2% vs. 49.8% randomized to intensive treatment; P = 0.770).

Metabolic health and cardiovascular risk

During a median follow-up of 3.22 years, 440 primary outcome events occurred. MUH participants were on average 3.5 years younger than MH participants (65.4 vs. 68.9 years), despite carrying greater metabolic risk burden. In unadjusted analysis, there was no significant association between metabolic health status and the primary outcome (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.90–1.32; P = 0.375; Table 2). However, adjustment for age alone increased the HR from 1.09 to 1.35, accounting for nearly the entire shift observed between unadjusted and fully adjusted models (HR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.12–1.66; P = 0.002). After full multivariable adjustment, MUH remained independently associated with higher CVD risk (HR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.03–1.57; P = 0.024). Age-stratified analyses were consistent: within-stratum HRs for MUH versus MH were 1.28 (95% CI, 0.91–1.80) for participants < 65 years, 1.24 (95% CI, 0.87–1.76) for those 65–74 years, and 1.40 (95% CI, 1.02–1.91) for those ≥ 75 years, each exceeding the unadjusted estimate of 1.09 and confirming that age confounding had masked the true association (Supplementary Table 1).

Among secondary outcomes, MUH was independently associated with higher risk of stroke (HR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.02–2.26; P = 0.041), but not with all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, heart failure, or cardiovascular death (Table 2). In post hoc analyses, elevated triglycerides (HR, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.02–2.38) and low HDL-C (HR, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.05–2.56) were each significantly associated with incident stroke, whereas impaired fasting glucose and obesity were not. In mutually adjusted models, point estimates were attenuated and no individual component reached statistical significance, suggesting that the stroke association reflects the combined burden of atherogenic dyslipidemia rather than any single metabolic abnormality (Supplementary Table 3).

Effect of intensive blood pressure treatment by metabolic health status

Intensive blood pressure lowering significantly reduced the risk of the primary outcome in both metabolic health groups (Table 3). Among MH participants, intensive treatment reduced CVD risk by 24% (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.59–0.97; ARR, 1.65%; NNT, 61). Among MUH participants, intensive treatment reduced CVD risk by 27% (HR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.54–0.97; ARR, 1.77%; NNT, 56). There was no evidence of effect modification by metabolic health status (P-interaction = 0.854).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

The association between metabolic health status and the primary outcome was consistent across alternative definitions (Supplementary Table 2). Using a stricter threshold of ≥ 3 abnormalities, the association was strengthened (HR, 1.58; 95% CI, 1.22–2.05), consistent with a dose-response relationship. Using a lower BMI threshold of ≥ 25 kg/m2, the association was attenuated and non-significant (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.89–1.33), likely reflecting greater misclassification. An intermediate BMI threshold of ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 yielded intermediate results (HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.98–1.48). The absence of treatment effect modification was consistent across all definitions (all P-interaction > 0.70).

The risk of the primary outcome in MUH compared with MH participants was generally consistent across subgroups and treatment arms, with no significant 3-way interactions observed for age, sex, race/ethnicity, baseline CKD, or BMI (all P-interaction ≥ 0.10) (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

In this secondary analysis of SPRINT including 7,299 hypertensive participants without baseline CVD or diabetes, metabolic dysfunction was independently associated with higher CVD risk, though this association was only apparent after adjustment for age. Intensive SBP lowering reduced CVD risk similarly in both metabolic health groups, with modestly greater absolute benefit in MUH individuals.

A key finding was that the association between metabolic risk burden and incident CVD was not apparent without age adjustment. MUH participants were 3.5 years younger than their MH counterparts (65.4 vs. 68.9 years; P < 0.001), and the unadjusted HR of 1.09 (P = 0.375) increased to 1.35 with age adjustment alone. This negative confounding suggests that younger individuals with multiple metabolic abnormalities may represent a distinct phenotype with more aggressive pathophysiology [13,14]. This interpretation is consistent with literature on early-onset metabolic syndrome, which has been associated with accelerated vascular aging and heightened long-term cardiovascular risk [15,16,17,18]. Without adequate age adjustment, the prognostic significance of metabolic health in older hypertensive populations may be substantially underestimated.

In the general population, metabolic syndrome is associated with an approximate 2-fold increase in cardiovascular events and 1.5-fold increase in all-cause mortality [19,20]. Prior analyses of SPRINT and The Antihypertensive and Lipid-Lowering Treatment to Prevent Heart Attack Trial (ALLHAT) have confirmed that metabolic syndrome confers higher cardiovascular risk independent of blood pressure [21,22]. However, these studies included participants with prevalent CVD, which may confound the relationship between metabolic health and incident events. Our analysis extends these findings to a primary prevention population and highlights the critical role of age adjustment in unmasking the true metabolic health–CVD relationship. Notably, because SPRINT excluded individuals with diabetes, the MUH group in our analysis predominantly represents subclinical metabolic dysfunction (prediabetes and dyslipidemia) rather than fully manifested metabolic syndrome. The finding that even the lesser degree of metabolic burden confers independently elevated CVD risk emphasizes the clinical relevance of identifying subclinical metabolic abnormalities in hypertensive patients.

A central question of this analysis was whether the cardiovascular benefits of intensive SBP lowering differ by metabolic health status. We found that intensive treatment reduced CVD risk by 24% in the MH group and by 27% in the MUH group, with no evidence of effect modification (P = 0.854). Although relative risk reductions were similar, ARRs differed modestly: 1.77% in the MUH group compared with 1.65% in the MH group, corresponding to numbers needed to treat of 56 and 61, respectively. These differences are small but illustrate a fundamental principle of cardiovascular prevention: individuals at higher baseline risk derive greater absolute benefit from effective therapies, even when relative risk reductions are comparable [23]. In settings where treatment intensification carries costs or risks, prioritizing patients with higher baseline risk may optimize the efficiency of preventive interventions.

Importantly, participants in the MH group also derived statistically significant and clinically meaningful benefit from intensive blood pressure lowering (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.59–0.97). Metabolic health status alone should not be used as a basis for withholding intensive blood pressure targets from these patients. The consistent benefit across both groups supports the broad application of intensive SBP targets in high-risk hypertensive adults regardless of metabolic status [24].

The pathophysiological mechanisms linking metabolic dysfunction to CVD include chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and arterial stiffness [25,26]. These pathways could attenuate the benefits of blood pressure lowering if they operate independently of hemodynamic stress. Alternatively, they could augment benefits if MUH individuals have greater pressure-dependent vascular injury. Our findings do not support either hypothesis. Instead, they suggest that the hemodynamic benefits of blood pressure reduction are preserved regardless of underlying metabolic status.

This conclusion aligns with prior analyses such as SPRINT and ALLHAT, that found no treatment interaction using the traditional metabolic syndrome definition [21,22,27]. Our findings confirm that this conclusion holds in a primary prevention cohort. Sensitivity analyses using alternative metabolic health definitions further supported this conclusion (all P-interaction > 0.70). The consistency across trials and classification approaches strengthens confidence in the broad applicability of intensive blood pressure targets regardless of metabolic status [3,28,29].

Among secondary outcomes, MUH status was independently associated with incident stroke (HR, 1.52; 95% CI, 1.02–2.26; P = 0.041). No significant associations were observed for myocardial infarction, heart failure, or cardiovascular death. The specific association with stroke, while based on a limited number of events and requiring cautious interpretation, is noteworthy and consistent with prior literature linking metabolic syndrome components to cerebrovascular disease [30,31,32]. To explore which metabolic components drove this association, we examined each component individually and in mutually adjusted models. Elevated triglycerides and low HDL-C were each significantly associated with stroke risk when modeled separately, with stroke rates approximately 60% higher in individuals with these abnormalities. Impaired fasting glucose and obesity were not significantly associated with stroke. In mutually adjusted models, point estimates for triglycerides and HDL-C were attenuated and no longer statistically significant, likely reflecting shared pathophysiological pathways of atherogenic dyslipidemia and the limited number of stroke events (n = 116). These findings suggest that the risk of stroke associated with metabolic dysfunction is primarily attributable to dyslipidemia rather than dysglycemia or obesity. This is consistent with proposed mechanisms including accelerated atherosclerosis of the carotid and intracranial vasculature, increased thrombotic tendency, and endothelial dysfunction affecting cerebral perfusion [33].

This finding should be considered hypothesis-generating given the limited number of secondary outcome events; nonetheless, it suggests that MUH hypertensive patients may warrant heightened attention to stroke prevention, with particular focus on management of atherogenic dyslipidemia. Whether metabolic health status should inform additional stroke prevention strategies beyond established risk factor management remains to be determined. Notably, intensive blood pressure lowering reduced stroke risk in both metabolic health groups, underscoring the importance of optimal blood pressure management in this population.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this was a secondary analysis of a randomized trial not specifically designed to test effect modification by metabolic health status. Our findings should be interpreted within this context. Second, we used a modified definition of metabolic syndrome that substituted BMI for waist circumference due to data availability. Although BMI and waist circumference are correlated and both predict cardiovascular risk, waist circumference more specifically captures central adiposity [34,35]. Our classification may have resulted in some misclassification of metabolic health status, potentially underestimating associations with central adiposity-driven cardiovascular risk. Because indices of abdominal obesity may be stronger discriminators of cardiovascular risk factors than BMI, this limitation warrants particular consideration when interpreting our findings [36]. Third, metabolic health was assessed at a single baseline timepoint. We were unable to evaluate whether changes in metabolic status during follow-up influenced outcomes or treatment response. Fourth, we excluded participants with baseline CVD to focus on primary prevention. Our findings may not apply to secondary prevention populations. Fifth, despite the relatively large sample size, subgroup analyses may be underpowered to detect modest interactions. The absence of statistically significant effect modification should not be interpreted as proof that no interaction exists. Lastly, SPRINT enrolled older, high-risk hypertensive adults under trial conditions with close follow-up, frequent medication titration, and standardized protocols. In routine clinical practice, adherence may be lower and adverse effects less promptly identified and managed. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution when considering extrapolation beyond the SPRINT population.

CONCLUSIONS

In this secondary analysis of SPRINT, hypertensive adults without baseline CVD, MUH status was associated with higher risk of incident cardiovascular events independent of traditional risk factors. Despite this elevated baseline risk, both MH and MUH individuals derived similar relative benefit from intensive blood pressure lowering, with modestly greater absolute benefit in MUH participants. Although the relatively limited number of clinical events and use of a modified metabolic health classification warrant caution, these findings suggest that intensive blood pressure treatment targets may reduce cardiovascular risk regardless of metabolic health status. Confirmation in larger, prospective studies using standard metabolic syndrome definitions with waist circumference is warranted.
