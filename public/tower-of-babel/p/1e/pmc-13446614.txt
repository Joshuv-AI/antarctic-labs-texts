Nutritional adequacy and diet diversity following a tube weaning program
Authors: Maisa Keski‐Kuha, Josefine Limnell, Mari Ahlroos, Päivi Peldan, Laura Merras‐Salmio, Jetta Tuokkola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLimited research has examined nutritional changes following tube weaning. The main objective of this study was to investigate the adequacy and quality of nutrition, growth, and gastrointestinal symptoms in children undergoing a 3‐week outpatient tube weaning program based on hunger provocation.MethodsPatient records from all children treated with a tube weaning program at Helsinki University Children's Hospital between May 2016 and May 2024 were reviewed retrospectively for nutritional intake, gastrointestinal symptoms, and growth.ResultsThe study included 51 patients (median age: 2.7 years [interquartile range, IQR 2.1–4.2]). Median duration of tube feeding before program was 2.4 years (IQR 1.9–3.3). In the last available follow‐up, 37/51 (72.5%) of the children were successfully weaned off tube feeding. After the program, the median proportion of total energy intake derived from regular food and drinks increased from 6.2% to 77.2%, and the median energy derived from tube feeding decreased from 92.9% to 0.0% (p < 0.05 for all). Dietary diversity increased during follow‐up, and a decrease in gastrointestinal symptoms was observed. After weaning, the intake of several vitamins and minerals from regular food alone did not meet the recommended levels and required supplementation.ConclusionsOur tube weaning program was effective in decreasing tube dependency, improving oral intake, and increasing dietary diversity. To ensure adequate energy, vitamin, and mineral intake, a sufficiently long‐term follow‐up after weaning and appropriate dietary supplementation are essential to prevent potential deficiencies. Securing growth and nutritional status requires a regular evaluation of dietary adequacy by a nutrition specialist.

INTRODUCTION

Adequate nutrition is essential for supporting healthy growth and development in children. Various chronic conditions and prolonged hospital stays, unpleasant medical procedures, and recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms may lead to long‐term eating disturbances. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4

Tube feeding is an effective and safe way to support the nutritional needs and growth of a chronically ill child. 2 , 3 However, prolonged use of tube feeding may lead to dependency, despite the resolution of the underlying medical condition. 5 , 6 , 7 Tube weaning programs apply different approaches to help children transition from tube feeding to oral intake. The effectiveness and safety of these programs have been increasingly studied. 6 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 An average of 67%–69% of children participating in various weaning programs successfully discontinue tube feeding. 13 In tube weaning programs based on the Graz model (hunger provocation), success rates reach 80%–90%. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

Evidence on the effectiveness of tube weaning programs is growing, whereas research on dietary quality and adequacy post‐weaning is limited. Previous studies have examined the proportion of energy derived from tube feeding versus oral intake, 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 the number of different foods consumed before and after the tube weaning, 26 , 28 , 29 and the adequacy of total energy and protein intake. 30 However, evidence regarding the adequacy and quality of children's nutrition before and after the tube weaning is lacking.

The objectives of this study were to assess the adequacy and quality of nutrition, growth, and gastrointestinal symptoms in pediatric patients who participated in a hunger provocation‐based tube weaning program.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the Helsinki University Hospital Ethics Committee. Due to the retrospective chart‐review setting, informed consent was not required.

Weaning and research procedure

Our tertiary pediatric hospital provides a multidisciplinary family‐included tube weaning program, based on hunger provocation, since 2016. The child's sense of hunger is increased by gradually reducing the amount of tube feeding, while simultaneously increasing the child's experience of autonomy in eating. By organizing meal sessions, children, their carers, and the multidisciplinary feeding team share a picnic‐like meal together during weekdays. The objective is to promote self‐initiated exploration of foods among children and to reduce food‐related fears and aversions.

The tube weaning is carried out in a 3‐week program with 4–5 children per group. Tube feeding is discontinued within the first 3 days. Somatic well‐being is closely monitored throughout the weaning process. The multidisciplinary team consists of pediatric gastroenterologists, pediatricians, dietitians, speech and language therapists, and nurses.

Medical records from children attending the tube weaning program at Helsinki University Children's Hospital between May 2016 and May 2024 were reviewed up to November 30, 2024. Hospital patient records, growth charts, and dietetic software were reviewed for information on demographic background factors, onset of weaning, age at the start and duration of tube feeding, type of tube at the time of weaning, previous swallowing difficulties, previous tube feeding and parenteral nutrition, as well as growth and nutrition data before and after the tube weaning program. Data were collected at three points: Pre‐weaning (pre), 0–1 month before the start of the program; Follow‐up 1 (F1), approximately 1 month after the program; and Follow‐up 2 (F2), at the most recent comprehensive nutritional data available. In addition, the latest available data on growth and weaning outcome were collected.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Before the program, patients are clinically assessed for suitability and the safety of tube weaning. Eligible children have stable medical conditions, committed families, and their oral eating is considered safe. We excluded patients who discontinued the program early, received parenteral nutrition throughout the program, or had been weaned from tube feeding prior to the program.

Measurements

Weaning outcome

For the present study, patients were considered weaned if no energy was administered via tube after finishing the program. Children who received medication and/or no energy‐containing fluids through the tube were considered weaned.

Nutritional measurements and gastrointestinal symptoms

Nutritional data were collected from medical records, including the amount and type of tube feed (mL/day and kcal/day), special diets, micronutrient supplements, oral nutritional supplements (ONS), and/or high‐energy foods or drinks, number and frequency of different types of foods and food groups consumed, meal frequency, and gastrointestinal symptoms.

The number of foods consumed was counted at each time point. The food groups examined were vegetables and fruits, cereals and potato, dairy products, protein sources, and dietary fats. Information about gastrointestinal symptoms was collected from patient records before, during, and after the weaning.

Families filled out 3‐day food diaries before and approximately 1 month after the program and at follow‐up visits. The availability of food diaries varied, and the number of days covered ranged between 1 and 4 days. All food diaries and the calculations already made from them were collected, carefully reviewed, corrected for database errors when needed, and analyzed again. Food diaries were calculated using nutrition calculation softwares AivoDiet version 2.2.01 (Mashie FoodTech Solutions Finland Oy) and AromiDiet version 14.11.4.6 (CGI) using a national database of foods and nutrients. The patients' energy and nutrient intakes were assessed and compared between “total intake” (which covered all oral nutrition, ONS and tube feeding) versus intake from “regular food alone.”

If the child did not complete a food diary, the patient records were checked for a detailed description of a dietary recall. The assessment was made in collaboration with three researchers (M.K.K., J.T., and M.A.). The patient's nutrition was calculated based on patient records (1) if the child was exclusively tube‐fed and the exact amount of tube feeding was recorded or (2) if the text contained a detailed description of a previously returned food diary. Otherwise, the patient's nutritional intake was not calculated. Nutritional data based on food diaries and all available sources were analyzed separately and compared.

The estimated energy requirement was determined based on each child's age and weight according to Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2012 (NNR 2012), 31 and this was compared with the energy intake calculated from the dietary records. Macronutrient and fiber intakes were compared with recommendations 31 and micronutrient intakes were compared with age‐specific reference values from the NNR 2012 and expressed as percentages of the recommended daily intakes. 31

Growth parameters

Height‐for‐age standard deviation (SD) scores and weight‐for‐height percentage were used to evaluate growth. 32 We also calculated age‐ and sex‐specific ISO‐BMI values, based on the International Obesity Task Force criteria. 32 , 33 , 34

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, Version 29 (IBM Corp.). Normality of the variables was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Results were presented using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired continuous variables and McNemar test for paired nominal data were used for statistical analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Between May 2016 and May 2024, 13 tube weaning groups were organized. Of the participating 57 children, 51 were included in this study (Table 1). Two children participating in 2020 completed the final week of treatment at home via remote connection because of the COVID‐19 pandemic; their follow‐up matched that of the other participants. Six children did not meet the study inclusion criteria and were excluded: one child discontinued the program after 3 days, two children received parenteral nutrition throughout the program, and three children had been weaned before the start of the program.

Weaning outcome

Immediately after the program, 34/51 (66.7%) of the children successfully weaned off tube feeding; two of these returned to partial tube feeding shortly after the program. In the first follow‐up, 33/51 (64.7%), in the second follow‐up, 34/49 (69.4%), and in the last available follow‐up, 37/51 (72.5%) of the children were successfully weaned. Weaning was most successful in premature children and those with GI abnormalities (88.9%) and least successful in children with neurodevelopmental diagnoses (50.0%; Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

After the weaning, a total of five children received energy once or twice through a feeding tube when the child was ill. These five children were considered weaned.

Nutrition

Nutritional data based on food diaries and all available sources were analyzed separately and compared. There were no significant differences between these two, thus we used all available nutritional data in our analysis. Dietary records were available for 46, 31, and 33 patients at pre, F1, and F2 timepoints, respectively.

After the program, tube feeding volume (mL/day) and energy intake via tube (kcal/day) decreased significantly from pre‐weaning to F1 and F2 (p < 0.001). Median daily tube feed volume declined from 640 mL (IQR 488–870 mL) before weaning to 0 mL (IQR 0–250 mL) at F2 (p < 0.001). Similarly, median daily energy intake via tube decreased from 900 kcal (IQR 720–1080 kcal) to 0 kcal (IQR 0–340 kcal) (p < 0.001). The proportion of children using ONS was 31.4% before weaning and 36.7% at F2 (p = 0.648 for difference). The use of high‐energy drinks and foods (17.6% vs. 71.4%, p < 0.001) and micronutrient supplements (66.7% vs. 93.8%, p = 0.004) increased between pre‐weaning and F2.

At all time points, energy, fiber, and macronutrient intakes from regular food alone were significantly lower than total intakes (p < 0.001) and showed an increase from pre‐weaning to F1 and F2 (p < 0.001; Supporting Information S1: Table S2). Between F1 and F2, energy and fiber intake from regular food continued to increase (p < 0.05). The median proportion of energy intake obtained orally from regular food and drinks increased from 6.2% at pre‐weaning to 77.2% at F2, while the median proportion of energy derived from tube feeding decreased from 92.9% at pre‐weaning to 0.0% at F2 (p < 0.05 for all; Supporting Information S1: Table S3).

Between pre‐weaning and F2, total energy (1023 vs. 1258 kcal/day; p = 0.05) and protein (2.2 vs. 2.7 g/kg; p = 0.015) intakes increased (Figure 1) and fat (39.8 vs. 36.1 E%) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (9.7 vs. 6.8 E%) decreased (p < 0.05 for both, Supporting Information S1: Table S2). No significant differences were observed in total carbohydrate or saturated fatty acids (SAFA) intakes between time points (Supporting Information S1: Table S2).

Total intakes of most micronutrients decreased when tube feeding was reduced. Gastrointestinal symptoms

The frequency of vomiting decreased during and after the program compared to baseline, as did mealtime vomiting and gagging (p < 0.001; Supporting Information S1: Tables S8 and S9), and reflux symptoms (p = 0.039). Vomiting was likewise reduced in those who continued tube feeding (p = 0.031).

Growth

The median weight loss during the 3‐week program was −6.8% (IQR − 8.4% to −3.9%). Of the children, 7/51 (13.7%) lost more than 10% of their baseline weight. The median height SD decreased from pre‐weaning to F2 (−1.5 vs. −1.7 SD; p = 0.045, Supporting Information S1: Table S10). The children's median weight‐for‐height and ISO‐BMI values decreased between pre‐weaning and all follow‐up points (p < 0.001; Supporting Information S1: Table S10), although they stayed within the normal range.

DISCUSSION

We found that after the tube weaning program children's energy and macronutrient intake showed a consistent upward trend, indicating a steady improvement in oral feeding. However, micronutrient supplementation was necessary.

After weaning median oral energy intake from regular food and drinks increased significantly. Previous studies have assessed the adequacy of oral energy intake relative to children's calculated energy needs, with similar findings: before weaning, intake was 14.6%–30%; immediately after, 63.4%–85%; and at final follow‐up, 85%–92% of estimated needs. 19 , 21 , 23 , 24 In their meta‐analysis, Killian et al. 13 reported a 64% average increase in oral energy intake, after weaning programs.

In our study, the median proportion of total energy intake derived from tube feeding decreased between pre‐weaning and both follow‐ups (92.9%–0.0%). A similar trend was observed in the study by Stokes et al., 26 where energy intake via feeding tube decreased between pre‐weaning and last follow‐up (90.0%–0.0%). Comparable results have also been reported in two additional studies. 22 , 25

We found that children's total energy intake remained adequate throughout follow‐up (≥100%), whereas Sadeh‐Kon et al. 30 reported a significant drop during a 3‐week weaning program (80% vs. 56%). After 1 year intake returned to baseline but remained below 100% of estimated needs. 30 We also found that median protein intake stayed adequate, consistent with Sadeh‐Kon et al., 30 the only program that has previously reported children's pre‐ or post‐weaning protein intakes.

To our knowledge, no previous study has reported pre‐ or post‐weaning intakes of micronutrients. In our study, the total intake of nearly all micronutrients decreased after the discontinuation of tube feeding (Figure 2). During follow‐up, appropriate supplementation contributed significantly to ensuring adequate intakes. Despite a significant increase in micronutrient intake from regular food, median intake remained below recommended levels for several nutrients at the final follow‐up. This finding highlights the need for long‐term follow‐up and post‐weaning supplementation to prevent potential deficiencies.

The macronutrient intakes remained within recommended ranges after weaning, except for the median intake of SAFAs, which were consistently high throughout the follow‐up. Similarly, in a large cross‐sectional study of healthy Finnish children, the diet contained too much SAFAs. 35 The decrease in total PUFA intake (Supporting Information S1: Table S2) reflects the decrease in consumption of commercial enteral tube feeds, which are high in PUFAs; after weaning, children received PUFA‑rich foods in varying amounts, comparable to healthy Finnish children. 35 Children's fiber intake remained low throughout the follow‐up, and most children were using medication for constipation. Effective management of constipation, choosing fiber‐rich foods, and ensuring adequate fluid intake are essential. 36

We found that children's dietary diversity increased significantly after the weaning program. A similar result was observed in the study by Stokes et al., 26 where the proportion of children consuming more than 30 different foods increased significantly from the baseline (4.9%) to the 1‐year follow‐up (81.5%). Two other studies have reported similar findings on dietary diversity. 28 , 29

Our study was the first to examine changes in the variety and frequency of foods across food groups. Both increased, also reflected in micronutrient intakes. By the last follow‐up, median intakes of vitamin B12, niacin, vitamin K, and phosphorus from regular food were adequate, reflecting higher consumption of animal‐based foods. The largest increase was in cereals, contributing to selenium, copper, and manganese intake. These trends mirror the Finnish diet's impact on children's nutrient intake.

ONS and high‐energy foods and drinks helped to ensure adequate energy intake after weaning. For many, regular food alone did not meet estimated energy needs even at the last follow‐up. Healthy children also show variations in day‐to‐day energy intake, occasionally falling below estimated needs. 37 , 38 Similarly, Kyttälä et al. 35 reported that while most micronutrient intakes met recommendations among healthy Finnish children, vitamin D, vitamin E, and iron were often inadequate.

We found that the occurrence and frequency of vomiting and gagging decreased during and after the program, consistent with previous studies. 22 , 29 , 36 , 39 However, after weaning 17.6% of children still had increased vomiting and 23.5% constipation, similar to findings by Blinder et al. 36

The median weight loss in our study was comparable to previous studies. 20 , 22 , 30 , 40 Weight increased during follow‐up, but median height SD declined, a trend also noted by Krom et al. 40 who found that 44% of weaned children were malnourished after 1 year. In our cohort, severe underlying conditions and comorbidities likely slowed growth, possibly compounded by illness‐related starvation or eating disorders, despite adequate energy and protein intakes. In young children, recovery from malnutrition and catch‐up growth can raise energy needs, 41 , 42 though evidence is limited. Our findings underscore the importance of long‐term follow‐up and regular nutritional assessment.

Immediately after the program, 66.7% of children were weaned off tube feeding; by final follow‐up, 72.5% maintained success, consistent with prior meta‐analyses. 9 , 13 We found that even children not fully weaned showed reduced tube‐feeding and vomiting, and greater dietary diversity, reflecting improvements in eating skills. These improvements, also noted in other studies 20 , 21 , 23 , 24 , 30 and highlighted in the meta‐analysis by Sharp et al., 9 are significant for the quality of life of both children and their families.

This study has limitations that may affect evidence quality: food diary returns and recorded days varied, some estimates relied on dietitian assessment, and random variation may affect representativeness. Data size and follow‐up duration differed, and complete nutritional data were not available for all participants. As a retrospective chart review, data came from routine clinical care rather than research collection.

Future studies should prospectively examine the adequacy and quality of nutrition in children weaned from tube feeding.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our outpatient hunger‐provocation based tube weaning program enabled successful weaning for many children, improving oral intake, reducing gastrointestinal symptoms, and increasing dietary diversity. Long‐term follow‐up and supplementation remain essential to support growth and prevent deficiencies, as most children require high‐energy foods and/or micronutrient supplements. Even partial weaning improves eating skills and gastrointestinal symptoms. Oral eating often continues to progress beyond intervention. Future nutritional monitoring requires a more rigorous protocol and longer follow‐up.
