Osteoclast-derived exosomes promote medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw by delivering glycoRNA to disrupt the blood–lymphatic vascular network
Authors: Jiankang Zhang, Junming Tao, Chengzhi Zhao, Jian Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeMedication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is a metabolic disorder of the jaw characterized by bone necrosis. Its pathogenesis remains unclear, and elucidating the underlying mechanisms and pathological changes is essential for addressing the clinical challenge of refractory jaw necrosis in MRONJ. Glycosylated RNA (glycoRNA), a recently identified class of small RNAs modified by glycosylation, plays important roles in intercellular communication, signal transduction, and autoimmunity; however, its role in the onset and progression of MRONJ has not yet been investigated.Materials and methodsExosomes derived from zoledronate-induced osteoclasts (OCZA-Exosomes) were isolated by ultracentrifugation. GlycoRNA was visualized using Ac4ManNAz metabolic labeling combined with Northern blot analysis. Scratch assays, Transwell migration assays, tube-formation assays, EdU staining, and TUNEL staining were performed to evaluate the effects of OCZA-Exosomes on the proliferation, migration, and tube-forming capacity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs). In MRONJ model rats, the impact of OCZA -Exosomes on jaw necrotic lesions was further assessed.ResultsGlycoRNA was shown to undergo intercellular transfer via exosomes. In vitro, OCZA- Exosomes inhibited the proliferation, migration, and tube-forming abilities of HUVECs and HLECs, and induced apoptosis. In MRONJ model rats, local injection of OCZA-Exosomes into the tooth extraction sockets disrupted the blood–lymphatic vascular network within the jaw tissue and accelerated the progression of jaw necrosis.ConclusionThese findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which zoledronate contributes to MRONJ: exosomes-mediated intercellular transfer of glycoRNA disrupts the blood–lymphatic vascular network and promotes jaw necrosis.Keywords: Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw(MRONJ), Exosomes, Blood—lymphatic vascular network, GlycoRNA

Introduction

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) refers to a jaw metabolic disorder and bone necrosis caused by the use of bisphosphonates (BPs),1 anti-angiogenic agents,2 or denosumab3 for conditions such as malignant bone metastasis and osteoporosis. According to the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS),4 the typical clinical presentation includes exposed necrotic bone or a fistula that probes to bone within the oral cavity and persists for more than eight weeks. Bisphosphonates are currently first-line agents for anti-osteoporosis and anti-tumor therapy, and they remain the most common medications associated with the development of MRONJ. Owing to their strong affinity for bone, bisphosphonates bind to hydroxyapatite and are subsequently internalized by osteoclasts, thereby inhibiting osteoclast activity and disrupting the homeostasis between bone formation and resorption in the jaw.5 On the other hand, bone tissue in MRONJ lesions exhibits pronounced microvascular destruction, 6 which constitutes the pathological basis for persistent infection and non-healing necrosis. However, it remains unclear how osteoclasts, after internalizing bisphosphonates, contribute to microvascular damage.

As a fundamental unit of metabolic circulation within tissues and organs, the blood–lymphatic vascular transport network plays an essential role in fluid circulation and waste clearance. While the coupling between osteogenesis and angiogenesis is now well recognized as a key process in bone regeneration,7 far less is known about the relationship between bone tissue and the lymphatic system. Using light-sheet imaging combined with functional genetic approaches, Biswas et al.8 were the first to demonstrate the presence of lymphatic vessels within bone tissue and to further establish their critical role in bone regeneration. However, current research on MRONJ remains largely focused on the anti-angiogenic effects of bisphosphonates,9 and the role of the blood–lymphatic vascular network in the development and progression of MRONJ is still poorly understood. In particular, how osteoclasts, after internalizing zoledronate, regulate the fate of lymphatic vessels within the jawbone has not yet been investigated.

GlycoRNA is a class of small RNAs recently discovered to be widely present in mammalian cells and modified by glycans,10,11 primarily localized on the cell surface. GlycoRNA is broadly involved in immune regulation and intercellular communication. Sharma et al.12 have demonstrated that glycoRNA can be transferred between cells via exosomes. In human cells, glycosylated proteins and lipids are abundant, and these glycosylated biomolecules participate in the regulation of protein stability and folding, cell adhesion, and signal transduction.13 These observations suggest that glycoRNA may also function as a signaling molecule, modulating physiological and pathological processes through intercellular communication. The pathological basis of MRONJ is jawbone necrosis driven by excessive inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation. Previous studies focusing on protein- and miRNA-related immunoregulatory mechanisms have been unable to fully elucidate the specific etiology of MRONJ. The work of Huang et al. not only demonstrated the stable presence of RNA on the cell membrane surface but also revealed that such RNA widely participates in intercellular communication and immune regulation, with glycosylation being essential for the stable existence of this membrane-associated RNA. Therefore, whether glycoRNA represents a previously overlooked novel mechanism in the pathogenesis and progression of MRONJ remains unclear. In MRONJ, it remains to be further investigated whether osteoclasts, after internalizing bisphosphonates, transfer glycoRNA via exosomes to mediate disruption of the blood–lymphatic vascular network within bone tissue, thereby contributing to persistent necrosis characteristic of MRONJ.

In this study, we isolated exosomes derived from zoledronate-treated osteoclasts and demonstrated that glycoRNA can be transferred via exosomes between osteoclasts and vascular or lymphatic endothelial cells, mediating the disruption of the blood–lymphatic vascular network during the progression of MRONJ lesions.

Materials and methods

Osteoclast induction and exosomes isolation and characterization

Femurs were harvested from 7-week-old rats, and the bone marrow cavities were flushed to collect the lavage, which was left in 6-cm culture dishes overnight. The supernatant was collected, lysed for red blood cells, and counted. Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured in osteoclast-induction medium consisting of α-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Every Green, Hangzhou, China), 1 % penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, USA), 50 ng/mL M-CSF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and 50 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems). The medium was replaced every 2 days until numerous multinucleated cells were observed under the microscope, indicating mature osteoclast formation. At this point, the control group (Ctrl) medium was replaced with exosomes-depleted medium (SBI, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and supernatants were collected after 48 h. For the ZA (ZA) group, cells were treated with 10 μmol/L ZA (CSNpharm, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) in exosomes-depleted medium for 48 h before supernatant collection. Exosomes were isolated following standard ultracentrifugation procedures, sterilized by filtration, and subsequently used for downstream experiments.

Cell culture

Immortalized human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were used as vascular endothelial cells in this study. HUVECs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in 5 % CO2. Human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs, Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in endothelial cell medium supplemented with 5 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % penicillin–streptomycin, and 1 % endothelial cell growth supplement.

Metabolic labeling with Ac4ManNAz

Ac4ManNAz was dissolved in sterile dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 500 mM stock solution.14 For cell experiments, a final working concentration of 100 μM was applied at 37 °C for 2 h. Metabolically labeled cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Copper-free click chemistry, RNA gel electrophoresis, and blotting

Biotin-modified dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO-biotin, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was mixed with RNA, reacted at 55 °C for 10 min, followed by the addition of 80 μL DEPC water. RNA binding buffer and 100 % ethanol were then added at 2× and 3× volumes, respectively. Purification was performed using Zymo spin columns, and the purified RNA was used for subsequent gel electrophoresis.12

The concentration of ManNAz-labeled RNA was measured prior to Northern blotting. RNA samples were loaded onto pre-stained 1 % agarose-formaldehyde denaturing gels (Northern Max Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and electrophoresed at 75 V for 30 min. Total RNA was visualized under a UV gel imaging system to ensure equal loading and RNA integrity. Following electrophoresis and quality verification, Northern blotting was performed according to the Northern Max protocol. RNA was transferred onto 0.45 μm nitrocellulose membranes for 2 h and crosslinked under UV light. Membranes were blocked with Odyssey blocking buffer for 30 min and incubated with streptavidin-IR800 (Li-Cor Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) in hybridization buffer for 30 min. After hybridization, membranes were washed three times with PBST and visualized using an imaging system.

MRONJ rats model

All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University and performed following humane practices to minimize suffering. A total of 24 adult female Sprague–Dawley (SD) (Dossy, Chengdu, China) rats weighing 300–350g were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 8 in each group). Rats in the model group received intraperitoneal injections of ZA (0.2 mg/kg; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) and dexamethasone (2 mg/kg; Solarbio, Beijing, China) dissolved in saline twice per week for 8 consecutive weeks.15,16 The first left maxillary molars were extracted under anesthesia with Avertin (Biotopped, Beijing, China). Extraction sockets were immediately filled with GelMA hydrogel loaded with 100 μg of total exosomal protein of OC-Exo, OCZA-Exo, or OCZA-Exo/NGI-1, and further packed with gelatin sponge. Half of the rats were sacrificed at 2 and 4 weeks postextraction, and maxillae were harvested. Specimens were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h and decalcified in 10 % EDTA, with solution changes twice per week until complete decalcification.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with hydrogen peroxide. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer at 70 °C, followed by blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA). Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against CD31 (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), LYVE1 (1:200, Cell Signaling Technology (CST), Danvers, MA, USA), VEGF-A (1:200, CST), and VEGF-C (1:200, CST). The following day, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 60 min at room temperature, followed by DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. TUNEL (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and TRAP (Beyotime) staining were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained sections were washed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin, mounted, and imaged under a bright-field microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as individual data points and bar graphs as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to assess differences among three groups. P < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Results

Induction of osteoclasts and the isolation and characterization of exosomes

Bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) induced with MCSF and RANKL were subjected to TRAP staining and Factin/DAPI staining. A large number of mature osteoclasts were observed under the microscope on day 6 (Fig. 1A), characterized by intact and continuous cell membranes and multiple nuclei distributed within the membrane, indicating that the osteoclasts had reached a functionally mature stage. Therefore, osteoclasts induced for 6 days were selected for exosomes isolation.

The supernatants from normally cultured osteoclasts and zoledronic acid (ZA) treated osteoclasts were collected separately, and OC-derived exosomes (OC-Exosomes, OC-Exo) and zoledronate-induced OC–derived exosomes (OCZA-Exosomes, OCZA-Exo) were isolated using ultracentrifugation. Characterization of the two types of exosomes showed that both exhibited the typical “saucer-like” morphology with a diameter of approximately 100 nm under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 1B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) demonstrated that the peak particle sizes of both exosomes populations were below 200 nm (Fig. 1C). Notably, ZA–treated osteoclasts secreted a markedly higher quantity of exosomes, suggesting that ZA may influence bone homeostasis by enhancing osteoclast-mediated paracrine activity. Western blot analysis revealed positive expression of exosomal marker proteins CD63, Tsg101, and CD81 in both exosomes preparations (Fig. 1D).

OCZA-Exo mediated intercellular transfer of glycoRNA

We first examined the uptake capacity of HUVECs and HLECs toward OCZA-Exo (Fig. 2A). After co-incubating Dil-labeled OCZA-Exo with the two types of endothelial cells, confocal microscopy revealed (Fig. 2B and D) that both cell types began internalizing exosomes as early as 1 h, followed by progressive intracellular accumulation. Even after 20 h of co-culture, distinct exosomes signals remained detectable around the perinuclear region. Northern blot analysis of total RNA extracted from co-cultured cells demonstrated (Fig. 2C and E) the presence of glycoRNA in both endothelial cell types, with the strongest signal detected at 1 h, followed by a gradual decrease, yet still detectable at 20 h. Finally, treatment with the glycoRNA-specific inhibitor NGI-1 significantly suppressed glycoRNA synthesis (Fig. 2F) in a dose-dependent manner. NGI-1 also effectively reduced glycoRNA levels within exosomes (Fig. 2G), thereby blocking the exosomes-mediated intercellular transfer of glycoRNA.

OCZA-Exo inhibits endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation

Exosomes were isolated from the supernatants of ZA–treated osteoclasts and zoledronic plus NGI-1–treated osteoclasts, with exosomes derived from normally induced osteoclasts serving as controls. Upon co-culture with HUVECs, OCZA-Exo exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 3A and B). At the same time points, the migration area of HUVECs in the scratch assay was significantly smaller in the OCZA-Exo group compared with the control group. Similarly, in Transwell migration assays (Fig. 3C and D), the number of migrated HUVECs was markedly reduced in the OCZA-Exo group. Further assessment of HUVECs tube formation in vitro (Fig. 3E and F) revealed that cells in the OCZA-Exo group failed to form well-defined tubular structures. Notably, NGI-1 treatment abolished the inhibitory effects of OCZA-Exo on HUVECs. Moreover, increased apoptosis and reduced proliferation were observed in HUVECs treated with OCZA-Exo (Fig. 3G–J), which may be related to glycoRNA acting as a cell-surface receptor to trigger apoptotic responses.

OCZA-Exo inhibits alveolar socket healing in a MRONJ-like rat model

H&E staining revealed (Fig. 5A and C) that at 2 weeks postextraction, the extraction sockets in the OCZA-Exo group remained clearly discernible, filled with fibrous connective tissue and exhibiting marked inflammatory infiltration. Numerous empty lacunae were observed in the surrounding alveolar bone, indicating that local fluid circulation had not been effectively re-established. In contrast, extraction sockets in the NGI-1 group showed signs of localized remodeling, with no obvious inflammatory infiltration within the sockets. These findings suggest that inhibition of glycoRNA formation can significantly mitigate the adverse effects of ZA on socket healing. At 4 weeks post-extraction, persistent inflammatory infiltration was observed within the extraction sockets of the OCZA-Exo group, resulting in low levels of fibrous connective tissue mineralization. The surrounding alveolar bone exhibited continuous empty lacunae and numerous irregular basophilic deposition lines, indicating disordered bone mineralization.

Masson’s trichrome staining at 2 weeks post-extraction (Fig. 5B and D) showed that the alveolar bone surrounding the sockets in the OCZA-Exo group lacked evident vascular and lymphatic structures, suggesting disrupted local fluid circulation and severely impaired osteogenic activity. At 4 weeks post-extraction, histological sections revealed localized areas of necrotic tissue within the alveolar bone, indicating severely impaired socket healing, compromised blood–lymphatic circulation, and progression of bone tissue toward necrosis.

OCZA-Exo mediates disruption of the angiogenic–lymphatic network and promotes MRONJ

Immunohistochemical analysis of mandibular sections at 4 weeks post-extraction revealed that the expression levels of vascular markers VEGF-A and CD31 (Fig. 6A and C), as well as lymphatic markers LYVE1 and VEGF-C (Fig. 6B and D), were significantly reduced in the alveolar bone surrounding the extraction sockets of the OCZA-Exo group compared with the control group. Treatment with NGI-1 partially alleviated this inhibitory effect.

Additionally, the number of apoptotic cells in the alveolar bone surrounding the extraction sockets was markedly increased in the OCZA-Exo group (Fig. 6E). Numerous apoptotic cells were observed within necrotic bone and its surrounding tissues, whereas such apoptosis was absent in the control and NGI-1 groups. Finally, only a few osteoclasts were detected in the alveolar bone surrounding the sockets in the OCZA-Exo group (Fig. 6F), indicating a significant suppression of osteoclastic activity. In contrast, the control and NGI-1 groups exhibited a higher number of osteoclasts, demonstrating that the normal balance between bone formation and resorption was maintained in these groups.

Discussion

With the ongoing global aging trend, the prevention and treatment of age-related skeletal disorders have become increasingly challenging. Bisphosphonates, as first-line drugs for the treatment of osteoporosis17 and the prevention of bone metastases in malignant tumors,18 are being administered to a growing population. The expanding patient population and the increasing potency of these drugs have contributed to a rising incidence of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ), a severe adverse effect of bisphosphonates. Currently, there is no effective treatment for MRONJ.19 Surgical removal of necrotic bone rarely promotes lesion healing, and persistent fistulas lead to recurrent infections and secondary bone necrosis. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenesis and pathological changes of MRONJ is critical for addressing the clinical challenges associated with its management.

MRONJ is widely considered to result from suppressed bone remodeling20 and angiogenesis,9 creating a susceptible condition that, combined with triggers such as tooth extraction, leads to disease onset. Osteoclasts play a central role in maintaining bone homeostasis by secreting exosomes that mediate intercellular communication with endothelial cells and osteoblasts.21–23 Zhang et al.24 reported that exosomes secreted by osteoclasts after internalizing ZA inhibit endothelial cell proliferation and migration, as well as osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stem cells. These findings suggest that the anti-angiogenic and anti-bone remodeling effects of ZA can be propagated via exosomes. However, the specific molecular mediators responsible for this intercellular signaling remain unclear. Sharma et al.12 demonstrated that glycosylation may be a key process for selectively loading RNA into exosomes, highlighting a potential role for glycoRNA in intercellular communication. The biogenesis of exosomes involves complex intracellular sorting mechanisms, in which the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) plays a crucial role. Sharma et al. inhibited the activity of Hepatocyte Growth Factor-regulated Tyrosine Kinase Substrate (HGS), a key component of this process, using Manumycin A (MA), thereby disrupting ESCRT-dependent exosome formation. This intervention further resulted in a significant decrease in glycoRNA content within exosomes. These findings suggest that targeting the intracellular sorting mechanisms of exosomal cargo represents a potential novel approach for immunomodulation in MRONJ. However, the specificity of such targets and the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. Our study shows that exosomes secreted by ZA-treated osteoclasts can transfer glycoRNA to both vascular and lymphatic endothelial cells. Although the precise role of this exosomes-mediated, glycoRNA-dependent signaling in MRONJ remains to be fully elucidated, our findings indicate that it may contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis.

The anti-resorptive effects of bisphosphonates and their associated anti-angiogenic activity have attracted considerable attention in MRONJ research.25–27 Allen et al.28 reported significant accumulation of necrotic bone in the mandibles of animals treated with alendronate for 3 years. In an in vitro 3D angiogenesis model, Pabst et al.29 observed strong inhibition of microvascular sprouting by bisphosphonates. Borke et al.,6 using vascular cast micro-CT, confirmed that bisphosphonate treatment alters mandibular vascular architecture in rats, with a significant reduction in vascular connectivity, indicating the negative effects of bisphosphonates on the vasculature in vivo. Our study demonstrates both in vitro and in vivo that ZA significantly suppresses angiogenesis. Importantly, this inhibitory effect is mediated by the exosomes-dependent intercellular transfer of glycoRNA, and that treatment with NGI-1, an inhibitor of glycoRNA formation, can effectively block these negative effects.

The vascular–lymphatic network plays a critical role in systemic fluid circulation.30,31 However, the existence of lymphatic vessels within bone has long been debated, and their role in MRONJ has been largely overlooked. Only after Biswas et al.8 demonstrated the presence of lymphatic vessels in bone and their importance in bone regeneration did attention begin to focus on the vascular–lymphatic network in skeletal diseases. In the present study, we investigated changes in lymphatic vessels in MRONJ. In vitro, exosomes derived from ZA–treated osteoclasts exerted a strong inhibitory effect on human lymphatic endothelial cells (HLECs), manifested as decreased proliferation, migration, and tube formation, along with a significant increase in apoptosis. Similarly, in alveolar bone surrounding extraction sockets in MRONJ rats, the expression levels of lymphatic markers LYVE1 and VEGF-C were markedly reduced. Consistent with our previous findings, NGI-1 blocked the transmission of these inhibitory effects. While prior studies have primarily focused on vascular inhibition in MRONJ, the importance of lymphatic vessels has been largely neglected. Our results not only reaffirm the inhibitory effect of bisphosphonates on angiogenesis but also reveal the role of bisphosphonate-mediated disruption of the vascular–lymphatic network in MRONJ, filling a knowledge gap regarding lymphatic involvement in disease pathology.

This study has several limitations. First, while it demonstrates the regulatory role of RNA glycosylation in bisphosphonate-induced osteonecrosis of the jaw, the specific type(s) of RNA involved were not identified. Second, although our findings suggest that exosome-mediated transfer of glycoRNA contributes to MRONJ progression―and that inhibition of glycoRNA formation by NGI-1 can block this effect―the broad inhibitory effect of NGI-1 on glycosylation may also influence protein glycosylation processes. Future studies will require innovative technical approaches to dissect the distinct contributions of RNA glycosylation versus protein glycosylation in the pathogenesis of MRONJ.

In summary, our study demonstrates that glycoRNA derived from ZA–treated osteoclasts can be transferred intercellularly via exosomes. This exosome-mediated signaling disrupts the vascular–lymphatic network in the jawbone, leading to impaired angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis, and further contributes to the development and progression of MRONJ.
