Preoperative Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Brain Metastases: A Phase 1 Dose Escalation Study Demonstrating Reduced Leptomeningeal Disease
Authors: John M Hoyle, James M Markert, Kristen O Riley, Christopher D Willey, Samuel R Marcrom, Richard A Popple, Markus Bredel, John B Fiveash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePreoperative stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) has been proposed as a strategy to reduce nodular leptomeningeal disease (nLMD) after resection of brain metastases by devitalizing tumor cells before surgical manipulation. This study aimed to determine the safety of preoperative SRS via a phase 1 dose escalation trial and compare outcomes—including nLMD, classical LMD, local control, and overall survival (OS)—between preoperative and postoperative treatment cohorts.Methods and MaterialsA phase 1 trial evaluated the safety of single-fraction preoperative SRS at escalating doses (12 and 15 Gy) in patients with tumors 2 to 6 cm. Adverse events were assessed per National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0, with dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) defined as high-grade neurologic or wound complications. Retrospective analysis included an expanded preoperative cohort (n = 95) and a historical postoperative cohort (n = 107). Outcomes were analyzed with Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsFifteen Gy was well tolerated in the phase 1 cohort, with no DLTs in larger tumors (4-6 cm) and 3 DLTs in smaller tumors, not meeting thresholds for dose de-escalation. In the expanded analysis, preoperative SRS significantly reduced rates of nLMD (7.4% vs 27.1%, P = .002), while rates of classical LMD (4.2% vs 4.5%) and local failure (14.6% vs 18.7%) were similar between groups. OS was also similar (median 12.8 vs 12.3 months). Multivariable analysis confirmed preoperative SRS as protective against nLMD (hazard ratio = 0.18, 95% CI, 0.07-0.43, P < .001).ConclusionsPreoperative SRS to 15 Gy is safe for tumors 2 to 6 cm and significantly reduces nLMD without compromising local control or OS. These findings support preoperative SRS as a viable treatment strategy and justify further investigation into optimal dosing and patient selection.

Introduction

Metastatic disease within the brain has historically been a life-limiting condition with extremely poor long-term prognosis. Over the past several decades, advancements have made significant strides in treatment for this condition, with a combination of surgery and radiation providing the best outcomes for large, resectable tumors.1,2 Initially, whole-brain radiation therapy was the preferred radiation paradigm, but postoperative stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) has been shown to provide similar local control with improved cognitive function outcomes.3 Surgical resection of brain metastases provides rapid symptomatic improvement but has been associated with increased rates of leptomeningeal disease (LMD), thought to be because of seeding during resection.4, 5, 6, 7 Increased prevalence of postsurgical SRS has been complicated by increased rates of LMD, likely as a result of the leptomeninges not being covered in the treatment volumes for SRS.8 Rates of LMD with surgery and postoperative SRS have been found to be as high as 30% in some series.9 However, more recently distinctions have been drawn between true dissemination to the leptomeninges termed classical lemptomeningeal disease (cLMD) and spread to the pachymeninges termed nodular leptomeningeal disease (nLMD).9,10

Preoperative SRS has been proposed as a mechanism to decrease rates of postoperative nLMD by devitalizing tumors before possible seeding in surgery. This approach also provides a practical advantage with the easier target delineation and the theoretical possibility of reduced radiation dose, but there are limited data on the optimal dose for preoperative SRS. Previous studies have shown that postoperative doses of 15 to 19 Gy in a single fraction provided excellent local control.11, 12, 13 For large treatment volumes, 15 Gy in a single fraction has been observed to be well tolerated.14, 15, 16

The present study was conceptualized and run as a phase 1 dose escalation study to determine the safety of treating brain tumors preoperatively with SRS. Once the prospective recruitment was accomplished, additional patients were treated preoperatively with single-fraction SRS in regular practice. These additional patients were added to the phase 1 cohort for retrospective comparison to a database of patients treated postoperatively. Together, these cohorts provided an opportunity to compare rates of cLMD, nLMD, local control, and overall survival (OS) between the 2 treatment orders.

Methods and Materials

Patient selection

This study was performed under the oversight of the Institutional Review Board at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. For the phase 1 dose escalation trial of preoperative radiation (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT01252797), patients aged 19 or older were required to have histologically confirmed malignancy with a new diagnosis of brain metastases. Patients were allowed to have up to 4 brain metastases by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed within 6 weeks of study entry. At least 1 tumor must have been 2 to 6 cm in maximal diameter and deemed appropriate for surgical resection. Karnofsky performance status of 60 or greater and life expectancy of 12 weeks or greater were required. Exclusion criteria consisted of patients with small cell lung cancer, lymphoma, more than 4 metastases on MRI, prior whole-brain radiation therapy, and insufficient recovery from prior therapies. Pregnant and nursing women as well as subjects deemed to be poor surgical candidates were also not included.

The prospective data set was subsequently supplemented by reviewing all patients treated with our institution’s preoperative SRS dose, 15 Gy in a single fraction, and selecting those who were treated preoperatively with SRS since the initiation of the original phase 1 study. This total preoperative cohort was compared against an updated version of a previously published historical cohort of postoperative SRS.17

Dose escalation

The phase 1 trial was a dose escalation study with 3 possible dose levels all to be given in a single fraction. These dose levels were 10 Gy (level 0), 12 Gy (level 1), and 15 Gy (level 2). Recruited patients were divided into 2 groups based on the size of the index tumor (group A: 2-4 cm, group B: 4-6 cm). Patients in group A were assigned initially to level 2 and patients in group B were started at level 1. For each dose level, 3 patients were to be recruited and assessed for dose-limiting toxicity (DLT). DLT was defined as any grade 4 or 5 neurologic or dermatologic event as well as any grade 3 or higher seizure, irreversible somnolence, edema complications, or wound complications occurring within 30 days of surgery. Following this initial group, if no patients experienced DLT, then the dose level was considered safe for escalation if appropriate. If there was a single DLT in the first 3 patients, another set of 3 patients was recruited. Again, if no dose-limiting toxicities were observed, then the dose level was considered safe and escalation could be considered if appropriate. However, if more than a single DLT was observed in the first 6 patients or if there were 2 or more DLTs in the first 3 patients, then the dose for this group was de-escalated. Dose escalation beyond level 2 was not permitted.

Treatments

Patients who were surgical candidates and met the inclusion criteria for the study were enrolled at the appropriate dose level per study protocol. Computed tomography simulation was obtained and fused to the planning MRI. Gross tumor volume was drawn to include contrast-enhancing index tumor as visualized on planning MRI, with any additional abnormality on computed tomography included. There was no additional margin added. Dose was prescribed to the minimum isodose line that covered gross tumor volume, which was typically the 40% to 85% line. All preoperative patients were treated via linear accelerator-based radiosurgery with a Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy technique. Our institutional guidelines for this process have been published previously.18

The details of the postoperative historical cohort have also been previously published,17 but these patients were treated on a mix of linear accelerator-based radiosurgery as well as gamma knife treatments. There was also a mix of single fraction plans ranging from 10 to 20 Gy as well as hypofractionated courses of 5 fractions with a total dose of 25 Gy or 30 Gy. The target volume included the cavity and optionally 1 to 3 mm of adjacent dura.

Surgery was completed within 30 days after preoperative radiation. Gross total resection (GTR) was obtained whenever possible. GTR was defined as no evidence of residual tumor after surgical resection as reported on the operative note and postoperative MRI interpretations.

Follow-up

Patients had an MRI on postoperative day 1, which helped to determine the extent of resection. Patients typically received follow-up clinical examination and repeat MRI 1 month after surgery. Standard practice from there would be repeated imaging and follow-up every 2 to 3 months unless earlier follow-up was clinically indicated.

Endpoint definitions and statistical methods

cLMD was defined as focal or diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement of the brain, spinal cord, cauda equina, or cranial nerves consistent with subarachnoid involvement, while nLMD was defined as enhancement of the pachymeninges that was greater than 5 mm from the index metastasis or other previously treated tumors. These definitions were chosen to be similar to those established by Cagney et al.10 Each case of LMD was confirmed by multiple authors, and 2 examples are shown in Fig. 1. Local failure was similarly defined as the development of new nodular contrast-enhancement within 5 mm of the resection cavity. Local recurrences were also confirmed by multiple authors. OS was calculated from the time of surgery to the date of death or the last clinical follow-up. Publicly available records and the medical chart were used to identify patient survival. Toxicities were graded according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0.

Abbreviations: cLMD = classical leptomeningeal disease; nLMD = nodular leptomeningeal disease.

Because the majority of patients considered between the 2 patient cohorts were analyzed retrospectively, various characteristics were evaluated to account for potential differences at baseline. These characteristics included the number of lesions at the time of treatment, the size of the index lesion, the primary disease histology, the location of lesions, the preoperative involvement of the dura, and the extent of surgery. The number of intracranial metastases at the time of treatment was dichotomized between patients with a single metastasis at the time of evaluation and those with multiple lesions. Additionally, the size of the index metastasis was dichotomized for patients with lesions less than or equal to 4 cm and those with lesions greater than 4 cm to be consistent with the group A and B definition on the protocol.

Baseline characteristics were compared between preoperative and postoperative groups, with continuous variables evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test and categorical variables compared with the χ2 test. Leptomeningeal disease rate (cLMD and nLMD), local control, and OS were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method, measured from the initiation of treatment, with estimates between groups. Multivariate models for nLMD, cLMD, local control, and OS were constructed using the Cox proportional hazard model and evaluating factors such as histology, size of index lesion, number of metastases at time of treatment, location of tumor, presence of preoperative involvement of the leptomeninges, and extent of resection. Statistical tests were performed using R version 4.4.1 in RStudio.

Results

Dose escalation

For this phase 1 dose escalation study investigating preoperative radiation, 20 patients were enrolled between January 2011 and December 2015. Three patients were recruited to group B and treated with 12 Gy with no toxicity attributable to radiation. Over the course of the study, 6 additional patients were treated in group B with 15 Gy. None of them experienced adverse effects attributable to radiation within the prespecified study cutoff of 1 year. The breakdown of grade and category of adverse effects between the dose levels is shown in Table 1. For the group A patients, there were 3 DLTs noted with 2 grade 3 wound infections and 1 grade 4 altered mental status following surgery with associated edema. The frequency of these toxicities did not meet the criteria for dose de-escalation. Patient-level breakdown of adverse events is shown in Table 2. There was a single grade 5 toxicity experienced by a patient with metastatic mesothelioma. This patient experienced respiratory compromise leading to death on postoperative day 6 and was excluded from further analysis and replaced. There was also a single patient in group B who sustained grade 4 radionecrosis after a single course of radiation to 15 Gy that was not considered a DLT because of occurring more than 1 year following treatment. Finally, a second patient in group A received a second course of SRS to a dose of 17 Gy with direct overlap to the previous treatment bed because of local recurrence 7 months after initial preoperative 15 Gy SRS. This patient did go on to develop radiation necrosis 2 and a half years after the initial course.

Cohort comparison

In order to investigate whether preoperative radiation improves the rate of LMD after surgery and if there is any difference in local control based on the order of treatment, a retrospective analysis of preoperative patients treated at this institution was performed and compared to a previously published postoperative cohort. Including the 20 preoperative patients from the phase 1 study, 95 patients were treated between study enrollment in January 2011 and August 2023. The postoperative cohort included 107 patients. The median follow-up time for both cohorts was 9 months. The most common histology in both cohorts was non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (35.8% preoperative; 46.7% postoperative), followed by melanoma (20.0% preoperative; 15.9% postoperative) and breast cancer (17.9% preoperative; 11.2% postoperative). Both cohorts had the majority of patients with solitary lesions at the time of treatment (66.3% preoperative; 68.2% postoperative) as well as more patients with tumors less than 4 cm (74.7% preoperative; 64.5% postoperative). Fifty-nine percent of patients had only supratentorial disease in the preoperative group, while this number was 60.7% for postoperative patients. Enhancement of the dura was present in 77.9% of patients at the time of treatment in the preoperative cohort and 66.4% of the time in the postoperative cohort. Based on postoperative MRI, most patients achieved GTR (86.3% preoperative; 75.7% postoperative). There were no significant differences between the groups (Table 3). Both cohorts were evaluated for development of radionecrosis by date of death or last clinical follow-up with a single incidence in the postoperative cohort who received gamma knife-based SRS to a dose of 16 Gy to the 51% isodose line. There were no additional cases beyond the 2 identified in the phase I component for the preoperative cohort.

Leptomeningeal disease

In the retrospective cohort of patients treated preoperatively with SRS, 4 of 95 patients (4.2%) developed cLMD, and 7 developed nLMD (7.4%). The postoperative comparison cohort had 5 (4.5%) and 29 (27.1%) patients develop cLMD and nLMD, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier curves for these cohorts are shown in Fig. 2. Log-rank comparison between groups demonstrated a significant difference between groups for nLMD (P = .002) but not for cLMD (P = .79). At 12 months, there was already a significant difference between rates of nLMD free survival with 90% versus 61.3% (P < .001). Multivariable analysis of the groups showed that tumors of unknown primary origin were significantly associated with cLMD compared to NSCLC (hazard ratio [HR] = 42.89; 95% CI, 1.69-1091; P = .02). Notably, there were only 4 patients of unknown primary. Increasing number of intracranial mets at the time of treatment was significantly associated with the rate of nLMD (HR = 1.15; 95% CI, 1.03-1.28; P = .01), and preoperative SRS was protective against the development of nLMD (HR = 0.18; 95% CI, 0.07-0.43; P < .001).

Local failure

In the preoperatively treated patient cohort, 14 out of 96 (14.6%) patients developed local failure. This is compared against 20 out of 107 (18.7%) in the postoperative cohort. Kaplan-Meier curves are shown in Fig. 2 as well. The log-rank comparison between groups with respect to local failure did not demonstrate a significant difference (P = .39). On multivariable analysis, an increasing number of metastases at the time of treatment was associated with increased risk of local failure (HR = 1.30; 95% CI, 1.09-1.54: P = .003). Primary cancer type of “other” approached a significant association with increased risk of local failure (HR = 2.71; 95% CI, 0.95-7.75; P = .06) relative to the reference group of NSCLC. This group included 21 patients with a variety of primary tumors, the most prevalent of which were ovarian primary tumors and esophageal adenocarcinoma with 3 patients each.

Overall survival

The median OS for the entire cohort was 12.4 months, where the postoperative cohort had a median OS of 12.3 months compared to 12.8 months in the preoperative group. There was no significance between groups regarding survival with the Kaplan-Meier curve shown in Fig. 2 and nonsignificant log-rank comparison (P = .19). Multivariable analysis demonstrated that metastases in both supratentorial and infratentorial locations at the time of treatment were significantly associated with decreased survival compared to supratentorial metastases only (HR = 3.11; 95% CI, 1.79-5.42; P < .001), while preoperative involvement of the dura was associated with prolonged survival (HR = 0.62, 95% CI, 0.42-0.90; P = .01). The median survival time from diagnosis of cLMD was 1.6 months versus 5.3 months for nLMD.

Discussion

Currently, the standard of care for resected brain metastases is postoperative SRS to the resection cavity.19 Preoperative radiation is included as a consideration, but additional evidence is needed to demonstrate benefit. Practical benefits of preoperative radiation include easier target delineation and the opportunity to treat tumor cells prior to potential seeding of the pachymeninges. However, preoperative timing of radiation does raise a logistic challenge in coordinating treatment between service lines. For patients who are acutely symptomatic, delaying resection of brain metastases for simulation and treatment planning is not reasonable; however, there is evidence demonstrating the feasibility of the preoperative approach for the majority of well-selected patients.20 The present study further contributes to the body of literature demonstrating the tolerability and efficacy of treating large brain tumors with preoperative radiation and defines a prescription dose of at least 15 Gy preoperatively as safe in tumors up to 6 cm in diameter.

The phase 1 study of dose escalation for patients treated preoperatively with SRS demonstrated that doses of 15 Gy were not associated with significant adverse effects for tumors 2 to 6 cm. Among group B patients, there were no DLTs related to radiation at either level 1 or 2 within the prespecified study endpoint of 1 year, suggesting that 15 Gy is safe in this population. There were 3 DLTs noted on the study, of which 2 were wound infection and the third was altered mental status because of cerebral edema in the immediate postoperative setting. The frequency of these events did not meet the prespecified criteria for de-escalation and therefore does not raise significant concern for the safety of treatment. Notably, there were no adverse effects because of poor wound healing, which has been hypothesized to be a complication of preoperative radiation because of vascular disruption. Good postoperative wound outcomes have also been observed in other preoperative SRS studies with consistently low rates of wound complications.20, 21, 22 The lone grade 5 toxicity among protocol patients occurred in a patient who unfortunately experienced respiratory failure because of mesothelioma and died on postoperative day 6, completely unrelated to her radiation. In total, these results suggest that preoperative 15 Gy in a single fraction is a safe dose for tumors 2 to 6 cm in maximal dimension for follow-up of at least 1 year.

In the longer term, radiation necrosis would be the most significant expected side effect. Our prospective cohort had 2 patients develop radiation necrosis. One of these cases was complicated by a local recurrence that required an additional course of radiation to 17 Gy, which confounds drawing conclusions about the safety of 15 Gy alone. There was a single incidence of radiation necrosis in a phase I patient who had a tumor 5.4 cm in diameter treated. Notably, there were 8 other patients with tumors over 5 cm in diameter treated preoperatively, but only 2 survived longer than 6 months. Further data would be needed to definitively determine whether there would be unacceptable rates of radiation necrosis in this population at longer follow-up intervals.

Malignant spread to the leptomeninges is a serious condition that can be difficult to diagnose. In an attempt to prevent underdiagnosis in our cohort, we employed a somewhat generous definition of LMD, which was consistent between retrospective cohorts and first described by Freilich et al.23 We further subdivided the identified patients into those exemplifying cLMD and nLMD to be in keeping with more recent developments suggesting that these entities behave differently.9,10 In this setting, preoperative SRS would be expected to reduce rates of nLMD without impacting cLMD, because direct seeding of the pachymeninges would necessarily predispose toward nLMD. The results of the analysis do indeed support this finding, with no observed difference between cohorts for rates of cLMD versus a highly significant reduction in rates of nLMD with preoperative SRS. This does support the hypothesis that preoperative radiation makes tumor cells nonviable before potential seeding of the pachymeninges and could provide clinical benefit to a significant proportion of patients.

Other characteristics that have been identified as risk factors for LMD in the postoperative treatment of brain metastases include multiple intracranial metastases at the time of treatment, breast histology, and infratentorial tumor location.2,7,24 None of these factors proved to be significantly associated with cLMD on multivariate analysis in our cohort; however, an increasing number of intracranial metastases was associated with nLMD. No additional factors were associated with nLMD, but “unknown” histology was statistically associated with cLMD. This finding likely represents the aggressive nature of dedifferentiated tumors without a clear primary site. This finding is also susceptible to scrutiny because of only 4 patients within this category. Notably, these results are limited based on the low number of events in the preoperative treatment setting and warrant further investigation in the future.

While 15 Gy was demonstrated to be well tolerated and effective at reducing nLMD, this is a lower dose than typically given in the postoperative setting; therefore, it was important to evaluate whether lower dosing would have a detrimental impact on local control. There was no significant difference between the rates of local failure between the groups. Multivariable analysis did show that an increasing number of metastases within the brain at the time of treatment was associated with worse local control. The number of metastases was also identified as a risk factor for local recurrence on the dedicated postoperative series that was previously published.17 Interestingly, the type of tumor did approach significance but instead of breast cancer as the higher risk histology, it was tumors labeled as “other” (not NSCLC, breast, melanoma, colon, or renal cell carcinoma). Given the low numbers of each individual cancer type within this category, it is difficult to take much prognostic value from this result, but it may be better evaluated in light of future results. Our local recurrence rate was in keeping with historical cohorts at ∼15%. Interestingly, Agrawal et al25 found superior local control with 15 Gy in a single fraction to tumors up to 5 cm, although half of the treated metastases were in the 0 to 2 cm group. A potential alternative method to improve local control is to use hypofractionation for preoperative treatment. This requires additional time for treatment delivery before surgery, but has shown some promising results to date.26 The question of optimal dosage for preoperative radiation continues to be investigated. A recent dose escalation study beyond 15 Gy was published by Murphy et al21 and showed tolerability of 21 Gy for tumors 2 to 4 cm and 18 Gy for tumors 4 to 6 cm. Additionally, the International Collaboration of Neoadjuvant SRS for Brain Metastases study did show excellent rates of local control and LMD failure with a median dose of 18 Gy; however, the median tumor volume was 10.7 cc.3,27 Given that multiple series have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of doses up to at least 18 Gy in smaller tumors, escalation should be strongly considered. Whether dose escalation will lead to better local control and acceptable toxicity in larger tumors remains a question to be investigated by further studies.

OS was not different between the preoperative and postoperative cohorts which likely reflects viable salvage options with radiation.10 The only factor that was significantly related to decreased OS on multivariable analysis was having both supratentorial and infratentorial disease. This may represent more widespread disease within the brain at the time of treatment; however, the number of metastases was not associated with OS. Further research would be needed to validate this finding. Interestingly, preoperative dural involvement was associated with prolonged survival in our analysis. There is no obvious reason for this, because preoperative contact with the dura has been considered a negative prognostic factor in the past17 and additional research would be needed to further characterize this interaction between OS and tumor touching dural surfaces before treatment.

This study does have a number of limitations. A notable 1 is the low number of patients enrolled in a prospective manner. Even those enrolled prospectively were part of a phase 1 study, which by its very nature had a narrow scope. The retrospective comparison between preoperative and postoperative cohorts was potentially susceptible to sampling differences because of when patients were treated, differences in treatment modality and fractionation, and potential differences in patient characteristics. This was mitigated by analysis showing that there was no significant difference in patient characteristics at baseline. Additionally, because the majority of patients were evaluated retrospectively, there was no protocol-specific follow-up, which may have contributed to shorter follow-up data for some patients. The diagnosis of LMD (both nodular and classical) and local failure was reviewed by multiple authors for every case, but there is necessarily some amount of ambiguity in the radiographic diagnosis. The broad categorization of LMD to include any leptomeningeal enhancement distant from the treatment site was adopted to help mitigate underrecognition of LMD and subsequently separated into nLMD and cLMD to more appropriately represent the clinical scenario. The NRG-BN012 is a phase III study that is currently accruing to compare preoperative versus postoperative SRS for patients with brain metastases with the composite primary endpoint of local failure, radiation necrosis, and nLMD, which may help to shed additional light on many of the findings of the present study.

Based on this study, preoperative treatment of brain metastases between 2 cm and 6 cm with single fraction treatment to 15 Gy is well tolerated and safe for at least 1 year. Retrospective analysis of preoperative versus postoperative SRS showed improved nLMD free survival without any difference in local control, cLMD, or OS. Optimal dosing and potential for hypofractionation are matters for future investigation, but preoperative SRS for brain metastases should be considered for appropriate patients.
