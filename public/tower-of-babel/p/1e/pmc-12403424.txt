Total knee arthroplasty for ankylosed knees in the extended position with hemophilia: a medium- and long-term single-center experience
Authors: Zhongyi Zhang, Yi Tang, Zhaokai Jin, Lei Chen, Xinyu Hu, Yichen Gong, Shineng Lin, Guoqian Chen, Shuaijie Lv, Peijian Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the curative methods for patients with end-stage ankylosed knees. However, the existing methods are difficult to enable patients with hemophilic ankylosed knees to recover good functional activities.MethodsBetween January 2008 and December 2021, 22 male patients with ankylosed knees in the extended position due to hemophilia underwent TKA. The average age of the patients was 41.3 years (range, 19 to 52 years), and the average hospital stay was 33.4 days (range, 14 to 50 days). TKA was performed using quadriceps tendon Z-lengthening plasty, staged osteotomy, and comprehensive soft-tissue release. A variety of methods were used to evaluate the postoperative clinical outcomes and complications, including range of motion (ROM) assessment, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Knee Society Score (KSS), and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC).ResultsAt the last follow-up, the average KSS increased from 75.14 ± 34.63 before surgery to 148.41 ± 27.74 (P < 0.001), the ROM increased from 0 to 86.14 ± 24.69 (P < 0.001), the WOMAC score changed from 28.05 ± 11.20 before surgery to 10.82 ± 11.00 (P < 0.001), and the average VAS score decreased from 2.86 ± 1.86 to 0.73 ± 1.20 (P < 0.001).ConclusionAlthough the recovery of range of motion is lower than normal and there is a relatively high incidence of complications, TKA can still significantly improve the flexion and extension functions, mobility, and quality of life of patients with ankylosed knees.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03102-5.

Introduction

Hemophilia is a rare X-linked genetic disorder characterized by recurrent bleeding into joints, which can eventually lead to hemophilic arthropathy [1]. As hemophilic arthropathy (HA) progresses, patients experience persistent pain, loss of joint mobility, and progressive deformities, which significantly impair their quality of life [2]. In some severe cases of HA, factors such as joint fibrosis, soft tissue contracture, and excessive epiphyseal growth can ultimately lead to hemophilic ankylosed knee osteoarthritis. Ankylosed knee is defined as having a range of motion of 0 degrees, characterized by minimal functional loss in the extended position but severe restriction in flexion. This condition poses significant challenges to patients'daily lives and social integration. Radiographic examination typically shows Arnold-Hilgartner stages IV-V, with severe narrowing and disruption of the joint space [3]. For patients with ordinary ankylosed knees in the extended position, the high complication rates, particularly skin necrosis, and improper surgical techniques have imposed extremely high demands on total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [4, 5]. In patients with hemophilia, tendon contractures, joint stiffness, and inflammatory rigidity are more pronounced. These conditions not only impair patients'quality of life but also impose a significant societal burden [6].

The complex conditions of joints, soft tissues, and coagulation dysfunction pose significant difficulties and challenges for the implementation of TKA [7]. With advancements and widespread adoption of TKA techniques, combined with modern rehabilitation methods, an increasing number of patients with hemophilic ankylosed knees are undergoing TKA. However, controversies and challenges remain regarding surgical exposure, soft tissue release and balancing, as well as precise osteotomy, which require further investigation and discussion.

There is still a lack of high-quality clinical studies discussing the surgical techniques for TKA in hemophilic ankylosed knees. Additionally, the postoperative outcomes and satisfaction for this type of surgery remain suboptimal [8]. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective analysis to evaluate the clinical efficacy and functional outcomes of TKA using our standardized “Three-Balance” protocol combined with Z-lengthening quadriceps release in patients with hemophilic ankylosed knees. The specific aims were to (1) quantify improvements in range of motion, pain, and functional scores, (2) detail reproducible surgical criteria and decision-making algorithms, and (3) assess complication profiles and mid-to-long-term implant survival. By doing so, we aim to provide clear, evidence-based recommendations for the management of this challenging patient population.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study retrospectively analyzed cases from the Orthopedic Trauma Center database of Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2008 and December 2021. All patients had read and signed informed consent forms.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Hemophilic arthropathy with significant functional impairment; (2) Ankylosed knees with a range of motion (ROM) of 0, including patients with visible joint space on X-rays but an ROM of 0; (3) Arnold–Hilgartner stage IV–V; (4) Patients undergoing unilateral TKA during a single hospital admission.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Knee extensor muscle strength less than grade II; (2) Presence of coagulation factor inhibitors; (3) Patients with severe organic diseases of the heart, liver, lungs, or kidneys; (4) Patients with recent systemic infections; (5) Patients with psychiatric or psychological disorders and poor treatment compliance; (6) Patients in the acute bleeding phase.

The cases were independently reviewed by two authors; disagreements were adjudicated by a third. Twenty-two patients met all criteria. Pre-operative and follow-up data at 6, 12 and 24 months postoperatively, were recorded by blinded assessors. Variables included ROM, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Knee Society Score (KSS) and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC). Due to the retrospective nature of our cohort, the Pettersson Score and Haemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS) were not applied; we elected to use KSS, WOMAC, and VAS as the primary outcome measures. Complications such as prosthesis position, prosthesis infection, abnormal pain, and bleeding were also documented. Clinical data, X-rays, and other relevant information for each patient were stored and used for analysis. A detailed description of the patients'preoperative demographics is provided in (Table 1). Preoperative assessment and imaging

Before surgery, patients underwent a detailed physical examination and imaging evaluation. A complete preoperative medical history was collected, and preoperative ROM, VAS, KSS, and WOMAC scores were recorded. Preoperative imaging assessments consisted primarily of standing, full-length, weight-bearing hip-to-ankle radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans to evaluate alignment and overall joint anatomy. Only in a minority of cases—when both radiographs and CT scans failed to resolve extensive fibrosis or ossification—was magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) obtained strictly as an adjunct to confirm residual soft-tissue planes and collateral ligament integrity.

Alignment planning and digital templating

All long-leg radiographs were imported into digital templating software to establish the mechanical axis (hip-knee-ankle angle) and calculate the individualized femoral valgus cutting angle. Intraoperatively, an intramedullary femoral alignment guide was set to the templated valgus angle, and an extramedullary tibial guide was used to reproduce the planned tibial mechanical axis.

Surgical technique

We employ the “Three-Balance” technique (1) “gap balance” for symmetric joint space via precise osteotomy and release; (2) “structure balance” through staged preservation and release of muscle–tendon and ligamentous structures to address fibrosis; and (3) “dynamic balance” by intraoperative tendon-bone tension adjustment—to optimize exposure, stability, and function in hemophilic arthropathy.

Patients were positioned supine, with a tourniquet applied after exsanguination. All cases used a midline skin incision with a medial parapatellar approach. Dissection was performed through the subcutaneous tissue using electrocautery until the patella and quadriceps tendon were exposed. During patellar eversion, efforts were made to preserve patellar bone, maintaining a thickness of approximately 2.5 cm. When the patella was too thin, part of the femoral condyle bone was allocated to the patella to ensure the integrity of the extensor mechanism. In knees with preoperative lateralized patellae, we limited patellar eversion and performed a controlled lateral retinacular release, preserving medial retinacular fibers to maintain tracking and reduce the risk of avulsion or maltracking complications. Additionally, appropriate release of the patellar ligament attachment was performed to avoid avulsion fractures of the patella or tibial tuberosity.

The anteromedial capsule and deep MCL were thoroughly detached from the tibia to the medial angle of the knee joint. Residual menisci and cruciate ligaments were excised. With the knee in a flexed position, the medial and lateral collateral ligaments were preserved, and initial ligament balancing was completed during the initial exposure. All patients underwent quadriceps tendon Z-lengthening plasty, staged osteotomy, and comprehensive soft-tissue release to maintain the integrity of the extensor mechanism. Specific steps: For cases where patellar eversion and knee flexion could not be achieved (Fig. 1A), the quadriceps tendon was first separated (Fig. 1B). Distal detachment combined with proximal detachment was performed to achieve lengthening (Fig. 1C, D), eventually enabling patellar eversion and knee flexion (Fig. 1E).Fig. 1Pre- and postoperative radiographs of Case 1 showing joint space restoration

The tibial bony fusion area was stripped from both sides tightly along the periosteum, extending to the popliteal fossa, with careful protection of the neurovascular bundle at the posterior popliteal region. Tibiofemoral fusion was separated using a staged osteotomy. Thorough release and balancing of the extensor mechanism, joint capsule, and collateral ligaments were performed again through the gap created by the osteotomy, converting the ankylosed knee in the extended position into a mobile joint. Secondary osteotomy and prosthesis bed preparation were then carried out.

Perioperative hematologic and pain management

All patients received a 2-week course of coagulation factor replacement therapy, with perioperative factor replacement regimens prescribed by hematologists. Factor VIII and Factor IX were administered via bolus injections to achieve appropriate preoperative levels. On the day of surgery, the peak coagulation factor concentration was maintained at 100%, followed by 60% on postoperative days 1–3, 50% on days 4–6, and 40% on days 7–10. Coagulation factor levels and coagulation parameters (PT, APTT, TT, FIB) were monitored on postoperative days 1, 4, 7, and 14.

All patients wore compression stockings and received mechanical thromboprophylaxis; no pharmacological thromboprophylaxis was used throughout the study period. Immediately after prosthesis implantation and before wound closure, a tibial-canal “cocktail” infusion was performed: 10 mL of the province-orthopedic cocktail (2 × 20 mg ropivacaine + 1 × 80 mg gentamicin + epinephrine 10 drops, diluted to 60 mL with saline) was instilled into the proximal tibial intramedullary canal. Subsequently, 30 mL of the same solution was injected periarticularly (posterior capsule, medial and lateral collateral ligaments, quadriceps tendon sheath, and subcutaneous tissues) as a periarticular multimodal injection (PMI).

Postoperative analgesia combined this local regimen with continuous periarticular infiltration of ropivacaine (0.2%, 50 mL) at wound closure, intravenous patient-controlled analgesia with sufentanil (100 µg over 48 h), scheduled oral celecoxib (200 mg twice daily) and acetaminophen (500 mg four times daily) for 7 days, plus adjunctive gabapentin (300 mg three times daily for 5 days).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

Continuous passive motion training began on postoperative days 1–3 to improve joint range of motion, along with muscle strength training, which included isometric contraction of periarticular muscles on the surgical side and isotonic or isokinetic contraction of muscles on the non-surgical side. From days 3–7 post-surgery, patients progressed to active-assisted joint mobility exercises, gradually transitioning to active joint mobility training.

Detailed information of the patient series

The NexGen Legacy Posterior Stabilized (LPS) system (Zimmer, Warsaw, Indiana) was used to evaluate joint stability. All patients underwent preoperative bone mineral density assessment by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) to quantify systemic osteoporosis; severe osteoporosis was defined as a T-score ≤ −2.5. Radiographic bone defects were identified as cortical thinning or cavitary lesions on weight-bearing knee X-rays. Extended stems or constrained condylar prostheses were reserved for those with T-scores ≤ −2.5 or with radiographically evident defects compromising implant fixation. A rotating-hinge implant was reserved only for cases with both severe bone loss (T-score ≤ –2.5) and insufficient collateral ligament support. Among this cohort, 5 knees were implanted with the Legacy Constrained Condylar Knee (LCCK) prosthesis (Zimmer, USA), 14 knees received the LPS prosthesis, and 3 knees were implanted with LPS prostheses featuring extended stems. Outcome assessment and follow-up schedule

All functional and patient-reported outcomes were gathered by two assessors (a physiotherapist for ROM and KSS measurements, and a research nurse for questionnaire administration) who were blinded to the surgical procedures and patients’ perioperative details. Assessments occurred at standardized intervals of 6-, 12- and 24-months post-surgery. Instruments included the KSS-2021 [9], WOMAC[10], VAS-pain [11], the haemophilia-specific quality-of-life measure Haem-A-QoL [12] and the generic health utility index EQ-5D-5L [13]. ROM was measured three times with a long-arm goniometer by the physiotherapist and the mean value recorded.

Statistical analysis

Primary outcomes (ROM, KSS, WOMAC, VAS, Haem-A-QoL, EQ-5D-5L, satisfaction) were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with patient as a random intercept and time (preoperative 6, 12, 24 months follow-up) as a fixed effect to account for the repeated measures and variable follow-up intervals. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD; categorical data as n (%). Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD; categorical data as n (%). Normality of residuals was inspected via Shapiro–Wilk test; when violated, non-parametric Friedman's test was applied for overall time effects, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pairwise comparisons. A priori power calculation indicated that 20 patients would provide 90% power (α = 0.05) to detect a 15° change in ROM (SD = 20°) in a repeated-measures design. Missing values (< 5%) were imputed via multivariate chained equations. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in R 4.3 (lme4, nlme) and SPSS 25.0 (IBM).

Complications occurred in 54.5% of knees and were categorized as early (≤ 30 days), mid-term (31–180 days), and late (> 180 days): early bleeding/hematoma in 3 knees (13.6%), managed with targeted factor replacement and drainage; mid-term superficial skin infection in 2 knees (9.1%), resolved with oral antibiotics; late extension lag in 1 knee (4.5%), addressed by intensive physiotherapy; and implant infection in 1 knee (4.5%), successfully treated with debridement and antibiotic cement spacer. Mixed-effects logistic regression identified age > 45 years (OR 3.2; 95% CI, 1.1–9.3; P = 0.04) and baseline ROM = 0° (OR 4.1; 95% CI, 1.2–13.8; P = 0.03) as independent predictors of early complications.

Discussion

Our retrospective series of 22 male patients with hemophilic ankylosed knees (fixed in full extension) demonstrates that total knee arthroplasty (TKA) using the novel “Three-Balance” protocol and Z-lengthening quadriceps approach can dramatically improve knee function and quality of life. Preoperatively all knees had 0° motion; at latest follow-up mean flexion was 86.1°, with Knee Society Scores rising from 75.1 (± 34.6) to 148.4 (± 27.7) (P < 0.001). This mean flexion of 86° enables patients to perform essential activities of daily living such as sitting, stair climbing, and rising from a seated position with minimal assistance, reflecting a substantial improvement in independence and quality of life. Similarly, WOMAC pain/function scores and VAS pain scores showed highly significant improvement. These gains translated into substantially better mobility and activities of daily living, as even modest flexion confers much of patients’ pre-ankylosis function. Importantly, cohort stratification into early (2008–2014) and late (2015–2021) periods with mixed-effects interaction analyses revealed no significant period × time effects for any outcome, confirming that surgeon experience did not materially influence the observed improvements and underscoring the robustness of our technique over time. While our final range of motion is lower than typical TKA in non-hemophilic knees, 86° of flexion in previously immobile knees represents a major functional restoration. In clinical terms, patients who were once essentially bedridden or housebound could now sit, stand, and ambulate with limited assistance, markedly reducing disability. Thus, the main finding is that even end-stage hemophilic knee ankylosis – long regarded as a contraindication for surgery – can be effectively treated with careful technique, yielding significant pain relief and functional improvement. These results are clinically meaningful because they demonstrate that TKA can break the cycle of pain and immobility in these otherwise irreversibly stiff joints, justifying the risk of complex surgery.

In comparing our outcomes with the literature on hemophilic TKA, several points emerge. Fenelon et al. recently meta-analyzed 1210 hemophilic TKAs and reported an average Knee Society Score (KSS) increase of 36 points (function) and HSS knee score increase of 45 points, with ROM gains of 22° [14]. Feng et al. (Chinese Med J 2023) similarly found HSS improvements of 40 points and ROM gains of 24° at 10–17 year follow-up [6]. Our cohort started from far lower baselines (ankylosed at 0°), so direct comparison is limited; nevertheless, our KSS rose by 73 points (on a 200-point combined scale) and flexion by 86°, far exceeding those nominal gains. These findings suggest that when baseline motion is zero, larger absolute gains are possible, even if residual stiffness remains. Notably, our mean postoperative flexion (86°) essentially matches the final flexion in other series of severe stiff knees. For example, Song et al. (2018) reported an average postoperative flexion of 85.6° (preop 81°) in 131 hemophilic knees [15] and Feng et al. achieved 84.8° (from 60.6° preop) [6] That our fully ankylosed patients reached similar flexion underscores the efficacy of our exposure and balancing techniques. Pain and subjective function gains in our study were also concordant with prior reports: Song et al. documented large WOMAC improvements [15], and Fenelon et al. noted that hemophilic TKRs generally relieve pain and improve patient-reported scores [14]. The consistency of these results confirms that TKA is beneficial even in hemophilic arthropathy, and our data extend this conclusion to the previously unstudied subgroup with complete ankylosis. Mechanistically, the improvement derives from removing the bony ankylosis and releasing contracted soft tissues, which instantly opens the joint. However, the relatively limited final ROM (mean 86°) also reflects the long-standing muscle fibrosis, atrophy, and joint incongruence intrinsic to hemophilia. This agrees with Ernstbrunner et al. (2017), who noted that hemophilic knees often never achieve full expected flexion due to chronic soft-tissue changes [16]. In summary, our findings align with and indeed surpass many published outcomes for hemophilic knee arthroplasty, and they demonstrate that even the most rigid hemophilic knees can attain functional flexion and score improvements comparable to other series.

The technical innovations of our approach likely underlie these good outcomes. We devised the “Three-Balance” strategy (gap balance, structural balance, and dynamic balance) in response to the challenges of severe hemophilic arthropathy. In the literature, standard TKA for stiff knees emphasizes meticulous gap balancing and exposure, but specific guidance for ankylosed knees in hemophilia is scarce. Traditional exposure techniques in stiff knees have well-known drawbacks: quadriceps V–Y plasty, while allowing lengthening, carries risks of patellar devascularization and extensor weakness; oblique quadriceps incisions and tibial tubercle osteotomies can injure the patellar tendon or fail to heal [17]. For instance, Berk et al. reported ischemic patella and tendon ruptures with V–Y plasty, and Lehane et al. highlighted tibial tubercle osteotomy fractures and removal complications [18, 19]. To avoid these, we adopted a quadriceps Z-lengthening: lengthening the extensor by 2–3 cm without detaching the tubercle or tendon. In our series this reliably permitted adequate exposure and eversion, and importantly we observed no extensor mechanism tears or patellar necrosis, addressing historical concerns. Similarly, our staged gap-balancing protocol extends prior concepts of measured resection. We performed a careful two-step osteotomy of the fused joint and incremental releases (including medial collateral and posterior capsule) while repeatedly checking flexion/extension gaps. This approach is more nuanced than simply releasing widely: by limiting bony resection and fine-tuning soft-tissue release, we achieved balanced gaps (within 2 mm mediolaterally) without over-resecting femur or tibia. Debette et al. (Int Orthop 2014) and others have emphasized the difficulty of obtaining balance in stiff knees, and our results suggest that the “Three-Balance” protocol provides a reproducible framework [20]. In short, our study adds to the field by articulating a detailed operative strategy: it integrates known principles of gap balancing and ligament balancing in a novel way tailored to hemophilia. This contributes new technical knowledge that complements the primarily outcome-focused prior literature, illustrating one way to overcome the limitations they encountered.

We also examined the complication profile in the context of existing reports. Our overall complication incidence was 54.5% (12 events in 22 knees), notably higher than many published series. Fenelon et al. reported a 28.7% complication rate (19.3% requiring reoperation) across general hemophilic TKAs [14], and Song et al. described complications in only 13% of cases [15]. The most common issues in our study were superficial infections (2 cases), abnormal bleeding/hematoma (3 cases), one deep prosthetic infection, and one extension lag (Table 5). We classified complications temporally as early (bleeding/hematoma), mid-term (superficial infection) and late (extension lag), and have detailed their management and long-term follow-up outcomes, which showed no implant failures. Bleeding is a known problem in hemophilia: Song et al. saw hemarthroses in 5% of knees, and Fenelon et al. found 7.6% had postoperative hemarthrosis [14, 15]. We had 3 such bleeding episodes (13.6%), consistent with the idea that transection of fibrotic synovium and extensive dissection in hemophilia predispose to persistent oozing. We also noted 3 patients (13.6%) with infection; Feng et al. similarly reported late periprosthetic infection as a leading cause of reoperation, and the meta-analysis by Fenelon noted infection rates of 6.2% (pre-2000) falling to 3.9% (post-2000) [14]. Our higher infection rate reflects both the severity of ankylosis and the necessity for aggressive soft-tissue release in this population. Despite this, all infections were managed successfully without prosthesis removal, underscoring that, with vigilant perioperative protocols, these risks are manageable. The one case of delayed extension lag may relate to quadriceps deconditioning-a known risk after extensive V–Y lengthening in other series-but with our Z-lengthening this was rare. Importantly, these complications did not diminish the overall benefit: even symptomatic knees had improved pain/function scores. In short, while our complications were more frequent, the pattern (bleeding, infection, stiffness) aligns with prior reports [21]. Mechanistically, we believe that soft-tissue contracture and poor baseline muscle envelope in hemophilia (with hemosiderin-laden synovium and ligament ossification) both limit ROM gains and elevate risk [22]. These findings carry practical implications for orthopedic teams worldwide, especially those in lower-volume or resource-limited settings. Hemophilic ankylosed knee is so rare that most surgeons will never encounter it, yet our experience shows it can be treated successfully with careful planning. First, multidisciplinary preparation is essential: coordination with hematologists for factor replacement, anesthesiologists familiar with bleeding risk, and rehabilitation specialists is crucial. Our center’s protocol (preoperative factor loading, intensive postoperative monitoring) can serve as a model even if factor availability is limited. Second, detailed preoperative templating and patient education pay dividends: we enforced inclusion criteria (e.g. quadriceps strength ≥ II) to select cases likely to benefit. Third, the “Three-Balance” and Z-lengthening techniques themselves are practical, not requiring exotic technology. For example, we used standard PS prostheses in most cases (adding stems only for osteoporosis), suggesting that centers without custom implants can still achieve balance by technique. We encourage surgeons in less-specialized hospitals to adopt the principle of incremental release and medial-to-lateral sequencing rather than imposing uniform massive cuts, as this can prevent instability and stiffness. Fourth, postoperative care must anticipate prolonged recovery: our mean stay was 33 days, reflecting intensive physiotherapy to overcome flexion contractures. In many resource-poor settings, such prolonged rehab may be challenging; our experience suggests that even partial flexion recovery (e.g. achieving 70–80°) yields worthwhile function, so a focus on achieving and maintaining any motion is warranted. Finally, although our study centered on hemophilia, many strategies apply to any “arthritic stiff knee”—for instance, the Z-plasty may be useful in severe rheumatoid or ankylosing cases to avoid osteotomy-related complications. In sum, this work provides a blueprint for surgery in extremely stiff knees: by following these steps, even teams with modest resources can safely navigate these operations and achieve outcomes comparable to specialist centers.

Our study’s strengths include addressing a critical gap in the literature: to our knowledge, this is the first series focusing on TKA for ankylosed hemophilic knees with a standardized technique. The detailed description of the surgical protocol and the use of consistent scoring (KSS, WOMAC, etc.) over an extended follow-up (mean 3.8 years, range 1.25–16.2 years) add robustness. All procedures were performed by an experienced arthroplasty team, reducing inter-surgeon variability. Nevertheless, there are limitations: the retrospective case series design and absence of a contemporaneous control group introduce selection bias, temporal bias, and significantly limit generalizability and external validity. We stratified the cohort into early and late periods and tested period × time interactions within our mixed-effects models, finding no significant interactions for any outcome, which indicates minimal learning-curve and temporal bias. First, without a matched control arm (e.g., conventional TKA in ankylosed knees), we cannot definitively attribute outcomes to our techniques rather than to patient selection or institutional factors. Second, although 22 cases represent a sizable cohort for this rare condition, the small sample size limits statistical power, precludes meaningful subgroup or risk-factor analyses, and a formal power calculation was not feasible. Third, the exclusive inclusion of young Chinese male patients with access to specialized hemophilia care and multidisciplinary rehabilitation restricts applicability to other genders, ethnicities, and healthcare settings where such resources may not be available. Fourth, as a retrospective study, outcome assessments could not be fully blinded and occurred at variable intervals (“12 months and annually thereafter”), with non-standardized handling of missing data, introducing potential measurement bias and data heterogeneity. Fifth, the standard orthopedic outcome measures (KSS, WOMAC, VAS) were developed for primary osteoarthritis and may not fully capture the unique functional goals or quality-of-life concerns of hemophilic patients with ankylosed knees; we also did not employ hemophilia-specific instruments (e.g., Haem-A-QoL) or objective functional assessments (e.g., gait analysis, activity monitoring). Sixth, important variables such as rehabilitation compliance and detailed postoperative physiotherapy protocols were not consistently recorded, which may affect the interpretation of functional recovery. Seventh, the relatively high complication rate may reflect both a learning curve and the extreme severity of ankylosis at our center; external benchmarking and systematic complication classification (early, mid-term, late) are needed. Eighth, we did not evaluate the economic and resource utilization aspects of this approach—including direct hospitalization costs, length of stay breakdown, factor replacement therapy expenses, rehabilitation resource requirements, and long-term healthcare utilization—which limits assessment of cost-effectiveness and policy implications. Finally, although implant survival remains 100% to date, the heterogeneity in follow-up duration necessitates ongoing, standardized surveillance for late failures. Despite these limitations, the consistency of clinical improvements supports the validity of our conclusions, but confirmation in larger, ideally prospective or multicenter studies would strengthen the evidence.

Conclusions

TKA for ankylosed knees in the extended position due to hemophilia requires extremely high technical requirements and has a relatively high complication rate. We innovatively propose the “Three-Balance” technique. During long-term follow-up, an increase in joint range of motion and improvement in quality of life have been observed. Moreover, in terms of functional gain, the effect far exceeds that of knee joint range of motion. In conclusion, for patients with severe ankylosed knees in the extended position caused by hemophilia, TKA using the “Three-Balance” technique is an effective and valuable surgical procedure.
