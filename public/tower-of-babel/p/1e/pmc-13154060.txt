Pseudo-Ischemic ECG Changes in a Patient With Profound Mixed Metabolic Acidosis Due to Metformin Use
Authors: John J Grant, David P Wilson, John T Schindler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetformin-induced lactic acidosis can cause electrocardiographic (ECG) changes and precipitate cardiac arrest.Case SummaryA 58-year-old woman presented to the emergency department after a ventricular fibrillation arrest. Laboratory findings on admission showed severe lactic acidosis, and ECG showed ischemic changes concerning for a ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. After treatment of the patient's sepsis, urinary tract infection, and acute kidney injury, she improved, and the pseudo-ischemic ECG changes resolved.DiscussionMetformin is a known instigator of lactic acidosis, particularly in patients with renal failure. On rare occasion, such lactic acidosis secondary to metformin can produce lethal arrhythmias. This case demonstrates the need for providers to consider metabolic derangements in patients with ventricular fibrillation arrest and subsequent ECG changes.Take-Home MessageIt is crucial for providers to recognize metabolic acidosis as a precipitator of cardiac arrest and to identify patients who take metformin who are at risk for metformin-associated lactic acidosis.Key words: acid-base imbalance, ACS ECG mimicry, acute coronary syndrome, diabetes mellitus, electrocardiogram, electrolyte imbalance, metabolic acidosis, metformin associated lactic acidosis (MALA), pseudo-STEMI, sepsis, ventricular fibrillation

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old woman called 911 with a chief complaint of shortness of breath. Upon the arrival of emergency medical services, she sustained a witnessed ventricular fibrillation arrest. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 14 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation and 2 defibrillations, and the patient was intubated and transported to our facility. On arrival to the emergency department, the patient was hypotensive, tachycardic, and normothermic. Examination showed cool extremities, dry mucous membranes, and decreased skin turgor. Arterial blood gas showed an elevated anion-gap metabolic acidosis, with a lactate level of 18 mmol/L (Table 1). Initial electrocardiogram (ECG) obtained in the emergency department showed nonspecific ST-segment changes in leads V3 and V4, in the setting of a right bundle branch block with left-axis deviation and left ventricular hypertrophy, raising concern for acute coronary syndrome (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and anxiety. Of note, she had been admitted multiple times in the past for acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis, with the most recent episode being 1 month prior.

Differential Diagnosis

The following were included in the patient's differential diagnosis: acute coronary syndrome, sepsis, elevated anion-gap metabolic acidosis, and AKI.

Investigations

The patient received routine initial laboratory work-up, which demonstrated her metabolic acidosis and AKI (Table 1). Serial ECGs were performed. The second ECG, taken a half hour after the first, showed sinus rhythm with ventricular bigeminy, first-degree atrioventricular block, left fascicular block, and diffuse nonspecific ST and T-wave changes (Figure 2). Further ECGs throughout the patient's stay demonstrated ischemic changes reminiscent of reperfusion patterns after an acute coronary event (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, Supplemental Material). The patient was not taken for emergent cardiac catheterization given a lack of prehospital chest pain syndrome combined with a bedside transthoracic echocardiogram that had failed to reveal any segmental wall motion abnormalities. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with contrast showed no pulmonary embolism. CT of the head without contrast was negative for any intracranial pathology. Urinalysis demonstrated a urinary tract infection and pyelonephritis. Coronary CT angiography on hospital day 9 revealed no hemodynamically significant stenosis in the coronary arteries (Table 2). After discharge from our facility, the patient was seen elsewhere and underwent a 2-dimensional transthoracic echocardiogram, cardiac magnetic resonance, and left heart catheterization, which showed no significant abnormalities. Specifically, the left heart catheterization showed patency of all coronary arteries, with minimal atherosclerosis. Because these procedures were performed at a different facility, we were unable to obtain images demonstrating these findings. Additionally, she received a right upper quadrant ultrasound on hospital day 10 owing to persistently elevated liver function tests that showed some gallbladder sludge and minimal perihepatic ascites and possible chronic liver disease.

Management

The patient was intubated upon arrival at the emergency department and remained so until hospital day 2, when she was extubated in the cardiovascular intensive care unit (CVICU). Norepinephrine was given for her hypotension. She received a standard sepsis work-up including intravenous fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics, later tailored based on sensitivities. Aggressive intravenous 8.4% sodium bicarbonate and D5W (dextrose 5% in water) fluids were given to correct the metabolic disturbance. In the emergency department, the patient had been given amiodarone for ventricular arrythmia prophylaxis; this was switched to lidocaine in the CVICU out of concern for her kidney and liver function. During her hospital course, she received subcutaneous heparin for deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis and sliding-scale insulin to manage her diabetes. On hospital day 5, she was moved from the CVICU to the floor where she was kept pending her coronary CT angiography and for management of her AKI and possible left lower lobe pneumonia. On hospital day 11, the patient was discharged after leaving against medical advice despite efforts by staff encouraging her to stay. Despite the patient leaving, her metformin was replaced with an SGLT2 inhibitor, and she was started on oral carvedilol 6.25 mg twice daily until follow-up.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After the metabolic acidosis correction, the patient's kidney function improved, creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate returned to baseline (Figure 7), and ECG patterns returned to the patient's standard patterns (Figure 6). By day 3 of her admission, the patient's pH had normalized, bicarbonate had risen, creatinine was stable, and estimated glomerular filtration rate was >90 (Figure 7). The patient was extubated on day 2 and was neurologically intact, with no deviation from baseline.

Discussion

Profound metabolic acidosis (pH <7.0) is a trigger for ventricular fibrillation, and regardless of etiology, it can produce a spectrum of ECG changes mimicking acute myocardial infarction.6,7 The mechanisms at work in metabolic acidosis contribute by direct H+ inhibition of fast sodium channels and impaired gap-junction conduction,2,3 leading to heterogeneous prolongation of action potential duration followed by promotion of early and delayed afterdepolarizations. Metformin can cause lactic acidosis by inhibiting mitochondrial complex I, thereby impairing oxidative phosphorylation and promoting pyruvate oxidation to lactate. In susceptible patients (underlying renal impairment or hypoxia), lactate accumulates faster than it can be cleared, leading to metabolic acidosis with elevated anion gap.4,8

In this patient, mixed type A (septic shock with tissue hypoperfusion) and type B (metformin accumulation) lactic acidosis created a state of profound acidosis, triggering both malignant arrhythmia and pseudo-ischemic ECG changes. Arrhythmias due to metformin-associated lactic acidosis, while rare, do occur.7,9

Most cases of metformin-associated lactic acidosis occur in the context of an acute precipitant. The most common culprits are sepsis, dehydration, or AKIs.7,9 The patient's recurrent unexplained acidosis episodes suggest she may have been having subclinical acidosis events for months. The rapid and complete reversibility of ECG abnormalities and ventricular arrhythmia after correction of acidosis, without coronary intervention, strongly supports metabolic rather than ischemic pathophysiology.

This case adds to a growing literature of acidosis-induced pseudo-STEMI patterns (including classic ST-segment elevation, new bundle branch blocks, and Brugada-like changes) that resolve with pH normalization.7 It underscores the importance of considering severe metabolic derangement in any patient with new ECG changes and ventricular fibrillation arrest before reflexively activating the catheterization laboratory, particularly when clinical factors suggest alternative explanations.1,5,10

Conclusions

This case highlights an essential factor to consider when evaluating ECG changes. Prompt recognition of comorbid factors or history inconsistent with acute coronary syndrome can help to identify other pathologies contributing to the presentation. Profound metabolic acidosis (pH <7), whether from sepsis, metformin, or another cause, is associated with reversible arrhythmias and ECG changes. Rapid recognition of acidosis as the driver can safely defer emergent catheterization in unstable patients. Furthermore, metformin discontinuation when the patient has a history of episodic acidotic presentations is essential.
