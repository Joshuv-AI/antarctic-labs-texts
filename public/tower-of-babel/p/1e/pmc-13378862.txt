Effectiveness of expert-assisted video-based learning on disease-specific knowledge among hospitalized chronic obstructive pulmonary disease patients
Authors: Anirudh R. Bhat, Madhura Anjan Padmashali, Meghana Sanil, Mohan K. Manu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Effective management of chronic conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) requires adequate knowledge of the disease and its treatment among patients. COPD patients can receive education through various modalities. This study aims to assess the effectiveness of expert-assisted video-based learning (EAV) on the retention of disease-specific knowledge among patients with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A pre-post study was conducted among 58 participants with COPD at a tertiary care hospital. Phase I included translation and validation of the Bristol COPD Knowledge Questionnaire (BCKQ) into Kannada and the development of a video based on its components. In Phase II, participants’ knowledge was assessed before and after the intervention using the BCKQ. EAV was provided, and knowledge retention was evaluated after 48 h post-intervention.RESULTS:Majority of participants were male (74.1%) from the rural areas (77.6%), with a mean age of (63.6 ± 7.68), and had an educational status of below 10th (72.4%). The mean baseline knowledge score (pre-intervention) was 33.9 ± 6.35, which significantly improved to 43.5 ± 5.87 post-intervention (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:EAV is effective in improving disease-specific knowledge among those with COPD. This approach can be incorporated into in-hospital, out-hospital, and healthcare center settings to improve disease-specific knowledge and health-related quality of life among patients with any chronic lung condition.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory illnesses involve disorders of the airway and other lung structures, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, and Occupational lung diseases.[1] COPD, a combination of chronic bronchitis and emphysema, ranks among the top three causes of death worldwide and is a significant public health concern due to its prevalence and the fact that it is both preventable and treatable, and can be treated with 90% education and 10% medication.[23] The severity of COPD is associated with the severity of the airflow limitation. The severity of the disease can be assessed using spirometry values, such as the fraction of exhaled volume in 1 s (FEV1). It can be classified based on the GOLD Criteria, where GOLD 1 suggests mild- if FEV1 ≥80% predicted, GOLD 2 suggests Moderate if FEV1 is within the range 50%–80% predicted, GOLD 3 suggests Severe if FEV1 is within the 30%–50% predicted. GOLD 4 suggests very severe if FEV1 is less than 30% predicted.[4]

Most of the COPD patients have inadequate or a lack of knowledge about their disease condition and self-management strategies.[567] Educating them regarding disease-specific knowledge and increasing patient awareness will help in symptom control, reduce the incidence of exacerbation and hospital readmission, and enhance medication adherence and better clinical outcomes. Patients with COPD can receive education in several ways, using various modalities.[89] Educating an individual refers to the deliberate sharing of knowledge and experience, usually a stimulus to an individual’s intellectual and psychological development. Traditional teaching methods include direct interaction and the use of PowerPoint presentations, blackboard, or whiteboard. This provides more opportunities to engage in interactive, practical educational activities. However, the cost of resources and physical infrastructure may be higher.[1011]

Video-based education is a technique in which patients can access videos to retain knowledge. It incorporates a variety of multimedia resources, such as visuals, graphics, animations, and interactive exercises, to make learning more engaging. This method of learning is both flexible and feasible, allowing individuals to access course content at any time and from any location, which may be more efficient than traditional classroom learning.[12] In today’s changing healthcare environment, videos are valuable resources for educating patients and caregivers. These aid in acquiring knowledge, reduce anxiety, enhance coping mechanisms, and promote self-care practices, making them effective teaching tools for patients [Figure 1]. They combine vocal and visual information in a teaching approach that is often easy for individuals to comprehend and remember.[13] Many studies have assessed disease-specific knowledge among COPD patients, but those examining the effects of different educational methods on this knowledge are scarce. Educating individuals through video-based instruction is more effective for information retention, as it utilizes multi-sensory engagement. The video-based teaching-learning process is effective among younger populations. Therefore, we sought to identify effects in the older population; hence, the purpose of this study was to assess the effects of Expert Assisted Video-based learning (EAV) on disease-specific knowledge among hospitalized COPD patients.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This was a pre-post study conducted in the pulmonary wards of Kasturba Hospital, Manipal, Karnataka, India, between August 2024 and February 2025. Participants were recruited using a purposive sampling method and included all individuals diagnosed with COPD according to the GOLD criteria.

Study participants and sampling

The participants who were above 18 years of age with a physician-diagnosed COPD as per GOLD guidelines, who were able to speak and read Kannada, a local language of one of the states of South India, those who were conscious, oriented, and hemodynamically stable on room air, intermittent oxygen therapy, or non-invasive ventilation were included. Participants were excluded if they were older than 80 years, admitted to the intensive care unit, had asthma or asthma–COPD overlap syndrome, had intellectual disabilities, visual or hearing impairments, or were receiving invasive ventilation or continuous non-invasive ventilation.

Sample size

At a 5% level of significance and 80% power, with an anticipated effect size of 0.4 and a 10% dropout rate, the minimum required sample size was 58 participants.

Data collection tools and techniques

Instruments

Bristol chronic obstructive pulmonary disease Knowledge Questionnaire

The Bristol COPD Knowledge Questionnaire (BCKQ) was used to assess patients’ disease-specific knowledge. BCKQ is a 65-item questionnaire that evaluates understanding and knowledge of COPD and its treatment. The questionnaire comprises 65 questions covering 13 knowledge topics, each with 5 statements. The topics covered involve epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, breathlessness, phlegm, infections, exercise, smoking, vaccination, inhaled bronchodilators, antibiotics, oral steroids, and inhaled steroids. There are three possible answers for each statement: “true,” “false,” or “don’t know.” One point is awarded for a correct response, and zero points are awarded for a wrong answer or “don’t know.” The total score range is from 0 to 65; a higher score indicates greater knowledge of COPD. The questionnaire used in this study was previously validated, and formal permission was obtained from the original author to translate it into Kannada, the local language.[14]

Video content for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease knowledge as per the Bristol chronic obstructive pulmonary disease Knowledge Questionnaire questionnaire

A COPD educational video based on the BCKQ content was developed by an expert fluent in Kannada, who also narrated the material. The recording was done using a Canon EOS 250D DSLR camera (24.1-megapixel sensor, DIGIC 8 processor) mounted on a tripod. To enhance comprehension, animations were created in Canva, a free design software. The completed video was reviewed and validated by five experts involved in COPD patient care, representing the departments of Respiratory Therapy, Respiratory Medicine, and Critical Care, each with at least five years of professional experience. The validation process assessed the video’s objective, clarity, structure, and presentation, effectiveness, and language clarity, with each parameter scored as 0 or 1. The final video had a total duration of approximately 15 minutes.

Procedure

Phase I involved developing the video and translating the BCKQ questionnaire. The BCKQ was translated into the Kannada language and was validated. The translated questions were sent to a group of experts fluent in both English and Kannada, and the content was verified for similarity to the original questionnaire. The translation process began only after receiving the required permissions from the questionnaire’s original author. In Phase II, participants were recruited and informed consent was obtained. Demographic details and comorbidities were documented using a structured proforma. To assess baseline disease-specific knowledge, patients were administered the translated BCKQ before receiving any intervention. After completing the questionnaire, the video was displayed to participants at the bedside on a laptop, and the expert clarified any doubts. The video was shown only once and was not made available online to prevent them from reviewing it. Retention was evaluated 48 h after the intervention using the same translated BCKQ questionnaire.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional ethics committee of Kasturba Medical College (EC/NEWIINSTI2022/KA/0042), and the study was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/08/072190).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Jamovi software (Version 2.6.44), The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency distributions and percentages, while quantitative variables were described using means and standard deviations. The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to compare quantitative outcomes across different socio-demographic groups. When the data were not normally distributed, appropriate non-parametric tests were applied. The effectiveness of the video-based intervention over the two time points was evaluated using a paired t-test. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The EAV showed a significant improvement in retained knowledge, as assessed with BCKQ, by 9.6 points (P < 0.001). Additionally, secondary analysis, which compared demographic factors such as age, gender, location, educational status, smoking status, other relevant history, and medications with baseline and post-intervention mean scores, did not show any significant differences.

Discussion

Impact of expert-assisted video-based learning on disease-specific knowledge

Our study is the first study to implement an EAV approach to improve disease-specific knowledge among COPD patients. Our findings indicate that baseline knowledge before the intervention was lower, which gradually improved after EAV education, suggesting that the EAV approach is effective in enhancing disease-specific knowledge among COPD participants. This aligns with findings by Tembhurne and Dumbre, suggesting that the video-assisted pranayama teaching program among older adults with COPD has a positive impact and helps raise awareness of their health concerns.[15] Similarly, findings from a study conducted by Shohreh et al. in 2025 suggested that video-based education among patients with bipolar disorders improved anxiety and fear, suggesting that video-based education should be incorporated into practice.[16] Our study revealed a significant improvement in the disease-specific knowledge at 48 h post-intervention. This enhanced change of 9.6 points may be attributed to the Video-based learning approach, which combines audio and visual elements into a teaching modality that is easy to comprehend and retain. This educational video, assisted by an expert, helps the patient with problem-solving and a better understanding of the disease. In an article published by Sajith et al., patients were verbally counselled and provided with an information leaflet as a supplement, which helped them improve their disease-specific knowledge.[17] Verbal counselling at each visit, supplemented by the information leaflet, might have contributed to enhanced disease-specific knowledge among those patients. Similarly, in our study, an educational video on COPD was developed, which involved several aspects like epidemiology, etiology, symptoms, smoking, infections, exercises, and management involving knowledge on oral and inhaled steroids and bronchodilators, antibiotics, and vaccination, which was assisted by an expert who helped in clarifying and resolving the doubts of the participants.

Association of demographic factors with knowledge improvement

In our study, the majority of participants were male. One possible reason to have many male participants with COPD is that men are more susceptible to risk factors like tobacco smoking, outdoor air pollution, and occupational exposures compared to women.[17] In our study, males had higher baseline knowledge than females. Most subjects were from rural areas and had an educational status below class 10th, which may have adversely affected their health due to a lack of understanding of the disease. This finding is supported by Partridge (2008 who claims that a reduced literacy level results in a lower understanding of the illness and a worse state of health.[18] Patients with higher educational attainment had greater baseline disease-specific knowledge than those with lower educational attainment. A potential reason is that individuals with lower levels of education are less likely to understand medical labels, instructions, and basic health information.[17] The two main risk factors for COPD are thought to be cigarette smoking and biomass smoke.[19] In our study, most participants were ex-smokers, and 34.5% were nonsmokers, which is similar to Wong and Yu.[6] In our research, ex-smokers had higher baseline knowledge than current smokers. One possible explanation is effective medical counselling, linked to awareness of harmful health issues, which makes the patient more health-conscious. Participants recruited in our study were mainly working in the agricultural and industrial sectors for several years, which might have contributed to the development of COPD, as supported by Lytras et al., study, which states that 20 years of occupational exposure to biological dusts, fumes, gases, and pesticides were related to an increased risk of COPD.[20] Those working in the agricultural sector were predominantly farmers and had lower baseline disease-specific knowledge than those in the corporate sector. Patients with low socioeconomic status are more likely to have poorer COPD outcomes than those with high socioeconomic status.[21] None of the subjects had attended any kind of educational intervention or talk on COPD before, which can also be associated with having a poor baseline disease-specific knowledge. Therefore, clinically stable patients with COPD and their proxies should receive education on general health and COPD, regardless of the severity of their illness.[22]

Video education and its significance

In our study, we incorporated a video-based learning approach assisted by an expert, which led to significant improvement in the retention of disease-specific knowledge among hospitalized COPD patients. The enhancement in disease-specific knowledge post-intervention was observed because the technique combines audio and visual elements into a teaching approach that is often easy for people to comprehend and retain. This EAV approach can be used in hospital settings to improve disease-specific knowledge. The video can be made available to COPD patients in other healthcare settings and can also be given as an educational supplement. Adding a QR code to the video education can be helpful and easily accessible to patients with smartphones.[22]

Strengths and Limitations

One of the strengths of our study was the use of a disease-specific video intervention, structured to ensure focused knowledge enhancement relevant to clinical outcomes. The use of a validated regional-language questionnaire is a contextual novelty but not a conceptual breakthrough. Video-based education for COPD has been studied before, but the expert-assisted component and immediate clarification of doubts add a new layer.

We encountered a few limitations in our study. First, as a single-center prospective study with a relatively small sample size, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. A pre-post design without a control group limits causal inference. Additionally, the EAV module was developed in Kannada, restricting its applicability to regions with Kannada-speaking populations. Lastly, we were unable to assess long-term knowledge retention, as follow-up beyond 48 h was not feasible due to participants’ discharge and resulting data loss.

Conclusion

The study’s findings indicate that EAV is effective in improving disease-specific knowledge among individuals with COPD. EAV aligns with key health policy benchmarks by enhancing disease-specific knowledge among individuals with COPD while promoting patient-centered care and health literacy. This instructional strategy may enhance health-related quality of life, encourage self-management, and reduce avoidable exacerbations and hospital readmissions, all of which could contribute to the provision of high-quality, reasonably priced healthcare. Larger sample sizes and multicenter prospective studies might also improve the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, future studies that assess long-term knowledge retention using randomized controlled trials might produce stronger results.
