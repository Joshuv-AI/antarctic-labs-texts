Serological biomarkers of extracellular matrix proteins are associated with hidradenitis suppurativa disease severity and treatment response
Authors: Helena Port, Signe Holm Nielsen, Valdemar Wendelboe Nielsen, Nikolaj Holgersen, Nadja Højgaard Pedersen, Jacob Pontoppidan Thyssen, Alexander Egeberg, Simon Francis Thomsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic inflammation in hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) disrupts tissue architecture and basement membrane integrity. Biomarkers of extracellular matrix (ECM) and basement membrane degradation (BGM, C7M, C4M) increase with disease severity, while NIC (linker protein degradation) decreases. Elevated baseline C7M is significantly associated with greater improvement in IHS4 following TNF-α inhibitor therapy. ECM remodelling biomarkers reflect tissue destruction and may aid in identifying patients at risk of disease progression and in monitoring treatment response.

Dear Editor, Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, recurrent and debilitating inflammatory disease with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 1%.1 The disease is characterized by aberrant tissue remodelling processes driven by persistent inflammation. The basement membrane (BM) is essential for maintaining ­epidermal–dermal cohesion and skin homeostasis, and is mainly composed of type IV and type VII collagen (C4M and C7M, respectively).2 C4M forms the core scaffold that confers stability and mechanical support.3 Type VII collagen acts as an anchoring fibril protein attaching the BM and the papillary dermis, and forming the epidermal–dermal junction.4 In addition, glycoproteins such as nidogen serve as a molecular linker between C4M and other BM components.3 Conversely, biglycan, mainly found in the dermis and, to a lesser extent, in the epidermis, ­regulates collagen fibrillogenesis and functions as an immunomodulatory molecule; upon proteolytic degradation, it acts as a danger-associated molecular pattern.5

Chronic inflammation in HS disturbs tissue architecture and compromises BM integrity. Inflammatory cell infiltrates, including macrophages and neutrophils, release proteolytic enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cathepsin S, which exacerbate the BM and dermis degradation, impairing barrier repair and function.1 The result of the proteolytic cleavage products further amplify local inflammation and may enter the circulation, where they can be detected as potential biomarkers of disease activity.6 While some inflammation-related biomarkers such as C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate have been associated with disease activity in HS,7 these are unspecific systemic inflammatory biomarkers and yet the clinical implications remain unclear. Despite significant advances in HS treatment, such as biologics, there remains a need to identify fast ­progressors early, within the therapeutic window when tissue damage may still be reversible,8 and to apply biomarkers as tools for treatment monitoring. In this study, we investigated a panel of biomarkers related to BM and dermal turnover as potential indicators of disease activity and treatment response.

Serum samples were collected from patients included in the Copenhagen Hidradenitis Suppurativa cohort, a well-characterized cohort of 430 consecutive, newly referred patients with HS attending a tertiary referral centre (Department of Dermatology, Bispebjerg Hospital, Copenhagen, Denmark) from 2016 to 2022.9 For a healthy reference range, serum samples from healthy donors acquired by BioIVT (London, UK) were measured. Extracellular matrix (ECM) BM remodelling was assessed using blood-based biomarkers reflecting MMP-degraded biglycan (BGM), C7M and C4M, and cathepsin S-degraded nidogen (NIC). The technical papers from the measured biomarkers can be found in Table S1 (see Supporting Information). Reference levels were obtained from serum sample measurements in commercial healthy donors. Differences in biomarker levels between disease severity groups based on Hurley stage and International Hidradenitis Suppurativa Severity Score System (IHS4) scores, as well as between areas of involvement [only lower (groins and buttock), only upper (axillae) or both] and by the presence of draining tunnels, were analysed using linear models adjusting by covariates age, sex and body mass index (BMI); biomarker data were log-transformed to obtain normality. Spearman correlations were used to investigate the association between baseline biomarker levels and the change in IHS4 scores over 3–6 months according to different types of treatment.

Patients were predominantly women (n = 290/430; 67.4%), and had a mean (SD) age of 43.9 (14.4) years and a mean (SD) BMI of 29.2 (6.7) kg m–2 (Table S2; see Supporting Information). BGM and C7M were significantly elevated in patients with more severe disease, as assessed by Hurley stage (I vs. III and II vs. III; both P < 0.001; Figure 1a), as were C4M levels (stage I vs. II, I vs. III and II vs. III; P < 0.01, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively). In contrast, NIC levels were significantly lower in more severe disease stages (III vs. I and III vs. II; both P < 0.001). Similarly, using the IHS4 score, BGM levels were significantly higher in patients with moderate and severe disease compared with those with mild disease (P < 0.001; Figure 1b). This was also observed for C7M (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001, respectively) and C4M (P < 0.05 and P < 0.001). Conversely, NIC levels were significantly lower in patients with severe disease compared with those with mild or moderate disease (P < 0.001; Figure 1b). BGM, C7M and C4M levels were significantly higher in patients with both areas of involvement compared with those with only lower areas (P < 0.05, P < 0.05 and P < 0.001) (Figure S1a; see Supporting Information), and C4M was also higher compared with patients with only upper areas involved (P < 0.05). NIC levels were significantly lower in patients with both areas affected compared with those with only lower or only upper areas involved (P < 0.001 and P < 0.05, respectively). Patients with draining tunnels had higher BGM, C7M and C4M levels than those without (P < 0.01, P < 0.01 and P < 0.05, respectively) (Figure S1b; see Supporting Information), whereas NIC levels were lower (P < 0.01). Higher baseline levels of BGM and C7M and lower baseline levels of NIC were associated with improvements in IHS4 among patients treated with tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors, although only C7M showed a significant association (R = −0.33, −0.38 and 0.34, respectively) (Table S3; see Supporting Information). For patients receiving topical treatment only, these biomarkers showed weak nonsignificant correlations with IHS4 changes (R = −0.22, −0.22 and 0.18, respectively). No significant associations were observed in patients treated with systemic antibiotics. C4M showed no association with IHS4 improvement across any treatment groups.

Biomarker levels are shown (a) by Hurley stage (I: n = 188; II: n = 191; III: n = 51) and (b) by IHS4 disease severity (mild: n = 247; moderate: n = 109; severe: n = 74). In this study, we found that biomarkers of BM and dermal turn­over were associated with disease activity. Specifically, for C7M degradation, patients with high baseline levels had a higher improvement in the IHS4 score after 3–6 months of biologic treatment. We previously investigated a broader panel of biomarkers of tissue turnover in a subset of the Copenhagen Hidradenitis Suppurativa study and found that biomarkers of tissue degradation and immune cell activity were associated with Hurley stage.10 With the current findings we emphasize that the evaluated biomarkers are end products of proteolytic activity, reflecting tissue destruction that can be quantified systemically in circulation and help identify patients at risk of disease progression, as well as identifying patients that will respond to a determined treatment supporting the window of opportunity intervention.

A limitation of this study is the lack of sequential biomarker data at 3–6 months of treatment. In summary, BM-specific and dermal ECM biomarkers were associated with disease severity in patients with HS, with elevated levels mildly associated with IHS4 improvement after 3–6 months of biologic treatment. These biomarkers may serve as objective measures of proteolytic activity present in HS, supporting disease severity assessment and treatment response evaluation.
