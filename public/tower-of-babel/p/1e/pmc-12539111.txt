Dual-channel heterogeneous feature fusion neural network for the prediction of post-transcriptional gene expression in Escherichia coli
Authors: Zihe Wang, Zilun Mei, Xiaogang Wang, Jinpeng Zhang, Zhenghong Xu, Fei Liu, Xiaojuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The sequences and structures of the 5′ mRNA regions in prokaryotes significantly impact transcript stability and translation efficiency at the post-transcriptional level. However, the structure–function relationship of the post-transcription regulation region remains elusive. Here, we present a dual-channel neural network integrating sequence-structure features to predict post-transcriptional gene expression in Escherichia coli. Our model combines Word2Vec and K-mer encoding for initial sequence representation, followed by parallel feature extraction via CNN and BiLSTM. An attention mechanism dynamically prioritizes critical elements within both channels. The feature vectors from these modules are concatenated and fed into a fully connected network for final prediction. Evaluated on randomized train-test splits, the model demonstrates robust performance in classifying expression levels based on 5′ mRNA regions and can identify post-transcriptional regulation regions with high translational efficiency, with accuracy reaching 93%. This framework provides a computational tool for optimizing synthetic biology designs by linking sequence architecture to expression outcomes, enhancing the efficiency of biological sequence design.

Introduction

In prokaryotes, gene transcription converts genetic information from DNA to mRNA, generating complementary mRNA strands that carry codons to the ribosome for protein synthesis [1, 2]. Transcript abundance, a key indicator of mRNA synthesis rate and quantity, is determined not only by transcription activity but also by post-transcriptional regulation. Specifically, this regulation is influenced by two main factors. First, variations in mRNA sequences and structures affect their ability to resist RNase degradation [3–5]. Second, differences in mRNA’s ribosome recruitment efficiency not only impact translation initiation but also indirectly enhance mRNA stability through ribosome protection [6, 7]. Together, these factors lead to significant differences in gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.

These post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms are crucial in determining the final protein expression level, as mRNA abundance directly impacts the rate of protein synthesis during translation [8, 9]. As such, mRNA abundance serves as a key indicator of gene expression regulation, offering valuable insights into biological processes, disease mechanisms, and drug development.

In discussing the mRNA abundance of the gene, it is essential to consider that the structural and functional characteristics of this 5′ region of each mRNA. It can be divided into three distinct functional regions: the translational standby site (TSS), the Shine-Dalgarno (SD) sequence and N-terminal coding sequence (NCS). The Shine-Dalgarno (SD) sequence is essential for ribosome binding and translation initiation near the start site, and also indirectly influences mRNA stability via ribosome interactions [10]. The Translational Standby Site (TSS), located upstream of SD, regulates post-transcriptional processes dynamically: its secondary structure can shield mRNA from RNases (enhancing stability) [11, 12], facilitate ribosome accommodation if linearized (potentially aiding initiation), or encapsulate SD to inhibit ribosome binding (reducing initiation, dependent on structure stability/position) [13]. In essence, the TSS plays a dynamic role in regulating post-transcriptional processes through these multiple mechanisms. The SD, TSS, and NCS elements within the PTRR are highly interconnected, with their complex interplay critically governing post-transcriptional regulation [14, 15]. The N-terminal coding sequence (NCS), adjacent to SD, frequently forms secondary structures involving SD, impacting stability, translation efficiency, and protein abundance [16–21]. The elements within the PTRR—including TSS, SD and NCS—are highly interconnected, with complex interplay significantly impacting post-transcriptional regulation.

A significant gap exists in computational models for predicting gene-specific post-transcriptional regulation levels driven by the PTRR. While models exist for transcript-level or overall expression prediction, dedicated tools for post-transcriptional regulation remain scarce. This absence hinders synthetic gene design and biological sequence interpretation.

This study aims to fill this gap by developing a model to predict post-transcription levels based solely on PTRR sequences. By utilizing variants expressed under an identical promoter, we isolate PTRR’s regulatory effect on transcript abundance. Our model provides a tool to optimize PTRR design for desired expression levels in synthetic biology, reducing experimental burden and enabling deeper gene regulation.

Data-driven machine learning, particularly deep learning, excels at learning complex patterns from biological sequences for accurate prediction [22]. This capability has been successfully demonstrated in diverse applications, including variant detection (e.g., DeepVariant using CNNs [23]) and modeling basic regulatory elements like promoters (e.g., CNN/GAN-based activity prediction and generation [24, 25]) and non-coding functions (e.g., the hybrid CNN-BiLSTM DanQ model [26, 27]).

While machine learning-based sequence prediction models have demonstrated strong potential in analyzing basic regulatory elements such as promoters and RBS, there are still significant gaps and development potential in the application on the prediction of PTRR at post-transcription level.

Building upon these advances, the study proposes a deep learning model based on dual channel heterogeneous feature fusion for gene sequence classification tasks. This model adopts a parallel architecture design, which processes gene sequence information in different representation forms through two independent feature extraction branches.

This study proposes a dual-channel deep neural network for sequence analysis. The first channel processes genetic sequences using word embeddings: k-mers are generated, converted to distributed vectors via a pre-trained Word2Vec model, and then processed by multi-scale CNNs with max-pooling for local feature extraction. The second channel utilizes a Bi-LSTM with attention mechanism on one-hot encoded sequences, capturing bidirectional context while dynamically weighting critical nucleotide positions. High-order features from both channels are concatenated, transformed through fully connected layers, and fed to a SoftMax classifier. This end-to-end architecture jointly learns localized semantic patterns and global contextual dependencies through heterogeneous feature fusion.

Our study delivers the first deep learning framework specifically designed to predict integrated post-transcriptional regulatory effects mediated by prokaryotic PTRR sequences. We pioneer a heterogeneous dual-channel architecture that synergistically combines semantic embeddings (CNN-processed k-mer/Word2Vec features) with attentional sequence contexts (BiLSTM-analyzed one-hot encodings), explicitly optimized for PTRR analysis. This fusion of distributed semantic and positional representations demonstrably enhances regulatory prediction efficacy. The resulting model provides an actionable synthetic biology tool for designing expression-optimized PTRR variants, enabling precision gene construct engineering while reducing experimental screening burdens.

Methods

Data preparation

Dataset selection

Deep learning models necessitate the availability of experimental data for effective feature extraction. In this study, under the regulation of the same promoter, we meticulously designed a total of 12 structurally diverse translational standby site (TSS) sequences, ensuring a wide representation of potential translational standby site. Furthermore, we selected 6 highly conserved Shine-Dalgarno (SD) sequences, which vary in length from 4 to 9 nucleotides, to capture the essential elements involved in ribosome binding and translation initiation. In addition to the TSS and SD sequences, we synthesized 7 N-terminal coding sequence (NCS) that exhibit varying lengths and structural characteristics, contributing to the complexity and variability of the dataset. The same promoter was applied to regulate the transcription activity to make sure that the differences that we observed regarding the transcript abundance attributed to the regulation at post-transcription level.

The dataset for this study is sourced from Zhang’s work [28], which systematically designed 576 unique post-transcription regulatory regions (PTRR) through combinatorial assembly of three functional modules: translational standby site (TSS), Shine-Dalgarno (SD) sequences, and N-terminal coding sequences (NCS) (Raw next-generation sequencing data for PTRR mutant libraries in Zhang’s study were submitted to the NCBI, with Accession No. [PRJNA1133087] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1133087)). High-expression PTRR exhibited distinct structural features: (1) TSS regions with optimized stem-loop configurations (10–12 nt stem length and ≤ 4 nt loop size) to balance mRNA stability and ribosome accessibility; (2) SD sequences contains 6–8 nucleotides, which enhances ribosome binding efficiency; (3) long linear NCS that facilitate ribosome sliding during elongation, increasing protein yield compared to structured NCS. This diverse dataset, covering structural variations in mRNA folding energy and codon adaptation index, is crucial for training robust deep learning models capable of capturing sequence-structure–activity relationships.

K-means clustering analysis

For this dataset, while the classification model requires the dataset to be divided into three groups—high, medium, and low—based on their post-transcription levels. Due to the high imbalance in the distribution of samples across post-transcription levels, we employed the K-means clustering analysis method. K-means is a widely used unsupervised learning algorithm primarily utilized for data clustering analysis. It partitions the dataset into K distinct clusters, ensuring that data points within the same cluster are as similar as possible [29].

The steps of the K-means algorithm are as follows: First, determine the number of clusters (K), which is based on prior knowledge; in this case, the data is divided into three categories: high, medium, and low (K = 3). Next, randomly select K initial cluster centers from the dataset. For each data point, calculate its distance to each cluster center (for one-dimensional data, the absolute value of the difference is sufficient) and assign the data point to the nearest cluster center. After that, recalculate the cluster centers by computing the mean of all data points assigned to each cluster. Finally, repeat the assignment and update steps, recalculating the distances between data points and cluster centers, and reassigning data points until the cluster centers converge or a predetermined number of iterations is reached.

Data partitioning and sampling

Prior to each training iteration, the dataset was randomly partitioned into training and testing subsets at a fixed 1:4.76 ratio (approximately 20% training, 80% testing), ensuring robust evaluation on unseen data. To maintain class distribution integrity across high/medium/low expression groups, the training set underwent stratified sampling, mitigating potential biases. Crucially, each partitioning was controlled by a unique random seed to ensure reproducibility, with the entire training-evaluation cycle repeated 10 times using distinct random seeds. Model performance was rigorously assessed by computing the mean and standard deviation of key metrics across all 10 iterations, providing statistically reliable estimates of generalization capability while minimizing partitioning variability effects. This comprehensive approach enhances feature learning efficacy and ensures robust prediction performance for novel PTRR variants.

Feature encoding

After completing the label classification of the dataset, we need to perform feature encoding suitable for deep learning on the input dataset. Feature encoding converts raw gene sequence data into matrix or vector form that is conducive to deep learning algorithm processing. The appropriate feature encoding method can enable the model to effectively obtain the features of input data, which can effectively improve the learning efficiency and prediction accuracy of deep learning models. In our study, we adopted a coding method that combines Word2Vec and one-hot to extract features.

One-hot encoding, also known as one-bit effective encoding, is a method that encodes n different character types into a binary sequence of length n, where only one bit is set to 1. The position of the 1 indicates the different characters. This method is commonly used to convert categorical data into a numerical format that can be processed by computers. In gene sequence analysis, one-hot encoding is used to convert the bases (such as A, T, C, G) in DNA or RNA sequences into binary vectors. It is often combined with the k-mer method to construct a single hot coding matrix for the entire DNA sequence. This encoding strictly preserves the positional information and base class of the sequence, without any information loss. Under the one-hot encoding method for a single base (k = 1 in k-mer), the one-hot encoding sequence is easy to implement and interpret, computationally efficient, and suitable for capturing local positional features of the sequence. However, with the increase in k values, the problem of high dimensionality and sparsity in the encoding sequence can lead to computational redundancy and the inability to construct contextual dependencies between bases [30].

The k-mer mentioned above is a commonly used concept for DNA sequence preprocessing. Due to the fact that DNA sequences are composed of four different bases (A, T, C and G), k-mer is often used to capture local information of the sequence. K-mer divides a long sequence into several consecutive subsequences of length k. For example, when K = 2, the sequence “ATTAG” can be divided into four consecutive subsequences: “AT”, “TT”, “TA”, and “AG”. In this study, we explored different k-mer lengths (k = 2, 3, 4, 5) and evaluated their performance on our downstream task using cross-validation. Based on the evaluation results, k = 3 was selected as it yielded the optimal performance. The Word2Vec model was then trained from scratch on the k-mer sequences (k = 3) generated from our PTRR dataset. The Word2Vec model can treat these subsequences as words and capture their co-occurrence relationships in the sequence through context learning, thereby modeling the semantic information of local patterns [31].

Word2Vec is a technique for natural language processing that captures semantic relationships between words by mapping them to a vector space. Word2Vec learns vector representations of words by training a neural network. Each word is represented as a high-dimensional vector that captures the semantic relationships between words [32, 33].

Model structure design

The post-transcription level prediction model designed in this article is a three class classification model constructed using a dual channel input approach. Firstly, the original sequence data is processed into continuous subsequences through 3-mer, and the pre trained Word2Vec model encodes a feature matrix composed of continuous sequence vectors as input for the subsequent convolutional network part. At the same time, the original sequence is processed by one-hot encoding to form another new one hot matrix as input to the BiLSTM network layer with attention mechanism. Finally, the outputs of the two channels are concatenated along the feature dimension and input into a fully connected neural network to be transformed into the final input. The output dimension of the fully connected layer is 3, representing the three classification problem. The output result contains three values, representing the probability that the original sequence belongs to low, medium, and high levels, respectively. The model is trained using cross entropy as the loss function and uses Adam optimizer to iteratively update the weight parameters generated during the training process. Convolutional neural network

CNN is a deep learning model whose key concept is to utilize convolutional layers to extract features from input data. Its inspiration comes from the visual mechanisms of biological organisms. In a convolutional neural network model, the neurons in the convolutional layers are capable of extracting higher-level abstract features from the features extracted by the previous layers. Convolutional neural networks (CNN) have been widely applied in the analysis of biological information data, demonstrating excellent performance in tasks such as chromatin accessibility, prediction of DNA and RNA binding protein binding sites, and methylation analysis [34]. CNN was first introduced by LeCun et al., with the original intention of processing grid-like data, primarily traditional image data [35]. Key feature processing methods in CNN include convolution, activation, and pooling. After the convolution operation, the features are activated using an activation function, which is typically a nonlinear mapping function in common CNN architectures. Subsequently, the model performs pooling on the activated features. Pooling is a down-sampling technique that reduces the size of feature maps or feature vectors. For instance, in the case of images, this method decreases the resolution of the feature maps, filters out redundant details, and expands the receptive field while extracting potential high-dimensional features, allowing the model to capture more relevant characteristics.

Bidirectional long short-term memory

RNN is another type of deep learning model designed to process sequential data by utilizing recurrent layers to capture temporal dependencies within the input. While, traditional RNNs may struggle to effectively capture long-term dependencies in sequences due to the vanishing gradient problem, leading to a decline in performance. Furthermore, RNNs have limited memory capacity, allowing them to retain only a finite length of memory, which may hinder their ability to capture global information in longer biological sequences. LSTM (long short-term memory) is an improved type of recurrent neural network specifically designed to address the long-term dependency issues present in traditional RNNs [36]. LSTM introduces a gating mechanism, which includes a forget gate, an input gate, and an output gate, to help the network better control the flow of information and memory. Through these gating mechanisms, LSTM can effectively capture long-term dependencies, making it suitable for processing sequential data that requires long-term memory. BiLSTM (bidirectional long short-term memory) is another common structure of recurrent neural networks. Unlike traditional LSTM, BiLSTM incorporates a bidirectional structure that runs two independent LSTMs at each time step: one from the beginning to the end of the sequence (forward LSTM) and the other from the end to the beginning (backward LSTM). This allows the network to consider both forward and backward information from the input sequence. The bidirectional structure enables the network to better understand the contextual information of the entire sequence, which helps improve the accuracy of sequence modeling. Compared to unidirectional LSTM, BiLSTM can more fully utilize the information within the sequence by considering both past and future contexts, enhancing the model’s ability to understand the sequence. Like traditional LSTM, BiLSTM can effectively capture long-term dependencies and performs better in tasks involving long sequences, especially those that require simultaneous consideration of both the beginning and the end of the sequence [37].

In our study, we applied a bidirectional long short-term memory network (BiLSTM) combined with attention mechanism (attention) to the one-hot encoded DNA sequence to capture the global dependencies of the sequence and focus on key functional regions, thereby improving the performance of gene expression prediction or functional site recognition. Attention layer

The attention layer is a crucial component of modern neural network architectures, playing a key role particularly in the fields of natural language processing and computer vision. Its core idea is to mimic human cognitive processes, enabling the model to dynamically focus on specific parts of the input data. The attention mechanism determines the importance of input elements by calculating the relevance between Query, Key, and Value. This approach allows the model to effectively capture complex dependencies within the data, enhancing feature representation capabilities and demonstrating exceptional performance when handling long sequences or complex data [38]. The application of attention layers is widespread, including tasks such as machine translation, text summarization, and image classification, significantly improving the accuracy and flexibility of models. By introducing the attention layer after the Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory network (BiLSTM), the model can dynamically assess and weight the importance of each time step in the input sequence, thereby enhancing its focus on key features [39]. This mechanism not only improves the model’s efficiency in utilizing contextual information but also enhances its feature selection capabilities, leading to significant improvements in performance and adaptability in complex sequence tasks. For the BiLSTM layer’s input, DNA sequences were first represented using one-hot encoding. Due to the inherent variation in sequence lengths within our dataset, all sequences were padded to a uniform maximum length using zero vectors appended to the end (end padding). This fixed-length representation is required for efficient batch processing by the BiLSTM layer. The padded sequences were then fed into BiLSTM layer to capture contextual information from both forward and backward directions. Subsequently, an attention mechanism was applied to the BiLSTM outputs, which inherently learns to focus on the informative non-padded regions of the sequences and down-weight the influence of the padding positions, to compute a weighted context vector representing the most salient features for the final classification task.

Feature fusion

Feature fusion is implemented through direct concatenation of the convolutional pathway’s output—which captures localized structural motifs—and the BiLSTM-Attention pathway’s output, representing global contextual dependencies. Specifically, the CNN stream delivers multi-scale structural fingerprints following adaptive max-pooling, while the BiLSTM-Attention stream generates context-aware sequence embeddings. These complementary representations are concatenated along the feature dimension to form an integrated feature matrix. This fusion strategy preserves the unique contributions of each pathway while enabling downstream fully connected layers to model complex sequence-structure–function relationships underlying regulatory phenotypes.

Fully connected layer

The fully connected layer is the final processing layer of the model, which takes the output of the convolutional network concatenated according to the feature dimension and the input of the BiLSTM network with attention mechanism as inputs. It achieves data dimensionality reduction by concatenating multiple fully connected layers and predicts the classification of DNA sequences through ReLU activation function.

Conclusion

In this study, a neural network framework (CNN-BiLSTM-Attention) integrating Word2Vec and one-hot coding dual channel parallel input was proposed to systematically predict post-transcriptional regulation levels mediated by PTRR in Escherichia coli. The model achieved a 93% accuracy and an F1 score of 0.9309 on the test dataset, with prediction accuracies of 92%, 95%, and 83% for low, medium, and high post-transcription levels, effectively screening for fragments at low to medium levels. Meanwhile, the effectiveness of the model indirectly reveals the synergistic interactions among TSS, SD, and NCS, offering actionable targets for PTRR engineering.

Compared to focusing on a single regulatory element (such as a promoter or RBS), our study pioneered the use of fragment combination sequences PTRR to predict post-transcriptional regulation levels and achieved quantitative modeling of PTRR combination effects for the first time. Our findings highlight the importance of considering the PTRR in gene expression regulation and provide actionable targets for the rational design of PTRR elements in synthetic biology. By enabling the design of PTRR sequences that meet specific post-transcription level requirements, our model facilitates the optimization of gene expression, reduces the need for extensive experimental testing, and enhances the efficiency of biological sequence design. This represents a significant advancement in understanding and manipulating gene expression at the post-transcriptional level.

Our model’s predictions align with experimental studies on PTRR design. For instance, the preference for structured TSS (10–12 nt) mirrors findings that moderate secondary structures enhance mRNA stability by shielding RNase cleavage sites, while excessively long stems may hinder ribosome access. Similarly, the model’s emphasis on SD conservation corroborates the role of strong ribosome binding in both translation initiation and mRNA protection. Notably, the exclusion of structured NCS in high-level predictions supports experimental evidence that secondary structures in NCS impede ribosome progression, reducing both translation efficiency and mRNA stability[16]. The high-expression signatures identified by our model—6–8 nt SD sequences mirroring ribosomal binding constraints, TSS stem-loops (10–12 nt stems, ≤ 4 nt loops) balancing mRNA protection and accessibility, and unstructured NCS avoiding translational pausing—collectively align with established principles of E. coli post-transcriptional regulation documented across experimental and structural studies. These consistent synergies highlight the model’s capability to capture biologically relevant features, bridging computational predictions with mechanistic insights.

In summary, this research not only addresses a critical gap in the prediction of post-transcriptional regulation but also offers a valuable resource for researchers aiming to optimize gene expression in E. coli. Future work will integrate causal inference to resolve sequence-function causality and establish a closed-loop “predict-synthesize-validate” system for iterative refinement. Meanwhile, we will focus on expanding the model to other prokaryotic systems and further refining the prediction accuracy through additional experimental data. This work will bridge the gap between correlative analysis and mechanism-driven design in gene expression regulation, providing a transformative tool for metabolic engineering and synthetic biology.
