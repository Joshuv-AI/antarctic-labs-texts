Designed guide plate assisted cortico-cancellous Iliac crest bone grafting for alveolar cleft repair
Authors: Luxi Weng, Xiaoyan Feng, Yiming Wang, Jiaqi Shao, Xu Huang, Yapei Han, Huiming Wang, Jun Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlveolar cleft is a common developmental malformation in the maxillofacial region, often occurring with cleft lip or palate. The traditional “gold standard” iliac bone grafting implants only cancellous bone in the alveolar cleft region. However, this approach provides insufficient initial mechanical support and is prone to structural collapse and accelerated bone resorption. This study used digital guide plate assisted cortico-cancellous iliac crest bone grafting to repair alveolar clefts, aiming to provide better structural support while reducing bone resorption.MethodThis study retrospectively reviewed 39 patients with unilateral alveolar clefts, including 21 cases in the autogenous iliac cancellous bone grafting group (cancellous bone group, mean age 15.33 ± 5.36 years) and 18 cases in the digitally guided autogenous iliac cortical-cancellous composite bone grafting group (composite bone group, mean age 14.17 ± 4.05 years). Preoperative and 6-month postoperative assessments were performed using CBCT for three-dimensional evaluation. The study compared the bone graft volume, new bone fill rate, alveolar width in the grafted area, and bone density between the two groups.ResultPostoperatively, the composite bone group exhibited markedly greater bone graft volume (698.58 ± 197.43 mm³ versus 378.23 ± 176.65 mm³, P < 0.01) and significantly higher new bone fill rate for volume, area, and height relative to the cancellous bone group. The composite bone has a clear advantage in maintaining alveolar ridge width compared to cancellous bone.ConclusionCompared with the traditional “gold standard” grafting, digital guide plate assisted cortico-cancellous iliac crest bone grafting for alveolar cleft repair significantly increased the bone graft volume. The surgical method introduced in this study provides a new option for the precise repair and functional reconstruction of alveolar clefts and has a broad application prospect.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06917-z.Keywords: Digital guide plate, Iliac cortical bone, Iliac cancellous bone graft, Unilateral alveolar cleft repair

Background

Cleft lip and palate is a common craniofacial developmental anomaly, with a high incidence rate of alveolar cleft among affected individuals [1–3]. Alveolar cleft, a common congenital oral and maxillofacial deformity, refers to the gap in the anterior part of the maxillary dental arch, leading to a discontinuous alveolar ridge that interferes with the normal development and arrangement of the maxilla and dental arch, significantly affecting patients’ oral function and appearance [4–7]. The primary objectives of treating alveolar cleft are to reconstruct the continuity of the maxilla, restore the height of the alveolar bone, create a suitable alveolar bone environment for the eruption of canines and lateral incisors on the cleft side, reduce periodontal defects to maintain teeth in the long term, eliminate oronasal and vestibular fistulas, and restore the fullness of the lips and the appearance of the nose [7–9]. Alveolar cleft bone grafting is regarded as a reliable treating method [10, 11]. Since its introduction, autologous iliac cancellous bone has been considered the gold standard for alveolar cleft repair due to its convenient harvesting procedure, abundant source, good osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties, and ability to form a bone structure similar to the alveolar bone [12]. Iliac cancellous bone is a porous three-dimensional structure composed of bone cells, osteoprogenitor cells, and osteoinductive growth factors, which has good osteogenic effects [13, 14]. However, a significant drawback of cancellous bone grafting is the high bone resorption rate During the first six months post-surgery, which can reach 30%-55% [15], prompting the development of new surgical techniques to address this issue. Among these approaches, the addition of iliac cortical bone to autologous bone grafts has emerged as a particularly effective method. This includes the use of block bone that contains both iliac cortical and cancellous components [16, 17] composite grafts consisting of double-layered iliac cortical bone with iliac cancellous bone [18] and mandibular cortical bone [19]. Recent studies have demonstrated several advantages of cortical bone, including its rigidity, ability to resist soft tissue migration, reduced resorption, support for nasal ala, and decreased surgical trauma [19–21]. For example, one study employed iliac cortical bone and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) as a “ceiling effect” technique for alveolar cleft bone grafting and nasal floor reconstruction [23]. Another study adopted a double iliac cortical-cancellous bone plate grafting technique [18]. In this method, researchers harvested bone from the iliac crest and cut it into two cortical-cancellous bone pieces for alveolar cleft bone grafting. The smaller piece was adapted to the sutured nasal mucosa and covered with cancellous bone on top, while the larger piece was placed over the labial cleft margin, achieving satisfactory outcomes.

Although the addition of cortical bone to grafts has many advantages, the forementioned grafting techniques that include cortical bone often require the surgeon to shape the bone according to the cleft after harvesting, which undoubtedly increases the difficulty, time, and workload of the surgery [24]. The degree of match between the graft and the cleft is also difficult to ensure with precision. Therefore, the use of a digital guide plate in harvesting cortical bone ensures a better match to the defect shape, while saving time and reducing errors in carving. However, there have been few similar reports, making it difficult to conduct a comparison. Moreover, the clinical effectiveness of this method compared to the “gold standard” has not yet been systematically evaluated. Therefore, there is an urgent need in the clinical field to develop a minimally invasive and effective method for precise cortical bone harvesting. Since 2020, digital guides have been utilized in our hospital to assist in harvesting autologous iliac cortical bone for alveolar cleft bone grafting, achieving good clinical results (Figure S1). This study systematically evaluates the efficacy of this technique from both clinical and radiological perspectives, aiming to reduce bone resorption rates and improve transplantation success.

Methods

Subjects

This study was a retrospective study. A total of 39 patients with unilateral alveolar cleft who underwent bone graft surgery in the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine from January 2018 to December 2023 were incorporated. Among them, 21 patients underwent autologous iliac cancellous bone graft (13 males, 8 females, aged 8 to 20 years, with an average age of 15.33 ± 5.36), and 18 patients underwent digital guide plate-assisted cortico-cancellous iliac crest bone graft (7 males, 9 females, aged 8 to 20 years, with an average age of 14.17 ± 4.05).

The detailed inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, and sample size calculation method could be found in Supporting Information.

Treatment methods

Digital guide plate preparation

Patients underwent preoperative orthodontic treatment to correct the horizontal width and height discrepancies of the grafting alveolar bone and had a CBCT scan taken before surgery. The data were saved in DICOM format and imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 software (Materialise, BE) to calculate the three-dimensional model of the maxilla and were saved as an STL file. The Geomagic Studio 2013 (Geomagic, USA) and 3D printing software Magics 25 (Materialise, BE) were then used to establish the outer surface of the labial and palatal repair bone plates and the corresponding bone harvesting guide plates based on the alveolar cleft, as shown in Fig. 1, and the iliac bone harvesting guide plate was printed by a 3D printer Objet260 (Stratasys, USA) with a rigid and transparent resin MED610™ (Stratasys, USA). The surgery was performed by the same group of oral and maxillofacial surgeons.

Surgical methods

(1) Autologous Iliac Cancellous Bone Graft Group (Cancellous Bone Group).

Preparation of the bone graft bed: An incision was made along the gingival margin mesial and distal to the tooth on the labial side of the alveolar cleft, the gingiva was stripped, and the flap was turned over to the bone surface to expose the bone defect of the alveolar cleft. The soft tissues on the labial side, as well as the nasal floor and palatal side, were loosened as much as possible around the cleft to form a labial gingival mucoperiosteal flap that could completely cover the surface of the transplanted bone. The nasal floor soft tissue and the palatal mucoperiosteum were sutured tightly to prevent the formation of oro-nasal fistula.

The soft tissues on the labial side, as well as the nasal floor and palatal side, were loosened as much as possible around the cleft to form a labial gingival mucoperiosteal flap that could completely cover the surface of the transplanted bone. The nasal floor soft tissue and the palatal mucoperiosteum were sutured tightly to prevent the formation of oro-nasal fistula.

Iliac bone harvesting: An incision was made at the skin of the anterior superior iliac crest area on the same side, the muscle layer was separated to reach the bone surface, and the cortical bone was chiseled open with an ultrasonic bone knife along a rectangular incision while keeping the periosteum intact. Cancellous bone was scraped out with a curette for later use. The chiseled cortical bone was repositioned, and the layers were sutured.

(2) Digital Guide Plate-Assisted Autologous Iliac Cortical Combined with Iliac Cancellous Bone Graft Group (Composite Bone Group).

Preparation of the bone graft bed: The same as the cancellous bone group.

Preparation of the bone graft bed: The same as the cancellous bone group.

Digital Guide Plate Assisted iliac bone harvesting: The patient’s hip was elevated on the side from which the bone was harvested. The position of the anterior superior iliac crest was marked, and an incision was made 1 cm medial to this landmark to avoid injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. The incision was made parallel to the iliac crest, with its length adjusted according to the size of the bone to be harvested, typically around 5 cm. The subcutaneous tissue, muscle, and periosteum were dissected layer by layer to expose the medial aspect of the iliac crest. After exposure, the guide plate was placed on the inner side of the iliac crest, which was relatively flat and well-suited for placement of the guide plate. Once its outline was traced, the cortical bone was cut along the mark with an ultrasonic osteotome and lifted off with a chisel(Fig. 2). Cancellous bone was harvested with a curette as needed. Bone wax was applied to the cut surface, and the layers were sutured to achieve hemostasis. The cortical bone and cancellous bone were soaked in physiological saline for later use.

Clinical follow-up observation

Clinical follow-up observations of the postoperative pain and wound healing conditions of the bone graft area and the bone harvesting area were conducted 1–2 weeks and 3–6 months after surgery. Cases with the following conditions were considered as bone graft failure: the formation of oro-nasal fistula, the formation of sequestra, or the loosening of the bone graft block.

Radiological measurement and analysis

CBCT was taken before surgery and 6 months after surgery for all patients, using the same CBCT device (NewTom VGi, Verona, Italy). CBCT data in DICOM format were imported into Mimics Medical 21.0 (Materialise, BE) and Dolphin Imaging 11.95 (Chatsworth, CA, USA) to generate a 3D craniofacial model. The CBCT coordinate axis was adjusted to generate a standard head position (Figure S2). Statistical methods

All data were imported into SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for statistical analysis. The normality of the data distribution was analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The independent sample t-test was used for comparison between the two groups, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

In this retrospective study, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of age, gender, the proportion of clefts on the left and right sides, and the impaction and movement of adjacent teeth (Table S2).

Comparison of clinical follow-up situations

Patients in the cancellous bone group had a mean surgical Duration of 124.38 ± 32.23 min and a mean blood loss of 32.38 ± 12.61 ml. The composite bone group had a mean surgical Duration of 100.94 ± 40.01 min and a mean blood loss of 26.67 ± 13.28 ml. There were no significant differences in surgical time and blood loss between the two groups (P > 0.05).

The peak postoperative Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores were 2.61 ± 1.29 in the composite bone group and 2.14 ± 1.20 in the cancellous bone group, with no statistically significant difference.

The oral soft tissue and iliac incisions of the patients in both groups healed well postoperatively. At suture removal 1–2 weeks post-operatively, standardized photographs of the wounds were taken and blindly scored using the Modified Landry–Turnbull–Howley Healing Index described by Pippi et al. [26] In the cancellous bone group, 15 cases (71.4%) scored 5 and 6 cases (28.6%) scored 6; in the composite bone group, 13 cases (72.2%) scored 5 and 5 cases (27.8%) scored 6. No statistically significant difference in score distribution was observed between the two groups. Within the 6-month observation period after surgery, neither group experienced serious adverse reactions such as severe infection in the oral surgical area, oronasal fistula, or sequestrum formation, nor did they experience complications such as numbness or functional limitation in the iliac surgical area. Impacted teeth and adjacent teeth within the cleft area successfully erupted or moved, with no instances of tooth mobility or loss after movement.

Comparison of hard tissue measurement indicators

Evaluation of bone graft volume and new bone fill rate

In this study the mean preoperative defect volume was 1090.08 ± 455.55 mm [3] for cancellous bone group, and 1166.48 ± 324.96 mm [3] for composite bone group. There was no significant difference in the preoperative defect volume between the cancellous bone group and the composite bone group, which excluded the influence of the size of the defect volume on new bone volume and postoperative outcomes (Fig. 4A). The mean bone graft volume at 6 months postoperatively was 378.23 ± 176.65 mm³ in the cancellous bone group and 698.58 ± 197.43 mm³ in the composite bone group, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.01). The results (Fig. 4B) showed that the composite bone grafting technique significantly increased the proportion of new bone volume fill rate compared to the cancellous bone group (P < 0.01). The results in Fig. 4C showed that the alveolar crest height fill rate was significantly higher in the composite bone group than in the cancellous bone group (P < 0.01). As shown in Fig. 4D, the composite bone group had higher new bone area fill rates at all three height planes compared to the cancellous bone group. However, statistical significance was only observed at plane a (P < 0.01), whereas no significant differences were found at planes b and c (P > 0.05).

Evaluation of alveolar ridge width in the bone graft area

According to the results in Fig. 4E, the alveolar ridge width ratio in the graft side to the contralateral side increased significantly in the a, b, and c planes (all P < 0.01), indicating that the composite bone had a clear advantage over the cancellous bone in maintaining the width of the alveolar ridge.

Evaluation of bone density in the bone graft area

The bone density of the alveolar ridge was reflected by the HU value (Fig. 4F), and the ratio of bone densities in plane a was significantly higher in the composite bone group than in the cancellous bone group (P < 0.01), while in planes b and c, there was no significant difference in the bone density ratio between the two groups (P > 0.05).

Discussion

Bone grafting surgery can provide essential bone support for the eruption of permanent teeth adjacent to the cleft, allowing for the spontaneous eruption of canines and lateral incisors, closure of oronasal fistulas, restoration of dental arch continuity, stabilization of the maxilla, support of the nasal ala base, improvement of nasal appearance, and creation of favorable bony conditions for implantation [21, 27]. However, during the early stages of bone formation, iliac cancellous bone lacks sufficient structural integrity to withstand the pressures exerted by surrounding soft tissues and scar tissues in the surgical area. As a result, it is highly susceptible to compression, which can lead to significant bone resorption [23]. Although iliac cancellous bone aids in the eruption of canines during the mixed dentition period, its high resorption rate often means that patients require multiple bone grafts due to insufficient bone volume [16]. Compared to cancellous bone, iliac cortical bone exhibits lower biological activity, with less porous surface and cellular matrix, providing better structural support and rendering it more resistant to bone resorption [22].

There are numerous studies on the use of cortical bone or block bone to reduce the bone resorption rate [26–28]. Some scholars have employed a technique that involves fixing cortical bone plates with screws on both the labial and palatal sides of the alveolar defect, while filling the intervening space with cancellous bone to repair alveolar clefts [18, 24]. Other researchers have harvested cortical bone from the mandibular ramus or chin for use in bone grafting procedures [19, 29]. Both methods achieving good clinical results. In our study, the grafted composite bone side showed similarity to the contralateral side in terms of alveolar width, the bone density on the grafted side was lower than that on the contralateral side, and the crestal bone resorption was minimal, proving that composite bone is a reliable method for alveolar cleft reconstruction. This is consistent with the conclusions of the forementioned studies. Compared with the surgical procedures used by Omara et al. [18] and Nhan et al. [24]this work has made several modifications. Omara et al. and Nhan et al. used cortical bone to overlap the cleft margins, extending more than 3 mm mesial and distal to the cleft margin, and fixed them with screws, which required a second surgery for removal. In contrast, digital guide plate technology is adopted to harvest cortical bone, which could be precisely fitted into the opening of the cleft margin. Therefore, the integration of digital guide plate technology improves the graft-to-defect precision and reduces the need for intraoperative adjustments. Because the transplanted bone plates fit the defect shape, they can be inserted into the alveolar defect, did not need to be stabilized with a particular fixation technique because the transplanted grafts were locked into the openings of the alveolar process defect, and then firmly wrapped in the mucoperiosteal flap, thus avoiding the need for fixation with screws and eliminating the requirement for a secondary surgery for removal. Mikoya et al. [29] likewise mentioned that in most cases cortical bone plates did not need to be stabilized with a particular fixed technique because the arranged grafts were locked into the openings of the alveolar process defect and then firmly wrapped in mucoperiosteal flaps. The bone provided by the mandible is primarily cortical bone, which lacks the rich cancellous bone content and cannot be compared with the iliac crest in terms of the volume of bone supply [19, 29]. When the alveolar cleft is wide, mandibular bone grafting is not a viable option. However, for narrower clefts, the use of cortical bone from the mandible in combination with tissue-engineered bone may represent an emerging trend [30].

The advantages of three-dimensional assessment exemplified by CBCT are significant [31, 32]. In this study, three-dimensional volume measurements and comparisons of preoperative and postoperative alveolar bone height and width data were also used to accurately reflect the grafting effects of different groups. In this study, it was observed that the new bone fill rate in the composite bone group exceeded that of the cancellous bone group, indicating that the composite bone group had better postoperative outcomes compared to the cancellous bone. This volumetric assessment method offers precise and convenient means of comparing various cleft grafting materials and quantifying surgical techniques. The most crucial objective is to establish bony continuity across the cleft alveolar arch, with sufficient bone volume to support orthodontic tooth movement, ensure the survival of the teeth, and achieve a functional and aesthetically pleasing arch alignment [33, 34]. Adequate width of the bone bridge facilitates the eruption of teeth, particularly canines and lateral incisors, which are crucial for maintaining the aesthetic and functional aspects of the dental arch [20]. Higher bone density provides a better environment for osteointegration, which is the process by which new bone forms and integrates with the grafted bone. This integration is essential for the long-term success of the graft, as it ensures that the bone bridge remains structurally sound and functional [35]. A bone bridge with adequate width and density is less likely to require revision surgeries. So sufficient width and density of the bone bridge can minimize the need for such interventions. In our study, the width and density of the bone bridge of the composite bone group was significantly higher than that of the cancellous bone group at the 6-month follow-up. However, long-term outcomes require further monitoring.

From the mechanical perspective, cortical bone plates, compared to cancellous bone, can resist pressure from the oral and nasal mucosa to a greater extent, maintaining the height of the graft and the width in the labial-palatal direction. For example, cortical bone lining technology was used for alveolar bone grafting in patients, and the results showed that cortical bone lining technology was superior to traditional methods [20]. Cortical bone lining technology can achieve physical closure of nasal side mucosal fistulas under higher difficulty and can exert sufficient pressure on the cancellous bone on the cortical bone plate, demonstrating the mechanical effectiveness of cortical bone. In this work, the ratio of the width of the alveolar crest in the grafted area to the contralateral side in the composite bone group was significantly higher than in the cancellous bone group, showing that composite bone has a clear advantage over cancellous bone in maintaining the width of the alveolar crest. The structural support from cortical bone maintains alveolar ridge width, providing a stable environment for adjacent tooth eruption and improving occlusal function. This finding is similar to the double iliac cortical-cancellous bone plate grafting results of the previous work [18] where all grafted sides showed similarity to the contralateral side in terms of alveolar width and bone density, with minimal resorption. The bone density on the grafted side was slightly lower than that on the normal side, but the difference was within an acceptable range. From the perspective of blood supply system reconstruction, good blood circulation is essential for the graft bone to integrate with the surrounding bone of the defect and is a crucial step in the formation of new bone. Studies have found that the fewer the bone walls, the greater the impact on the vascular, cellular, and structural support required for bone repair, and the poorer the bone volume recovery effect [36, 37]. Although cancellous bone can quickly undergo vascular reconstruction after implantation, its initial strength is poor, and the presence or absence of the palatal bone wall is an important influencing factor for alveolar cleft bone grafting effect. The palatal bone wall may increase the bone contact area and provide a more stable grafting environment, thereby resisting the contraction force of soft tissues and the stimulation of adverse stress, reducing bone resorption, and improving the grafting effect [38]. A large contact area with the graft bed is advantageous for promoting blood supply and facilitating the ingrowth of osteoblasts. In this study, the composite bone demonstrated mechanical resistance comparable to that of block bone and a contact area with the graft bed comparable to that of cancellous bone. Thus, considering both mechanical properties and blood supply, the composite bone is considered to be relatively superior.

It is believed that the grafting effect is better before the eruption of permanent canines [39, 40]. When planning bone grafting for patients with impacted canines, it must be ensured that enhancing the graft’s mechanical support does not hinder the canine’s subsequent eruption. In this work, it is observed that the composite bone grafting performed before eruption did not impede subsequent canine eruption. Therefore, it is believed that composite bone can be used before the eruption of canines. For patients whose canines have already erupted, the transplanted bone lacks the traction of periodontal tissue during the eruption of impacted canines, and the bone graft resorption tends to be more pronounced [41, 42]. Consequently, a more “anti-resorptive” grafting strategy is required. This point is supported by a previous study, which suggested that for older patients whose canines have already erupted, block iliac bone transplantation is better than pure cancellous iliac bone transplantation in retaining bone volume and maintaining bone density [17]. In sum, the composite graft is suitable for patients whether the permanent canine has erupted or not.

Orthodontic treatment combined with bone grafting surgery is currently the standard treatment method for patients with Alveolar cleft [43]. All patients in this study had undergone orthodontic treatment or orthodontic visits before surgery to ensure that the width and height discrepancies of the alveolar bone were corrected, the teeth were aligned, and the edentulous space in the graft area was opened before grafting. An optimal alveolar arch shape can improve the accessibility of the surgical area and create a favorable environment for grafting.

From the perspective of complications, in this study, the oral soft tissue and iliac incisions of both the cancellous bone group and the composite bone group healed well postoperatively, and no adverse reactions in the oral surgical area were observed 6 months postoperatively, and there were no various complications in the iliac surgical area, indicating that there was no significant difference between the composite bone group and traditional cancellous bone grafting from the perspective of complications.

The limitations of this study are as follows: The cases in this study are relatively homogeneous, the sample size is not large enough, and the age range is wide. The six-month follow-up period in this study may limit the evaluation of long-term outcomes. In subsequent studies, we plan to enlarge the cohort and perform age-stratified analyses by dividing patients into a pediatric group and a young-adult group. We will modify bone-harvesting method for the pediatric subgroup, extend the observation period, and thereby enhance the precision, reliability, and clinical relevance of our conclusions.

Conclusion

This work developed a method for alveolar cleft bone grafting assisted by digital guides for autologous iliac cortico-cancellous bone harvesting. The advantages of this method are mainly as follows: (1) Using digital technology, the size and shape of labial and palatal bone defects can be accurately assessed preoperatively. This allows for the precise preparation of bone harvesting guides, simplifies the bone harvesting surgery, and minimizes patient trauma; (2) The composite bone offers excellent mechanical properties, structural support, blood supply reconstruction, and nasal plastic surgery outcomes; (3) Combined with pre-graft orthodontics, the method achieves a high success rate for bone grafting, and the impacted canines in the adjacent area can erupt normally after grafting.
