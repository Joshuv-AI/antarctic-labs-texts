Sinonasal Inflammatory Burden on Computed Tomography in Adults With Obstructive Eustachian Tube Dysfunction: A Case–Control Study
Authors: Dachan Kim, Jeong Gum Lee, Seong Hoon Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionChronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) and Eustachian tube dysfunction (ETD) frequently coexist, yet objective data quantifying their relationship remain scarce. Because both conditions can reflect shared mucosal inflammation, we investigated whether adults with objectively verified ETD harbor a larger paranasal sinus inflammatory burden on computed tomography (CT) than controls, and whether this burden varies by drainage pathway or ear laterality.MethodsThis single‐center, retrospective CT case–control study compared 140 adults who underwent balloon tuboplasty for obstructive ETD (January 2017–April 2025) with 312 vestibular schwannoma controls without inflammatory middle‐ear disease. Sinonasal opacification was graded using the Lund–Mackay (LM) scoring system with an LM score ≥ 5 used as a CRS‐related radiologic threshold. Anterior and posterior compartment scores were analyzed separately. Ear‐level analysis distinguished ipsilateral and contralateral sinonasal cavities. Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher's exact test.ResultsThe total LM score was more than twice as high in the obstructive ETD group as in controls (1.77 ± 2.94 vs. 0.80 ± 1.51; p = 0.002; d = 0.47). The proportion of patients with an LM score ≥ 5 on CT was higher in the obstructive ETD group than in controls (17.9% vs. 4.2%; OR, 5.00; 95% CI, 2.47–10.11; p < 0.001). Both anterior and posterior compartments showed greater opacification in obstructive ETD (p = 0.002 and p < 0.001), with no significant between‐compartment difference in effect size. Ipsilateral and contralateral cavities showed comparable inflammatory burden, and both exceeded controls.ConclusionsAdults evaluated for obstructive ETD had a higher bilateral sinonasal inflammatory burden on CT than controls, involving both anterior and posterior drainage pathways. These findings support awareness of coexisting sinonasal inflammation in obstructive ETD and warrant prospective studies on integrated sinonasal and otologic management.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: balloon Eustachian tuboplasty, chronic rhinosinusitis, computed tomography, Eustachian tube dysfunction, Lund–Mackay score

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) and Eustachian tube dysfunction (ETD) are common debilitating conditions frequently observed together in clinical practice [1, 2, 3, 4]. The close relationship between these two disorders is attributed to their anatomical proximity and shared pseudostratified columnar epithelium lining the paranasal sinuses, nasal cavity, and Eustachian tube [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Inflammatory processes and mucosal edema within the sinonasal region, along with purulent secretions, can extend to and obstruct the Eustachian tube orifice, impairing the vital functions of ventilation, pressure equalization, and mucociliary clearance in the middle ear [9, 10, 11]. This dysfunction can lead to a range of otologic symptoms, including ear fullness, pain, pressure, muffled hearing, and crackling or popping sounds, and can contribute to middle ear pathologies such as otitis media with effusion (OME) and acquired cholesteatoma [12, 13]. Studies have documented a high prevalence of ETD symptoms in patients with CRS, with rates reported between 15% and 68% [2, 10, 11].

Despite this recognized association, prior research has certain limitations. The correlation between subjective patient‐reported symptoms, such as those indicating ETD severity, and objective radiological findings, such as computed tomography (CT) scans for CRS, remains ambiguous or inconsistent across studies. While some studies found little or no correlation between Eustachian tube dysfunction questionnaire (ETDQ‐7) scores and Lund–Mackay (LM) CT scores [8, 10, 11], others indicated significant associations with objective measures such as tympanometry [1, 12] or with specific ear‐related symptom subdomains of the 22‐item Sinonasal Outcomes Test (SNOT‐22) [14]. Furthermore, the diagnosis of ETD lacks a universally accepted standard, with a consensus statement recommending a combination of subjective symptoms and objective findings [8, 15, 16]. Many studies have relied solely on questionnaires and have potentially overlooked objective ETD measures. Additionally, many studies regarding this topic have been constrained by small sample sizes and short follow‐up periods, which limit the generalizability of their findings and the ability to establish definitive causal relationships.

To address these knowledge gaps, we conducted a single‐institution CT‐based case–control study. The primary purpose of this study was to quantitatively assess the sinonasal inflammatory burden in patients with objectively diagnosed obstructive ETD compared with a contemporaneous control group without inflammatory middle‐ear disease. Using the LM scoring system, we aimed to provide robust and objective evidence to clarify the relationship between ETD and CRS. Such data are needed to inform clinical practice regarding the necessity of comprehensive sinonasal evaluation in patients presenting with ETD, and to guide the development of more integrated treatment approaches.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This single‐center, retrospective, CT‐based case–control study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. We reviewed patients who underwent balloon Eustachian tuboplasty (BET) between January 2017 and April 2025. In accordance with previously published criteria [17, 18], obstructive ETD was deemed present when any one of the following objective criteria was met: (1) a modified nine‐step Eustachian‐tube function test showing a maximal middle‐ear pressure change < 13 daPa after a single swallow; (2) sustained negative middle‐ear pressure, defined as a tympanometric peak pressure ≤ −100 daPa on standard 226 Hz tympanometry; or (3) a history of recurrent acute otitis media (AOM), that is, ≥ 3 distinct AOM episodes within 6 months or ≥ 4 episodes within 12 months. A total of 217 patients were enrolled based on the criteria ascertained through a retrospective medical chart review. Subsequently, participants were excluded if: (1) individuals were 17 years of age or younger (6 patients); (2) those without a head‐and‐neck CT scan encompassing the paranasal sinuses and Eustachian tube (8 patients); and (3) patients who had undergone concurrent middle ear surgery such as mastoidectomy, tympanoplasty, and ossiculoplasty at the time of BET (63 patients). Ultimately, 140 participants were included in the final analysis. The control group comprised patients diagnosed with vestibular schwannoma who underwent temporal bone CT imaging during the same period (January 2017–April 2025). To minimize potential confounding from concurrent otologic inflammation, patients were excluded from the control group only if they had known inflammatory middle‐ear disease.

LM Scoring Protocol

High‐resolution CT scans of the paranasal sinuses (1‐mm sections) were reviewed in a bone algorithm window by a rhinology specialist who was blinded to the clinical group allocation. All included scans had adequate bilateral coverage of all six LM subsites. At our institution, the wide‐field temporal bone CT protocol extends from the hard palate to the frontal sinus and skull base, allowing standardized LM scoring. Scans with incomplete coverage of any LM subsite were excluded. Each of the six sinonasal subsites on the left and right sides—maxillary, anterior ethmoid, posterior ethmoid, sphenoid, frontal, and osteomeatal complex (OMC)—was scored as 0 (no opacification), 1 (partial opacification), or 2 (complete opacification), according to the standard LM system. The total bilateral scores ranged from 0 to 24.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 using pandas 1.5, scipy 1.11, and Statsmodels 0.14. Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation (SD) or median (IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons of LM scores were performed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. The proportion of patients with an LM score ≥ 5 between ETD and control patients was compared using Fisher's exact test, and the effect size was expressed as an odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Effect sizes for continuous outcomes were reported as Cohen's d, adjusted to Hedges' g for small sample bias when comparing the anterior and posterior compartments. The Hedges–Olkin z‐test was used to assess whether ASC and PSC effect sizes differed. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For side‐specific analyses, ipsilateral and contralateral LM scores among unilateral BET patients were compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Comparisons with control ears were performed using generalized estimating equation models with patient‐level clustering to account for correlation between two ears from the same individual.

Ethics Approval

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB 4‐2025‐0793). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study, and all data were anonymized using IRB‐approved procedures.

Results

The final analysis included 140 patients with ETD and 312 control patients. Mean age was comparable between groups (47.9 ± 16.5 vs. 48.1 ± 14.6 years; p = 0.864, Welch's t‐test), and sex distribution did not differ significantly (male 49.3% vs. 44.6%; p = 0.406, χ 2 test). Smoking history was also comparable between groups (10.7% vs. 8.0%; p = 0.372). Prior sinonasal surgery was more frequent in the ETD group than in controls, although the absolute number was small (3.6% vs. 0.6%; p = 0.032). Within the ETD cohort, surgery laterality was bilateral in 48 patients (34.3%), left‐sided in 55 (39.3%), and right‐sided in 37 (26.4%), corresponding to 188 diseased ears available for ear‐level analyses (Table 1).

Any sinonasal opacification, defined as an LM score > 0, was more frequently observed in the ETD group than in controls (69 of 140, 49.3% vs. 114 of 312, 36.5%; p = 0.013). Patients with ETD also had a statistically higher mean total LM score than controls (1.77 ± 2.94 vs. 0.80 ± 1.51). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.002, Mann–Whitney U‐test), with a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.47, Figure 1). Consistent with the established literature [19, 20], a total LM score ≥ 5 was used as a CT‐based threshold for greater sinonasal opacification. Twenty‐five of 140 patients with ETD (17.9%) had an LM score ≥ 5 on CT compared with 13 of 312 controls (4.2%). This corresponds to approximately fivefold higher odds of meeting the LM ≥ 5 radiologic threshold among patients with ETD (OR = 5.00, 95% CI 2.47–10.11; p < 0.001, Fisher's exact test, Figure 2).

To capture how sinus drainage direction might shape ETD‐related inflammation, we split the six LM subsites into two functional compartments. The anterior sinonasal compartment (ASC), comprising the frontal, anterior ethmoid, maxillary sinuses, and osteomeatal complex, drains forward through the middle meatus, whereas the posterior sinonasal compartment (PSC), comprising posterior ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses, empties posteriorly into the sphenoethmoidal recess. From our data, the ASC score averaged 1.35 ± 2.09 SD in patients with ETD compared with 0.69 ± 1.29 in controls (p = 0.002), while the PSC score was 0.42 ± 1.11 vs. 0.11 ± 0.42 (p < 0.001). Although both compartments were significantly elevated in ETD, the effect size difference between them was minimal (Hedges g 0.42 vs. 0.44; z = −0.13, p = 0.894), indicating that ETD influences the anterior and posterior drainage pathways to a similar extent (Figure 3).

To examine whether CT‐defined sinonasal opacification was lateralized to the side selected for BET, we performed a side‐specific analysis among patients who underwent unilateral BET. The mean LM score was 0.96 ± 1.66 on the ipsilateral side and 1.07 ± 1.77 on the contralateral side, with no significant ipsilateral–contralateral difference by Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (n = 92; p = 0.173). In generalized estimating equation models accounting for patient‐level clustering, both ipsilateral and contralateral ears showed higher LM scores than control ears (p = 0.002 and p < 0.001, respectively). These findings indicated that the sinonasal inflammatory burden was elevated bilaterally in ETD, independent of the side selected for BET (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this single‐institution, case–control study, we demonstrated that adults with objectively defined obstructive ETD have a higher burden of sinonasal mucosal disease on CT than controls and that this excess inflammation involves both the anterior and posterior drainage pathways as well as both ears of affected patients. Collectively, the data reinforce the concept that ETD is not simply an isolated tubal obstruction, but part of a broader pan‐nasal inflammatory process.

Three converging analyses supported this conclusion. First, the total LM score in ETD cases was more than double that of controls, and one in six ETD patients had an LM score ≥ 5, compared with approximately one in 25 controls. Second, when LM subsites were partitioned according to drainage pathway, both ASC and PSC showed significantly higher scores in ETD patients than in controls. A similar degree of inflammation detected in both the anterior and posterior drainage pathways indicated that the disease was not confined to the middle meatus alone. Instead, it suggests that ETD is accompanied by a wider, sinonasal “field effect,” with mucosal changes extending throughout the entire nasal and sinus complex. Third, ear‐level analysis showed that the contralateral cavity, which is anatomically remote from the operative ear, carried the same inflammatory load as the ipsilateral cavity, reinforcing the bilateral nature of the disease. By showing bilateral compartment‐independent LM elevation, our data suggest that patients with obstructive ETD may benefit from concurrent sinonasal therapy before or alongside BET.

Several pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the clustering of sinonasal inflammation with ETD. Cytokine profiling studies have found elevated levels of interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha in both middle‐ear effusions and CRS sinonasal secretions, indicating a shared pro‐inflammatory milieu [4, 21, 22]. Obstruction of the Eustachian tube orifice by hypertrophic torus tubarius or adenoid tissue can arise from contiguous mucosal edema in CRS, whereas impaired mucociliary clearance, documented in both CRS and ETD, may further prolong pathogen or allergen exposure [20, 21]. In addition, negative middle ear pressure can drive reflex hyperemia of the nasal mucosa, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates both conditions [5, 23].

Our study had several strengths: (i) the largest CT‐based ETD series to date, (ii) objective ETD diagnosis requiring at least one functional or tympanometric abnormality, and (iii) a laterality‐aware analytical framework that clarifies how sinus disease tracks with the affected ear. These features overcome many of the limitations of prior studies, such as the reliance on symptom questionnaires alone or small single‐ear analyses.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design precluded causal inference. Clinical CRS could not be diagnosed because standardized sinonasal symptoms, symptom duration, and nasal endoscopic findings were not consistently available. Therefore, an LM score ≥ 5 on CT should be interpreted as a marker of greater CT‐defined sinonasal opacification, not as confirmed clinical CRS. In addition, CT was assessed at a single time point, and the persistence of sinonasal findings for more than 12 weeks could not be determined. Second, although the control group consisted of otherwise healthy patients with vestibular schwannomas, it may not perfectly represent the general population. Although smoking history and prior sinonasal surgery were assessed, standardized information on sinonasal symptoms, symptom duration, recent upper respiratory tract infection, and allergic rhinitis was not consistently documented, particularly in the control group. Therefore, residual confounding from undocumented sinonasal disease or recent infection cannot be excluded. Third, LM scores capture only structural opacification and cannot discriminate between inflammatory phenotypes (eosinophilic vs. neutrophilic CRS) or subclinical infections. Fourth, subjective symptom assessments, such as the ETDQ‐7, were not included in our analysis, precluding a correlational analysis between objective CT findings and patient‐reported outcomes. Finally, the absence of postoperative data made it impossible to evaluate the therapeutic effects of BET on sinonasal inflammation.

Our findings suggest that clinicians should remain aware of coexisting sinonasal disease in patients with obstructive ETD and consider sinonasal evaluation when clinically indicated. Conversely, sinonasal surgeons should be aware of the possibility of subclinical ETD when treating patients with CRS. Future prospective studies using endoscopic Eustachian tube grading, mucociliary clearance assays, and longitudinal symptom scores will be necessary to unravel temporal relationships and test whether combined medical–surgical treatment of CRS improves outcomes in patients with ETD.
