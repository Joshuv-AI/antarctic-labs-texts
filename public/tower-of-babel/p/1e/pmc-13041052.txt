Background

Approximately 80% of the global cattle population is concentrated in tropical and subtropical regions, including North America (Mexico and southern United States), Central and South America, Africa, Asia, and Oceania [1]. The predominant breeds raised in these regions are Bos taurus indicus, also known as zebu or indicine cattle, along with their crosses with Bos taurus taurus (taurine cattle). Brazil and India are the countries with the largest zebu cattle populations [2]. For example, Brazil has a national beef cattle herd containing approximately 73 million cows [3], in which approximately 90% of the animals have Zebu contributions, mainly from the Nellore breed [4]. Compared with taurine breeds, zebu breeds tend to adapt better to harsh environments [5] and are suitable for tropical regions characterized by high temperatures and humidity, the use of pasture-based diets, and the prevalence of tropical diseases and parasites [6]. The Nellore breed is highly competitive because of its superior female traits, such as high reproductive ability and calf vigor [4].

Understanding the genomic architecture of the Nellore cattle population is essential to optimize the performance of genomic prediction and association studies, as well as to conserve genetic diversity within the breed. Key population parameters, such as linkage disequilibrium, Ne, ROH, and signatures of selection, are often used to genetically characterize populations (e.g., 15, 8, 16, 17, 7, 18). Specifically, for Nellore cattle, a few studies have estimated these population parameters but have used a limited number of genotyped animals. For example, to investigate the level of linkage disequilibrium, previous studies used samples ranging from 100 to 795 genotyped individuals [7, 8]. Studies on ROH have used samples ranging from 278 to 9,386 genotyped animals [9, 10], whereas research on signatures of selection has included between 150 and 763 individuals [11, 12]. More recently, the GPSM method has been proposed to detect polygenic selection within a population [8, 13, 14]. The GPSM method looks for changes in allele frequencies by associating an individual’s generation number (or a suitable proxy) with the allelic frequency [15, 16]. This approach allows for the reconstruction of selection history in a population, and it seems to be effective in identifying QTLs with strong allele frequency changes due to polygenic selection while accounting for population and family stratification. The GPSM method has already been tested in taurine cattle [8, 15] and in purebred and crossbred pigs [10]; however, to the best of our knowledge, it has not yet been used in Nellore cattle.

Therefore, this study aimed to perform a comprehensive genomic characterization of Nellore cattle raised in Brazil via the complete official dataset from the Brazilian Association of Zebu Breeders (ABCZ). The specific objectives of this study were to (1) estimate population parameters (i.e., linkage disequilibrium, Ne, and ROH) to describe the population and (2) investigate the selection architecture, using the GPSM method, to reconstruct the history of selection in Nellore cattle.

Methods

Genotypic data

The ABCZ provided pedigree information for approximately 14 million Nellore animals raised in Brazil. Among these, 309,640 animals were genotyped (or imputed to) with a high-density (HD) SNP panel (777k SNPs). Imputation was performed in two steps. First, all LD and MD genotypes were imputed to a custom SNP panel containing 120,615 unique SNPs. This panel was created by taking the union of all unique SNPs present in the post-QC from two versions of the 50k and 70k panels and removing duplicates after harmonizing genomic positions. Imputation was performed using the FImpute software [15], and the overall imputation accuracy exceeded 0.93 for all SNP panels. Additional details about the imputation process for this population are provided in Campos et al. (2025). A summary of the number of animals and SNPs in each panel is shown in the Supplementary Material S1. After imputation, quality control was performed, and SNPs with a genotyping call rate (CR) < 0.90, minor allelic frequency (MAF) < 0.01, and deviation from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) > 0.15 were removed. Finally, a total of 305,181 animals genotyped with 391,721 SNPs remained after quality control for further analysis.

Population parameters

Linkage disequilibrium

Pairwise linkage disequilibrium (r2) was estimated via the –r2 flag available in PLINK 1.9 software [16]. Thus, linkage disequilibrium was calculated as the squared correlation between two alleles at different loci. The r2 values between all pairs of SNPs from all autosomes were grouped according to pairwise physical distances into intervals of 10 kb for distances ranging from 0 to 100 kb, 50 kb for distances ranging from 100 kb to 1 Mb, and 500 kb for distances ranging from 1 Mb to 10 Mb. The overall mean values of r2 for SNP pairs in each interval were obtained by calculating the means across all autosomes.

Runs of homozygosity

PLINK software [16] was used to identify ROHs, following the parameters used by Mulim et al. [21]. The ROHs were defined by sliding windows of 50 SNPs, which require a minimum of 30 consecutive SNPs to characterize the ROH, and a minimum length of 500 kb. A SNP density of at least 1 SNP per 50 kb was needed, with a maximum gap between consecutive SNPs set at 1000 kb. A window threshold of 0.05 was applied, allowing for 1 SNP heterozygous and 1 missing SNP in each window. Once identified, the ROHs were categorized into groups on the basis of length: <2 Mb, 2–4 Mb, 4–8 Mb, 8–16 Mb, and > 16 Mb. The ROH that exhibited an unusually high frequency in the population (i.e., ROH that appeared in at least 50% plus one animal) were defined as ROH islands in this study [21].

Generation proxy selection mapping

We used the GSPM approach to detect alleles whose frequency changed over time [8, 14]. This method uses a genome-wide linear mixed model with an individual’s generation number as a dependent variable. We analyzed two subsets of data by randomly selecting 50,000 individuals for each scenario. The first subset included only animals born between 2009 and 2022 (YOUNG), which is when more genotyped animals started to become available. The second subset considered all birth years (FULL). The number of genotyped animals per year of birth is included in the Supplementary Material S2.

Investigation of the identified genomic regions and functional analyses

Significant genomic regions identified in the GSPM analyses, along with the SNP markers present in the ROH islands identified in this study, were selected for gene annotation and functional analysis. The GALLO package [23] was used to perform gene and QTL annotation via Bos taurus data from the Ensembl database (version ARS-UCS1.2; www.ensembl.org/Bos_taurus/Info/Index) [24] and the Animal QTL database [25], respectively. A genomic window spanning 100 kb upstream and downstream of the markers was considered. Functional enrichment of the annotated genes was carried out with the gprofiler2 R package [26]. Analyses were performed for Bos taurus (“btaurus”) against Gene Ontology (biological process, molecular function, cellular component) and pathway databases (KEGG, Reactome, WikiPathways). We considered only statistically significant terms (p < 0.05) using FDR correction for multiple testing. Results were summarized by retaining enriched terms with adjusted P-values < 0.05.

Results

Population parameters

Linkage disequilibrium and effective population size

Linkage disequilibrium levels can indicate the probability of success when genomic selection is implemented in a population. In Nellore cattle, the average r2 ± SD and distance between adjacent SNPs across all chromosomes were 0.282 ± 0.271 and 31,567.6 bp, respectively. The analysis revealed that linkage disequilibrium decreased quickly as the physical distance between the markers increased (Fig. 1A). For example, at distances of 40–60 kb, the average r2 was 0.189 ± 0.253, and between 0.1 and 0.5 Mb, the average r2 was 0.079 ± 0.134. The average r2 decreased significantly as the distance between the markers increased. It decreased to approximately 40% of its initial value by ~ 40 kb. The average linkage disequilibrium was 0.543 ± 0.405 at distances of 1 kb and 0.234 ± 0.288 at distances of 40 kb.

A Decay of linkage disequilibrium (r2) as a function of the intermarker distance of the Nellore population; (B) estimated effective population size (Ne) as a function of past generations of the Nellore population

Figure 1B shows the Ne decay over generations. The Ne was determined to be 225 in the current Nellore population. However, Nellore’s decreasing historical trend of Ne was reversed in the fifth generation, followed by an increase up to the current generation. The Ne decreased from 50 (i.e., Ne equal to 505) to 5 (Ne equal to 196) generations ago and began to increase again from 5 to 1 generation ago. Overall, the observed trend for Ne reflects the historical process of domestication and breed formation.

Runs of homozygosity

The classification and distribution of the ROHs per chromosome, as well as the percentage of the genome covered by ROHs, are shown in Fig. 2. Most ROHs were classified as < 2 Mb, representing approximately 83.25% of the ROHs. Long ROHs (> 8 Mb) represented approximately 3.13% of the cases. On average, the animals had 154.92 ± 20.90 ROH, with an average length of 1.63 ± 0.40 Mb. All the animals had at least one ROH identified. With respect to ROH coverage, BTA28 had the highest proportion covered by ROHs (approximately 5.12% of the chromosomes were covered by ROHs).

Generation proxy mapping selection

The GREML method was used to estimate the PVE by SNPs in Nellore cattle via imputed HD data from both the FULL and YOUNG subsets. The birth year was used as the dependent variable to represent the generation proxy. The estimated PVE was 0.591 (0.006) for the FULL subset and 0.560 (0.006) for the YOUNG subset. In this context, after the Bonferroni correction, the GPSM approach identified 47 significant SNPs for the FULL subset and 77 significant SNPs for the YOUNG subset. All significant SNPs in the FULL subset were also present in the YOUNG subset. Three of the five most significant SNPs were found in both subsets. The YOUNG subset presented more significant SNPs, indicating more consistent genotype sampling across birth years. Fig. 4 shows the allele frequency patterns over time for the five most significant SNPs in each subset. The average allele frequencies related to the five most significant SNPs changed over time for both subsets across the years (the slopes of the regressions ranged from − 0.006 to 0.007).

Discussion

Population parameters

The linkage disequilibrium levels indicate the potential for success in the implementation of genomic selection in a population. On the basis of the findings of Meuwissen et al. [27], for genomic selection to be effective, the average r2 should be greater than 0.2, which is the case in the Brazilian Nellore cattle population. The average r2 (0.28) determined via imputed data for the entire population was slightly greater than that reported in other studies involving Nellore cattle (Fig. 1A). For example, in a study performed by Espigolan et al. [9] using a sample of 995 genotyped Nellore bulls, the average r2 between marker pairs (measured using r2) was 0.17. When investigating linkage disequilibrium levels in various indicine and taurine breeds, O’Brien et al. [7] reported that the use of an HD SNP panel with an average SNP distance of approximately 5 kb can yield r2 values of approximately 0.39 for indicine cattle and 0.59 for taurine cattle. This discrepancy is due to lower r2 values in Zebu breeds, such as Nellore, as a result of higher heterozygosity and genetic variability than in taurine breeds [7]. Consequently, Bos taurus indicus cattle exhibit less linkage disequilibrium and more rapid decay at shorter distances than Bos taurus cattle do [28].

Previous studies investigating population parameters in Nellore cattle often relied on considerably smaller datasets, different SNP densities, or reduced genomic coverage, which likely contributed to discrepancies relative to our findings. For instance, LD and ROH patterns are known to be highly sensitive to the number of individuals analyzed and marker density; therefore, studies using 50 K or medium-density panels typically capture shorter LD phases and underestimate long ROH segments. In contrast, the present study incorporated more than 300,000 animals imputed to a high-density 777 K panel, resulting in more accurate haplotype reconstruction and greater power to detect long-range autozygosity and subtle selection signatures. Differences in linkage map resolution, quality-control thresholds, and sampling strategies across years also contribute to variation between studies. Together, these factors help explain why our estimates of LD decay, ROH distributions, and selection signals diverge from earlier reports that evaluated substantially smaller genomic resources.

Ne can be used to predict the effective number of independent chromosome segments (Me) represented in a population [29]. Furthermore, the accuracy of genomic selection is closely linked to Ne, as higher accuracy is achieved with lower Me [30]. Cattle populations with high Ne, such as indicine breeds, exhibit lower relatedness among individuals, decreased linkage disequilibrium, and a greater Me. In our study, the Nellore cattle population presented a greater Ne than the taurine breeds did (Fig. 1B). For example, Campos et al. [31] calculated an Ne of 197 for Brazilian Angus cattle on the basis of a sample of 1,295 animals. Biegelmeyer et al. [32] estimated Ne values of 174 for Hereford populations and 220 for Braford populations via linkage disequilibrium analysis. Figure 2B shows an increase in the Ne trend in the last five generations, possibly due to efforts to diversify the population by introducing new animals from India in recent years (personal information). Nevertheless, Ne values from more recent generations should be interpreted with caution, as the method used in this study may not be accurate for recent generations.

With respect to the ROHs (Fig. 2), most identified ROHs were small in length, indicating that they were created in more ancient generations [33]. It is estimated that these ROHs were formed 25–50 generations ago and have persisted in the population over time [34]. Similarly, the same genomic region identified in this study as an island (Fig. 3) was also found by Ventura et al. [35] in Nellore cattle (BTA7: 46.04–46.67 Mb). This suggests a possible signature of selection in the Nellore population for this region. The consistency in findings between different studies emphasizes the robustness of the identified regions under selection, reinforcing the significance of these genomic regions in the Nellore cattle population.

The genes within this region were identified as candidate genes associated with carcass merit, particularly average backfat thickness (i.e., JADE2, SAR1B, SEC24A) and hot carcass weight (DDX46) [36]. Additionally, the CASPER3 gene was identified as an important gene related to sperm hyperactivated motility and male fertility in various mammalian species (e.g. [37, 38]), . Notably, gene ontology terms related to reproduction and fertility were also identified in the analysis. The identification of ROHs and their associated genes provides insights into the historical breeding practices that are being used to select the Nellore cattle population in Brazil.

Generation proxy selection mapping

Over time, the artificial selection of beef cattle for economically important traits by breeding values and selection indices has led to changes in allele frequency at hundreds or even thousands of causal loci. This shift also affects nearby loci linked to the selected locus due to the linkage disequilibrium between them [13]. However, mapping this type of selection (i.e., polygenic selection) is more challenging than identifying selective sweeps because it does not leave prominent specific signatures of selection on the genome [14] and creates only modest changes in allele frequency. With the increase in the number of genotyped animals over time, including influential sires, dams, and individuals in recent generations, the data can be used to detect small changes in allele frequencies due to ongoing selection [16, 39]. Genotyped animals from different categories provide valuable data that can be used to detect small shifts in allele frequencies due to ongoing selection [14]. These changes occur because alleles differ between older generations and more recent generations.

The PVE when an individual’s birth year was used as a dependent variable was found to be consistent with other studies in beef cattle utilizing the GPSM approach [12, 24]. For example, Rowan et al. [12] determined the PVE to be 0.52 and 0.62 for the Simmental and Red Angus breeds in the US, respectively. In a separate study involving three beef cattle breeds [24], reported that the PVE ranged between 0.45 and 0.59. The authors also revealed, on the basis of a simulation study, that PVE is influenced by population demographics, the intensity of selection, and genotyping sampling over the years. Therefore, our results suggest that the PVE is not equal to a unit (1) because not all animals in our population were genotyped and because the genotypes are not evenly distributed across years.

We used the GPSM method to identify several important SNPs associated with birth year in Nellore cattle. Owing to computational limitations, two subsets of 50,000 individuals were used for the GPSM analyses. Despite the reduced sample size, we were able to obtain meaningful results. This reduction was necessary, as obtaining for more than 100,000 genotyped animals is challenging [40]. One potential approach for testing large datasets in GWASs is the single-step genomic BLUP (ssGBLUP) [41], which uses the algorithm for proven and young (APY; [42]). In a study by Leite et al. [41] involving 50,000 genotyped animals and 1.5 million individuals in the pedigree, the same genomic regions with p values were identified via different methods. Additionally, obtaining p values via the approximation based on significantly reduces both the computational time and memory requirements compared with the exact inversion of . Therefore, we believe that future studies using large datasets could test the potential of ssGBLUP using APY for optimizing GPSM analyses.

According to the simulated results presented by Rowan et al. [14], another potential challenge for the GPSM method is the distribution of generation proxy phenotypes due to nonnormality in genotyped livestock populations. Our analysis of the residuals indicated that they followed a normal distribution, so we decided not to use transformed phenotypes. However, we split our dataset into two subsets (i.e., FULL and YOUNG) to validate our findings, as the YOUNG subset has a more uniform distribution of genotypes over the years than does the FULL subset. As our results were similar, with over 50% of the SNPs in common between both subsets (the YOUNG subset identified all the SNPs identified by the FULL subset plus additional ones), we decided to focus on the discussion about the FULL subset. Detailed information about the YOUNG subset is available in Supplementary Material S4.

The GPSM approach detected small changes in allele frequencies over time, as expected when the selection of quantitative traits is ongoing (Fig. 4). The method identified several SNPs significantly associated with birth year across all chromosomes, as shown in Supplementary Material S4, reinforcing the idea that selection in this population is likely polygenic. This idea is supported by the selection indices used by ABCZ, which have changed over the years and focused on several traits. For example, the original selection criteria used in Nellore cattle, which focused mainly on type traits and weight, have been replaced by a more balanced selection index over the years. The main selection indices currently used by ABCZ (iABCZ) include growth traits (e.g., weaning, yearling weight, and postweaning gain), reproduction traits (e.g., age at first calving, stayability and scrotal circumference), and maternal traits (e.g., milk and total maternal). Although the GPSM analysis captured consistent allele frequency shifts across birth years, we acknowledge that part of the temporal variation may also reflect random genetic drift, particularly in years with fewer influential sires or uneven genotyping representation across cohorts. Drift and sampling variance can generate modest frequency changes even in the absence of deliberate selection. Nonetheless, the recurrence of significant signals in both the FULL and YOUNG subsets, and their overlap with known QTLs for economically important traits, suggests that directional selection contributes to the observed patterns. Nonetheless, it is important to mention that GPSM alone cannot fully disentangle genetic drift from true selection pressure.

After performing enrichment analyses to understand the traits under selection in the Nellore population, we identified multiple significant QTLs for several economically important traits in both subsets (Figs. 5 and 6). For example, we pinpointed significant QTL enrichment for production, carcass traits, and maternal ability, as well as QTLs related to male and female reproductive traits (Fig. 6). Interestingly, we also identified a QTL linked to disease and tick resistance, possibly due to historical selection for adaptation traits in Zebu breeds.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that the Brazilian Nellore population exhibits levels of linkage disequilibrium compatible with successful implementation of genomic selection, and that the estimated effective population size remains sufficient to maintain genetic diversity. The ROH pattern indicates that most homozygous segments originate from older ancestral generations, with only one major ROH island suggesting potential historical selection pressure. The GPSM results revealed genomic regions undergoing small but consistent allele frequency shifts over time, reflecting ongoing directional forces likely associated with selection practiced in the breed. While these patterns indicate genomic changes consistent with selection, evaluating the realized effectiveness of selection would require analyses of genetic trends for specific traits, which were beyond the scope of this study. The SNPs and QTLs identified here provide valuable candidate regions for future studies and for designing customized genomic tools for Nellore cattle.
