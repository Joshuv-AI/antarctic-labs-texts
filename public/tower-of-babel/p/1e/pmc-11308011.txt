A Glimpse Beyond Typical Dyspnea
Authors: Miriam Compagnone, Fabio Felice Tarantino, Simone Grotti, Gabriele Pestelli, Marcello Galvani, Gianni Dall'Ara
Published: 2023

A 69-year-old man with persistent breathlessness underwent a diagnostic workup that ruled out common possible causes, such as respiratory disease and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Clinical suspicion and specific imaging tests allowed the diagnosis of the rare platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome (POS), secondary to a patent foramen ovale (PFO). In this context, the PFO tunnel and the intracardiac shunt are often large due to cardiac conditions favoring blood shunting and a distorted geometry, a consequence of concomitant thoracic anatomical alterations. Effective percutaneous PFO sealing was obtained using a device dedicated to atrial septal defect closure because of the large size and the aim of obtaining immediate relief from symptoms. Intraprocedural sizing was performed with techniques based on PFO tunnel stretching, to determine its actual size.

Clinical case

A 69-year-old man was recurrently admitted to hospital for a 2-month history of breathlessness, dry cough, and worsening dyspnea on effort. Fever, chest pain, and palpitation were absent. Treatment with steroids and antibiotics was started without clinical improvement. The medical history included systemic hypertension, dyslipidemias, previous smoking habit, a remote myocardial infarction treated with percutaneous revascularization, and SARS-CoV-2 infection 6 months before. His chronic medical therapy consisted of acetylsalicylic acid, ivabradine, and statin.

He was referred to the Department of Pulmonary Disease for hypoxemic normocapnic respiratory failure, and oxygen therapy was required. His body mass index was 31 kg/m2, and lung auscultation revealed mild crackles in the right lung base. Laboratory tests excluded anemia, thyroid dysfunction, and kidney failure. The electrocardiogram was normal, while chest x-ray showed an interlobular septal thickening. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) documented left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, normal ejection fraction, and moderate left atrial enlargement. Valvular disease and pulmonary hypertension were excluded. A high-resolution computed tomography chest scan showed bilateral, peripheral, and reticular changes without ground glass opacities or honeycombing patterns. Pulmonary function tests revealed only a mild impairment of vital capacity and a low reduced diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide. In consideration of the mild interstitial lung disease, which did not justify the clinical status, the diagnostic suspicion was heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). However, during hospitalization, clinical improvement was not achieved despite diuretic therapy, and a significant pulse oximetry reduction was observed upon standing up and resuming walking. POS was hypothesized. Transcranial Doppler demonstrated the presence of a shunt. Moreover, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a PFO, with a large tunnel (9 mm), severe right-to-left shunt during Valsalva maneuver, ectatic thoracic aorta, atrial septum aneurysm, and persistent Eustachian valve (Figure 1A-C). PFO is typically associated with paradoxical thromboembolic episodes; therefore a cerebral computed tomography scan was performed and showed a previous silent focal cerebellar ischemic event. The patient underwent percutaneous PFO closure with deployment of an Amplazer Septal Occluder 11 mm (Abbott). The correct sizing was obtained using intraprocedural TEE measurement, plus inflation of a 24 mm balloon across the PFO (Figure 1D-F). Discussion

Platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome is a rare disorder characterized by dyspnea (platypnea) and arterial desaturation (orthodeoxia) in the upright position with improvement in the supine position. Diagnostic criteria include a decrease in arterial oxygen pressure >4 mm Hg, or oxygen saturation >5% while standing.1 In addition to PFO and atrial septal defects (ASD), this syndrome has been described in association with intrapulmonary shunting (eg, pulmonary arteriovenous malformation), hepatopulmonary syndrome, ventilation-perfusion mismatch (eg, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease), and in case of amiodarone-related pulmonary toxicity.2 However, the diagnosis can be quite challenging even after careful clinical evaluation, imaging, and pathological tests, especially in patients with multiple possible causes of dyspnea. In this report, the patient was obese and had a history of ischemic heart disease and diastolic dysfunction that could be associated with HFpEF.3 In addition, the presence of interstitial lung disease may have also contributed to a ventilation-perfusion mismatch phenomenon. The careful clinical evaluation, step-by-step execution of diagnostic investigations, and multidisciplinary approach were key factors for the correct diagnosis. We acknowledge that an invasive hemodynamic evaluation, eventually with diastolic stress testing, could have helped in the differential diagnosis of HFpEF. Nevertheless, in our opinion, POS should be strongly suspected in cases of dyspnea sine materia and poor response to diuretic therapy, because late diagnosis often causes chronic disabling symptoms. Pulse oximetry test in different positions and TTE with bubble-test should be performed earlier in the workup. In the absence of clinical suspicion, a PFO may result undetected during TTE evaluation, as in our case. However, some “red flags” such as ectatic aorta, atrial septum aneurysm, and persistent Eustachian valve should have raised attention in identifying an intracardiac shunt.

Noteworthy, PFO anatomy is often distorted by an altered relationship between mediastinal structures, for instance, due to aortic enlargement, kyphoscoliosis, diaphragmatic paralysis, or paraesophageal hernia.2 Intraprocedural TEE study measured a large tunnel allowing a continuous shunt, a condition that functionally resembles an ASD more than the valve-like mechanism of the classic PFO. Therefore, PFO closure is often more challenging, particularly because the closure device might not completely seal the orifice. In our case, we obtained TEE tunnel sizing while the supportive guide was positioned through the PFO, stretching its inferior-superior diameter, and by the inflated balloon technique.4 Notably, balloon inflation can be TEE-guided until the shunting through the tunnel stops. We chose a device typically used for ASD closure, obtaining complete sealing with no residual shunt and immediate symptom relief. One reason is purely anatomical. In case of a large channel (ie, from 8-9 mm upwards), a self-centering ASD closure device could be more effective in obtaining adequate sealing, provided there is sufficient space to avoid contact with adjacent structures (eg, aorta). A second reason is that in POS, an effective closure of the PFO is pursued to immediately relieve symptoms. This approach may differ from that of the secondary prevention of stroke where, occasionally, a significant reduction of the shunt is accepted at the time of the procedure, awaiting complete PFO sealing in the following weeks thanks to device endothelization.

Conclusion

Platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome is responsible for chronic and disabling symptoms that pose a problem of differential diagnosis with other causes of dyspnea. Treatment by PFO closure can be decisive although it may be complex due to anatomical peculiarity.
