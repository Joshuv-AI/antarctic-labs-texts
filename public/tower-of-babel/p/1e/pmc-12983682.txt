Reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the Nurse Prescribing Self-Efficacy Scale
Authors: Mengxin Xue, Xu Cheng, Yanfei Sun, Jianhui Jiao, Huiwen Xu, Qian Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to translate the Nurse Prescribing Self-Efficacy Scale (NP-SES) into Chinese and evaluate its reliability and validity.DesignA systematic methodological framework was used.SettingThe study’s setting was hospitals and nursing schools in Jiangsu province, China.ParticipantsA convenience sample of 309 practising nurses and nursing graduates was recruited.Outcome measuresThe reliability and validity of the scale were assessed. Item screening was based on critical ratio values and item–total correlation coefficients. Validity was evaluated through content validity, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability.ResultsEFA identified three common factors, accounting for 78.608% of the total variance. CFA indicated that model fit indices met acceptable thresholds, supporting the scale’s structural validity. Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability for subdimensions ranged from 0.907 to 0.957 and from 0.867 to 0.944, respectively.ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the NP-SES demonstrates good reliability and validity and is suitable for assessing clinical nurses’ self-efficacy in prescribing.Keywords: China, Clinical Decision-Making, Nursing research, Nurses

Introduction

Major global health challenges, including ageing populations, the high prevalence of chronic diseases and shortages of physicians, have prompted many countries and regions to expand and strengthen the roles of health professionals such as nurses, midwives and others in the health system.1 Expanding the responsibilities of nurses is particularly critical in addressing these issues. Evidence indicates that nurse prescribing is as effective as physician prescribing across various conditions, including chronic diseases. Furthermore, nurse prescribing increases patient access, reduces treatment time and healthcare costs, improves the quality of healthcare services and has a positive effect on disease management. It also enhances nurses’ professional identity.2,4 Currently, countries such as the USA, the UK and Australia have legislated nurse prescribing rights, a measure that effectively alleviates shortages in medical resources and insufficient service coverage.5 Nurse prescribing refers to the authority of nursing practitioners to prescribe the most effective treatment for a patient’s health issue within nursing practice.6 The International Council of Nurses has issued the world’s first Guidelines on Prescriptive Authority for Nurses, which acknowledges the vital role of nurses in promoting public health and calls for measures to advance nurse prescribing rights globally, reflecting that granting such rights has become a global trend.7

In contrast to registered nurses, Chinese physicians undergo longer training and are relatively fewer in number, resulting in a mismatch between available medical resources and patient healthcare need.8 9 Although nurse prescribing has not yet been fully authorised by national law in China, there have been recent exploratory efforts. For example, in 2018, the People’s Government of Beijing Municipality advocated for the training of advanced practice nurses with limited prescriptive authority.10 Between 2020 and 2021, several members of the National People’s Congress and the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference proposed granting trained and qualified nurses the authority to prescribe specific categories of drugs.11 The National Health Council responded by initiating a study on the feasibility of granting nurses prescriptive authority.7 In 2017, the Anhui Provincial Health Commission piloted nurse prescribing and granted non-drug prescribing rights to 78 nurses, yielding positive results.12 In 2022, Shenzhen revised the Medical Regulations of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone, leading the country in granting partial prescribing rights to specialised nurses through local legislation.13 These developments indicate that, in response to real needs and national priorities, granting qualified nurses in China prescribing rights is both a developmental trend and a key strategy to optimise nursing resource allocation and address population health needs.8

Clinical training and research on nurse prescriptive authority have been implemented in some regions of Anhui Province and in Hong Kong and Taiwan, with certain effectiveness.8 14 15 Nonetheless, nurse prescribing practice faces various internal and external barriers.5 16 External obstacles often include physicians’ attitudes towards nurse prescribing17 and insufficient organisational support.18 Internal barriers are primarily related to the nursing population, such as nurses’ educational background,15 lack of confidence in prescribing judgement16 and low self-efficacy regarding their prescribing authority.19 For instance, a study reported that nurses attending a national prescribing course expressed less confidence in their prescribing decisions during case studies than medical and pharmacy students, which hinders the advancement of nurse prescribing.20 Fong et al have argued that confidence in prescribing may positively affect patient care and professional development for practising nurses.16 Therefore, to ensure the effectiveness and safety of nurse prescribing, it is necessary to assess nurses’ self-efficacy in prescribing, which informs the development and implementation of educational interventions to enhance this competency.19

Bandura defines self-efficacy as an individual’s judgement of their capability to organise and execute actions required to achieve specific behavioural goals.21 Self-efficacy, or perceived confidence in one’s abilities (ie, self-efficacy), may more accurately predict motivation and professional growth than objective ability assessments.22 Individuals with high self-efficacy are typically more motivated and inclined to select tasks that facilitate knowledge acquisition, skill development and competency enhancement, thereby advancing their professional growth.22 23 Bandura emphasises that only specific self-efficacy accurately explains and predicts targeted behaviours.21 This implies that common self-efficacy assessment tools cannot accurately and comprehensively evaluate nurses’ confidence in prescribing.24

Current research on assessment tools in the field of nurse prescribing remains scarce. Existing studies primarily focus on surveying nurses’ perceptions and attitudes toward prescribing, such as the Nurse Authority Beliefs and Attitudes in Nursing Practice scale and surveys on knowledge and attitudes related to nurse prescribing authority.25 26 Self-efficacy plays a pivotal role in the practice of nurse prescribing. Therefore, comprehensive and scientifically sound measurement of this concept is particularly crucial for assessing nurses’ confidence levels in prescribing and developing targeted intervention strategies.

As far as we know, the Nurse Prescribing Self-Efficacy Scale (NP-SES), developed by Galiana-Camacho et al, is currently the only instrument with demonstrated reliability and validity for assessing self-efficacy in the nurse prescribing process.19 This scale demonstrates sufficient reliability and validity in assessing nurses’ self-efficacy in prescribing, thereby facilitating the design of targeted interventions to enhance job performance. The NP-SES comprises three dimensions: clinical assessment and pharmacological knowledge, supplemental prescribing and evidence-based practice and independent prescribing and patient education. Systematic measurement of these dimensions in clinical nurses allows for the identification of specific competency gaps and supports the development of targeted competency-building systems centred on prescribing rights. The NP-SES has demonstrated favourable psychometric properties among Spanish-speaking and Galician-speaking nurses.19 27 However, the absence of a validated Chinese version of the NP-SES (NP-SES-C) limits the ability to assess this competency reliably among nurses in China. Without a culturally adapted and psychometrically sound instrument, systematic evaluation, cross-regional comparison and evidence-based training in this field remain constrained. In this context, the present study systematically examines the psychometric properties of the NP-SES-C to provide a standardised tool for evaluating prescribing self-efficacy among Chinese nurses.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in Jiangsu province, China from May to June 2023. Convenience sampling was used to recruit eligible participants. The inclusion criteria were: (1) nursing graduates or clinical nurses holding a certificate of nursing practice in China, (2) aged 18 years or older and (3) voluntary participation with signed informed consent. The sample size was determined as 5–10 times the number of items in the scale.28 Given that the NP-SES comprises 19 items, a minimum of 95 cases was required. Considering the requirements for validity testing, particularly the non-duplication of exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) samples, a minimum of 200 cases was required for CFA.29 Accordingly, at least 295 participants were necessary: 95 for EFA and 200 for CFA. The study adhered strictly to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination of our study as it pertained to nurses and nursing graduates only.

Instruments

Demographic characteristics questionnaire

The questionnaire collected information on gender, age, education, hospital level, professional title and years of work experience.

Nurse Prescribing Self-Efficacy Scale

The NP-SES, developed by Galiana-Camacho et al, was used to assess nurses’ self-efficacy in the prescribing process.19 The scale includes 19 items across three dimensions: clinical assessment and pharmacological knowledge (items 1–5), supplemental prescribing and evidence-based practice (items 7–10) and independent prescribing and patient education (items 6 and 11–19). Each item is scored on an 11-point scale, ranging from ‘absolutely cannot do’ to ‘absolutely can do’, with responses from 0 to 100 in 10-point increments. The total possible score is 1900, with higher scores reflecting greater self-efficacy in prescribing. The original scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s α of 0.96.

Procedures

Scale translation

With authorisation from Dr Teresa Galiana-Camacho, the original developer, the NP-SES was translated according to Brislin’s Translation Model and Guidelines for Cross-Cultural Adaptation.30 31 First, two independent Chinese versions were produced by an English-speaking expert and a nursing expert fluent in English. These were reconciled through discussion with a researcher to generate Version A. Second, Version A was back-translated into English by two separate experts with no prior knowledge of the NP-SES. The resulting back-translations were reviewed and discussed with a researcher to form back-translated Version B. Third, Version B was sent to the original authors for feedback, which was incorporated through further expert consultation to produce Version C. Fourth, Version C was used for expert consultation, with six experts invited by email to evaluate the relevance and clarity of each item and suggest modifications. The results of this consultation were discussed by the research team to generate Version D. Finally, a pre-survey was administered to 15 clinical nurses who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Feedback regarding comprehension and acceptability was gathered, and the final version (Version E) was established after further discussion among team members and translation experts.

Data collection

The questionnaire was first distributed to faculty at nursing schools or head nurses in hospitals online, who were informed of the inclusion and exclusion criteria and survey details. Then, they used WeChat to forward it to the nurses and graduates. The survey was administered using the ‘Questionnaire Star’ platform (Wenjuanxing, http://www.wjx.cn). An introductory page explained the study purpose and procedures, with participation being voluntary. Participants were assured of confidentiality, and all data were used solely for research purposes. A total of 343 questionnaires were collected; after excluding invalid responses, 309 valid questionnaires remained, resulting in a validity rate of 90.1%, which met the minimum sample size requirements.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.22.0 and AMOS V.24.0. Categorical variables were described by frequency and percentage, and continuous variables by mean and SD. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participants’ characteristics. Item analysis included calculation of item–total correlation coefficients and critical ratio (CR) values. Items with correlation coefficients >0.4 and CR values >3 were considered to have good discrimination and representativeness.32 33 Validity was assessed via content and construct validity. Content validity was evaluated using the Item Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and Scale Content Validity Index (S-CVI), with values >0.78 considered acceptable.34 35 Construct validity was assessed through EFA and CFA. EFA was conducted after confirming sample adequacy with Bartlett’s test of sphericity and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin index.36 37 CFA was performed in AMOS to evaluate model fit, with all model fit indices required to meet acceptable thresholds.38 Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability, with values ≥0.70 considered acceptable.39 Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Experts’ consultation

Six experts from Jiangsu Province completed the expert consultation, all of whom were women. The average length of work experience is 25.00±9.56 years. Among them, there is one person with an intermediary professional title and five with senior professional titles: one nursing education specialist and five nursing administration and clinical nursing specialists (covering internal medicine, surgery, infectious diseases, traditional Chinese medicine and maternal and child health). Five individuals hold bachelor’s degrees, and one holds a doctoral degree.

Pre-survey

A total of 15 questionnaires were distributed and all were recovered. The effective questionnaire recovery rate was 100 %, the completion time was 10–20 min, and there was no modification or supplement.

Pilot survey

Sample characteristic (participants characteristics)

A total of 309 valid questionnaires were analysed. The majority of participants were female (90.0%), and most were aged between 20 and 30 years (58.9%). Participants comprised both nursing graduates (n=138) and clinical nurses (n=171). Most held a bachelor’s degree or above (91.6%). Approximately 41.4% were employed at tertiary hospitals. Notably, 34.3% (n=106) had more than 10 years of professional experience, whereas 44.7% (n=138) were graduate nurses without professional experience. Validity

Content validity

Content validity of the NP-SES-C was assessed using the I-CVI and the S-CVI. An I-CVI value of ≥0.78 was considered acceptable. Six experts independently rated the relevance of each item to its theoretical dimension using a four-point Likert scale (1=not relevant, 2=somewhat relevant, 3=quite relevant, 4=highly relevant). Responses of 3 or 4 were categorised as ‘relevant’, whereas responses of 1 or 2 were considered ‘not relevant’. All items achieved I-CVI values above 0.78. The S-CVI, calculated as the mean I-CVI across all items, was 0.842, indicating acceptable content validity. Expert ratings are provided in table 4.

Construct validity

EFA (sample 1 n=109)

The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value for the NP-SES-C was 0.955, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (2245.241; p<0.001), confirming suitability for factor analysis. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was conducted, and the number of extracted factors was fixed at three, consistent with the original scale structure. The cumulative variance explained was 78.608%. Factor loadings are provided in table 5. Double loading was observed for items 5, 6 and 11, and the extracted factor structure differed from that of the original scale. Following established recommendations,29 further analysis and consultation with six experts confirmed that items 5, 6 and 11 should be classified according to the dimensions of the original scale. Specifically, item 5 was retained under the factor ‘Clinical assessment and pharmacological knowledge’, while items 6 and 11 were retained under the factor ‘Independent prescribing and patient education’. The NP-SES-C scale maintains consistency with the original scale, with common factors comprising clinical assessment and pharmacological knowledge (items 1–5), supplemental prescribing and evidence-based practice (items 7–10) and independent prescribing and patient education (items 6 and 11–19).

Reliability

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability. The NP-SES-C demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.972. Given the odd number of items, the unequal-length split-half reliability coefficient was applied. The Spearman-Brown split-half reliability coefficient for the overall scale was 0.939. The Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability for the three subdimensions ranged from 0.907 to 0.957 and from 0.867 to 0.944, respectively. Discussion

The use of scientifically validated instruments to assess nurses’ prescribing self-efficacy is essential for the development of effective interventions. Currently, no assessment tool specifically measures self-efficacy in nurse prescribing within the Chinese context. The NP-SES, developed by Dr Teresa Galiana-Camacho, addresses this gap.21 Rigorous forward–backward translation and pretesting, as recommended by cross-cultural adaptation guidelines, are critical for ensuring the quality and reliability of adapted scales.40 In this study, the NP-SES was translated and culturally adapted into Chinese through a systematic process that included translation, back-translation, expert review and pretesting. Expert evaluations and repeated modifications based on feedback and pilot testing ensured the reliability and validity of the final instrument, which was subsequently validated among Chinese nursing students and nurses. The resulting NP-SES-C provides a standardised tool for assessing self-efficacy in nurse prescribing among Chinese nurses, thus offering a scientific basis for evaluating prescribing confidence.

Reliability and validity analyses indicated that the NP-SES-C has good psychometric properties. Reliability refers to the consistency, accuracy, stability and reproducibility of results obtained from a measurement instrument.33 In this study, Cronbach’s α coefficients and split-half reliability of the NP-SES-C exceeded 0.70,39 indicating a high level of internal consistency. Although the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the ‘Independent prescribing and patient education’ dimension (0.957) and the total scale (0.972) were notably high, these values do not suggest item redundancy. Instead, they reflect the inherent psychometric characteristics of the present self-efficacy scale. Specifically, high internal consistency is commonly observed in self-efficacy instruments, particularly domain-specific ones, due to their focused, practical and highly homogeneous item content. Furthermore, the original version of the scale also demonstrated a similarly high total alpha value (0.96), supporting the stability of this measurement property.19 Finally, the adequate model fit confirmed by CFA further verified the structural validity of the scale, indicating that the high alpha values stem from genuine scale consistency rather than redundant items. Validity reflects the extent to which a scale accurately measures the intended construct.41 Both the item-level and scale-level content validity indices (I-CVI and S-CVI) met or exceeded the threshold of 0.78,34 35 demonstrating that the scale items were representative, had strong content validity and effectively assessed nurses’ self-efficacy in prescribing. EFA identified three factors, accounting for 78.608% of the total variance, with all items loading >0.40 on their designated dimensions and no items deleted.42 CFA further supported the acceptable fit and sound construct validity of the NP-SES-C.38 It should be noted that some items demonstrated double loading in EFA. To achieve an acceptable model fit, post-hoc modifications were incorporated based on modification indices and theoretical justification. Specifically, covariances were added between the error terms of items 5 and 9, 7 and 10, and 8 and 12. These reflect plausible, content-based overlaps: shared focus on individualised safety (items 5 and 9), external consultation for decision-making (items 7 and 10) and the context of treatment initiation (items 8 and 12). Furthermore, covariances were added between the error term of item 10 and the clinical assessment and pharmacological knowledge (CAPK) factor, and between the error term of item 11 and the supplemental prescribing and evidence-based practice (SPEP) factor. This indicates that collaborative decision-making (item 10) draws on CAPK, and that the ability to differentiate prescribing modalities (item 11) is intrinsically linked to the core competency of SPEP. Items 5, 6 and 11, in particular, showed loading on multiple factors and diverged from the original scale’s dimensionality.19 After thorough consultation with experts, these items were assigned in accordance with the original scale’s structure. Specifically, item 6 (‘Treat any adverse or allergic reaction due to prescribed medicines, dressings or other products’) and item 11 (‘Differentiate situations in which I can prescribe independently from those in which I need to follow existing protocols’) were categorised under independent prescribing, reflecting the assessment of nurses’ competence in this domain. Item 5 (‘Identify the side effects or adverse reactions of prescribed drugs regardless of patients’ health situation’) was categorised under pharmacological knowledge. The classification of these items followed established principles for cross-cultural adaptation.30 These principles emphasise decentering, the use of multiple methods to assess equivalence, and the priority of functional and conceptual equivalence. Accordingly, the assignment of items 5, 6 and 11 was not determined by statistical results alone. This expert judgement is consistent with the original scale’s theoretical framework and was considered essential for preserving the structural validity and functional equivalence of the adapted scale. Therefore, these items were retained in their originally hypothesised dimensions. Future research should further examine the structural validity of the NP-SES-C.

Limitations

This study translated and validated the NP-SES in a Chinese nursing population, demonstrating favourable psychometric properties and providing a tool suitable for assessing nurse prescribing self-efficacy in China. However, certain limitations should be considered. First, all data were based on self-reported measures, which may introduce bias. Second, our study employed convenience sampling, with participants consisting solely of nurses and nursing graduates from various healthcare institutions within Jiangsu province. Given regional variations in nurse prescribing practices, these findings cannot be automatically generalised to nurses in broader geographical contexts. Third, during CFA, specific error covariances were required to achieve good model fit. These correlations may indicate nuanced, context-specific practices within the Chinese nursing context that are not entirely independent across the theoretical dimensions. This is a known phenomenon in cross-cultural validation, where respondents from different cultures may perceive subtle relational aspects between items differently. Future studies could explore these nuances qualitatively. Future research should include multicentre, large-sample studies to further validate the NP-SES-C and investigate additional factors affecting nurse prescribing self-efficacy. Such work will contribute to the development of targeted clinical training and interventions to improve self-efficacy and ultimately enhance the quality of patient care.

Conclusion

The NP-SES-C has been validated as a reliable and valid assessment tool for evaluating nurse prescribing self-efficacy among Chinese nurses. This instrument provides a scientific basis for the development of educational and training programmes aimed at strengthening nurses’ confidence and competency in nurse prescribing practice.
