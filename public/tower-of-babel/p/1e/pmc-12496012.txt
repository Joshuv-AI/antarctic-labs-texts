Frequency of lab HIV and self-reported STI history and its predictors among a sexually compulsive clinical sample: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Nisa Regina Bubola Lima, Isabelle Vera Vichr Nisida, Marco de Tubino Scanavino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsIndividuals with compulsive sexual behavior (CSB) are at increased risk for sexually transmitted infections (STI) and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV).AimTo investigate the frequency of STIs and HIV in a CSB clinical sample and the associated predictors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between 2012 and 2021 in a specialized outpatient clinic at a large tertiary hospital in São Paulo, Brazil. We disclosed the study through noticeboards in the institution and advertisements on the official website and social media. Participants underwent a psychiatric interview to assess CSB and exclusion criteria. Individuals who sought treatment for difficulty in controlling sexual impulses and got confirmation of a CSB diagnosis were included. Those who did not were included as controls. Healthy volunteers who sought to participate as controls and did not confirm CSB diagnosis were also included. Comprehensive laboratory tests were requested. We investigated sexual compulsivity, impulsivity, patterns of CSB, condomless anal and vaginal sex and sex under the influence of alcohol/drugs, self-reported STI diagnoses or history, and screening tests. Logistic regression analysis was used.OutcomesLaboratory results of HIV tests and self-reported STI diagnoses or history.ResultsA total of 275 participants (67.5%) met the criteria for CSB, and 132 (32.5%) were eligible as controls. The frequency of HIV in tested participants was 10.8% (n = 23) in the CSB group and 2.4% (n = 2) among controls. Regarding self-reported STI diagnoses or history, 43.9% of CSB outpatients and 17.8% of the control group reported it [χ2(1) = 25.58, P < .001]. The HIV-positive test was associated with self-identifying as gay or bisexual [OR 31.58, 95% CI 4.09-243.72, P < .05] and sex under the influence of drugs [OR 6.49, 95% CI 1.10-38.35] in the final multivariate analysis model, adjusted for other variables. Sexual compulsivity [OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.00-1.07, P < .05], excessive casual sex [OR 1.84, 95% CI 1.07-3.17, P < .05], and self-identifying as of African descent [OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.29-0.88, P < .05] behave as predictors of self-reported STI.Clinical ImplicationsWe suggest incorporating frequent and periodic screening tests for HIV/STI in the management protocols of CSB patients, considering the potential morbidity and social burden of STI.Strengths and LimitationsTo our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the frequency of HIV in a sexually compulsive clinical sample using laboratory tests, including women and participants of diverse sexual orientations. However, the prolonged data collection time was a limitation and many outpatient CSB participants commonly avoided taking exams and even left before this step. Because many tests for non-HIV were missing, self-reported history data of STI were preferred. Sensitivity analyses suggest that sexual orientation may have a modifying effect on the relationship between other predictors and HIV, which should be explored in further studies.Conclusionsthe current study shows distinct predictors for HIV and STI considering the CSB clinical sample, pointing out specific clinical care and prevention.Keywords: sexual compulsivity, sexually transmitted infections, HIV, sexual risk, behavior

Introduction

Compulsive sexual behavior disorder (CSBD) was recently included in the ICD-11 revision as an impulse control disorder that comprises a persistent pattern of failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses or urges resulting in repetitive sexual behavior. Over a prediod of at least 6 months, repetitive sexual activities become a central focus of the individual’s life, engaging in these behaviors despite adverse consequences. Efforts to control or reduce repetitive sexual activities are often unsuccessful.1,2 The latest estimated lifetime prevalence of CSBD is 8.17% for men and 2.42% for women, and 6.46% for gender-diverse individuals.3

The terminology in studies of out-of-control sexual behaviors varies broadly due to different approaches to understanding these terms (eg, excessive sexual drive, sexual addiction, hypersexuality). In this paper, as Grubbs et al.,4 we use a more general terminology, namely compulsive sexual behaviors (CSB), to designate a point in common in most of those studies: the out-of-control behavior patterns in individuals who seek treatment. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the diverse conceptualization according to distinct nomenclature in previous studies.

Negative outcomes like sexually transmitted infections (STI) and HIV infection may become an issue of particular concern in CSB individuals. Indeed, most initial studies about CSB gained emphasis from the 1980s onwards due to the increased prevalence of HIV infections. The concept of sexual compulsivity was extensively explored in relation to greater vulnerability to HIV, particularly for its notable association with the personality trait of impulsiveness or seeking new sensations.5

Some studies have reported alarming levels of HIV and STI in CSB samples6-9 exposing HIV rates as high as 22%10 and 78% to other STI.9 This may be related to sexual risk behaviors such as unprotected anal and vaginal intercourse,11,12 multiple casual partners,12,13 and sex under the effect of alcohol and drugs in CSB individuals.14,15

However, much of this data comes from studies with small sample sizes, almost exclusively MSM, or without confirmatory laboratory tests. Therefore, the following research questions were answered at the end of this study: “Do CSB outpatients have an elevated frequency of HIV infection and self-reported STI history compared to the non-compulsive group?”, “Do psychopathological variables, sexual risk behaviors, and problematic behaviors act as predictors of the presence of HIV/STI?”

This study aimed to examine the frequency of HIV-positive lab tests among a sexually compulsive clinical sample and self-reported STI history, comparing it to a non-compulsive group with a broader and more diverse sample considering sexual orientation and gender. A secondary goal was to investigate the role of predictors such as sex under the influence of alcohol and drugs, condom use, severity of sexual compulsivity, impulsiveness, and patterns of CSB in the presence of HIV/STI.

Methods

Participants

This paper used data from a cross-sectional study conducted in a large tertiary hospital in an outpatient unit known for caring for people with CSB. From 2012 to 2021, we disclosed the study through “notice boards in the institution” and advertisements on the official website and social media. They were recruited men and women over 18 years old who sought the outpatient unit spontaneously both as seeking treatment as a healthy volunteer.

To evaluate CSB, we used ICD-10 criteria, code F52.7 16 through questions such as “why do you believe your sexual behavior is excessive?”; “why did you come to the outpatient clinic?”; “describe your sexual behavior”; “describe situations where you have lost control over your sexual behavior”. Were eligible as CSB outpatients who reported frequent situations of losing control over their sexual behavior consistently for at least 6 months. Goodman’s Criteria17 were also applied; it encompasses seven questions adapted from the DSM-IV criteria for substance dependence, which assess symptoms such as tolerance, withdrawal, frequent sexual behavior that continues despite negative outcomes, unsuccessful efforts to control it, considerable time spent in preparation for it, social or occupational activities diminishing for at least 12 months.

Two sources formed the control group: participants who sought treatment and did not met the criteria for CSB, corresponding to 53.8%, and employees and other members of the hospital community who sought to participate in the study as healthy volunteers and did not met the criteria for CSB either, who completed 46.2%. Sociodemographic and psychopathological variables were compared across the two groups to guarantee there were no significant differences.

To reduce the effects of comorbid psychopathology, were excluded those who met the criteria for gender identity disorder, schizophrenia and correlated mania or hypomania episodes, and other mental disorders owing to brain dysfunction, injury, or physical disease.

Procedures

Participants completed a psychiatric interview to evaluate CSB criteria and exclusion criteria. On another occasion, an infectious diseases specialist asked about the self-reported history of STI (both clinical and laboratory diagnoses), self-reported history of STI screening tests and requested comprehensive laboratory tests for HIV and other STI.

The study procedures were approved in 2020 by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Clinical Hospital of [masked for review], number 3.799.024. All subjects were informed about the study, and all provided informed consent.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

We asked for age, gender, race, sexual orientation, marital status, years of education, income, and employment status.

Sexually transmitted infections

The following items were investigated: laboratory analysis of blood samples examined the serological status of HIV (research for antibodies against HIV 1/2 and the p24 antigen of HIV1). The answers to questions about the self-reported history of STI and self-reported history of STI screening tests were “yes” or “no”.

Compulsive sexual behavior

To assess the severity of sexual compulsivity, we used the Sexual Compulsivity Scale (SCS), a 10-item Likert-type scale validated in Brazil with good reliability (Cronbach’s alfas have ranged from .85 to .91) which investigates questions as “I have to struggle to control my sexual thoughts and behavior” and “I think about sex more than I would like to”. Answers can range from 1 (not at all like me) to 4 (very much like me), and the highest sums indicate more sexual compulsivity.18

Psychopathology measures

The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) is a short diagnostic structured interview based on DSM-III-R, DSM-IV, and ICD-10 diagnostic criteria that explore mental disorders like major depressive disorder, current or lifetime manic episodes, alcohol and drug dependence, panic disorder, agoraphobia, social phobia, simple phobia, generalized anxiety disorder, lifetime bulimia, current or lifetime psychotic syndrome.19 In Brazil, it was validated by Amorim (2000)20 with a satisfactory reliability index (Kappa > .60), and we used it to explore eligibility and exclusion criteria.

To assess the severity of impulsivity, we utilized the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11), an inventory with 30 items with Likert-type answers that asked questions such as “I do things without thinking” and “I act on impulse”. The total score varies from 30 to 120, with the highest total scores indicating more impulsiveness. The transcultural translation and validation of the Portuguese version, the BIS-11, had good internal consistency with an alpha of .62.21–23

Sexual risk behaviors

Predictors related to sexual risk behavior were estimated through the Sexual Behavior Risk Assessment, developed by Scanavino et al. (2018)12 based on previous researchers.24,25 This self-report questionnaire asks about sex practices with casual and stable partners in the last 6 months, such as the frequency of condom use in vaginal and anal intercourse. For this analysis, we aggregated the responses into a binary variable “none/up to 25% of the time” and “use in 26% to 100% of the time”. The Cronbach’s alpha was .83. Complementary questions about sex under the influence of drugs or alcohol were asked, with the possible answers “never/sometimes” and “frequently/always”.

Additionally, we asked the participants, “Which of the following sexual behaviors might have become a problem for you?” with options such as cruising for sex, multiple partners, excessive casual sex, problematic masturbation, and problematic pornography use. Answers could be “yes” or “no”, and more than one option was possible.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17 (StataCorp)26 with a significance level of P < .05. For descriptive analysis, we presented proportions of categorical variables, means, and standard deviations for continuous variables with normal distribution and median and interquartile ranges for non-normal continuous variables. We used Chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann–Whitney for continuous variables for group comparisons. We compared the two groups of non-sexually compulsive individuals according to their sexual compulsivity scores, impulsivity scores, alcohol and drug use, HIV and STI tests, STI self-report history, and condom use. To test our first hypothesis, we calculated the proportion of participants who had HIV/STI laboratory diagnoses and self-report data regarding those not affected and tested differences between CSB outpatients and controls. To investigate our second hypothesis, we first examined bivariate associations between sociodemographic characteristics, psychopathologic and behaviors predictors with outcomes “HIV infection” indicated in lab tests and reported “self-reported history of STI”. Logistic regression models were made, including predictors with P < .20 on bivariate analysis in a backward stepwise regression approach. If sexual orientation is found to be strongly associated with the primary outcome of “HIV lab infection”, because there is strong evidence in literature of connection with HIV risk behavior, we will conduct sensitivity analysis to investigate its influence on the outcome.

Sexually transmitted infections

Two hundred ninety-six participants underwent HIV lab tests and 111 did not. The frequency of HIV infection in tested participants was 10.8% (95% CI 7.3-15.8) in the CSB group and 2.4% (95% CI 0.5-9) among controls (Table 2). Two hundred and fifty-five CSB outpatients and 129 controls answered the question about the history of STI, while 23 participants did not report it. Considering the primary outcome “HIV lab infection”, bivariate regression analysis with sociodemographics and sexual behavior variables found that being single (OR 5.61, 95% CI 1.87-16.8; P < .001), self-declaring gay or bisexual (OR 51.3, 95% CI 6.83-385.8; P < .001), sex under the influence of drugs (OR 16.6; 95% CI 3.48-79.68; P < .001) and reported excessive casual sex (OR 3.46, 95% CI 1.32-9.04, P = .01), multiple partners (OR 5.61, 95% CI 2.14-14.72, P < .001) and compulsive cruising for sex (OR 5.21, 95% CI 2.05-13.25, P = .001) were significantly associated with outcome. Both psychopathological variables, the severity of compulsivity (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.02-1.15; P = .004) and impulsivity (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01-1.06; P = .003), were also closely associated. Those variables were also included in multivariate regression analysis: being retired (OR 2.45; 95% CI 0.63-9.41; P < .19), higher education level (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.17; P = .18), condomless in anal sex with a casual partner (OR 1.93, 95% CI 0.72-5.15, P = .18), and problematic pornography use (OR 2.23, 95% CI 0.80-6.21, P = .12).

To assess the potential influence of sexual orientation on the associations identified in our logistic regression model with the primary outcome “HIV lab infection”, we conducted sensitivity analyses. First, we removed sexual orientation from the model and found that additional variables became significantly associated with HIV status: marital status of being single or divorced (P = 0.006), engaging in sex under the influence of drugs (P = 0.016), and sexual compulsivity (P = 0.008) were independently associated with HIV-positive status.

Second, we stratified the sample, conducting separate analyses among homosexual and bisexual participants, which revealed marital status of being single or divorced (P = 0.054), and sexual compulsivity (P = 0.068) were marginally associated with HIV-positive status in this subgroup. We attempted to conduct a separate analysis among heterosexual participants; however, due to the extremely low number of HIV-positive individuals in this group, the model could not be estimated reliably.

Considering the primary outcome “self-reported history of STI”, bivariate regression analysis revealed that self-declaring gay or bisexual (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.41-3.37; P < .001), sex under the influence of drugs (OR 5.28; 95% CI 1.37-20.29; P = .01) problematic masturbation (OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.26-3.29, P = .004), excessive casual sex (OR 3.05, 95% CI 1.92-4.83, P < .001), multiple partners (OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.75-4.46, P < .001) and cruising for sex (OR 3.5, 95% CI 1.66-7.36, P = .001) was associated with higher odds of reporting it, while being Black/African-descent with reduction of risk (OR 0.67; 95% CI 0.42-1.05; P = .08). Also, the severity of compulsivity (OR 1.06; 95% CI 1.03-1.08; P < .001) and impulsivity (OR 1.02; 95% CI 1.01-1.04; P < .001) were associated with an increase in the outcome. The following variables were included in multivariate regression analysis: age (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.99-1.03; P = .13), sex under the influence of alcohol (OR 1.67; 95% CI 0.75-3.74; P = .20).

Sexual orientation and sex under the influence of drugs were independent predictors of HIV infection after controlling for age, race, legal marital status, employment status, and income. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the frequency of HIV in a sexually compulsive clinical sample using laboratory tests, including women and participants of diverse sexual orientations. Almost 11% of CSB outpatients had a positive blood test for HIV (Table 2), substantially higher than in the local general population, which ranges from 0.12% to 0.4%.27,28 However, this rate is slightly close to inquiries assessing MSM community samples, which report 7.3% in California, USA,8 9.5% in Indianapolis, USA,29 15.4% in Sao Paulo, Brazil30 and 16% in New York City, USA,7 as well as CSB treatment-seeking sample, that point to 10.7% in New York City, USA9 and 22% in São Paulo, Brazil.10

The control group exhibited a 2% HIV frequency, which is also more significant than the general population. Some were treatment-seeking and did not met the criteria for CSB; the other part was from specific recruitment between institution employees and the healthy community members. These characteristics could justify the HIV rates because participants who sought the study seemed more interested in sexual behavior.

Self-declaring gay or bisexual was an independent predictor of a positive lab test for HIV in our clinical sample (Table 4). About 96% of HIV-positive participants were MSM, contrasting with 36% of the total sample. It reinforces a well-established knowledge of MSM as a critical population in the HIV epidemic. There is a wide range of factors associated with the increasingly concentrated new cases in this population, such as the high per-act probability of transmission in receptive anal intercourse,31 but also the insufficient response of public health policies throughout the epidemic and, more recently, the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.32,33 Even in populations with high levels of sexual compulsivity, as in this study, sexual orientation outweighs other factors when it comes to HIV risk. This should raise concerns about how sexual (and gender) minorities may be exposed to higher levels of stressors such as stigma, social vulnerability, depression and substance abuse, which can lead to condomless anal sex.34 Moreover, the sensitivity analyses suggest that sexual orientation may act as an effect-modifying variable in the relationship between other predictors and HIV status, which should be explored in future studies.

Another independent predictor of a positive lab test for HIV was engaging in sexual intercourse under the influence of drugs (Table 4). Although we did not investigate which drugs participants used during sexual events, studies have shown elevated levels of HIV in MSM who practice sexualized drug use, including polydrug users other than GBL/ketamine/methamphetamine.35,36 The use of sexualized drugs is referred to as intensifying sexual feelings and intimacy,37 but it is also associated with high-risk sexual behaviors38 and injury risk.39 Those aspects are likely related to the high risk of HIV transmission among those who engage in sex under the influence of drugs.

Despite the elevated levels of sexual compulsivity, the SCS score was not associated with HIV infection in our CSB clinical sample (Table 4). Over the decades, the literature has mentioned the relationship between sexual compulsivity and HIV as a logical presuppose,11,40 mainly due to its association with unprotected anal sex.41–44 However, few studies have performed analyses in which HIV was an outcome per se and when they did, sexual compulsivity was not related to infection,8,14,29 like our study.

It is possible that HIV infection occurs, in most cases, independently of sexual compulsivity but linked to a more sexually active life with a predominance of casual and multiple partners45,46 and increasing trends in condomless anal sex47,48 in MSM groups. Some authors proposed that sexual compulsivity can behave as a component of a syndemic framework, participating in epidemic fragilities that interact and better explain HIV risk in vulnerable populations.14,49,50

The self-reported history of STI was significantly higher in the CSB group when compared to controls (Table 2). Our data is similar to surveys that assessed CSB clinical samples, with 28.4%51 and 78.6%9 of self-reported STI in diverse states of the USA. The severity of sexual compulsivity behaved as an independent predictor of STIs self-reported history, independent of sexual orientation, race, or gender in a treatment-seeking sample of CSB (Table 4). This finding is recurrent even in different samples, such as straight men from the community,6 MSM from the community,29 and men and women receiving STI clinic services.52

Curiously, self-declaring Black or Africandescent in our sample reduced the odds of reporting a history of STI by 50% (Table 4). This is not a common finding, as racial disparities are a recurrent risk factor in surveys, usually associated with social inequalities and healthcare system coverage.53,54 Our sample differs from the general population in terms of participants presenting better educational levels, income, and reports being more employed,55 which is usually linked to better access to information and health care. Additional studies will be able to investigate patterns of behavior or sociodemographic issues in this sample related to race.

Excessive casual sex reported as problematic was positively associated with a self-reported STI history, resulting in almost double the chance of presenting the outcome. This makes sense as some bacterial STI are more transmissible than HIV and can occur through direct contact without intercourse or fluid exchange. Excessive casual sex has been associated with STI in diverse samples but only if linked to risk behaviors,56 which may suggest that sexual compulsivity can work as an aggravating factor.

Regarding limitations, many outpatient CSB participants commonly avoid taking exams and even leave before this step. According to Table 2, 23% of CSB outpatients and 36% of controls did not take blood tests for HIV. Regarding non-HIV STI data, up to 48% and 95% of them, respectively, had some missing exams (Table 2). This limited the number of participants used in the analyses and some investigations with condom use, for example. Because many tests for non-HIV were missing, self-reported history data of STI were preferred. The data available in the literature suggest that the avoidance of blood/urine tests may be related to stigmatized attitudes toward people with HIV,57 fear of positive results58 and anticipated HIV stigma.59 When evaluating CSB populations, facilitating access to tests can help. It is essential for HIV/STI screening testing and rapid treatment as much as possible. We suggest incorporating frequent and periodic screening tests for HIV/STI in the management protocols of CSB patients, considering the potential morbidity and social burden of STI. At an institutional level, implementing point-of-care testing for STI, multiplex diagnoses, and reduced time to result in the notification can reduce barriers.60–62

Another limitation was the prolonged data collection time. The frequency of lab HIV and self-reported STI history may have been influenced by behavioral and psychopathological trends that were not considered, including the years of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Finally, it is important to point out that HIV transmission is a multifactorial process, determined by factors including partner serostatus, network-level exposure, mental burden, and violence exposure, particularly in relation to the disproportionate transmission dynamics among MSM, which were not addressed in the scope of this study. However, we examined sexual behaviors such as having multiple partners, condom use during anal intercourse with both stable and casual partners, and sexual activity under the influence of alcohol or drugs, which are also factors related to HIV transmission. Future studies addressing the connections among the HIV transmission factors in the context of the PreP era will be important, considering individuals with CSB.

Conclusion

In this clinical sample of individuals with CSB, the frequency of self-reported STI and laboratory-confirmed HIV infection was elevated. Regarding self-reported STI, the severity of sexual compulsivity and excessive casual sex were independent predictors, whereas self-identification as of African descent emerged as a protective factor.

For HIV infection, self-declaring as gay or bisexual and having sex under the influence of drugs are associated with increased odds but not the severity of the sexual compulsivity. Additional studies may further explore the distinct patterns of predictors between self-reported STI and HIV.

Finally, CSB individuals should be considered an priority group regarding HIV/STI risk, being essential to approach sexual behavior and encouraging prevention and self-care.
