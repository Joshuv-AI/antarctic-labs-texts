Caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding postpartum depression: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Cimei Li, Hui Yang, Jingjing Bi, Shexia Yuan, Shuangling Ma, Aili Xiong, Lina Chao, Cixia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postpartum depression (PPD) is a prevalent psychiatric condition with significant implications for maternal and infant health. However, the role of caregivers in its management remains underexplored, particularly regarding their specific knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP). To address this gap, this study aimed to quantify KAP levels regarding PPD among caregivers. This cross-sectional study collected 503 surveys from primary caregivers of postpartum women in Henan, China, using a validated questionnaire between November 2024 and February 2025. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with KAP outcomes, while structural equation modeling (SEM) was applied to explore the interrelationships among KAP dimensions. The results showed that mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 10.63 ± 3.36 (possible range: 0–26), 38.31 ± 4.80 (possible range: 9–45), and 30.28 ± 6.49 (possible range: 8–40), respectively. Multivariable logistic regression showed that lack of access to professional psychological counseling was associated with poorer knowledge, while higher knowledge and more positive attitudes were significantly associated with proactive caregiving practices (all P < 0.001). SEM demonstrated that knowledge had significant direct effects on attitude (β = 0.300, P < 0.001) and practice (β = 0.217, P < 0.001), while attitude had a strong direct effect on practice (β = 0.703, P < 0.001). In conclusion, caregivers demonstrated limited knowledge but relatively positive attitudes and practices regarding PPD. Targeted educational interventions and improved access to psychological support may help enhance caregiver knowledge and support for postpartum mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54922-1.

Introduction

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common psychiatric condition affecting women during the puerperium period, primarily characterized by significant depressive symptoms and emotional disturbances1. The global prevalence of PPD is estimated to range from approximately 10–15%2, with epidemiological data revealing more specific patterns—averaging around 13% in high-income countries while reaching up to 19% in low- and middle-income countries1. In China specifically, the prevalence rate stands at approximately 16.3% and has exhibited a concerning upward trend in recent years3. The temporal manifestation of PPD typically follows a predictable pattern, with symptoms usually emerging between four and eight weeks postpartum and reaching peak intensity within the first few weeks, although delayed onset can also occur4. Notably, research has documented that approximately 7.2% of mothers develop depressive symptoms later, at nine to ten months postpartum5. The clinical presentation encompasses a spectrum of symptoms, including fatigue, anxiety, irritability, anhedonia, sleep and appetite disturbances, diminished self-esteem, feelings of parental inadequacy, persistent sadness, guilt, cognitive impairment, and, in severe cases, suicidal ideation6. The pathogenesis of PPD involves a complex interplay among neuroendocrine alterations, psychosocial factors, and environmental stressors7.

Management of postpartum depression necessitates an integrated therapeutic approach that combines pharmacological interventions, psychological treatments, and robust social support systems8. Treatment strategies are typically stratified by symptom severity. For mild cases, non-pharmacological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal psychotherapy are generally preferred, as these modalities effectively address distorted thinking patterns and enhance adaptive coping mechanisms9. In contrast, moderate to severe postpartum depression often necessitates pharmacological treatment with SSRIs combined with psychotherapy, while cases complicated by suicidal ideation or psychotic features require urgent psychiatric referral and, when indicated, intensive interventions such as electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), or novel FDA-approved agents including brexanolone and zuranolone10–13.

Beyond pharmacological approaches, research has increasingly recognized the critical role of caregivers in influencing treatment outcomes and recovery trajectories for women with PPD. Family or primary caregivers are generally understood as unpaid individuals who provide ongoing, close support to someone with health or functional needs, often encompassing physical, emotional, and daily living assistance14,15. Studies have demonstrated that adequate caregiver knowledge and supportive behaviors can significantly enhance treatment adherence, reduce symptom severity, and accelerate recovery from postpartum depression16. A systematic review by Dennis and Dowswell found that psychosocial and psychological interventions delivered by caregivers reduced the likelihood of depression persistence by 34% compared to standard care17. Furthermore, Letourneau et al. highlighted that caregiver-led supportive interventions yielded comparable efficacy to professional-led interventions for mild to moderate PPD cases, underscoring caregivers’ pivotal function in the therapeutic alliance18. A concerning trend emerges from existing evidence, which indicates that caregivers frequently lack adequate skills to identify depressive symptoms and provide appropriate supportive interventions, thereby underscoring the necessity for targeted educational programs19,20. Partner-based support interventions have demonstrated considerable efficacy in Western contexts, with systematic reviews showing that partner involvement can reduce PPD symptom severity by 25–30% and improve treatment adherence21.

Knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) surveys are grounded in a theoretical framework that conceptualizes health-related behavior as a sequential process in which knowledge shapes attitudes, and attitudes, in turn, influence practices. As a structured methodological approach, KAP instruments are widely used to systematically assess individuals’ understanding, perceptions, and behavioral responses to specific health conditions and to identify gaps that can inform targeted educational and intervention strategies22–24. Given the pivotal role of caregivers in the identification, support, and management of postpartum depression, systematically evaluating their knowledge, attitudes, and practices is of substantial clinical and public health relevance. However, to the best of our knowledge, existing KAP studies related to postpartum depression have predominantly focused on pregnant and postpartum women25,26, with a notable absence of investigations targeting caregivers. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the levels of KAP regarding PPD among primary caregivers in Henan, China, to identify sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with KAP, and to examine the interrelationships among KAP dimensions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Henan Province from November 1, 2024, to February 10, 2025. The target population comprised primary caregivers or individuals who served as the daily companions of postpartum women, including spouses, parents, maternity nannies, or other family members. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinxiang Central Hospital (Approval No. 2024-609), and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Eligible participants were required to meet the following criteria: (1) principal caregiver or daily companion of a postpartum woman, maintaining close and regular contact; (2) at least 18 years old and possessed the capacity for independent judgment and effective communication; (3) provided continuous care for a minimum of one month and were well-acquainted with the woman’s daily life and emotional changes; (4) gave informed consent to participate in the study; (5) possessed sufficient language skills to complete the questionnaire in Mandarin or another commonly used language; (6) had no diagnosis of major psychiatric or cognitive disorders.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following condition: directly involved in healthcare, psychological counseling, or research activities related to this project that could compromise the objectivity of their responses.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire used in this study was developed de novo for the purposes of the current research, and has not been used in any previous studies. Following the initial development of the questionnaire, revisions were made based on 5 expert feedback to enhance clarity and content validity. A pilot study involving 31 participants was subsequently conducted, of which 29 completed the questionnaire validly. Reliability analysis demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.896. Specifically, the knowledge subscale yielded a coefficient of 0.749, the attitude subscale 0.878, and the practice subscale 0.884, indicating good reliability across all dimensions.

The finalized questionnaire, administered in Chinese, comprised 42 items organized into four domains: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, attitude, and practice. The demographic section included 18 items. The knowledge domain contained 7 items, where item 6 served as a trap question to exclude respondents without relevant experience, and item 7 was a multiple-choice item. In the knowledge section, items K1 to K5 were scored on a three-point scale, with “very familiar” responses receiving 2 points, “heard of it” 1 point, and “unclear” 0 points. Item K6 was not scored. For item K7, which comprised eight sub-items, each correct response was awarded 2 points, whereas each incorrect response received 0 points. The total possible score for the knowledge dimension ranged from 0 to 26. The attitude section included 9 items, while the practice section consisted of 8 items. Scoring for each domain was adapted to the format and intent of individual items. The attitude section included both positively and negatively worded statements. Positive attitude items (A1, A3–A9) were scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 5 (“strongly agree”) to 1 (“strongly disagree”), while the reverse scoring was applied to the negatively worded item A2. This resulted in a total possible score range of 9–45. The practice dimension comprised exclusively positively worded items, also rated on a five-point scale from 5 (“always”) to 1 (“never”), yielding a score range of 8–40. A score exceeding 70% of the maximum possible value within each domain was interpreted as indicative of adequate knowledge, a favorable attitude, and proactive practice behaviors27.

Distribution of questionnaires

Participants were recruited using a consecutive convenience sampling method, whereby all individuals meeting the inclusion criteria and readily accessible during the study period were invited to participate. A dedicated research team, composed of healthcare staff from the obstetrics wards and follow-up clinics, provided potential participants’ caregivers with a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures, emphasizing the anonymity and voluntary nature of participation. It was clearly stated that declining to participate would not affect the provision of medical care, and participants could withdraw at any time. The research team assisted in completing the questionnaire without influencing the participants’ responses. Data collection was carried out using an electronic questionnaire developed via Wenjuanxing (www.wjx.cn; Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., China). Participants accessed the survey through a QR code or web link, which was displayed in the obstetrics and gynecology department or distributed via WeChat (Tencent Holdings Ltd., China). To ensure data quality and prevent duplicate responses, each IP address was restricted to a single submission, and all items in the questionnaire were mandatory. The research team subsequently reviewed all submitted questionnaires to confirm completeness, logical coherence, and internal consistency.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this cross-sectional study was calculated using the standard formula for estimating the minimum required sample size for prevalence studies:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = \left( {\frac{{Z_{1} - \alpha /2}}{\delta }} \right)^{2} \times p \times \left( {1 - p} \right)$$\end{document}

Assuming a significance level (α) of 0.05, a corresponding Z value of 1.96, a margin of error (δ) of 0.05, and an expected proportion (p) of 0.5 to account for maximum variability, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 384 participants. Considering a projected valid response rate of approximately 80%, the final target sample size was adjusted to a minimum of 480 to ensure sufficient statistical power after accounting for potential non-response or incomplete data.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted using AMOS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In this study, the dependent variables were defined as the caregivers’ levels of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding postpartum depression. The independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education, income) and relevant clinical variables (e.g., delivery mode, history of mental health issues, access to counseling). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and scores across the knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) dimensions. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). To assess differences in KAP dimension scores across demographic subgroups, continuous variables were first tested for normality. Bivariable analyses were performed to examine the associations between participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and KAP scores. For variables with a normal distribution, independent-samples t-tests were used to compare two groups, while analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among three or more groups with homogeneity of variances. In cases where data were not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test were used for two-group and multi-group comparisons, respectively. Correlations between the KAP dimension scores were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed data. For regression analyses, knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were categorized into adequate knowledge, favorable attitude, and proactive practice behaviors using a cut-off value set at 70% of the maximum possible score within each respective domain. Univariate logistic regression analyses were first conducted to screen potential predictors. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in univariate analyses were subsequently entered into multivariate logistic regression models using the enter method to identify independent factors associated with each KAP outcome. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The hypothesized SEM model assumed that caregivers’ knowledge directly influences both attitudes and practices, and that attitudes also have a direct effect on practices. Specifically, SEM was used to test the following hypotheses: (H1) knowledge directly affects attitude, (H2) knowledge directly affects practice, and (H3) knowledge indirectly affects practice through attitude. Model fitting for SEM was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including the chi-square/degrees of freedom ratio (CMIN/DF), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), incremental fit index (IFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and comparative fit index (CFI), with acceptable fit defined as CMIN/DF ≤ 5, RMSEA < 0.08, and IFI, TLI, and CFI > 0.80. All p-values were two-sided and reported to three decimal places, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic information on participants

Initially, a total of 524 questionnaires were collected, with 10 questionnaires excluded due to logical errors in answering trap questions and 11 questionnaires excluded for age-related anomalies. A total of 503 valid questionnaires remained, with an effective rate of 95.99%. The mean age of the postpartum women was 30.39 ± 4.51 years, and the mean age of the infants was 3.00 ± 6.76 months. Among them, 276 (54.87%) were male, 282 (56.06%) were lived in urban area, 280 (55.67%) had bachelor’s degree/master’s degree or above, 217 (43.14%) had monthly income per capita of 2000–5000 Yuan, 297 (59.05%) were parents of the postpartum woman. Among postpartum woman, 319 (63.42%) had 1 child, 379 (75.35%) had a painless delivery, 292 (58.05%) were first-time deliveries (Table 1).

Status of caregivers’ KAP levels regarding PPD

The mean knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were 10.63 ± 3.36 (possible range: 0–26), 38.31 ± 4.80 (possible range: 9–45), and 30.28 ± 6.49 (possible range: 8–40), respectively. Knowledge scores differed significantly by education level, employment status, monthly income, relationship with the postpartum woman, number of children, mode of delivery, painless delivery, first delivery, and professional psychological counseling (all P < 0.05). Attitude scores varied significantly by education, employment status, income, number of children, painless delivery, and first delivery (all P < 0.05). Practice scores varied significantly by gender, education, income, relationship with the postpartum woman, painless delivery, first delivery, prenatal mental health history, and professional psychological counseling (all P < 0.05) (Table 1).

The three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the ‘Unclear’ option were ‘Do you understand the difference between postpartum depression and the “baby blues” (mild emotional fluctuations after childbirth)?’ (K3) with 37.97%, ‘Do you know how to recognize the early signs of postpartum depression in women?’ (K4) with 29.03%, and ‘Do you know that untreated postpartum depression can have long-term negative effects on both the mother and the baby?’ (K5) with 23.06% (Table 2).

Discussion

Caregivers demonstrated limited knowledge but generally positive attitudes and proactive practices regarding postpartum depression, with knowledge exerting both direct and indirect effects on attitudes and behaviors. Targeted educational interventions and access to professional psychological support are recommended to enhance caregivers’ understanding of PPD, which may, in turn, strengthen their supportive practices and improve maternal mental health outcomes.

Although many caregivers expressed supportive attitudes and engaged in helpful practices, their foundational understanding of postpartum depression remained limited. This discrepancy between what caregivers believe and do, versus what they actually know, is not only statistically evident but reflects a broader trend observed in studies on mental health literacy among the general population. Previous research has indicated that while caregivers are often emotionally invested, their conceptual grasp of psychological disorders, particularly postpartum-related conditions, is often shaped by fragmented information and societal narratives rather than evidence-based knowledge28. Moreover, knowledge was strongly associated with both attitudes and practices, suggesting that while people may act out of empathy or social expectation, their efforts may not always align with best practices in mental health support. These findings echo those from studies in comparable healthcare systems, which noted that even well-intentioned caregivers may unknowingly contribute to delayed care due to misconceptions about emotional recovery following childbirth29.

The item-level distribution analysis provided further context to these patterns. Knowledge items revealed that most caregivers had heard of postpartum depression but could not identify its causes or differentiate it from milder conditions. This pattern has been observed in multiple regions, especially where public health communication focuses more on maternal physical recovery than mental wellbeing30. Caregivers also demonstrated low levels of recognition regarding early symptoms of postpartum depression and its potential long‑term adverse effects on both maternal wellbeing and child development, indicating a pervasive gap in understanding the full clinical and psychosocial impact of the condition. This limited awareness may hinder timely help‑seeking and contribute to delayed intervention, which is critical given evidence that untreated postpartum depressive symptoms can be associated with impairments in mother–infant bonding and adverse socioemotional outcomes in early childhood31.

Similarly, while attitude items showed strong agreement with statements about the importance of early intervention and professional help, questions regarding social stigma and family openness received more mixed responses, suggesting persistent discomfort with discussing mental health in familial settings. Notably, nearly one quarter of participants endorsed the belief that emotional fluctuations during the postpartum period are normal and do not warrant particular attention, reflecting a common misperception that may contribute to under‑recognition and delayed help‑seeking for clinically significant mood disorders. Such views, while less common than in past decades, remain prevalent in contexts where emotional resilience is socially expected of mothers, and where psychological distress is still viewed through a moral or familial lens rather than a clinical one32. Such attitudes align with population survey findings indicating that perinatal mood disturbances are often perceived as part of normal postpartum adjustment rather than as potential clinical conditions requiring evaluation, which may impede timely identification and intervention, thereby increasing the risk of adverse maternal and child outcomes if depressive symptoms persist or worsen33,34.

Practice items revealed that while caregivers were involved in daily support, many lacked confidence or initiative in seeking medical advice or accessing structured postpartum support networks, a problem also reported in studies on caregiving burden and informational isolation35. More than one‑third of respondents reported never reading books or articles on postpartum depression, attending lectures or seminars, or obtaining information on its prevention and treatment through online or other media, indicating a substantial gap in active information‑seeking behaviors. This pattern highlights the need for targeted educational outreach to improve caregiver awareness and proactive support‑seeking. This mirrors previous findings in studies of caregiver behavior, where individuals may rely on past experience or familial norms rather than actively seeking updated health knowledge36.

The relationship among the three KAP dimensions was not only supported by simple correlation but further confirmed by path analysis, which showed that knowledge influences attitudes and practices directly, and also indirectly through shaping perceptions. This aligns with existing theoretical models that view knowledge as a necessary precondition for meaningful behavioral change37. It also suggests that without sufficient understanding, caregivers’ positive attitudes may lack the clarity needed for sustained or targeted support.

The influence of contextual variables offers further insight into these patterns. For instance, caregivers who had received professional psychological counseling displayed a higher level of knowledge, underscoring the role of healthcare engagement in shaping mental health literacy. Similar observations have been made in studies from settings where structured mental health interventions are more widely available, suggesting that the accessibility and normalization of psychological services can influence not just individuals, but their family networks38. Gender differences in practice also emerged, with female caregivers reporting lower scores despite often taking on more caregiving responsibilities. This paradox may be explained by higher expectations placed on female caregivers, leading to more critical self-assessment or greater emotional burden. Some studies have suggested that women in caregiving roles are more likely to internalize stress and feel inadequate when unable to meet perceived caregiving standards, which could explain the observed discrepancies39. Moreover, the age of the infant appeared to influence practice scores, with a gradual decline in caregiving engagement over time. This likely reflects a shift in perceived urgency, as early postpartum concerns give way to the routine of childcare.

These findings point to several concrete implications. First, health systems need to embed caregiver-focused mental health education within routine maternal and child health services. Such education should not be limited to clinical settings, but extend to community centers, family education programs, and digital platforms. Visual tools, story-based education, and culturally grounded examples could help bridge abstract medical concepts with caregivers lived experiences. Second, more opportunities should be provided for caregivers to interact with mental health professionals—not only during crises but as part of regular care. Group counseling or family-oriented workshops could help reduce stigma and clarify misconceptions. Programs that integrate caregiver training into prenatal classes or postpartum home visits have been effective in other regions and may be similarly applicable here40.

From a policy perspective, improving caregiver support for postpartum mental health requires coordination across multiple sectors. Mental health must be better integrated into maternal health policies, and public health campaigns should explicitly include caregivers as part of their target audience. Furthermore, frontline healthcare workers—particularly nurses, midwives, and community health workers—need to be trained not only to identify signs of PPD in mothers but to assess and educate the caregiving environment. Resources must be distributed equitably, ensuring that low-income and rural caregivers have access to the same informational tools and services as their urban counterparts. Long-term success will depend on consistent policy investment, cross-sector collaboration, and the establishment of measurable standards for caregiver engagement in maternal mental health care41.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships among caregivers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices, limiting the ability to infer temporal or directional effects. Second, data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to response biases, including social desirability bias, whereby participants might overreport positive behaviors or attitudes to conform to perceived expectations. Third, although we targeted primary caregivers, the study population included maternity nannies (yuesao), who are paid professional caregivers, in addition to family members, meaning the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of unpaid familial caregivers. Fourth, as the survey was conducted in a single province, the generalizability of the results to caregivers in other regions with different cultural norms or healthcare systems may be limited. Future research should consider longitudinal designs to explore causal pathways, include a broader and more representative sample of unpaid caregivers, and examine the impact of cultural and systemic factors on caregiver KAP regarding postpartum depression.

Conclusion

In conclusion, caregivers demonstrated insufficient knowledge yet generally positive attitudes and proactive practices toward postpartum depression, with significant interrelations observed among the three KAP dimensions. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions and accessible psychological counseling services to enhance caregivers’ understanding and support capabilities in the context of PPD.
