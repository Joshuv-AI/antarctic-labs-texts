Warming and vegetation greening drive recent surge in flash droughts
Authors: Jun Li, Yao Zhang, Emanuele Bevacqua, Shijie Jiang, Xing Yuan, Sha Zhou, Jinghao Qiu, ZhaoLi Wang, Jakob Zscheischler, Kaicun Wang, Shilong Piao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flash droughts have become more frequent, yet their underlying mechanisms of such changes remain unclear. Using an explainable artificial intelligence–based clustering framework with reanalysis products and Earth system models, we show that the major driving mechanisms of global flash droughts have shifted from precipitation dominance to a compound effect of temperature, transpiration, and precipitation. This transition drives a surge in flash droughts over the past decade, with more rapidly developing, severe, and enduring soil droughts. Anthropogenic warming and vegetation greening are the main drivers of the observed surge. The transition, which has emerged beyond natural climate variability since 2017 and is mainly detected in Eurasia, Amazon, and Africa, exposes ~650 million people under threat and reduces gross primary productivity by 0.15 ± 0.1 petagrams of carbon per year. Our results demonstrate that biosphere and atmosphere responses to anthropogenic forcing have altered flash drought drivers, emphasizing the urgent need for targeted mitigation strategies.

INTRODUCTION

The frequency, severity, and duration of droughts have increased substantially in many regions under anthropogenic forcing, with far-reaching consequences for both human societies and terrestrial ecosystems (1–3). Of particular concern is the emergence of “flash droughts,” which are events characterized by the rapid onset of extreme soil moisture deficits, typically within a few weeks (4–6). The rapid development of these events often challenges operational forecasting systems, limiting the effectiveness of early warnings (7–9), and frequently results in widespread crop failures, water shortages, and terrestrial carbon loss (10–12). While extensive progress has been made in understanding drought trends (13, 14), recovery patterns (15, 16), propagation (17, 18), and related impacts (2), the underlying mechanisms driving the development of flash drought events and their changes under anthropogenic forcing are not well understood.

The depletion of soil moisture is often attributed to a prolonged period of rainfall deficits, mainly driven by natural climate variability (19). However, recent studies suggest that anthropogenic forcing may exacerbate this process by extending the intervals between rainfall events (19, 20). Beyond precipitation deficits, global warming can increase atmospheric energy and amplify soil evaporation, accelerating soil moisture loss to the atmosphere (21). Additionally, plants substantially influence soil moisture through root uptake and transpiration (22). For instance, the increase in global vegetation leaf area has overall enhanced soil moisture consumption (23, 24), despite reductions in transpiration due to CO2-induced stomatal closure (25). Overall, precipitation, temperature, and transpiration are among the most direct factors influencing soil moisture dynamics, with the latter two undergoing substantial changes in response to anthropogenic forcing (21–23, 26, 27). Given the substantial anthropogenic impacts in the past decades (28, 29), we hypothesize that vegetation and temperature responses to anthropogenic forcing may have shifted the major driving mechanisms of global flash drought events, potentially contributing to their recent surge worldwide. Considering the immense repercussions of flash drought events (8), it is imperative to test this hypothesis and understand the underlying mechanisms to better inform adaptation strategies and project future flash droughts.

To test our hypothesis, we use an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)–based clustering framework (30, 31) to investigate the major driving mechanisms of global flash droughts and their temporal evolution from 1951 to 2022 (fig. S1). By using three global reanalysis products and outputs from 13 Earth system models (ESMs) in Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), we first construct a long short-term memory model to learn the relationship between potential drivers (e.g., temperature, transpiration, and precipitation) and soil moisture changes over the entire time series. We then apply the expected gradient (EG) method to estimate the individual contributions of drivers to soil moisture changes during development stages of flash drought events. The development stage is defined as the period from onset to the first point of lowest soil moisture. By using a cluster analysis to identify distinct flash drought types, we evaluate their occurrence over time. Subsequently, we quantify the contribution of anthropogenic forcing to flash drought changes. Last, we assess potential risks to both humans and terrestrial ecosystems associated with the surge in flash droughts (see Materials and Methods).

RESULTS

Shift in flash drought driving mechanisms

We identify two major types of flash droughts with distinct climatic and biophysical drivers (Fig. 1A). The first type, termed compound-dominated cluster (Ccomp) is characterized by the synergistic effect of elevated temperature, enhanced transpiration, and reduced precipitation. This type accounts for 32% of the total flash drought events. The second type, termed precipitation-dominated cluster (Cprec), is mainly driven by precipitation deficits and accounts for the rest 68% of the events. The characteristics of these two types of flash droughts also differ (Fig. 1, B to D), with compound-driven flash droughts being characterized by more rapid development, longer duration, and greater severity compared to precipitation-dominated events.

We then analyze the temporal changes in the frequency of global flash droughts associated with these two clusters from 1951 to 2022 (Fig. 1E). We observe a substantial shift in the major driving mechanisms of global flash droughts. Before approximately 2008, the frequency of global flash droughts shows little variation for both types (fig. S2), in line with overall drought trends (14); precipitation-dominated events occur four times more frequently than compound-driven events, accounting for about 73% of the total flash drought events (Fig. 1E). This is consistent with the expectation that precipitation deficits, being more influenced by internal climate variability, play an important role in drought development (4, 32, 33). However, after around 2008, global compound-driven events increase substantially and become more frequent than precipitation-dominated events after 2017, contributing to an overall surge (P < 0.05) in global flash droughts (Fig. 1F). ESMs and reanalysis products generally agree with these patterns (Fig. 1, A to F).

Our results remain robust across extensive sensitivity analyses (figs. S3 to S5). For instance, we find that the influence of additional factors such as wind speed, air pressure, and humidity is very limited (figs. S3 to S5), likely due to their indirect effects on soil moisture via precipitation or evapotranspiration (17, 18). Collectively, we demonstrate a robust shift in the major driving mechanisms of global flash droughts from precipitation deficits to compounding factors, resulting in a surge in flash droughts.

Anthropogenic warming and vegetation greening as major drivers

We further test the hypothesis of whether anthropogenic forcing has dominated the shift in driving mechanism of flash drought by analyzing single-forcing simulations and assessing the potential contributions from anthropogenic warming and vegetation greening. Specifically, we include three single-forcing experiments: simulations forced by anthropogenic greenhouse gases only (GHG), natural forcings only (NAT), and anthropogenic aerosol forcing only (AER). Additionally, we analyze simulations forced by combined anthropogenic and natural forcings (ALL).

We reveal a significant increase (P < 0.05) in compound-driven events under GHG forcing, with minor changes detected under the other two forcings (Fig. 2A and fig. S6). Precipitation-dominated events exhibit insignificant changes under all three forcings. Compound-driven events under GHG simulations contribute to the overall positive trend in global flash droughts, with a magnitude comparable to those under ALL simulations and reanalysis products. These findings indicate that the shift in the driving mechanisms of flash droughts can be explained if the effects of anthropogenic forcing are included but cannot be explained by natural climate variability. The overall trends (i.e., all, third column in Fig. 2A) under ALL simulations slightly exceed the combined trends from the three single-forcing experiments, suggesting the potential influence of additional anthropogenic factors under ALL simulations, such as land use and cover changes, which can modify land-surface processes amplifying climate extremes (34, 35). Additionally, the contribution of precipitation is minor in Ccomp under GHG simulation (fig. S6), which likely reflects a diminishing role of precipitation in compound-dominated flash droughts as precipitation variability increases due to anthropogenic forcing (19).

To further investigate how anthropogenic forcing affects compound-driven flash droughts, we conducted two analyses: (i) assessing the contribution of anthropogenic forcing to soil moisture decline in compound-driven events (relative to the total contribution of Ccomp shown in Fig. 1A) and (ii) evaluating the impact of anthropogenic forcing on the increase in compound-driven flash drought events (Fig. 1F). To achieve the first goal, we compared the contributions of each variable (e.g., precipitation and temperature) under ALL simulations with those derived after removing model-estimated anthropogenic trends (28) (see Materials and Methods). Notably, to quantify the effect of vegetation greening, we removed the signal of leaf area index (LAI; a proxy for vegetation greening) in transpiration, given its important role in transpiration (fig. S7, A to D) (36–38). Our analyses reveal a strong positive effect of anthropogenic trends on Ccomp since approximately 2008 (indicated by the bars without hatched lines in Fig. 2B), corresponding to the surge in flash droughts during this period. Temperature changes contribute ~32% (the first bar without hatched lines in Fig. 2B). Additionally, we identify a large contribution from vegetation greening (about 18%), while the role of precipitation remains uncertain. The contributions of anthropogenic changes in temperature and precipitation are comparable to those found in the 2022 European drought attribution study (29). Collectively, these factors contribute to about 60% of the total contributions under ALL simulation (i.e., the fourth bar without hatched lines in Fig. 2B), emphasizing the dominant role of anthropogenic trends in driving the compound effect during the recent decade. In contrast, the effect of anthropogenic trends on Ccomp before about 2008 is relatively weak (hatched lines in Fig. 2B), coinciding with little changes in droughts before approximately 2008 (14).

To estimate changes in the frequency of global compound-driven flash drought events due to anthropogenic forcing, we applied a regression method capable of separating anthropogenic trend signal and natural noise (39) to decompose the trend into additive components (see Materials and Methods). We find that the increase in compound-driven events is predominantly driven by changes in temperature and LAI, contributing to about 88% of the increase (the fourth bar in Fig. 2C). The contribution of these two factors was generally consistent across growing seasons (fig. S8), suggesting that anthropogenic effects on flash drought can be detected at any pentad (39). Moreover, our analysis of the LAI trend shows a good alignment between ESMs and observations only when anthropogenic forcings are included (fig. S7, E and F). Our results remain robust across extensive sensitivity analyses, including those using alternative break points other than 2008 (figs. S9 and S10). For example, when the LAI trend is retained, the dominant contribution of anthropogenic forcing to Ccomp is not found (fig. S10Z), suggesting the important role of vegetation greening in recent flash drought intensification. Together, we demonstrate that anthropogenic forcing has dominated the shift in the major driving mechanisms of global flash droughts, with anthropogenic warming and vegetation greening acting as the primary contributors to the observed surge in flash droughts.

Spatial patterns and attribution

Understanding when and where anthropogenically driven flash droughts emerge beyond historical variability is essential for mitigating regional drought risks. By analyzing compound-driven events in reanalysis products, we show a widespread occurrence of compound-driven events across mid-to-high latitudes, Africa, and Australia (inset in Fig. 3A). Most subregions exhibit a significant (P < 0.05) increasing trend (table S1), with hotspots detected in Eurasia, the Amazon, and Africa (Fig. 3A). The patterns from ALL and GHG (Fig. 3, B and C) simulations largely align with the results from reanalysis, whereas those from NAT and AER simulations do not (fig. S11, A and B). This suggests that anthropogenic forcing also dominates the flash drought increases at regional scales. Additionally, we find that the increase in compound-driven events, which are driven by anthropogenic warming and greening, is generally consistent with observations in northern regions (Fig. 3, D and E). In contrast, precipitation-dominated events are widely distributed across mid-to-low latitudes but exhibit weak changes for each region (fig. S11, C to F). Overall, these findings suggest that widespread shifts in the driving mechanisms of flash droughts are already clearly affected by human-induced global changes for most regions.

We also estimate when anthropogenic flash droughts emerged beyond natural climate variability by calculating the time of emergence (ToE) for a locally substantial increase in compound-driven events. ToE is defined as the timing when the signal-to-noise ratio of compound-driven flash droughts exceeds 0.5 (40) (see Materials and Methods). We find that the signal of increasing compound-driven events emerges from natural climate variability across the extensive Northern hemisphere and the Amazon region around 2017 (Fig. 3F), suggesting that our society and the natural ecosystems have faced detectable anthropogenic-driven flash droughts.

Human exposure and ecosystem impacts

The surge in flash droughts can substantially increase potential risks to both humans and terrestrial ecosystems. By analyzing compound-driven events in reanalysis products, we estimate the population exposure to such events as well as the associated reductions in terrestrial productivity. We find a large increase in population exposure from about 100 million people in 2001 to around 650 million people during 2015–2020 (Fig. 4A). Populations in regions with low and medium levels of human development show higher levels of exposure. The changes in spatial patterns of flash drought also drive a shift in the age distribution of the exposed population, with a large increase in children (under 5 years old) and elderly adults (more than 50 years old) (Fig. 4A), who are considered more vulnerable to climate disasters (41). The exposed population is mainly distributed in developing regions, including northern Africa, Southeast Asia, and India (fig. S12A). These findings suggest that anthropogenic-driven flash droughts may exacerbate the climate inequality crisis (42), with potentially greater risks for children, the elderly, and developing regions, who bear less responsibility for climate change.

Furthermore, our analyses reveal a significant (P < 0.05) decline in terrestrial gross primary productivity (GPP) associated with compound-driven events from 2001 to 2019 (Fig. 4B). The mean annual rate of decline is ~−0.01 Pg C year−1, resulting in a mean annual reduction of about −0.15 ± 0.1 Pg C over the most recent decade. These reductions are widespread in the northern mid-to-high latitudes (fig. S12B), a region that contributes substantially to the terrestrial carbon sinks and is particularly sensitive to climate change (43). Considering the certain contribution of flash drought–induced GPP reductions in controlling the interannual variations of GPP (inset of Fig. 4B), the antagonistic co-occurrence of widespread greening (36) and elevated flash drought–induced productivity reductions (43) in response to anthropogenic forcing may amplify uncertainties in future land carbon sink projections.

CMIP6 ESMs underestimate compound-driven population exposure and GPP reductions (fig. S12, C and D), particularly in low latitudes (fig. S12, E and F). This underestimation may be due to limitations in representing critical processes, such as vegetation physiology, or to coarse spatial resolution that misses fine-scale effects in low-latitude regions (10, 15, 20). This finding suggests the crucial need for ESMs to more effectively represent the processes that contribute to climate-change impacts.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we observe a substantial increase in compound-driven flash droughts since about 2008, contributing to the recent surge in global flash drought occurrences. Our finding provides direct evidence that flash droughts have shifted from being predominantly driven by natural variability to being driven by anthropogenic forcing. Droughts are typically considered prolonged and slow-developing phenomena, often persisting for several months or even decades, primarily as a result of natural climate variability (32, 44). In response to enhanced anthropogenic forcings, however, short-term anomalies in meteorological conditions and vegetation water use can exert a dominant influence on soil-moisture depletion over a short period (figs. S13 to S15). In a CO2-enriched planet, the drivers of flash droughts combine to create much more frequent and intense flash droughts that rapidly emerge outside of natural variability.

The pronounced increase in compound-driven flash droughts is mainly found in high latitudes, which could be related to amplified high-latitude warming. High-latitude regions are warming faster than the global mean due to Arctic amplification. This warming can directly increase soil evaporation and can also indirectly enhance transpiration by promoting vegetation greening and extending the growing season as vegetation activity in high-latitude regions is temperature-limited (23, 36). These processes accelerate soil-moisture depletion. At the same time, warming-induced changes in atmospheric circulation and increased precipitation intermittency can lead to short-term precipitation deficits (19, 45). When these temperature-driven increases in evapotranspiration, through both direct and vegetation-mediated pathways, coincide with anomalously low precipitation, soil moisture can decline rapidly, favoring the development of flash droughts. Such development of flash drought can also affect the regional climate and further enhance the warming, strengthening this feedback loop. Overall, the high-latitude hotspots reflect a shift toward more tightly coupled temperature-vegetation-hydrological interactions, which increases the likelihood of flash drought occurrence and contributes to Arctic amplification.

Our findings on the impacts associated with anthropogenic-driven flash droughts have large implications on ecosystem stability and human society. While widespread vegetation greening can contribute to climate mitigation through carbon sequestration and evaporative cooling, it can also lead to more frequent droughts (22, 46, 47), which could subsequently reduce ecosystem productivity and greenness. This amplified variability in vegetation status signals a decline in ecosystem resilience, potentially triggering a catastrophic shift to a less productive state (48). For example, enhanced greening in previous years may deplete deep soil moisture, leading to widespread tree mortality and potentially transforming forests to shrublands (49). Moreover, the increased biomass from greening can add to the fuel load, which, after drying out, may further intensify wildfires during flash droughts (50). When such fires occur in carbon-rich ecosystems, such as boreal forests or peatlands, they can release large amounts of stored carbon into the atmosphere, potentially offsetting carbon gains associated with prior greening. Repeated flash drought–fire events may also reduce biodiversity and promote an irreversible shift to grasslands when many tree species are lost (51). The resultant decline in ecosystem carbon sequestration, coupled with the potential amplification of positive carbon-climate feedback, will require a recalibration of allowable anthropogenic emissions to maintain alignment with established temperature targets. The impacts on ecosystems underscore the urgent need for a comprehensive impact-based forecasting approach (52) to complement the recent advancements in hazard-based forecasting. Our findings on flash drought drivers could support the development of such a system, which should ideally integrate climate, ecological, and human dimensions to address the wide spectrum of drought-related impacts. Such an improved system can benefit stakeholders involved in drought preparedness, risk management, and response planning, thereby enhancing resilience to rapid climatic changes.

Global vegetation greening reflects the combined influence of anthropogenic and climatic drivers. Rising atmospheric CO2 is the dominant global driver, enhancing photosynthesis and water-use efficiency and increasing leaf area (36, 53). Climate change modulates this response regionally, promoting greening in high-latitude and high-elevation regions while exerting mixed or negative effects in warmer, water-limited ecosystems. In addition, land-use and land-cover changes, such as afforestation and agricultural intensification, have driven pronounced greening in intensively managed regions, particularly in China and India (54). Together, these processes feedback on the climate system. Our findings on the effects of anthropogenic vegetation greening highlight the need to explicitly consider atmospheric CO2 concentration, in addition to global mean temperature targets, when proposing climate policy to mitigate high-impact climate extremes (55). Effectively mitigating the climate crisis necessitates immediate and substantial reductions in CO2 emissions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Datasets

We used three reanalysis products from 1951 to 2022, namely GLDAS/VIC (GLDAS_VIC10_3H version), GLDAS/Noah (GLDAS_NOAH10_3H version), and ERA5. The first two reanalysis products simulate transpiration (Tran) and soil moisture (SM) driven by observed meteorological forcing, whereas ERA5 provides estimates for these variables using a coupled atmosphere-ocean-land model. We also collected daily precipitation (Prec), mean temperature (Tas), transpiration (Tran), evapotranspiration (converted from latent heat flux divided by the constant of vaporization), LAI, maximum Tas, wind speed, air pressure, and SM from 13 state-of-the-art CMIP6 ESMs. The CMIP6 models were selected on the basis of their available data from experiments using anthropogenic and natural forcing (ALL), only anthropogenic greenhouse-gas forcing (GHG), natural forcing (NAT), or only anthropogenic aerosol forcing (AER). Additionally, these models have incorporated dynamic responses of stomatal conductance and vegetation cover to elevated CO2 and climate change. To match the temporal range of reanalysis data, historical ALL simulations were extended to 2022 following the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway 3 Representative Concentration Pathway 7.0 (SSP3-7.0) emission scenario. We used one ensemble member per model, that is, “r1i1p1f1” or “r1i1p1f2” in ALL experiments. The models used in this study are presented in table S2. While ESMs may underestimate the interannual variability of flash drought frequency, they capture the long-term trends and aggregated occurrence of flash droughts (fig. S16). ESMs are appropriate for the purposes of this study.

We obtained three satellite LAI datasets from 1982 to 2018, i.e., GIMMS LAI3g (54), GLOBMAP (56), and GLASS (57) LAI. The three satellite LAI datasets have been validated with field measurements, showing low temporal noise and high quality (54, 56–58). We obtained three GPP datasets from 2001 to 2019, i.e., VODCA2 (59), GOSIF (60), and FluxSat v2.0 (61) GPP. VODCA2, GOSIF, and FluxSat GPP are developed on the basis of satellite-estimated vegetation optical depth, the OCO2-based solar-induced fluorescence product, as well as the MODerate-resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer and eddy covariance tower site, respectively. These products generally exhibit a high correlation with global flux tower GPP (59–61). We collected estimates of human population distributions from WorldPop (62), including spatial distributions of total population counts and age-specific populations. We collect the human development index from (63).

We used the root-zone SM data provided by GLDAS reanalysis. Considering the different depths of SM across the ERA5 and ALL simulations, we linearly interpolated the profiles of each model and then extracted the top 1-m SM, which is more closely associated with root-zone SM and strongly coupled to the local atmosphere (4). However, very few models provide multilayer SM for the NAT, GHG, and AER experiments. For these three experiments, we obtained the top 1-m SM by multiplying total SM by the ratio of top 1 m to total SM from the ALL experiments. We also used top 1.5-m and 2-m SM, as well as root-zone SM from GLEAM v4.2 for sensitivity analyses.

Few CMIP6 models provide daily Tran, and ERA5 daily Tran contains a high number of missing values. Given that Tran is part of evapotranspiration and their partition is relatively stable over the seasonal cycle across years (64, 65), we obtained daily Tran through multiplying daily evapotranspiration by the ratio of the associated monthly Tran to evapotranspiration. For CMIP6, this ratio is derived from ALL simulations, while for ERA5, it is calculated from GLDAS. We collected the MPI-ESM1-2-LR large ensemble that provides daily Tran for sensitivity analyses. The large ensemble, with different initial conditions, can also represent a wide range of potential climate system trajectories. All datasets used in this study (except for population data) are regridded to a common 1.5° × 1.5° grid using bilinear interpolation (excluding deserts, Greenland, and Antarctic).

XAI framework for identifying major driving mechanisms of flash drought

Following (30, 31), we identified the major driving mechanisms of flash droughts during 1951–2022 via the XAI-based clustering framework, which has been effectively used to analyze flood or drought mechanisms. XAI methods are well suited for quantifying complicated relationships between predictor and predictand and disentangling the contribution of various drivers from large datasets, using an interpretable method to bridge existing knowledge gaps. The framework encompasses three main steps (fig. S1). We first constructed an artificial intelligence (AI) model, i.e., long short-term memory (LSTM), to learn the relationship between potential drivers and SM over the entire time series. Second, we applied an AI interpretation algorithm, i.e., the EG method, to interpret the trained models to quantify the contributions of individual input variables to the SM dynamics during flash drought events. Third, we used cluster analysis, i.e., K-means clustering, to group the contributions of input variables from all events exhibiting similar patterns into several types, from which we obtained an overview of all contributions and thus identify major flash drought mechanisms and their changes.

The XAI framework was applied to identify the driving mechanism for each reanalysis dataset, CMIP6 ALL, NAT, GHG, and AER simulations, separately. We conducted extensive experiments to ensure that the contributions of various drivers to flash drought development were robust and reliable, as identified by XAI. Our analyses suggested that the optimal number of clusters is 2, consistent across reanalysis datasets and simulations (details provided in the following sections). Based on the identified clusters, we classified flash droughts into two types with distinct mechanisms: compound-dominated cluster (Ccomp) and precipitation-dominated cluster (Cprec). Compared to traditional approaches that identify compound effects based on fixed thresholds of variable anomalies, the XAI framework provides a data-driven, model-informed classification of flash droughts without relying on subjective criteria. By incorporating sequential time-series into LSTM, the framework also captures potentially lagged effects of predictors. Moreover, it quantifies the contributions of each driver, enabling a more mechanistic understanding of flash drought. Detailed explanations of the methods are given below.

AI model construction

We used the classical LSTM network as the AI model, a widely used AI architecture particularly suited for analyzing sequential time-series data such as meteorological and SM dynamics. The effectiveness of LSTM partly stems from the comparability between its formulation and the effects of meteorological and vegetation factors, such as Prec, on SM. The backbone of the LSTM network consists of recurrent cells designed to capture and store information from the input time sequences, which is conceptually similar to how SM dynamics retain information about past and present meteorological and vegetation conditions. The physically realistic mapping from inputs to outputs facilitates gaining meaningful insights from subsequent model interpretations.

Following (31), we used SM percentiles as target variable, and Prec, Tas, and Tran anomalies as input variables, given their known impact on SM dynamics. The anomalies are computed using z-score standardization for each calendar pentad. We found a relatively weak dependence between the Tas and Tran anomalies (fig. S17, A and B). Moreover, consistent with previous studies (37, 38), our analyses also indicated that interannual changes in Tran were largely related to the increase in vegetation leaf area in both observation and ALL simulations (fig. S7, A to D). These findings suggested that the impacts of temperature-driven evaporation and vegetation-driven Tran on SM can be largely independent. We also considered wind speed, air pressure, humidity, maximum Tas, and LAI as input variables for sensitivity analyses. Additionally, we excluded vapor pressure deficit (VPD) because it is inherently linked to Tas and primarily influences SM indirectly by modulating Tran. Including VPD could therefore introduce redundancy with Tas or Tran and complicate the attribution of their individual roles in anthropogenic effects.

The input layer of LSTM includes the current pentad and the antecedent 35 pentads (i.e., X−35Prec,…,X−1Prec,X0Prec;X−35Tas,…,X−1Tas,X0Tas;X−35Tran,…,X−1Tran,X0Tran, in which “X” represents z-score standardized values, whereas the subscript “0” and “−n” represent the current pentad and the n pentads before the current pentad, respectively). The output layer produces the SM percentile of the current pentad. The SM percentile is calculated by converting the pentad-mean (5 days) SM values from the same calendar pentads across each year within study periods into percentiles. Given that precipitation is not uniformly distributed, often containing zero and relatively large values, pentad-scale data throughout the same month for each year, providing sufficient samples, were used for z-score standardization to minimize distributional errors and expedite convergence during the training process. The selection of 36 time steps has been thoughtfully considered to capture the subseasonal to seasonal timeframes (spanning weeks to months) relevant to flash drought events (8). We also used 18 and 54 time steps for sensitivity analyses. To balance model performance and computational efficiency, we built the model with a single LSTM layer and a dense layer with 30 units. The training epoch number, batch size, and dropout rate were configured to 50, 528, and 0.1, respectively, with the Adam optimizer. These hyperparameters were selected on the basis of preliminary experiments. For a thorough explanation of the fundamentals of LSTM network and architecture, we refer readers to (66).

Given the computational demands (as using global data to train the model would result in a high computational burden due to the extended time steps and study period) and the distinct nature of regional surface processes, we trained each independent LSTM model for each subregion (fig. S17C), which exhibits similar hydrometeorological and vegetation characteristics. This strategy ensures that the models account for hydrometeorological and land surface processes localized within subregions that might otherwise be diluted in a global model due to differing dynamics across regions. The dataset in each subregion was divided into 10 temporal blocks, where each block represents a specific and consistent time period across all grid points within the subregion. These temporal blocks were then sampled uniformly across all grid points, such that the same 7 blocks were randomly selected as the training set for all grid points, and the remaining 3 blocks were used as the validation set, thereby maintaining the independence of the training and validation datasets. The correlation coefficient (r) and Kling-Gupta efficiency (KGE) indicated that the LSTM models perform satisfactorily, with both median coefficient of determination (R2) and KGE values exceeding 0.65 and 0.62, respectively (fig. S17, D to G).

Flash drought identification

Using reanalysis and CMIP6 products, we identified flash drought events following (67) (here referred to as Qing’s method) in the main text, given its demonstrated effectiveness in identifying global flash droughts (67). Here, we briefly introduce this method. A flash drought event is identified when SM decreases from above the 40th percentile to below the 20th percentile, with an average decline rate of at least the 5th percentile for each pentad. Additionally, the onset phase of the flash drought should last for fewer than five pentads, and SM remains below the 20th percentile for at least three pentads. Last, a flash drought event is considered to have ended when the SM rises to the 20th percentile. Moreover, we considered two widely used alternative methods that consider the rapidly evolving nature of flash droughts: the method proposed in (68, 69) (here referred to as F&L’s method) and (4) (here referred to as Yuan’s method). We here focused on the development stages of flash droughts (70) during the growing season (April to September for the Northern Hemisphere and October to March for the Southern Hemisphere). Specifically, we examined the period from the onset of a flash drought to the first point of lowest SM, which includes the onset stages of flash droughts (fig. S18). Development speed is the difference in SM percentiles between two adjacent pentads (5-day periods), duration denotes the number of pentads that a flash drought lasts, and severity is the cumulative deficit of SM during flash drought. A flash drought event usually lasts less than 12 pentads (60 days) (70). If a detected event lasts longer than 12 pentads, then we focus only on the first 12 pentads.

Interpretation method and cluster analysis

The EG technique (71) was used to interpret the trained models, obtaining the timewise feature importance of the input variables for each flash drought event. The EG method is a gradient-based interpretation technique that uses the gradient of the model’s output to its input features to quantify the specific contributions of the inputs. The EG score for the input feature x at the ith time step (i.e., ΦiEG) is expressed as followsΦiEG(f,x)=Ex,∼D,α∼U(0,1)(xi−xi,)×∂fx,+α(x−x,)∂xi,(1)where U(0,1) denotes a uniform distribution between 0 and 1. ∂fx,+α(x−x,)∂xi, denotes the local gradient of the network (f) at a point interpolated between the baseline input (x,) and target input (x). D denotes the underlying baseline distribution. Here, a more negative EG value indicates a greater impact, such as a Prec deficit contributing to the SM decline, whereas a positive EG value indicates a favorable condition that mitigates flash drought. Note that a fundamental property of the EG method is completeness, which states that the sum of EG scores for all input features equals the difference between the model output at the input to be explained and its output at a baseline. The baseline is not a single fixed value but rather a set of samples randomly drawn from the background dataset (e.g., the training set). These multiple baselines aggregate contributions across the dataset, ensuring that the attributions capture the variability and diversity of the data distribution. The difference between the output and the baseline output can be decomposed into the collective contributions of all individual features. By adding up the EG scores for each input variable across all time steps, we can assess the contribution of each variable. A comprehensive explanation of the EG method can be found in (71).

Before carrying out the cluster analysis, we averaged each EG matrix (here, a matrix refers to ϕ−35Prec,…,ϕ−1Prec,ϕ0Prec;ϕ−35Tas,…,ϕ−1Tas,ϕ0Tas;ϕ−35Tran,…,ϕ−1Tran,ϕ0Tran) during the development stages of a flash drought and then aggregated the sequence of each variable into a low-dimensional contribution vector with three elements (i.e., [∑ϕPrec,∑ϕTas,∑ϕTran], where ∑ϕ represents the sum of contribution of a variable across all time steps). This aggregation is enabled by the additive property of EG values, which ensures that ∑ϕ reflects the variable’s overall impact. In (30, 31), a high-dimensional EG array (comprising all EG matrices from all events) was clustered directly or divided into different periods, followed by summation and clustering of the EG sequences. However, such a calculation would be computationally intensive and time-consuming when applied to a large global sample. Additionally, we aim to investigate the overall picture of driving mechanisms and their changes under anthropogenic forcing. It might not be essential to explore the detailed timewise changes in the contribution of each driving factor at the event level within the scope of this study. Moreover, while the XAI framework does not explicitly capture higher-order interactions, it offers a first-order approximation of compound effects in identifying drivers. Concurrent contribution of multifactor provides a straightforward and robust approach for assessing compound effects.

To gain a major overview of the aggregated contributions, we used the K-means method to cluster the results from all flash drought events. We used the silhouette score (72) to determine the optimal cluster number. The silhouette scores (figs. S19 and S20) suggested that the optimal cluster number is two across both reanalysis and simulations. To ensure comparability of the feature importance values across various spatial and temporal scales, we normalized each contribution vector by its Manhattan norm (i.e., dividing each element by the sum of its absolute values while keeping its sign) and then used the opposite of the values for clarity (e.g., the values in Fig. 1A, in which positive values contribute to SM depletion and flash drought conditions). While previous studies (30, 31, 73) have shown that K-means is suitable for clustering EGs, we also used Gaussian mixture models for sensitivity test, yielding similar results.

To account for the possible randomness and uncertainty in the training process, we independently ran the above XAI framework, including both LSTM and interpretation, five times for each reanalysis and ALL simulations. In each iteration, we created different but independent training and validation sets to ensure that the results are not sensitive to a specific data partitioning. We found consistent results across five runs (Fig. 1 and fig. S21), regardless of variations in the training datasets used for the XAI framework. To conserve computational resources, we ran the XAI framework once for NAT, GHG, and AER simulations, as well as other sensitivity analyses.

Quantifying the impact of anthropogenic trends on the increase in flash drought

We quantified the contribution of the anthropogenic trends within Ccomp and assessed their impact on the changes in compound-driven flash drought frequency (fig. S1). Following a similar analysis (28), we estimated the importance of anthropogenic trends as the difference between the importance derived from the above EG technique and those recalculated contributions after removing model-estimated anthropogenic trends from the input variables. We used the multimodel ensemble mean (MEM) from the CMIP6 as the signal of anthropogenic trends, as the internal variations among ensemble runs are largely smoothed out through averaging over a large number of ensemble simulations (74, 75). The MEM was calculated by averaging over the ALL simulation across all 101 realizations (table S3), with equal weighting. Following (75–77), we regress the time series of the driver of interest for each grid cell onto the global MEM and remove the fitted component over 1951–2022 (i.e., the effects of anthropogenic trends). The residual of the input variables, which were considered to be dominated by natural variability, is used to recalculate the associated contributions.

Given that anthropogenic forcing is detectable on any single day globally (39) and that flash droughts are identified on a pentad scale, the anthropogenic trend is removed independently for each calendar pentad using the global MEM at the corresponding pentad. We also considered monthly global MEM to remove anthropogenic trends for each calendar pentad within a month (fig. S10W). Again, given the important role of vegetation leaf area in regulating Tran (fig. S7, A to D) (37, 38), we removed the vegetation signal from Tran using similar linear regression and global MEM LAI to characterize the effect of vegetation change. The change in global MEM LAI is comparable to satellite record (fig. S7F). Additionally, to match the format of the input variables, the global MEM was calculated after standardization. However, the selection of a baseline period for standardization can affect the detected amplitude of the anthropogenic signal. Because the ideal baseline period should fully capture natural variability (32), we used the period 1850–1899 to standardize all realizations in the main text. We also considered the 1951–2022 period as a baseline but find that the detected anthropogenic contribution appeared relatively low (fig. S10X) because any recent anthropogenic forcing is included in this period (14, 32).

Second, building on approaches by Sippel et al. (39), who used ridge regression to detect the anthropogenic trend signal based on global spatiotemporal data for each day, and Jung et al. (78), who used linear regression to decompose carbon flux changes into several additive components, we used ridge regression to decompose interannual changes in the annual frequency of global compound-driven flash drought events (i.e., ∆Fre) into additive components, which are driven by the interannual differences in Tas, LAI, and Prec at each pentad across global land regions. It is expressed as the partial derivative obtained from a ridge regression∆Fre≈∑∂Fre∂LAIi,j×δLAIi,j+∑∂Fre∂Tasi,j×δTasi,j+∑∂Fre∂Preci,j×δPrei,j(2)where ∂Fre∂LAIi,j, ∂Fre∂Tasi,j, and ∂Fre∂Preci,j represent the sensitivity of global compound-driven event frequency to LAI, Tas, and Prec at locations i and j on global land regions, respectively, as derived through ridge regression; δLAIi,j, δTasi,j, and δPreci,j represent the interannual trend in LAI, Tas, and Prec at the pentad scale, respectively; ∑ denotes the sum of the effects of each driver across global land areas.

Global changes in compound-driven events can be associated with variations in Tas, LAI, and Prec across various spatiotemporal scales, which are influenced by anthropogenic forcing. Thus, we considered Tas, LAI, and Prec across global grid cells as specific forcing signals. The major advantage of the approach is that the response of spatial pattern to anthropogenic forcing is encapsulated in the regression coefficient, but regions with large internal variability receive less weight (39). Therefore, ridge regression optimizes anthropogenic trend signal against “climate noise” (e.g., natural climate variability) at any single pentad (39), thereby effectively partitioning the interannual changes in compound-driven events.

We implemented a cross-validation scheme to determine the ridge regression parameter and extract the sensitivity. Using 12 ALL simulations, we randomly selected 11 sets of compound-driven events along with Tas, LAI, and Prec for training the regression and obtaining the sensitivity coefficients, whereas the remaining set of Tas, LAI, and Prec was used for decomposition. We note that Eq. 2 is fitted separately for each calendar pentad, using the annual frequency of compound-driven flash droughts (i.e., aggregating flash drought occurrences across the 73 pentads within each year). The training data are pooled across all grid cells within the global domain, ensuring that the anthropogenic fingerprints are extracted from coherent spatiotemporal patterns (39). For each calendar pentad at the global scale, the total number of samples used to fit the ridge regression is 4,828,032, corresponding to 12 models × 72 years × 5588 grid cells. To ensure that each set in the training step was equally weighted, we subsampled the number of individual pentads used for training. To robustly capture anthropogenic signals in CMIP6 and consider uncertainty, compound-driven events from each model were trained and predicted 100 times using different Tas, LAI, and Prec from 101 realizations. Last, the median of decompositions from the 100 predictions was used for analysis. Our evaluation showed that ridge regression can effectively replicate the global annual frequency of compound-driven flash droughts, with NSE and KGE values of about 0.80 (fig. S17H).

Calculating the ToE

Following (40), the ToE, which indicates a substantial positive increase in compound-driven events due to anthropogenic forcing and thus its emergence from natural climate variability, is defined as the moment at which the signal-to-noise ratio of compound-driven events exceeds a threshold of 0.5. To ensure sufficient sample sizes, we aggregated the annual occurrence of flash drought in each subregion. Because there was a minimal change under the NAT simulation, with no anthropogenic signal (figs. S6D and S11A), we used the compound-driven events from the NAT simulations as the baseline and noise (N). Additionally, the 70-year period (1951–2020) is long enough to include both interannual and interdecadal timescales of natural variability in our estimate of N. Thus, the signal (S) is defined as the difference between the annual compound-driven events (for the reanalysis and ALL simulations) and the average events across the NAT simulations. The N was calculated as the average of the individual SDs of the compound-driven flash droughts across the NAT simulations. The ToE range can be 1951–2022. We computed the median years of the timing, when both conditions are met: (i) At least half of the individual datasets show an S/N greater than 0.5, and (ii) the median of compound-driven flash drought trends shows a significant (P < 0.1) increase. If multiple intersections occur between the S/N curve and the 0.5 threshold, then we conservatively used the last intersection as the ToE to ensure that the anthropogenic signal has consistently emerged beyond natural variability.

Estimating population exposure and GPP reduction

Similar to (41), we calculated an annual population exposure count, in which a population grid cell is counted as exposed if it experienced at least one occurrence of compound-driven events. Following (79), we analyzed GPP reduction associated with compound-driven events. To focus on GPP reduction, the linear trend was first removed from all GPP datasets individually to avoid misleading results due to regional positive (or negative) trends induced by CO2 concentration and climate change, which shifted the overall mean (43). Additionally, as GPP showed distinguishable seasonality, the annual seasonal cycle was subtracted from all datasets for each grid cell individually. In general, local negative GPP extremes (referred to as GPP reduction throughout the manuscript) were detected using the 20th-percentile threshold of the local detrended and deseasonalized GPP time series at each grid cell level (a common and standard for detecting GPP reduction) and were further classified according to the occurrence of anomalies below the threshold. The GPP reduction was then attributed to compound-driven flash droughts on the basis of coinciding climate anomalies. Specifically, if a GPP reduction coincides with compound-driven flash droughts, then it is considered to be related to this drought, because the identified flash drought experienced SM below the 20th percentile.
