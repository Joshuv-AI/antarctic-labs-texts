Addressing real-world data gaps: estimating the uk population cost of crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis using a flexible cost-of-illness model informed by the optimal patient journey and IBDUK patient survey 2023
Authors: Rachael MacDonald, Nick Hex, Barbara Uzdzinska, Beth Umpleby, Jessica Pocock, A. Barney Hawthorne, Richard Pollok, Jonathan Blackwell, Shahida Din
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), are chronic conditions affecting around 500,000 people in the UK and carries rising prevalence and substantial economic burden. Despite expanding therapeutic choice, contemporary UK cost analyses are scarce. A flexible cost of illness model was developed to estimate the excess costs associated with CDUC in the UK at a population level, including direct NHS costs and societal costs. The model estimates how the direct healthcare and indirect societal costs of IBD change over time, helping to analyse and manage key cost drivers. This supports planning and policy decisions by outlining how interventions and external influences affect long-term spending patterns.MethodsThe study used public data, published research, and the 2023 IBD UK survey to create a cost model covering prevalence, diagnosis, management, complications, and mortality of CD and UC. Clinical management inputs included resource use associated with the ideal pathways in line with IBD Standards. This model estimated and compared healthcare use and complications in people with CD and UC to the general population over 15 years, including costs from flare-ups, remission, and societal impacts.FindingsThe estimated total annual direct healthcare cost based on an optimal patient journey for CD and UC is £3 billion. Ongoing management makes up around 93% of this cost (£2.8 billion), of which 84% of this makes up remission-related treatment in people with active disease. The ongoing management costs of active disease is almost 3 times higher costs in remission, at £185,903,000 and £67,557,000 respectively. The annual lost productivity cost of CD and UC is estimated at around £0.8 billion, mostly due to inability to work. The total healthcare costs are £3.8 billion. In the absence of robust population based real-world data, and given the complexity of IBD care, this model offers a practical solution for estimating population costs, which are likely underestimated, and can be adapted as more accurate datasets become available.InterpretationAlthough pharmacological treatment accounts for a substantial proportion of IBD expenditure, effective control of inflammation and maintenance of remission are likely critical for reducing downstream complications, hospitalisation, surgery, disability, and productivity losses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15060-6.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is the term for a group of lifelong disorders, the most common of which are ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), that result in chronic and relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract [1]. In the UK, IBD is estimated to affect around 500,000 individuals [2]. Whilst the incidence of IBD in the UK is stabilising, the prevalence of IBD is continuing to rise due to compound prevalence [3]. IBD management aims to maintain symptom remission, heal the mucosa and prevent complications through treatments such as medications and surgery [4]. IBD mainly affects adults of an economically productive age, with peak incidence between the ages of 15 and 40 years old, and may result in several surgeries over the course of the chronic disease [5]. Therefore, there is a substantial potential economic impact.

Both CD and UC are associated with considerable direct and indirect costs to the healthcare system [6]. These costs can vary substantially due to delayed presentation, absence of a national diagnostic performance framework, non-standardised referral pathways for suspected IBD, and inequitable access to healthcare services [7]. Direct costs are those involved in the diagnosis, assessment, treatment of symptoms, and subsequent complications including hospitalisations, surgery, and cancer. Indirect costs are those associated with absence from education (affecting future employment attainment), paid work including sick leave, the cost of unemployment benefits, and loss of income tax revenue, early retirement, reduced employment, and early mortality (loss of work); reduced productivity of paid work (presenteeism); and reduced opportunities for unpaid activities (loss of leisure)[8].

Direct UK costs have been reported through complementary approaches using individual patient data and population-based studies [5, 9]. Other non-UK studies have explored direct annual healthcare costs and out-of-pocket costs for people with IBD [10–14]. The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology Commission into the cost of inflammatory bowel disease in high-income settings, not including the UK, estimated that in the period of 2010 to 2017, there was a mean annual direct cost of around $12,000 for treating CD and almost $9,000 for UC per patient [15]. Indirect costs accounted for a substantial proportion of the total spent on patients with IBD, with considerable variation between countries. Other UK and non-UK research has considered the indirect costs of IBD including absenteeism, presenteeism and informal care [16, 17].

In contrast to previous research, the present study adopts a flexible economic modelling approach to quantify the evolving cost burden of Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis in the UK. This model incorporates both direct National Health Service (NHS) expenditures and indirect costs related to lost productivity.

Exploring the factors that drive the costs of CD and UC is important to help decision makers understand the need for interventions in these disease areas. This is particularly important in relation to interventions that aim to prevent or reduce complications and the associated cost to the NHS.

Methods

This cost of illness (COI) study adopted a broad perspective to estimate the direct and indirect costs associated with CD and UC in the UK, excluding unclassified IBD or microscopic colitis. A prevalence-based epidemiological approach was used to consider healthcare resource use costs and a human capital approach (i.e. lost earnings due to disease-related disability) was adopted for societal lost productivity costs. Due to an absence of linked patient-level data, a bottom-up approach was not possible, but some data were drawn from a patient-reported survey. Secondary evidence sources were obtained through focused literature searches in MEDLINE, UK published data, clinical guidelines and expert clinical opinion.

A group of clinical and patient experts was convened and consulted during the development of a comprehensive cost framework, to identify key factors that contribute to CD and UC direct and indirect costs. The experts were recruited directly by Crohn’s & Colitis UK and experts from England, Scotland, and Wales were recruited to give a broad perspective. This framework included prevalence and incidence, management, diagnosis, complications, and mortality of CD and UC. Prevalence was broken down by nation and subregion, by health board in Scotland and integrated care board in England, for example.

A COI model was developed in Microsoft Excel driven by the cost framework, comparing rates of optimal resource use and complications for people with CD and UC versus the general population to estimate the excess costs of CD and UC over a 15-year time horizon. This was agreed with the steering group, and evidence shows that it can take up to 15 years for innovations to be fully adopted into the NHS [18, 19]. Data for CD and UC were presented independently and combined for overall costs from an NHS and societal perspective. The model was used to estimate the costs of CD and UC but can also be used to develop scenarios to consider the impact of future prevention programmes on prevalence of complications and, therefore, costs. All costs are from the 2021/2022 financial year and forward projections of cost are based on the predicted future increases in prevalence of CD and UC, not accounting for cost inflation.

Prevalence and incidence of CD and UC

Prevalence of CD and UC is the key driver of the COI model. Prevalence inputs use Office for National Statistics (ONS) UK population estimates to derive total population numbers for UC and CD [20]. Baseline prevalence inputs, sourced from a Crohn’s & Colitis UK report [2], are included in the model for Scotland, England, Wales and Northern Ireland by calculating weighted averages for each nation based on population size (Table 1). ONS population growth projections are included within the model which assumes that all nations will grow at the same rate. Management and complication costs are applied to the relevant proportions of the total population numbers. The number of new diagnoses of CD and UC are estimated using the incidence of new cases, which is assumed to stay constant over the 15-year time horizon (Table 2) [3, 21]. Diagnosis

Diagnosis costs were calculated from the estimated resource use involved in an optimal patient diagnostic journey of IBD in the UK, derived from published guidance (Table 3). The estimated resources included visits to primary care, a range of blood and stool tests in primary care, hospital admissions for a proportion of people with a new diagnosis, colonoscopies and sigmoidoscopies, CT and MRI scans, and examinations under anaesthesia. Small bowel capsule endoscopy was not included. Clinical estimates are that people with suspected CD will have an average of 0.43 CT scans and all will have an MRI scan or multiple MRI scans until diagnosis [22, 23]. Management

Clinical management inputs in the model include resource use associated with the population newly diagnosed with CD or UC, including a range of primary and secondary care appointments and tests with initial interventions, derived from NHS websites and clinical guidelines. The model also includes resource use involved in the day-to-day management of the prevalent populations with CD and UC, for both people with active disease and those in remission. The model was used to estimate the additional resources used by people with UC and CD compared with the wider population, to identify the costs of clinical management. Drug costs were estimated using a combination of clinical guidelines, the British National Formulary, and the drugs and pharmaceutical electronic market information (eMIT) (Supplementary Table 5). Biosimilar costs of the originator drugs were conservatively estimated at 80% reduction which is similar to other published studies on healthcare resource use [24, 25]. The model assumes that resource use associated with managing UC and CD is different between people who are suffering from flare ups of their disease and those who are not. The costs and resource use for management of CD and UC are, therefore, based on baseline resource use for all people with CD and UC with additional costs allocated for flare ups (Tables 4 and 3). The number of flare ups per year for CD and UC was extrapolated from data analysed from the IBD Patient survey completed by a sample of people with CD and UC. From the survey approximately 71% of CD and 73% of UC will have one flare per year and the average number of flares was 3 for CD and 2.6 for UC. Complications

Complications in the model include extraintestinal manifestations, drug-related toxicity and other complications (Table 7 and Supplementary Table 6). Complication inputs are presented as the proportion of people that experience each complication for CD and UC populations, as well as the general population. The model estimated excess complication-associated healthcare utilisation in people with CD and UC relative to the general population. Where a complication refers to a specific event, the input refers to the proportion of the population that experience that event within the year. The proportion of the population who experience the event at any time is accounted for within the cost calculations.

Societal costs of lost productivity

The analysis considers societal costs in terms of productivity losses due to absenteeism and early mortality (Table 8). Absenteeism costs were estimated using the excess annual number of sickness absence days among people with CD and UC relative to the general population. Excess days absent were converted into working weeks and multiplied by average UK earnings to estimate productivity losses. The economic value of informal caregiving is incorporated into these calculations through the inclusion of the non-working age population. The human capital approach was used to value productivity losses for people of all ages, not just those of working age. Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the effect of varying the total IBD population size to account for any uncertainty around undiagnosed cases and changes in the rate of mortality associated with IBD. In the absence of specific point estimates from literature, the consensus from clinical advisors was that the prevalence and incidence inputs should be increased by 20% and the mortality rate should be increased by 100%. Given the large number of model inputs and limited availability of robust parameter estimates, inclusion of all parameters within deterministic sensitivity analysis or probabilistic sensitivity analysis was not considered likely to provide meaningful additional insight so three key inputs only were considered.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that the costs of IBD are substantial, despite the prevalence of CD and UC being less than 1% in the population. Most of the costs relate to management of IBD, including the cost of treatment for people experiencing flare ups or exacerbations in their condition and the cost of helping to maintain people in a state where they are not experiencing flare ups. In the absence of robust population based real-world data, and given the complexity of IBD care, this model offers a practical solution for estimating population costs, which are likely underestimated, and can be adapted as more accurate datasets become available.

IBD predominantly affects the young productive age group [48]. Therefore, the indirect costs associated with loss of productivity such as absenteeism, days lost from schooling, education, and work, are substantial [49–52]. Lifetime costs are also increased with a paediatric diagnosis [53]. Active disease has been associated with a reduced quality of life and ability to work [54–57].

To conceptualise the impact of IBD on both patients and healthcare systems, NHS RightCare scenarios explored the costs associated with suboptimal compared with optimal care pathways [58]. The overall cost of IBD care was substantially higher in the suboptimal pathway, with considerable impacts on quality of life, confidence, personal relationships, employment, and productivity. These findings highlight a central paradox in IBD healthcare economics: treatment itself represents one of the major drivers of expenditure, while simultaneously being one of the principal mechanisms through which costly downstream complications may be prevented. Delayed diagnosis, poorly controlled inflammation, and fragmented care pathways are associated with increased hospitalisation, surgery, disability, and societal costs. Consequently, the objective is not necessarily to minimise treatment expenditure, but to optimise use of effective therapies and coordinated care pathways to reduce cumulative inflammatory burden and long-term healthcare utilisation.

The total annual direct healthcare cost of approximately 3 billion for UC and CD is largely composed of ongoing management, which makes up around 93% of the total cost at 2.7 billion. Earlier diagnosis combined with timely and effective treatment may help people with IBD achieve more sustained disease control, potentially reducing future healthcare utilisation and costs associated with ongoing inflammation, disease progression, hospitalisation, and complications. An important consideration is that the present model estimates average population-level costs and does not stratify patients according to disease severity, disease phenotype, or age. In clinical practice, healthcare utilisation differs substantially between mild, moderate, and severe disease. Moderate-to-severe CD and UC are associated with substantially higher biologic exposure, hospitalisation rates, corticosteroid use, surgical intervention, imaging, and multidisciplinary care requirements [15]. In particular, the cost burden associated with CD is likely to be strongly influenced by progressive disease behaviour. A substantial proportion of patients with CD develop stricturing, penetrating, or fistulising disease over time, contributing to increased healthcare utilisation and representing important drivers of long-term expenditure [25]. In contrast, many patients with mild UC may remain stable on relatively low-cost 5-ASA therapy with infrequent monitoring requirements [27]. Furthermore, the model does not explicitly account for demographic ageing or age-specific disease trajectories, which may influence future prevalence patterns and healthcare utilisation over time. Consequently, a relatively small proportion of patients with complex, refractory, or progressive disease are likely to account for a disproportionately high proportion of total healthcare expenditure [59]. Two paired publications have demonstrated that effective treatment significantly reduces serious worsening of IBD [60, 61], and the increase and earlier use of effective therapies is associated with a reduction in surgical operations [62, 63]. An updated health economic analysis of the PROFILE trial has demonstrated that using effective therapies earlier in the disease course is cost-effective [25]. Strategies to reduce the future costs related to IBD care include investing in preventing or delaying the onset of disease, earlier diagnosis with effective use of medical therapies, use of surgery as the primary therapeutic option for some patients, and national negotiations for the cost of maintenance therapies which currently varies across the devolved 4 nations in the UK.

New studies have suggested several associations which may predict future onset of IBD. Genetic variance and a family history of IBD in first degree relatives account for approximately 15 and 8% to 12%, respectively, of the risk for developing IBD [64]. Evidence suggests that modification of dietary (intake of fruit, vegetables and fibre) and lifestyle factors such as, smoking, use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical activity may prevent or delay the onset of IBD [65]. Further work is needed to define the optimal window for lifestyle interventions.

A substantial proportion of the management costs are attributed to long term use of effective therapies. Some studies have explored how drug treatment could be reduced but this comes at the cost of a future risk of disease flares and additional healthcare usage. Strategies to identify patients in whom drugs could be withdrawn without destabilising disease are needed. An alternative to medical treatment is surgery, which is often deferred until end stage disease. The National Confidential Enquiry into Patient Outcome and Death (NCEPOD) inquiry highlighted several key features to improve the care of patients with Crohn’s disease in whom surgery is more frequently needed in the emergent state. The inquiry identified the need for strategic early intervention by reframing surgery as a valid treatment option. The 2017 LIR!C trial concluded that laparoscopic ileocaecal resection was a cost effective treatment for adults with CD, with similar quality of life outcomes to treatment with infliximab at one-year follow up [66]. The retrospective follow-up study suggested improved outcomes with ileocaecal resection compared with infliximab therapy [67]. Health economic analyses in IBD may become rapidly outdated due to changing biologic pricing, biosimilar uptake, evolving treatment algorithms, and shifts towards subcutaneous and oral therapies. For example, conclusions from earlier economic evaluations comparing infliximab and surgery may not remain directly applicable in the contemporary biosimilar era where drug acquisition costs have changed substantially. However, surgical approaches may still offer important long-term advantages for selected patients beyond direct healthcare costs alone, including improved quality of life, reduced treatment burden, and lower cumulative inflammatory disease activity. In a Danish population-based nationwide registry study, the risk of the composite outcome, including hospitalisation, repeat CD-related surgery, systemic corticosteroid exposure, and perianal CD, was lower with ileocaecal resection compared with anti-tumour necrosis factor agents as primary therapy [68]. Approximately half of those with primary surgery were on no treatment at five years of follow-up.

The cost of maintenance therapies is the main source of the high-cost burden after the first year of diagnosis. Currently, in the UK there is no standardised costing framework, regulation, or national negotiations for the cost of new or biosimilar therapies. The costs of new medications are largely set by industry, with patents that protect the high drug costs for several years. Once the patent has expired, biosimilars can be released in the market, often at heavily discounted prices compared with the originator. Achieving an affordable and sustainable care model may require strategies aligned to disease severity and phenotype. In mild UC, optimisation of 5-ASA adherence and early disease control may reduce escalation to corticosteroids or biologic therapy. Dietary therapies may also have a role in selected patients with mild CD or as adjunctive therapy, but should not be interpreted as substitutes for appropriately escalated treatment in moderate-to-severe disease. Encouraging healthy lifestyle behaviours may also contribute to improved long-term disease management [24].

In addition to drug acquisition costs, biologic therapies generate substantial administration and infrastructure costs including infusion suite capacity, nursing time, pharmacy preparation, biologic homecare delivery services, cold-chain logistics, patient support programmes, and associated administrative infrastructure. These components represent an increasingly important part of modern IBD care and are not routinely captured in conventional drug costing analyses, meaning the present model may underestimate the true healthcare costs associated with biologic treatment pathways. Furthermore, challenges within homecare medicines services, including delays in medication supply, coordination difficulties, workforce pressures, and increasing service demand, may negatively affect treatment delivery, patient experience, and long-term sustainability of IBD care pathways [69]. Variation in access to specialist IBD services, advanced therapies, and multidisciplinary care across UK regions may also contribute to differences in outcomes and healthcare utilisation. Achieving sustainable high-quality IBD care therefore requires not only effective and affordable therapies, but also resilient medicine delivery and support systems. Increasing availability of subcutaneous and oral therapies may alter future cost structures by reducing hospital-based administration requirements, although the wider economic implications of these evolving delivery models require further formal evaluation.

Strategies to improve treatment persistence, including supporting patient adherence and compliance with medication, such as using immunomodulator combination therapy and therapeutic drug monitoring with anti-TNFs would also reduce use of super-high-cost drugs. Lower-cost immunomodulator therapies such as thiopurines (azathioprine and mercaptopurine) are used as combination therapy to anti-TNF drugs. They may also remain relevant as monotherapy in selected patients with UC, particularly as maintenance options after remission has been achieved, but their role should be interpreted cautiously given evolving treatment pathways, tolerability concerns, monitoring requirements, and the increasing availability of advanced therapies. Methotrexate monotherapy is not generally supported for induction or maintenance of remission in UC and has a more limited role in contemporary IBD treatment pathways. Strategies aimed at sustainable long-term care delivery should therefore be informed by contemporary real-world data on treatment persistence, steroid-sparing effect, adverse events, escalation to biologic or small-molecule therapy, and long-term outcomes rather than acquisition cost alone. More radical solutions may include national tendering processes for negotiating ‘best value for money’ deals with industry. This has been achieved in England for Adalimumab biosimilars and provides a framework for future national costing negotiations [70] as more patents expire that could benefit not only IBD but other licensed indications across all the devolved UK nations.

Taken together, these approaches to achieve universal, affordable, effective IBD care would require accurate risk stratification and disease classification to identify patients with IBD who are most likely to benefit from these strategies.

The projected rise in the prevalent population with IBD necessitates a better understanding of the healthcare costs. Clinicians are now treating people who have IBD and a range of co-morbidities, making it vital that there is innovation in care delivery such as personalised medicine and multidisciplinary care models [71].

An important area for further exploration is the burden of delayed diagnosis on NHS resources, particularly in emergency departments. These delays are known to exacerbate pressure on the healthcare system, prolong treatment waits and negatively impact the mental and physical health of people with IBD [72]. The National Primary Care Diagnostic Pathway for lower gastrointestinal symptoms gives guidance to healthcare professionals on screening and testing of lower gastrointestinal symptoms and is currently being implemented across the UK [73, 74]. An ongoing study is exploring the cost impact of delayed diagnosis of IBD.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this model is its adaptability, allowing the model to generate alternative results when more accurate data sets are available. The study was limited due to the lack of high-quality, publicly available individual-level data on IBD costs. As a result, it relied on aggregate data from multiple sources across different countries and time periods, and used assumptions where needed. The incorporation of patient reported survey data may be biased and not fully represent the UK IBD population. The model was not designed as a severity-state, age-stratified, or patient-level microsimulation model and therefore could not stratify costs according to disease severity, disease behaviour, treatment-refractory status, or demographic age structure. This limitation reflects the absence of linked national real-world datasets capturing disease phenotype, treatment exposure, complications, longitudinal healthcare utilisation, and age-specific disease trajectories. Consequently, the model applies population-level prevalence growth assumptions and does not explicitly model age-specific incidence, mortality, or demographic ageing effects. Future prevalence projections may therefore overestimate or underestimate long-term disease burden depending on changes in UK population structure and evolving patterns of disease complexity. Complication costs were estimated using excess prevalence relative to the general population and therefore reflect association rather than direct causal attribution. Some complications may share underlying risk factors with IBD rather than arising directly from inflammatory bowel disease itself. Sensitivity analysis was used to explore the extent of the impact of uncertainty on the costs. Major investment is required to develop comprehensive and structured clinical datasets encompassing IBD classification [75], key performance indicators [76], hospital episode statistics, and measures of quality of life, enabling real-time data collection to support more accurate health economic evaluations. The uncertainty around the model inputs is evident in the heterogeneity of costs in literature [5, 10, 11, 77, 78]. In considering the literature, it is important to be aware that the costs of IBD will be influenced by a range of factors including the inclusion and exclusion of resources by different researchers, variation in costs in different countries, and changes in treatment over time. The impact of the introduction of biologic drugs is an example of this [79, 80].

As such the study did not account for any additional differential impact due to sex, race or ethnicity, which are protected characteristics and not available without consent. A previous study has suggested that women have higher productivity-loss costs [81].

Conclusions

The impact of IBD on the NHS, society, and individuals is profound, with costs projected to rise dramatically over the next 15 years. Delays in diagnosis, inadequate access to timely care, and prolonged treatment pathways are placing an increasing burden on patients and healthcare services alike. This study highlights the financial and societal pressures IBD imposes, emphasising the urgency for comprehensive action. Understanding the economic costs associated with IBD is essential for informing policy changes aimed at improving care and outcomes for those affected. Although advanced therapies contribute substantially to healthcare expenditure, effective control of inflammation and maintenance of remission are likely essential for reducing costly downstream complications, hospitalisation, surgery, disability, and productivity loss. Prioritising the needs of people with IBD will not only enhance patient outcomes but may also reduce the substantial cost burden to the NHS.
