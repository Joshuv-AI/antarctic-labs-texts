Burden and Projections of Anemia Among Children and Women of Reproductive Age: A Systematic Analysis for the GBD Study 2021
Authors: Yuping Wei, Yimiao Xue, Siyi Du, Juan Tian, Yan Su, Rong Li, Junmin Chen, Lijun Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnemia is a significant global public health issue with substantial health consequences, particularly affecting children and women of reproductive age. This study analyzes the global burden and epidemiological trends of anemia in these specific populations from 1990 to 2021 and projects its trajectory to 2040.MethodsUsing data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study 2021, we assessed the age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) of anemia. Nordpred models were used to project prevalence and YLDs for anemia from 2021 to 2040.ResultsIn 2021, 599 million children under 15 years old and 657 million women of reproductive age globally had anemia. The most common cases were mild and moderate anemia. Mild and moderate anemia accounted for most prevalent cases, while moderate anemia contributed the largest share of YLDs. Nutritional deficiencies remained the leading cause of anemia-related YLDs, followed by other non-communicable diseases. Population growth, especially in lower Socio-Demographic Index regions, significantly influenced the rise in YLDs. Projections suggested that the age-standardized prevalence rate of anemia would increase among women of reproductive age from 16,722.91 per 100,000 population in 2021 to 17,585.71 per 100,000 population in 2040, whereas that among children would slightly decrease from 8,682.60 to 8,558.84 per 100,000 population during the same period.ConclusionAnemia remains a major health burden, necessitating urgent and effective strategies to address its diverse challenges.Keywords: age-standardized rates, anemia, estimated annual percentage changes, prediction, prevalence

Introduction

Anemia, characterized by a decrease in the number of red blood cells or the hemoglobin (Hb) concentration within them, significantly impacts global health.1 This condition is associated with serious consequences, including increased maternal and perinatal mortality, impaired cognitive development in children, and reduced physical capacity and work productivity in adults.2 While the causes of anemia vary based on its underlying pathophysiology, nutritional deficiencies such as iron, vitamin B12, and folate deficiencies are predominant in children, whereas chronic diseases like renal failure and inflammatory disorders commonly contribute to anemia in older adults.3,4 In 2019, the global age-standardized point prevalence rate for anemia was 23,176.2 per 100,000 population.5

As a critical indicator of both poor nutrition and overall health, anemia affects one in four individuals worldwide. It is most prevalent among women of reproductive age and children,6 who suffer due to the high iron demands for rapid growth and during pregnancy, compounded by iron loss during menstruation.7,8 Additionally, the global burden of anemia is significantly influenced by geographical region and economic development levels. In regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, the prevalence of anemia surpasses that in North America and Western Europe.9

Disparities in healthcare resources and diagnostic capacity mean that many patients, particularly those in low-SDI regions, may not receive timely diagnosis, standardized care, or effective treatment. However, the extent to which the burden of anemia differs by population group, severity, cause, region, and demographic change remains insufficiently characterized among children and women of reproductive age. We hypothesized that anemia burden would remain disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI regions and that population growth and epidemiological change would contribute differently to temporal changes in children and women of reproductive age. Therefore, this study used data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database to address three research questions: first, what were the global, regional, and national patterns of anemia burden among children and women of reproductive age from 1990 to 2021; second, how did severity, cause, SDI level, population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change contribute to this burden; and third, how may anemia prevalence and YLDs change by 2040 based on Nordpred projections. These analyses may provide evidence to support targeted public health strategies and resource allocation for anemia prevention and control in vulnerable populations.

Materials and Methods

Overview

This study used the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool) to explore the burden of anemia and its causes in children aged <15 years and women of reproductive age aged 15–49 years. A comprehensive description of the original data and analytical methods used in the GBD 2021 study has been reported previously.10,11

Data Source and Case Definition

This dataset offers estimates of anemia prevalence and years lived with disability (YLD) categorized by three severity levels (ie., mild, moderate, and severe anemia), and age across 204 countries/territories from 1990 to 2021. In this study, we extracted data concerning the burden of anemia, including prevalence cases and YLD rates.

In GBD 2021, anemia is defined as a decrease in Hb concentration in the blood, independent of the underlying cause, red blood cell morphology, or function.12 The criteria for diagnosing anemia and its severity are based on the World Health Organization (WHO) hemoglobin thresholds (expressed in g/L).13 The GBD 2021 anemia collaborators (2023)14 detail these thresholds, which vary by age, sex, and pregnancy status by WHO guidelines. Mild anemia is identified when Hb levels are at the lower end of the normal range; for example, in males aged ≥15 years, the range is 110 to 129 g/L, and in non-pregnant females of the same age group, it is 110 to 119 g/L. Moderate anemia indicates a more significant drop below normal levels, with thresholds set between 80 and 109 g/L for both males and non-pregnant females aged ≥15 years. Severe anemia is characterized by critically low Hb levels, classified as below 80 g/L for both genders in the aforementioned age group.

Anemia Causal Attribution

Many different diseases can cause anemia. The GBD 2021 study categorizes the following Level 2 causes associated with anemia: communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases (HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections; neglected tropical diseases and malaria; other infectious diseases; maternal and neonatal disorders; and nutritional deficiencies) and non-communicable diseases (diabetes and kidney diseases; digestive diseases; and other non-communicable diseases).

Socio-Demographic Index

The Socio-demographic index (SDI) is a comprehensive indicator that incorporates the education level of the population over the age of 15 years, the fertility rate among those under 25 years, and lag-distributed income per capita. The SDI value is scaled from 0 to 1, where higher values indicate a greater level of economic development.15 According to the SDI, the 21 countries/territories were classified into five groups: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high SDI.16

Statistical Analysis

We aimed to estimate the global, regional, and national burden of anemia among children aged <15 years and women of reproductive age aged 15–49 years. A descriptive analysis was performed to summarize anemia burden by population group, year, severity, etiology, SDI category, GBD region, and country or territory. The main outcomes included prevalent cases, YLDs, age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized YLD rate, expressed per 100,000 population where applicable.

Age-standardized rates were calculated using the GBD 2021 world standard population to improve comparability across regions and time periods with different age structures.17 For GBD estimates, uncertainty was reported as 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), which were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 posterior draws. For EAPC estimates, uncertainty was reported as 95% confidence intervals (CIs) derived from the log-linear regression model. Results were presented as counts, rates per 100,000 population, and corresponding 95% UIs or 95% CIs, as appropriate.

Estimated Annual Percentage Change

EAPC is a widely used summary measure of age-standardized rates (ASRs) over a specified time interval. To calculate the EAPC, a regression line is fitted to the natural logarithm of the ASR, expressed as y = α + βx + ε, where y = ln (ASR) and x = calendar year. The EAPC is then computed using the formula 100*(eβ-1) with its 95% CI to reflect the temporal trend in ASR. An upward trend in ASRs is indicated when both the EAPC and the lower bound of the 95% CI are positive. Conversely, a downward trend is indicated when both the EAPC and the upper bound of the 95% CI are negative.

Decomposition Analysis

The decomposition analysis method is used to identify the factors contributing to changes in the absolute number of age-related disease burdens. This technique discerns the additive effects of variations in factors between two groups (specifically, the populations in 1990 and 2021) and their influence on the overall disease burden. We performed decomposition analysis to evaluate changes in the number of prevalent cases and YLDs between 1990 and 2021, focusing on three components: age structure, population growth, and epidemiological change. This analysis allowed us to quantify the contribution of each component to changes in anemia prevalence and YLDs.

Prediction

Future trends in anemia prevalence and YLDs from 2021 to 2040 were projected using the Nordpred age-period-cohort model. The model was fitted using observed age-specific counts and corresponding population data from 1990 to 2021. The Nordpred model accounts for age, period, and cohort effects and uses recent trends to project future disease burden. To avoid unrealistic exponential growth in long-term forecasts, the model attenuates the drift component in future projection periods. Projected numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs were then combined with projected population estimates to calculate the corresponding ASPR and age-standardized YLD rate. Because the projections were based on historical trends and model assumptions, they should be interpreted as scenario-based estimates rather than deterministic predictions.18,19 All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2; http://www.Rproject.org).

Discussion

This study used the GBD 2021 database to analyze the epidemiological trends and disease burdens of anemia among children and women of reproductive age from 1990 to 2021 and projected the future burden of anemia up to 2040. These findings showed that anemia remained a substantial burden among children and women of reproductive age, although temporal patterns differed by population, severity, region, and disease burden metric. Additionally, regions with low and lower-middle SDI are more significantly affected by anemia. These results highlight the severity of anemia in specific populations and regions, underscoring the urgent need for targeted public health strategies and optimal resource allocation to more effectively address this widespread health challenge.

The divergent projected trends between children and women of reproductive age should be interpreted in light of differences in severity composition, age-specific risk exposure, and demographic pressure. Among children, the projected decline in overall anemia prevalence was modest and appeared to be largely driven by reductions in moderate anemia, which may reflect long-term improvements in child nutrition, infectious disease control, malaria prevention, vaccination coverage, and maternal-child health services. In contrast, the projected increase among women of reproductive age was mainly related to the increasing burden of mild anemia, while moderate anemia remained relatively stable and severe anemia declined. Women of reproductive age are continuously exposed to recurrent anemia-related risks, including menstrual blood loss, pregnancy, childbirth, lactation, and increased iron requirements. In low- and low-middle SDI regions, expansion of the population of women of reproductive age may further offset improvements in nutrition and healthcare access. Therefore, the different projected trajectories between children and women may reflect distinct biological, reproductive, demographic, and public health determinants rather than a simple contradiction in anemia trends.

In this study, we observed that mild and moderate anemia were predominant, particularly in low and lower-middle SDI regions. These findings are consistent with results reported in previous studies.20,21 Economic factors play an important role in low SDI regions, where residents cannot access sufficient foods rich in iron and vitamins.22 Furthermore, the inadequate allocation of public resources further obstructs access to basic healthcare and nutritional support for residents.23 Additionally, limitations in educational attainment exacerbate this situation, leading to a lack of fundamental nutritional knowledge and health awareness. Moreover, the scarcity of medical resources severely obstructs the timely screening and treatment of anemia; inadequate healthcare facilities and limited access to high-quality medical services contribute to this challenge in these regions. Lastly, the absence of targeted public health interventions, such as child nutrition support programs, exacerbates the severity of anemia issues.

Anemia can result from a range of factors, including nutritional deficiencies (iron, vitamin B12), infections and inflammatory diseases (HIV, tuberculosis), and genetic hemoglobin disorders (thalassemia, sickle cell disease).24 Our study reveals that globally, nutritional deficiencies are the primary cause of YLDs from anemia, followed by other non-communicable diseases. Neglected tropical diseases and malaria rank third, disproportionately impacting children and women in low SDI countries. Although HIV/AIDS and sexually transmitted infections contribute less significantly to anemia among children and women of reproductive age, there has been a notable increase in these causes of age-standardized YLDs over the past three decades. Nutritional deficiencies, particularly in iron and vitamin B12, are among the most common causes of anemia worldwide. Iron is a crucial element for red blood cell production, while a lack of vitamin B12 can impair the formation and maturation of red blood cells.25,26 These nutrients are often deficient due to unbalanced diets, especially in low-income areas where access to high-nutritional-value foods is limited. Furthermore, non-communicable diseases such as renal disease and cancer can also lead to anemia by impairing the body’s ability to produce red blood cells or directly affecting red blood cell production.27,28 Additionally, neglected tropical diseases and malaria disproportionately affect children and women in low SDI countries, causing anemia either by destroying red blood cells or inhibiting their production. The severity of these issues is exacerbated by a lack of public health resources in these regions. Cao et al29 underscores the complexity of anemia in individuals with HIV/AIDS, illustrating that the indirect effects stemming from immune dysfunction and antiretroviral therapy are significant. These treatments can induce bone marrow suppression and nutritional malabsorption, both of which are crucial factors in the development of anemia.

Decomposition analysis indicated that population growth significantly contributed to the rise in YLDs due to anemia among children and women of reproductive age, with more pronounced effects observed in lower SDI regions. This trend can be attributed to several factors including higher birth rates in these areas, which increase the population at risk, coupled with insufficient healthcare access that limits effective anemia prevention and treatment. To mitigate these impacts, it is essential to strengthen healthcare services, enhance nutritional programs, expand educational efforts, and increase research and monitoring to adapt interventions effectively. Addressing these areas can help reduce the anemia burden and improve health outcomes for vulnerable populations in lower SDI regions.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this study was based on GBD 2021 estimates, and the accuracy of the results depended on the availability and quality of primary data sources. Data scarcity may be particularly relevant in low-SDI regions, where surveillance systems and diagnostic capacity remain limited. Second, the analysis was conducted using aggregated population-level data. Therefore, individual-level factors, such as dietary intake, pregnancy status, menstrual blood loss, comorbidities, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare services, could not be directly evaluated. Third, although GBD provides standardized cause attribution for anemia, some causes were grouped into broad categories, such as “other infectious diseases” and “other non-communicable diseases,” which limited the ability to identify specific etiological contributors. Fourth, the decomposition analysis quantified the relative contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change, but it could not establish causal relationships. Finally, projections based on the Nordpred model were derived from historical trends and should be interpreted cautiously, because future changes in nutrition programs, healthcare access, infectious disease control, migration, economic development, and public health policy may alter the actual burden of anemia.

In summary, anemia represents a significant global public health challenge. Effectively controlling the burden of anemia requires the development of integrated interventions that specifically target the most vulnerable groups, particularly children and women of reproductive age.
