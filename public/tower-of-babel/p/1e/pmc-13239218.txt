Early‐Onset Mucocutaneous Findings and Isolated Progressive Thrombocytopenia in a Child With a DKC1 A353V Variant
Authors: Harun Kasapoğlu, Aslı Turgutoğlu Yılmaz, Berkin Berk Akbeyaz, Neslihan Çalışkan, Yılmaz Ay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDyskeratosis congenita (DC) is a rare telomere biology disorder characterized by mucocutaneous abnormalities and progressive bone marrow failure. We report a 10‐year‐old boy with a hemizygous DKC1 c.1058C > T (p.Ala353Val) variant who presented with unusually early mucocutaneous findings, including oral leukoplakia at 2 years of age, followed by reticular skin hyperpigmentation, nail dystrophy, and later isolated, slowly progressive thrombocytopenia. Bone marrow examination revealed hypocellularity with reduced granulocytic and megakaryocytic precursors, supporting an inherited bone marrow failure syndrome, which was confirmed by genetic analysis. Hemoglobin and leukocyte counts remained stable during follow‐up, and no transfusion requirement occurred. This case highlights that early mucocutaneous manifestations and isolated thrombocytopenia may represent initial features of DKC1‐related DC, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and long‐term multidisciplinary surveillance.Keywords: bone marrow failure, DKC1 A353V, dyskeratosis congenita, oral leukoplakia, telomere biology disorder, thrombocytopenia

Introduction

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is a rare inherited telomere biology disorder characterized by progressive multisystem involvement. Although classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, and oral leukoplakia, these features may not appear simultaneously, often leading to diagnostic delay [1]. DC is caused by pathogenic variants in genes involved in telomere maintenance, including DKC1, TINF2, TERT, TERC, NOP10, and NHP2, resulting in critically shortened telomeres and premature cellular senescence [2, 3].

Beyond hematopoietic dysfunction, telomere shortening can affect multiple organ systems, including pulmonary, hepatic, gastrointestinal, skeletal, and immune systems, leading to a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations [4]. Progressive bone marrow failure develops in up to 80% of affected individuals and represents the major cause of morbidity and mortality in pediatric‐onset disease [5]. In addition, patients with DC have a markedly increased risk of hematologic and solid malignancies, particularly acute myeloid leukemia (AML), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [6].

Management of DC remains challenging. Supportive care is essential, whereas hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is reserved for severe bone marrow failure due to the increased risk of transplant‐related toxicity in telomere biology disorders [7]. Here, we report a pediatric patient with dyskeratosis congenita caused by a DKC1 A353V variant, presenting with early mucocutaneous manifestations followed by isolated progressive thrombocytopenia, a pattern that may contribute to delayed recognition in pediatric practice.

Case Details

A 10‐year‐old boy was referred for evaluation of chronic thrombocytopenia that had been monitored at another center for 2 years, with platelet counts ranging between 80 and 100 × 109/L. His clinical history revealed early mucocutaneous manifestations, including oral leukoplakia at 2 years of age, reticular hyperpigmentation at 4 years, and nail dystrophy at 5 years. Thrombocytopenia was first detected at 7 years. Additional medical history included dyslexia and attention deficit disorder. The parents were second‐degree cousins.

Vital signs on admission were within normal limits. Physical examination revealed short stature and low body weight (weight 23 kg, height 125 cm, both below the 3rd percentile), reticular hyperpigmentation over the chest, neck, palms, and soles, fingernail and toenail dystrophy, and pectus excavatum (Figure 1). Neurological examination confirmed global developmental delay. No hepatosplenomegaly or peripheral lymphadenopathy was detected, and cardiopulmonary findings were normal.

Laboratory testing revealed moderate thrombocytopenia (platelet count 90 × 109/L) with otherwise preserved hematopoiesis, including normal hemoglobin, leukocyte count, and mean corpuscular volume. Peripheral smear showed reduced platelet numbers without blasts or atypical cells. Biochemical analyses, inflammatory markers, vitamin B12, folate, thyroid function tests, hemoglobin electrophoresis, viral serologies, and autoantibody panels were unremarkable. Ophthalmologic examination and abdominal ultrasonography were normal. Serial hematologic assessments showed stable hemoglobin and leukocyte counts, with a gradual decline in platelet levels over time (Table 1). Neurocognitive evaluation using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children‐Revised revealed performance below age expectations.

Bone marrow aspiration demonstrated hypocellularity without blast excess, dysplasia, or maturation arrest. Bone marrow biopsy demonstrated a cell‐to‐fat ratio of 35/65 with markedly reduced granulocytic precursors, mildly decreased erythroid precursors with membrane irregularities, and decreased but morphologically normal megakaryocytes.

Cytogenetic and molecular studies revealed a normal male karyotype (46,XY) in 10 metaphases, a negative MDS panel, and no abnormalities on DEB testing or paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria clone analysis. Cranial magnetic resonance imaging was normal. Next‐generation sequencing using an inherited bone marrow failure gene panel (150 genes) identified a hemizygous DKC1 c.1058C > T (p.Ala353Val) variant on the X chromosome, classified as pathogenic according to ACMG criteria, confirming the diagnosis of DC. Telomere length testing was not performed due to its limited availability at our institution; however, the diagnosis was established on the basis of clinical findings, bone marrow evaluation, and confirmatory genetic analysis.

Differential Diagnosis

In a child presenting with isolated thrombocytopenia accompanied by mucocutaneous abnormalities, both acquired and inherited conditions were considered. Immune thrombocytopenia was initially evaluated; however, the absence of clinical or laboratory findings suggestive of immune‐mediated platelet destruction, together with persistent mucocutaneous features such as oral leukoplakia, reticular hyperpigmentation, and nail dystrophy, made an immune cause unlikely. Attention therefore shifted to inherited bone marrow failure syndromes. Fanconi anemia was excluded by normal chromosomal breakage testing, and Shwachman‐Diamond syndrome by the absence of pancreatic insufficiency. MDS was also considered, but the patient's young age, stable blood counts apart from thrombocytopenia, absence of dysplasia on bone marrow examination, and normal cytogenetic and molecular studies argued against this diagnosis. Overall, the clinical picture was most consistent with dyskeratosis congenita.

Follow‐Up

Following diagnosis at our institution, the patient has been under active multidisciplinary surveillance for approximately 2 years. Mucocutaneous findings remained stable, and no new neurological, pulmonary, or gastrointestinal manifestations emerged. Thrombocytopenia continued to deepen gradually, consistent with the natural history of DKC1‐related disease, though no transfusion requirement occurred and hemoglobin and leukocyte counts remained stable. Bone marrow findings were consistent with hypocellularity without progression to multilineage cytopenia or blast excess. Otorhinolaryngologic and ophthalmologic evaluations were performed and yielded normal results. A comprehensive surveillance plan was additionally implemented, including semiannual dental examinations, yearly pulmonology assessments, routine monitoring of calcium and vitamin D levels, and periodic liver function testing and abdominal ultrasonography.

The patient is an only child; however, given the X‐linked inheritance pattern of DKC1‐related dyskeratosis congenita, the family was referred for genetic counseling regarding future pregnancies. Maternal carrier testing confirmed that the mother is a carrier of the DKC1 A353V variant, highlighting the importance of genetic counseling and prenatal screening in affected families. Close hematologic monitoring with regular complete blood counts is also maintained, given the risk of progression to MDS or AML.

Overall, the patient has remained clinically stable without progression to multilineage cytopenia to date. This course supports continued close monitoring and proactive, multidisciplinary surveillance given the known long‐term risks associated with dyskeratosis congenita.

Discussion

Our patient with X‐linked dyskeratosis congenita (DC) due to the recurrent DKC1 c.1058C > T (p.Ala353Val; A353V) variant demonstrated the classical mucocutaneous triad followed by hematologic involvement. Oral leukoplakia appeared at 2 years of age, which is notably earlier than the typically reported onset range of 5–14 years in published pediatric cohorts, where mucocutaneous manifestations usually precede cytopenias but rarely present this early [1, 8, 9]. The A353V variant is the most frequently reported missense mutation in DKC1, accounting for nearly one‐third of X‐linked DC cases [10, 11]. Despite its recurrence, the phenotype associated with A353V is variable, ranging from mild cytopenias to early severe marrow failure within affected families [9, 10, 11]. The isolated and slowly progressive thrombocytopenia observed in our patient represents the milder end of this variability, although continued monitoring is essential because carriers of the same variant may later develop multilineage cytopenias.

Approximately 80% of individuals with DC experience progressive bone marrow failure by adolescence [1, 4, 8]. Macrocytosis and multilineage cytopenias are common early indicators of declining marrow reserve [11]. In contrast, our patient maintains stable hemoglobin and leukocyte counts without transfusion requirements, supporting a conservative management approach. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative therapy for bone marrow failure, but toxicity related to pulmonary, hepatic, and mucosal complications is substantial in telomere biology disorders [12, 13]. Although reduced‐intensity conditioning has improved outcomes, survival remains suboptimal [13]. Androgen therapy, including danazol, has been associated with hematologic improvement and telomere elongation in select cases and may serve as a bridging strategy before HSCT when indicated [14]. Emerging non‐cellular therapies, including PAPD5 inhibitors, represent a promising area of investigation; these small molecules stabilize the telomerase RNA component TERC and have demonstrated telomere restoration in preclinical models of DC, though clinical data remain pending [15].

Neurocognitive impairment is not typically associated with classical DC but characterizes more severe telomere biology disorders such as Hoyeraal‐Hreidarsson and Revesz syndromes [6, 16, 17]. Our patient demonstrated developmental delay despite having normal neuroimaging, which broadens the reported neurocognitive spectrum of classical DC and underscores the importance of routine developmental assessment in these patients.

Cancer predisposition remains a major concern in telomere biology disorders. Studies have shown markedly increased risks for squamous cell carcinoma and other solid tumors, with cumulative incidence approaching 40% by mid‐adulthood [5]. Risk is further elevated among transplanted patients compared with non‐transplanted individuals [12]. Our multidisciplinary surveillance plan—including dermatologic, dental, otorhinolaryngologic, ophthalmologic, pulmonologic, and hepatic evaluations—aligns with best‐practice recommendations for early detection of malignancy and systemic complications [1, 18].

Conclusion

This case illustrates a classical yet phenotypically distinctive presentation of X‐linked dyskeratosis congenita caused by the recurrent DKC1 A353V variant, characterized by unusually early mucocutaneous onset, isolated progressive thrombocytopenia, and developmental delay despite normal neuroimaging. These findings reinforce the broad phenotypic variability inherent to this variant and underscore that bone marrow failure need not be overt for the diagnosis to be considered. Early recognition, prompt genetic evaluation, and long‐term multidisciplinary surveillance remain the cornerstones of optimal management in pediatric telomere biology disorders. Taken together, this case emphasizes that early mucocutaneous findings combined with isolated thrombocytopenia should prompt consideration of telomere biology disorders, even in the absence of overt bone marrow failure.
