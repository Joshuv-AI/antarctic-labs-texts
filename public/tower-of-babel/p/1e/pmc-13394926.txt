Mutational Signature‐Based Biomarker for Phase II Trial of Olaparib Maintenance in Advanced High‐Grade Ovarian Cancer
Authors: Katsutoshi Oda, Daisuke Shintani, Akira Nishijima, Mayuyo Mori, Kazuyoshi Kato, Shoji Nagao, Noriomi Matsumura, Masahiro Kagabu, Masataka Takenaka, Kosuke Yoshihara, Yosuke Konno, Junzo Hamanishi, Sho Sato, Shiro Takamatsu, Keiko Ueda, Kosuke Kashiwabara, Takashi Aoki, Kunihiro Nishimura, Kenji Tatsuno, Hiroyuki Aburatani, Kosei Hasegawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe aimed to develop the mutational signature‐based BioMarker (MSBM) to define homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) in newly diagnosed, advanced ovarian cancer and overcome limitations of genomic scar‐based assays. We conducted a phase 2 trial (MSBM‐OL) to evaluate the efficacy of olaparib maintenance monotherapy and to validate MSBM as a novel HRD biomarker. The MSBM was designed to integrate three whole‐exome sequencing‐derived features without using genomic scars: tumor‐BRCA mutation status, Mutational Signature 3 similarity score > 0.5, or score > 0.25 without CCNE1 amplification. Among 170 patients with stage IIIC/IV high‐grade ovarian carcinoma, 58 HRD‐positive patients responding to platinum‐doublet chemotherapy (without bevacizumab) were enrolled. Thirty‐two were randomized 2:1 to olaparib or placebo, and 26 enrolled after switching to a single‐arm study. The primary endpoint was progression‐free survival (PFS), evaluated against a predefined median of 9 months. Of 170 patients, 169 were evaluable for HRD (99.5%) and 123 (72.4%) were HRD‐positive by MSBM, including 21.1% with tumor‐BRCA mutations. The olaparib arm (n = 45) showed a median PFS of 22.3 months, significantly exceeding the 9‐month threshold (p < 0.001), meeting the primary endpoint. The placebo arm (n = 11) with a median PFS of 16.7 months was not powered for comparison. Grade ≥ 3 adverse events included anemia (25.5%) and neutropenia (12.8%). In a subset (n = 16), MSBM showed 81% concordance with myChoice CDx and correlated with genomic instability scores (R = 0.63). MSBM enables robust HRD evaluation from a whole‐exome sequencing assay. Olaparib monotherapy demonstrated clinical benefit for first‐line maintenance in HRD‐positive ovarian cancer without bevacizumab.Keywords: advanced high‐grade serous ovarian cancer, clinical trial, homologous recombination deficiency, mutational signature‐based biomarker, olaparib maintenance monotherapy

Introduction

High‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) is the most common histological type of advanced‐stage ovarian carcinoma and is often unresectable at diagnosis [1, 2]. Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), often driven by germline (gBRCAm) or somatic (sBRCAm) BRCA1/2 mutations, is a key determinant of sensitivity to platinum‐based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. In ovarian cancer, sBRCAm accounts for > 20% of tumor BRCA mutations (tBRCAm).

In the phase III PAOLA‐1/ENGOT‐ov25 trial, adding the PARP inhibitor olaparib to bevacizumab maintenance therapy prolonged progression‐free survival (PFS) and showed favorable overall survival (OS) in newly diagnosed, advanced ovarian cancer with HRD‐positivity [12, 13]. Olaparib maintenance monotherapy was suggested to improve the 7‐year OS in a first‐line gBRCAm setting [14]. However, whether olaparib maintenance monotherapy is effective in HRD‐positive patients without gBRCAm (mainly BRCA‐wt) remains to be determined. Niraparib, whose first‐line maintenance indication was restricted by the FDA in June 2025 to HRD‐positive ovarian cancer, currently lacks an FDA‐approved companion diagnostic (CDx), highlighting the need for new HRD biomarkers [15, 16, 17].

The MyChoice CDx test is used to assess HRD using a genomic instability (GI) score combined with tBRCAm status; a GI score ≥ 42 or a positive tBRCAm status defines HRD positivity [18, 19]. The GI score reflects genomic scarring—loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic imbalance, and large‐scale state transitions [18]. FoundationOne CDx has likewise been used to calculate loss‐of‐heterozygosity scores in relapsed high‐grade ovarian carcinoma [20, 21].

Base substitution patterns, or mutational signatures, are powerful indicators of carcinogenesis, including DNA repair defects [22, 23, 24]. In HGSOC, Signature 3 (also known as the BRCA signature) is associated with biallelic BRCA loss and HRD (originally defined in COSMIC Mutational Signatures v2) [24]. Alongside the age‐related Signature 1, these two signatures account for nearly all HGSOC cases, with the BRCA signature present in roughly 70% of cases [25, 26]. Tumors dominated by the BRCA signature show a broad spectrum of base substitutions, whereas those with the Age signature mainly exhibit C > T transitions (5mCG to TG).

Copy number‐based HRD measures are particularly vulnerable to reduced tumor purity, with copy number variation calling accuracy dropping sharply at ≤ 20%, whereas mutational signatures remain largely unaffected by tumor purity or structural variation [27, 28]. Signature 3 correlates well with genomic‐instability scores and predicts PARP inhibitor response in breast and ovarian cancers, whereas CCNE1 amplification defines an HR‐proficient, PARP‐resistant subtype and is largely exclusive of HRD [29, 30, 31, 32]. Whole‐exome or whole‐genome sequencing (WES/WGS) provide these mutational signatures together with a full somatic/germline mutation profile, including CCNE1 amplification, offering advantages over MyChoice CDx and conventional comprehensive genomic profiling.

We developed the Mutational Signature‐based BioMarker (MSBM), which integrates Signature 3, tBRCAm, and CCNE1 copy number to identify HRD phenotypes and guide PARP inhibitor treatment. To validate the MSBM, we performed a prospective multicenter phase II trial of olaparib maintenance monotherapy, without bevacizumab, in patients newly diagnosed with stage IIIC/IV HGSOC whose tumors were MSBM‐positive following first‐line platinum therapy, with PFS as the primary endpoint. This trial highlights the clinical relevance of MSBM as a potential alternative to genomic scar‐based HRD assays and demonstrates the efficacy of olaparib monotherapy in this setting.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This multicenter phase II study of olaparib maintenance monotherapy was conducted at 11 sites in Japan and registered in the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT 2080223978). Eligible patients had newly diagnosed stage IIIC/IV high‐grade ovarian, peritoneal, or fallopian tube cancer with residual disease following primary surgery (interval debulking was allowed). Patients were required to have achieved a complete or partial response after completing 6–8 cycles of first‐line platinum‐doublet chemotherapy without bevacizumab. Tumor tissues were collected prior to chemotherapy and paired with blood samples for DNA extraction. Full eligibility criteria and IRB information are provided in Doc. S1 (Supplementary Methods) and Doc. S2 (Study Protocol).

DNA Extraction and WES

DNA was extracted from freshly frozen tumor tissues and matched blood samples [33]. WES was performed at ISO 15189‐certified laboratories (Sysmex Inc. or the University of Tokyo Hospital). Sequencing quality control included cluster density, Q30 scores, and error rates. Variant annotation and copy number analyses were conducted using a unified pipeline based on public databases (COSMIC, ClinVar, and OncoKB). Mutational signature analysis was performed using deconstructSigs to estimate similarity to COSMIC mutational signatures, requiring a minimum of 10 somatic SNVs per sample. SBS3 thresholds (> 0.5 and > 0.25 without CCNE1 amplification) were defined based on a biologically motivated framework reflecting the predominance of Signature 3 versus Signature 1 observed in the TCGA/AOCS cohort. Further technical details are available in Doc. S1. The MSBM results, including germline and somatic BRCA status, were disclosed to investigators, enabling genetic counseling for gBRCA‐positive patients.

Transition From Randomized to Single‐Arm Design and Study Endpoints

Although the study was initially designed as a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled trial enrolling MSBM‐positive patients in a 2:1 ratio to olaparib (tablet form, 300 mg twice daily) or placebo, it was amended to a single‐arm format following the 2019 approval of olaparib for gBRCAm‐positive patients in the first‐line setting (SOLO1) [34]. As a result, only 11 patients were enrolled in the placebo arm, which was included for reference purposes, whereas the primary analysis was based on the single‐arm design. In total, 58 MSBM‐positive patients were enrolled (32 in the randomized phase and 26 in the single‐arm phase). After exclusion of 2 ineligible patients from the olaparib arm, the full analysis set (FAS) comprised 56 patients (olaparib, n = 45; placebo, n = 11). Baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. Details of the design amendment are provided in Doc. S1.

The primary endpoint was PFS from enrollment. The primary analysis used a one‐sample log‐rank test against a historical control, assuming a median PFS of 9 months in the placebo group, based on previous trials (GOG‐0262, GOG‐218) [35, 36]. A 7‐month improvement (HR: 0.56) was hypothesized [16, 35, 37]. Sample size was calculated assuming exponential PFS distribution and allowing for dropout (details in Doc. S1). The secondary endpoints included OS, time to second progression (PFS2), time to treatment discontinuation or death (TTD), adverse events (AEs), and concordance between MSBM and myChoice CDx.

Treatment and Assessment

Patients continued to receive the study treatment for up to two years or until objective radiological disease progression as per RECIST as assessed by the investigator, whichever occurred earlier, and if in the investigator's opinion, they benefited from treatment and did not meet any other discontinuation criteria. Patients underwent tumor assessments according to RECIST (version 1.1) at baseline and every 12 weeks (±1 week) up to 157 weeks and then every 24 weeks (±1 week), until objective radiological disease progression according to RECIST.

Statistical Analysis

The efficacy analysis included all patients who received olaparib (FAS, n = 45) (Figure 1A). PFS was analyzed using a one‐sample log‐rank test against an exponential distribution with a median PFS of 9 months as the null hypothesis. HRs, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p‐values were calculated based on investigator‐assessed RECIST progression. Initial analyses of OS, PFS2, and TTD were performed concurrently using the same methodology. The safety analysis set included all treated patients (olaparib n = 47, total n = 58) (Figure 1A). myChoice CDx results were obtained for 16 patients after regulatory approval in November 2020.

Results

Establishment of the MSBM Based on WES and Definition of “HRD”

An algorithm for extracting mutational signatures was developed using non‐negative matrix factorization and model selection from a catalog of cancer genomes [22]. To develop the MSBM as a biomarker, we used deconstructSigs to calculate similarity with each of the 30 mutational signatures [38]. Definitions of the predominant mutational signatures, CCNE1 amplification, and tBRCA status are summarized in Figure S1. We defined the MSBM on the basis of the predominance of the BRCA signature from the training dataset (n = 474), derived from our own previous data (n = 78) (https://humandbs.dbcls.jp/en/hum0030‐v2) and publicly available data (n = 396) of WES and WGS [5, 25] (Figure 1B). MSBM positivity was defined as meeting one of the following criteria: (i) presence of tBRCAm, (ii) Signature 3 similarity score > 0.5 with deconstructSigs, or (iii) Signature 3 similarity score > 0.25 (and higher than the Signature 1 similarity score) without CCNE1 amplification.

MSBM Analysis by WES

As part of the screening phase, 201 patients were recruited, and WES was performed for 190 patients between December 2018 and September 2021. Twenty patients were excluded owing to unmet histological criteria, including 11 with clear cell carcinoma (Table S1). Among the remaining 170 patients with eligible histology (160 high‐grade serous and 10 other high‐grade epithelial carcinomas), 169 (99.5%) were suitable for mutational signature analysis.

Mutational Signature 3 (BRCA signature) was predominant in 135 cases (79.4%), whereas Signature 1 (Age signature) was predominant in 20 cases (11.8%). tBRCAm was detected in 36 patients (21.2%), including 27 with gBRCAm and 9 with sBRCAm. tBRCA1 mutations were observed in 26 patients and tBRCA2 in 10 patients. All but two tBRCAm cases exhibited predominant Signature 3 (Figure 2A and Table 2). In addition, variants of unknown significance (VUS) in tBRCA1/2 were identified in 21 patients (12.4%) (Table S2). A typical example of a low‐purity tumor (estimated tumor content < 20%) evaluated as MSBM‐positive is shown in Figure S2, with a Signature 3 similarity score of 0.269 (> 0.25) and no clear copy number variations, likely due to low tumor content.

Based on the predefined criteria, 123 of the 170 patients (72.4%) were classified as MSBM‐positive (Table 2). Of these, 61 patients who achieved a complete or partial response to platinum‐doublet chemotherapy (without bevacizumab) were enrolled in the phase II trial, and 58 proceeded to treatment. The study initially followed a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled design, but was later amended to a single‐arm format (Figure 1A).

The distribution of somatic SNV counts (median 210, range 10–1957) and tumor purity (median 53.0%, range 12.0%–99.0%) across the cohort is shown in Table S3 and Figure S3. The MSBM‐positive rate was stable across different SNV thresholds (66.3%–68.5%) and across tumor purity strata (58.3%–67.9%), supporting the robustness of mutational signature assignment across varying SNV counts and tumor purity levels (Table S4 and Figure S3).

To assess the robustness of MSBM classification, we evaluated the impact of different COSMIC signature catalogs (v2, v3.2, v3.5) and normalization modes on SBS3 estimation in both the TCGA/AOCS training set (n = 474) and the trial cohort (n = 190) (Table S5, Figure S4). Under COSMIC v3.2/v3.5, the median SBS3 score was 0 in both cohorts (vs. 0.287–0.332 under v2), and the correlation between v2 and v3.2 SBS3 scores was modest (Spearman ρ = 0.34 in the training set), suggesting substantial redistribution of BRCA‐associated mutational patterns across multiple signatures in v3.x. Applying the original v2‐derived MSBM thresholds to v3.x resulted in reclassification of 70–80 of 89 MSBM‐positive non‐tBRCA cases as negative, with overall concordance reduced to 45%–57%, compared with 71%–75% under v2 normalization modes.

To provide a statistical reference for the SBS3 thresholds, we performed a post hoc ROC/PR analysis using tBRCA mutation status as a surrogate for HRD in the trial cohort (n = 190) (Figure S5, Table S6). The Youden‐optimal threshold was 0.345, which is higher than the predefined threshold of > 0.25. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.633 (95% CI: 0.536–0.718), with substantial overlap in SBS3 scores between tBRCA‐positive and tBRCA‐negative cases.

For germline BRCA1/2‐mutated cases, variant allele frequency (VAF) patterns in tumor samples, in conjunction with tumor purity estimates, were generally compatible with biallelic inactivation (Table S7). Among non‐BRCA HR genes, one case with a pathogenic somatic alteration in PALB2 showed VAF patterns suggestive of biallelic inactivation; however, this case overlapped with a gBRCAm tumor. In addition, one case each with truncating alterations in RAD51C and RAD51D showed VAF patterns compatible with biallelic inactivation. Notably, the RAD51C‐ and RAD51D‐altered cases showed Signature 3 similarity scores of 0.556 and 0.616, respectively, and were MSBM‐positive (Table S2). One case showed an extremely low tumor VAF (2/107), which was inconsistent with the expected biallelic pattern.

Patient Characteristics in the Phase 2 Trial

Among 123 MSBM‐positive patients, 61 with a CR/PR response to platinum‐based chemotherapy were enrolled, and 58 received the investigational drug, including 11 in the placebo arm, between April 2019 and March 2022 (Figure 1A). Two patients from the olaparib arm (n = 45) were deemed ineligible for inclusion. The FASp included 45 patients from the olaparib arm (21 from the randomization and 24 from the single‐arm phase) and 11 from the placebo arm, totaling 56 patients (Figure 1A). The characteristics of the 56 patients (all high‐grade serous carcinomas), including four with gBRCAm (7.1%) and six with sBRCAm (10.7%), are detailed in Table 1.

The reasons for non‐enrolment of the remaining 62 MSBM‐positive patients included: (i) physician/patient choice, including the use of approved olaparib therapy for gBRCAm; (ii) incomplete consent for phase 2 participation; (iii) absence of CR/PR to chemotherapy; and (iv) failure to meet the criteria (e.g., bone marrow suppression and renal dysfunction).

Efficacy

At the data cutoff (January 31, 2024), the median follow‐up time was 29.1 months. The median PFS in the olaparib arm (n = 45) was 22.3 months (95% CI, 16.4–33.3), which significantly rejected the null hypothesis of the one‐sample log‐rank test (p < 0.001; HR: 0.35; 95% CI: 0.24–0.50) (Figure 2B). Sensitivity analyses using alternative null median PFS assumptions of 12, 14, and 16 months showed consistent treatment effects, with hazard ratios relative to each null hypothesis ranging from 0.461 to 0.614, and statistical significance maintained even under the most conservative null assumption of 16 months (Table S8). PFS analyses stratified by MSBM subgroups showed favorable outcomes consistent with known sensitivity to PARP inhibition in the tBRCA‐positive group, while no clear difference in clinical outcome was observed between SBS3‐high (> 0.5) and intermediate (0.25–0.5 without CCNE1 amplification) subgroups (Figure S6 and Table S9). The median PFS in the placebo arm (n = 11) was 16.7 months (95% CI: 2.8 m‐NR) and did not show a statistically significant difference compared to the olaparib arm (p = 0.109 by Log‐rank test) (Figure 2C). As most placebo patients switched to olaparib after unblinding, follow‐up was censored early, resulting in a shorter observation period and limited interpretability of PFS in the placebo arm. The PFS and OS rates at 24 months in the olaparib arm were 48.6% (95% CI: 33.4%–62.2%) and 78.6% (95% CI: 62.8%–88.3%), respectively (Figure 2B,D), and median PFS2 was 24.4 months (95% CI: 16.5–39.0) (Figure S7A). PFS2 was evaluated among patients who received post‐progression therapy (n = 33), with 21 events observed. Overall survival remained immature, with 11 events among 56 patients. Patients in the placebo arm who crossed over to olaparib were censored at the time of database unblinding according to Doc. S2. Censoring was primarily driven by cross‐over in the placebo group and by patients remaining alive at the data cutoff in the olaparib group. The median TTD was 16.9 months (Figure S7B). Post‐progression therapies were administered in 27 of 45 patients (60.0%) in the olaparib group and 4 of 11 patients (36.4%) in the placebo group. The distribution of subsequent treatments was broadly consistent with standard clinical practice, with platinum‐based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitor maintenance commonly used (Tables S10, 11). Exploratory analysis showed that the median PFS durations in non‐BRCAm HRD‐positive patients and the tBRCAm subgroup in the olaparib arm were 20.7 months and not reached, respectively (Figure 2E). The 24‐months PFS rate in non‐BRCAm HRD‐positive patients was 44.1%. The median PFS in the CR group (including no residual tumor by interval debulking surgery) was 30.4 months and that in the PR group was 16.6 months (Figure S7C). OS at 24 months in the placebo arm was 62.5% (Figure S7D).

Adverse Events

Adverse events (AEs) are summarized in Table 3. Among 47 patients who received at least one dose of olaparib, all experienced at least one AE of any grade. The most frequent events were nausea (55.3%), anemia (53.2%), and fatigue (36.2%). Grade ≥ 3 AEs occurred in 23 patients (48.9%), most commonly anemia (25.5%), neutropenia (12.8%), and infection (8.5%). Secondary cancers, including myelodysplasia and leukemia, were not observed in either arm. One case of breast cancer occurred in the placebo arm but not in the olaparib arm. Overall, olaparib monotherapy was well tolerated in this setting; most AEs were manageable with dose adjustments or temporary interruption, and no treatment‐related deaths were reported (Table S12).

Concordance of the Biomarker Analysis and Translational Research

We obtained BRACAnalysis data for 23 patients and myChoice CDx data for 16 patients out of 170. Both tests were approved in Japan during the clinical trial, providing certain patients with access to the CDx as an additional testing option. We evaluated concordance between WES and these CDx. Two of the seven VUS detected by WES (c.5318C > T, p.T1773I and c.80 + 2 T>C, p=) were deemed pathogenic/likely pathogenic in BRACAnalysis (Figure 3A). One VUS (c.140G > A, p.C47Y) was reclassified as likely pathogenic by myChoice CDx (Table S13). Additional details of individual mutations and classification comparisons are presented in Table S13.

A paired comparison between MSBM and myChoice CDx was available in 16 cases for binary HRD status and in 12 cases for continuous GI scores. Concordance between MSBM and myChoice CDx was 81% (13/16), with a positive percent agreement of 82% (9/11) and a negative percent agreement of 80% (4/5) (Figure 3B and Table S14). Based on myChoice CDx classification, 2 of the 10 HRD‐positive cases were MSBM‐negative, and 1 of the 6 HRD‐negative cases was MSBM‐positive. Among the three discordant cases, only one case had an available GI score (67) and showed a Signature 3 similarity score of 0.238, just below the MSBM cutoff of 0.25, whereas the remaining two cases lacked continuous GI score data, although binary HRD calls were available (Table S14). Six of 7 GI‐high cases had Signature 3 similarity scores of ≥ 0.444 (Figure 3C,D). Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was 0.63, with a p‐value of 0.017. Although concordance with myChoice CDx was a prespecified secondary endpoint, the analysis remained exploratory and was not powered for formal statistical inference.

Genomic Features by WES

Somatic mutation and copy number alteration profiles from 170 patients are summarized in Figure S8 and Tables S15 and S16. The most frequently altered gene was TP53 (84.1%), followed by MYC amplification (24.7%), and amplification of FGF3/4/19 or FGFR3 (7.7%–11.2%). CCNE1 amplification (copy number > 4.0) was observed in 7.1% of patients. Additional potentially actionable alterations included FGFR1 amplification (4.1%), KRAS mutations (4.1%), PIK3CA mutations (4.1%), and ERBB2 amplification (2.4%). Overall, the mutation landscape was consistent with previously reported genomic profiles of high‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma. Germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in either RAD51C or RAD51D were identified in two patients (1.2%), both of whom were MSBM‐positive and tBRCA wild‐type. Two additional patients had germline pathogenic variants in BLM and MRE11, the relevance of which to HRD context remains uncertain (Table S17).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the novel WES‐based biomarker that we developed, MSBM, enables robust evaluation of HRD in ovarian cancer and that olaparib maintenance monotherapy provides meaningful PFS benefit in the first‐line setting, even without bevacizumab. Although first‐line trials, such as SOLO1 and PAOLA‐1, have demonstrated the benefit of PARP‐inhibitor maintenance, the efficacy of olaparib alone in HRD‐positive patients had not been clarified, particularly for those ineligible for bevacizumab. We therefore conducted a multicenter phase II trial of olaparib monotherapy in stage IIIC/IV HGSOC with MSBM‐positive tumors following platinum therapy. At trial initiation, olaparib plus bevacizumab had not yet been approved, and olaparib monotherapy was still unapproved for HRD‐positive BRCA‐wild‐type disease. Although originally planned as a randomized, placebo‐controlled study, the trial was amended to a single‐arm design after the June 2019 approval of olaparib for gBRCAm patients [34]. Following this change, gBRCAm patients were largely excluded, yielding gBRCAm and sBRCAm rates of 8.9% and 11.1%, respectively, with only 11 placebo patients enrolled before the amendment. The study design and key findings are summarized in Figure 4.

Graphical summary of the MSBM‐OL study. The WES‐based MSBM workflow was used to classify HRD‐positive patients with stage IIIC/IV high‐grade ovarian cancer for enrollment in a phase II trial of olaparib maintenance monotherapy. Key findings include high evaluability of MSBM, concordance with myChoice CDx, robustness in sensitivity analyses, and clinically meaningful PFS benefit observed in the olaparib arm. HRD, homologous recombination deficiency; MSBM, mutational signature‐based biomarker; PFS, progression‐free survival; SNVs, Single nucleotide variants; WES, Whole‐exome sequencing.

The primary endpoint of PFS in HRD‐positive (MSBM‐positive) patients was met and remained robust under more conservative null median PFS assumptions. The observed median PFS of 22.3 months exceeded this predefined threshold and was confirmed in a one‐sample log‐rank test, demonstrating clinical efficacy without bevacizumab, which some patients cannot receive due to contraindications. This benefit was achieved despite the exclusion of gBRCAm cases, supporting olaparib monotherapy for BRCA‐wild‐type, HRD‐positive disease. Although not a direct comparison, the outcomes were similar to those reported in high‐risk HRD‐positive populations of PAOLA‐1 (olaparib + bevacizumab, median PFS 20.3 months) and PRIMA (niraparib, 19.4 months) [17, 39]. At a median follow‐up of 29.1 months, PFS2 and OS data remained immature, but 24‐month PFS and OS rates (48.6% and 78.6%) indicated durable benefit, with nearly half of patients completing two years of therapy without progression. Post‐progression therapies were heterogeneous and not protocol‐defined; therefore, their potential impact on PFS2 and OS could not be formally assessed. The unexpectedly long PFS in the small placebo group (median 16.7 months) may reflect frequent use of dose‐dense paclitaxel/carboplatin [40], interval debulking surgery, and early crossover to olaparib, so this result should be interpreted with caution. AEs, including nausea, anemia, and neutropenia, were consistent with those reported in previous olaparib studies [12, 13, 14, 34] and were manageable with standard protocols.

Tumor‐only, GI‐based HRD tests have inherent limitations. Low tumor purity can render up to 18% of cases inconclusive, and they cannot distinguish gBRCAm from sBRCAm [15, 41, 42]. To address these problems, we developed the WES‐based MSBM, which integrates three features—Signature 3 similarity, gBRCAm/sBRCAm status, and CCNE1 amplification—to exclude HR‐proficient tumors. Importantly, MSBM was evaluable in nearly all patients (99.5%), which reflects the robustness of the WES‐based platform. Although rare co‐occurrence of tBRCAm and CCNE1 amplification was observed, MSBM nonetheless provides a biologically grounded, practical framework for HRD assessment. The high MSBM positivity rate (72.4%) may reflect high HRD prevalence in Japanese ovarian cancer [7], and an inclusive design that broadly identifies HRD while excluding clear HR‐proficient cases. The marked reduction in SBS3 scores under COSMIC v3.x likely reflects fragmentation of BRCA‐associated mutational processes into multiple signatures, which may not align with the biologically distinct two‐signature structure observed in HGSOC. Accordingly, COSMIC v2 may be more suitable for MSBM‐based HRD classification in this disease context. Although the SBS3 thresholds were biologically defined, post hoc ROC analysis yielded a higher optimal cutoff (0.345 vs. > 0.25). This discrepancy primarily reflects the use of tBRCA status as a surrogate endpoint, which does not fully capture the broader HRD landscape, including non‐BRCA HRD tumors, intentionally addressed by the MSBM. The similar outcomes observed between high and intermediate SBS3 subgroups suggest that inclusion of intermediate cases does not substantially compromise specificity. We acknowledge that copy number estimation without tumor purity correction may underestimate true copy number, particularly in low‐purity samples. A lower threshold (copy number > 3) was used to mitigate this effect, although purity‐adjusted CNV calling may further refine CCNE1 amplification detection.

Although not all MSBM‐positive tumors demonstrated uniform clinical responses, our analyses support that the MSBM captures an HRD‐enriched population rather than a single homogeneous subgroup. This study was not designed to compare outcomes between biomarker‐positive and biomarker‐negative populations, and therefore does not establish the full comparative clinical utility of MSBM as a selection assay. MSBM showed some discordant cases that may reflect differences in underlying biology and assay principles. The comparison between MSBM and myChoice CDx was limited by the small number of paired samples and should be interpreted as exploratory. Larger datasets with systematically collected paired assays will be required for robust cross‐platform validation. In addition, although WES‐based HRD assessment may offer biological advantages, including broader genomic characterization and potential robustness in low‐purity samples, its current clinical role should be considered complementary rather than a replacement for established assays such as myChoice CDx.

All genetic analyses in this study were based on WES of fresh‐frozen tumors. However, recent advances have made it feasible to perform WES, and even WGS, using formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded samples, possibly broadening clinical applicability [43, 44, 45]. As sequencing costs fall and comprehensive genomic profiling becomes routine, HRD testing via WES or WGS is anticipated to enter routine precision oncology.

This study has several limitations. First, due to the transition from a randomized to a single‐arm design, the efficacy of olaparib monotherapy cannot be conclusively determined. Second, the median follow‐up of under 30 months limits evaluation of long‐term outcomes and acquired PARPi resistance. Third, the CCNE1 amplification threshold of > 3.0 copies may be too lenient given the polyploidy of HGSOC, and the low tumor cellularity in fresh‐frozen tissue could have led to an underestimation of copy number. To reduce false‐positive HRD calls, MSBM positivity was defined as a Signature 3 similarity > 0.5 regardless of CCNE1 status. The finding of two tBRCAm cases with CCNE1 copy number > 5 suggests a rare co‐occurrence and indicates that the optimal CCNE1 threshold has yet to be defined. Fourth, direct comparison of their predictive value for olaparib efficacy was not feasible, as only MSBM‐positive patients received the intervention.

In conclusion, the MSBM framework developed in this study offers a feasible and robust alternative approach to HRD assessment using WES. This trial demonstrated that olaparib maintenance monotherapy suggested a clinically meaningful PFS benefit in patients with HRD‐positive (MSBM‐positive) advanced ovarian cancer, without the need for bevacizumab. These findings may support future investigations of olaparib‐based therapy in a broader HRD‐defined population.
