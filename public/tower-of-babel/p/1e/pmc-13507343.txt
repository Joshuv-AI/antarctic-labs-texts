In situ mapping of late-stage biomechanical coordination during clathrin-mediated endocytosis
Authors: Bin Wu, Tian Luo, Yu Zhu, Xin Zhang, Dongliang Song, Yanling Lin, Yuanfang Sun, Teng-Xiang Huang, Xiaodong Cheng, Ning Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) requires precise coordination between membrane deformation and force-generating protein assemblies, yet how these forces are dynamically organized in living cells remains poorly defined. Using multi-dimensional single particle tracking (SPT), we indirectly visualize the mechanical motions of individual clathrin-coated pits during the late stages of CME. We uncover a temporally ordered sequence of rotational behaviors that reflect distinct modes of membrane remodeling preceding vesicle scission. While dynamin-dependent in-plane twisting is a common feature of productive CME events, an additional out-of-plane rotational deformation, hereafter referred to as a “swing” motion, is selectively observed at a subset of endocytic sites that recruit actin. This mechanical heterogeneity correlates with differences in membrane deformation and scission efficiency, rather than representing an obligatory step in CME. By capturing these force-generating transitions in situ and under physiological conditions, our work provides a dynamic, biophysical framework for understanding how endocytic protein machines remodel membranes in living cells.

Introduction

Clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) is a fundamental cellular process responsible for the internalization of a wide range of cargos and plays essential roles in nutrient uptake, signal transduction, and membrane homeostasis1–5. Over the past decades, extensive work has elucidated the molecular components and assembly dynamics of the endocytic machinery6,7. However, beyond molecular recruitment and disassembly, CME is inherently a mechanical process that requires precise coordination of force-generating protein machines to deform, constrict, and sever the membrane. How these forces are dynamically organized and executed in living cells remains incompletely understood.

In the final stages of CME, clathrin-coated pits (CCPs) must undergo a series of energetically unfavorable transitions, including membrane invagination, neck constriction, and vesicle scission. These steps are thought to be driven by the collective action of membrane curvature, protein scaffolding, and active force generation by molecular machines such as dynamin and the actin cytoskeleton8–14. In mammalian cells, actin recruitment to CME sites is heterogeneous and often spatially asymmetric, in contrast to the symmetric actin organization observed in yeast, and such asymmetry has been shown to contribute to directional pit closure15–20. Dynamin represents a second major force-generating protein machine at late-stage CME, assembling at the vesicle neck to drive membrane constriction and scission21,22. Although actin and dynamin exhibit partially overlapping recruitment dynamics23, how these two protein machines are mechanically coordinated remains poorly understood, largely because existing approaches infer force generation from protein localization or lifetime measurements rather than directly monitoring the mechanical behavior of CCPs themselves in living cells. As a result, the actual mechanical motions of CCPs during these critical late-stage transitions have remained largely uncharacterized in situ.

To overcome this limitation, we employed anisotropic nanoparticles as endocytic cargos and used SPT based on differential interference contrast (DIC) and dark-field (DF) microscopy to indirectly monitor the orientation and rotational dynamics of individual clathrin-coated pits. By tracking these motions in real time, SPT captures the mechanical transitions of the endocytic membrane itself, rather than relying solely on molecular localization. This approach enables reconstruction of a dynamic sequence of force-generating events during CME under physiological conditions24–27.

Using this strategy, we reveal that late-stage CME is not mechanically uniform. While dynamin-mediated twisting represents a common scission-associated motion, additional rotational deformations are selectively engaged at a subset of endocytic sites. These observations support a model in which multiple mechanical pathways can lead to vesicle scission, with cytoskeletal forces acting as context-dependent modulators rather than obligatory components.

Results

Swing and Twist: two fundamental rotational modes of CCPs

To visualize the real-time dynamics of CCPs during CME, we employed transferrin-modified gold nanorods (AuNRs@TRF) as cargos (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). These AuNRs with dimensions of 40 nm × 80 nm, are comparable to the size of CCPs (70–150 nm)28. We validated that AuNRs@TRF are internalized via the CME pathway27,29–32, as confirmed by their co-localization with both clathrin light chain A and dynamin 2 in a gene-edited SK-MEL-2 cell line (hCLTAEN/hDNM2EN, Supplementary Fig. 1d)33, as well as by pharmacological perturbation experiments showing that chlorpromazine (CPZ), a classical inhibitor of CME, significantly reduced AuNRs@TRF uptake, whereas methyl-β-cyclodextrin (mβCD), an inhibitor of caveolae/lipid raft–related endocytosis, had no significant effect (Supplementary Fig. 1e). In addition, under the incubation and imaging conditions used in this study, neither AuNR treatment alone nor AuNR treatment combined with illumination caused an obvious reduction in cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 1f), supporting that the imaging conditions did not induce detectable global toxicity over the tested time window. Due to the specific ligand-receptor binding and subsequent involvement of the CME machinery, the nanorod cargos become effectively immobilized within the nascent pit, ensuring their motion indirectly reflects the dynamics of the CCP34.

To characterize these rotational modes, we used Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) to quantify the correlation between the bright and dark intensities of the AuNRs in DIC microscopy (as detailed in the Supplementary Notes). This analysis revealed that the value of r directly correlates with the rotational mode of the particle, with the r value approaches +1 for a predominant in-plane rotation or −1 for an out-of-plane rotation35. Based on this criterion, we defined two distinct rotational patterns: a “swing” motion, corresponding to an out-of-plane rotation and characterized by a negative correlation between the bright and dark DIC intensities (e.g., r = −0.45; Fig. 1a–c), and a “twist” motion, corresponding to an in-plane rotation and characterized by a positive correlation (e.g., r = 0.84; Fig. 1d, e). To directly verify that these rotational signatures indeed occur at clathrin-coated pits, we performed complementary measurements in gene-edited SK-MEL-2 cells expressing CLTA and DNM2. Actin-independent and -dependent rotational features in the final stage of CME

To investigate the distinct mechanical events of CME, we generated a genome-edited A375 cell line using CRISPR/Cas9 technology that co-expresses N-terminal genomic fusions of Tag-RFP to the β-actin gene and C-terminal genomic fusions of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) to the dynamin2 gene (hACTBEN/hDNM2EN) (Supplementary Fig. 3a–e). Control experiments showed that this endogenous ACTB tagging did not detectably alter overall ACTB expression or transferrin uptake efficiency, indicating that CME dynamics were not significantly perturbed by the gene-editing strategy (Supplementary Fig. 3f–h). We then employed total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (TIRFM) to distinguish individual CME events as either actin-negative (-actin) or actin-positive (+actin) (Supplementary Fig. 4a–f)13,15. While previous studies have shown that the movement of CCPs could indirectly reflect protein activity, direct quantification of the mechanical forces generated by actin during membrane invagination and constriction remains elusive. Our approach bypasses this limitation by providing indirect, yet powerful, evidence of these forces through an analysis of rotational dynamics.

Stage I: Static period (both in -actin and +actin events)

This stage is characterized by minimal translational and rotational motions of the AuNRs (stage I in Figs. 2b and 3b). During this interval, ACTB and/or DNM2 fluorescence signals typically increase from the baseline. Consistent with the general progression of CME protein recruitment reported in prior studies6,20,36–38, AuNR trajectories remained largely confined with only slow fluctuations and negligible net displacements, indicating a stable, confined state of the endocytic sites. Note that Time 0 denotes the start of the recorded time-lapse for each event (start of acquisition), not the start of protein recruitment. In some events, the fluorescence signal was already present at t = 0, indicating recruitment began before acquisition, these events were included because the late-stage transitions were still fully captured.

Stage Ⅱ: Swing motion (only in +actin events)

This stage is defined by a significant out-of-plane rotation of the particle, termed the “swing” motion. Actin recruitment peaked around 26 s, coinciding with the particle’s motion from 24.52 s to 26.46 s, with a calculated correlation coefficient r = −0.23 (Stage Ⅱ in Fig. 3b). We interpret the swing phase as reflecting a late-stage regime in which the cargo is mechanically constrained at the endocytic site and experiences a measurable out-of-plane rotational component that coincides with peak actin recruitment. Stage III: Twist motion (both in -actin and +actin events)

Dynamin2 recruitment peaked around 14 s and 26 s, respectively, for the two event types (stage Ⅲ in Figs. 2b and 3b). This correlated with a noticeable in-plane rotational “twist” of the particle from 15.56 s to 16 s (for the -actin event) and 26.48 s to 26.62 s (for the +actin event). The calculated correlation coefficients for these motions were r = 0.9 and r = 0.95, respectively. This behavior aligns with our previously reported “super twist” mechanism27: a short, burst-like dynamin-associated torsional characteristic immediately preceding vesicle release/scission.

Stage IV: Constrained rotational motion (only in +actin events)

At this stage, the CCP remains partially constrained, exhibiting slow, irregular rotational movements accompanied by slight displacement fluctuations from 26.64 s to 35.18 s (stage Ⅳ in Fig. 3b). This interval coincides with a decline of the ACTB fluorescence signal from its peak level. We interpret this behavior as a post-peak, partially constrained regime in which the endocytic structure has not yet fully transitioned to free diffusion. This interpretation is further supported by population-level analysis showing that, in +actin events, the time interval between super twist and complete vesicle detachment is markedly prolonged compared with −actin events (Supplementary Fig. 8b), and that post-twist motion is significantly more restricted, with a lower diffusion coefficient and a smaller power-law exponent α (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Moreover, by the time of complete detachment, actin disassembly had progressed to 57% ± 16.9% in +actin events (Supplementary Fig. 8a), suggesting that residual actin assembly persists even as vesicle release is being completed. Together, these results support the idea that residual cytoskeletal or membrane-associated constraints, potentially including remaining actin structures, transiently limit the motion of the newly formed vesicle before full release39.

Stage Ⅴ: Completely detached stage (both in -actin and +actin events)

This final stage is characterized by the detached CCP gaining increased freedom of movement and displaying rapid, irregular rotational motion (stage Ⅴ in Figs. 2b and 3b). This is consistent with the CCP fully detaching and diffusing into the cytoplasm, a process that frees it from membrane constraints. Specifically, in the −actin event, detachment happens at 15.2 s and occurs nearly concurrently with the “super twist,” which begins at 15.56 s. In the +actin event, the CCP detaches at 35.18 s, following the “super twist” at 26.62 s. We interpret this regime as being consistent with release from strong membrane-associated confinement and subsequent motion away from the original site. During this stage, the cargo often diffuses rapidly and may exhibit out-of-plane (z) motion, which can transiently reduce tracking robustness and weaken DIC contrast, making the DIC traces appear noisier than in the confined pre-scission stages. Trajectory segments after release can appear step-like because the cargo alternates between confined and more mobile regimes; post-release tracking can be less stable due to rapid diffusion and occasional out-of-plane motion. The concurrent decay of ACTB/DNM2 fluorescence is consistent with post-scission departure/disassembly.

That swing precedes super-twist is actin-dependent

Using SPT of CME events acquired under physiological conditions, we analyzed 51 datasets that met predefined inclusion criteria (see Supplementary Notes) and classified them into 17 +actin and 34 –actin events based on ROI-based ACTB recruitment criteria (Figs. 2, 3, Supplementary Movies 1, 2 and Supplementary Fig. 7). Our statistical analysis revealed that in most +actin events, particles consistently underwent a pronounced out-of-plane rotational “swing” motion preceding the onset of the “super-twist” phase (Fig. 4a). Quantitative analysis showed this “swing” phase lasted approximately 1 – 3 s (Fig. 4b) and consistently occurred immediately prior to the “super-twist” phase (Fig. 4c). Time-lapse TIRF imaging demonstrated that the ACTB fluorescence peak typically preceded the dynamin2 peak by ~1.3 s (Fig. 4d), consistent with our SPT data. On average, the “swing” occurred within 1.4 s around the ACTB fluorescence peak (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, using the ACTB fluorescence intensity as a proxy for actin assembly, we found that particles began to “swing” when the ACTB signal reached approximately 82.7% of its maximum intensity (Fig. 4f), highlighting a strong correlation between actin polymerization and rotational behavior. These findings collectively indicate that in actin-positive CME events, actin polymerization precedes and likely facilitates the onset of dynamin2-mediated scission (Supplementary Fig. 9a).

To quantitatively characterize the mechanical forces underlying the observed “swing” motion, we employed a DF-based SPT, as previously described (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11)27. Using SK-MEL-2 cells, which exhibit substantially lower background scattering than A375 cells, we quantified the angular amplitude of this motion. In total, we identified 10 similar events with measured polar angle shifts ranging from 26° to 78° (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Importantly, this quantitative angular information provides further support for a model in which actin assembly exerts directional force on CCPs, thereby tilting the vesicle out of plane before dynamin2-driven constriction. The expected precision of angular retrieval should be interpreted in the context of data quality, as discussed in our recent methodological work40. The observation that “swing” precedes the peak of dynamin2 recruitment across multiple imaging modalities suggests that actin-mediated rotational torque may serve as a preparatory mechanical step, priming the vesicle for efficient scission (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 13b)13,15.

The polarized enrichment of actin around CCPs, further confirmed by 3D-Airyscan imaging (Fig. 5b, c), is consistent with prior live-cell imaging observations15. However, it is important to note that not all +actin events display lateral displacement or canonical swing behavior. In a subset of events, actin exhibited symmetric distribution patterns around the CCP (Supplementary Fig. 14a), and in these cases, the characteristic “swing” was occasionally reduced or not readily detectable (Supplementary Figs. 13a and 14b). The local membrane tension, curvature, or protein crowding at the endocytic site may modulate the geometry of actin nucleation, possibly through curvature-sensing proteins such as F-BAR or N-BAR domain-containing scaffolds20,41. This structural plasticity allows cells to fine-tune vesicle scission mechanics in response to local mechanical cues or specific endocytic demands, which may be critical for maintaining the robustness of CME across diverse cellular contexts.

We next examined whether actin-driven asymmetric constriction of the membrane affects the efficiency of dynamin-mediated scission. In SK-MEL-2 cells, CCPs showing prior asymmetric membrane constriction (+asym) completed the subsequent “super-twist” phase significantly faster than those without (−asym) (Fig. 5h). Moreover, +asym events required a smaller total twist angle to achieve scission (Fig. 5i), suggesting that actin-mediated preconditioning of the vesicle neck reduces the mechanical work needed for dynamin-driven constriction.

At the level of individual endocytic events, multidimensional SPT showed that CK666-treated cells no longer displayed the characteristic swing phase prior to constriction. Instead, the tracked AuNR underwent only a slower twist-like rotational event without a preceding anti-correlated swing signature (Fig. 6d–f and Supplementary Fig. 16). Notably, this slow twist-like motion was not followed by an obvious increase in displacement, and the subsequent rotational behavior remained relatively quiescent, lacking the characteristic post-scission features normally seen after vesicle release. This pattern is consistent with late-stage stalling of the CME event before productive scission. These results indicate that the pre-twist swing motion is actin-dependent and that loss of actin polymerization compromises efficient progression through late-stage CME13,15.

We next examined whether elevated membrane tension alters the balance between actin-negative and actin-positive CME. Under hypotonic treatment, the fraction of DNM2 puncta co-localized with ACTB increased substantially compared with isotonic conditions (Fig. 7a, b), indicating enhanced actin recruitment at endocytic sites. Consistent with this shift, CLTA and DNM2 lifetimes were prolonged under hypotonic conditions (Fig. 7c), suggesting slower progression of CME14. Moreover, single-particle tracking revealed that hypotonic treatment markedly extended individual trajectories, particularly Stage I and Stage II, before the final rotational transition and release (Supplementary Fig. 17). Discussion

Over the past 25 years, the role of actin in clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) has been intensively studied, with recruitment frequently occurring at late stages of pit maturation9,39,42,43. Membrane tension is a major determinant of this recruitment: elevated tension enhances actin assembly at a subset of CME sites44 and slows endocytic progression14,45. Our data are consistent with this view. Under hypotonic conditions, which increase membrane tension, the fraction of ACTB-positive CME events increased markedly, accompanied by prolonged CLTA and DNM2 lifetimes and substantially extended individual SPT trajectories, particularly during the confined Stage I and Stage II intervals (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 17). These observations support the idea that actin acts as a load-adaptive mechanical responder that is increasingly engaged when membrane deformation becomes more demanding.

Actin involvement in CME is heterogeneous across cell types and even across events within the same cell type13,15. In our SPT dataset, ~33% of events were classified as +actin, consistent with the fraction measured by TIRF under isotonic conditions. Within this heterogeneous framework, our study identifies a distinct out-of-plane rotational behavior, termed “swing”, that is selectively associated with actin-positive CME events and occurs around the peak of ACTB recruitment. The asymmetric and dynamic distribution of actin around CCPs suggests that swing reflects a localized mechanical contribution of branched actin assembly rather than a generic consequence of increased actin mass (Fig. 5d–f). Notably, when actin appeared more symmetrically distributed around the pit, the canonical swing signature was weakened or not readily detectable, further supporting the idea that directional asymmetry, rather than total actin signal alone, is critical for generating a pronounced out-of-plane rotational component (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). Thus, swing likely reflects an actin-associated asymmetric force regime that mechanically biases the pit before scission.

The perturbation experiments further strengthen this interpretation. Inhibition of branched actin polymerization by CK666 reduced actin-positive CME events, impaired transferrin and AuNR uptake, and abolished the obvious pre-twist swing signature in representative SPT trajectories, leaving only a slower twist-like transition (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 16). Conversely, under hypotonic treatment, increased membrane tension enhanced actin recruitment and prolonged the confined pre-scission phases before late rotational transition and release (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 17). Together, these observations support a load-adaptive model in which actin polymerization is specifically required for the swing-associated pathway and becomes increasingly important when CME proceeds under higher mechanical resistance.

In addition to swing, we observed a rapid in-plane rotational event, “super-twist”, that coincides with peak dynamin2 recruitment and is present in both +actin and –actin events. This burst-like in-plane rotation is consistent with rapid late-stage neck remodeling associated with dynamin activity, and prior perturbation in the same AuNR tracking framework showed that the super-twist signature is abolished and internalization is strongly reduced when dynamin GTPase activity is inhibited (DNM2K44A)27. High-speed DIC imaging revealed that this “super-twist” is completed extremely rapidly—within 0.21 ± 0.1 s on average. Interestingly, although fewer +actin events were recorded than -actin events, the super twist in +actin events (0.17 ± 0.05 s) were faster than in -actin events (0.24 ± 0.11 s) (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This trend was further supported by DF imaging data: in events where actin-driven rotational deformation had occurred (+swing events), the super-twist was completed more efficiently, requiring a smaller azimuthal angle change (88 ± 38°) in a shorter time (0.13 ± 0.04 s) compared to -swing events, which required a larger azimuthal angle shift (182 ± 67°) and longer duration (0.37 ± 0.12 s) to complete the super-twist (Fig. 5h, i). These results collectively suggest that actin polymerization facilitates more efficient dynamin-mediated constriction, enabling the super-twist to be executed with a smaller rotational amplitude and within a shorter duration. We propose two potential mechanisms for this enhancement. First, actin may play a role in promoting the robust and timely recruitment of dynamin2 to the vesicle neck, thereby accelerating the self-assembly of dynamin into helical structures13,46. Second, the asymmetric deformation from the preceding actin-driven “swing” may precondition the membrane neck, reducing the mechanical resistance or energy barrier for subsequent dynamin-mediated constriction and scission47.

Our results therefore support a model in which late-stage CME in mammalian cells can proceed through at least two related mechanical routes (Fig. 8). In one route, vesicles undergo dynamin-dominated super-twist and detach with minimal preceding out-of-plane deformation. In the other, asymmetric actin assembly first generates a swing-like rotational bias, partially remodels or preconditions the endocytic structure, and is followed by a faster and mechanically facilitated dynamin-dependent constriction. Elevated membrane tension appears to shift events toward this actin-assisted route, whereas inhibition of actin polymerization suppresses it. Rather than representing an obligatory universal intermediate, swing may therefore reflect a context-dependent mechanical solution that cells deploy when local resistance to vesicle formation is high.

Several limitations should also be noted. Our mechanical readout is indirect and inferred from cargo rotation rather than direct force measurement. In addition, the ACTB reporter does not distinguish F-actin from G-actin, and fluorescence imaging has lower temporal resolution than DIC acquisition. Another limitation is that the rotational signatures were quantified using transferrin receptor–targeted AuNR cargos (40 × 80 nm); cargo size and geometry could, in principle, influence pit dynamics and/or actin recruitment. Extending this framework to additional cargo sizes, geometries, and receptor systems will therefore be an important direction for future work. Despite these limitations, tracking the rotational motions of individual CCP-associated cargos in living cells provides a unique window into the biomechanical coordination of endocytic protein machines in situ.

Experimental model and subject details

Cell lines

All cell lines used in this study are derivatives of commercially available SK-MEL-2 cells and A375 cells. These are human melanoma cells. SK-MEL-2 cells (hCLTAEN/hDNM2EN) are kindly provided by Drubin (UC Berkeley). Constructions of A375(hACTBEN/hDNM2EN) and culture conditions are described in detail below.

Cell line authentication

Gene-edited cells were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Methods

Multi-dimensional imaging system

The multi-dimensional imaging system was built around a Nikon Eclipse Ni-E upright microscope equipped with a temperature-controlled stage (Bioscience Tools). For dark-field imaging, a Nikon oil-immersion dark-field condenser (NA 1.20–1.43) was combined with a 60× CFI Plan Fluor oil-immersion objective (NA 0.5–1.25, fixed at 1.0). Scattering signals from AuNRs were isolated using a bandpass filter (FBH650-40, Thorlabs). Because AuNR cargos were expected to enter from the upper (non-basal) cell surface rather than the coverslip-attached basal surface, imaging was performed on an upright microscope. In this geometry, highly inclined laminated optical sheet (HILO) illumination was used to achieve shallow optical sectioning at the apical cell surface, reducing background fluorescence and photobleaching while enabling near-surface imaging of endocytic events. Spectral registration under the HILO imaging condition was calibrated using multicolor fluorescent microspheres acquired under the same optical configuration as the experimental data. Excitation of EGFP (488 nm) and TagRFP (561 nm) was achieved using collimated lasers (Oxxius). Emission signals were cleaned with notch filters (ZET488NF and ZET561NF, Chroma) and further separated from scattering signals with a long-pass dichroic mirror (BLP01-635R, Semrock). The dark-field signal was split by a beam splitter (BSS10R, Thorlabs) into an in-focus channel (30% intensity) for positional information and a defocused channel (70% intensity) for angular analysis. Fluorescence signals were recorded with a Tucsen Aries 16 camera, while dark-field signals were detected with a Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion BT camera27.

For DIC imaging, a complete Nomarski DIC optical system (two Nomarski prisms, two polarizers, and a quarter-wave plate) was used31. Samples were illuminated with a halogen lamp through a Nikon oil-immersion condenser (NA 1.40), and optical signals were collected using a 100× Apo TIRF oil-immersion objective (NA 1.49). DIC images at specific wavelengths were obtained by inserting the corresponding bandpass filter (FBH650-40, Thorlabs) into the light path. The fluorescence optical path was identical to that used for dark-field imaging.

All DIC and DF videos were recorded at 50 frames per second, whereas fluorescence images were acquired with a 200 ms exposure and a 2 s delay between frames. The nominal output powers of the laser modules were 5 mW (561 nm) and 15 mW (488 nm). Under the HILO configuration used for imaging, the typical total excitation powers measured immediately below the objective were 5.14 μW for 561 nm excitation and 5.68 μW for 488 nm excitation. A mechanical shutter was used to trigger and synchronize image acquisition across both cameras. Acquired images were processed using ImageJ and MATLAB.

Functionalization and characterization of gold nanorods

Gold nanorods (40 nm × 80 nm) were purchased from Nanopartz (A12-40-650-Citrate). We used AuNRs of this size because their optical anisotropy enables orientation readout in DIC/DF tracking, while this size provides a practical balance between sufficient scattering contrast for robust tracking in live cells and compatibility with CCP-scale endocytic structures. Surface functionalization with transferrin (11096-37-0, Sigma-Aldrich) was performed following the published protocols27. Briefly, 4 μL of 50 mM borate buffer (pH 8.5) and 5 μL of transferrin solution (3 mg mL−1) were added to 100 μL of the gold nanorod suspension, followed by gentle mixing and incubation at room temperature for more than 3 hours. The mixture was centrifuged at 2348 × g for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the process was repeated twice. Finally, the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of 2 mM borate buffer (pH 8.5).

For negative staining, the nanorods were incubated with 1.6% phosphotungstic acid for 30 s and subsequently examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to evaluate surface modification. Zeta potentials of gold nanorods before and after transferrin conjugation were measured with a Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical).

Cell culture

Human malignant melanoma A375 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco). Human malignant melanoma SK-MEL-2 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (11320-033, Gibco). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10091-148, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; 15140-122, Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

Transfection of A375 cells to express CLTA-EBFP2

The pCMV-CLTA-EBFP2 plasmid was obtained from Miaoling Biotech. A375 cells were seeded into glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis, Cat# D35-20-1.5-N) and cultured to ~80% confluency. Cells were then transfected with 0.5 μg of plasmid DNA using PEI (K1029, APExBIO). After 48 hours of incubation, the cells were imaged under a microscope.

Hypo-osmotic media treatment

Isotonic imaging media contained DMEM (C11995500BT, Gibco) with 5%vol/vol FBS (10091-148, Gibco). The media was diluted with an inorganic salt solution containing 10 mM CaCl2, 0.3 mM MgCl2 and 0.1 mM MgSO4 (CMM) to maintain concentrations of critical ions. The 150 mOsm hypotonic imaging media contained 1:1 vol/vol CMM solution in DMEM. Five percent vol/vol FBS was present in all hypotonic solutions14.

CK666 treatment

For the CK666 treatment experiments, a 10 mM CK666 (SparkJade, SJ-MX1566) stock solution was prepared in DMSO and kept at −80 °C. Live cell imaging was performed 5 min after adding an equal volume of medium containing CK666 or DMSO into the imaging chamber to achieve a final concentration of 100 μM CK666 or 2% DMSO15.

Transferrin uptake assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates in advance to achieve 60–80% confluency on the day of the experiment. Alexa Fluor 647-labeled human transferrin (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 009-600-050) was added to the cells at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Cells were then washed with ice-cold PBS and detached by incubation with 0.25% trypsin/EDTA for 1 min. After centrifugation, cells were subjected to an acid wash with ice-cold buffer (pH 5.0) to remove surface-bound ligand, washed again, and resuspended in PBS. Fluorescence intensity was subsequently analyzed using a Quanteon flow cytometer (ACEA Biosciences).

AuNRs@TRF internalization assay

Cells were seeded in culture dishes in advance to reach approximately 80% confluency on the day of the experiment. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing either chlorpromazine (CPZ; final concentration, 25 μM), a classical inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, or methyl-β-cyclodextrin (mβCD; final concentration, 150 μM), which depletes plasma membrane cholesterol and is commonly used to inhibit caveolae-mediated endocytosis. Cells were preincubated with the inhibitors at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of transferrin-functionalized AuNRs to a final concentration of 3.7 × 109 mL−1. After 2 hours of incubation, AuNR uptake was examined by DIC microscopy at 650 nm, and internalized AuNRs were identified and quantified27.

SIM-TIRF live-cell imaging

Cells were seeded into glass-bottom dishes (Cellvis, Cat# D35-20-1.5-N) two days prior to imaging. Live-cell imaging was performed using a multi-modal super-resolution microscope equipped with TIRF optics (SIM-ultimate, CSR Biotech) provided by Guangzhou CSR Biotech Co. Ltd. In the HIS-SIM system, a top-hat beam shaper was used to transform collimated Gaussian beams into collimated top-hat beams, thereby providing more uniform illumination across the field of view. A live-cell incubation system maintained the culture environment at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Images were acquired using a 100×/1.5 NA oil immersion objective (Olympus). Channels were acquired sequentially at a 2 s interval and 200 ms exposure time over 5 min. Events were extracted from cmeAnalysis48 and processed in MATLAB.

SIM images were collected and analyzed as described previously49. Sparse deconvolution was carried out to further improve the image quality50. Specifically, Wiener reconstruction was applied with the following parameters: defocus elimination (level 2), image denoising (level 2), and artifact removal (level 2). Subsequently, the images were further processed using the general mode with level 3 sparse deconvolution.

For SIM-TIRF multicolor imaging, spectral registration was calibrated using four-color fluorescent microspheres acquired under the same imaging and deconvolution conditions as the experimental data. For ACTB–DNM2 colocalization analysis, background was first corrected using rolling-ball subtraction (radius = 50 pixels). The ACTB and DNM2 channels were then thresholded independently using Otsu’s method. This procedure was used to minimize bias arising from differences in background level, signal distribution, and contrast between the two channels before colocalization analysis (see Supplementary Fig. 15 for spectral registration calibration and spatial-randomization control).

Airyscan imaging

Sample preparation for live-cell imaging followed the procedure described in the “SIM-TIRF live-cell imaging” section. Cells were imaged under a controlled environment of 37 °C and 5% CO₂ using a Zeiss LSM 980 inverted microscope equipped with an Airyscan2 detector. Images were acquired and processed using the ZEN 3.8 system. Channels were acquired sequentially for each line with 0.2 s/frame intervals over 4 min. Images were processed using the Airyscan module integrated within ZEN 3.8.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software and MATLAB code, and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were independently performed at least three times. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an unpaired two-tailed t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.
