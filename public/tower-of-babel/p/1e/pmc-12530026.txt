A Thermoreversible Gelation Pathway to Mesoporous Aerogel Monoliths Based on Poly(methyl methacrylate) Stereocomplex Crystals
Authors: Saadman Sakib Rahman, Anthony V Tuccitto, Zeineb Ben Rejeb, Patrick C Lee, Chul B Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn pursuit of lightweight, functional thermoplastic materials, novel monolithic aerogels composed entirely of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) and its stereocomplex crystals are reported. Herein, PMMA aerogels are fabricated using a one‐pot thermoreversible gelation procedure, coupled with supercritical CO2 (scCO2) drying. These aerogels exhibit either mixed macro‐ and mesoporous, or exclusively mesoporous character, with low densities (0.03–0.4 g cm−3) and high porosities (97.3–66.6%), depending on the concentration of PMMA used in sol–gel processing. These aerogels also exhibit among the highest melting temperature and enthalpy reported for PMMA stereocomplex crystals, a consequence of scCO2 drying that promotes chain reorganization processes. Despite the characteristically brittle nature of PMMA and aerogels with similar densities, these aerogels exhibit an unusually remarkable balance between stiffness, strength, and toughness that arises from their nanometric‐crystalline‐ligament‐containing structure. These monoliths are also superoleophilic (sorption capacity: 2.9–29.8 g g−1), are well‐suited for oil‐water separation, and are effective thermal insulators (k: 24–65 mW m−1 K−1). Importantly, these aerogels can be easily recycled via chemical recycling and melt‐processing. These findings provide the foundation for future fabrication of recyclable thermogel derivatives, and reveal process–structure–property relationships for stereocomplex crystal‐based monoliths.Keywords: fringed‐micelle, lamella, reticulated structure, sol–gel, supercritical CO2 , thermogel

Introduction

Thermoreversible gelation is a powerful sol–gel assembly approach that yields micro‐ or nanostructured gels, called “thermogels,” through the simple thermal treatment of homogeneous (one‐phase) polymer–solvent mixtures. Many solvent–polymer systems have been reported to form thermogels, including poly(l‐lactide) in N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF),[ 1 ] polycaprolactone (PCL) in DMF,[ 2 ] nylon 11 in formic acid,[ 3 ] poly(vinylidene fluoride) in diethyl adipate,[ 4 ] among others.[ 5 ] While the mechanism inducing such supramolecular gelation depends on the solvent–polymer combination as well as the applied thermal treatment, mechanisms inducing polymer–solvent complexes,[ 4 ] including guest–host complexes (i.e., clathrates)[ 6 , 7 , 8 ] can be harnessed to construct a desired micro‐ or nanostructure. Indeed, the dependence of the gelation mechanism on both the composition and interactions between material constituents yields sol–gels with highly variable internal structures that encompass particulate and/or reticulated structures, depending on the thermodynamic gelation pathway applied. As a result, thermogels can oftentimes be precursors for high‐value, multifunctional materials including ionic electrolyte gels,[ 9 , 10 ] hydrogels,[ 11 ] xerogels,[ 12 ] and aerogels.[ 13 ]

In recent decades, aerogels specifically have found use in applications ranging from water remediation[ 14 , 15 ] to infrared thermal stealth,[ 16 ] owing to attributes including high internal surface area (typically 100–1000 m2 g−1) and porosity (typically 90–99% void), which impart unique bulk properties to monoliths. More recently, polymer‐based aerogels have found growing use, offering flexibility and the potential recyclability that cannot be achieved easily with silica and carbon‐based aerogels. Hence, thermoreversible gelation has become an increasingly important gelation pathway in the fabrication of polymer aerogels and is most commonly applied to semicrystalline polymers owing to their propensity to self‐aggregate via crystallization.

Thermodynamic differences among solvent–polymer characteristics, as well as processing‐related parameters (e.g., polymer concentration, thermal treatment parameters) yield potential in achieving intriguing micro‐ or nanostructures and bulk properties. For example, Talley et al.[ 17 ] fabricated poly(ether ether ketone) (PEEK)‐based porous monoliths in dichloroacetic acid, followed by solvent exchange with water and freeze‐drying. Here, the authors obtained mechanically robust monoliths with densities ranging from 0.1 to 0.26 g cm−3, owing to the formation of a uniform network of globular crystals (≈200 nm in diameter), interconnected via fibrillar structures. By contrast, Spiering et al.[ 18 ] fabricated PEEK‐based monoliths, but using 1,3‐diphenylactone as a solvent. Despite achieving a similar range of densities to Talley et al. (0.09–0.25 g cm−3), these aerogels exhibited a morphology comprised of strut‐like crystalline lamellar aggregates called axialites (strut thickness ≈400–500 nm, strut length ≈2–3.5 µm); these morphological and topological variations were linked to the selection of the gelation solvent, and its role on crystal nucleation density. Beyond PEEK, Krishnan et al.[ 19 ] developed PLA‐based monoliths via a thermoreversible gelation method in DMF, followed by freeze‐drying. The resulting microstructure varied depending on whether additional thermal treatments (e.g., annealing) were applied during sol–gel processing. These variations stemmed from changes in gel crystal polymorphism (i.e., homocrystal vs stereocomplex crystal) due to crystallization‐related processes occurring during sol–gel treatment.

Although various polymers have been employed to fabricate aerogels, to our knowledge, there are no prior reports of aerogel monoliths composed solely of PMMA (and its stereocomplex crystals), a thermoplastic polymer which is widely recognized for its excellent processability, recyclability, weather resistance, optical transparency, and moderate stiffness. While PMMA aerogel fibers have previously been prepared via electrospinning, this fabrication pathway necessitated the addition of additives (i.e., carbon black and a water‐based fluoropolymer) to attain aerogel fibers,[ 20 ] with this approach limited to fabrication of fibers. Nevertheless, there have been a considerable number of accounts of PMMA sol–gel systems based on stereoregular isotactic (iPMMA) and syndiotactic PMMA (sPMMA).[ 21 ] While these studies report gelation of iPMMA in solvents including 2‐butanone,[ 22 ] butyl acetate,[ 23 ] and o‐xylene[ 24 ] and gelation of sPMMA in solvents including o‐xylene,[ 21 ] toluene,[ 25 ] and bromobenzene,[ 26 ] the thermal stability of these gels (and their derivatives) is rather limited.

Fortunately, depending on the material purity (i.e., tacticity), the ratio of iPMMA and sPMMA, as well as mixing conditions (e.g., temperature, pressure, screw speed, solvent type, etc.), PMMA can form stereocomplex crystals via mixing of iPMMA and sPMMA. There has been much debate in literature regarding the structure of the PMMA stereocomplex, with some claiming it to possess a double helix structure somewhat analogous to DNA,[ 27 , 28 ] with more recent claims of a triple helix structure[ 29 , 30 , 31 ] via approaches like atomic force microscopy analysis, analogous to triple‐stranded helical biopolymers like collagen.[ 32 ] Specifically, it is believed that a double helix of two iPMMA chains is encapsulated in a sPMMA helix[ 30 ] via van der Waals interactions.[ 33 ] This intricate structure makes thermally reversible gelation of iPMMA/sPMMA a fascinating assembly pathway for fabricating aerogels. There have been a handful of accounts of gelation of iPMMA/sPMMA blends in so‐called strong‐complexing solvents (i.e., solvents that promote stereocomplex formation), including DMF, acetone, acetonitrile, and tetrahydrofuran, among others.[ 34 , 35 ] While the previous decades have focused on clarifying the morphological and topological interactions between iPMMA and sPMMA in stereocomplex helices,[ 30 , 36 , 37 ] very few studies have focused on exploiting these phenomena from an application perspective. Besides a few studies investigating this phenomenon in the context of polymer foaming,[ 38 ] and in the fabrication of gel electrolytes for battery applications,[ 39 ] there is still much untapped potential using this unique gelation pathway in the fabrication of micro‐ or nanostructured materials for advanced applications, like highly porous, multifunctional aerogels. We posit that this underexplored technology is in fact an exciting means of fabricating multifunctional materials.

In this work, we develop a novel monolithic aerogel system composed only of PMMA and its stereocomplex crystals, using a simple thermoreversible gelation process. Building on previous literature,[ 38 , 40 , 41 ] we identify a one‐pot, thermoreversible gelation pathway which yields gels over accessible temperature conditions, and without the need for curing agents. Via calorimetric analysis, we confirm that thermogel formation is in fact a crystallization‐related phenomenon and is thermally reversible. The thermogels were transformed into aerogels through supercritical CO2 (scCO2) drying. The microstructure, thermal transitions and degradation temperatures, mechanical properties, thermal insulation performance, and oil–water separation capabilities of the aerogels were investigated. These findings provide valuable insights for the future design of both thermogel and aerogel systems and contribute to the development of process–structure–property relationships.

Experimental Section

Materials

Here, iPMMA (95%, M w = 300 000 g mol−1) and sPMMA (85%, M w = 50 000 g mol−1) were purchased from Scientific Polymer Products.[ 42 ] DMF (99.8% purity) was obtained from Fisher Chemical, and ethanol (anhydrous, ρ ≈ 0.785 g mL−1) was purchased from Greenfield Global. CO2 (99.8% purity) was supplied by Messer Canada Inc. Methylene blue hydrate (≥97.0% purity) and Oil Red O (≥75% purity) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich. Canola oil (100% Pure canola oil, ρ ≈ 0.95 g mL−1), hexanes (Certified ACS ≥ 98.5% purity, ρ ≈ 0.669 g mL−1, η ≈ 0.310 mPa s), diesel (ultralow sulfur diesel, ρ ≈ 0.840 g mL−1, η ≈ 3.350 mPa s), xylenes (Certified ACS, ≥98.5% purity, ρ ≈ 0.865 g mL−1), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, anhydrous, ≥ 99.9% purity, ρ ≈ 1.10 g mL−1) were obtained from No name, Fisher Scientific, Petro‐Canada, Fisher Scientific, and Sigma‐Aldrich, respectively.

Preparation of Thermoreversible Gels

First, iPMMA and sPMMA were annealed in an oven at 150 °C for 30 min prior to further processing. At the same time, 100 mL of DMF, a strong complexing solvent, was charged to a round‐bottom flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer, and heated to 100 °C. After annealing, 1.666 g of iPMMA and 3.334 g of sPMMA were added to the DMF‐containing flask, and the mixture was stirred rapidly (900 rpm) for 2 h at a temperature of 100 °C. Herein, the weight ratio of iPMMA and sPMMA was fixed as 1:2, as this ratio was widely reported to maximize the fraction of stereocomplex crystals formed. After 2 h, both iPMMA and sPMMA dissolved completely in DMF, forming a homogeneous transparent solution. The solution was then dispensed into flat‐bottomed 50 mL plastic vials in 10 mL aliquots and allowed to cool naturally to ambient temperature. Gelation occurred within minutes of removing the solution from active heating, resulting in transparent, rigid gels containing 5 wt%/v of the iPMMA/sPMMA blend in DMF. The plastic vials could be inverted without any deformation or movement of the gels, indicating their solid‐like characteristics. To ensure complete gelation, the gels were cooled in a freezer at −22 °C for 48 h. Likewise, gels containing 1 and 10 wt%/v of the iPMMA/sPMMA blend in DMF were prepared (see Table S1 in the Supporting Information). Notably, gelation of the solution containing 10 wt%/v of the iPMMA/sPMMA blend in DMF occurred within 2 min of removing the solution from active heating, while gelation of the solution containing 1 wt%/v of solid in DMF occurred during cooling in the freezer. All gels, regardless of the solid concentration, remained highly transparent even after complete gelation. For comparison, an atactic PMMA grade (Plexiglas VM‐100, Trinseo) was dissolved in DMF (concentration of 10 wt%/v PMMA), under the same conditions; however, the solution did not undergo gelation, even after being stored at −22 °C for 2 weeks (see Figure S1 in the Supporting Information).

Supercritical Drying

First, the gels were subjected to solvent exchange. After being removed from the freezer, gels were allowed to naturally warm to ambient temperature. Approximately 10 mL of ethanol was then added to the plastic vials containing the thermogels, to replace the DMF in the gels. After 5 h, the ethanol in the plastic vials was replaced again with fresh ethanol. This cycle was repeated 5 times to ensure the complete replacement of DMF, over a 48 h period. Later, the gels were subjected to solvent exchange with liquid CO2 at a pressure of 10.34 MPa (1500 psi) using a high‐pressure syringe pump, followed by supercritical drying under CO2 at 40 °C. This resulted in the transformation of these alcogels into aerogels with a 3D porous network. The aerogels of the iPMMA/sPMMA blend were termed scPMMAx (where x represented the wt%/v of iPMMA/sPMMA, ranging from 1 to 10 wt%/v) hereafter. Recycling/Reprocessing of Aerogel Monoliths

To demonstrate the recyclability/reprocessability of these aerogels, scPMMA5 aerogels were subjected to either solvent‐based or melt reprocessing. Prior to both procedures, aerogels were dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 6 h to remove any bound moisture. For solvent processing, aerogels were crushed into a coarse powder and were dissolved in 4 mL of DMF (concentration of 5 wt%/v) maintained at 100 °C under magnetic stirring. The prepared aerogels fully dissolved in DMF within 30 min. Once homogenized, the solutions were simply removed from heat and allowed to gel, with gelation transpiring within 5 min of removal from active heating. For melt reprocessing, scPMMA5 was sandwiched between two Kapton sheets, melted on a Carver Hydraulic press for 4 min at 215 °C, and subjected to compression at 41.36 MPa (6000 psi). This process produced a transparent film of the scPMMA5 aerogel blend.

Results and Discussion

Gelation Mechanism

Polymer gels can be regarded as 3D networks of chains linked by either physical or chemical bonds. They exhibit solid‐like behavior, meaning they are incapable of macroscopic viscous flow and when deformed, their response is that of elastic bodies, yet they can absorb and hold large amounts of liquids and gases.[ 43 ] Gels can be classified based on their structural arrangement into four types: well‐ordered lamellar structures (Type I), covalent polymer networks (i.e., covalent cross‐links, Type II), physically aggregated polymer networks (Type III), and particulate, disordered structures (Type IV).[ 43 ]

Type III gels are the most common and usually form in solvent–polymer systems. In these gels, bundles of chains are often assembled into crystallites or multistrand helices that serve as junction points (i.e., physical cross‐links).[ 44 , 45 ] These junction points hold the amorphous chains together, consolidating an interconnected network structure that extends throughout the entire system. The concentration of chains in the solution must exceed a certain percolation threshold (i.e., critical gel concentration) for forming a self‐supporting gel.[ 43 , 44 , 46 ] What distinguishes Type III gels from others is that their gelation is reversible. This means that the interconnections responsible for the formation of the network can be easily disentangled and reformed in response to external stimuli, such as temperature.[ 47 ] The class of Type III gels possessing thermally labile physical cross‐links that can be disentangled and reformed via thermal treatment is referred to as “thermoreversible gels” (i.e., thermogels).[ 17 ]

DSC analysis was performed to verify whether the gels fabricated in this study are indeed thermoreversible in nature. The cooling thermograms of the thermogels (wet gels) obtained at cooling rates of 20, 10, 5, and 2 °C min−1, and subsequent heating thermograms obtained at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, are illustrated in Figure 2a,b. It should be noted that as the concentration of iPMMA/sPMMA in DMF decreases, there is a corresponding decrease in both the crystallization enthalpy (and enthalpy of fusion) and the peak crystallization temperature of the gels. Therefore, only the heat flow signals for gels with 5 wt%/v (see Figure S2 in the Supporting Information) and 10 wt%/v of iPMMA/sPMMA are shown, as the heat flow signal for the gel with 1 wt%/v of iPMMA/sPMMA was too weak to be reliably interpreted.

DSC thermograms corresponding to the scPMMA10 thermogel (wet gel), illustrating normalized heat flow with respect to temperature a) obtained over various cooling rates ranging from 20 to 2 °C min−1 and b) obtained after each cooling treatment using a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1, demonstrating the thermally reversible nature of this gelation mechanism. c) DSC 1st heating thermograms of iPMMA, sPMMA, and scCO2‐dried aerogels, illustrating glass transition temperature (T g), melting temperature (T m), and melting enthalpy (ΔH m).

Regardless of the applied cooling rate, a single crystallization exotherm was observed with a peak temperature (T c,Thermogel) ranging from 81 to 88 °C (see Figure 2a). This temperature range aligns well with the temperature at which the gelation was observed to initiate during thermogel preparation. The exotherm is caused by the formation of PMMA stereocomplex crystals (i.e., junction points) as the system cools. As anticipated, T c,Thermogel was found to decrease with an increase in the cooling rate due to thermal lag. To further corroborate the formation of stereocomplex crystals, the corresponding heating thermograms are also shown. As can be seen in Figure 2b, a single endotherm emerged at around 112 °C after each cooling cycle; this peak corresponds to the melting of crystals within the thermogels (T m,Thermogel). The total melting enthalpy (≈6 J g−1) and T m,Thermogel were almost constant for all heating cycles. This indicates that the structure of crystals is largely unaffected by prior cooling treatments, unlike other polymer–DMF gels, such as PCL–DMF gels.[ 2 ] Moreover, the presence of only one crystallization exotherm and melting endotherm indicates that there is only one distinct population of ordered crystallites or helices. The DSC results also confirm that the gels form through physical cross‐linking enabled by the crystallites or helices rather than any chemical bonding. The gels can melt and form a clear liquid solution at higher temperatures, and when the temperature is reduced below the critical temperature (i.e., T c,Thermogel), crystallization occurs, and the system exhibits solid‐like behavior. Since the gelation process of these gels is temperature‐dependent, they can be classified as thermoreversible gels.

All thermogels, regardless of the iPMMA/sPMMA concentration, were clear and transparent (see Figure S3 in the Supporting Information). This implies that over the range of PMMA concentrations investigated in this work, the size of the features formed within the gels is finer than the wavelengths of visible light (less than 400 nm). The thermoreversible nature of the gelation process, as well as the high degree of transparency unambiguously indicates that the mechanism forming these gels is driven by nanocrystal formation. It should be noted that this gelation process is curative‐free, with the developed structures being disentangled and reformed through the application of controlled thermal profiles, yielding fully thermoplastic aerogel precursors.

Thermal Transitions and Crystalline Structure

DSC analysis was conducted to determine the glass transition temperature (T g), melting temperature (T m), and melting enthalpy (ΔH m) of the fabricated aerogels, and to characterize their crystalline structure. The calorimetric heating profiles of the materials are presented in Figure 2c. The iPMMA sample displayed a T g of ≈56 °C, while the T g of sPMMA was recorded at around 131 °C. It is important to note that sPMMA does not crystallize on its own,[ 30 , 48 ] it only cocrystallizes with solvents.[ 25 , 34 ]

The T g values of scPMMA1, scPMMA5, and scPMMA10 aerogels were found to be 87.9, 96.7, and 100.0 °C, respectively. More importantly, the T m of these dried materials were 193.7, 197.7, and 195.3 °C, with the corresponding ΔH m of 38.1, 39.9, and 41.2 J g−1, respectively. Commercial PMMAs (e.g., Plexiglas) are amorphous in nature with a T g of ≈90 °C. Since they do not have a crystalline structure, they do not exhibit melting endotherms in their DSC profiles. The thermal stability of commercial PMMAs is largely determined by their T g. The aerogels presented here displayed distinct T m values between 193 and 197 °C with a notable degree of crystallinity, proving that they possess significantly greater geometric–thermal stability compared to commercial PMMAs that relax above T g owing to a lack of crystalline rigidity. Moreover, ΔH m was found to increase slightly with increasing iPMMA/sPMMA content in the thermogels, which leads to a greater tendency for iPMMA and sPMMA chains to associate and cocrystallize, given their closer proximity in gels with greater polymer fractions. It should be noted that the T m and ΔH m values reported in this work are among the highest ever reported for scPMMA.[ 33 , 38 , 49 ]

Finally, it is evident from the thermal property evaluation that the fabricated aerogel monoliths can be recycled or reprocessed via conventional recycling methods. Figure S4 (Supporting Information) demonstrates the recyclability of the aerogel monoliths via chemical recycling (i.e., redissolution in DMF and subsequent gelation, see Video S1 in the Supporting Information) as well as melt‐processing (i.e., compression molding). This addresses a key life‐cycle limitation associated with traditional aerogel materials, which are based on permanently cross‐linked structures (e.g., silica, polyimide) and cannot be recycled or reprocessed due to the use of curatives that consolidate the gel structure.

Heating traces of the prepared aerogels do not exhibit distinct multimodal melting endotherms, which were previously attributed to the fusion of PMMA stereocomplex crystals possessing both fringed micelle and lamellar morphologies. These structures generally form concurrently unless specific conditions, such as nanoconfinement, are applied during stereocomplexation.[ 50 ] The high melting temperature endotherm has been linked to the fusion of crystals formed by predominantly lamellar growth.[ 50 ] This suggests that drying under scCO2 promotes the association of fringes protruding from stereocomplex crystals possessing fringed micellar morphology, forming lamellar structures. Specifically, we believe the characteristic irregularity of the outer helical structure in scPMMA‐based junctions[ 26 ] may undergo some degree of structural reorganization during scCO2 treatment. Mizumoto et al.[ 49 ] studied stereocomplex formation in iPMMA/sPMMA films of varying degrees of stereoregularity under varying scCO2 pressures and found similar findings, with only a single stereocomplex crystal melting endotherm observable during calorimetric heating. However, the melting temperatures in our work are notably higher than what was previously identified and may be ascribed to thermal profile differences during our sol–gel methodology and the use of DMF as a strongly complexing solvent. Nevertheless, these authors observed that scCO2 does not inhibit stereocomplex crystal formation in PMMA, despite scCO2 being a Lewis acid. This finding is supported by a related study investigating the crystallization behavior of iPMMA/sPMMA under various temperatures, times, and CO2‐pressure exposure conditions and found that CO2 treatment can induce PMMA stereocomplexation to a notable degree.[ 38 ]

The crystal structure of the prepared aerogels was analyzed using XRD to validate our calorimetric analysis (Figure 3a). For iPMMA and sPMMA, only amorphous halos were observed, with no distinct diffraction peaks, confirming their amorphous nature. By contrast, both scPMMA5 and scPMMA10 aerogels exhibited multiple diffraction peaks near 11°, 15°, and 19°, indicating the presence of PMMA stereocomplex crystals,[ 51 , 52 ] with crystallinity of 30.7% and 31.0% for scPMMA5 and scPMMA10, respectively. Previous studies have suggested that the weak peak around 11° might correspond to the helical pitch of sPMMA or the distance between two double‐stranded helices, while the peak around 15° could be attributed to the helical pitch of iPMMA and the x‐ and y‐plane spacings.[ 31 , 53 ] The calculated lattice spacings for these crystals were 0.82, 0.55, and 0.43 nm for scPMMA5, and 0.81, 0.58, and 0.46 nm for scPMMA10, closely matching reported values for PMMA stereocomplex crystals. Specifically, the ≈0.82 nm lattice spacing corresponds to the pitch of the outermost sPMMA helix in the stereocomplex, while the ≈0.55 nm spacing is associated with the helical pitch of the double‐helix structure encapsulated by the sPMMA helix.[ 54 , 55 ] Finally, the ≈0.43 nm spacing likely represents the separation between sPMMA and iPMMA chains within the stereocomplex.

a) X‐ray diffraction patterns of sPMMA, iPMMA, scPMMA5, and scPMMA10. b) Full FTIR transmittance spectra, and c) FTIR transmittance spectra zooming into 1600–1000 cm−1.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis was performed to confirm the crystal structures present in the aerogels (Figure 3b,c). Notably, the v(C─O) peak near 1258 cm−1, corresponding to the trans chain conformation of isotactic and syndiotactic PMMA,[ 56 ] along with slight shifts in the v(C═O) peak around 1728 cm−1, indicate the presence of PMMA stereocomplex crystals within the aerogels.

Pore Structure and Internal Surface Area

Figure 4a–c presents representative SEM images of the prepared aerogel monoliths, while additional SEM images are available in Figure S5 (Supporting Information). All prepared aerogels exhibited an open‐cell, reticulated fibril structure, composed of fibrous cylindrical nanocrystals or nanorods.[ 26 , 30 , 50 , 57 ] Interestingly, the density of the fibrils increased with increasing PMMA content used in the sol–gel process. Pore sizes were estimated from the SEM images and were found to decrease with increasing PMMA content. For instance, the average pore size for scPMMA1 was estimated to be 148 ± 88 nm, decreasing to 58 ± 15 nm for scPMMA5 and further to 40 ± 16 nm for scPMMA10 (see Figure S6 in the Supporting Information). The aerogels also exhibited excellent homogeneity of these fine structural features throughout the sample volume, as is characteristic of aerogels fabricated via scCO2 drying. This drying process is known to yield effectively isotropic aerogels.[ 58 ]

SEM images of a) scPMMA1, b) scPMMA5, and c) scPMMA10 aerogels. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of d) scPMMA1, e) scPMMA5, and f) scPMMA10 aerogels.

BET analysis was performed to determine the internal surface area and pore size distribution of the aerogel monoliths. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of the aerogels are presented in Figure 4d–f, while the pore size distributions can be found in Figure S7 (Supporting Information). The scPMMA1 and scPMMA5 aerogels showed Type H3 hysteresis loops (IUPAC classification), which suggests that they possess a combination of macro‐ (≥50 nm) and mesopores (2–50 nm) in their structure. On the other hand, scPMMA10 exhibited a Type H2b hysteresis loop, indicating a structure composed solely of mesopores. These results are consistent with the findings from our SEM analysis. It is important to highlight that the development of mesoporous aerogels has been difficult with other thermoplastic polymers.[ 19 ] Such predominantly mesoporous aerogel monoliths can typically only be achieved in silica,[ 59 ] carbon,[ 60 ] thermoset,[ 61 ] and some cellulose‐based[ 62 ] material systems. S BET, determined from the adsorption branch of the hysteresis loops, were 357.9, 398.2, and 367.8 m2 g−1 for scPMMA1, scPMMA5, and scPMMA10, respectively. These remarkably high S BET values likely resulted from the applied scCO2‐drying process, which minimizes capillary forces that could otherwise cause the collapse of such fine pores.[ 63 , 64 ]

The S BET of aerogels depends on both their porosity (or density) and pore size/geometry. Herein, as the PMMA content increased, the pore size of the resulting aerogels decreased due to greater shrinkage during aerogel fabrication (see Table 1). This also resulted in a marked reduction in porosity, dropping from 97.3% in scPMMA1 to 66.6% in scPMMA10. Given this trend, scPMMA5 is expected to have an optimal S BET, as it presents a more balanced combination of porosity and pore size compared to scPMMA1 and scPMMA10. These findings are consistent with trends reported in literature,[ 65 ] which demonstrate that the porosity and pore size/geometry of an aerogel cooperatively define its S BET.

Bulk Properties

The average density and porosity of the aerogel monoliths are listed in Table 1. As expected, the average density of the aerogels increased, while the average porosity decreased as the PMMA concentration in the thermogels increased. The changes in density and porosity can be rationalized from a molecular perspective. The formation of PMMA stereocomplexes in strongly complexing solvents like DMF is believed to occur in a two‐step process.[ 27 ] First, iPMMA and sPMMA coils become entangled, via van der Waals interactions between the ester group of iPMMA and the α‐methyl group of sPMMA. This leads to the development of primary stereocomplex particles consisting of locally ordered stereocomplex crystals. Over time, more iPMMA and sPMMA chains incorporate into the particles, causing a significant increase in their apparent molecular weight, while the radius of gyration of the particles remains about the same. Later, nonassociated chain segments that protrude from the particles into the solution participate in intermolecular stereoassociation, leading to aggregation into larger particles and an increase in viscosity and radius of gyration. Intermolecular stereoassociation is much more prevalent when larger concentrations of polymer are used in the sol–gel process. As such, we observed greater compaction/densification of the gels at higher PMMA concentrations. We also believe that this secondary association process not only occurs during solution‐mixing conditions or during gel ageing but can also transpire during scCO2 drying.

Uniaxial compression tests were performed on the aerogel monoliths, and the resulting stress–strain curves are presented in Figure 5a. These curves reveal three distinct deformation regions: i) the Hookean region (i.e., linear elastic region), ii) the collapse region (i.e., plateau region), and iii) the densification region, which correspond to microstructural bending, buckling, and compaction‐related phenomena, respectively.[ 66 ]

The compressive modulus, taken as the initial slope of the linear elastic region, was 7.04 ± 2.17 MPa for scPMMA5 and 79.3 ± 1.45 MPa for scPMMA10. This translates to specific modulus values of 41.4 and 198.3 MPa cm3 g−1. Additionally, the compressive strength of the aerogels was evaluated at 20% strain (1 mm min−1). The scPMMA5 aerogel exhibited a compressive strength of 1.05 ± 0.37 MPa, which increased to 8.74 ± 0.08 MPa for scPMMA10. The comparatively lower modulus and strength observed in scPMMA5 can be attributed to its lower density, a trend that aligns with the general observation that the modulus and strength of aerogels with similar backbone chemistry tend to increase with their density.[ 67 ] It is worth mentioning that the scPMMA1 monolith was too brittle to conduct a reliable uniaxial compression test. It should also be noted that scPMMA10 reached its strain limit at ≈50% due to geometric constraints associated with its initial height and aspect ratio. Specifically, the relatively low starting height of this sample (ascribed to the gel's shrinkage tendencies) restricted the total displacement range that could be applied while maintaining reliable data acquisition.

The nature of the stress–strain curves of both scPMMA5 and scPMMA10 aerogels resembles that of tough materials. Even when compressed to 50% strain, the aerogels did not fracture; instead, they plastically deformed into dense compacted discs (see Figure S8 in the Supporting Information). This behavior is rather unexpected, as aerogels with similar densities typically exhibit catastrophic brittle failure due to the inability of the network of ligaments to bend under compressive loading.[ 68 ] The nonintuitive failure mechanism in our aerogels can be attributed to two factors: the fine but uniform (i.e., isotropic) void morphology and the nanoscopic dimensions of the ligaments comprising the aerogels. The uniformity of the voids imparts a homogeneous stress distribution throughout these aerogels, while the fine ligaments with high aspect ratio effectively provides bendability, resulting in aerogels with an unexpectedly large linear elastic and plateau region during compression, as opposed to immediate brittle failure via crack propagation through the ligaments. Moreover, highly crystalline polymers typically display brittle behavior, particularly in the presence of large crystallites.[ 69 ] Our findings indicate that the failure mode of porous, crystalline aerogels such as our stereocomplex‐based aerogel monoliths can be controlled by adjusting the ligament thickness, with finer ligaments possessing high aspect ratio permitting greater deformability under compressive loading. It should be noted that crystalline reorganization processes are not believed to occur under the selected compressive testing conditions, as is evidenced in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), with no significant changes in melting enthalpy or T m after compressive testing (40.8 J g−1, and 195.4 °C, respectively). Although our aerogel monoliths exhibited a tough mechanical response, their modulus and strength values were higher than or comparable to those of other reported aerogel systems in literature.[ 19 , 70 , 71 ] Such stiff, strong, yet tough aerogels are highly sought after but remain challenging to fabricate. Most silica‐, carbon‐, and polymer‐based aerogels are inherently brittle, which limits their practical applications.[ 19 , 72 , 73 ] Here, we propose that one potential strategy to overcome this challenge lies in reduction of the ligament thickness in such aerogels.

The thermal stability of the aerogel monoliths was investigated using TGA. The residual weight percent with respect to temperature is plotted in Figure 5b, with the degradation onset temperature, corresponding to a 5% weight loss (T 5%), highlighted. Additionally, the first derivative of TGA curves is shown in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). The T 5% values for iPMMA and sPMMA were ≈314 and 342 °C, respectively, while scPMMA5 exhibited a T 5% of ≈344 °C. The unexpectedly higher T 5% of the aerogel monolith, compared to its individual constituents, is attributed to the stereocomplex configuration, with encapsulation of the thermally labile iPMMA by sPMMA,[ 50 ] as was elucidated by calorimetric and X‐ray analyses. Following the initial stage of thermal degradation, all materials experienced a rapid weight loss, with the maximum weight loss occurring at 375 °C for iPMMA, ≈385 °C for sPMMA, and ≈382 °C for scPMMA5. The degradation mechanism is believed to be related to random chain scission.[ 74 , 75 ]

The scPMMA1 aerogel monolith had a WCA of 141.6 ± 8.9°, which decreased to 122.3 ± 5.9° for scPMMA5 and 103.7 ± 4.8° for scPMMA10 (see Table 1 and Figure S11 in the Supporting Information). Our BET and SEM analyses showed that increasing PMMA concentration in the thermogels led to higher aerogel monolith density. This resulted in greater internal interconnectivity and a higher solid fraction within the aerogels, making it easier for water droplets to wet the surface. Additionally, the transition from a macro‐ and mesoporous structure to an exclusively mesoporous structure also contributed to the reduction of the WCA value for scPMMA10.

Owing to their hydrophobicity, the aerogel monoliths also exhibit excellent oil–water separation ability. The canola oil sorption capacity values of the aerogel monoliths are listed in Table 1. The highest oil sorption capacity was shown by scPMMA1, reaching 29.8 ± 4.8 g g−1, which can be attributed to its very high porosity and combined macro‐ and mesoporous structure. As porosity decreased and the internal structure transitioned from mixed macro‐/mesoporous to solely mesoporous, the oil sorption capacity also declined. For instance, the scPMMA5 aerogel had a reduced capacity of 7.8 ± 1.1 g g−1. Nevertheless, this value remains comparable to the oil sorption capacities reported for polyimide aerogels.[ 76 ]

Figure S12 (Supporting Information) shows the kinetics of canola oil sorption by the aerogels. All three aerogels show a sharp increase in sorption capacity during the first few minutes. This nearly instantaneous uptake reflects the accessibility of the pores for oil sorption, with the initial sorbed amount showing a similar trend to porosity and pore size. The rate of uptake then plateaued, with each aerogel reaching saturation capacity within ≈10 min.

To further examine the selective sorption performance of the aerogels, the sorption capacity of scPMMA5 (as a representative sample) was tested with various fluids, including hexanes, ethanol, diesel, xylenes, canola oil, DMSO, and distilled water (Figure 5c). The sorption capacity exhibited a linear correlation with fluid density (excluding distilled water), as is expected.[ 77 ] The highest capacity, 9.8 ± 2.7 g g−1, was observed for the densest fluid, DMSO, while the lowest capacity, 4.3 ± 0.6 g g−1, was recorded for hexanes, which possess the lowest fluid density. As expected, the sorption capacity for distilled water was close to zero (<0.1 g g−1). This combination of high uptake for oils and solvents and negligible water sorption makes the aerogel well‐suited for oil–water separation, solvent recovery, and environmental remediation. A sequence of digital photographs taken at various stages of the oil–water separation using scPMMA5, is presented in Figure 5d, with a recording illustrating this process in Video S2 (Supporting Information).

The total thermal conductivity (k) of the fabricated aerogel monoliths, which mainly includes radiative heat transfer and heat conduction through solid and gaseous phases, was measured to assess their thermal insulation performance. As illustrated in Figure 6a, scPMMA1 showed a total thermal conductivity of 23.9 mW m−1 K−1, which increased to 29.7 mW m−1 K−1 for scPMMA5 and further to 65.1 mW m−1 K−1 for scPMMA10. Thermal transport in aerogels is largely determined by relative density, pore size distribution, morphological features such as skeleton size and shape, and the heat transfer properties of the solid phase. In this study, the increase in aerogel density led to a higher thermal conductivity, likely due to the enhanced heat conduction through the solid skeleton (i.e., fibrils). It should be noted that for scPMMA1 and scPMMA5, low thermal conductivities (<30 mW m−1 K−1) are possible owing to the absorptive tendencies of PMMA that tend to block radiation, despite the high void fraction that otherwise readily transmits heat through thermal radiation.[ 89 , 90 ]

The excellent thermal insulation performance of the aerogels is also evident in Figures S13 and S14 (Supporting Information), which illustrate aerogel surface temperatures extracted from thermal infrared images captured at various time intervals, after aerogels were placed on a hot plate set to 55 or 100 °C. When the hot plate temperature was set to 55 °C, the surface temperature of the scPMMA5 aerogel reached 36.5 °C after 6 min, and reached 56.3 °C after 6 min when the hot plate was set to 100 °C, blocking up to 44% of the heat. Additionally, when the hot plate was set to 55 °C, the surface temperature of the scPMMA10 aerogel reached 38.4 °C after 6 min, and reached 61.2 °C after 6 min when the hot plate was set to 100 °C. Figures S15 and S16 (Supporting Information) show representative thermal infrared images of the scPMMA5 and scPMMA10 aerogels captured at various time intervals.

Finally, we compared the performance of our aerogels with other material systems, particularly polymer‐based aerogels reported in literature, focusing on thermal conductivity and elastic modulus (Figure 6b). Moreover, Table S6 (Supporting Information) provides a detailed comparison of the bulk properties of the newly developed PMMA‐stereocomplex‐based aerogel monoliths, with those of various state‐of‐the‐art polymer aerogels. Our PMMA aerogels exhibited a well‐balanced combination of both thermal conductivity and elastic modulus. Notably, scPMMA5 showed one of the lowest thermal conductivities reported for polymer‐based aerogel systems with a notably high elastic modulus. This makes scPMMA5 a strong candidate for lightweight, sustainable thermal insulation. Furthermore, these aerogels offer a viable alternative to thermoset aerogels such as polyimide‐based aerogels,[ 76 ] delivering comparable performance while offering additional advantages like reprocessability (see Figure S4 in the Supporting Information) and hydrophobicity, features typically not achievable with polyimide‐based systems.

Conclusions

This work presents the development of a novel aerogel system composed entirely of PMMA and its stereocomplex crystals. Using a one‐pot thermoreversible gelation method involving iPMMA and sPMMA, we formed thermogels that, following solvent exchange and scCO2 drying, resulted in monolithic aerogels. The resulting aerogels exhibited very low densities (0.03–0.4 g cm−3) and high porosities (97.3–66.6 %). Depending on the PMMA concentration in the thermogels, the aerogels exhibited either mixed macro‐ and mesoporous character, or exclusively mesoporous character with S BET ranging between 357 and 398 m2 g−1. The PMMA stereocomplex crystals of the aerogels showed among the highest T m and ΔH m reported for PMMA stereocomplex crystals. Despite the inherent brittleness of PMMA due to its rigid backbone and high T g, the fabricated aerogel monoliths offered an unusual balance between stiffness, strength, and toughness, attributed to their nanometric fibrillar ligament networks. Moreover, the prepared aerogel monoliths showed excellent oil sorption capacity (2.9–29.8 g g−1), oil–water separation capability, and thermal insulation ability (k of 24–65 mW m−1 K−1). Finally, unlike traditional silica, polyimide, or other chemically cross‐linked aerogels, these PMMA aerogel monoliths can be easily recycled via chemical recycling and melt‐processing. These findings highlight the potential of the reported aerogels as a greener alternative for oil–water separation and thermal insulation applications, while also offering a promising framework for designing thermogels and their derivatives including electrolyte gels, hydrogels, cryogels, xerogels, and aerogels from similar thermoplastic systems.
