IL-6+ fibroblasts contribute to shaping the pro-inflammatory microenvironment in severe periodontitis after non-surgical periodontal therapy
Authors: Jun Chen, Yichen Xiong, Binjie Liu, Wenjie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo reveal the cellular composition and molecular environment of the inflammatory infiltrates in residual deep pockets of severe periodontitis following non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT). This may provide critical insights for targeted therapy in managing severe periodontitis.MethodsSingle-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) and bulk RNA-seq of periodontal tissues from patients with severe periodontitis following NSPT and healthy donors were performed. Eligible public datasets of scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq were also used for analysis. Multiplex immunohistochemical immunofluorescence was used to validate the identified cell subtypes further.ResultsWe identified a diverse inflammatory microenvironment landscape in the residual deep pockets of severe periodontitis after NSPT, in which IL-6+ fibroblasts were enriched in the deep pocket granulation tissue of severe periodontitis. The hypoxic microenvironment orchestrated by IL-6+ fibroblasts was closely associated with the persistent inflammation in severe periodontitis after NSPT. The infiltration of IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages exhibited a strong correlation, suggesting a potential crosstalk between the two cell subtypes. The coexistence of IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages was associated with sustained inflammatory responses, response to hypoxia and oxidative stress, osteoclast differentiation, and bone resorption in severe periodontitis after NSPT.ConclusionsOur study unveiled the presence of a fibroblast-mediated network localized in the hypoxic region of the severe periodontitis that functionally linked macrophages and fibroblasts, which collaborate to exacerbate the periodontal inflammation microenvironment. These findings may offer a new perspective for the targeted treatment of severe periodontitis, especially the targeted treatment against IL-6+ fibroblasts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07132-8.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease associated with dysbiosis of the microbial community and immune dysregulation. Severe periodontitis includes stage III/IV periodontitis as well as previous aggressive periodontitis [1]. Epidemiological data show that the global prevalence of severe periodontitis surged from 11.2% (1990–2010) [2]. to 23.6% (2011–2020) [3], indicating a significant increase. The escalating prevalence underscores that periodontitis, particularly severe periodontitis, remains a persistent global public health issue and burden.

Severe periodontitis is characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, which may eventually lead to tooth mobility and loss [1]. The primary goals of treating severe periodontitis are to control infection and inflammation, prevent further periodontal tissue damage, and promote periodontal tissue regeneration [4, 5]. Non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT), including supragingival scaling and subgingival debridement, is an essential approach in treating periodontitis [6, 7]. NSPT may prevent the degradation of periodontal tissues, but limited tissue regeneration occurs, especially in terms of alveolar bone regeneration [6, 7]. For severe periodontitis, NSPT alone is often insufficient, while periodontal surgery is frequently required to reduce periodontal pockets further and achieve periodontal tissue regeneration [4, 5]. However, the uncertainty and invasiveness of periodontal surgery, coupled with the financial, physical, and psychological burdens it brings, dissuade some severe periodontitis patients from opting for the procedure. Without undergoing pocket reduction surgery or regenerative surgery, residual deep periodontal pockets, especially intra-bony defects, represent the frontline of disease progression, associated with an increased risk of disease advancement [8]. Therefore, elucidating the cellular and molecular environment of residual deep pockets in severe periodontitis after NSPT is not only of scientific significance but also of clinical significance, which may provide critical insights for targeted therapy in managing severe periodontitis.

Bulk RNA-seq provides a comprehensive overview of the molecular events underlying periodontitis but fails to capture cellular heterogeneity within periodontal tissues. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables transcriptomic profiling of individual cell types in human tissues, facilitating novel insights into understanding the diversity and functions of distinct cell types within periodontal tissues [9]. Specifically, scRNA-seq provides powerful tools for comparing healthy and diseased tissues, identifying novel or rare cell types, analyzing stem cell differentiation, reconstructing cell trajectories, and identifying cell–cell interactions [9]. For instance, the identification of new epithelial [10], fibroblast [11], keratinocyte [12], and neutrophil [13] subtypes in periodontal tissues has enhanced our understanding of how cellular heterogeneity contributes to periodontal pathogenesis. Additionally, scRNA-seq supports the development of molecular diagnostic models [14] and early-warning biomarkers [13] for periodontitis, as well as the identification of potential therapeutic targets [15, 17]. For example, studies have confirmed that NLRP3⁺ macrophages play a central role in periodontitis by participating in pro-inflammatory responses, tissue damage, immune regulation, and pathogen clearance. Targeting these macrophages by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome or blocking downstream cytokines has been proposed as a promising therapeutic strategy [16]. Despite the growing body of scRNA-seq research advancing our understanding of periodontal disease mechanisms, the periodontal microenvironment within infrabony pockets of severe periodontitis following initial therapy, along with its formation mechanisms, remains incompletely elucidated. To address this unresolved issue, we aimed to investigate the cellular composition and molecular landscape of inflammatory infiltrates in residual deep pockets of severe periodontitis following NSPT. These findings may offer novel insights into the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for severe periodontitis.

Methods

Patients and samples

Human periodontal samples were obtained from consenting patients undergoing routine periodontal surgical procedures. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya Stomatological Hospital, Central South University (No. 20230001). The study conformed to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

For healthy controls, only individuals with no signs of periodontal disease (no gingival/periodontal inflammation, a probing depth < 3 mm, and no bleeding on probing) were enrolled. Healthy samples were collected from healthy volunteers during a crown-lengthening procedure. For periodontitis, only individuals with stage I or III/IV periodontitis according to the new classification of periodontitis [18] were enrolled. Periodontitis patients who had received comprehensive NSPT more than 6~8 weeks before the study were selected. The exclusion criteria for all participants are as follows: (a) smokers; (b) systemic diseases; (c) pregnancy or lactation; (d) antibiotic use in the past 6 months. Characteristics of all participants were shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Stage I periodontitis samples were collected from patients during gingivectomy. Stage III/IV periodontitis samples were collected from the intra-bony defects of patients who underwent open-flap debridement (guided tissue regeneration procedure). The principle of tissue sampling was not to damage the patient’s periodontal health, and all the obtained periodontal tissues were those that needed to be removed during the operation.

scRNA-seq analysis of public database

We used the keyword “periodontitis” to search the gene expression datasets in GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). Inclusion criteria were that the test samples were from Homo sapiens, and the gene expression profiles included severe periodontitis tissues and healthy controls. Meanwhile, these periodontitis samples were obtained during periodontal surgery after NSPT. Two scRNA-seq datasets, GSE152042 and GSE207502, met the inclusion criteria. GSE152042 contained two healthy gingival tissues, one mild periodontitis tissue, and one severe periodontitis tissue. GSE207502 contained three periodontitis tissues, but only one sample (GSM6290650) met the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, two healthy samples, one mild periodontitis sample, and two severe periodontitis samples were included for scRNA-seq analysis. The datasets were integrated using the canonical correlation analysis-based anchor method [19].

The “Seurat” package in “R” was used for data processing of the raw data Counts matrix. Cells with < 200 genes, >8000 genes, and >20% mitochondrial genes were filtered out. A total of 22,387 filtered cells were selected for analysis. The “NormalizeData” function was used to standardize the gene expression data. The “ScaleData” function was used for regression analysis to correct inter-batch differences. The “Harmony” package in “R” was used to correct inter-batch differences between samples [20]. The normalized expression matrix was used for cell cluster and dimension reduction. The “Seurat” package “R” was used for cell cluster and dimension reduction. 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were selected from the corrected expression matrix, and their eigenvalues were extracted by the “FindVariableGenes” function in the “Seurat” package. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for the first 2000 HVGs using the “RunPCA” function. The “FindClusters” function was used for cell cluster in the PCA space. The parameter resolution was set to 0.8, and only the major cell types were identified, such as T cells, B cells, macrophages, or fibroblasts. The clustered cells were projected into a two-dimensional space using the “RunUMAP” function. The clustering results were visualized using the “DimPlot” function.

The “Seurat” package was used to perform the “findmarker” function to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of each cluster. Cell type identification was performed based on DEGs in each cluster, and manual examination was performed based on previous studies. The “SingleR” package was used to annotate cell clusters based on known cell markers. Cell clusters expressing the same cellular markers were merged.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication mediated by ligand-receptor complexes is essential for coordinating a variety of biological processes, such as development, differentiation, and inflammation. Study of these ligand-receptor interactions is important for understanding cell behavior and complex biological processes. The CellPhoneDB was used to analyze the cell–cell communications [21]. Based on the interaction and the normalized cell matrix achieved by the “Seurat” normalization, the significant mean and cell communication significance (p value < 0.05) were calculated.

Pseudotime analysis

Pseudotime analysis can identify cells at the beginning, middle and end states of trajectory inference, reveal the dynamics of gene expression between cells [22]. Pseudotime analysis was performed by using Monocle2 (R package) to understand the relationship between the disease progression of periodontitis and changes in gene expression over the cell trajectory [23].

Differentially expressed genes detection and enrichment analysis

The DEGs of each cluster were identified by the “MAST” test implemented in the Seurat package (FindAllMarkers function). Then, the DEGs were passed to the compareCluster function in the clusterProfiler package [24] for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis. Q-value was set at 0.05.

Transcription factor regulon analysis

The pySCENIC was performed to analyze the regulatory network and regulon activity. Using the AUCell module of the pySCENIC, the regulon activity (quantified by AUC) was analyzed. The determination of active regulons was carried out according to the AUCell default threshold. In the R package Seurat, the differential-expression regulon was identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test within the “FindAllMarkers” function. A heatmap was generated using the scaled expression of regulon activity. To examine the gene expression of transcription factors (TFs) independently, we obtained Genes encoding TFs from four public datasets: JASPAR87 (http://jaspar.genereg.net/), DBD88 (http://www.transcriptionfactor.org/), AnimalTFDB89 (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/AnimalTFDB/), and TF2DNA90 (http://www.fiserlab.org/tf2dna_db/). We intersected the TFs with the quantified DEGs mentioned above and identified the most specifically expressed TFs in each cluster.

Characterization of cell-type infiltration based on single-cell expression matrix

To determine the proportions of our defined 4 fibroblast subtypes and 5 macrophage subtypes from GSE16134 (expression profiling by array), we used the online tool CIBERSORTx28 to create a reference signature matrix from our scRNA-seq dataset and estimate the cell type proportions from GSE16134 based on the constructed cell type reference. To create the signature matrix, when running CIBERSORTx, quartile normalization was enabled, and all other parameters were used with their default settings. For the normalization of cell fractions, quartile normalization was enabled, the permutation parameter was set to 500 times, and all other parameters were set to their default values. To assess the associations between the proportions of cell-type infiltration, Spearman’s correlation analysis was implemented. A correlation was regarded as significant when |Rs| >0.3 and FDR < 0.05. To assess and visualize the Euclidean distance of cell infiltration across cell types in severe periodontitis cohorts, we utilized the unsupervised clustering function within “Heatmap.plus” in “R”.

scRNA-seq analysis of self-test data

Fresh periodontal tissues were collected for cell preparation, which was performed immediately according to the 10x Genomics Single Cell Protocols. The RNA extracted from the barcoded cells was then reverse transcribed, and the scRNA-seq library was prepared using reagents from a Chromium Single Cell 30 v3 reagent kit provided by 10X Genomics, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) in Novogene BIO (Tianjin, China). The scRNA-seq data were subjected to dimensionality reduction, unsupervised clustering, annotation, and visualization in the same manner as described above.

Bulk RNA-seq data processing

Fresh periodontal tissues were collected for bulk RNA-seq. Total RNA was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA by using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. Fragmentation was carried out using divalent cations under elevated temperature in First Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5×). First strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primer and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase, then used RNaseH to degrade the RNA. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and dNTP. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities. After adenylation of the 3’ ends of DNA fragments, Adaptors with hairpin loop structure were ligated to prepare for hybridization. In order to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 370–420 bp in length, the library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA). Then PCR amplification, AMPure XP beads purified the PCR product, and the library was finally obtained.

In order to ensure the quality of the library, the library needs to be tested. After the construction of the library, the library was initially quantified by Qubit2.0 Fluorometer, then diluted to 1.5 ng/ul, and the insert size of the library was detected by Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. After the insert size meets the expectation, qRT-PCR is used to accurately quantify the effective concentration of the library (the effective concentration of the library is higher than 2nM) to ensure the quality of the library. After the library was qualified, sequencing was performed by Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The end reading of 150 bp pairing is generated.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) can categorize genes into different modules via a biological network, helping in uncovering important gene modules relevant to sample traits. The expression spectrum data of self-test bulk-seq and the grouping information (normal, stage I periodontitis (mild periodontitis), and stage III/IV periodontitis (severe periodontitis)) were used as three traits for WGCNA. The “WGCNA” package in “R” was used to screen gene modules. The correlation between modules and specific traits was analyzed. The modules with the most positive correlation were identified.

GSEA

GSEA can be used to analyze the function and pathway of the target gene, related to the gene set co-expressed by the gene. Detailed steps of the procedure that appeared in the GSEA have been carried out in “JAVA” and “R”. GSEA packages were downloaded at www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp. The canonical pathways gene sets (c2.cp.v4.0.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MsigDB) (http://www.broad.mit.edu/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) were used for enrichment analysis. Gene sets represented by at least 15 genes were preserved. The gene signatures or pathways with FDR < 0.05 were considered as significantly enriched [25, 26].

Histology and multiplex immunohistochemical (mIHC)

Morphological evaluation of human periodontal soft tissue biopsies was performed using H&E staining. In order to conduct fluorescence immunostaining, the human periodontal soft tissue biopsy samples were diluted with primary antibodies in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) / phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies used include IL-6 (1:100, EM170413, HUABIO), FCN1 (1:100, 11775-1-AP, PROTEINTECH), LUM (1:50, ab168348, Abcam), CD68 (1:500, ab213363, Abcam). Incubate with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. Signal amplification was performed by using TSA fluorescence dyes, incubated for 10 min in the dark. DAPI was used for nuclei counterstaining. The ZEISS Axioscan 7 Multispectral Imaging System was used for image acquisition. The ZEN 3.3 software was used for image reading and analysis.

Results

A single-cell transcriptomic atlas of healthy controls, mild periodontitis, and severe periodontitis

The scRNA-seq datasets (GSE152042 and GSE207502) from the GEO database were analyzed. The results of data quality control and normalization were shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. After filtration, a total of 22,387 cells from mild periodontitis patients, severe periodontitis patients, and healthy controls were retained. We identified 19 cell clusters, which were visualized using the UMAP (Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Table 2). These cell clusters were assigned to 14 distinct cell types using known marker genes (Fig. 1c , d): lymphatic endothelial cells (marked with CCL21 and TFF3), venular endothelial cells (marked with CCL14 and SELE), vascular mural cells (marked with TAGLN and RGS5), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) (marked with MYBL2 and PLD4), conventional dendritic cell (cDCs) (CLEC9A and WDFY4), transit amplify cells (TACs) (DLGAP5 and PCLAF), mast cells (TPSAB1 and TPSB2), cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (NKG7 and CCL5), CD4+ T cells (LTB and CD3D), memory B cells (CD83 and MS4A1), plasma B cells (IGKC and IGHG4), macrophages (LYZ and CXCL8), epithelial cells (FDCSP and KRT14), fibroblasts (LUM and DCN) were identified (Fig. 1e , f).

Although all the 14 cell types were presented in normal healthy gingival tissues, mild periodontitis tissues, and severe periodontitis tissues from the five patients (Fig. 2a), the grade of infiltration for each of these major cell types was different, possibly reflecting differences in the stage of periodontitis progression. We found that the percentage of fibroblast cells gradually increases across the healthy, mild, and severe groups. Moreover, the percentage of fibroblasts in severe periodontitis was higher than that in healthy and mild periodontitis (Fig. 2b , c), suggesting that fibroblasts may play a key role in severe progressive periodontitis. The top 10 DEGs in fibroblasts across the normal healthy control group, mild periodontitis group, and severe periodontitis group were presented in Fig. 2d. We utilized hallmark gene sets of the MsigDB [26] to analyze the alterations in pathways of fibroblasts among normal controls, mild periodontitis, and severe periodontitis. The immune-related pathways, including NF-κB signaling, IL-6 signaling, TNF targets, EGF signaling were enriched in fibroblasts in severe periodontitis compared to healthy control and mild periodontitis, suggesting the involvement of fibroblasts in the immune response against severe periodontitis (Fig. 2e). Hallmark gene sets for hypoxia and oxidative stress, including Rho GTPases activate NADPH oxidases, hypoxia via ELK3 and HIF1α, hypoxia via KDM3A were more enriched in fibroblasts in severe periodontitis than those in normal healthy control and mild periodontitis (Fig. 2e). These features might reflect the crucial role of fibroblasts in hypoxic areas of severe periodontitis, remodeling the hypoxic microenvironment in severe periodontitis. In addition, dendritic cell maturation and senescence were enriched in fibroblasts in severe periodontitis compared to healthy control and mild periodontitis (Fig. 2e). Studies have shown that dendritic cells were critically linked to unresolved inflammation and alveolar bone loss in periodontitis [27]. Taken together, these findings indicate that fibroblasts contribute to shaping the inflammatory microenvironment of severe periodontitis.

IL-6+ fibroblasts contribute to shaping the pro-inflammatory microenvironment in severe periodontitis

Periodontal fibroblasts clustered into four subgroups according to specifically expressed marker genes (Fig. 3a , b). Violin plots illustrated that FDCSP+ fibroblasts, IL-24+ fibroblasts, IL-6+ fibroblasts, and SFRP4+ fibroblasts expressed elevated levels of FDCSP, IL-24, IL-6 and SFRP4, respectively (Fig. 3c). The IL-6+ fibroblasts were markedly enriched in severe periodontitis as compared to that from healthy control and mild periodontitis (Fig. 3d). Specific genes were expressed in IL-6+ fibroblasts, and GO terms such as p38MAPK cascade (GO:0038066), stress-activated MAPK cascade (GO:0051403), cellular response to oxidative stress (GO:0034599), response to hypoxia (GO:0001666), Th17 type immune response (GO:0072538), Th17 cell differentiation (GO:0072539), regulation of macrophage activation (GO:0043030), macrophage chemotaxis (GO:0048246), macrophage migration (GO:1905517), regulation of T cell activation (GO:0050863), regulation of IL-1β production (GO:0032651), regulation of IL-6 production (GO:0032675), regulation of collagen metabolic process (GO:0010712), osteoclast differentiation (GO:0030316), response to BMP (GO:0071772), bone remodeling (GO:0046849), regulation of bone resorption (GO:0045124), regulation of biomineral tissue development (GO:0070167) were enriched in IL-6+ fibroblasts (Fig. 3e). KEGG analysis showed that the TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cellular senescence, TGF-β signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway and ferroptosis were enriched in IL-6+ fibroblasts (Fig. 3f). The results indicated that the activation of these biological processes and pathways was related to the function of IL-6+ fibroblasts.

Pseudotime analysis on IL-6+ fibroblasts and the other fibroblast subtypes showed that IL-6+ fibroblasts likely originated from SFRP4+ fibroblasts (Fig. 3g, h). The differentiation of IL-6+ fibroblasts was meticulously coordinated by a complex network of transcription factors (TFs), which regulated each other and their effectors by interacting with their cofactors and downstream genes. To further determine the master regulator of IL-6+ fibroblasts, we performed pySCENIC analysis. The results indicated that JUND had the highest expression and activity levels in the regulatory network of IL-6+ fibroblasts (Fig. 3i , j), and might represent the master TF driving the differentiation pathway of IL-6+ fibroblasts. JUND not only participated in the regulation of apoptosis, ferroptosis, Th17 cell differentiation, cellular senescence, osteoclast differentiation, mineral absorption, but also engaged in critical pathways in immune and inflammation such as TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. 3k). These characteristics indicated a critical role of IL-6+ fibroblasts in severe periodontitis.

Cell–cell interaction of IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages contributed to the inflammation microenvironment in severe periodontitis

Cell–cell communication is critical in cell development, tissue homeostasis, and disease development [28]. The information exchange between cells is based on the complex reactions between ligands and their receptors and the activation of specific cell signaling pathways. The number of interactions existing between two types of cells was selected as the basis for evaluating the close communication between cell types. By utilizing the transcriptional profiles of each cell population, we used CellPhoneDB to construct a comprehensive intercellular network of potential ligand-receptor interactions between different cell types [21]. The number of potential receptor-ligand interactions between IL-6+ fibroblasts and macrophages was significantly greater than that between IL-6+ fibroblasts and other immune cells (Fig. 4a). In recent years, researchers have found that the classification of M1 and M2 macrophages does not fully reflect the diversity of macrophage phenotypes in the tissue microenvironment, especially in tissues in disease states. Usually, researchers use CD86 and CD163/CD206 to distinguish M1 type from M2 type. However, in many instances, the expression levels of CD86 and CD163/CD206 are not distinctly polarized but rather exhibit subtle variations [29, 30]. This makes it challenging to accurately distinguish between M1 and M2 phenotypes based solely on these markers. Similarly, in this study, we could not divide periodontal macrophages into M1 and M2 subtypes with conventional M1 and M2 markers (Supplementary Fig. 2).

scRNA-seq technology allows gene expression to be resolved at the single-cell level to reveal macrophage heterogeneity. Therefore, periodontal macrophages were clustered into five subtypes based on specific expressed marker genes (Fig. 4b). As shown by the UMPA plots, C1QA+ macrophages expressed elevated levels of C1QA, FCER1A+ macrophages expressed high levels of FCER1A, FCGR3B+ macrophages expressed elevated levels of FCGR3B, FCN1+ macrophages expressed elevated levels of FCN1, and LAMP3 + macrophages expressed high levels of LAMP3 (Fig. 4c). Further, we conducted cell–cell communication analysis on IL-6+ fibroblasts and the five subtypes of macrophages. The number of potential receptor-ligand interactions between IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages was significantly greater than that between IL-6+ fibroblasts and other macrophage subtypes (Fig. 4d).

Nowadays, some researchers not only use the traditional M1 and M2 markers, but also summarize the genes including cytokines related to M1 or M2, obtained in previous literature to obtain the gene sets of M1 and M2. M1 and M2 were distinguished by gene set scoring [31, 32]. In this study, the phenotypes of each periodontal macrophage subtype were further analyzed with the signature of M1 and M2 cells [32–34]. Results showed that FCN1+ macrophages expressed several classical M1 macrophage markers: NLRP3 and IL1B [32] (Supplementary Table 3). However, FCN1+ macrophages cannot be categorized as M1 or M2 (Fig. 4e , f). Our findings suggested that using scRNA-seq data to classify macrophage subtypes into M1 or M2 was not optimal. Instead, labeling them based on relevant markers might be more appropriate, consistent with several previous studies [35, 36].

To determine the relationship between IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages, we analyzed pairwise Spearman correlations within the infiltration patterns of IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages. We observed a significantly positive correlation between IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages (R = 0.263, p value = 0.01) (Fig. 4j, l). This result further suggested that IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages have cellular interactions in severe periodontitis.

Self-test bulk RNA-seq revealed the biological function of IL-6 and FCN1 in severe periodontitis

Self-test bulk RNA-seq data were analyzed by using WGCNA to identify gene modules associated with periodontitis severity. A total of 9120 variable genes were used for WGCNA. A soft-threshold power = 21 was selected to construct a scale-free network (Fig. 5a, b). A total of eight modules were identified (Fig. 5c). Notably, the MEgrey and MEtan modules exhibited significant positive correlations with severe periodontitis (Stage III/IV periodontitis) (Fig. 5d). Compared with normal healthy controls and mild periodontitis (Stage I periodontitis), IL-6 and FCN1 were significantly upregulated in severe periodontitis (Fig. 5e , f). Moreover, IL-6 and FCN1 were in the MEgrey module and MEtan module, respectively (Fig. 5g , h), both of which were the gene modules most closely related to severe periodontitis.

Further, we calculated the DEGs between IL-6High and IL-6Low, the DEGs between FCN1High and FCN1Low groups in our self-test periodontal bulk RNA-seq data and performed GSEA analysis subsequently. Genes upregulated in samples with IL-6High showed enrichment of hypoxia signature (Fig. 5i). Genes upregulated in samples with FCN1High showed enrichment of ROS pathway (Fig. 5j). These results indicated that, at the bulk transcriptomic level, IL-6 and FCN1 were pivotal in modulating the inflammatory microenvironment of severe periodontitis by regulating the biological processes related to hypoxia, which was consistent with the results at the single-cell level.

Discussion

Severe periodontitis exerts broad pathogenic effects and is a public health problem [38]. Severe periodontitis can lead to premature tooth loss and is related to systemic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and etc [39]. However, the management of severe periodontitis remains a global challenge. Unlike mild periodontitis, severe periodontitis often cannot achieve good therapeutic effects through comprehensive NSPT, and there are still intra-bony pockets and alveolar bone defects remaining. Elucidating the pathogenesis of severe periodontitis could offer insights into advancing its treatment strategies. Despite extensive studies having been conducted on the pathogenesis of severe periodontitis over the years, its precise mechanism remains elusive.

Diverse cell types exhibit distinct responses in severe periodontitis. Consequently, scRNA-seq emerges as an effective approach for obtaining the transcriptome of each cell type within periodontal tissues. We revealed a marked increase in the proportion of fibroblasts in severe periodontitis compared to healthy, mild groups (Fig. 2a–c), suggesting fibroblasts may play a pivotal role in severe periodontitis.

Fibroblasts, traditionally considered “immune neutral”, are primarily known for extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis and remodeling. However, it’s now clear that fibroblasts play a multifaceted role in health and disease. Fibroblasts are currently considered to be a “non-classical” branch of the innate immune system. Increasing evidence supports the notion that in chronic infections and inflammatory conditions, tissue-resident fibroblasts have become a key cell type in regulating immune response [40, 41]. In periodontitis, abnormal activation of fibroblasts is closely related to inflammation and tissue destruction, possibly due to excessive recruitment of white blood cells, secretion of proteolytic enzymes, and induction of osteoclast formation [42]. Similarly, our study found that the fibroblasts in severe periodontitis were enriched in NF-κB signaling, IL-6 signaling, TNF targets, EGF signaling and other immune-related pathways (Fig. 2e), suggesting that fibroblasts were involved in the immune response of severe periodontitis.

Fibroblast heterogeneity is crucial in modulating the periodontal immune microenvironment. Recent scRNA-seq studies have revealed stromal cell heterogeneity in periodontitis to some extent. Caetano AJ et al. identified seven fibroblast subpopulations implicated in stromal remodeling in periodontitis, with the S6 subgroup, characterized by CD74 and AREG expression, exhibiting pro-inflammatory properties [10]. Another of their study identified a rare pathogenic fibroblast subset (characterized by the expression of RAC2, LCP1, and TNFRS11B) in periodontitis, which was engaged in neutrophil and lymphocyte recruitment and formation of blood progenitor cells. Such a fibroblast subset may contribute to the transition from innate to adaptive immunity, as well as the development of the persistent inflammation characteristic of periodontitis [11]. Williams DW et al. have highlighted the importance of the fibroblast–neutrophil axis in the periodontal immune environment. In the context of periodontitis, stromal cells, including fibroblasts, exhibited enhanced activity, thereby facilitating the recruitment and migration of immune cells to the lesion sites. These fibroblasts, along with other stromal cells, not only promote the specific recruitment of neutrophils, but also secrete chemokines that attract additional white blood cells, including lymphocytes [43]. Similarly, studies have found that in stage III and IV periodontitis, there are inflammatory-promoting fibroblast subclusters (CXCL13+ fibroblasts) with enhanced intercellular communication networks [16]. TNFRSF21+ fibroblast subsets might help activate the pro-inflammatory transcriptome in periodontal tissue [44]. Gingival fibroblasts had the most interactions with NETs associated neutrophil subsets, which might contribute to the production of NETs via the MIF-CD74/CXCR4 axis in periodontitis [13].

Our study systematically interpreted the heterogeneity and characteristics of fibroblasts in periodontitis. Periodontal fibroblasts were clustered into four distinct subgroups. Notably, the IL-6+ fibroblasts were markedly enriched in severe periodontitis as compared to those from healthy controls and mild periodontitis (Fig. 3d), indicating a pivotal role of IL-6+ fibroblasts in the pathophysiology of severe periodontitis. Similarly, Xing L et al. found that severe periodontitis induced pro-inflammatory immune response was characterized by profound elevation of fibroblast-derived IL-6 by using scRNA-seq analysis. Moreover, compared with endotheliocytes, epithelium, and immunocytes, fibroblasts were the only cell type that contributed to IL-6 elevation in severe periodontitis [45]. However, the scRNA-seq dataset selected for their study was GSE164241 [45], and the periodontal tissue samples in this dataset were from untreated patients with severe periodontitis [43]. In contrast, the scRNA-seq datasets used in our study, including both public datasets and self-test scRNA-seq data, comprised periodontal tissue samples from patients with severe periodontitis who still had intra-bony defects following NSPT. These findings indicated that NSPT failed to alleviate the enrichment of IL-6+ fibroblasts in severe periodontitis, and IL-6+ fibroblasts might represent a key cellular subset responsible for the persistent inflammation in severe periodontitis.

IL-6+ fibroblasts, characterized by high expression of IL-6, also expressed a spectrum of chemokines, including CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL1, CCL2, CXCL13 and CXCL8 (Supplementary Table 4). These chemokines confer IL-6+ fibroblasts with an inflammation-promoting phenotype, with enhanced immune response, includes response to molecule of bacterial origin (GO:0002237), cellular response to lipopolysaccharide (GO:0071222), leukocyte chemotaxis (GO:0030595), neutrophil chemotaxis (GO:0030593), neutrophil migration (GO:1990266) (Supplementary Table 5). GO analyses also showed that IL-6+ fibroblasts were enriched in Th17 type immune response (GO:0072538, IL6, SMAD7, ZC3H12A, NFKBIZ), regulation of macrophage activation (GO:0043030, includes IL-6, JUND, ZC3H12A, MIF, LDLR), macrophage chemotaxis and migration (GO:0048246 and GO:1905517, includes CCL2, RARRES2, MIF) (Supplementary Tables 5 and Fig. 3e). Additionally, our study found that osteoclast differentiation (GO:0030316), response to BMP (GO:0071772), bone remodeling (GO:0046849), regulation of bone resorption (GO:0045124), regulation of biomineral tissue development (GO:0070167) were enriched in IL-6+ fibroblasts (Fig. 3e). This was consistent with the results of previous in vitro cell experiments showing that fibroblasts were involved in alveolar bone remodeling [46, 47]. The present findings suggested that IL-6+ fibroblasts provided a pro-inflammatory microenvironment and might be involved in alveolar bone destruction in severe periodontitis. Previous studies have confirmed that pro-inflammatory fibroblasts express cytokines and chemokines, suggesting that they play an important role in attracting and maintaining immune cells, which may contribute to persistent inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and cancer [40]. The IL-6+ fibroblasts identified in our study may contribute to persistent inflammation in severe periodontitis.

In this study, CellPhoneDB analyses showed that the FCN1+ macrophage subtype was the most relevant cell type that interacted with the IL-6+ fibroblast subtype (Fig. 4d). Immunoinfiltration analysis and mIHC further implied there was potential crosstalk between IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages in severe periodontitis (Figs. 4j–l and 6h–i). FCN1+ macrophages enriched in severe periodontitis exhibited pro-inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 4g). Moreover, GO terms including response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979) and response to hypoxia (GO:0001666) were enriched in both IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages (Figs. 3e, 4g, Supplementary Table 5~6). Our results also showed that IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages were closely involved in the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Figs. 3f and 4h), which is the typical hypoxia-induced pathway. GSEA analysis of self-test bulk RNA-seq data revealed that genes upregulated in samples with IL-6High exhibited enrichment of hypoxia signature (Fig. 5i) and genes upregulated in samples with FCN1High showed enrichment of ROS pathway (Fig. 5j). Consequently, it is reasonable to hypothesize the presence of a fibroblast-mediated network localized in the hypoxic region of the severe periodontitis that links macrophages and fibroblasts, which collaborate to exacerbate the periodontal inflammation microenvironment. However, we need to acknowledge that this is only the result of bioinformatics analysis. In the future, we need to conduct laboratory experiments to verify the results.

Receptor-ligand analysis showed that IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages might cross-talk with each other through ligand-receptor pairs CXCL12-CXCR4 (Fig. 4i). CXCL12 is a member of the chemokine family. CXCR4 is considered to be the main receptor of CXCL12 [48, 49]. The expression of CXCL12 and CXCR4 was upregulated under hypoxia, tissue injury, which mediates inflammatory diseases [48]. One recent study showed a positive association between CXCR4 expression and periodontitis [50]. CXCL12 binds to CXCR4 and activates downstream signaling pathways, influencing the occurrence and development of osteoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and osteoporosis [48, 49]. In rheumatoid arthritis, CXCL12 promoted fibroblast-like synovial cell proliferation and synovial hyperplasia, and also participated in bone resorption by promoting osteoclast formation and protecting osteoclast apoptosis [51]. The CXCL12-CXCR4 axis played a pro-inflammatory role in an experimental temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis model [52]. Periodontitis is a typical osteoimmune disease, but research of the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis on periodontitis is still in preliminary stages. Studies have shown that CXCR4 was upregulated in periodontitis tissues and acted as a risk factor for periodontitis, influencing leukocyte transendothelial migration and osteoclast differentiation, and altering the immune microenvironment of periodontitis [53]. CXCR4 played a key role in regulating oxidative stress damage in periodontitis [54]. CXCL12, CXCR4 were key autophagy-related genes and were associated with altered immune infiltration in periodontitis [55]. Our study revealed the enhanced interaction of IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages through CXCL12-CXCR4, implying a potential treatment target in periodontitis.

Collectively, our study unveiled that IL-6+ fibroblasts provide pro-inflammatory microenvironment for severe periodontitis after NSPT. IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages might cross-talk with each other through CXCL12-CXCR4, thereby regulating biological processes such as the production of inflammatory factors, response to hypoxia and oxidative stress, osteoclast differentiation, and bone resorption. The presence of a fibroblast-mediated network localized in the hypoxic region of the severe periodontitis that links macrophages and fibroblasts, which collaborate to exacerbate the periodontal inflammation microenvironment. In the future, reprogramming IL-6+ fibroblasts to differentiate into periodontal tissue regenerative cells might be considered as a way to treat severe periodontitis. Despite the above findings, the present study still has some limitations. Firstly, the sample size of the public scRNA-seq data was relatively small. Therefore, we used self-test scRNA-seq data, bulk-seq data and mIHC data to conduct multidimensional verification. Although the number of our self-test scRNA-seq samples was also limited, their role as an independent validation of public data enhanced the reproducibility of the research findings. Meanwhile, the concordance among the four data modalities (public scRNA-seq data, self-test scRNA-seq data, bulk-seq RNA data, and mIHC data) provided strong support for our research results. Further studies with a larger sample size could reduce the bias caused by individual heterogeneity. Secondly, scRNA-seq data cannot reflect the spatial and temporal distribution of cells and genes; future studies may expand spatial validation (e.g., performing spatial transcriptomics in larger cohorts). Spatial transcriptomics enables the identification of the spatial distribution of distinct cell clusters as well as their unique gene expression profiles. Specifically, if IL-6⁺ fibroblasts and FCN1⁺ macrophages are observed to be spatially adjacent in clusters, coupled with high gene expression levels in specific interaction pathways, their crosstalk can be validated. Thirdly, the ligand-receptor interaction was only predicted by CellPhoneDB. Therefore, further laboratory experiments are needed to validate the regulons and cell–cell interactions. The indirect co-culture study through the Transwell system will be used to validate the crosstalk of IL-6⁺ fibroblasts and FCN1⁺ macrophages. The pathway-specific antagonists will be employed (e.g., AMD3100 for the CXCL12-CXCR4 pathway) to validate the crosstalk pathway.

Conclusions

Fibroblasts are key contributors to chronic inflammation in severe periodontitis. Pathogenic fibroblasts not only fail to maintain the structure of periodontal tissues but also secrete chemokines to recruit immune cells, thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses and perpetuating periodontal tissue damage. Traditional approaches, such as NSPT, usually cannot address the core issue of excessive fibroblast activation in severe periodontitis. Our study identified that the hypoxic microenvironment orchestrated by IL-6+ fibroblasts was closely associated with the persistent inflammation in severe periodontitis after NSPT. JUND was identified as the master TF driving the differentiation pathway of IL-6+ fibroblasts. IL-6+ fibroblasts and FCN1+ macrophages might crosstalk via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, contributing to sustained inflammation, hypoxia/oxidative stress responses, osteoclast differentiation, and bone resorption in severe periodontitis after NSPT. These findings provide a novel perspective for targeted therapies in severe periodontitis, particularly those targeting IL-6⁺ fibroblasts. However, it should be noted that the relatively small sample size of this study may limit the generalizability of our results. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to reduce biases from individual heterogeneity, and in vitro/in vivo experiments should validate these findings.
