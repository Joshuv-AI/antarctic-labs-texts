Protocol to discover terrain-precipitation relationships with interpretable artificial intelligence
Authors: Hao Xu, Yuntian Chen, Zhenzhong Zeng, Nina Li, Jian Li, Dongxiao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe relationship between terrain and precipitation is a crucial aspect of climate science. Here, we describe a protocol for uncovering the underlying laws governing this relationship via interpretable artificial intelligence. We describe the steps for preparing datasets, constructing algorithms, and discovering explicit equations. We then detail the construction of an iterative optimization of knowledge generation and utilization for the projection of future precipitation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Xu et al.1

Before you begin

Background

Understanding the intricate relationship between terrain and precipitation is pivotal for addressing contemporary challenges in climate science, particularly under the influence of global climate change. While a growing body of literature has explored the terrain-precipitation relationship, existing approaches generally fall into two categories. On one hand, empirical regression models, such as multilinear regression and geographically weighted regression (GWR) offer interpretable equations based on predefined variables.2,3 However, these models often rely on expert-specified terms and linear formulations, which limits their capacity to capture complex nonlinear interactions in diverse terrains.4,5 On the other hand, AI-based models, including deep neural networks and generative architectures, have achieved impressive predictive performance by learning from large-scale data.6,7 Yet, these models typically operate as black boxes, lacking transparency and interpretability, and thus offer limited insight into the physical mechanisms linking terrain and precipitation.

Innovation

The shortcomings of existing methods necessitate the development of a robust, interpretable framework to uncover and project terrain-precipitation relationships with precision and clarity. Therefore, we recently propose an AI-driven framework combining genetic algorithm and geographically weighted regression. By integrating the optimization capabilities of genetic algorithms with the spatial adaptability of geographic regression, this framework provides a means to discover explicit, interpretable equations governing the terrain-precipitation relationship. These equations can not only enhance the understanding of the physical mechanisms driving precipitation but also adapt dynamically to shifting climatic patterns.

This protocol provides step-by-step instructions for discovering the terrain-precipitation relationships with interpretable artificial intelligence, including (1) preprocessing multi-source datasets by computing spatial gradients and applying normalization; (2) applying a knowledge discovery algorithm to discover explicit terrain-precipitation equations; (3) incorporating the discovered equations into a physics-informed neural network to improve model interpretability and performance; (4) using the trained model to project future precipitation under climate scenarios. This facilitates the knowledge discovery in climate science and other disciplines, and offers insights for managing the impacts of climate change on precipitation and related systems. This section details the process for discovering optimal governing equations of terrain-precipitation relationship using an AI-driven knowledge discovery framework, which combines the genetic algorithm and geographically weighted regression. This framework can search for the best-fitting equation by iterating through generations of candidate equations. For Steps 4–8, we detail the construction of the GA-GWR framework for unveiling new explicit equations that describe the intricate relationship between terrain variables and precipitation patterns. For the terrain-average annual precipitation relationship, the discovered governing equation is:(Equation 8)P=ω1H+ω2R+ω3cos(α)Hy+ω4RβHx+b,where P, H, R, α, and β are the average annual precipitation, altitude, relief, slope, and aspect, respectively; Hx and Hy are the altitude gradient between grids in the longitude and latitude directions, respectively. For the terrain-annual precipitation relationship, the discovered governing equation is:(Equation 9)P(t)=ω1(t)H+ω2(t)R+ω3(t)cos(α)Hy+ω4(t)RβHx+b(t),where P(t) is the annual precipitation varying with year t; the coefficients ω(t) and b(t) are both spatial-temporal fields for year t. This equation is a time-varying equation, where the coefficient field changes across years. The outcomes of this protocol aim to bridge the gap between data-driven AI models and physically grounded understanding by discovering and embedding explicit terrain-precipitation equations. Compared to conventional black-box approaches, this method offers improved interpretability and scientific traceability. The integration of symbolic equations into physics-informed neural networks not only enhances interpretability and accuracy, but also enables spatially explicit and physically consistent projections under future climate scenarios. These advances contribute to more trustworthy climate insights, particularly in complex terrain where current predictive methods often fall short.

Limitations

In this protocol, a closed-loop of knowledge discovery and knowledge embedding is constructed for discovering the underlying relationship between terrain and precipitation and making future precipitation projections under different climate scenarios. This section outlines the potential limitations of the protocol, highlighting areas where improvements or considerations may be necessary for broader applicability and enhanced accuracy.

The protocol primarily focuses on terrain and climate data within the Asian region. When extending its application to other geographic areas, several important considerations arise. First, data quality and availability vary substantially across regions, particularly in remote or under-resourced areas where observational networks are sparse and historical records may be incomplete. This heterogeneity can lead to increased uncertainty in discovered equations and reduced robustness of downstream predictions. Second, topographic complexity, such as the presence of highly irregular landforms or abrupt elevation gradients, may affect the stability of geographically weighted regression. To address these challenges, region-specific data harmonization procedures will be required, including bias correction, spatial interpolation, and uncertainty quantification. Moreover, the symbolic regression framework may need to incorporate additional terrain descriptors to enhance the generalizability of the protocol.

This protocol relies on mesoscale terrain, precipitation, and climate data. When these data are averaged over grid points, the effects of small-scale terrain features are often smoothed out, potentially introducing errors into the analysis. Meanwhile, the data resolution used in this protocol is fixed. Although the method can be applied to datasets with different resolutions, changes in resolution may affect the accuracy of equation discovery and the quality of results.

In the equation discovery process, the protocol represents equations as genomes composed of gene modules and basic genes. While this approach can identify interaction terms that may be difficult for humans to discover, the forms of equations that can be discovered are inherently limited. Specifically, the method cannot identify equations with fractions or complex forms, which may restrict its ability to fully capture non-linear or scale-dependent dynamics. To enhance the flexibility and expressive power of the discovered equations, future extensions could incorporate more advanced symbolic regression approaches, such as context-free grammar-based methods and tree-based genetic programming,17,18 which can accommodate a broader range of functional forms. Integrating such methods would allow the protocol to discover a more flexible and accurate equation. Notably, increasing the expressive flexibility of the equation space inevitably expands the search space and may significantly raise the computational burden and risk of overfitting. Therefore, balancing equation complexity with search efficiency will be a critical consideration in future methodological extensions. This trade-off may be addressed through the incorporation of domain-specific priors and complexity-penalized fitness functions, ensuring that the discovered equations remain both accuracy and parsimony.

For knowledge embedding, the training speed of PINN is inherently slow, particularly when dealing with large datasets. This limitation restricts the protocol’s ability to efficiently process large-scale meteorological data. Improving the computational efficiency of PINNs by balancing the contributions of data and knowledge19 or using alternative knowledge-embedding methods may help alleviate this constraint.

For future predictions, future precipitation predictions in this protocol require 23 climate variables to predict the coefficient field of the equation. If any of these climate variable values are missing or incomplete, it could significantly reduce the accuracy of the predictions. Robust imputation methods or alternative prediction strategies may be necessary to address situations where climate variable data are incomplete.

Problem 6

The protocol relies on 23 climate variables to predict the coefficients of the discovered equation. However, in operational or data-poor settings, some of these variables may be unavailable due to limitations in observational coverage or data access. This could potentially hinder the use of the model in such environments (related to Step 9c).

Potential solution

When a small number of input variables are missing, a practical strategy is to replace them with their long-term spatial or temporal mean values computed from the available training dataset. In our tests, this imputation approach introduces only marginal loss in prediction accuracy, as the learned mapping from climate variables to equation coefficients is relatively robust to minor perturbations in input values, particularly for variables with low importance in the model (as revealed by SHAP analysis).

Problem 7

The computational requirements for training the physics-informed neural network, particularly when applied to high-resolution spatial data over multiple years, can be restrictive for users without access to high-performance computing resources. Full training over the entire spatial domain may result in long runtimes or memory limitations on CPU-based environments (related to Step 9d).

Potential solution

To reduce computational cost, a collocation point strategy can be adopted. Instead of using all spatial grid points during each training iteration, a fixed number of points (e.g., 1000–5000) are randomly sampled from the spatial domain in each epoch. The physical loss is then computed only on these sampled points, serving as a representative approximation of the full-domain loss. Over multiple epochs, this stochastic approximation converges to the global behavior while significantly lowering memory usage and computation time.

Problem 8

The loss continues to decline at the end of training (related to Step 9d).

Potential solution

This may indicate insufficient epochs (e.g., maximum epochs set too low) or an overly small learning rate, potentially leaving the model under-converged and harming its generalizability. Therefore, we should increase the maximum epochs or the learning rate, and retrain the PINN.
