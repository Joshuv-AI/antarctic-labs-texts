Clinical Use of Combined First‐Trimester Screening Test for Aneuploidy in A Low‐Income Setting: Retrospective Study
Authors: Tesfaye Diress, Mekitie Wondafrash, Wondimu Gudu, Abdulfetah Abdulkadir Abdosh, Abraham Fessehaye Sium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough combined first trimester screening is effective at detecting fetal chromosomal anomalies which are trisomy 21, 18 and 13, there is scarce data on its practice and practice in low‐income countries. This study presents data on clinical use of combined first trimester prenatal screening tests among pregnant women in a Sub‐Saharan setting.MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted over 7 years between July 2016 and June 2023 at St. Paul's Hospital Millennium Medical College (Ethiopia). Risk assessment was done with Snibe 2000 software using maternal age, nuchal translucency, PAPP‐A, β‐hCG and other maternal characteristics. Statistical analysis was done using SPSS version 22. Fishers exact and Man Whitney U test were done as appropriate, and P‐value less than 0.05 was used to present significance of the results.ResultsA total of 141 patients were included in the final analysis. Overall high‐risk cases for aneuploidy based on the combined test was 24.8%. Screening positive for aneuploidy had an association with increased nuchal translucency [median (IQR) = 2.1 (1.7, 2.5) for screening negative vs 2.9 (2.4, 2.9) for screening positive test, p‐value = 0.021] and low PAPP‐A values ≤ 0.05 MoM (1 (5%) for negative and 5 (56%) for positive, p‐value = 0.005).ConclusionThe prevalence of screening positive for high‐risk aneuploidy in this study was higher than in previous reports which may be due to the fact that the study population is primary high‐risk women. Nuchal translucency and low PAPP‐A values were associated with screening positive for aneuploidy. Combined first trimester screening is feasible and effective for detecting fetal chromosomal anomalies in low‐income settings. Laboratory capacity for performing serum analytes and genetic testing, and availability of nuchal translucency ultrasound services should acted up on for creating a wider access to such vital prenatal genetic screening and testing services.Keywords: aneuploidy, combined test, Ethiopia, first trimester, low‐income, prenatal, sub‐Saharan

Introduction

Aneuploidy poses significant morbidity and mortality to the child and social and economic constraints to the family [1]. Non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for fetal aneuploidy detection is increasingly being offered in the clinical setting [2]. A number of professional societies such as the International Society for Prenatal Diagnosis (ISPD), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) have issued position statements on the use of NIPT for the detection of trisomy 21, 18, and 13 [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. NIPT with cffDNA has a high sensitivity and specificity for detection of aneuploidy. Its applicability is limited particularly in developing nations due to the significant cost incurred [8]. First trimester screening for aneuploidy and structural abnormalities is shown to be effective in most settings [9]. Isolated increment in nuchal translucency (3–3.5 mm) mandates chromosomal microarray analysis [10]. Combination maternal serum markers of free β subunit of hCG, pregnancy‐associated plasma protein A and maternal age uniformly demonstrate a sensitivity for Down syndrome of approximately 60% with a 5% false positive rate [11]. These first trimester serum markers are largely independent of nuchal translucency, which has resulted in the development of a combined serum and sonographic screening protocol that would be more effective for screening than either component alone. Combined first‐trimester screening helps to detect 90%, 97%, 92% of trisomy 21, 18, and 13 respectively [12].

First‐trimester screening should be integrated in local prenatal care guidelines. Population based studies including the four risk parameters (maternal age, NT, PAPP‐A, B‐hCG) shown to increase detection of aneuploidy from 4.4% to 4.8% [13]. Combining NT with other sonographic markers of aneuploidy (nasal bone, Ductus venosus) improves the detection of aneuploidy in a setting where serum marker testing not easily available [14, 15].

Though combined first‐trimester aneuploidy screening is widely practiced in the developed world, it is underutilized in low‐income settings like ours mainly due to lack of resources (experts and laboratory set‐up) at public health facilities and unaffordable cost of the tests in private settings. A recently published study from Ethiopia underscores the importance of diagnostic prenatal testing in a Sub‐Saharan Africa setting [16]. This study presents one of the first evidences on the use of prenatal combined first trimester tests for aneuploidy screening from the sub‐Saharan setting (low‐income setting).

Methods and Materials

Study Design, Study Period, Study Population, and Study Setting

This retrospective cross‐sectional study was conducted on 141 high‐risk pregnant women who undergone screening with the combined first‐trimester screening test over 7 years (from July 2016 to June 2023) at St. Paul Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC). The Maternal fetal medicine unit at this hospital offers a wide variety of services including prenatal care up for high‐risk pregnancies, one out of which is comprehensive first and second‐trimester anatomic scanning, diagnostic procedures like amniocentesis and therapeutic procedures like intrauterine transfusion. We operationally defined the key terms mentioned in this study as follows:

First‐trimester combined screening: measurement of nuchal translucency, pregnancy‐associated plasma protein A [PAPP‐A], and the free beta subunit of human chorionic gonadotropin (β‐hcg) at 10 weeks 1 day through 13 weeks 6 days of gestation.

High risk cases: cases with software‐generated a likelihood ratio greater than 1 in 300, we adopted this as a standard of the fetal medicine foundation

Low risk cases: cases with a likelihood ratio of less than 1 in 300.

Positive cases: high risk cases who tested trisomy 21, 18, and 13 on genetic testing

Pregnant women (primarily high‐risk) who had combined first‐trimester screening were included in the study. The screening test (both ultrasound NIPT and serum bio‐markers‐serum analytes) was done at gestational age of 11 to 13 + 6 weeks. Exclusion criteria were incomplete documentation and second trimester Quad or triple tests. We also excluded twin and higher order pregnancies because of difficulty in standardization of the serum biomarkers results for multiple pregnancies. Primary outcome of this study was proportion of women who screened positive for aneuploidy upon undergoing combined first‐trimester screening and factors associated with positive screening results. Secondary outcome was the percentage of testing positive for aneuploidy among those women who tested positive for the combined screening test and had prenatal amniotic genetic testing. Genetic amniocentesis was done at 16–18 weeks with 18–20 mL of amniotic fluid sample for genetic testing. Genetic testing was based on risk and we used both FISH and karyotype for genetic testing. For those tested positive cases, an option of termination of the pregnancy and further genetic counselling was provided but we did not collect data on pregnancy outcomes such as abortion, still birth, or live birth as women continued their prenatal care and delivery services at different institutions following screening at our center.

Data Collection

Data on socio‐demographic characteristics, obstetric factors and screening parameters was collected retrospectively using data extraction sheet. The questionnaire forms were checked for completeness and consistency by the principal investigator before data entered to SPSS version 22. Cases for screening were selected based high‐risk status for aneuploidy like women having increased nuchal translucency, older age greater than 35 years, IVF pregnancy, and previous bad obstetric performance. Ultrasound assessment of nuchal translucency done between gestational age of 11 to 13 + 6 weeks. Biochemical markers like pregnancy‐associated plasma protein‐ A(PAPP‐A) and β‐human chorionic gonadotropin(β‐hCG) was performed from maternal blood between gestational age of 11 to 13 + 6 weeks at LABCO advanced laboratory. Risk of aneuploidy was calculated using SNIBE 2000 software by entering dual serum markers (PAPP‐A and β‐hCG), NT and maternal characteristics with a cut‐off point of 1:300. Around 10 ml of amniotic fluid sample was taken and subjected for cytogenetic analysis. Genetic test was performed at MRC‐ET advanced diagnostic laboratory for those cases considered high risk.

Sample Size and Data Management

No sample size formula was applied. All consecutive samples based on inclusion and exclusion criteria during study period were included in the study.

The data was entered into SPSS version 22 software packages for analysis. Simple descriptive analysis of baseline characteristics was done. Fishers exact test for categorical variables and Man‐Whitney U test for continuous variables was used to see association of dependent and independent variables with p‐value < 0.5 is considered significant.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance was received from IRB of SPHMMC. Confidentiality and data anonymity was maintained throughout the data collection. The requirement for obtaining informed consent from patients was waived by this ethics committee.

Results

A total of 183 high‐risk women were screened for eligibility and 141 of them were included in the final analysis, after excluding 43 cases due to incomplete data. The mean age of women is 31.5 years with range of 20 to 45 years with SD of 4.891 years (Table 1). The screening was done at gestational age of 11 to 11 + 6 weeks in 27.7%, 12 to 12 + 6 weeks in 39% and 13 to 13 + 6 weeks in 33.3% of pregnant women who had the combined first trimester screening.

Those cases who tested positive for aneuploidy have increased nuchal translucency with median of 2.9 mm (2.4,2.9) as compared to high‐risk cases who tested negative with median of 2.1 mm (1.7,2.5).

This study did not find a statistically significant association between maternal age and serum free β‐hCG level in MoM. This could be due to potential influence of ethnicity and selection bias in our study (only patients with risk were screened).

Discussion

In the present study, the prevalence of screening positive for high‐risk aneuploidy is higher than in previous reports which could be due to patient selection bias as primarily high‐risk women were included in the study. Nuchal translucency and low PAPP‐A values were associated with screening positive for aneuploidy. However, one in seven among those screened positive and counseled for genetic testing had no prenatal amniotic genetic testing.

Non‐invasive prenatal screening using combined first‐trimester screening tests, is not a standard of care globally, especially in low‐income countries such as the Sub‐Saharan African countries. Even in some centers where it is available accessibility by pregnant women is variable. This is mainly due to limited resources (both human and financial) and bandwidth available to each institution [17]. For example in Africa, the too few studies available on this topic were conducted in South Africa and the evidence from these studies show that despite access to the different screening tools [18], a recent survey has shown that still only one in three cases of trisomy 21 is diagnosed prenatally in private practice in South Africa [19]. Many of the options are too expensive or otherwise inaccessible to the average patient [20].

Studies from the developed world show that combined first‐trimester screening methods using maternal serum markers (B‐hCG and PAPP‐A) and sonographic marker of nuchal translucency (NT) enables to identify 90% of aneuploidies with a false positive rate of 5% [21, 22, 23]. In our study, 141 pregnant women who had combined first‐trimester aneuploidy screening were found from data retrieved from July 2016 to July 2023. Among this, 35 cases (24.8%) were found to be high risk for aneuploidy with the cut‐off value of 1:300. The overall positive screening result of 35 (24.8%) is much higher than in a similar 3 years retrospective study done in India which found a 2.58% high‐risk rate [24] and another study done in US in which found of 452,901 women screened that found 17,435 (3.8%) screen‐positive for Down syndrome only; 433 (0.1%) for trisomy 18 only; 1,689 (0.4%) for both Down syndrome and trisomy 18 [25]. These large difference between our results and findings of these previous studies can be explained by the selection bias in our study – because it was not universal screening rather women who had risk were screened. The other explanation is the cut of level we used to declare screen‐positive, which is cut‐off 1:300, instead of 1: 250.

Among the interesting findings of our research is the significant association of positive test for aneuploidy with nuchal translucency [median (IQR) = 2.1 (1.7, 2.5) for screening negative vs 2.9 (2.4, 2.9) for screening positive test, p‐value = 0.021] and low PAPP‐A values ≤ 0.05 MoM (1 (5%) for negative and 5 (56%) for positive, p‐value = 0.005). This is consistent with the findings of previous studies. Among 23 805 pregnancies who had Fetal nuchal translucency thickness successfully measured in Germany, Austria and Switzerland the estimated risk for trisomy 21 based on maternal age and fetal nuchal translucency thickness for crown–rump length was 1 in 300 or greater in 13.0% (2800 of 21 475) normal pregnancies, in 87.6% (184 of 210) of those with trisomy 21 and in 87.2% (239 of 274) with other chromosomal defects [26]. In another large study done in UK, in which 96127 pregnancies were included found using NST and maternal age for invasive testing by this method allows the detection of about 80% of affected pregnancies [27]. Similarly, a study done in US, the fetal karyotype was abnormal in 2,168 (19.2%) out of 11,315 pregnancies. In the study, the incidence of chromosomal defects increased with nuchal translucency thickness from approximately 7% for those with nuchal translucency between the 95th centile for crown‐rump length and 3.4 mm to 75% for nuchal translucency of 8.5 mm or more. In majority of fetuses with trisomy 21, the nuchal translucency thickness was less than 4.5 mm, whereas in the majority of fetuses with trisomy 13 or 18 it was 4.5–8.4 mm, indicating The distribution of nuchal translucency is different for each type of chromosomal defect [28]. A study done in Thailand to evaluate the effectiveness of first‐trimester genetic sonogram (NT) for detection of aneuploidy showed 55% detection with false positive rate of 5% [29].

Conducted in a low‐income African setting, findings of our study support previous evidence on the importance and effectiveness of combined first‐trimester screening for prenatal detection of aneuploidy. Most importantly it demonstrates the practicality of this prenatal tests in such a low‐income setting and the possibility of establishing and advancing maternal‐fetal medicine care, which is key in solving this long‐standing puzzle of unavailability of these tests and unaffordability of it by patients if it exists at all in private health facilities. However, the finding of high screening positivity need to be carefully interpreted due to the section bias introduced in our study.

Strengths of our study include being the first study on using combined first‐trimester prenatal screening for detection of aneuploidy from the Sub‐Saharan setting. The main limitations of our study are selection bias, small sample size, and lack of confirmatory testing in all screen‐positive cases. Patient selection bias (primarily high‐risk women were included in our study) limits the generalizability of the results of this study. Some clinical data are incomplete, particularly the lack of information on pregnancy outcomes (births, miscarriages, termination decisions). Lack of this data further limits the conclusion of this study. Adding further muti‐variate regression analysis could have given the results of our study, hence it be noted as the other limitation of this study.

In summary, findings of our study in general demonstrate that combined first‐trimester screening for the detection of aneuploidy prenatally is feasible and effective in a Sub‐Saharan setting. The high prevalence of screening positive for high‐risk aneuploidy indicates patient selection bias (primarily high‐risk women were included in the analysis), which limits the generalizability of the results. The issue of affordability should be given adequate emphasis by policy makers by acting on availing such services in public health institutions. Further research should be conducted with a larger number of patients that could generate stronger results.
