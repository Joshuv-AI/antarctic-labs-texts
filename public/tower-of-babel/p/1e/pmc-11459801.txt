Optimizing modern surgical simulation through instructor feedback – insights from a retrospective observational study in a tertiary hospital
Authors: Yu-San Tee, Chi-Tung Cheng, Pei-Hua Li, Ming-Ju Hsieh, Chien-Hung Liao, Chih-Yuan Fu
Published: 2024

Background

Since the 1970s, laparoscopic surgery has been a standard procedure for patients who require abdominal surgery [1, 2]. However, training in this process can be challenging for surgeons. Learning the techniques associated with laparoscopy requires extensive training, is time consuming and may raise concerns regarding patient safety. At present, traditional, one-on-one instruction may not be applicable in this context due to stricter work time regulations and stronger concerns with patient safety [3–5].

To address this dilemma, simulation-based training (SBT) has increasingly been used in the medical field and has become an essential component of the early stages of the process of acquiring skills pertaining to laparoscopic surgery [6–10]. A simulator is designed to allow trainees to operate in a virtual environment that mimics real-life work and scenarios. This approach has been used in several different fields, such as piloting, well drilling, car driving, and scientific research. Laparoscopic simulators were introduced in the early 2000s [7, 11, 12] to support minimally invasive surgery training. Technological advances have allowed such laparoscopic simulators to provide a safe, ethical and repeatable alternative to real-world practice by offering surgeons the opportunity to mimic real-world surgeries and offering educators the opportunity not only to share their clinical experience but also to provide objective assessments of trainees’ performance.

While the use of SBT in surgical residency is increasing, there is insufficient evidence about the ability of a simulator to assess the surgical skills of trainees. As effective assessment is important to ensure skill acquisition, questions remain about the LapSim simulator’s ability to provide reliable evaluations of surgical performance. Additionally, the evolving role of instructor in the simulator training era is unclear. Hence, the aim of our study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a laparoscopic simulator to assess surgical residency performance in a tertiary center and to identify the status of the instructor’s current role in SBT through individual trainee’s feedback and questionnaires. By addressing the gaps, we hope to provide insights into the use of modern simulators not only as tools but also as potential assessment instruments in surgical education.

Materials and methods

A retrospective study was carried out from 23rd August 2021 until 15th November 2021. First- to fifth-year surgical residents of the Department of Surgery of Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital were included in the study. The participants were categorized into two groups: the “junior” group consisted of first and second-year surgical residents, while the “senior” group consisted of third- to fifth-year surgical residents.

The LapSim (Surgical Science Ltd, Gothenburg, Sweden) laparoscopic simulator was used in the study [7, 13, 14]. All enrolled residents completed a designed training program, which consisted of a 1-hour orientation and a 4-hour simulator training session. After the simulator training program had been completed, posttraining feedback was provided by one instructor to each trainee. Trainees were randomly assigned by and received feedback from three qualified surgeons who had performed at least 200 laparoscopic surgeries each. After receiving the posttraining feedback, each junior trainee was required to complete a basic skill task, i.e., pattern cutting [15], and each senior trainee was required to perform a complete laparoscopic appendectomy [16], on the LapSim simulator.

The first performance (LapSim score 1) was assessed by LapSim parameters (Supplementary Table 1), which are grouped according to the Global Operative Assessment of Laparoscopic Skills (GOALS) rating scale [17]. The GOALS (Supplementary Fig. 1) was observed and recorded by an instructor during the first performance. These scoring systems that were applied during the first performance were compared later to evaluate the compatibility between the laparoscopic simulator and instructor assessments.

The trainees received feedback from the instructor after the first performance and were required to repeat the procedure after the feedback. A second LapSim score (LapSim score 2) was recorded. The one-to-one feedback was informally structured, focusing on the weakness on trainees’ weakness during LapSim training.

Descriptive statistics were calculated, whereas frequency tables were generated for categorical variables. LapSim score 1 and LapSim score 2 are presented as the median and interquartile range (25–75%). The Mann–Whitney U test was used for nonnormally distributed data. The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used for paired comparison of prefeedback and postfeedback LapSim scores. A significance level of α = 0.05 was used. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Microsoft Excel (V16.19) was used for data entry and to create associated figures.

Results

The study design is shown in Fig. 1. A total of fifty-eight surgical residents were trained in the surgical department of LinKou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. Twenty-six trainees were excluded due to resignation, personal reasons, or incomplete feedback after the training programs. After exclusion, thirty-two surgical residents were enrolled. Table 1 shows the demographics of the residents. Seventeen junior residents and fifteen senior residents completed the training program and posttraining assessments and received the instructor feedback. The median LapSim Score 1 was 86.0 (IQR 54.25, 90.0). GOALS was also recorded by the instructors during the first performances. The median GOALS for the first performances was 20.0 (IQR 16.0, 22.0). After the instructor feedbacks, median LapSim score 2 improved modestly to 88.0 (IQR 77.25, 97.0).

Trainees’ performance after receiving instructor feedback was recorded (LapSim score 2). The junior group exhibited a modest improvement of less than 10% in terms of the median LapSim score, which increased from 90.0 to 97.0. In contrast, the senior group exhibited an improvement of nearly 50% in their median LapSim score, which increased from 52.0 (IQR 42.5, 70.5) to 77.0 (IQR 57.0, 83.5).

The median procedure times before and after the feedback was received are illustrated in Fig. 2. Overall, the median procedure time decreased from 234.4s (IQR 138.9s, 578.9s) before the instructor feedback was received to 199.2s (IQR 147.3s, 449.4s) afterward. While subgroup analysis revealed that similar amounts of time were spent before and after the feedback was received by the junior group (from 140.9s to 148.3s), the amount of time spent by the senior group was reduced by nearly 40% after the feedback was received (from 762.5s to 464.2s).

To further investigate the capability of the simulator to assess surgical skills, the correlations between the simulator-based (LapSim score 1) and instructor-based assessments are illustrated in Table 3. Overall, GOALS was weakly correlated with LapSim score 1 (adjusted R2 = 0.149, p = 0.017). Notably, the subgroup analysis showed that the junior group had a higher correlation between simulator-based and instructor-based scores (Junior GOALS adjusted R2 = 0.285, p = 0.016). This result was not observed in the senior group.

A post-training questionnaire was completed by all participating trainees and the results are summarized in Supplementary File 1. Overall, the trainees expressed high satisfaction with the SBT, with a mean satisfaction 9.3/10. Furthermore, 96.4% of trainees demonstrated a willingness to use modern surgical simulators as part of their training. Additionally, 75% of residents believed that the SBT could potentially reduce training time on real patients, 83.6% felt it could decrease clinical errors, 89.1% reported it could smoothen learning curves, and improve their future surgical performances. All participants agreed that three was good interaction between them and the instructor, and 98.2% of trainees felt that instructor provided appropriate feedback during their sessions on the LapSim.

Discussion

The 100-year-old Halstedian apprenticeship model – “see one, do one, teach one” [18] – is becoming less applicable due to increasingly strict work time regulations and increasingly notable concerns regarding patient safety. While some individuals have attempted to deviate, low self-confidence resulting from lower levels of training and experience has come to represent a practical dilemma for surgical residency training worldwide. Hence, simulation-based training (SBT) has increasingly been used as an effective tool in surgeon training [6, 7, 19]. This approach has been applied in many different disciplines and fields, such as piloting and well drilling. In the surgical field, SBT provides a safe and controlled environment in which residents can acquire the necessary surgical knowledge and skills pertaining to laparoscopic surgery while avoiding the risks to patient safety and long learning curves associated with surgeon training [20–23].

In addition to improvements in haptic feedback, the metrics and parameters in modern surgical simulators have been well designed to provide feedback and meet training requirements. The metrics of the simulator used in the current study were established based on GOALS. However, the low overall correlation (adjusted R2 = 0.149) between LapSim scores and instructor GOALS indicated a potential discrepancy between the instructor’s and simulator’s assessments. This discrepancy may be attributed to the instructor’s subjectivity, the device’s settings, and complexity of the procedure. Further subgroup analysis revealed that a significantly higher correlation between simulator’s metrics and instructor’s assessments in junior residents, showing its potential in assessing young residents’ surgical performance. Nevertheless, it is important to note that our evaluation primarily focused on correlations between instructor scores and automated LapSim metrics, which represent only one aspect of validity evidence. As procedures became longer and more complex, the simulator’s capacity to assess the performance of senior residents appeared to decrease. These observations highlight the need for a more comprehensive approach to validity assessment in future studies, including integration of frameworks such as Kane’s or Messick’s to better understand the strengths and limitation of simulator-based assessments.

Currently, the role of instructors in surgical SBT is less well defined. In addition, the quantity and quality of SBT in medical education is limited [24–26]. A randomized study performed by Stranbygaard et al. found that instructor feedback increases efficiency in a complex task on a virtual reality simulator [27]. Moreover, a randomized trial by Snyder et al. found that instructor feedback is less advantageous in basic laparoscopic task learning and suggested that self-directed practice was more important than expert instruction to achieve proficiency [28].

In this training program, each resident received individual feedback from one of the three qualified surgeons after assessing their actual performances on the LapSim simulator (Fig. 3A and B). Overall, the participants’ performances improved following instructor feedbacks. Notably, this feedback was found to be more beneficial for a senior resident proceeding with a more complex procedure (complete laparoscopic appendectomy) than for a junior resident performing a basic task (pattern cutting). Based on these findings, we believe a self-directed SBT can be effective for junior residents who are learning basic laparoscopic techniques such as depth perception and bimanual dexterity. However, instructor feedback remained a key component in more complex laparoscopic surgeries.

Instructor feedback is not only a way to pass on their experiences and the details of the procedure but to also convey the surgical concepts to the young surgeon, highlighting the importance of instructor feedback in the later stage of surgical training. Besides, the role of the actual instructor is also important in designing a step-by-step simulation-based training (SBT) curriculum tailored to the residents’ experience, and provides a more comprehensive assessment. While simulators offer a safe, controlled, and ethical training environment, they lack the ability to evaluate the sequence of actual (and complicated) surgical steps, which is important in real-world surgery. In addition, instructors can offer valuable insights based on their own experiences, including managing emotions during surgery, handling complications and variations commonly encountered in actual surgeries—situations that simulators cannot fully replicate. Therefore, the presence of an instructor enriches the learning by bridging the gap between standardized simulator assessments and the complex realities of surgical procedures.

The post-training questionnaire in the study showed that modern SBT is widely accepted by the participating trainees. Nearly 90% of the residents believed they need at least ten actual experiences before they can independently perform a laparoscopic surgery, and more than 40% believed they would need over 20 surgeries to fully acquire the necessary skills. This is challenging to achieve in the current clinical environment, emphasizing the importance of SBT in modern training. Additionally, the presence of a surgical instructor was shown to benefit training outcomes, as evidenced by the high satisfaction rates among trainees regarding the instructor’s involvement (Supplementary File 1, Q6).

It is important to realize that the current study was limited by its retrospective nature and small number of participants. Different tasks were performed by the participants, as senior residents were assumed to be familiar with basic skills after two to three years of surgical training. In addition, the effect of the timing and frequency of the feedback on the surgical performance was not evaluated in the current design, and there was also confounding factors introduced by repeated exposure to simulation versus instructor feedback, without control group lacking instructor feedback. A potential ceiling effect was also noted, particularly in the junior group, as their performance on the basic tasks was already high prior to feedback, which may have limited the impact of feedback. However, to our knowledge, no other studies have directly addressed the role of the simulator or instructor feedback at different stages of surgical training.

Conclusion

To avoid any ethical concerns, a well-designed step-by-step SBT can be a valuable tool in laparoscopic surgical training. Simulators can be advantageous in the early stages of surgical training. In later stages of training, instructors should provide intuitive feedback to ensure correct learning.
