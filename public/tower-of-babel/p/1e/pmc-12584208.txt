Parameter-driven habitat imaging based on intravoxel incoherent motion MRI for preoperative prediction of muscle invasion in bladder cancer
Authors: Junting Guo, Zhichang Fan, Ding Li, Qianmeng Chen, Wenlong Qin, Yan Li, Bin Wang, Yongfang Wang, Xiaochun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo develop a parameter-driven habitat imaging based on intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) MRI and to validate its effectiveness in preoperatively predicting muscle invasion in bladder cancer (BC).MethodsThis prospective study enrolled 693 pathology-confirmed BC patients who preoperatively underwent multi-b-value diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). DWI images were acquired using a 3.0 T MRI scanner with 11 b values. Patients were randomly divided into training and testing sets (7:3). K-means clustering was applied to diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f) maps to assign all voxels to distinct intra-tumoral habitats. The voxel proportion (p) and corresponding IVIM values were extracted for each habitat. Features were selected using intraclass correlation coefficient and Pearson correlation analysis. Three random forest models were then constructed: a habitat model, a clinical-radiologic model, and a combined model integrating both. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) was used to interpret feature contributions.ResultsTwo habitats (K = 2) were identified, including a low perfusion and restricted diffusion (LP-RD) habitat with decreased D, D* and f, and a high perfusion and unrestricted diffusion (HP-UD) habitat with increased D, D* and f. The habitat model achieved AUCs of 0.950 and 0.814 in the training and testing sets, respectively. Incorporating clinical-radiologic factors into the combined model improved its performance, reaching AUC values of 0.987 and 0.832. SHAP analysis identified the p and D* values in the HP-UD habitat, and tumor location as the top contributors to the combined model’s predictions.ConclusionsThe parameter-driven habitat imaging based on IVIM MRI offers a promising and noninvasive tool for preoperative assessment of muscle invasion in BC. It may help guide treatment stratification and reduce unnecessary interventions. Future studies will validate these findings in multi-center cohorts and advance integration into clinical workflows.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-025-00948-z.

Background

Bladder cancer (BC) accounts for the ninth most common cancer globally, with a great threat to public health worldwide [1, 2]. Muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC), characterized by the presence of muscular invasion, results in poorer prognosis, higher recurrence rate, and more metastasis than non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). Furthermore, NMIBC is managed initially with transurethral resection of bladder cancer (TURBT), with or without subsequent adjuvant intravesical therapy, while MIBC frequently requires more aggressive radical resection [3, 4]. TURBT and biopsy are standard methods for assessing the extent of muscular invasion. However, the limitations of current methods, including invasiveness, potential sampling bias, and time consumption, underscore the need for developing a noninvasive and more effective imaging technique for accurate staging.

Habitat imaging is an advanced imaging analysis approach that identifies intra-tumoral subregions by applying unsupervised clustering to delineate areas with similar pathophysiological behaviors [5, 6]. Each habitat represents a unique microenvironment, with different compositions of cells, vessels, extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules [7]. K-means, a widely used unsupervised learning algorithm, partitions data into K clusters by iteratively updating centroids and reassigning data points to minimize the within-cluster sum of squares [5]. Habitat analysis has been applied to a wide variety of tumors, contributing to advances in tumor classification, treatment response evaluation, preoperative risk prediction, and tumor aggressiveness assessment [8–14].

The concept of intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) was first proposed by Le Bihan et al. in 1988, based on a bi-exponential mathematical model that distinguishes pure water molecular diffusion from capillary perfusion [15]. To date, IVIM has shown promising performance in the staging, grading, and evaluation of tumor aggressiveness in BC [16–19]. However, conventional IVIM analysis typically relies on whole-lesion averaged parameter values, which may overlook the underlying intra-tumoral heterogeneity. This heterogeneity limits the ability of conventional imaging approaches to accurately identify subregions most associated with muscularis propria invasion. To address this limitation, we hypothesize that integrating quantitative parameters, including diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f), into habitat analysis may enable subregional characterization of bladder tumors, thereby enhancing staging accuracy and clinical decision-making. Therefore, this study aims to develop a parameter-driven habitat imaging based on IVIM MRI and to validate its effectiveness in preoperatively predicting muscle invasion in BC.

Methods

Patient selection

This single-center prospective study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of our hospital (No. K-K112), and consecutively involved a total of 1391 participants with suspected bladder tumors from January 2022 to August 2024. All patients provided written informed consent. Inclusion criteria: (a) patients underwent MRI examinations and were suspected of having bladder tumors, with scheduled radical cystectomy or TURBT for subsequent pathological confirmation. Exclusion criteria: (a) no surgery, or the interval between the MRI examination and surgery exceeded two weeks; (b) pathologically confirmed benign or malignant lesions other than urothelium carcinoma; (c) lesion diameter < 5 mm or poor image quality; (d) patients with chemoradiotherapy or immunological treatment before MRI scanning. The enrolled patients were divided into training and testing sets in a 7:3 ratio. A flow chart of patient selection details is shown in Fig. 1.

MRI acquisition

All MRI examinations were conducted using a 3.0-T scanner (MAGNETOM Vida, Siemens Healthcare) with an 18-channel body coil. Participants were instructed to void their bladder 1–2 h prior to MRI and to avoid further fluid intake or urination until the scan was completed. The imaging protocol included T1- and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and multi-b-value DWI. Detailed sequence parameters are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Image processing and segmentation

The IVIM model was fitted from multi-b-value DWI using in-house-developed software, BoDiLab, built on the open-source Python library DIPY (https://dipy.org). Where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{b}$$\end{document} is the signal intensity at a given b-value, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document} is the signal intensity at b = 0 s/mm2. The IVIM model generates three parameters: D, the true diffusion coefficient; D*, the pseudo-diffusion coefficient reflecting blood velocity; and f, the volume fraction of microvascular flow [15, 20, 21]. Due to the instability of D* under low signal-to-noise ratio and limited sampling conditions, full three-parameter nonlinear fitting is often unreliable. To improve robustness, we employed a segmented fitting approach. In the first step, D was estimated using high b-value data (b ≥ 200 s/mm²), where perfusion effects were negligible. In the second step, with D fixed, the remaining parameters (D* and f) were estimated using the full range of b-values.

Volumes of interest (VOI) were manually delineated on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document} images using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0; http://www.itksnap.org). Two experienced radiologists (G.J.T. with over 150 bladder MRI scans, F.Z.C. with over 300 bladder MRI scans) independently outlined the entire lesion, blinded to pathological results, by referring to T2WI and DWI. In cases with multiple lesions, the largest-sized tumor was chosen. 100 randomly selected lesions were re-segmented by G.J.T after a one-month interval. Final VOIs were transferred to D, D*, and f maps for quantitative analysis.

Habitat feature analysis

Three quantitative parameters were extracted for each voxel within the BC region to form a feature vector [8]. K-means clustering was applied to partition the lesion into distinct habitats, reflecting intratumor heterogeneity. The optimal number of clusters (K) was determined using the elbow method based on the sum of squared errors (SSE). Cluster labels were assigned to each voxel and mapped back to the tumor region to generate habitat maps. For each habitat, we calculated its proportion, defined as the number of voxels within the habitat divided by the total number of voxels in the lesion. Additionally, the corresponding mean values of three parameters were extracted. All image intensities were normalized to the range [0, 255] for consistency.

Feature selection

Reliability screening and redundancy control were performed prior to modeling. The inter-observer and intra-observer agreement of habitat features were evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC (2, k)] analysis. Features with ICC < 0.70 were excluded. To reduce redundancy, Pearson correlation analysis was performed among the remaining habitat features. For any pair with a correlation coefficient > 0.80, the feature with the higher average correlation with all other features was removed. If the averages were equal, one was randomly discarded. In addition, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted among clinical and radiological variables to explore potential risk factors for muscle invasion.

Model construction and evaluation

In the training set, five-fold cross-validation was performed on the random forest (RF) model using the selected features as input. Based on the source of features, three models were constructed: a habitat model, a clinical-radiologic model, and a combined model integrating both features. The diagnostic performance of models was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and a confusion matrix was calculated based on the optimal classification threshold to further assess the actual classification performance. Differences in AUC between models were statistically compared using the DeLong test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility. Calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow test).

Feature importance analysis

To interpret and quantify the contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions, we employed Shapley additive explanations (SHAP). As a breakthrough in interpretable machine learning, SHAP leverages cooperative game theory to assign prediction contributions to each input feature in a principled manner. Unlike other global approximation techniques, this unified approach excels in both fairness and consistency. Additionally, it not only quantifies feature importance at the model level but also uncovers feature-prediction relationships for individual instances.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 3.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing; https://www.r-project.org) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM; https://www.ibm.com/spss). Continuous variables were examined for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Differences in characteristics between MIBC and NMIBC were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Two-sided p values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 693 patients (581 men, 112 women, median age: 67 years [IQR: 60–73 years]) were included in this study. Of these, 485 (403 men, 82 women, median age: 67 years [IQR: 59–73 years]) were assigned to the training set. In this group, 116 (23.9%) patients were classified as MIBC and 369 (76.1%) as NMIBC. The remaining 208 (178 men, 30 women, median age: 67 years [IQR: 62.3–73 years]) were assigned to the testing set, with 50 (24.0%) classified as MIBC and 158 (76.0%) as NMIBC. No significant differences between the training set and testing set were found in terms of age, gender, smoking, total cholesterol, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, number of lesions, tumor location, and tumor stalk status (all p > 0.05). Habitat generation and subregion mapping

The cluster numbers ranging from 2 to 10 were initially evaluated. Clustering was performed at the cohort level rather than the patient level to ensure consistent habitat allocation across all patients. The SSE curve exhibited a clear inflection point at K = 2, indicating that two clusters provided the best trade-off between within-cluster compactness and overfitting. The two habitats were defined as follows: a “low perfusion and restricted diffusion” (LP-RD) habitat, characterized by decreased values of D, D*, and f, and a “high perfusion and unrestricted diffusion” (HP-UD) habitat, characterized by increased values of D, D*, and f. The median proportion (pLP−RD) of LP-RD habitat was 0.54 [IQR: 0.35–0.68], which was significantly higher than that (pHP−UD) of the HP-UD habitat (0.46 [IQR: 0.32–0.65]) (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S2). The quantitative characteristics of the two habitats are summarized in Table 2. In the training set, the pLP−RD was significantly higher in MIBC compared with NMIBC (0.65 [IQR: 0.52–0.80] vs. 0.51 [IQR: 0.33–0.64], p < 0.001). Given that pLP−RD and pHP−UD were complementary (sum to 1), this also indicated a significantly lower pHP−UD in MIBC (0.35 [IQR:0.20–0.48] vs. 0.49 [IQR:0.36–0.67], p < 0.001). Figure 3 presents representative MRI images. The quantitative features of each habitat demonstrated good inter-observer and intra-observer agreement, with ICC values ranging from 0.737 to 0.989 based on manual segmentation.

Correlation analysis and feature selection

The corresponding correlation matrices are shown in Fig. 4A and B, respectively. A negative correlation was observed between pLP−RD and pHP−UD (r = -1.00, p < 0.001), while DLP−RD was positively correlated with DHP−UD (r = 0.85, p < 0.001). Since pLP−RD and pHP−UD shared the same mean correlation value (r = 0.328), pLP−RD was randomly excluded by the feature selection algorithm. The DLP−RD with the higher mean correlation value (r = 0.316) to the DHP−UD (r = 0.284) was discarded. All clinical-radiologic features showed correlation coefficients below 0.80 and were thus incorporated into the final model.

Model construction and performance evaluation

D*LP−RD, fLP−RD, pHP−UD, DHP−UD, D*HP−UD, and fHP−UD were combined to construct a habitat model, while age, gender, smoking status, lipid profile indicators, lesion number, tumor location, and stalk status were utilized to construct a clinical-radiologic model.

In the training set, all three models demonstrated excellent performance, with AUCs exceeding 0.95 (Table 3; Fig. 4C). The habitat model achieved an AUC of 0.950 (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.929–0.972), with an accuracy (ACC) of 89%, sensitivity of 87%, specificity of 90%, and a high negative predictive value (NPV) of 96%. The combined model achieved the best performance (AUC: 0.987, 95%CI: 0.976–0.998, ACC: 95%), with balanced sensitivity (96%) and specificity (95%). In the testing set (Fig. 4D), the habitat model maintained robust predictive capacity, with an AUC of 0.814 (95% CI: 0.740–0.888) and an ACC of 82%. Although the sensitivity decreased to 69%, the specificity remained high at 86%, and the NPV reached 90%. The combined model also outperformed the others, achieving an AUC of 0.832 (95% CI: 0.757–0.908) and a sensitivity of 83%, significantly higher than that of the clinical-radiologic model (p < 0.001), as the DeLong test showed in Supplementary Table S3.

SHAP-based feature contribution analysis

The SHAP-based feature importance analysis revealed that pHP−UD, D*HP−UD, and tumor location were the most influential features in predicting muscle invasion in the combined model (Fig. 6). Specifically, pHP−UD had the highest mean SHAP value (0.0804), followed by D*HP−UD (0.0268) and tumor location (0.0256) (Supplementary Table S4). The SHAP summary plot further indicated that a higher value of pHP−UD was associated with a negative probability of muscle invasion, while higher values of D*HP−UD and tumor location contributed to an increase in the prediction score. Notably, pHP−UD and D*HP−UD were the top two influential features in the habitat model, and tumor location was the most important feature in the clinical-radiologic model.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a parameter-driven habitat model based on IVIM MRI for noninvasive, preoperative assessment of muscle invasion in BC. The habitat model showed good diagnostic performance, with AUCs of 0.950 (95% CI: 0.929–0.972) in the training set and 0.814 (95% CI: 0.740–0.888) in the testing set. By integrating habitat-derived features with clinical-radiologic variables, a combined model was constructed, showing best performance with AUCs of 0.987 (95% CI: 0.976–0.998) and 0.832 (95% CI: 0.757–0.908) in the training and testing sets, respectively. SHAP-based interpretability analysis identified pHP−UD, D*HP−UD, and tumor location as the most influential features in the combined model. These findings underscore the potential value of multi-modal approaches for improving preoperative staging and individualized treatment planning in BC.

To date, our study represents the largest sample size for habitat analysis based on the IVIM model, compared to previously published studies [8, 22]. While most existing studies have concentrated on DWI and dynamic contrast-enhanced or perfusion-weighted imaging, each offering either diffusion or perfusion information, our work utilizes noninvasive IVIM imaging, which uniquely provides both types of information simultaneously [10, 11, 13, 23–29]. By applying unsupervised clustering on voxel-wise IVIM parametric maps, we delineated biologically distinct tumor habitats that reflect intra-tumoral heterogeneity. The IVIM-guided habitat approach accounts for tumor heterogeneity and enables more refined feature analysis, which may contribute to improved diagnostic performance compared to whole-tumor methods [30]. Our findings highlight the value of IVIM imaging not only for functional assessment, but also as a foundation for more biologically-informed radiomic modeling.

In our study, the BC was split into two subregions utilizing SSE, including LP-RD and HP-UD. It implies that two clusters are sufficient to capture the essential variation within the data while preventing unnecessary complexity in the clustering model. For pedunculated bladder tumors, Panebianco et al. incorporated stalk morphology into the Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) criteria, typically assigning scores of 1–2 [31]. However, in clinical practice, approximately 9.5% to 17% of pedunculated tumors are pathologically confirmed as MIBC [32, 33]. Our result clearly showed that the voxel proportion of LP-RD is higher than that of HP-UD in MIBC. Instead of relying solely on morphological features, assessing the proportion of tumor habitats may provide a more direct and reliable approach to identifying muscle invasion in pedunculated tumors. In addition, it may allow us to identify tumor subregions that are more relevant to our research objectives. For example, preoperative habitat maps can pinpoint LP-RD-rich subregions, which may be more strongly associated with muscle invasion risk. These maps could help guide targeted deep resection, margin optimization, and selective sampling, potentially reducing understaging and early re-resection. Accordingly, patients with higher LP-RD proportions might be considered for more intensive surveillance (earlier cystoscopy and shorter imaging intervals), whereas those with lower proportions could follow standard schedules.

The SHAP-based interpretation of the combined model highlighted several key features that contributed most to the prediction of muscle invasion, including pHP−UD, D*HP−UD, and tumor location. The feature pHP−UD was negatively associated with muscle invasion; that is, a smaller proportion of the HP-UD habitat corresponds to a higher likelihood of muscle invasion. This may be because HP-UD subregions tend to have greater blood supply and less restriction of water diffusion, which together imply relatively sufficient oxygenation. Prior work has shown that a habitat of low perfusion and high restriction is indicative of hypoxia [34]. Hypoxia in BC has been extensively studied as an adverse factor linked to epithelial-mesenchymal transition, matrix metalloproteinase activation, genomic instability, and impaired immune infiltration, thereby promoting muscle invasion [35]. The proportion feature has been included in predictive models across several studies, demonstrating its significant value in outcome prediction [8, 9]. We also observed that, within the HP-UD habitat, D* was positively associated with muscle invasion. A plausible explanation is that D* primarily captures the velocity and intermittency of microvascular flow rather than effective capillary exchange or oxygen delivery. In aggressive tumors, angiogenesis and vascular remodeling can yield structurally abnormal vessels with high-flow shunts. Such “pathological” microcirculation can increase D* while remaining inefficient for oxygenation, thereby sustaining hypoxia. These associations are in line with previous studies linking perfusion characteristics to tumor invasiveness [17]. The inclusion of tumor location as a top predictive feature further supports the clinical relevance of anatomical positioning in disease progression, consistent with earlier reports that tumors located at specific bladder regions (e.g., trigone, bladder neck) are more prone to invade the muscle layer [36]. Importantly, the SHAP analysis demonstrates that multi-modal feature integration not only improves predictive performance but also enhances interpretability, allowing clinicians to understand which features drive the model’s decision.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted prospectively at a single center and lacked external validation, thereby limiting the generalizability of the results and their applicability to broader populations. It’s necessary for us to further validate through larger, multi-center studies. Second, during the selection of clinical-radiologic features, VI-RADS was not considered in the analysis. The exclusion of VI-RADS may overlook valuable insights into muscle invasion assessment. Third, some feature distributions differed between the training and testing sets. This discrepancy may be attributed to the limited sample size. Last, the manual segmentation of the tumor was a time-consuming process, and the imaging measurements of tumors are subjective, which may carry a risk of data bias. In the future, we plan to explore the performance of an automatic segmentation model. It can significantly reduce bias caused by human factors while improving efficiency.

In conclusion, our study suggests that parameter-driven habitat imaging based on IVIM MRI may be a promising approach for the noninvasive assessment of muscle invasion. The combined model developed in this study, which integrated habitat and clinical-radiologic independent risk factors, showed the best performance for describing muscle invasion status. While preliminary, these findings may have implications for treatment planning and prognostic assessment in BC and indicate potential clinical utility.
