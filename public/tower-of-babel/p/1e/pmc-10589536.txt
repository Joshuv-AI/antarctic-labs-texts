Effects of dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart on serum nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels and pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Na Yang, Runli Guo, Yan Guo, Yongmei Wei, Nan An
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
To analyze the effects of dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart on the serum levels of nesfatin-1, C1q/TNF related protein-12 (CTRP12), and pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). In this retrospective cohort study, 513 women with GDM admitted to Tangshan Central Hospital (Tangshan, China) between January 2019 and December 2022 were selected and divided into an observation group (dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart therapy; n = 284) and a control group (insulin aspart therapy, n = 229). The general characteristics, clinical outcomes, serum nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose levels, pregnancy outcomes, and perinatal outcomes of the 2 groups were compared. After treatment, the total effective rate in the observation group was significantly higher than that of the control group (97.54% vs 86.03%, respectively; P < .001). Compared with the pretreatment levels, nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels were decreased in both groups; nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels in the observation group were significantly higher than those in the control group. After treatment, the preprandial and 2-hour postprandial blood glucose levels in the observation group were significantly lower than those in the control group. Compared with the control group, the observation group had significantly fewer cesarean sections, and a significantly lower incidence of postpartum hemorrhage, premature rupture of membranes, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes. After treatment, the risks of preterm birth, macrosomia, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and other adverse perinatal outcomes were significantly lower in the observation group than in the control group. In pregnant women with GDM, dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart can improve clinical outcomes; reduce nesfatin-1, CTRP12, and blood glucose levels; and reduce the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

1. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common clinical obstetric disease, reaching prevalence of 30% among high-risk patients,[1,2] mainly refers to the first degree of abnormal glucose metabolism, the cause of the disease has not been clarified.[3] The disorder may have no obvious clinical signs and symptoms in its early stages, but if left untreated, it can cause adverse pregnancy outcomes, including infection, polyhydramnios, premature birth, macrosomia, and preterm labor, and may also lead to abnormal glucose metabolism in patients after delivery and in neonates.[4,5] Women with GDM are prone to develop impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes several years after delivery, which has a great impact on their physical and mental health.[6] Therefore, early detection and rational intervention are important to improve the prognosis of women with GDM.[6,7]

Nesfatin-1, a feeding regulatory factor synthesized and secreted by the hypothalamus, is implicated in impaired glucose regulation and diabetes onset. It is closely associated with insulin resistance, elevated insulin levels, and disordered glucose metabolism.[8] Aberrant serum nesfatin-1 expression is an independent risk factor for GDM development.[9] Complement-C1q/tumor necrosis factor-related protein-12 (CTRP12) is a novel adipokine that is structurally similar to adiponectin. It performs various functions, including glucose and lipid metabolism regulation, exerting anti-inflammatory effects, and adjusting insulin sensitivity.[10,11] Good glycemic control during pregnancy can reduce complications in all stages of pregnancy.[5–7] Moreover, treatment of women with GDM using drugs, nutritional guidance, and exercise can effectively control blood glucose levels and reduce the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Insulin aspart is commonly used in the treatment of diabetes. It can effectively reduce blood sugar levels and the impact of hyperglycemia on mothers and infants. However, most patients reportedly do not pay attention to dietary interventions after initiating drug treatment, leading to limited effectiveness of drug treatment.[12,13] In some patients with diabetes, dietary control can fully regulate blood glucose and lipid metabolism through reasonable dietary intervention.[14,15] This study explored the effect of dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart on serum nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels and pregnancy outcomes in pregnant women with GDM.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study participants

This was a retrospective study. The sample size was calculated based on the prevalence of the disease. It was computed using the following formula: n = Z2 (p*q)/d2, where Z (Z-score) = 1.96, P = .8, q = (1−p), and d is a fraction of p. Based on these calculations, the clinical data of 513 patients with GDM admitted to Tangshan Central Hospital (Tangshan, China) from January 2019 to December 2022 were divided into an observation group (dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart treatment, n = 284) and a control group (insulin aspart treatment, n = 229) according to the treatment that they received. In the observation group, age ranged 22 to 36 years (mean age 29.24 ± 1.34 years); gestational age ranged 24 to 28 weeks (mean 25.87 ± 1.12 weeks). In the control group, age ranged 21 to 37 years (mean age 29.41 ± 1.62 years); gestational age ranged 24 to 28 weeks (mean 25.97 ± 1.12 weeks). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tangshan Central Hospital.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: complete clinical data available; patients met the diagnostic criteria for GDM[16]; follow-up data available; natural conception, and singleton pregnancy. The exclusion criteria were: patients with primary diabetes; lack of medical records; elevated blood glucose caused by other factors; patients with other systemic or organic diseases.

2.3. Treatment methods

The control group was treated with insulin aspart (from Zhuhai Federal Pharmaceutical, Co., Ltd., Zhuhai, China; National drug approved S 20210028; specification 30 U) injected subcutaneously 0.5 hours before meals, 3 times daily. The first dose was 0.2 U/kg (1 dose/day), the dose was adjusted according to the change of blood glucose (4–8 U/time), and the 3 doses each accounted for 1/3 of the total dose.

The observation group received a dietary intervention based on the control group. On admission, the patient nutritional status was initially evaluated based on their body mass index. An investigation of the patients’ past dietary habits and behaviors was conducted to understand their dietary habits. Information was collected on meal preferences, dietary predilections, and cooking methods to comprehensively assess their nutritional condition. The total energy required per day was calculated based on the patient nutritional status. The daily energy coefficient was also determined with reference to the nutritional guide for patients with diabetes, considering factors such as the age, body mass index, activity level, food intake, gestational weight gain, and fetal condition.[17] Assistance was provided to the patient in ensuring that the proportions of dietary nutrients, ensuring that daily intake requirements, such as vitamins, calcium supplements, and iron supplements, were met. In pregnant women weighing <80% of the standard weight, the total calorie requirement was assumed to be 167 kJ/kg; in those with a normal weight, it was assumed to be 125 kJ/kg; and in those weighing >120% of the standard weight, it was assumed to be 100 kJ/kg.[18,19] Carbohydrates should constitute 40% to 50% of the total daily energy intake, proteins 20% to 25%, and fats 20% to 25%. Patients were instructed to adhere to the principle of eating smaller, more frequent meals, increasing the number of meals to 5 to 6 times per day (with the proportion of energy intake in the morning, afternoon, and evening being 20%, 30%, and 30%, respectively, with snacks accounting for 20%), and adjusting their diet based on their blood sugar levels. Interventions began during the midgestation period at 24 to 28 weeks and lasted for 12 weeks in both patient groups.[20,21]

Before and after 3 months of treatment, 6 mL of venous blood was extracted on the morning of 2nd, placed in a vacuum centrifuge tube, shaking the test tube and mixed thoroughly, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes to obtain upper serum, and frozen in a −20°C freezer until testing.

2.4. Observed indicators

We compared the general characteristics of the 2 groups, including gestational age, pregnancy time, and duration of labor.

We compared the clinical outcomes between the 2 groups. Significant effect: after treatment, fasting blood glucose was maintained below 5.3 mmol/L, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose was maintained below 6.7 mmol/L; effective: fasting blood glucose and postprandial blood glucose decreased after treatment, but fasting blood glucose was not below 5.3 mmol/L, and 2-hour postprandial blood glucose was not below 6.7 mmol/L; ineffective: no significant change in blood glucose after treatment. The total response rate was defined as (significant + effective)/100% of total patients.[22]

We compared the serum nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels between the 2 groups before and after treatment. The levels of nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 were measured using a radioimmunoassay (Shanghai Eride Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The normal range of nesfatin-1 was <1.5 µg/L, and that of CTRP12 was <600 µg/L.[23]

We compared blood glucose levels before and 2 hours after treatment between the 2 groups. Blood glucose levels were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer. The blood glucose criteria were as follows: fasting glucose <5.3 mmol/L and 2-hour postprandial glucose <6.7 mmol/L.[24]

We compared pregnancy outcomes after treatment between the 2 groups. Data on pregnancy outcomes were collected by telephone interview or during the on-site follow-up visit after 6 months of treatment. Pregnancy outcomes included cesarean section, postpartum hemorrhage, and premature rupture of membranes.

We compared the perinatal outcomes between the 2 groups, including prematurity, macrosomia, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, and neonatal hypoglycemia.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and groups were compared using t tests. Categorical data are expressed as the frequency and percentage and the groups were compared by using χ2 tests. P values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

4. Discussion

With improvements in social living standards, the number of patients with GDM is increasing annually.[21] According to epidemiological statistics, the incidence of GDM in China has reached approximately 10%, and GDM is associated with threatened abortion, infection, macrosomia, premature birth, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes that are more harmful to patients and fetuses/neonates.[25,26] Therefore, interventions are required to reduce the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in patients with GDM.

Drugs, particularly insulin aspart, are often used to control blood glucose levels in patients with GDM. The use of insulin aspart can significantly reduce blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin concentrations in patients with GDM; however, it is often ineffective due to the poor dietary control of patients.[27,28] Dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart treatment is effective for controlling the blood sugar of patients with GDM and optimizing the treatment outcomes.[29] In this study, the overall effectiveness of treatment was significantly higher in the dietary intervention group than in the control group, which is consistent with previous research indicating that dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart is more effective than insulin aspart alone. The analysis suggests that insulin aspart plays a role in blood sugar regulation, and the dietary intervention through a formulated meal plan also contributes to blood sugar control. Consequently, the combination of these 2 approaches achieves optimal therapeutic efficacy.[30] The results of this study show that after treatment, nesfatin-1 and CTRP12 levels in both groups decreased, and were significantly higher in the observation group than those in the control group. After treatment, the blood glucose levels before and 2 hours after meals in were significantly lower in the observation group than those in the control group. The results indicate that dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart reduce nesfatin-1, CTRP12, and blood glucose levels. The analysis suggests that abnormal expression of nesfatin-1 can cause obesity and related abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism. CTRP12 is involved in fat accumulation and brown adipogenesis, is closely related to diet-induced obesity; and nesfatin-1, CTRP12, and blood sugar levels are associated with insulin resistance. Moreover, a large amount of literature has confirmed that insulin aspart treatment can greatly reduce nesfatin-1, CTRP12, and blood glucose levels.[31,32]

Another study found that the incidence of adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes in patients with GDM is greatly reduced by dietary interventions and insulin aspart treatment.[33] The results of this study showed that, after treatment, the proportion of adverse pregnancy outcomes in the observation group was significantly lower than that in the control group, and the proportions of premature infants, macrosomia, fetal distress, neonatal asphyxia, and neonatal hypoglycemia in the observation group were significantly lower than those in the control group. These results are consistent with those of the aforementioned study, which indirectly confirmed that dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart therapy can reduce adverse pregnancy and perinatal outcomes. The analysis suggests that after dietary intervention, auxiliary insulin aspart maintains the patient blood sugar level in the optimal control range and prevents the occurrence of a series of complications, laying a good foundation for pregnancy.[34,35]

In conclusion, dietary intervention combined with insulin aspart administration in pregnant women with GDM can improve the clinical outcomes; reduce nesfatin-1, CTRP12, and blood glucose levels; and reduce the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.
