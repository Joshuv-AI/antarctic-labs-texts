Dataset of AI adoption usage, expectations, attitudes, perceptions, and motivations for learning in higher education
Authors: Zaky Farid Luthfi, Wibowo Heru Prasetiyo, Beti Indah Sari, Atri Waldi, Arief Muttaqiin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset presents the use of artificial intelligence (AI) among university students as a tool for completing academic tasks. A total of 535 data points were collected from students participating in an online survey conducted across 152 universities and 20 provinces in Indonesia. The dataset includes demographic information, educational level, field of study, and the types of AI tools used. It also captures students' use of AI, including performance expectations, challenges in using AI, attitudes toward AI use, perceptions of AI, and motivational intent to use AI. All collected data are described according to the results of descriptive statistical analysis, which aims to both describe and explore the relationships between the constructs. Moreover, this dataset offers significant potential for reuse and further comparative studies for researchers interested in the application of AI in academic contexts. It can serve as a reference for future investigations into the potential determinants influencing AI use in higher education settings. Potential applications include the development of advanced AI tool features tailored to students' specific characteristics and usage priorities.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, AI adoption, Students’ expectations, Students’ attitudes, Students’ motivation, Higher education

Background

Artificial intelligence (AI) has seen an accelerated integration into higher education, revolutionizing teaching, learning, and administrative processes. AI-based tools such as intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) and AI-driven chatbots provide students with real-time, personalized feedback and assistance [1,2]. These technologies adapt to students' learning styles, allowing for customized educational experiences that were previously difficult to achieve in traditional settings. AI can also enhance language learning, as seen in its applications for writing assistance, reading comprehension, and vocabulary acquisition [3,4]. AI-powered grading systems can evaluate large volumes of assignments and exams, reducing the workload on educators while ensuring consistency in grading. Automated essay scoring systems have been implemented to assess students’ writing, providing instant feedback to improve their academic performance [5]. AI has also streamlined administrative processes in higher education institutions. Universities utilize AI-driven virtual assistants to handle student inquiries, assist with course registration, and provide academic counseling. For example, AI-powered chatbots, such as IBM’s Watson at Deakin University, offer round-the-clock assistance to students, reducing administrative burdens on human staff [4]. However, this integration is not without significant challenges and ethical concerns. Issues such as data privacy [6], algorithmic bias [7], academic integrity [8], overdependency [9], and transparency in AI-driven decision-making [10] raise critical questions that must be addressed to ensure responsible and equitable AI use in higher education settings.

Despite some issues in using AI, it has provided students with new opportunities for academic assistance and skill development. A study by Chan and Hu [11] found that most university students viewed generative AI as a valuable tool for learning, particularly for tasks such as writing assistance, brainstorming, and research analysis. Similarly, Sami et al. [12] reported that medical students found AI tools to be effective and credible learning aids, helping them grasp complex medical concepts more efficiently than traditional study methods. Additionally, AI’s role in enhancing creativity, productivity, and research capabilities has been well-documented, with students reporting increased efficiency in their academic tasks due to AI-generated insights and suggestions [11]. Moreover, a significant gap remains in terms of sufficient data that can systematically assess the impact of AI adoption across diverse student populations. The absence of structured, large-scale datasets limits the ability to analyze long-term trends, compare different educational contexts, and formulate data-driven policies that ensure equitable AI integration. Our dataset is intended to fill this gap by offering empirical insights into AI adoption in higher education, capturing students’ expectations, attitudes, perceptions, and motivations in using AI for academic purposes.

Additionally, several theoretical frameworks are relevant to better understand the function and adoption of artificial intelligence in Indonesian higher education, providing the foundation for interpreting students’ behaviors, perceptions, and motivations toward AI usage. AI adoption by students in higher education is explained through three theoretical frameworks. Based on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), students’ perceptions of AI’s ease of use and usefulness influence their motivation and behavioral intentions to adopt AI tools. Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) highlights how students’ self-efficacy and social learning shape their confidence and behavior in using AI technologies. Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) theory helps explain how students’ experiences and satisfaction with AI tools impact their engagement and continued use. Together, these frameworks provide a comprehensive understanding of the cognitive, behavioral, and experiential factors influencing AI adoption in higher education settings [13,14].

Furthermore, Indonesia presents an ideal context for large-scale AI data collection due to its unique higher education landscape, characterized by institutional diversity, regional disparities in digital infrastructure, and a rapidly expanding student population. With over 4,500 higher education institutions, ranging from top-tier research universities to community colleges, Indonesia offers a heterogeneous academic environment that mirrors the challenges faced in many developing regions. Additionally, one of the primary concerns in AI adoption is digital accessibility. While urban centers such as Jakarta and Surabaya benefit from advanced technological infrastructure, rural and underdeveloped regions often struggle with limited internet access and inadequate digital resources [15,16]. Understanding how these disparities affect AI adoption and student learning outcomes can provide valuable insights for other nations facing similar challenges. Indonesia’s demographic composition also makes it an essential case for understanding generational and cultural attitudes toward AI in education. With a high proportion of young learners entering higher education, data collected from Indonesian universities can highlight how digital natives adapt to AI tools, how traditional educational structures accommodate technological shifts, and what strategies are most effective for AI literacy training.

This dataset also contributes to the growing role of AI adoption for personalized learning environments and student engagement. It is expected to help researchers and AI developers analyze trends, explore new determinants of AI usage, and enhance AI-based educational technologies. By providing publicly available data, educators and educational policymakers can use this data as a reference for shaping AI's responsible and effective integration in higher education.

Data Description

The data was collected through an online survey involving 535 students from both public and private universities across 20 provinces in Indonesia. The survey utilized a questionnaire containing questions related to demographic information (e.g., gender, province of origin). This dataset includes respondent demographic characteristics such as the type of university, gender, location (province), level of education, academic discipline, and the type of artificial intelligence (AI) tools used by students. Additionally, the survey gathered responses from students regarding their attitudes, challenges, motivations, and expectations related to the use of AI in their learning experiences.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The recent study adapted several indicators from the instrument developed by Venkatesh et al. [17]. Data were collected through the distribution of online questionnaires administered via Google Forms between August and October 2024. The questionnaire contained items related to students’ learning experiences as influenced by their use of artificial intelligence (AI). This data collection method was selected because it allows the researchers to reach respondents from various locations through simple random sampling. The collected data, stored in an Excel file, was then coded and imported into IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 for analysis to assess data normalization through the consistency of standardized response scores. Limitations

The dataset has several limitations, including a geographic limitation, as it primarily represents AI adoption among students in 152 universities and 20 provinces in Indonesia, which may not be generalizable to other regions or countries. The scope of variables is also limited, as it focuses on specific factors like demographics, expectations, and motivations but excludes other important determinants such as digital literacy or institutional support. Additionally, being a cross-sectional dataset, it provides only a snapshot of AI usage at a specific time, not accounting for potential trends or changes over time. Lastly, the dataset lacks qualitative insights, as it is based solely on quantitative survey responses, missing deeper, contextual information that could provide a more comprehensive understanding of students’ AI experiences.

Ethics Statement

The authors confirmed that participation in the study was voluntary, with respondents providing their information willingly. All personal data collected from respondents was used solely for research purposes and will be handled in accordance with the principle of anonymity. Before data collection, the project was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Universitas Negeri Padang, under approval code 1634/UN35.15/LT/2024. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time and for any reason without facing any penalties or losing any entitled benefits. The online survey was designed to ensure respondent anonymity and to protect the confidentiality of their personal information.
