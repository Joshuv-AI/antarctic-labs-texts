Defining thoracic impedance thresholds for rescue ventilation: a laboratory study
Authors: Philip Jarrett, Rithika Prakash, Bhaskar Thakur, Jordan Thomas, Dazhe Cao, Mary Chang, Betty Yang, Sarah Shaver, Ahamed Idris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo establish evidence-based peak amplitude thresholds for thoracic bioimpedance monitoring of ventilation and characterize the relationship between peak amplitude and tidal volume across two defibrillator devices used in pre-hospital care during cardiopulmonary resuscitation.MethodsThirty healthy adults underwent mechanical ventilation while thoracic bioimpedance was monitored using LifePak12 and HeartStart MRx defibrillators at five tidal volumes (250–800 mL). Peak amplitude measurements were extracted from thoracic bioimpedance waveforms. Linear mixed-effects models accounted for repeated measurements. Sex-stratified analyses examined device-specific sensitivity differences. Breath detection thresholds were calculated at various target sensitivities.ResultsLinear relationships were observed for both devices. The LifePak 12 device showed tidal volume slopes of 0.0022 Ω/mL for females and 0.0013 Ω/mL for males. The HeartStart MRx device demonstrated slopes of 0.0030 Ω/mL for females and 0.0016 Ω/mL for males. Males showed 41–47% lower sensitivity to tidal volume changes than females across both devices. Model performance showed intraclass-correlations of 0.713–0.783 with prediction correlations exceeding 0.97. For the 250 mL breath, peak amplitude thresholds were 0.630/0.40 Ω (female/male) for LifePak12 and 0.45/0.30 Ω for HeartStart MRx at 90% detection sensitivity.ConclusionThis study provides preliminary sex- and device-specific peak amplitude thresholds for breath identification using thoracic bioimpedance waveforms. The observed linear relationships and provided thresholds may improve categorical breath detection in resuscitation research. These findings require validation in cardiac arrest patients to ensure generalizability for clinical application.

Introduction

Effective ventilation during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) remains poorly standardized due to limited respiratory monitoring capabilities prior to advanced airway placement.1, 2, 3, 4 While successful ventilation is associated with improved survival with favorable neurologic outcome, patients receiving 30:2 CPR with bag-mask ventilation frequently fail to receive adequate breaths.5, 6

Thoracic bioimpedance, a standard feature in modern defibrillators, continuously monitors changes in electrical impedance from cardiac activity, chest wall movement, and respirations.7, 8, 9 Beyond its traditional use for chest compression feedback, thoracic bioimpedance enables ventilation research via measurement of peak amplitude and other waveform characteristics that may correlate with delivered tidal volume.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Previous research suggested a peak amplitude cutoff of 0.5 Ω (approximately 250 mL tidal volume) for breath characterization during 30:2 CPR.5, 6 However, this threshold was derived from seven healthy individuals without empirical validation. While thoracic bioimpedance demonstrates a linear relationship with tidal volume across clinical settings, patient-level variability has limited its direct application as a spirometer.14, 15, 16

Prior work by Berve et al. demonstrated that age, body mass index, and sex significantly influence thoracic bioimpedance measurements,15 suggesting that sex-specific thresholds may improve breath detection accuracy. This cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study aimed to characterize the peak amplitude to tidal volume relationship across breath sizes clinically relevant during CPR using two defibrillator devices. The primary objective was to develop sex-specific peak amplitude thresholds for breath identification across two commonly used defibrillator platforms. Secondary objectives included quantifying device-specific differences in bioimpedance sensitivity and establishing thresholds at various sensitivity levels for future CPR research applications.

Methods

Study design and ethics approval

This cross-sectional diagnostic accuracy study was conducted in accordance with the Utstein-Style Guidelines for Uniform Reporting of Laboratory CPR Research.17 The study was reported following STARD guidelines, with the completed checklist provided in Supplementary Material 1.18 The study protocol was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center Institutional Review Board (Protocol #STU-092016-052) as presenting no greater than minimal risk. The ethics-approved protocol is available in Supplementary Material 2. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Subject recruitment

Thirty adult volunteers without acute illness were recruited from faculty and residents of a United States Emergency Medicine training program through convenience sampling. Participants underwent controlled mechanical ventilation while thoracic bioimpedance was recorded with two defibrillator devices: the LifePak 12 (Physio-Control) and HeartStart MRx (Philips).

Data collection

The study protocol involved delivering six breaths at each of five predetermined tidal volumes (250, 300, 400, 600, and 800 mL) to conscious subjects while continuously measuring thoracic bioimpedance waveforms for each defibrillator device. To avoid interference between the circuits of the two defibrillator brands, measurements were performed separately with each device for each subject. The tidal volume range was selected to include the minimum breath size considered adequate for ventilation during CPR based on current guidelines, with 250 mL approximating the minimum tidal volume needed to overcome anatomical and physiological dead space.5, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23

Subjects used a mouthpiece connected to a Servo-I mechanical ventilator (Maquet) during controlled breathing sessions while in a supine position. Breaths were delivered using volume control to ensure achievement of target tidal volumes. Prior to data collection, subjects underwent multiple practice rounds receiving positive-pressure ventilation at each of the pre-specified tidal volumes to ensure familiarity and compliance with the procedure. Subjects were permitted to remove the mouthpiece at any time for comfort. During both practice and final data acquisition, pressure–volume loops were continuously monitored by a certified respiratory therapist to detect overbreathing, air leaks, and sudden changes in airway resistance. When an adequate six-breath period was captured without these issues, that sequence was flagged for inclusion in the analysis. Continuous thoracic bioimpedance waveforms were recorded using the selected defibrillator brands with pads placed in the standard anterolateral position.

Peak amplitude measurement

Thoracic bioimpedance waveforms were analyzed using device-specific software. CODE-STAT 10 was used to analyze impedance data from the LifePak 12 device, while HeartStart Event Review Pro was used to analyze data from the HeartStart MRx device. Peak amplitude measurements of the flagged breaths at each of the five pre-specified tidal volumes were extracted and tabulated for statistical analysis. Threshold development

Sex- and device-specific peak amplitude thresholds for defining target tidal volumes were calculated using percentile-based approaches. Thresholds were derived corresponding to sensitivity targets of 99%, 95%, 90%, and 75%. This approach enables study-specific threshold selection in future resuscitation research based on the desired balance between sensitivity and specificity.

Statistical analysis

Subject demographics and anthropometric characteristics were summarized as counts and percentages for categorical variables and as means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Peak amplitude was summarized descriptively as medians with first and third quartiles (Q1, Q3) within each device-sex-tidal volume combination.

To model the peak amplitude to tidal volume relationship while accounting for repeated measurements within subjects, we fit linear mixed-effects models with subject-specific random intercepts and random slopes for tidal volume. Sex was treated as an effect modifier based on prior research demonstrating substantial sex-related differences in bioimpedance sensitivity.15 The primary models were stratified by both device and sex: peak_amplitude ∼ tidal_volume + (tidal_volume|subject_id). Models were estimated by restricted maximum likelihood. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for model parameters were Wald-based, and hypothesis tests were two-sided with α = 0.05. Missing peak amplitude values were rare and were handled by listwise deletion. Analyses were conducted in Stata 18.0 and Python 3.13.3.

Model assessment

To determine whether a linear specification for tidal volume was appropriate, we performed diagnostic tests comparing continuous versus categorical specifications using the Akaike Information Criterion, Bayesian Information Criterion, and likelihood-ratio tests. We assessed potential non-linearity by adding quadratic and cubic terms to the continuous specification and testing their significance. Residuals were examined against fitted values and tidal volume to detect patterned departures. The continuous specification was retained when it was not outperformed by the categorical alternative, higher-order terms were non-significant, and residual diagnostics showed no systematic patterns.

The study included 30 healthy adults. All 30 subjects completed LifePak 12 measurement; device malfunction prevented HeartStart MRx measurement in 3 subjects. The LifePak 12 dataset contained 899 observations from 30 subjects with a single missing peak amplitude measurement, while the HeartStart MRx dataset contained 808 observations from 27 subjects with two missing peak amplitude measurements. Device differences

Both devices demonstrated effect modification by sex with differences in overall sensitivity. The HeartStart MRx device showed higher baseline sensitivity to tidal volume changes, with female slopes 36% higher and male slopes 23% higher compared to LifePak 12. Despite these differences, the relative effect of sex remained consistent (41–47% reduction in male sensitivity).

Model performance

The linear mixed-effects models achieved intra-class correlations of 0.713 (LifePak 12) and 0.783 (HeartStart MRx), indicating substantial between-subject variability that supported the random-effects modeling structure. Model fits demonstrated correlations exceeding 0.97 for both devices when estimating peak amplitude from known tidal volumes (Fig. 3). Discussion

This study confirms sex as a modifier of the relationship between thoracic bioimpedance peak amplitude and tidal volume. Across two widely adopted defibrillator platforms, males demonstrated 41–44% lower sensitivity to tidal volume changes compared to females, indicating that sex-specific thoracic bioimpedance thresholds may improve breath identification accuracy in defibrillator files during early resuscitation. For the 250 mL breath size, differences in detection thresholds between the sexes were substantial: female thresholds exceeded male thresholds by approximately 50% across both devices. Previous CPR research using a global 0.5 Ω threshold for breath detection may have introduced systematic bias.5, 6

The consistent magnitude of sex differences across both LifePak 12 and HeartStart MRx devices suggests a biological rather than technical origin. This finding aligns with known physiological differences in chest wall compliance, body composition, and thoracic diameter between males and females.15, 24, 25, 26 Given that female lung volumes are 10–12% smaller than those of males, women may experience greater changes in thoracic impedance for a given change in tidal volume.15, 24, 25, 26 These observations are consistent with prior work by Losert et al., who demonstrated linear peak amplitude to tidal volume behavior with reduced error using patient-specific models,14 and Berve et al., who identified age, body mass index, and sex as significant contributors to model performance.15

While Berve et al. previously investigated age, BMI, and sex effects on thoracic bioimpedance measurements,15 our study extends this work by providing specific peak amplitude thresholds at multiple sensitivity levels that can be directly applied to breath detection in existing datasets. Additionally, we characterized these relationships across two different defibrillator platforms commonly used in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest research, enabling device-specific threshold selection.

The empirically derived, sex-stratified thresholds provide evidence-based options for breath detection ranging from liberal (99% sensitivity) to conservative (75% sensitivity), enabling study-specific optimization. For the 250 mL breath size, the median peak amplitude was 0.90 Ω (female) and 0.55 Ω (male) for LifePak 12, and 0.80 Ω (female) and 0.50 Ω (male) for HeartStart MRx (Table 2). These thresholds allow investigators analyzing existing pre-hospital datasets to match sensitivity targets to study aims while accounting for sex and device effects. Consistent with Berve et al.,15 additional anthropometric factors such as BMI and age may further refine thresholds, but such data are often unavailable during resuscitation; therefore, we prioritized sex-based stratification.

These thresholds may be applied to retrospective analysis of existing out-of-hospital cardiac arrest datasets that contain thoracic bioimpedance recordings, such as those from large cardiac arrest registries and multicenter trials.27, 28 Specifically, investigators can apply device-specific, sex-stratified peak amplitude cutoffs to bioimpedance waveforms to identify individual breaths during the pre-intubation period. This categorical breath detection may then be used to estimate ventilation frequency, timing relative to compressions, and associations with patient outcomes. Reliance on a global 0.5 Ω cutoff likely under-detected breaths in males and over-counted artifacts in females, which may have biased estimates of the association between bag-mask ventilation effectiveness and outcomes.5, 6

Device differences between LifePak 12 and HeartStart MRx systems indicate that peak amplitude thresholds should be tailored to each device. The higher sensitivity of HeartStart MRx devices may reflect differences in bioimpedance measurement algorithms, electrode configuration, or signal processing methods. These platforms were selected because they are widely represented in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest research infrastructure, facilitating application of device-specific thresholds.27, 28

The model fitting accuracy demonstrated in Fig. 3 supports the practical utility of these models. Correlations exceeding 0.97 for both devices indicate that peak amplitude can provide reliable estimates of tidal volume when sex is appropriately considered. The high intraclass correlations (0.713–0.783) confirm substantial between-subject variability even after accounting for sex, supporting the mixed-effects modeling approach and emphasizing subject-level heterogeneity.

Limitations

This study was conducted in conscious, healthy volunteers under controlled conditions, which limits generalizability to cardiac arrest patients. Pathophysiological changes during cardiac arrest may affect thoracic bioimpedance measurements differently than observed in healthy subjects. Additionally, the study was limited to mechanical ventilation by mouthpiece at predetermined tidal volumes with excellent user compliance and anterolateral pad placement; real-world CPR involves variable ventilation techniques, inconsistent breath delivery, and patient-related factors such as airway obstruction that may further affect bioimpedance measurements. Findings from this research also may not generalize to untested defibrillator models.

The study population consisted of relatively young adults (mean age 32 years) with normal body mass index (mean 23.8 kg/m2, SD 3.5), preventing incorporation of other known effect modifiers such as age and obesity into the models. The relatively small sample size, while adequate for model development, prevented cross-validation to evaluate model generalizability. Future studies should include larger, more diverse populations and validate these findings in cardiac arrest patients to determine whether the relationships observed in healthy volunteers apply during active resuscitation.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary sex- and device-specific peak amplitude thresholds for breath identification using thoracic bioimpedance waveforms from healthy volunteers. The observed linear relationships enable categorical breath detection, which may improve the accuracy of breath identification in existing pre-hospital cardiac arrest datasets. These findings require validation in cardiac arrest patients before application to real-time clinical monitoring.
