Novel Treatments for Adolescent Vaping Cessation: Are We There Yet?
Authors: Jasleen Kaur, Amanda Rossi, Sofia Adams, Mariginder S Varowal, Lakshit Jain, Mohsin Raza, Zouina Sarfraz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractE-cigarette use among adolescents is a growing public health concern, driven by discreet designs, appealing flavors, and social influences. Despite high addiction rates, most teen users express a desire to quit, though evidence-based cessation tools remain limited. Behavioral interventions, including tech-based programs like This Is Quitting and smokeSCREEN, show promise. Pharmacotherapy, particularly varenicline, may support quitting, though its role in adolescents is still under investigation. Pediatricians must address this issue through empathetic, multifaceted approaches that combine education, behavioral support, and emerging treatments to curb early nicotine dependence and its long-term health consequences.Keywords: Adolescents, E-cigarettes, Vaping cessation, Nicotine addiction, Varenicline

Introduction

E-cigarette (EC) use among adolescents is a major concern for healthcare workers. The basic components of an EC, or vape pen, are a battery, cartridge, and heating element which vaporizes the liquid. In addition to nicotine, the liquid often may contains carcinogenic molecules, heavy metals, and volatile compounds.1 In 2023, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that 7.7 % of adolescents were using EC, making them the most popular form of nicotine in this age group.2 while initially they were marketed to adults as a tool to aid in smoking cessation, these devices have now caused nicotine addiction to flourish at a young age.1,3 Addressing this problem is laborious and requires a bi-pronged approach addressing both prevention and cessation.

Understanding why teens use EC and the appeal of these devices is instrumental in designing prevention efforts. The discrete nature of the devices, which closely resemble a USB flash drive or writing instrument, makes the product easy to conceal.4 Some ECs deliver nicotine at a much higher concentration than traditional cigarettes. The emissions produce an inconspicuous cloud of relatively scentless smoke. A subset of adolescents report using ECs specifically to perform vape tricks, such as blowing vapor rings or creating swirling vapor patterns, which adds a recreational and social element to use. The devices come in different sizes, shapes, and colors, which are qualities that symbolize a toy rather than a harmful substance.3,4 Once adolescents try EC, roughly half continue to use them frequently.2

While these products are addictive, adolescent users display a strong intention to quit. In the 2020 National Youth Tobacco Survey (NYTS) 67.4 % of current vapers had attempted to quit within the past year.4,5 Despite the desire to quit, there are currently no Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved treatments or guidelines for vaping cessation. Varenicline has shown efficacy in adult nicotine vapers, 6 but has uncertain results in children under 16.7–9

The 2019 outbreak of e-cigarette, or vaping, product use-associated lung injury (EVALI) was strongly linked to the use of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)-containing e-cigarette products that contained vitamin E acetate as an additive. Vitamin E acetate was identified in product samples tested by the FDA and state laboratories, as well as in lung fluid samples from patients across multiple states. This additive was commonly used as a thickening agent in THC-containing vaping products obtained from informal sources, such as friends, family, or in-person or online dealers. The CDC recommended that individuals avoid using THC-containing e-cigarette or vaping products, particularly those acquired from informal sources, due to the strong association between vitamin E acetate and EVALI cases. Notably, the pediatric population accounted for 15 % of cases. These cigarette alternatives contain nicotine and hazardous substances. The chemicals are shown to be equivalent to the exposures of secondhand smoke. Nicotine use adversely impacts brain development and contributes to mood disorders. Using nicotine-containing products ultimately leads to impaired decision making and increased likelihood of future misuse of other drugs and alcohol.3

Interventions

Any form of inhaled nicotine is associated with elevated risk of future cigarette smoking.10 This is why it is imperative to design and implement high quality resources to support cessation. In this age group, it is beneficial to examine the appeal of EC, to appropriately tailor patient counseling and education. Of 185 adolescents between 14 and 19 years old surveyed, the most common method for quitting is “cold turkey” or unassisted quitting. The other prevalent method for quitting included advice from a friend.11 Although these may be the most popular quitting methods, there are concerns for long-term cessation effectiveness.

Positive predictors of attempting to quit were higher perceived harm of vaping and perceived potential for addiction. The latter had a significant link to willingness to use pharmacological methods for cessation. Interestingly, only 3.8 % endorsed ever using medications to aid them in quitting ECs, but 28.8 % said they had future interest in using this method.11 Adolescents cite numerous reasons for using EC, so the solution must be multifactorial. Addressing vaping prevention and cessation through various lenses is the most effective way to tackle this epidemic.4

Technology

Adolescents are avid users of technology to the extent that it has become a vital platform when conveying necessary information. Multiple studies have been conducted testing various technologic resources’ effectiveness in preventing vaping use and promoting cessation. Two new programs in particular, smokeSCREEN for prevention,12 and This Is Quitting for cessation,13 may play a pivotal role in getting through to teens.

smokeSCREEN examined the ability of, an interactive video game, on reshaping adolescents’ perceptions, beliefs, and desires about EC use. This educational platform was found to successfully altered adolescents’ perceptions about the harms associated with vaping, which is likely to prevent future use of ECs.11 Another study examined an individualized interactive text message program focused on achieving vaping cessation in adolescents entitled This Is Quitting. The program considers the patient’s age, start date, and desired quit date. Participants received peer support and cognitive- behavioral training to develop coping skills. They also received encouraging messages centered around managing cravings and urges to vape. Results indicated that adolescents who participated in this platform were over 30 % more likely to quit vaping.13

Pharmacotherapy

Behavioral modifications and psychotherapy are not always sufficient to achieve abstinence. Medications are often utilized as an adjunctive treatment to manage addiction. Among adult populations, varenicline has shown to be most efficacious.9 Combustible and electronic cigarettes both contain nicotine, so theoretically drugs used for smoking cessation could prove advantageous for vaping cessation. Emerging research supports this theory in adults with EC dependence that are motivated to quit. A recent study showed that varenicline significantly reduced cravings and produced higher abstinence rates than placebo. The continued abstinence rates at week 24 for varenicline compared to the placebo group were 34.4 % and 17.2 %, respectively.6 The anticipation is that comparable studies will show benefit in the adolescent population.

In the past, varenicline was solely FDA approved for people over 18 years old. A recent update expands the recommended ages to now include 16- and 17-year-olds.9 It is important to acknowledge that the FDA does not support use for children under 16 years old because there is limited data on efficacy, rather than significant safety concerns. Safety data is supported by a study on its use for smoking cessation in children ages 14–21. Varenicline was tolerated well and did not result in greater neuropsychiatric events than placebo. Unfortunately, the primary outcome (i.e. continuous abstinence at week 12) showed no differences between the experimental and control group. Secondary outcomes showed that varenicline-treated adolescent smokers were twice as likely to report seven consecutive days of abstinence, and they achieved abstinence quicker.7

Although no medication is FDA approved for vaping cessation, American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) states that varenicline can be used off-label for adolescents with high nicotine dependence and desire to quit.3 In order to reconcile these differences, research is ongoing to determine its potential role in vaping cessation in adolescents.

A randomized controlled trial7 is in progress to assess the efficacy of varenicline in adolescent vaping cessation. This trial will analyze vaping cessation on three groups including varenicline with behavioral counseling, placebo pill and behavioral counseling, and enhanced usual care. All groups will receive access to the This Is Quitting text messaging program that has yielded positive results in previous studies.12 A video-monitoring platform will be utilized to ensure medication adherence, since this is a concern among adolescents. Additionally, participants will receive incentives of up to $2 per day for video submissions.7

Conclusion

Vaping poses a serious threat to adolescent health, driven largely by peer pressure, targeted advertising via social media, and a lack of awareness about the addictive nature of nicotine and cannabis-containing products. Pediatricians play a crucial role in addressing this epidemic through non-judgmental conversations that explore why adolescents vape, whether they recognize its addictive risks, and gradually guide them toward cessation. While behavioral interventions remain the mainstay, emerging research on pharmacologic aids like varenicline may offer future support. A proactive, empathetic approach can empower youth to quit and prevent long-term harm.
