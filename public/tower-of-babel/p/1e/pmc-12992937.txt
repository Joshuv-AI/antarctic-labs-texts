Protocol for quantifying the oxidative cleavage of amyloid-β using oxygen-18 isotope labeling and LC-MS/MS
Authors: Yiying Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for quantifying the oxidative cleavage of amyloid-β using oxygen-18 labeling coupled with high-resolution LC–MS/MS. We describe procedures for amyloid-β peptide preparation, copper(II)/ascorbate-mediated oxidative cleavage, oxygen-18 incorporation, LC–MS/MS acquisition, and isotopologue-based quantitative data analysis. By incorporating oxygen-18 into newly generated C-termini during metal-driven cleavage and applying defined isotope conditions, this protocol enables site-specific identification of true cleavage-derived fragments while discriminating them from background signals arising from natural isotopic overlap and pre-existing impurities. This strategy supports confident identification of oxidative cleavage products and can be adapted to studies of peptide degradation in related systems.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Yang et al.1

Before you begin

Amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides are central to Alzheimer’s disease pathology (See Figure 1 for the Aβ42 sequence). Their aggregation, metal-binding properties, and oxidative cleavage pathways contribute to neurotoxicity. Copper, zinc, and iron ions modulate Aβ structure and reactivity, with copper in particular promoting both aggregation and site-specific oxidative cleavage of Aβ peptides.1,2,3,4 Stable isotope labeling with oxygen-18 (18O) water enables selective marking of newly generated C-termini, allowing quantitative analysis of oxidative cleavage events by LC–MS/MS.1 Recent work further highlights the importance of metal ion homeostasis in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis.5Figure 1Amino acid sequence of Amyloid-β 1-42 (Aβ 42)

Innovation

This protocol establishes a streamlined 18O-labeling LC–MS/MS workflow to quantify oxidative cleavage of Aβ peptides with high specificity. Conventional MS analysis cannot distinguish true oxidative cleavage fragments from background peptides that are always present due to spontaneous breakdown. By incorporating 18O into newly generated C-termini during copper/ascorbate-mediated cleavage, our method produces a diagnostic +2 Da shift that directly marks cleavage products.

A key advancement is the use of two controlled isotopic conditions (50% and 100% H218O), which are sufficient to resolve natural isotopic overlap from actual 18O incorporation. The 50% condition provides an internal reference where both light and heavy isotopologues coexist, while the 100% condition confirms complete labeling. This two-point strategy minimizes experimental complexity compared with full isotopic gradients, while still enabling rigorous discrimination of true cleavage events from isotopic noise.

Coupled with high-resolution Orbitrap MS and Skyline-based isotopologue quantification, this approach achieves reproducible, site-specific cleavage kinetics. Beyond Aβ, the workflow can be broadly adapted to study oxidative fragmentation in other aggregation-prone peptides or proteins.

Expected outcomes

Successful reactions yield distinct peptide isotopologue pairs separated by a +2 Da mass shift, corresponding to 18O incorporation at peptide C-termini (Figure 2). At 0% H218O, light (16O) peptides predominate; however, a minor +2 Da peak is consistently observed due to the natural isotopic distribution of peptides rather than isotope labeling.

At 50% H218O, both light (16O) and heavy (18O) peptides are detected and co-elute, providing an internal calibration that distinguishes natural isotope peaks from true 18O incorporation. At 100% H218O, the heavy isotopologue predominates, confirming efficient isotope labeling at cleavage-derived C-termini.

Quantitative analysis reveals increased heavy-to-light ratios (AUC18O/AUC16O) for oxidative cleavage fragments, whereas cleavage-resistant peptides, such as intact Aβ42, display unchanged ratios across isotope conditions (Figure 3). Extracted ion chromatograms demonstrate co-elution of light and heavy isotopologues with baseline-resolved peaks, supporting accurate quantification (Figure 2). Technical replicates exhibit high reproducibility, with consistent isotopologue ratios across independent runs.

Importantly, comparison of two isotope conditions is sufficient for robust interpretation: the 50% H218O condition distinguishes natural isotopic peaks from true labeling, whereas the 100% H218O condition verifies complete incorporation at cleavage sites. This two-point strategy simplifies the workflow while maintaining analytical rigor. A representative quantitative dataset underlying the heavy-to-light ratio analysis, including peptide identities and isotopologue intensities, is provided in Table S1.

Limitations

This protocol requires access to a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap or Time-of-flight platform) to resolve the +2 Da isotopologue peak from the natural isotope distribution of peptides. Lower-resolution instruments such as ion traps or standalone quadrupoles are not suitable, which may limit accessibility in some laboratories. In addition, back-exchange of 18O can occur during prolonged drying or reconstitution steps, leading to an underestimation of labeling efficiency. This risk can be minimized by careful sample handling and minimizing drying times.

Another limitation is that the method is currently demonstrated using synthetic Aβ peptides under controlled buffer conditions. While this ensures high reproducibility, it does not fully replicate the complexity of in vivo Aβ–metal interactions, where factors such as post-translational modifications, binding partners, aggregation states, and redox environment strongly influence cleavage dynamics. When applied to biological samples such as plasma or brain homogenates, high background peptide complexity and co-eluting species may interfere with accurate isotopologue detection, making 18O quantification less reliable than in purified systems. Careful sample preparation and enrichment strategies are therefore recommended for complex matrices.

The sensitivity of detection is also dependent on peptide solubility, chromatographic stability, and careful tuning of MS acquisition parameters. Finally, a minor +2 Da peak is consistently observed even at 0% H218O, reflecting the natural isotopic distribution of peptides rather than labeling. This phenomenon has been reported in earlier studies of Aβ oxidation,1 and highlights the necessity of including a 50% H218O control to discriminate natural isotope peaks from actual 18O incorporation.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low or inconsistent 18O incorporation in oxidative cleavage products (related to Steps 4–6 and 22–26).

In some experiments, cleavage-derived peptides may show low or variable 18O incorporation across replicates. This is commonly caused by isotope dilution from atmospheric moisture, partial evaporation during incubation, or reduced oxidative activity due to degraded reagents. Such variability can compromise quantitative interpretation of cleavage behavior.

Potential solution

•H218O handling: Use freshly opened H218O (≥97 atom %) and minimize exposure to air. Keep reaction tubes tightly sealed throughout incubation to prevent isotope dilution.•Evaporation control: Avoid prolonged incubation in loosely capped tubes and minimize drying time during sample preparation to reduce 18O back-exchange.•Reagent freshness: Prepare copper and ascorbate stocks immediately before use, as oxidized ascorbate reduces oxidative cleavage efficiency and 18O incorporation.

Problem 2

Apparent 18O signal in controls, inaccurate heavy-to-light ratios, or ambiguous cleavage site assignment due to natural isotope peaks, incorrect identification, or inconsistent integration (related to Steps 19–20, 22–26, and 27–29).

A minor +2 Da signal may be detected even in 0% H218O samples due to the natural isotopic distribution of peptides. In addition, inaccurate heavy-to-light ratios or ambiguous cleavage site assignments may arise from incorrect peptide identification, inclusion of interfering signals during extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) extraction, or inconsistent integration boundaries. These effects can lead to misinterpretation of 18O incorporation and oxidative cleavage behavior if not carefully evaluated.

Potential solution

•Natural isotope background: Expect a small +2 Da peak in 0% H218O samples.
