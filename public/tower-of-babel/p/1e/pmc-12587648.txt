Can carbon emission trading policy promote enterprise green management innovation?
Authors: Yindan Wang, Muwu Li, Junjun Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of China’s “dual carbon” goals, whether carbon emission trading, as a typical market-based environmental regulation instrument, can promote corporate green management innovation has become an important topic of academic inquiry. Based on panel data of Chinese listed enterprises from 2008 to 2022, this study investigates the effect of the carbon emission trading policy on green management innovation and explores the underlying transmission mechanisms. The estimated results show that (1) the carbon emission trading policy significantly promotes green management innovation among enterprises, and this effect remains robust after a series of sensitivity tests; (2) mechanistically, the policy facilitates green management innovation primarily by reducing managerial myopic behavior and increasing access to green credit; and (3) the policy exerts a stronger effect on enterprises with environmentally experienced executives, higher managerial capability, higher carbon intensity, greater industry competition, and larger scale. The findings provide empirical evidence that enhancing green innovation can support the green and low-carbon transition of enterprises in developing countries.Keywords: Carbon emission trading policy, Greenmanagement innovation, Managerial myopic behavior, Green credit

Introduction

Against the urgent global backdrop of climate change, carbon emissions have become a central challenge to sustainable development. According to the World Bank’s State and Trends of Carbon Pricing 2025, by 2025 there are 80 carbon pricing mechanisms in operation worldwide, covering 28% of global greenhouse gas emissions, with an economic impact exceeding 100 billion USD. Carbon pricing has clearly emerged as a critical policy instrument in global climate governance. Among these mechanisms, the carbon emission trading policy, as the most representative market-based approach to emission reduction, directs enterprises to optimize resource allocation and foster low-carbon innovation through price signals, playing a pivotal role on a global scale. Among these mechanisms, the carbon emission trading policy is the most representative market-based approach to emission reduction. Through price signals, it directs enterprises to optimize resource allocation and foster low-carbon innovation, and it plays a pivotal role on a global scale.

As the world’s largest carbon emitter, China has placed strong emphasis on developing its carbon market [1]. Since the launch of pilot carbon emission trading programs in 2011, market construction has advanced steadily, culminating in the establishment of the national carbon emission trading market in 2021, which marked a new stage of market-based regulation in China’s carbon reduction efforts. The carbon emission trading policy ultimately targets key emission-intensive enterprises, with the core objective of incentivizing green and low-carbon transformation [2]. Whether enterprises can achieve this transformation effectively depends not only on technological improvements but also on systematic adjustments in management and strategy. Compared with passive end-of-pipe abatement, enterprises need to pursue low-carbon development in a more proactive and comprehensive manner.

Green management innovation is therefore regarded as a critical pathway for enterprises to achieve low-carbon transformation. Its essence is to embed low-carbon management concepts into daily operations and to align sustainability goals with production and business activities. This integration improves resource efficiency and strengthens environmental protection. Green management innovation also emphasizes the optimization of production processes and management systems. By doing so, it reduces carbon intensity at the source and plays an irreplaceable role in advancing corporate green transition [3]. In this context, an urgent question arises: can the carbon emission trading policy promote corporate green management innovation, and through which mechanisms? Addressing this question is vital for evaluating the effectiveness of carbon market policies. It also helps clarify how such policies can act as a driver of corporate green management innovation. In turn, this provides empirical support for accelerating low-carbon transformation and achieving sustainable development goals.

Based on panel data of China’s A-share listed firms from 2008 to 2022, this study finds that the carbon emission trading policy significantly and consistently promotes corporate green management innovation. Its impact operates mainly through two channels: mitigating managerial short-termism and improving the supply of green credit. The policy effects are more pronounced in firms led by executives with environmental backgrounds, those with stronger managerial capabilities, higher carbon intensity, greater industry competition, and larger firm size. This study not only broadens the research perspective by addressing gaps in governance and management but also identifies and verifies two micro-level transmission mechanisms: internal behavioral constraints and external financing supply. Moreover, by examining boundary conditions, it highlights that policy effectiveness depends on differences in corporate governance capacity and market environment. These findings provide actionable implications for differentiated carbon market design and the optimization of corporate governance.

Literature review

The impacts of carbon emission trading policies

Since the establishment of the carbon trading market, scholars have extensively examined the implementation effects of carbon emission trading policies. The related literature is already substantial and can be broadly divided into three streams. First, studies at the regional level focus on outcomes such as carbon emissions [4–6], air pollution [7]– [8], carbon prices [9], green production performance [10], and carbon lock-in [11]. Second, research at the industry level investigates impacts on the industrial structure of the power sector [12], the green transition of manufacturing [13], industrial green efficiency [14], the spillover effects of carbon reduction in transportation [15], carbon abatement in provincial industrial sectors [16]– [17], and technological innovation in high-carbon industries [18]. Third, studies at the firm level analyze effects on carbon reduction [19], green innovation [20]– [21], employment [22]– [23], environmental investment [24]– [25], financing constraints [26], and total factor productivity [27]– [28].

Research on the effects of environmental regulation on green innovation

Research on the effects of carbon emission trading policies on green innovation has yielded mixed conclusions. Most studies suggest that the policy exerts a positive influence. For example, Du et al. [29] find that emission trading policies significantly promote green innovation in pilot regions but suppress innovation in neighboring areas. Liu and Li [30] argue that the policy stimulates green innovation in pilot enterprises through an internal mechanism of cost–benefit trade-offs and external pressure constraints. Bai et al. [31] highlight that carbon trading enhances green innovation efficiency by optimizing industrial structure, energy structure, human capital, and foreign direct investment. Deng et al.(2023) report that the CETS pilot policy induces green invention and innovation, with stronger effects in state-owned enterprises than in non-state-owned firms [21]. Pu and Ouyang [32] contend that carbon trading fosters green innovation by alleviating distortions in factor markets, although this effect weakens when enterprises face operational difficulties. At the same time, some studies identify short-term negative effects. Zhang et al. [33] show that higher carbon prices and rising compliance costs may crowd out R&D investment and thus inhibit green technological innovation. By contrast, Xi and Jia [20] argue that carbon trading policies can stimulate green technological innovation by easing financing constraints and increasing R&D expenditure.

A review of the existing literature reveals a lack of research examining the impact of the carbon emission trading policy on enterprise green management innovation. This study attempts to fill this gap. Compared with prior research, its marginal contributions may be summarized as follows: (1) Most existing micro-level studies on the carbon emission trading policy focus on green innovation or green technological innovation, with limited attention to green management innovation. By using data from Chinese listed enterprises during 2008 to 2022, this study investigates the relationship between the carbon emission trading policy and enterprise green management innovation. It thereby supplements the literature on the impacts of carbon emission trading policies and opens a new analytical perspective on the drivers of green management innovation. (2) The study examines how carbon emission trading policies affect corporate green management innovation. It focuses on two dimensions: internal behavioral constraints and external financial support. Previous research has mainly highlighted industrial structure, energy use, human capital, foreign direct investment, market distortions, financing constraints, and R&D investment. In contrast, this paper emphasizes managerial short-termism and green credit as critical factors. This perspective helps to uncover the micro-level mechanisms linking carbon emission trading policies with corporate green management innovation and provides a deeper understanding of their connection. (3)The paper examines the heterogeneous effects of carbon emission trading policies on corporate green management innovation from the perspectives of managerial factors, firm characteristics, and external environmental conditions. Specifically, it analyzes heterogeneity across five dimensions: whether executives have an environmental background, managerial capability, carbon intensity, industry competition, and firm size. This analysis not only complements existing literature on carbon emission trading policies and green management innovation but also provides important insights for developing countries in building nationwide carbon markets and effectively incentivizing economic actors toward green and low-carbon sustainable development.

Policy background and research hypotheses

Policy background

Carbon emission trading is a key market-based mechanism for emission reduction under the global climate governance framework. It was first established in the Kyoto Protocol and initially implemented in the European Union and certain U.S. states. Since 2013, China has piloted carbon trading in seven regions—Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Chongqing, Hubei, Guangdong, and Shenzhen. Fujian Province officially joined in 2016, providing additional institutional and regulatory experience for a unified national market. In 2021, China officially launched its nationwide carbon emission trading system. The first phase covered more than 2,000 key emission units in the power sector, accounting for over 40% of national CO₂ emissions. This milestone marked China’s entry into a unified national carbon market. Overall, China’s carbon trading policy has evolved progressively from regional pilots to a national market, combining mandatory compliance with market-based trading. Starting with the power sector as an entry point, it has gradually expanded to other high-emission industries. This process has not only driven China’s domestic low-carbon transition but also enhanced the role of China’s carbon market in global climate governance and the international carbon trading system.

Research hypothesis

Carbon emission trading policies provide both external drivers and internal incentives for corporate green management innovation through price mechanisms and institutional constraints. On the one hand, the combination of cap-and-trade raises the explicit cost of emissions, compelling firms to incorporate environmental considerations into business decisions to avoid compliance risks and trading expenditures. On the other hand, the tradability of allowances creates additional revenue opportunities for firms with strong emission reduction performance. This positive incentive encourages firms to pursue green management innovation, reduce unit emissions, and improve resource efficiency. At the same time, the carbon trading system enhances transparency in environmental information disclosure, strengthens oversight from public opinion and capital markets, and motivates firms to advance green transformation for both reputational and financing benefits. Therefore, carbon emission trading serves not only as an environmental regulation tool but also as an institutional arrangement that transforms short-term cost constraints into a long-term strategy for green management innovation.

H1: The implementation of carbon emission trading policies promotes corporate green management innovation.

Managerial myopic behavior

Carbon emission trading policies can curb managerial short-termism through two channels: cost constraints and market incentives. On the one hand, the implementation of such policies internalizes carbon emissions costs, transforming them into actual operating expenses for firms and thereby reshaping managerial decision-making. Under traditional business models, environmental costs were excluded from corporate accounting systems, which led managers to adopt short-term strategies of “pollute first, control later” to secure immediate profits. After the implementation of carbon emission trading, firms must pay a market price for each unit of emissions. This cost constraint forces managers to weigh short-term profits against long-term environmental costs in their decision-making [30]. The cap-and-trade design of emission trading policies makes emission allowances a scarce resource. Firms that adopt short-term, high-emission strategies face the risk of rapidly exhausting their allowances and being forced to purchase additional ones at high prices. This constraint compels managers to incorporate long-term emission reduction plans into current decision-making. At the same time, the disclosure mechanism of the carbon market enhances the transparency of environmental performance. Mandatory monitoring, reporting, and verification requirements transform emission data from private internal information into publicly verifiable market information. This transparency limits managerial attempts to conceal environmental costs for short-term gains and encourages firms to pursue strategies consistent with long-term sustainability [34]. In addition, the carbon market generates price signals that provide managers with an objective benchmark. These benchmarks guide the evaluation of emission reduction costs and benefits across different time horizons. They also help to correct short-termist tendencies in decision-making.

On the other hand, the carbon allowance trading mechanism creates a financial channel for realizing long-term value. The trading market designed under the policy assigns asset attributes and liquidity to emission reduction outcomes, allowing surplus reductions achieved through technological innovation to be converted into tradable carbon assets. This value realization pathway provides a market-based return mechanism for corporate environmental investments. Once managers recognize that emission reduction not only ensures compliance but also generates direct economic benefits, their preferences shift naturally from short-term cost saving to long-term value creation. At the same time, price signals from the carbon market reshape corporate valuation systems [35]. Rising carbon price expectations send a clear signal of long-term policy direction, prompting investors to reassess firms’ carbon risk exposure. Firms with strong low-carbon performance can obtain valuation premiums and easier access to financing. This market feedback strengthens managerial attention to long-term emission reduction investments [36]. The carbon market reshapes the elements of corporate competition by integrating long-term environmental performance with short-term market outcomes, thereby providing managers with a strategic framework for decision-making that accommodates multiple objectives.

Once managers overcome short-termism, the concept of green management shifts. Environmental protection is regarded as a strategic investment rather than a cost burden. Managers proactively integrate sustainability goals into corporate strategic planning [37], seize opportunities for green development, and create differentiated competitive advantages through eco-design [38]. This conceptual change transforms green management from passive compliance to proactive engagement, establishing the foundation for continuous advancements in green management innovation. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis.

H2: Overcoming managerial short-termism constitutes a potential pathway through which carbon emission trading policies influence corporate green management innovation.

Green credit

The carbon emission trading policy promotes corporate access to green credit through two pathways: credit constraints and financing incentives. From the perspective of credit constraints, the policy enhances environmental information transparency by establishing a mandatory carbon emission disclosure system. This enables financial institutions to more accurately assess firms’ environmental risks and green value. Under traditional models, environmental performance information is often opaque, making it difficult for financial institutions to obtain timely knowledge of actual carbon emissions. With the implementation of emission trading, firms are required to disclose carbon emission data regularly, verified by third-party audits. These standardized and verifiable data provide banks and other financial institutions with reliable evidence for credit decision-making [32]. At the same time, corporate participation in carbon emission trading itself sends a credible signal to financial markets. Firms that actively engage in carbon markets and regularly disclose emission data demonstrate sound corporate governance structures and strong environmental risk management capabilities to investors [39]. This signaling effect helps alleviate information asymmetry in green project financing, making financial institutions more willing to provide green credit on favorable terms [20]. In addition, compliance records in the carbon market create a historical credit profile. Firms that maintain consistent compliance build a reputation for environmental responsibility, and this reputational capital offers financial institutions multidimensional evidence for risk assessment.

From the perspective of financing incentives, the carbon emission trading policy enhances corporate green financing capacity by establishing a value discovery mechanism for carbon assets. As a new tradable asset, carbon allowances are measurable, reportable, and verifiable. These characteristics make them high-quality collateral for financing. Based on their market value, financial institutions can develop innovative financial products such as carbon pledge loans and carbon repurchase agreements, thereby providing direct financing support for enterprises engaged in emission reduction [33]. In addition, the policy indirectly improves the financing environment through multiple transmission channels. On the one hand, price signals generated in the carbon market enable financial institutions to more accurately assess environmental risks and guide capital flows toward low-carbon projects through differentiated interest rates [40]. On the other hand, emission data accumulated by firms participating in carbon trading improve the quality of environmental disclosure and reduce the costs of green certification. This makes it easier for qualified firms to obtain green credit support [41]. Moreover, the introduction of carbon asset securitization products has created a direct channel linking green projects with capital markets, thereby providing long-term financial support for large-scale low-carbon transition. These financial innovations not only diversify the range of green credit instruments but also fundamentally ease the financing constraints traditionally faced by environmental projects [42].

Green credit provides dedicated financial support that directly alleviates funding constraints in the process of corporate green management innovation, enabling firms to experiment with advanced environmental management practices. Access to green credit also sends a positive signal of environmental responsibility to the market, and this certification effect enhances a firm’s reputational capital in sustainability [37]. To maintain and strengthen this reputational value, firms proactively reinforce their internal environmental management systems through institutional innovations, such as establishing dedicated green innovation departments and improving environmental accounting systems. A strong green reputation further helps enterprises attract environmentally conscious talent and responsible investors, thereby fostering an agglomeration effect for green management innovation. Accordingly, this paper proposes the following hypothesis.

H3: Expanding the scale of green credit constitutes a potential pathway through which carbon emission trading policies influence corporate green management innovation (Fig. 1).

Methodology

Data source

Considering data availability, the study selects A-share listed enterprises in China from 2008 to 2022 as the research sample. To ensure the reliability of the data, the following screening procedures are applied. First, financial and insurance enterprises are excluded due to significant differences in their financial structures and regulatory environments compared to other industries, as the analysis focuses on real-sector enterprises. Second, loss-making enterprises are excluded, including those labeled as ST, *ST, PT, or under delisting risk, due to potential data anomalies. Third, annual firm-level observations with substantial missing data are removed. Data on green management innovation, as well as financial and governance variables, are obtained from the CSMAR and CNRDS databases. Data on Managerial myopic behavior are manually collected from annual financial reports of listed enterprises published on the CNINFO website.

Variable selection

Dependent variable

Following the approach of Wang et al. [43], five indicators are constructed to comprehensively measure green management innovation (Gmi), and their sum is used to obtain a composite score. Specifically, considering data availability, the composite score is calculated based on whether an enterprise has obtained ISO 14,001 and ISO 9001 certifications, as reported in the Environmental regulation and certification disclosure section of the CSMAR environmental database. In addition, information on environmental management systems, environmental education and training, and environmental campaigns is extracted from the Management Disclosure section. The values of these five indicators are aggregated to serve as a proxy for enterprise green management innovation.

Independent variable

The core explanatory variable, carbon emission trading, is defined as a binary variable. This study includes all regions in China where carbon emission trading policy pilots have been implemented, including Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangdong, Hubei, Chongqing, Shenzhen, and Fujian. Among them, Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangdong, Hubei, Chongqing, and Shenzhen launched their pilot programs in 2013 and 2014, while Fujian began its pilot in 2016. A policy dummy variable is constructed based on the variation in the implementation timing across regions. The variable takes the value of 1 if an enterprise is located in a pilot region and the policy has already been implemented; otherwise, it is assigned a value of 0.

Control variables

To control for the influence of other factors on enterprise green management innovation, several key control variables related to governance structure and financial condition are included in the model. (1) Firm listing age (Age) is measured as the natural logarithm of the difference between the current year and the firm’s listing year. (2) Total debt ratio (Lev) is calculated as the ratio of total liabilities to total assets at the end of the period. (3) Firm size (Size) is measured as the natural logarithm of total assets at the end of the period. (4) Cash flow (Cash) is defined as the ratio of net cash flow from operating activities to total assets at the end of the period. (5) Return on assets (ROA) is the ratio of net profit to shareholders’ equity. (6) Nature of ownership (Soe) is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the enterprise is state-owned, and 0 otherwise. (7) Number of independent directors (Idp) is measured by the total number of independent directors on the board. Descriptive statistics for the variables used in this study are presented in Table 1.

Robustness tests

The core finding of this study is that the carbon emission trading policy significantly enhances the level of enterprises green management innovation. A series of robustness checks are conducted to ensure the validity of the model specification and the credibility of the estimated results.

Parallel trend test

To assess the applicability of the DID model, a parallel trend test is conducted prior to model estimation. This test verifies whether the trends in green management innovation are consistent between the treatment and control groups before participation in the carbon emission trading policy. To further examine the pre-policy parallel trend assumption and investigate potential lag effects, an event study is employed to evaluate the dynamic impact of the carbon emission trading policy. Using the year prior to a firm’s participation in the policy as the reference period, the estimated results presented in Fig. 2 indicate that the coefficients before the policy intervention are statistically insignificant. This suggests no significant differences in green management innovation between the treatment and control groups before the policy, supporting the parallel trend assumption. After the policy implementation, the estimated coefficients become statistically significant, indicating that the carbon emission trading policy significantly promotes green management innovation.

Parallel trend test

Placebo test

To address potential concerns that the positive effect of the carbon emission trading policy on green management innovation may be driven by unobserved confounding factors, a placebo test is conducted. Specifically, the impact of the carbon emission trading policy on enterprise green management innovation is randomly generated by computer and repeated 1000 times. The probability density distribution of the estimated coefficients in Fig. 3 indicates that the randomly generated policy shocks show no significant effects. This supports the conclusion that the observed positive effect of the carbon emission trading policy on green management innovation is genuine. Therefore, the effect is unlikely to be attributable to omitted variable bias.

To mitigate the potential bias caused by measurement error in green management innovation, this study adopts the approach of Li et al. [44] and remeasures green management innovation. Considering that the ISO 9001 standard encompasses fundamental requirements of quality management systems, whether an enterprise obtains ISO 9001 certification is used as a proxy variable. A value of 1 is assigned if the enterprise is certified, and 0 otherwise. The estimated results are reported in column (1) of Table 3. The coefficient is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that the previously identified relationship remains robust after replacing the dependent variable.

Excluding contemporaneous policies

Since 2010, the National Development and Reform Commission has launched three batches of low-carbon city pilot programs. Given that these pilot regions exhibit inherent differences in carbon trading mechanisms and institutional settings compared to non-pilot areas, a dummy variable, LC, is introduced. LC equals 1 if a city is subject to the pilot policy in a given year and 0 otherwise. This variable is included to control for the potential confounding effect of the low-carbon pilot policy. Estimated results reported in column (2) of Table 3 show that the regression coefficient remains significantly positive at the 1% level, confirming the robustness of the main conclusion.

Changing the sample period

To mitigate the potential influence of the 2008 global financial crisis and the nationwide launch of the carbon emission trading market in 2021, the sample period is adjusted to cover the years from 2009 to 2020. As shown in column (3) of Table 3, the implementation of the carbon emission trading policy continues to significantly enhance green management innovation among enterprises. These findings are consistent with the underlying theoretical mechanism and earlier empirical results, further validating the reliability of the study.

Controlling for industry fixed effects

Considering the possibility that the carbon emission trading policy may target specific industries, industry fixed effects are included to test the robustness of the empirical results. As reported in column (4) of Table 3, the carbon emission trading policy continues to exert a significantly positive impact on enterprise green management innovation after controlling for industry fixed effects. The coefficient remains significant at the 1% level, confirming consistency with the baseline findings.

Heterogeneity analysis

To capture the contextual boundaries of the carbon emission trading policy and enhance the interpretability of results, we conduct heterogeneity tests from five complementary perspectives: executives’ environmental background, managerial capability, carbon intensity, industry competition, and firm size. These perspectives correspond to four mechanism chains: cognition and governance, constraint, competition, and resources. Executives’ environmental background and managerial capability determine how well firms absorb policy information and translate it into organizational action. Carbon intensity reflects the urgency of environmental constraints. Industry competition indicates the degree of external pressure. Firm size illustrates the extent of resource endowment and organizational slack.

Distinguishing executives’ environmental backgrounds

Considering that executives with environmental expertise are more likely to transform carbon constraints into drivers of governance and strategic upgrading, firms led by executives with different environmental backgrounds may vary in their understanding of green policies and in their capacity to promote organizational change. Those with an environmental background tend to better comprehend the resource attributes of carbon emission rights and regard carbon constraints as strategic opportunities for technological upgrading rather than mere compliance costs [45]. Such executives are more inclined to adopt fundamental green management innovation strategies, including the establishment of carbon asset management systems and the implementation of life-cycle environmental assessments. In contrast, executives without an environmental background are more likely to view the carbon emission trading policy as a compliance requirement and respond with end-of-pipe solutions, such as purchasing carbon quotas or making partial technical improvements. Based on this, it is hypothesized that the carbon emission trading policy more significantly promotes green management innovation in enterprises led by executives with an environmental background than in those without.

The data on executives’ environmental background are obtained from personal resume information published on the Sina Finance website. If a resume contains keywords such as “environment”,“environmentalprotection”,“new energy”, “clean energy”, “ecology”,“low-carbon”, “sustainability”, “energy-saving” or “green”, the executive is considered to have an environmental background. Based on this, the number of environmentally oriented executives is calculated. Distinguishing the managerial capability of enterprises

Given that differences in organizational and execution capabilities affect the absorption of policy information and the speed of institutional implementation, variations in managerial ability lead to different capacities for overcoming short-termist behavior. Enterprises with stronger managerial capability typically possess well-developed strategic planning systems and long-term incentive mechanisms. When facing carbon quota constraints, senior managers in these enterprises are more likely to integrate carbon reduction goals into long-term development strategies rather than focus solely on short-term compliance. Strong organizational execution and resource allocation capabilities ensure the continuity of investment in green management innovation, thereby avoiding disruption due to short-term performance pressures [46]. In contrast, enterprises with weaker managerial capability often lack long-term strategic planning and effective incentive systems. When responding to the carbon emission trading policy, managers in these enterprises tend to favor low-cost, quick-result strategies such as filing for green utility patents, which represent relatively superficial forms of innovation. Due to limited resource allocation capacity, these enterprises are more likely to purchase carbon quotas or make basic optimizations to production processes rather than pursue substantive green management innovation. Based on this analysis, it is hypothesized that the carbon emission trading policy is more effective in promoting green management innovation among enterprises with stronger managerial capability.

This study further examines the heterogeneous effects of carbon emission trading policy on green management innovation by distinguishing the managerial capability of enterprises. Managerial capability is measured by management expenses, with the sample median used as the threshold. Enterprises with management expenses above the median are classified as having strong managerial capability, while those below are considered to have weak managerial capability. Distinguishing enterprise carbon emission intensity

Carbon intensity, as a key indicator of corporate environmental performance, directly affects a firm’s ability to secure green financial support, thereby leading to heterogeneous effects of the carbon emission trading policy. Enterprises with higher carbon emission intensity face stricter quota constraints and greater pressure to reduce emissions, which strengthens their motivation to achieve emission reductions through green management innovation. After the implementation of the carbon emission trading policy, these enterprises are more likely to obtain green credit support from financial institutions due to their scale advantages and more comprehensive environmental information disclosure.

On the one hand, higher carbon emission intensity indicates greater potential for emission reductions, which increases the willingness of banks to finance their green transformation projects [47]. On the other hand, such enterprises typically maintain more advanced carbon management systems and can provide the emission data and reduction plans required for green credit approval. In contrast, enterprises with lower carbon emission intensity often struggle to attract financial attention due to limited emission reduction potential. Furthermore, their weaker capacity for environmental disclosure and carbon management often fails to meet green credit evaluation standards. Limited access to green credit reduces the financial resources available for green management innovation. Distinguishing industry competition intensity

The degree of industry competition significantly influences the effectiveness of carbon emission trading policies in promoting green management innovation. This heterogeneity arises mainly from the moderating role of market competition pressure on corporate strategic decisions. In highly competitive industries, the implementation of carbon emission trading policies amplifies their effects through a dual mechanism that helps firms overcome managerial short-termism. On the one hand, intense market competition compels enterprises to pursue substantive innovation to maintain a competitive advantage. The constraint of carbon quotas further reinforces this motivation, encouraging enterprises to view green management innovation as a critical means of differentiation. On the other hand, the survival pressure in competitive markets increases managerial risk aversion [48]. By internalizing environmental costs, the carbon emission trading policy raises the operating risks associated with traditional high-carbon production models, thereby driving enterprises to shift toward green management innovation with long-term value.In contrast, enterprises in less competitive industries face weaker competitive pressure and insufficient market elimination mechanisms, leading to a more passive response to the carbon emission trading policy. These enterprises often satisfy only the minimum compliance requirements and tend to adopt short-term measures such as purchasing carbon quotas, rather than undertaking substantive green management innovation.

This study uses the HHI to measure the intensity of industry competition. Industries with HHI values above the median are classified as having high competition intensity, while those below the median are classified as having low competition intensity. The findings indicate that the carbon emission trading policy is more effective in promoting green management innovation among enterprises in highly competitive industries, thereby supporting the hypothesis.

Distinguishing by firm size

Considering that firm size influences the ability to acquire and allocate resources, it may play an important role in shaping how carbon emission trading policies affect green management innovation. The trading system requires strict information disclosure and compliance. Large firms benefit from stronger governance structures, greater financing capacity, and higher reputational pressure [49]. These advantages enable them to transform emission reduction performance into credit that is more readily recognized by financial institutions. As a result, they can more easily access green credit. This credit not only lowers financing costs but also provides sufficient funding for large-scale R&D and systematic green management innovation [50], turning external regulatory constraints into internal drivers of long-term green transition.

In contrast, smaller firms face disadvantages in transparency and credit conditions. Although carbon trading policies and related green financial products offer some financing opportunities, the scale of green credit they can obtain is limited. Most of these funds are directed toward process optimization or energy efficiency improvements, making their innovation pathway more incremental. Therefore, the impact of carbon trading policies on green management innovation differs significantly by firm size. Large firms, with resource and institutional advantages, are more likely to lead in the green transition. Based on this reasoning, we hypothesize that the positive effect of carbon emission trading policies on green management innovation is stronger for large firms than for small firms.

This study uses the median firm size as the threshold: firms above the median are classified as large firms, while those below are classified as small firms. Columns (1) and (2) of Table 8 report the regression results for small and large firms, respectively. The findings show that the carbon emission trading policy has a statistically stronger effect on promoting green management innovation in large firms, thereby confirming the previous hypothesis.

Based on the above findings, the effectiveness of carbon emission trading policies in promoting green management innovation depends on governance capacity and awareness, emission reduction constraints, market pressure, and resource endowment. Accordingly, regulators should implement differentiated benchmarks and tighter quota allocations, accompanied by performance-linked incentives for high-emission-intensity firms. On the financial side, green credit and capacity-building support should be directed toward firms with weaker governance or without executives who have an environmental background. In industries characterized by high competition or high concentration, carbon intensity benchmarks and stricter MRV should be enforced in coordination with antitrust policies. For small and medium-sized enterprises, transitional periods and cost-mitigation tools should be provided.

Conclusions and discussion

Based on panel data from A-share listed enterprises in China from 2008 to 2022, this study investigates the effect of the carbon emission trading policy on green management innovation. It also investigates heterogeneity related to executives’ environmental background, managerial capability, carbon intensity, industry competition, and firm size, as well as the mechanisms through which the policy influences corporate green management innovation. The findings are as follows:

(1) The carbon emission trading policy significantly promotes corporate green management innovation, and this conclusion remains robust after a series of robustness checks. Existing literature has mainly focused on the effects of the policy on green technology innovation [20, 32] and green innovation [21, 29–31, 33], while paying insufficient attention to its influence on internal management systems and governance practices. The evidence presented in this paper shows that carbon market policies not only stimulate innovation on the technology side but also enhance green management innovation by encouraging strategic adjustments and institutional improvements at the managerial level. This finding expands the analytical scope of carbon market research.

(2) From the dual perspectives of internal behavioral constraints and external financial support, this study reveals that carbon emission trading policies promote management innovation by mitigating managerial short-termism and improving the supply of green credit. Existing research has mostly emphasized how external pressure [30] and firm capabilities [31]– [32] compel firms to pursue green innovation, yet has paid limited attention to the ways policies influence innovation through internal governance and financing channels. The findings of this paper suggest that carbon markets also play an important role in optimizing firms’ governance structures and financing environments, providing new micro-level evidence to explain policy effects.

(3) This paper examines the heterogeneous effects of carbon emission trading policies from the perspectives of firm characteristics and market environment, offering a valuable supplement to the existing literature. Prior studies have highlighted the importance of internal conditions [33] and external scrutiny [29] in shaping green innovation. This study further demonstrates that firms led by executives with environmental backgrounds and those with stronger managerial capabilities are more likely to achieve improvements in green management innovation under the influence of carbon markets. Moreover, firms with higher carbon intensity, greater industry competition, and larger scale exhibit stronger policy responsiveness. These findings underscore the significance of governance capacity and market pressure while also revealing that executives’ environmental backgrounds amplify the transmission of policy effects.

In sum, the analysis confirms that the carbon emission trading policy significantly advances corporate green management innovation while also clarifying its mechanisms and heterogeneous impacts. These findings broaden the theoretical scope of carbon market research and contribute fresh evidence to the policy-behavior-performance nexus. On the practical side, they highlight how firm characteristics and market conditions condition policy outcomes, underscoring the need for adaptive, firm-specific approaches in implementation. The results provide a foundation for refining carbon market design, strengthening governance capacity, and enhancing the synergy between policy instruments and market forces. The following section outlines targeted policy recommendations.

First, improve carbon market policy design and establish differentiated incentive mechanisms. Given that carbon emission trading policies have stronger effects on promoting green management innovation in high-emission firms, it is advisable to adopt stricter quota allocation rules for key emission-intensive industries. At the same time, a dedicated subsidy fund should be created to support green management innovation. Firms that actively innovate could be rewarded with additional emission allowances or tax incentives, forming a “constraint–incentive” dual policy driver.

Second, strengthen executives’ environmental awareness and optimize corporate governance structures. Since executives with environmental backgrounds significantly enhance the policy response, it is recommended to establish training programs for executives and incorporate environmental performance into executive evaluation systems. Firms should also be encouraged to appoint independent directors with environmental expertise to improve decision-making in green innovation.

Third, promote synergy between market competition and carbon policies. As the policy effect is more pronounced in highly competitive industries, measures should be taken to maintain market vitality through antitrust enforcement. At the same time, carbon leakage protection mechanisms should be introduced in key industries to prevent industrial relocation caused by stringent carbon policies. For monopolistic industries, benchmark management based on carbon intensity could be implemented to stimulate innovation through industry comparisons.

It should be noted that this study has certain limitations. To begin with, the analysis is based primarily on a sample of A-share listed companies, which may involve selection bias and survivorship bias. The findings may not apply to non-listed firms, and future research should include small- and medium-sized enterprises by linking business registration databases and environmental reports. In addition, the measurement of green management innovation relies on public disclosure. Such data may suffer from strategic disclosure, inconsistent standards, and reporting delays, leading to errors in classification and intensity. Finally, regional carbon trading pilots differ in institutional design. Differences exist in quota allocation rules, the ratio of free to auctioned permits, verification and enforcement practices, and the use of price ceilings or floors. Future studies should conduct comparative analyses that take these regional differences into account.
