Adherence to HbA1c testing guidelines in type 2 diabetes patients: A mixed-methods retrospective cross-sectional study in Bahrain’s primary healthcare centers
Authors: Fatema Mohamed Alaradi, Bashayer H. Ebrahim, Fajer Ali Alhaddad, Eman Al Mulla, Sara Matrook, Mohammad Sameer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounts for over 90% of diabetes cases worldwide. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) testing helps manage it effectively. Despite national guidelines in Bahrain, physician adherence varies due to knowledge, resource, and system barriers. This study assesses adherence and influencing factors in Bahrain’s primary healthcare centers.Methods:A mixed-method retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at eight primary healthcare centers (PHCs) in Bahrain, including 352 T2DM patient’s electronic records from 2017 to 2020. Inclusion criteria comprised adult patients (≥18 years) with a confirmed T2DM diagnosis of at least one year’s duration and at least one PHC visit during 2020. Data regarding demographics, HbA1c requests, and non-communicable disease clinic attendance were collected. Additionally, 375 PHC physicians completed structured surveys assessing their adherence to guidelines and barriers they faced. SPSS was used for descriptive, comparative, and multivariate statistical analyses.Results:Among 352 T2DM patients (57.4% female), significant testing gaps were identified: 13.4% had no test requests, and 18.8% had no completed tests despite guideline recommendations. Non-communicable disease clinic attendance significantly correlated with higher HbA1c test requests and completions (P < 0.001). Key barriers to testing included resource limitations, time constraints, and patient non-compliance.Conclusion:This study highlighted the gaps between diabetes care guidelines and practice in Bahrain’s PHCs, and also emphasized the physician support, patient attendance, and targeted interventions for improvement.

Introduction

Over 90% of all diabetes cases are accounted by type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). It is characterized by insulin resistance, where the body's tissues exhibit a reduced response to insulin.[1] Insufficient insulin production or insulin resistance in the body results in the inability to maintain blood glucose at normal levels. T2DM is often related to lifestyle factors like sedentary behavior, obesity, and unhealthy diet.[234] T2DM management relies on regular monitoring for the prevention of complications and patient outcomes. The blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) must be monitored regularly.[5] Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) testing is an important diagnostic tool that can give an average blood glucose level over the previous 2 to 3 months.[6] HbA1c is commonly known as the gold standard for monitoring and treatment of T2DM. National and International guidelines recommend that patients with stable glycemic control undergo HbA1c testing at least every 6 months.[789]

Bahrain, like many countries, has implemented national diabetes guidelines stating the need for consistent HbA1c testing. In 2022, the T2DM prevalence in Bahrain was 9% and had an incidence of 14.7%.[10] The need for public health initiatives to reduce this burden of T2DM was stated in various studies.[111213]

Regular HbA1c testing is significant for early detection of poor glycemic control.[1415] Physicians may face challenges like time constraints, limited resources, administrative barriers, patient factors, or systemic barriers.[915]

This study is beneficial for practitioners as it identifies gaps in adherence to HbA1c testing guidelines, enabling improved clinical decision-making, optimized patient monitoring, and better allocation of healthcare resources. For patients, improved adherence can lead to the timely detection of poor glycemic control, reduction in diabetes-related complications, and overall enhancement in long-term health outcomes and quality of life.

This study aims to assess the adherence of physicians to HbA1c testing guidelines in T2DM patients in Bahrain’s primary healthcare centers (PHCs) and analyze the factors influencing adherence.

Materials and Methods

Study design

A mixed-methods retrospective cross-sectional study design was conducted among patients with T2DM attending general clinics in eight PHCs in Bahrain during 2020. The study comprised two components: (1) a retrospective audit of electronic health records (EHRs) and (2) a cross-sectional physician survey. These centers were selected to ensure broad representation of the healthcare delivery system across Bahrain, covering different geographical regions and patient demographics. This approach enabled assessment of variability in HbA1c testing practices across urban, suburban, and rural settings.

The study aimed to assess physicians’ adherence to national diabetes guidelines, with a focus on HbA1c testing practices, and to identify barriers to appropriate test utilization. The physician survey complemented the audit data.

Study population

The study included 352 T2DM patients selected from the EHR of eight PHCs using a stratified random sampling approach to ensure proportional representation across centers. Stratification was based on geographical location and patient volume to minimize selection bias. Inclusion criteria were: (1) adult patients (≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of T2DM for at least one year; (2) receiving either oral hypoglycemic agents, insulin therapy, or a combination; (3) at least one documented visit to the PHC during 2020; and (4) complete demographic and clinical data available in EHRs. Patients with gestational diabetes, type 1 diabetes, or incomplete records were excluded.

The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula with 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an expected adherence rate of 50% (based on previous regional studies), yielding a minimum required sample of 345 patients. The final sample of 352 patients thus provided adequate statistical power.

Additionally, a survey was conducted among 375 physicians working in PHCs across Bahrain, representing approximately 85% of the eligible physician population in these centers. Physician participation was voluntary and anonymous.

Data collection

Patient data

For determining frequency of HbA1c requests patient’s records from PHC were reviewed. The tests were conducted from 2017 to 2020. The data regarding patient demographics, such as age and gender, the number of HbA1c requests, attendance at non-communicable disease (NCD) clinics, and the number of tests performed were collected. The patient distributions across various PHCs were also well documented.

Physician survey

A structured questionnaire was distributed to physicians in PHCs to evaluate their knowledge of diabetes guidelines, particularly HbA1c testing, and to identify barriers to requesting the test. The survey was conducted online using Google Forms with participation confirmed by the respondent’s selection of the agree option in the consent form. The survey consists of details such as demographic information, education level, years of experience, physician role, and use of American Diabetes Association guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the patient and physician data, which includes frequencies, percentages, means, medians, and standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Comparative analysis was performed to identify associations between patient variables such as age and attendance at NCD clinics and the frequency of HbA1c requests or tests. Chi-square tests were used to analyze associations between categorical variables such as NCD attendance and HbA1c testing. Independent t-tests were applied to compare continuous variables between groups based on NCD clinic attendance.

Additionally, multivariate logistic regression modeling was performed to identify independent predictors of HbA1c testing adherence (defined as ≥2 tests per year as per national guidelines) while controlling for potential confounding variables including age, gender, PHC location, and attending physician characteristics. The P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Distribution across PHCs

Patients were distributed across eight PHCs in Bahrain that represent different geographic regions. The Al-Naim Health Center had the highest proportion of 71 patients (20.2%), and the Kuwait Health Center had 60 patients (17.0%). These patient numbers at these centers may indicate a higher population density in these areas or greater access to healthcare services.

However, Jidhafs Health Center had the lowest number of patients, 12 patients (3.4%). Other PHCs like Muharraq Health Center and NBB Health Center - Dair also had low patient numbers with 15 (4.3%) and 16 (4.5%) patients, respectively. From this, it can be observed that there were possible disparities in healthcare utilization or population size differences across the regions. Also, Ahmed Ali Kanoo Health Center and Sh. Jaber Al Ahmed Al Sabah Health Center had equal patient numbers that was 40 patients (11.4%) each. The Sh. Sabah Al-Salem Health Center had 28 patients (8.0%).

HbA1c testing frequency

From Figure 2, it has been observed that there was a gap between the number of HbA1c requests made and the actual tests conducted. In 352 patients, 47 patients (13.4%) did not have any HbA1c test requested. The majority of patients, about 31.5%, had two HbA1c tests requested, followed by 29.0% with a single request. Some patients had three (15.3%) or four (7.7%) requests for HbA1c testing. Requests beyond four were rare, with 2.3% had five requests, and only one patient each (0.3%) received six, seven, or eight HbA1c test requests.

Association between NCD clinic attendance and HbA1c testing

In patients attending the NCD clinic, the mean number of HbA1c requests was 2.38 (SD 1.308) versus 1.45 (SD 1.140) in non-attendees, a significant difference (P = 0.000). Likewise, mean tests performed were 1.83 (SD 1.091) compared to 1.18 (SD 0.988) among those not attending (P = 0.000). Attendance at the NCD clinic was positively associated with both more frequent requests and higher test completion rates. This suggests physicians in NCD clinics request and complete testing more often.

HbA1c requests and NCD clinic attendance

The association between the number of HbA1c requests and patient attendance at the NCD clinic in 2020 is shown in Figure 2. Attendance significantly correlated with requests (P < 0.001). Among non-attending patients, 80.9% had no tests requested, versus 19.1% who had at least one and attended. As the number of requests increased, attendance rose, respectively: one request, 26.5% attended; two, 51.4%; three, 66.7%; four, 77.8%; and five or more, 63.6% attended overall.

Figure 4 presents the association between the number of HbA1c tests done in 2020 and patient attendance at the NCD clinic. There is an overall significant association (P = 0.000) between NCD clinic attendance and the number of 2020 HbA1c tests. Among non-attendees, 75.8% had no tests and 65.0% had one. Clinic attendance increased with testing frequency, with 55.3% of those with two tests attended, 71.9% with three, and 78.6% with four. Of the small subgroup with five or more tests, 66.6% unexpectedly did not attend the clinic.

Multivariate predictors of HbA1c testing adherence

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed for identifying the independent predictors that adhere to the testing guidelines of HbA1c in Table 4. After the adjustment of potential confounding variables, the NCD clinic attendance was observed as a strong independent predictor of guideline adherence with odds ratio (OR) as 3.24, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.18–4.82 with P <0.001. Patient age also showed a significant relationship and it was observed that older patients over 65 years are less likely to get the guideline-concordant testing compared to younger patients with OR values as 0.68, 95% CI 0.47–0.98 and P = 0.039.

Discussion

This study evaluated adherence to HbA1c testing among T2DM patients in Bahrain’s PHCs and identified physician-related barriers to guideline adherence. Analysis of 352 patient records and 375 physician responses revealed disparities in testing practices, with 13.4% of patients lacking test requests and 18.8% failing to complete tests. Attendance at NCD clinics was associated with higher testing rates. The combined analysis of patient data and physician surveys enabled triangulation of practices and perceived barriers. Inclusion of all PHCs ensured broad primary care coverage; however, the single-country design may limit generalizability. Physician-reported barriers may involve recall bias, and lack of detailed socioeconomic and behavioral data limits individual-level insights. This study uniquely integrates patient adherence and physician perspectives, unlike prior studies focusing on either aspect alone.[916]

The gender distribution in this study was 57.4% female and 42.6% male among 352 T2DM patients, similar to Khalaf et al.,[10] where 67% of 613 participants were female, indicating a higher prevalence among Bahraini women. In contrast, Alawainati et al.[17] reported 49.5% females among 721 patients, suggesting near-equal distribution. These findings reflect gender-related factors rather than biological differences. El-Kebbi et al.[18] noted no clear sex predisposition, with obesity and sedentary lifestyle as key risks. Differences may also reflect healthcare-seeking behavior, as Azad et al.[19] reported greater access challenges for women.

Unlike earlier Bahraini studies that primarily described demographic or clinical profiles, the present study further links gender distribution with healthcare utilization patterns such as HbA1c testing adherence, providing a more functional interpretation of gender differences.

The relationship between NCD clinic attendance and improved HbA1c testing highlights the importance of structured diabetes management programs. Almousawi et al.[20] reported that poor testing adherence is associated with increased HbA1c values, while recommended testing improves glycemic control. Similarly, Imai et al.[9] demonstrated that higher adherence to testing frequency improves outcomes and reduces chronic kidney disease risk. This relationship extends beyond compliance, as patient–provider interactions enhance self-care behaviors.[2122] Evidence also shows that medication adherence and regular follow-up reduce HbA1c levels and complications.[2324] Regular monitoring enables early intervention and reduces complications such as chronic kidney disease.[2526] The present study highlights the role of structured PHC-based NCD clinics in improving adherence.

Our results were consistent with global patterns of suboptimal guideline adherence. Lian and Liang[16] reported that only seven percent of patients met recommended testing frequencies, while Renard et al.[27] found over half of a Luxembourg cohort had no HbA1c tests. Imai et al.[9] reported 32%–68% received minimum annual tests. In contrast, our study showed higher completion (81.2%), suggesting relatively better adherence in Bahrain’s PHC system despite persistent global gaps.

The barriers observed in our study were time constraints, patient non-compliance, and limited resources, and this reflects a complex role of system, provider, and patient factors. Financial constraints affect test adherence mainly for the socioeconomically disadvantaged patients.[2728293031] While Bahrain provides subsidized healthcare, indirect costs like transportation may affect adherence. Psychosocial factors may also influence compliance as Chepulis et al.[29] identified that inadequate disease understanding and poor patient–provider communication affects the self-care behaviors. Huang and Shiyanbola[32] showed that health education levels moderate intervention effectiveness. This suggests that the education strategies must be personalized for various populations. Additionally, recent regional and Asian studies emphasize that low health literacy, cultural beliefs, and regimen complexity significantly contribute to poor adherence and glycemic control.[3334] Compared to prior studies, the present research uniquely captures physician-reported operational barriers such as workflow limitations, which are less frequently quantified in earlier adherence studies.

Several pathways emerged from our physician-reported barriers. Adjustments of the workflow can help in addressing the time constraint, and also, the improved decision support tools combined into the EHR can help in facilitating the testing systematically. Alwazae et al.[30] identified that the reminder systems improved the screening adherence for the complications of diabetes. The education of patients shows another intervention point as the structured educational programs improving the self-management behaviors and clinical outcomes.[425] This aligns with newer evidence suggesting that digital health interventions, reminder systems, and personalized education significantly enhance adherence and long-term diabetes outcomes.[343536] The present study contributes by linking these intervention strategies directly with physician-identified barriers within PHCs, thereby providing actionable system-level recommendations.

Practical recommendations

Four strategies are proposed. First, integrate automated HbA1c reminders into PHC health record systems with notifications via patient portals, SMS, and email. Second, reserve protected testing time through dedicated point-of-care HbA1c slots within NCD clinics. Third, develop culturally sensitive, literacy-appropriate educational modules using visual aids, group workshops, and telehealth. Fourth, reduce indirect cost barriers through transport vouchers or mobile testing units.

Future research

Future studies should include randomized controlled trials to evaluate EHR-based reminders and protected clinic slots. Mixed-methods research is needed to explore patient-level factors such as cultural beliefs and health literacy. Longitudinal cohort studies linking HbA1c testing frequency to complications, healthcare utilization, and cost outcomes are essential for policy development.

Conclusion

The present study provides insights into adherence to HbA1c testing practices among T2DM patients in Bahrain’s PHCs. Disparities between guideline recommendations and real-world implementation were observed, with 13.4% of patients having no test requests and 18.8% not completing tests. Attendance at NCD clinics was associated with improved testing rates, highlighting the importance of structured care. Barriers such as resource limitations, time constraints, and patient compliance were identified. Physician demographics also influenced adherence levels. Targeted interventions, improved training, and better resource allocation are essential to enhance HbA1c monitoring and overall diabetic care in PHCs.

Limitations and future aspects

This study is limited by its single-country design and reliance on physician-reported data, which may introduce bias. The lack of detailed patient-level variables restricts deeper insights. Future research should include multicenter, longitudinal, and interventional studies to evaluate system-level strategies such as electronic reminders and structured workflows for improving HbA1c adherence and outcomes.
