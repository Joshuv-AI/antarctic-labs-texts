What Does Indian Dermatology Online Journal Expect in a Review Article?
Authors: Chander Grover, Dipankar De
Published: 2026

Indian Dermatology Online Journal (IDOJ) is a leading dermatology journal, with a growing number of submissions, readership, and impact factor. This growth is sustained by the quality of articles published in every issue. Good quality review articles are a significant draw because they provide readers a “one-stop” synthesis on a focused topic. Clinicians use reviews to update practice, and researchers use them to identify research gaps; they also inform clinical guidelines.[1] IDOJ therefore solicits high-quality reviews that enhance the practice of its readers. This editorial intends to inform prospective authors on the nitty-gritty of writing a good quality review.

From the journal’s point of view, a review article (or literature review) should collate relevant literature (through defined search strategies), analyze and synthesize existing research on a specific topic, summarize the current state of knowledge, and identify gaps and future directions.[2] It should offer practical clinical takeaways, rather than being a simple assembly of facts. In today’s fast-evolving AI world, it has become easy to generate an “AI Overview” that looks plausible but lacks clinical judgement, context, and critical appraisal. The purpose of a scientific review is to add the “human layer” of interpretation that helps readers make sense of the evidence.

Before writing, authors should choose a topic of relevance with a clear scope, and avoid redundancy. A communication with the editor-in-chief is strongly advised, to avoid duplication or doubling of effort. Reviews should be planned around clear question(s) and objectives, and should aim to answer what we know, what is uncertain, and what should be studied next in that topic?

A comprehensive and unbiased literature search is essential for all review types. Authors should transparently report the strategy followed for literature search, key words or MeSH words used, the databases accessed, quantitative results found (like number of studies or reports) and the basis for the selection of included studies or exclusion of other published work. IDOJ expects all submitted reviews to clearly specify three key elements: The question(s) asked, the methods used to collect relevant literature, and the synthesis of available research. The main text should therefore follow the IMRAD headings (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion). We currently require the abstract for reviews to be unstructured as per journal instructions.

Authors should specify the type of review they are submitting.[3] In IDOJ, systematic reviews (without meta-analysis), narrative reviews, and scoping reviews are considered under the “Review” section. Meta-analysis, when performed with pooled quantitative analysis, is generally considered an Original Article in this journal. For details on various review types and reporting guidelines, prospective authors can refer to published literature on this topic.[123]

A systematic review submitted to IDOJ should define a focused research question and use a rigorous, explicit, transparent, and reproducible methodology to retrieve and appraise literature with minimal bias. A protocol needs to be established beforehand, and with pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies should then be screened, evaluated, and synthesized accordingly. Reporting should comply with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) and other relevant frameworks when applicable.[456]

IDOJ also accepts narrative reviews, which remain valuable for clinically oriented topics and also for emerging areas where formal systematic reviews may not yet be feasible. Narrative reviews are interpretive by design, but their content should still be based on a comprehensive search of relevant literature.[78] Thus, a narrative review submitted to IDOJ should also follow IMRAD headings and transparently describe the search approach (databases and key words/MeSH terms) and the basis for including/excluding studies. Reporting methodology is required for transparency in the literature search, rather than enforcing the level of methodological rigor expected of systematic reviews. It helps avoid selective information collection. McAlister et al. analyzed review articles in medical journals, reporting that less than a quarter of the articles provided methods used for evaluation or synthesis of evidence, only one third focused on a topic, and only one-half provided actual quantitative data.[9]

A scoping review maps the existing literature to identify key concepts, theories, evidence types, and research gaps in a broad topic. It is a rapid review, more like a streamlined systematic review, but conducted in a shorter timeframe, to meet urgent decision-making needs. Authors should follow appropriate methodological guidance when undertaking a scoping review, such as the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis.

Umbrella, rapid, integrative, and critical reviews may synthesize and analyze findings from multiple existing studies, but they are heterogeneous in purpose and methodology. Depending on their scope and format, IDOJ may prefer to consider them under other relevant publication sections (for example, letters to editor).

In the current publishing environment, IDOJ frequently encounters submissions that are redundant, structurally weak, and overly dependent on AI-generated text. We encourage authors to avoid “information overload” and instead provide a “thoughtful synthesis” that reflects careful reading, clinical judgement, and transparent methodology. We are thankful to our authors for their efforts, and we request all prospective authors to go through the “instructions to authors” on our website and comply with the same, while planning a review and submitting it to the journal. The value of a review is associated with what has been done, what has been found and how these findings are presented. We hope that with a clarity on the types, purposes, and methods of reviews, researchers can better plan and execute their work, enhancing the quality and impact on readers.
