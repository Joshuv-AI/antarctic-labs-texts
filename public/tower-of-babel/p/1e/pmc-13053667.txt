Magnitude of Hypertension and Associated Factors Among Diabetic Patients at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Yazachew Mossu, Amanuel Anegagregn, Kinde Wubeshet, Akberet Semere, Ermias Abera, Endrias Markos, Teketel Tadesse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHypertension (HTN) is a frequent comorbidity in patients with diabetes mellitus (DM), increasing the risk of cardiovascular and renal complications. Despite global recognition of the coexistence of HTN and DM, region‐specific data in southern Ethiopia, particularly the Sidama region, remain limited. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of HTN and identify associated factors among diabetic patients attending Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH).MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted between September and November 2023 among 223 diabetic patients selected through systematic random sampling. Data were collected using structured questionnaire coupled with review of medical records. HTN was defined as blood pressure of ≥ 130/80 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive medication. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with HTN. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, with significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe prevalence of HTN was 52.9% (95% CI: 46.0–60.0) among diabetic patients. Of this, only 11.7% cases achieved target blood pressure control. Being female sex (AOR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.21–0.90), age ≥ 60 years (AOR = 5.90; 95% CI: 1.62–20.87), overweight status (AOR = 3.51; 95% CI: 1.58–7.77), family history of HTN (AOR = 3.61; 95% CI: 1.14–11.40), chronic kidney disease (AOR = 3.61; 95% CI: 1.04–12.50), and dyslipidemia (AOR = 3.03; 95% CI: 1.44–6.37) were significantly associated with HTN.ConclusionMore than half of diabetic patients had HTN. This highlights the need for focused screening and management strategies, emphasizing on identified modifiable risks to alleviate the problem and manage the cases.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disorder that defines the condition of having elevated blood glucose levels since the body has lost its function to produce sufficient insulin or utilize the produced insulin effectively, resulting in hyperglycemia, which is the clinical indicator of diabetes [1]. Across the globe, diabetes has been estimated to have an impact on 537 million adults and, according to forecasts, is set to rise to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045. While the population of the world itself is set to grow 20% during this period, that of diabetes is set to rise by 46% [2].

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the most frequent causative condition of morbidity and mortality in diabetic patients and has been estimated at $37.3 billion annually in cardiovascular diabetes‐related costs [3]. A diabetic patient has 2–4 times higher cardiovascular‐associated mortality compared to a nondiabetic patient [4]. Moreover, hypertension (HTN) is a frequent finding in type 2 and type 1 diabetes and is a significant risk factor for ASCVD and microvascular disease [3].

HTN defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg and or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 80 mmHg, a history of HTN, or current use of antihypertensive medication [5]. HTN prevalence among diabetic patients ranges from 20% to 60%, with a risk increased 1.5–3 times than those who do not have diabetes. The coexistence of HTN and diabetes doubles the risk of cardiovascular complications compared to having either condition alone [6]. Effective blood pressure control reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and microvascular complications in diabetic patients [6, 7, 8].

HTN is the most modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular and microvascular diseases and remains a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality in diabetes patients. Globally, approximately 1.28 billion adults living with HTN, with two‐thirds residing in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [9]. A majority of the world, as well as the African continent, has extremely high prevalence rates for HTN in diabetes patients. An example would include Studies done in Sri Lanka, Jordan, and Pakistan reported 59%, 80%, and 40.45% of the burden of hypertension among diabetic patients, respectively [10, 11, 12]. And also regional estimates ranking 77.1% of Central Africa, 69.1% of Southern Africa, 63.4% of Northern Africa, 53.0% of Eastern Africa, and 51.5% of Western Africa [13]. These are the possible factors of old age, obesity, chronic diabetes, and male sex, all of which are associated with HTN in diabetic patients [13]. The same is the case in Ethiopia to diabetics for the occurrence of HTN, finds research evidence reporting 55% among the country's patients with type 2 diabetes and varying regional percentages, for example, 32% in Dilla, 55% in Hossana, and 56.3% in Adama [14, 15, 16, 17].

The pathogenesis of HTN in diabetes is primarily driven by insulin resistance, which activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and inhibits natriuretic peptide activity, resulting in sodium retention and elevated blood pressure. Chronic hyperglycemia also augments HTN by inducing diabetic nephropathy by glomerular protein glycosylation [6, 8]. Insulin resistance also enhances sympathetic nerve activity that results in HTN. HTN in DM not only augments cardiovascular risk but also increases microvascular complications like diabetic nephropathy and retinopathy.

In Ethiopia, despite the high prevalence of hypertension among diabetic patients, blood pressure control remains suboptimal. Studies from Jimma and Addis Ababa reported target blood pressure control rates of 43.51% and 36.4%, respectively [18, 19]. Low physical activity, longer duration of diabetes, glycemic control, family history of hypertension, older age, higher body mass index (BMI), and higher waist‐to‐hip ratio are risk factors for HTN in Ethiopian diabetic patients [20, 21]. Angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (58.5%), calcium channel blockers (45%), and beta‐blockers (12.8%) are the most commonly prescribed antihypertensive medications [18].

The Sidama region represents a unique context within Ethiopia. As a relatively newly established regional state, it has evolving healthcare infrastructure with marked urban–rural disparities. HUCSH is the only tertiary referral center in the region, serving a catchment population of approximately 18 million people. This includes patients from rural districts who often have limited access to specialized diabetic and cardiovascular care, which may result in a higher concentration of more complex cases. Lifestyle transitions, dietary changes, variable physical activity patterns, and socioeconomic differences further influence the epidemiology of hypertension among diabetic patients in this population. Consequently, while our study population includes adult diabetic patients attending the HUCSH outpatient endocrine clinic, it may not fully represent the general diabetic population in Sidama. These contextual factors underscore the importance of region‐specific data and may limit the generalizability of findings from other Ethiopian regions to Sidama.

This study therefore addresses this gap by providing the first region‐specific analysis of HTN among diabetic patients in the Sidama region. Unlike earlier research, we not only report the prevalence but also explore the associated risk factors within this unique population. Additionally, our study applies the updated diagnostic threshold of ≥ 130/80 mmHg, ensuring alignment with current international guidelines and offering a more accurate estimate of disease burden. These findings are essential for informing local healthcare planning and implementing targeted interventions tailored to the specific needs of this population.

Early detection and management of hypertension in diabetic patients are critical for reducing the risk of CVD and improving overall health outcomes. This study provides a foundation for future research and regionally tailored public health strategies in Ethiopia and similar settings where both diabetes and hypertension are highly prevalent but under‐investigated.

Methodology

Study Area and Period

The research was conducted at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (HUCSH) in Hawassa town, Sidama zone, which is 270 km southeast of Addis Ababa. HUCSH serves a catchment population of over 18 million from the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples' (SNNP) region, Sidama, and neighboring Oromia region. The hospital provides specialized care in various departments, one of which is endocrinology, where patients with diabetes are being followed. The study was conducted between September 1 and November 30, 2023, using an institution‐based cross‐sectional design. The study included adult diabetic patients who visited the HUCSH adult endocrine clinic during the study period, excluding incomplete chart records, critically ill, newly diagnosed patients, pregnant patients, or nonconsenting patients.

As a tertiary referral center, HUCSH may disproportionately serve patients with more complicated or long‐standing diabetes, which could affect representativeness. Patients attending this clinic may differ from the broader diabetic population in the region, particularly those managed in primary health centers or private facilities. Rural patients may face transportation and financial barriers, and newly diagnosed or less severe cases may be underrepresented.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Techniques

Sample size was estimated by the formula for single population proportion. 95% confidence level (z = 1.96) and 5% margin of error (d) were assumed, and the estimated prevalence of hypertension among diabetic patients from an earlier study as 26.1%. As the source population was < 10,000, the sample size was reduced and a 10% nonresponse rate was added, and hence the final sample size was 223 patients. There were 648 diabetic patients who had routine follow‐ups in the HUCSH endocrinology clinic. Systematic random sampling was utilized with a sampling interval of 2.9 (648/223). The initial patient was randomly selected by lottery, and others were included every third patient until the desired sample size was met.

Operational and Term Definitions

Diabetic patient: Known diabetic patient on follow‐up who has a medical record number at HUCSH.

Hypertension: Blood pressure ≥ 130/80 mmHg which is measured more than one time with 10 min apart or taking antihypertensive medication for the diagnosis of hypertension [5]. For comparison, studies using a 130/80 mmHg threshold were considered.

To ensure clarity when comparing our findings with previous studies, we acknowledge that the definition of hypertension has evolved over time. While our study adopted the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline threshold of 130/80 mmHg, some of the cited studies used higher thresholds (e.g., ≥ 140/90 mmHg), which may affect the comparability of hypertension prevalence rates. Where possible, we have noted the criteria used in the respective studies or clarified if the original definitions were unavailable.

Target BP: Blood pressure < 130/80.

Good glycemic control: When a patient had HbA1c < 7% [3].

Uncontrolled blood sugar: Poor glycemic control is considered when a patient has HbA1c > 7% [3].

Dyslipidemia: Dyslipidemia was defined as the presence of at least one or more lipid profile abnormalities from the following: high TC ≥ 200 milligram per deciliter (mg/dL), high LDL ≥ 100 mg/dL, high TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, or low HDL (≤ 40 mg/dL for males and < 50 mg/dL for females) [22].

BMI: Body mass index (BMI) is a person's weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m²). The BMI cut‐off values according to the WHO classification (in Kg/m2) are: < 18.5 (underweight), 18.5–24.99 (normal), 25–29.99 (overweight), and > 30 (obese).

Waist circumference: High risk ≥ 94 cm in men and 80 cm in women [1].

Diabetes duration: The duration of DM was calculated as age at data collection minus age at the onset of diabetes.

CKD: CKD is defined for this study as CKD Stage 3–5 with eGFR ≤ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (eGFR 30–59, 15–29, and < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively), using creatinine‐based equation (CKD‐EPI) or ultrasound features of CKD that persist for 3 months [23].

Smoking status: Current cigarette smokers are those who smoke at least one cigarette per day for at least 6 months.

Former smokers: Those who stopped smoking more than 6 months before the study [24].

Alcohol consumption: Ever drinking of any type of alcohol (response will be either “yes” or “no”) and the frequency of drinking beer or other alcohol will used to evaluate the drinking status. Participants who answered “almost every day and 3–4 times a week” will coded as frequent drinkers; “once or twice a week” will included as less‐frequent drinkers; and “once or twice a month” will be regarded as barely drinking [25].

Khat chewing: Khat chewer is defined as khat chewing at least once a week while occasional use is defined as khat chewing less than once a week [26].

Physically active: An individual who performs physical exercise for at least 30 min/day for at least 5 days per week [27].

Physically inactive (sedentary): An individual who performs physical exercise for < 30 min/day for < 5 days per week [27].

A vigorous intensity activity: Defined as any activity that causes a large increase in breathing or heart rate (e.g., running, carrying, or lifting heavy loads, digging, or construction work) that continues for at least 30 min for a minimum of 3 days per week.

Moderate‐intensity activity: Defined as any activity that causes a small increase in breathing or heart rate (brisk walking or carrying light loads) that continues for at least 30 min for at least 3 days per week or 5 or more days of these activities for at least 20 min/day or ≥ 3 days of vigorous‐intensity activity per week of at least 20 min/day.

Data Collection Method and Tools

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer‐administered questionnaire through face‐to‐face interviews, patient record reviews, and physical examination. Clinical data and laboratory values were taken from the patient's chart, while blood pressure was measured using a well‐calibrated digital sphygmomanometer. Anthropometric measurements, including height and weight, were taken by trained nurses in the endocrine clinic. Height was measured in centimeters using a stadiometer with specific patient instructions, and weight was measured using a scale. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m2). Waist circumference was measured to the nearest 1 cm at the midpoint between the lower margin of the last palpable rib and the top of the iliac crest. Data collection occurred from September 1 to November 30, 2023, with three BSC nurses, two medical residents, two supervisors, and the principal investigator involved.

Data Quality Control

In order to ensure data quality, the principal investigator provided 1‐day training to the data collectors on the study objectives, the importance of the study, and the confidentiality of information. Pretest was done on 5% of the sample size, and corrections were made accordingly based on the findings. The data were checked manually daily for completeness, clarity, and consistency throughout the data collection. The data were then organized, cleaned, coded, and analyzed by SPSS version 26.0. Data were gathered through structured questionnaires and checklists.

Data Management and Analysis

Data were entered into EpiData version 3.1 and exported to IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 for analysis. Data cleaning and verification were conducted to ensure accuracy and consistency. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics: means and standard deviations (SDs) for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for non‐normally distributed variables, and frequencies with percentages (including numerators and denominators) for categorical variables. Bivariate logistic regression was performed to explore associations between each independent variable and hypertension, with variables having p < 0.25 considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and p values < 0.05 (two‐sided) were considered statistically significant. Model fitness was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test (p = 0.161), indicating an adequate fit. All analyses were conducted in accordance with the Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature guidelines.

Blood Pressure Control Among Hypertensive Diabetic Patient

Among 103 diabetic hypertensive patients identified, 12 (11.7%) achieved the goal blood pressure of < 130/80 mmHg. Enalapril was the most prescribed antihypertensive drug and was prescribed to 79 (76.7%) participants; it was also the most frequent monotherapy used and was given to 37 (35.9%) participants. The most common dual therapy was enalapril and amlodipine and was prescribed to 26 patients (25.2%), followed by the most common triple therapy of enalapril, amlodipine, and Lasix, prescribed to 4 patients (3.9%). Overall, the most common group of medications for the treatment of hypertension was ACE inhibitors, prescribed to 79 patients (76.6%) (Table 6).

Factors Associated to Hypertension

Sex, age, education level, physical inactivity, duration of diabetes, types of diabetes, BMI, waist circumference, family history of hypertension, high HgA1C, CKD, and dyslipidemia were all identified under bivariate logistic regression analysis as the possible risk factors with p < 0.25 and are hence qualified for consideration in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. In multivariable analysis, female sex, age ≥ 60, overweight, family history of hypertension, CKD, and dyslipidemia were significantly associated with hypertension. Female patients were 57% less likely to develop hypertension compared to male patients (AOR: 0.43, [95% CI: 0.21–0.90]). Patients aged ≥ 60 were 6 times more likely to develop hypertension compared to those aged < 40 years (AOR: 5.9, [95% CI: 1.62–20.87]). The patients who were overweight (25–29.99 kg/m2) were 3.5 times more likely to develop hypertension than the normally weighted patients (18–24.99 kg/m2) (AOR: 3.51, [95% CI: 1.58–7.77]). The patients who had a family history of hypertension were 3.6 times more at risk of hypertension than those who did not have any such family history (AOR: 3.61, [95% CI: 1.14–11.40]). CKD patients had a 3.6 times higher likelihood of developing hypertension compared to those who were not CKD patients (AOR: 3.61, [95% CI: 1.04–12.50]). Finally, dyslipidemic patients had a 3 times higher chance of developing hypertension compared to those who had a normal lipid profile (AOR: 3.03, [95% CI: 1.44–6.37]) (Table 7).

Discussion

The Sidama region represents a distinct epidemiologic and healthcare context within Ethiopia. As a newly established regional state, it is characterized by evolving health system infrastructure, mixed urban–rural demographics, and marked socioeconomic disparities. HUCSH functions as the sole tertiary referral center for an estimated catchment population of approximately 18 million people, receiving referrals of more complex and advanced diabetic cases from peripheral facilities with limited subspecialty services. Ongoing urbanization, dietary transitions, variations in physical activity patterns, and differences in healthcare accessibility may influence both the prevalence and control of hypertension among patients with diabetes. These contextual and health‐system factors may contribute to differences in disease burden and risk profiles compared to other Ethiopian regions, thereby limiting direct generalizability of findings and underscoring the importance of region‐specific data.

In this study, the prevalence of HTN among diabetic patients attending the adult endocrine clinic at HUCSH was 52.9%. While previous studies in the Sidama region have reported HTN prevalence rates of 24.5% among civil servants [24] and 12.3% for undiagnosed HTN in the general adult population [28], specific data on diabetic patients were lacking. This underscores the importance of updated, localized assessments, especially considering regional changes in lifestyle, nutrition, and health infrastructure.

Compared to 28% HTN prevalence reported among Ethiopian nondiabetic adults [29], our findings highlight the increased cardiovascular risk borne by diabetic patients, emphasizing the need for integrated screening and management approaches tailored to this vulnerable group. Although comparison with elderly nondiabetic patients using the current HTN threshold of ≥ 130/80 mmHg would be informative, such data are currently unavailable in our regional context and represent an important area for future research.

Compared to international and national data, our result is notably higher than reports from India (25.6%), Turkey (29%), Bahrain (38%), Brazil (16.2%), Jimma (37.4%), and a previous study from Sidama (26%) [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Our higher prevalence could be a function of the reduced diagnostic threshold for HTN (≥ 130/80 mmHg) as well as higher cultural prevalence of CKD and dyslipidemia, both conditions being risk factors for HTN. It is important to note that differences in hypertension prevalence across studies may be partly attributed to the use of varying diagnostic thresholds (e.g., ≥ 130/80 mmHg vs. ≥ 140/90 mmHg), which can substantially influence prevalence estimates and limit direct comparability between populations.

Conversely, our prevalence is lower than among Jordanian (80%), Iraqi (89.6%), Libyan (85.6%), Tanzanian (69.8%), and Cameroonian (86.2%) studies [11, 29, 36, 37], perhaps owing to differences in numbers and exercise levels between respondents. These results were nearer to other Japanese research (50%), Northern Sudan (47.6%), Nigeria (54.2%), Hosanna (55%), and Adama (56.3%) [16, 17, 38, 39, 40], suggesting a consistent pattern of hypertension burden among diabetic populations in similar resource‐limited settings.

Age was also significantly associated with HTN in our study. Patients of ≥ 60 years were six times higher than patients < 40 years for having HTN. This is in agreement with studies from Jordan, Iraq, North Sudan, Cameroon, Libya, Wolaita Sodo, Amhara region, Gamo zone, and Jimma [11, 20, 21, 26, 29, 33, 37, 40, 41, 42]. Aging has been associated with deteriorating physiological functions and high levels of noncommunicable disease, including hypertension, in the majority.

Weight‐wise, overweight patients (BMI 25–29.99 kg/m2) had 3.5 times higher odds of being hypertensive than those with normal BMI (18.5–24.99 kg/m2), consistent with Sri Lankan, Libyan, Hosanna, Wolaita Sodo, Amhara region, Gamo zone, Adama, Debretabor, and Jimma studies [10, 16, 18, 27, 33, 34, 39, 43]. Overweight is linked to increased cardiovascular risk, endothelial dysfunction, hemodynamic changes, and atherosclerosis and contributes to hypertension via insulin resistance and cholesterol accumulation.

In addition, a family history of HTN was independently associated with a 3.6‐fold increased risk of HTN, consistent with studies from Sri Lanka, Jordan, and the Amhara region [11, 12, 21], suggesting a genetic predisposition to HTN in diabetic patients.

CKD was in the current study also a specific risk factor for HTN. Patients with diabetic CKD had 3.6‐fold chances of HTN than diabetic patients without CKD. The finding is in line with other research studies conducted in Jordan and Wolaita Sodo [12, 43], where CKD has been linked with impairment of sodium excretion and RAAS and sympathetic nervous system activation that have roles in developing HTN.

Similarly, Dyslipidemia was also strongly related to HTN in our study. Diabetic and dyslipidemic patients were three times at risk for developing HTN when compared with patients having normal lipid profiles. This finding is in agreement with Sri Lankan and Jordanian research work [11, 12]. Elevated cholesterol may lead to the formation of plaques in the arterial walls, hardening the arteries and making them narrower and thus increasing the heart load and blood pressure.

Interestingly, the study showed that female patients were 57% less likely to be hypertensive compared to male patients. This is consistent with Libyan evidence [39], but contrary to results of the Nigerian study where no correlation existed.

With regards to blood pressure control, a mere 11.7% of our diabetic hypertensive patients achieved the goal blood pressure of < 130/80 mmHg. This is in agreement with those reported in Tanzania (15.5%) and Addis Ababa Zewditu Memorial Hospital (15%) [14, 22]. This is less than Jimma (43.5%), Nekemet (54.9%), and Addis Ababa (36.4%) [19, 30, 44], which shows the continuing issue of ideal control of blood pressure among diabetic patients.

One of the strengths of this study is the inclusion of a wide array of sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral variables, analyzed using multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes the establishment of temporal or causal relationships. Second, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, behavioral variables such as alcohol intake and smoking were self‐reported, introducing the possibility of reporting bias. Fourth, blood pressure was measured at a single visit, which may not accurately reflect long‐term blood pressure status. Furthermore, as this was an institution‐based study conducted at a tertiary referral center, the findings may not fully represent all diabetic patients in the Sidama region. Patients with more advanced disease, better health‐seeking behavior, or greater access to care may have been overrepresented, while individuals in remote areas or with milder disease may be underrepresented. In addition, some estimates demonstrated relatively wide CIs, which may reflect limited sample size within certain subgroups and variability in the data, potentially affecting the precision of the effect estimates.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Our study revealed hyper­tension in diabe­tes patients at very high prevalence rates and a strong correlation of HTN with female sex, overweight, age ≥ 60 years, family history of HTN, dyslipidemia, and CKD. Inadequate blood pressure control among diabetic hypertensive patients was also evident and reflects the need for improved management.

HTN was a well‐established risk factor for CVD, one of the foremost causative factors resulting in morbidity and mortality globally. Hypertension due to diabetes incurs vast augmentation of cardiovascular morbidities, and hence early intervention with antihypertensive is required in patients with diabetes. This becomes especially true according to our experience with poor control of blood pressure in diabetic patients who have HTN, an etiological factor leading to increased acceleration of CVD as well as other comorbid conditions.

The extremely high rate of prevalence of HTN being as common as in our diabetic patients is a very strong case in favor of the routine screening of HTN in such a highly susceptible population. Being highly interlinked conditions with obesity, old age, family history of HTN, and CKD, and certain screening programs must target such high‐risk groups in a bid to early detection of the disease and application of interventions early. Optimal control of blood pressure in diabetic individuals is required to reduce the risk of both microvascular and macrovascular complications, including heart disease, stroke, and renal failure.

Optimizing blood pressure control should be a priority and involve multicomponent strategies including pharmacologic therapy, lifestyle modification, exercise, weight reduction, and dietary changes. Modifiable risk factors such as overweight/obesity, dyslipidemia, and physical inactivity warrant special attention during treatment, as they were primary predictors of HTN in our study.

Patient education and awareness are critical components of comprehensive care for diabetic patients with HTN. Given the frequent coexistence of these conditions, educating patients on the importance of blood pressure control is essential for improving health outcomes.

In the context of future research, the present study highlights the necessity of conducting longitudinal, large‐scale research that can study determinants of suboptimal blood pressure control among diabetes patients. The research should take into account barriers to optimal hypertension control, such as provider‐level, patient‐level, and healthcare system barriers (e.g., awareness, treatment guidelines; medication adherence, knowledge, health habits; access, availability, respectively). In addition, it is important to understand the relationship between comorbidities such as dyslipidemia and CKD and how they interact with HTN in diabetic patients to learn more tailored and effective interventions.

In summary, frequent screening, early detection, and proper management of HTN in diabetic patients are critical. Combined multimodal therapeutic regimens including lifestyle modification, pharmacotherapy, and patient education may contribute to better blood pressure control and reduce the risk of cardiovascular and renal complications. More evidence is needed to guide the implementation of effective, context‐specific interventions for this high‐risk population.
