ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundEarly-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) is increasing worldwide and now accounts for a growing share of cancer deaths in younger adults. Data from middle- and low-income regions are scarce. We aimed to characterize the incidence, tumor stage distribution, lymph node positivity and metastasis patterns of EOCRC in Argentina.MethodsIn this retrospective, multicenter cohort study, we analyzed adult patients who underwent colorectal cancer surgery between April 2022 and December 2023. The registry included 1829 patients, of whom 961 were male (52.5%) and 868 were female (47.5%). Mean age was 41.6 years (range 18–49) in the EOCRC group and 68.8 years (range 50–98) in the late-onset colorectal cancer (LOCRC) group. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient and tumor characteristics. Comparisons between EOCRC (<50 years) and LOCRC (≥50 years) were performed using χ2 tests. Ethnicity data were not available.FindingsEOCRC accounted for 15.4% of surgically treated cases, with over two-thirds occurring among individuals aged 40–49 years. Compared with LOCRC, EOCRC patients more frequently presented with advanced tumor stages (T3–T4), higher lymph node positivity, and a greater prevalence of metastases. Tumor location differed between groups, with EOCRC predominating in the rectum and LOCRC showing a right-sided predominance.InterpretationThis national multicenter surgical registry analysis reveals substantial differences in stage and metastatic burden between younger and older colorectal cancer patients in Argentina. The high prevalence of advanced disease among individuals aged 40–49 years supports consideration of earlier initiation of colorectal cancer screening.FundingThe Universidad Abierta Interamericana provided partial funding for the publication of this work.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Embase and Scopus from inception to December 2023 for studies reporting on early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) incidence, clinical features and outcomes, using search terms including “early-onset colorectal cancer,” “colorectal cancer screening,” “tumor stage,” “lymph node positivity,” and “metastasis.” Although global trends show a steady increase in EOCRC over the past three decades, with pooled analyses indicating a doubling of cases between 1990 and 2019, data from middle- and low-income regions remain scarce. Existing evidence is largely drawn from high-income countries, with few multicenter registry analyses in Latin America and no comprehensive national studies in Argentina.

Added value of this study

This is the first analysis of EOCRC in a nationwide, multicentric registry in Argentina, encompassing 1829 surgically treated patients, specifically designed to prospectively collect high-quality data from public and private centers. Analyzing data until 2023, this study presents updated trends in EOCRC. We demonstrate that 15.4% of colorectal cancers occur before age 50, predominantly between ages 40–49, and that these younger patients present with significantly more advanced tumor stages (T3–T4), higher lymph node positivity and greater metastasis rates than their older counterparts. We further identify a rectal tumor predominance in EOCRC and detail age-stratified patterns of disease aggressiveness not previously described in the region.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings strengthen the growing international impetus to lower CRC screening thresholds by showing that Argentine patients aged 40–49 bear a disproportionately high burden of advanced and metastatic disease. Our results contribute to the debate and support consideration of earlier screening. Future research should evaluate prospective screening outcomes in younger cohorts and explore underlying biological and environmental drivers of EOCRC in Latin America to optimize prevention and early-detection strategies.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths.1 For the past two decades, CRC incidence has remained stable or declined in high-income countries possibly due to improvement in screening strategies, while low/middle-income countries with historically lower CRC rates have documented an increase in CRC incidence. In Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), CRC accounts for approximately 9% of cancer cases,2 while in Argentina, this number rises to 12%. It is projected that by 2030, CRC incidence in the Americas will grow by 60%, reaching approximately 396,000 new cases annually. A significant portion of this increase concerns LAC, where the increase in new cases is expected to double that of the United States and Canada.3

However, what is drawing increasing attention is the rise in multiple countries, including Argentina, of early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC), defined as CRC occurring in individuals under age 50.4, 5, 6 In the United States, EOCRC has now become the leading cause of cancer death among male under age 50 and the second leading cause among young female.7,8 Globally, EOCRC incidence has been steadily increasing without a clearly identified cause. From 1990 to 2019, EOCRC cases more than doubled, rising from approximately 95,737 to 226,782. In contrast, late-onset colorectal cancer (LOCRC) incidence, defined as CRC occurring in individuals aged 50 years and older, has remained stable or declined.9 Currently, most countries recommend CRC screening beginning at age 50.10

Interestingly, some countries have implemented screening programs since the 1980s, starting at age 40, which are now associated with a decrease in EOCRC rates. In these nations, the decline is especially seen in patients in their 40s, while younger age groups still exhibit an annual increase of around 3%.10 This underlines the importance of the starting age for screening programs. Based on these findings, the American Cancer Society recommended lowering the screening age to 45 in 2018.11,12 Similarly, in 2023, Australia adopted this change, as endorsed by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC).13 In Argentina, the National Program for the Prevention and Early Detection of Colorectal Cancer (PNCCR) was established in 2013.14 The program begins with a fecal occult blood test (FOBT) for individuals over 50 years old, followed by a colonoscopy if the test is positive.15

Despite growing awareness, much of the research on EOCRC has focused on high-income countries, leaving a gap in understanding the disease in middle- and low-income countries like Argentina. This study reports the analysis of the first national multicentric registry, designed to collect high quality-data from public and private healthcare centers from various provinces across Argentina. The main objective is to focus on EOCRC incidence, tumor stages, lymph node ratio, metastasis and tumor location in this database, thereby contributing to refine criteria that could help to improve early detection and treatment outcomes.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines, and applicable national and institutional research ethics regulations. Given the non-interventional, observational nature of the study and the exclusive use of anonymized data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived as permitted by law. All participating centers obtained ethics approval from their respective national or institutional review boards prior to initiating patient enrollment. No experimental or investigational procedures were performed. To safeguard patient confidentiality, only de identified data were collected. The electronic data capture system (REDCap) was hosted on a secure server at CEMIC University Hospital in Buenos Aires, Argentina. Access was restricted to authorized study personnel via encrypted, password-protected logins. The platform maintains a full audit trail and is compliant with international data protection frameworks, including the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), where applicable. Oversight of data integrity was coordinated by regional investigators, and data monitoring processes were implemented to ensure accuracy, completeness, and consistency of the collected information across participating sites.

Study design and setting

The national registry of patients operated for Colorectal Cancer in Argentina is a project that started 5 years ago, designed to obtain high quality information on the patients affected by colon and rectal neoplasms, and then use the data to implement improvement in care.

As an initial phase, during the year 2022, a pilot study was carried out in 8 academic hospitals in Buenos Aires, involving 317 recruited patients. The results of that study, as well as the process of creating the national registry, have already been previously published.16

Following the pilot study, recruitment of new centers was carried out. In the present work, we conducted a retrospective observational cohort study of cases from the national, registry of patients undergoing colorectal cancer surgery in Argentina. As such, the present analysis reflects a surgical cohort rather than all patients diagnosed with CRC in Argentina. Eligible cases from 32 public and private centers were entered into the registry between April 2022 and December 2023 and were subsequently analyzed for this study. The 32 participating centers comprised Capital Federal and the provinces of Buenos Aires, Cordoba, Corrientes, Entre Ríos, Formosa, Mendoza, Misiones, Neuquén, San Juan and Santa Fe.

For the purposes of this study, anonymized clinical information was completed by treating healthcare professionals through the RedCap platform.17 The database information was then used to compare tumor staging at different ages in the patients included in the database.

Inclusion criteria

Patients included in the registry were those over 18 years of age, who had signed informed consent for hospitalization (in Argentina, most hospitals have a consent form that includes permission to use hospitalization information for observational research), who had undergone surgery for an adenoma or adenocarcinoma located in the colon or rectum.

Patients with appendiceal tumors, extracolonic tumors that required colorectal resection due to invasion or metastasis, surgical procedures with palliative intent, and colorectal tumors with a histological subtype other than adenocarcinoma were excluded. Accordingly, the present analysis focuses on sporadic CRC cases.

Variables included in this study

Although presenting symptoms (e.g., rectal bleeding, change in bowel habits, abdominal pain, anemia, obstruction) often guided the indication for surgery in routine practice, these variables were not systematically captured in the registry and could not be analyzed in the present study.

Demographic and comorbidity variables

Age, sex, smoking, body mass index (BMI), comorbidity according to Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), anemia at the time of surgery defined by the Argentine Society of Hematology,18 previous abdominal surgery, previous history of colon cancer. Body mass indexes of 30 kg/m2 or higher were used to identify patients with obesity. Analyses were primarily descriptive. Comparisons between EOCRC and LOCRC were unadjusted and should not be interpreted as causal.

Variables related to colorectal cancer

Synchronous tumor, neoadjuvant chemotherapy and tumor location (right, transverse or left colon, rectum).

Operative information

Setting of surgery (urgent or elective), degree of specialization of the surgeon in charge of the procedure (colorectal or general surgeon), intraoperative complications and classification.19

Postoperative information

Postoperative complications, reoperation, rehospitalization and death within 30 days of surgery, tumor stage according to AJCC classification,20 number of lymph nodes harvested, number of positive nodes and metastasis.

Classification of tumor stages (T) and lymph node invasion

According to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC),20 the T1 stage indicates that the cancer has grown into the submucosa, the T2 stage, the tumor has invaded the muscularis propria. T3 means the tumor has grown through the muscularis propria into the surrounding tissues but it has not spread to nearby organs. In the T4 stage, the tumor has penetrated the serosa or has begun to invade nearby structures. T4a specifies that the tumor has perforated the lining of the visceral peritoneum, while T4b indicates that it has spread to or attached to nearby organs or structures.

The N classification refers to whether the cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes, and it indicates the number of lymph nodes affected by the tumor. Patients with N0 do not have any regional lymph node involvement. In the N1 category, the cancer has spread to between 1 and 3 regional lymph nodes. For more advanced lymph node involvement, the N2 category indicates that the cancer has spread to 4 or more regional lymph nodes. Within this category, N2a means the cancer has spread to 4 to 6 nodes, while N2b involves 7 or more lymph nodes.

The lymph node ratio (LNR) is defined as the number of lymph nodes found positive for tumor cell invasion divided by the total number of lymph nodes examined during surgery.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was to compare the lymph node ratio between early-onset colorectal cancer (EOCRC) and late-onset colorectal cancer (LOCRC) patients.

The secondary outcomes included comparisons of tumor stages, percentage of metastasis, and tumor location between the two groups; and also to describe perioperative outcomes and treatment patterns (including complications, adjuvant therapies, and 90-day mortality) between EOCRC and LOCRC.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were first examined using histograms and visual inspection to assess distributional assumptions. Variables with clearly skewed distributions are reported as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were compared between two groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. For comparisons of continuous variables across more than two age groups, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. When the global Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference, post hoc pairwise comparisons between age groups were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For each analysis, we used an available-case approach without statistical imputation, including all patients with non-missing data for the variable of interest. Consequently, the denominators vary across variables according to data completeness, and analyses of tumor location, LNR and metastasis are not restricted to the subset of patients with documented T-Stage.

Role of the funding source

The Universidad Abierta Interamericana did not have any role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation nor writing of the report.

Results

General characteristics of CRC patients

This study included a total of 1829 patients who underwent surgery for colorectal cancer across various public and private hospitals in Argentina between April 2022 and December 2023. The number of patients contributed by each center and their geographic distribution are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. Patients with familial polyposis syndrome or a family history of colorectal cancer were excluded. Among the cohort, there were 282 EOCRC patients (15.4%) and 1547 LOCRC patients (84.6%). The EOCRC group had an age range of 18–49 years, with an average age of 41.6 years. When divided by decade, 0.7% of patients were 10–19 years old, 3.9% were 20–29, 25.9% were 30–39, and 69.5% were 40–49 years old (Fig. 1). Sex distribution in the EOCRC group was nearly balanced, with 135 females (47.9%) and 147 males (52.1%). Tumor stage distribution in EOCRC

Patients were categorized according to tumor stage, and the distribution was analyzed for each group. It is important to note that tumor stage data was available for only a subset of patients, with very few records including this information. Consequently, this analysis was performed using only data from the patients for whom tumor stage was documented, comprising 64 EOCRC and 284 LOCRC cases, which represent approximately 19% of the overall cohort. Among EOCRC patients, 2 were classified as stage T1, 18 as stage T2, 29 as stage T3, and 15 as stage T4. In the LOCRC group, 32 patients were classified as stage T1, 68 as stage T2, 139 as stage T3, and 45 as stage T4. Notably, EOCRC patients had a higher percentage of advanced-stage tumors (T3 + T4) (Fig. 2B), with the 40–49 age group displaying the highest percentage of T3 + T4 cases among all age groups (Fig. 2b). In contrast, LOCRC patients exhibited a higher percentage of early-stage tumors (T1 + T2) (Fig. 2a). Lymph node invasion in EOCR and LOCRC patients

Given that EOCRC patients tend to present with more advanced T stage tumors, we also analyzed lymph node invasion in this population. Of the 1829 patients in the database, lymph node invasion data was available for 1575 individuals (245 EOCRC patients, 15.6%, and 1330 LOCRC patients, 84.4%). A total of 559 patients showed positive lymph node invasion. A higher proportion of EOCRC patients showed invaded lymph nodes (108/245, 44%) compared with the LOCRC group (451/1330, 34%). We also analyzed the lymph node ratio (LNR), defined as the number of lymph nodes positive for tumor invasion divided by the total number of lymph nodes examined during surgery. Because LNR was markedly right-skewed with a large proportion of zero values, it is reported as median (IQR) and compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Distribution of metastasis in EOCR and LOCRC patients

Finally, we examined the presence of distant metastasis among patients in the database. In this case, data was available for 1825 patients, among which 187 presented metastatic disease. In the EOCRC group (n = 282) 51 patients developed metastasis (18.09%), whereas in the LOCRC group (n = 1543), 136 patients were metastatic (8.81%). Thus, the metastasis rate in EOCRC patients was more than twice that of LOCRC patients (p < 0.001, Fig. 5a). We further analyzed metastasis rates across age groups and observed that the 40–49 age group had the highest rate (Fig. 5b). Discussion

National screening programs are an efficient approach to reduce CRC incidence and mortality. To design and adapt screening strategies, it is crucial to have detailed information on the characteristics and dynamics of the disease in the local population. Using the first National colorectal cancer surgical registry in Argentina, our study provides a comprehensive overview of 1829 patients treated across multiple public and private institutions in different provinces.17 This registry, created in 2022 with standardized data collection protocols, was specifically designed to enable high-quality data entry and to provide continuous up-to-date national information on CRC.

Our data reveal distinct general clinical characteristics between early-onset and late-onset colorectal cancer Argentinian patients, with differences that are expected given the age of the patients, such as a higher prevalence of non-communicable chronic diseases in the older group. Additionally, LOCRC patients had a higher 30-day mortality rate upon surgery, which could be associated with their poorer health status and greater number of comorbidities present at the time of surgery according to the Charlson index. Younger patients exhibited a lower prevalence of smoking, although, according to recent statistics in Argentina, no significant difference in tobacco use should be expected between these age groups.22 Many of these factors, such as age, obesity, smoking, and diabetes, have already been associated with a higher incidence of colorectal cancer.23 However, this association has primarily been studied in different global populations, which have their own cultural, nutritional and racial characteristics, which complicates the development of national public health policies, in developing countries such as Argentina. Due to the characteristics of tumor location and infiltration, and prevalence of rectum and metastatic cases; in accordance with the latest treatment guidelines for this disease, EOCRC patients had a higher prevalence of neoadjuvant treatment.24,25 The clinical comorbidities in the EOCRC and LOCRC patients represent different surgical risks, which would justify the higher number of perioperative deaths in LOCRC even though, according to this study, this would not be directly implicated in the efficacy of the surgical procedure as there was no difference in the presence of complications, the need for re-intervention and/or re-hospitalizations.25

Our study on the Argentinian CRC patient cohort revealed that EOCRC patients present a higher percentage of tumors located in the rectum than LOCRC patients which aligns with findings from other studies worldwide.23,26,27 Our data also indicate that EOCRC in Argentina tends to present at more advanced tumor stages than LOCRC, consistent with reports from other countries.28,29 Specifically, EOCRC patients exhibited higher T stages and significantly elevated LNR, which is associated with a more advanced N stage. Furthermore, we observed a higher incidence of metastasis in EOCRC patients, consistent with previous studies in other countries.26,30 Notably, according to our study, metastasis frequency in EOCRC patients more than doubles that of LOCRC patients, showing a remarkable increase in the population analyzed. The literature suggests several possible explanations for this trend. Lifestyle and environmental factors, such as higher rates of smoking, alcohol consumption, and poor dietary habits, might contribute to the increased aggressiveness of EOCRC.31, 32, 33 Additionally, genetic factors, including variations in tumor biology and mutations, could influence the differences in metastatic potential.34

Notably, the 40–49 age group within EOCRC has the highest proportion of LNR and distant metastasis. This finding underlines the need to align with international health policies and reduce the screening age in Argentina below age 50 years.35, 36, 37, 38 EOCRC patients are often diagnosed at more advanced stages with poorly differentiated tumors. This could be due to the absence of early screening programs for younger populations or to the inherently more aggressive nature of these cancers.33 Despite having generally better overall health and fewer comorbidities, younger patients do not achieve higher survival rates than older patients.39, 40, 41 This suggests that EOCRC may be biologically more aggressive. These distinctive characteristics of EOCRC emphasize the necessity of evaluating different patient subgroups and developing tailored screening and treatment strategies, particularly for younger individuals. Because patients with known hereditary syndromes or a documented family history of CRC were excluded by protocol, our findings pertain to apparently sporadic CRC and may not reflect the dynamics of EOCRC in high-risk hereditary families.

Regarding limitations of the study, its retrospective nature may introduce biases in data collection and analysis. T-stage information was available for only 19% of patients, thus, comparisons based on T stage should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. However, our main conclusions regarding aggressiveness derive from LNR and direct assessment of distant metastasis, for which data completeness exceeded 85% and 99%, respectively. It is worth noting that lymph node invasion is related to a worse clinical outcome. Another limitation is that the analyses of demographic and clinical variables were unadjusted, thus, residual confounding by age-related differences in comorbidities and treatment characteristics cannot be excluded. While the study includes a wide network of centers across diverse regions, the participating hospitals are primarily academic and high-complexity institutions, which may not fully represent the experience of lower-complexity centers or patients who do not reach surgery. Consequently, this hospital-based registry is not population-based, and selection at the institutional level may limit the generalizability of our estimates of stage distribution and metastatic burden to the overall Argentine population.

In our cohort, patients underwent surgery, following evaluation of visible symptoms such as rectal bleeding or opportunistic endoscopic detection rather than systematic screening, however, the registry did not capture detailed symptomatology for each case, which we acknowledge as an additional limitation. This information would be particularly relevant in the case of patients diagnosed with T1 o T2 tumors, since it may contribute to early detection.

Also, as participating centers differ in surgical case-mix, volume, and pathology protocols, we cannot exclude an effect of inter-center variability on lymph node yield and LNR. Although most centers are high-complexity institutions adhering to national guidelines, we did not model center-specific effects, which may contribute to unexplained variability in LNR. Another limitation is that the registry was not designed to capture ethnicity nor detailed socioeconomic information. Individual-level data on type of health coverage, educational attainment, employment status and income were either unavailable or incomplete and could not be analyzed. As a result, we were unable to formally assess how race and socioeconomic gradients might influence access to surgery, stage at presentation or metastatic burden in EOCRC versus LOCRC. Additionally, the lack of more detailed genomic or molecular data on tumor mutations and characteristics limits our ability to explore deeper biological differences between EOCRC and LOCRC.

Future work should build on this surgical registry by expanding its scope and granularity. First, extending case capture beyond surgically treated patients and incorporating systematic information on presenting symptoms, diagnostic delays and care pathways would allow a more comprehensive assessment of how young patients enter and move through the health system. Second, the collection of standardized socioeconomic data (including health coverage, education and employment) and molecular tumor characteristics would enable analyses of social and biological determinants of EOCRC.

This study offers the first comprehensive assessment of EOCRC in Argentina, providing valuable insights into the incidence, clinical characteristics, and differences in tumor progression compared to LOCRC. By incorporating data from a large, multicenter database that spans public and private healthcare centers across multiple provinces, the study achieves a broad geographic coverage and institutional diversity, likely including patients from a range of socioeconomic backgrounds. The use of advanced statistical methods and detailed clinical data collection, including tumor location, LNR, and metastasis, provides a solid foundation for the design of future screening and treatment strategies. Future work will be necessary to evaluate the effectiveness, feasibility and cost of introducing strategies that include younger populations in screening programs.
