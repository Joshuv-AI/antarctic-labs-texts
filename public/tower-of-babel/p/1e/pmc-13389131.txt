Ultra-broadband response and extreme low-light detection with a MoS2/SnS2 vertical heterostructure photodetector
Authors: Pengfan Dong, Jiaying Jian, Honglong Chang, Yaxin Nan, Enfu Li, Zengyun Jian, Yanfeng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Low-light detection technology is of strategic significance in military reconnaissance and deep space exploration. However, existing photodetectors often struggle to achieve both broadband response and ultra-low-light detection capabilities simultaneously. In this work, a MoS₂/SnS₂ vertical heterostructure photodetector was fabricated using an in-situ two-step chemical vapor deposition (CVD) method. The device successfully realized a broadband coverage ranging from ultraviolet to infrared regions (250-1064 nm) and demonstrated high-sensitivity photodetection of low-light signals. Under illumination at the intensity of 0.0008 mW/cm² and the wavelength of 520 nm, the device showed a responsivity of 6.89 A/W. This enhancement is attributed to the type-II band alignment of the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterojunction, which promotes efficient charge separation and suppresses carrier recombination. Under 30 mW/cm² illumination at 385 nm, the response time was reduced to 0.082/0.074 s (rise/fall time). At the wavelengths of 385 and 520 nm, the limit of detection reached as low as 0.0008 mW/cm², indicating a low-light detection capability one order of magnitude superior to that of commercial black-Si detectors. Furthermore, based on the measured noise characteristics and responsivity, we estimate a minimum detectable radiance of 6.11×10⁻¹³ W/(cm² sr μm) at 450 nm when normalized to a standard detector area of 4.91 mm². This theoretical value approaches the radiance level of nighttime atmospheric glow, indicating the potential of our heterostructure for ultra-sensitive photodetection applications after appropriate scaling.

Introduction

Low-light photodetectors hold important application value in fields such as military reconnaissance, space communications, biomedical imaging, and deep space exploration1–6. Primary strategies for enhancing detector performance focus on improving the intrinsic sensitivity of photosensitive materials, expanding the optical acquisition aperture, and employing signal amplification technologies7–9. Among these, optimizing material sensitivity is particularly promising, as it is less constrained by space and cost limitations. Conventional commercial low-light detectors are predominantly based on material systems such as InGaAs (refs. 10,11), black-Si (refs. 12–15), and HgCdTe (refs. 16,17). Typical examples include the fifth-generation full-color low-light-level night vision device from SiOnyx, which utilizes XQE detectors to enable observation in moonless environments12. Similarly, Xiaona Zhao et al.13 improved the responsivity of a Si detector to 0.367 A/W (at 1030 nm under 5 mW/cm²) by using sulfur-doped black-Si. Furthermore, S. Mukherjee’s team14 constructed a PN junction with MoS₂-modified black-Si, achieving low-light detection at 514 nm with an intensity as low as 0.5 μW/cm². However, these technologies are hampered by significant bottlenecks, including complex and costly doping processes and stringent requirements for low-temperature operation to suppress dark current and noise. These limitations have severely restricted their practical deployment. Therefore, it is urgently needed to develop new low-light detection materials that operate without cooling, boast compact structures, and are resistant to photodamage.

Two-dimensional materials, characterized by their dangling-bond-free surfaces, enable the construction of heterostructures with high lattice mismatch tolerance via van der Waals forces, offering novel paths for low-light detection. By employing a solution deposition method, Alexander Corletto et al.18 prepared a black phosphorus film. Furthermore, through optimizing the properties of both the black phosphorus film and its protective capping layer, they developed a black phosphorus infrared photoconductor exhibiting a specific detectivity of up to 4.0×10⁸ cm Hz¹/² W⁻¹ under illumination at 2.2 μm, and a fast response time of 30/60 μs (rise/fall). Huaxin Yi et al.19 synthesized MoS₂ and SnS₂ separately via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and constructed a heterostructure through positioning transfer, successfully expanding the detection wavelength range from 405-532 nm to 405-635 nm. Moreover, the device realized a light intensity response of 15.23 mW/cm² at the wavelength of 532 nm. Quangui Fu’s team20 fabricated a SnS₂/MoS₂ vertical heterostructure via one-step CVD, achieving broadband detection from 447 to 940 nm and a detection limit as low as 0.005 mW/cm² at 447 nm.

Previous research indicates that while traditional photodetector materials like black-Si exhibit commendable low-light detection capabilities in the infrared range, their detection range remains relatively narrow. In contrast, although photodetectors based on highly reactive two-dimensional materials such as black phosphorus perform excellently under low-light conditions, they are prone to performance degradation, which severely compromises their operational stability. Environmentally stable two-dimensional materials, represented by MoS₂ and SnS₂, offer a feasible route to broaden the detection spectrum by heterostructures when constructing photodetectors. The MoS₂/SnS₂ combination is particularly attractive because their electronic structures complement each other: MoS₂ is an n-type semiconductor with a moderate bandgap (~1.8 eV for monolayer), while SnS₂ is also n-type but with a slightly larger bandgap (~2.2 eV). When brought together, they form a type-II band alignment, where the conduction band minimum of SnS₂ lies above that of MoS₂, and the valence band maximum of MoS₂ lies below that of SnS₂. This staggered alignment enables efficient spatial separation of photogenerated electron–hole pairs at the heterointerface: electrons transfer from SnS₂ to MoS₂, while holes migrate in the opposite direction. Such interfacial charge separation suppresses recombination, extends carrier lifetime, and enhances photocurrent—a critical advantage for low-light detection applications.

However, conventional mechanical transfer techniques often introduce impurities and lattice strain, leading to the formation of high Schottky barriers that result in substantially increased dark current noise, thereby degrading the low-light signal detection performance21–23. Consequently, constructing high-quality van der Waals heterostructures using environmentally stable two-dimensional material systems has emerged as a pivotal technological route to simultaneously achieve broadband detection and low-light detection.

In this work, we adopted a two-step CVD method to achieve in-situ growth of SnS₂ on the surface of MoS₂, thus fabricating a full-stack MoS₂/SnS₂ van der Waals heterostructure. Compared with conventional approaches that rely on mechanical exfoliation followed by physical transfer or stacking, our in-situ growth strategy offers several critical advantages. First, it eliminates polymer residues and interfacial contaminants that are inevitably introduced during transfer processes, which can act as trap states and degrade device performance. Second, it preserves the structural integrity of the heterointerface, minimizing lattice strain and discontinuities that would otherwise impair carrier transport. Third, the in-situ approach ensures intimate contact between the two materials, maximizing the area of the type-II heterojunction and thereby enhancing charge separation efficiency. Collectively, these advantages make in-situ CVD not merely a fabrication convenience but a performance-critical strategy for achieving high-sensitivity, stable photodetectors.

From a fabrication perspective, the pre-patterned electrode arrays were designed with multiple channel lengths (5, 10, 15, and 20 μm) to accommodate the varying lateral sizes of the CVD-grown MoS₂ and SnS₂ flakes, which typically exhibited edge lengths of 10-20 μm. The smallest channel length (5 μm) was selected to maximize the probability of successful transfer alignment, given that the transfer process was performed manually with limited positioning precision. Additionally, the optical spot diameter (20 μm) is significantly larger than the channel length, ensuring uniform illumination of the entire active region regardless of the channel length selected.

From a device physics standpoint, a channel length of 5 μm is also advantageous for photodetection performance. Under a moderate bias voltage (3 V), this short channel generates a sufficiently high electric field (~0.6 V/μm) to facilitate efficient carrier extraction and suppress recombination—particularly important for low-light conditions where photogenerated carrier density is extremely low. The short channel also minimizes carrier transit time, contributing to the fast response speed (0.082/0.074 s). While even shorter channels would further reduce transit time, they would also increase the relative contribution of contact resistance. The 5 μm channel thus strikes a balance between efficient carrier collection and fabrication reliability.

It is worth noting that the responsivity of the single-component MoS₂ device (1.68 × 10⁻⁴ A/W) is lower than some literature values for CVD-grown MoS₂ photodetectors. This is primarily attributed to the unoptimized electrode contacts in our devices. All three devices were fabricated using the same polymer-assisted transfer method, where the as-grown materials were transferred onto pre-patterned Ti/Au electrodes. This process inevitably introduces mechanical strain and polymer residues at the material–electrode interface, leading to increased contact resistance and reduced carrier extraction efficiency compared to devices with electrodes directly deposited onto the material surface. Additionally, the absence of gate bias in our measurement configuration precludes field-effect modulation that could otherwise enhance photoconductive gain. These factors collectively contribute to the moderate responsivity values observed for the single-component devices. Critically, the heterostructure device, fabricated under identical conditions, exhibits substantially higher responsivity, demonstrating the effectiveness of the type-II band alignment in promoting efficient carrier separation despite the unoptimized contacts. Statistical analysis of multiple devices confirms the reproducibility of these performance trends (Supplementary Table S1; see also Supplementary Fig. S5 for the corresponding I-t curves).

To visually compare the photoresponse under identical conditions, measurements were conducted at a fixed wavelength of 385 nm and a power density of 30 mW/cm² (Fig. 2d). The response times of the photodetectors, defined by the rise time (tᵣᵢₛₑ) and fall time (tfₐₗₗ), were measured as tᵣᵢₛₑ/tfall = 3.658/3.593 s, 0.152/0.116 s, and 0.082/0.074 s, respectively. These results demonstrate that the heterostructure simultaneously achieves a 20-fold enhancement in responsivity and a 45-fold acceleration in response speed compared to its single-component counterparts.

The response time of the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterostructure (0.082/0.074 s at 30 mW/cm²) is slower than some literature reports of 2D heterostructure photodetectors, which typically exhibit response times in the μs-ms range. We attribute this relatively slower response to several factors related to our device fabrication approach. (1) the polymer-assisted transfer process, which inevitably introduces mechanical strain and residues at the material–electrode interface, degrading carrier extraction efficiency; (2) intrinsic defect states in CVD-grown MoS₂ and SnS₂, which act as carrier traps and slow the response; and (3) the absence of gate bias, which would otherwise accelerate carrier extraction.

Furthermore, the response time of the MoS₂/SnS₂ device decreases markedly with increasing optical power density. This trend is revealed by its power-dependent I-t curve under a 3 V bias (Fig. 2f) and the corresponding statistical analyses (Fig. 2g–i). The response time decreases with increasing optical power density, with the rate of decrease slowing at higher power densities. This behavior is consistent with the progressive saturation of trap states: as trap states become filled under intense illumination, the carrier dynamics become increasingly dominated by faster band-to-band recombination, leading to diminishing returns in response time reduction. In addition, its wavelength dependence (Fig. 2i) revealed the fastest response at 520 nm which is attributed to the enhanced band-edge absorption, while a slower response was observed in the 1064 nm band.

While the current response time is sufficient for low-light detection, further improvement for real-time imaging (requiring <30-40 ms) can be achieved in future work by optimizing the transfer process, introducing gate bias, and reducing defect densities (Supplementary Note 1).

Low-light detection of the MoS2/SnS2 heterostructure photodetector

Figure 3a shows the transient I-t response curves of the three photodetectors under a 3 V bias, measured at their characteristic wavelengths corresponding to the minimum detectable optical power density for each device. The respective incident optical power values applied in these measurements are explicitly annotated in Fig. 3b (bottom panel). Analysis of the photoresponse data in Fig. 3a (with the low incident power levels in 3b) and the responsivity spectra in Fig. 3b (top panel) consistently reveals that the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterostructure exhibits comprehensive advantages in photodetection performance compared to single-component MoS₂ or SnS₂ detectors. Specifically, the detection limit of the heterostructure is significantly reduced, while the responsivity is markedly enhanced. For instance, at a wavelength of 520 nm, the heterostructure exhibits a low-light detection limit that is only 2.67 × 10⁻⁵ and 0.08 times that of MoS₂ and SnS₂, respectively, whereas its responsivity reaches 41,000 and 265 times that of MoS₂ and SnS₂, respectively. In the infrared region at 1064 nm, the heterostructure still maintains a strong response, achieving a responsivity 53 times higher than that of SnS₂. In contrast, the pure MoS₂ device shows no response in this spectral range, even under high light intensity (3000 mW/cm2). This pronounced enhancement in performance can be attributed to the unique energy‑band alignment of the heterostructure. The optical bandgaps of MoS₂ and SnS₂ were experimentally determined from absorption spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. S2), yielding values of 1.82 eV and 2.24 eV, respectively. The near-infrared region around 1064 nm is of particular technological significance due to its widespread applications in optical communication, LiDAR systems, medical diagnostics, and night-vision technologies. Demonstrating photoresponse at this wavelength is therefore an important step toward practical device applications.

Notably, the photon energy at 1064 nm (~1.17 eV) is lower than the intrinsic bandgaps of both monolayer MoS₂ (~1.8 eV) and SnS₂ (~2.2 eV), making direct band-to-band absorption in either material unlikely. The observed infrared photoresponse therefore indicates the involvement of alternative absorption mechanisms. We attribute this response to defect-mediated absorption and interlayer charge-transfer transitions within the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterojunction.

First, the CVD-grown MoS₂ and SnS₂ layers inevitably contain structural defects (e.g., sulfur vacancies) that introduce mid-gap states. These defect states enable sub-bandgap photon absorption through a two-step process: a photon excites an electron from the valence band to a defect state, followed by another photon or thermal excitation to the conduction band. Second, the type-II band alignment at the heterointerface facilitates interlayer charge-transfer transitions, where photoexcited carriers can transfer across the interface even when the excitation energy is below the individual bandgaps. This mechanism is supported by the power-dependent response (Fig. 2g, h), where the responsivity at 1064 nm increases with incident power density, consistent with defect-assisted absorption that becomes more efficient under higher illumination. The slower response time at 1064 nm (rise/fall: 2.10/0.83 s) compared to shorter wavelengths (e.g., 0.10/0.07 s at 520 nm) further supports this interpretation, as defect-mediated processes typically exhibit slower carrier dynamics than direct band-to-band transitions.

Collectively, these observations indicate that the infrared response originates from defect states and interlayer coupling within the heterojunction rather than from the individual constituent materials. This interpretation is consistent with the enhanced performance of the heterostructure compared to single-component devices (Fig. 3c), where the type-II band alignment provides an additional pathway for carrier separation and collection.

To further confirm that the measured photoresponse originates solely from the heterostructure, a control experiment using a bare Si/SiO₂ substrate confirmed that the substrate does not contribute significantly to the photocurrent (Supplementary Fig. S3a).

The dark current-voltage (IV) characteristic of the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterostructure was measured under ambient conditions, as shown in Fig. S3b. The linear IV curve (R² = 0.994) with a resistance of approximately 747 GΩ confirms Ohmic contact behavior between the electrodes and the MoS₂ channel. The low dark current (~3.5 pA at 3 V bias) provides a favorable foundation for low-noise operation, as discussed below.

Device stability. To assess the practical viability of the MoS₂/SnS₂ heterostructure photodetector, we evaluated both its operational stability under repeated on/off cycling and its long-term environmental stability. As shown in Fig. S4a, the device was subjected to 1000 consecutive on/off cycles (5 s on, 5 s off) under 520 nm illumination (0.1 mW/cm², 3 V bias). The photocurrent remains stable throughout the entire test, with no observable degradation. The magnified views of the first, middle, and final cycles (insets α, β, γ) confirm reproducible switching behavior with consistent on/off current levels. For long-term stability (Fig. S4b), the device was stored in ambient air without encapsulation. I-t curves measured at three time points over five months show negligible degradation, with responsivity retention >98% and dark current remaining stable at ∼3 pA. These results demonstrate the practical viability of our heterostructure photodetector.

Switching characteristics and operating window. The repeated on/off cycling test was performed with a cycle period of 10 s (5 s on, 5 s off), corresponding to a switching frequency of 0.1 Hz. This condition was selected to ensure that the photocurrent reaches steady state during each on/off cycle, allowing reliable assessment of device stability. Based on the measured response time of the device (0.082/0.074 s), the theoretical upper limit for reliable tracking of light intensity variations is approximately 6 Hz, as the on/off period must be sufficiently longer than the response time to allow full stabilization. The 0.1 Hz condition used in our cycling test is well within this limit, ensuring stable and reproducible switching behavior. For applications requiring faster modulation, the operating frequency can be increased up to the limit set by the response time, though higher frequencies may lead to incomplete charge extraction and reduced effective responsivity.

Beyond D*, the noise equivalent power (NEP) serves as another key figure of merit for photodetector sensitivity. It is defined as the incident optical power required to achieve a signal-to-noise ratio of 1. Conclusion

A full-stack MoS₂/SnS₂ vertical heterostructure was fabricated via a two-step CVD method. The resulting type-Ⅱ band alignment enabled highly efficient carrier separation, which underpinned its superior photodetection performance across a broad band from 250 to 1064 nm. Under specific illumination conditions, the device exhibited a responsivity of 6.89 A/W at 520 nm, exceeding that of single-component MoS₂ and SnS₂ devices by factors of 41,000 and 265, respectively, while its response time was drastically reduced to 0.082/0.074 s (trise/tfall) at 385 nm, representing a 45-fold acceleration. Moreover, in the low-light regime, it achieved an experimental detection limit of 0.0008 mW/cm² at 385 and 520 nm.

When evaluated on a standardized system basis, the device exhibits a projected minimum detectable radiance of 6.11 × 10⁻¹³ W/(cm² sr μm) at 450 nm, surpassing commercial black-Si detectors by one order of magnitude. This performance demonstrates the device’s potential for ultra-sensitive photodetection, with theoretical projections suggesting that scaled-up versions could approach the sensitivity required for atmospheric glow detection. The combination of broadband response, high responsivity, and low noise makes the MoS2/SnS2 heterostructure a promising material platform for next-generation low-light-level imaging and sensing technologies. Notably, its response dynamics (0.082/0.074 s) align with the timescale of biological synaptic plasticity, suggesting potential for neuromorphic applications. Future work may explore this direction by systematically characterizing conductance modulation under pulsed optical and electrical stimuli.

Materials and methods

Construction of the MoS2/SnS2 heterostructure

A two-step CVD method was employed to fabricate the MoS₂/SnS₂ vertical heterostructure. Given the significantly higher thermal decomposition temperature of MoS₂ (~1370 °C) compared to SnS₂ (~600 °C), the MoS₂ substrate was first synthesized, followed by the deposition of a SnS₂ film on its surface to construct the vertical heterostructure. Synthesis of the monolayer MoS₂

Synthesis of the monolayer MoS₂

A dual-temperature zone tube furnace system was utilized. In brief, sulfur powder (S, 0.8 g) was placed in the low temperature zone, and molybdenum trioxide (MoO₃, 0.02 g) was positioned in the high temperature zone. The pre-cleaned substrate was placed 10 cm downstream of the molybdenum source. After three cycles of argon purging, a constant argon flow of 80 sccm was set. The temperature programming was as follows: the low temperature zone was heated to 200 °C to achieve sulfur sublimation, and the high temperature zone was maintained at 800 °C for 20 min, resulting in the deposition of the monolayer MoS₂.

In-situ growth of the SnS₂ film

The as-synthesized MoS₂ sample was transferred to a new reaction system and placed 15 cm downstream of the tin oxide (SnO, 0.02 g) source. Then, 0.8 g S was loaded in the low temperature zone for sublimation. After argon purging, a constant argon flow of 80 sccm was maintained. The low temperature zone was then held at 200 °C to sublimate sulfur, and the high temperature zone was set to 710 °C for 20 min of reaction, thus achieving in-situ growth of the SnS₂ layer on the MoS₂ surface.

Measurement of photodetector performance

A tunable single-wavelength laser system (wavelengths: 385, 520, 650, and 1064 nm) and a modulated xenon lamp source were used as light sources. The xenon lamp was modulated using an optical chopper, providing continuously tunable wavelengths from 250 nm to 850 nm. The sample was irradiated vertically with a spot diameter of 20 μm.
