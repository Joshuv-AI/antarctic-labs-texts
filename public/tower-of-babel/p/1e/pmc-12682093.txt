Can Rhodiola rosea supplementation mitigate digital eye strain? A triple‐blinded placebo‐controlled study
Authors: Paula M Lara, Jesús Vera, Daniel Marcos‐Frutos, Carlos Alix‐Fages, Pablo Jiménez‐Martínez, Amador García‐Ramos, Beatriz Redondo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to assess the impact of Rhodiola rosea (RR) supplementation on the dynamics of the accommodative response and digital eye strain (DES) symptoms following 30‐min visual tasks with different levels of cognitive demand.MethodsEighteen young adults (mean age ± SD: 24.6 ± 4.0 years) participated in this placebo‐controlled, triple‐blind, balanced crossover study. Participants completed four sessions separated by 1 week in a randomised order. The sessions differed in the supplement (RR or placebo) and cognitive demand of the visual task (Stroop test or control video). The supplementation consisted of 1200 mg of RR or placebo per day for 4 days (two 300 mg capsules every 12 h) and a washout period of 3 days was allowed before the subsequent condition. The lag and variability of the accommodative response were measured at 500, 40 and 20 cm using an open‐field autorefractor, and DES symptoms were assessed using a 10‐item questionnaire.ResultsRR supplementation caused a reduction in the variability of accommodation (p = 0.007) and the perceived levels of ‘Blurred vision when looking into the distance at the end of the near task’ (p = 0.009). However, the lag of accommodation (p = 0.15) and the remainder of the DES symptoms were unaffected by the ingestion of RR (p > 0.05 in all cases). The reduction in the variability of accommodation after RR intake was observed at 40 and 20 cm (p‐values <0.001 and 0.04, respectively), but not at far distance (500 cm, p = 0.40).ConclusionsThese findings show that RR supplementation (1200 mg/day for 4 days) produced a reduction in the variability of accommodation and one of the assessed DES symptoms, suggesting that it could be considered as a potential strategy to prevent or manage DES. However, further research is needed to determine its clinical relevance and optimise dosage and timing.Keywords: accommodation, computer vision syndrome, visual ergonomics, visual fatigue

INTRODUCTION

Digital eye strain (DES) or computer vision syndrome is defined as the combination of eye and vision problems associated with the use of digital devices. 1 Prolonged engagement in visually and cognitively demanding tasks causes symptoms such as eye strain and dry eye, while also impacting various ocular functions, including ocular accommodation and blink dynamics. 2 Given the widespread use of digital devices in both professional and academic settings, identifying effective strategies to minimise the effects of prolonged viewing on DES is of critical importance. Different strategies have been proposed to prevent and manage DES, including ergonomic approaches, visual interventions or nutritional supplements. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Regarding nutritional supplements, several studies have found promising results with compounds such as omega‐3 fatty acids, 7 anthocyanin‐rich extracts (e.g., bilberry and black currant extracts) 8 , 9 , 10 and carotenoids, 11 , 12 although the level of scientific evidence is limited. 13

Among potential supplements for managing visual fatigue, Rhodiola rosea (RR) has been recognised for its adaptogenic properties, offering potential benefits for mental fatigue, visuocognitive processing and physical performance. 14 , 15 , 16 Approved by the European Medicines Agency in 2011 for the temporary relief of stress and fatigue symptoms, RR exerts its effects primarily through its active compounds, rosavins and salidroside. 17 These bioactive components modulate several physiological systems related to stress and fatigue adaptation, including balancing the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, regulating monoamine neurotransmitters such as dopamine, serotonin and norepinephrine, reducing cortisol secretion and exerting antioxidant effects. 18 , 19 , 20

Prolonged exposure to mentally demanding visual tasks has been linked to reduced stability and accuracy of the accommodative response and increased visual symptomatology. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Since ocular accommodation is influenced not only by parasympathetic activity through the Edinger‐Westphal nucleus but also by higher‐order neural mechanisms related to arousal, attention and autonomic regulation, RR may contribute to stabilising accommodative responses and manage DES during visually and cognitively demanding tasks. 26 The neuromodulatory action of RR, particularly its effects on dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways, may enhance both autonomic stability and cognitive control, thereby influencing the dynamics of ocular accommodation.

Due to the proposed effects of RR on regulating autonomic nervous system function and mitigating fatigue symptoms, it is of interest to investigate its potential utility for the management of DES. Specifically, the main objective of this triple‐blind placebo‐controlled study was to evaluate the effectiveness of RR supplementation on the dynamics of the accommodative response and DES symptoms following a 30‐min visual task with different levels of cognitive demand. We hypothesise that RR supplementation may reduce symptoms of fatigue and accommodative changes associated with prolonged visually and cognitively demanding tasks. However, due to the limited research on its effects on ocular signs and symptoms, the hypothesis is tested here.

METHODS

Participants

The sample size calculation for this study was based on the changes found in the variability of the accommodative response after caffeine intake. 27 For this analysis, an effect size of 0.34 was considered (66% of the change found with caffeine), a power of 0.90, alpha of 0.05 and correlation among repeated measures of 0.50. The a‐priori power analysis projected a required sample of 17 subjects for this within‐subjects design. Eighteen young adults (mean age ± SD: 24.6 ± 4.0 years, age range 19–34 years, 6 females) were recruited from the University of Granada to participate in this study. All participants met the following inclusion criteria: (i) no history or presence of ocular surgery or disease, (ii) corrected visual acuity of +0.10 logMAR (6/7.5) or better in each eye, (iii) uncorrected spherical equivalent refractive error within ±1D and (iv) not pregnant or nursing. All participants wore their full refractive correction throughout the experimental sessions to ensure consistent accommodative demand. Refractive error was measured by non‐cycloplegic autorefraction (WAM‐5500, Grand Seiko, grandseiko.com/en/), with a mean spherical equivalent of −0.93 ± 1.31 D (range: −3.25 D to +1.00 D). To avoid vertex distance effects, soft contact lenses were worn by those requiring refractive correction during both the visual tasks and accommodative measurements. All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee (SICEIA) of the Andalusian Regional Government (SICEIA‐2024‐000283).

Experimental design and procedure

This placebo‐controlled, triple‐blind, balanced crossover study was designed to assess the effects of RR supplementation on the dynamics of the accommodative response and DES symptoms after a 30‐min visual task with two levels of cognitive demand (see Figure 1 for a schematic illustration of the study design). The study was conducted under a triple‐blind design, with participants, experimenters conducting measurements and data analysts being blinded to group allocation. An independent researcher managed randomisation, supplement coding and capsule preparation to ensure blinding integrity throughout the study. Participants visited the laboratory on five occasions separated by 7 days (one preliminary testing session and four experimental sessions) and all sessions were scheduled at the same time of day (±1 h) to control the influence of circadian variations on visual and cognitive performance. 28 , 29 Participants were instructed to obtain a regular night's sleep (≥7 h) and refrain from intense exercise or alcohol for 12 h prior to each session.

Schematic illustration of the experimental design. DES, digital eye strain; PLA, Placebo; RR, Rhodiola rosea.

In the preliminary session, a visual examination for assessment of the inclusion criteria and familiarisation with the Stroop task was conducted. Participants who required optical correction were fitted with daily disposable soft contact lenses (Proclear 1‐day, CooperVision, coopervision.com/), based on the corneal measurements and refraction, compensated for vertex distance. An experienced optometrist checked that the lenses were appropriately centred and had sufficient movement. All participants were habitual contact lens wearers, but it was considered appropriate to use the same type of lenses to avoid potential variability related to lens material or fitting considerations. The researcher provided the supplement (RR or placebo) and gave instructions for its consumption. The four experimental sessions followed the same protocol, differing only in the type of supplement (RR or placebo) and the mental task condition (Stroop test or control video). Participants were counterbalanced and randomly assigned to one of the four conditions using the Research Randomizer online software (randomizer.org), that is, (i) RR with the Stroop test, (ii) RR with the control video, (iii) placebo with the Stroop test and (iv) placebo with the control video. After performing the 30‐min visual task, participants completed the DES questionnaires, and the dynamics of the accommodative response were evaluated at three different distances (500 cm, 40 cm and 20 cm in random order).

For the supplementation procedure, RR or placebo were administered in capsules. The supplements were distributed using two blind‐coded containers. To maintain the integrity of the blind, the composition of each container was not disclosed until completing the statistical analyses by an independent third‐party researcher. Each RR capsule contained 300 mg of RR (RhodioZen, Pharmanager Ingredients, pharmanager‐ingredients.com/en/), standardised to 3% rosavins and 1% salidroside, as per the manufacturer's analysis. The placebo pills contained 300 mg of maltodextrin with excipients (Life Pro Nutrition, lifepronutrition.com/en/). Both capsules were identical in appearance, taste and colour to ensure consistent blinding across treatments. Participants were instructed to ingest two capsules every 12 h (twice per day) for 3 days before each experimental session. On the day of testing, participants ingested four capsules, 1 h before the scheduled session (a total of 1200 mg per day for 4 days). A washout period of 3 days was allowed before the subsequent condition, which has been shown to be sufficient for complete elimination and to prevent any carryover effects. 30

For manipulation of the cognitive demand during the 30‐min visual task, an incongruent Stroop task, which requires response inhibition and sustained attention, was employed to induce mental fatigue. 31 This type of task has been demonstrated to induce mental fatigue. 32 , 33 In the incongruent Stroop colour‐word task, participants viewed names of colours (e.g., ‘blue’, ‘red’, ‘yellow’ or ‘green’) displayed on a screen, where the ink colour did not match the word's meaning (e.g., the word ‘red’ appearing in blue ink). Participants had to identify the ink colour by using one of four coloured buttons on the keyboard rather than reading the word itself. They were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. After each response, a new word was presented. For the control condition, participants watched a neutral documentary for 30 min on the same 27‐inch screen (Samsung, samsung.com/) used for the Stroop task. Throughout both interventions, participants remained seated in the same position, maintaining a 50 cm distance from the screen.

Dependent variables

The lag and variability of the accommodative response were measured using a binocular open‐field Grand Seiko WAM‐5500 autorefractor in HI‐SPEED mode. This device, validated for reliable and accurate accommodation data acquisition at a temporal resolution of approximately 5 Hz and a sensitivity of 0.01 D, 34 was used under binocular conditions. Measurements were taken from the dominant eye, determined via the hole‐in‐the‐card method, 35 while participants placed their chin and forehead on the corresponding supports. Upon arrival in the laboratory for each experimental session, the monocular refractive state was measured in static mode to calculate the lag of accommodation. The baseline refractive state was measured monocularly using the autorefractor in static mode at 500 cm, obtaining five measurements for each participant and averaging them for subsequent calculations. After the 30‐min visual task (Stroop test or control video), the accommodative response was continuously recorded over 60 s as participants fixated on a stationary target (Maltese cross; Michelson contrast = 79%, base luminance = 31 cd/m2) at three different distances (500 cm, 40 cm and 20 cm). This duration has been demonstrated to assess the magnitude and variability of the accommodative response accurately with the WAM‐5500 autorefractor. 36 For these recordings, participants were instructed to fixate on the centre of the target as clearly and steadily as possible, and an experienced examiner ensured the reference marker of the instrument remained aligned with the pupil. A 3‐min break was given between consecutive recordings. Data were processed following standard guidelines, with any data points exceeding ±3 standard deviations of the mean spherical refraction removed as blinks or recording errors. 37 The lag of accommodation was calculated as the difference between the mean accommodative response (from dynamic mode recordings) and the corresponding accommodative demand (e.g., 2.5 D at 40 cm). To account for any residual refractive error, as recommended by Poltavski et al., 38 the baseline static refraction when viewing a far target was subtracted. For instance, if a participant had a mean finding of 1.90 D at 40 cm and a residual refractive error of +0.05 D, their near refractive state would be 1.85 D, resulting in a lag of accommodation of 0.65 D (i.e., −1.85 – [−2.50] = 0.65 D). The residual refractive error of the participants was −0.09 ± 0.27 D (range: −0.37 to +0.24 D). The variability of accommodation was defined as the standard deviation of the continuous accommodative data after excluding artefacts and blink‐related points (i.e., data exceeding ±3 SDs from the mean), with higher values indicating reduced stability of the accommodative response.

For the assessment of DES symptoms, immediately after completing the 30‐min visual task, participants reported their subjective levels of visual fatigue and discomfort using a 10‐item questionnaire developed by Hayes et al. 39 This questionnaire asks participants to rate 10 elements related to ocular symptoms (e.g., dry eye, blurred vision) using a Likert scale ranging from ‘0’ (not at all) to ‘10’ (extremely) (see Appendix 1). The total symptom score was quantified on a scale from 0 to 100.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive values of the dependent variables are presented as mean and SDs. The Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests were performed to assess the normality of the data and the homogeneity of variance, respectively. For the dynamics of the accommodative response, two separate repeated measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with the ‘supplement’ (RR and placebo), ‘cognitive demand’ (Stroop and control) and ‘target distance’ (500, 40 and 20 cm) as within‐participants factors were conducted for lag and variability of accommodation. For the different DES symptoms, separate repeated measures ANOVAs were performed with the ‘supplement’ (RR and placebo) and ‘cognitive demand’ (Stroop and control) as within‐participants factors. The level of statistical significance was established at p < 0.05, and multiple comparisons were corrected with the Holm–Bonferroni procedure. Standardised effect sizes were reported using the partial ƞ 2 (ƞp2) for Fs and Cohen's d (d) for t‐tests. All statistical analyses were performed using the JASP statistics package (version 0.19.0; JASP Team, 2024, jasp‐stats.org).

RESULTS

Accommodative lag

Analysis of the lag of accommodation revealed statistically significant differences for the main effect of ‘target distance’ (F 2,34 = 60.65, p < 0.001, ƞp2 = 0.78), but the main effects of ‘supplement’ (F 1,17 = 2.30, p = 0.15, ƞp2 = 0.12) and ‘cognitive demand’ (F 1,17 = 0.47, p = 0.50, ƞp2 = 0.03) did not reach significance. For the interaction effects, statistically significant differences were found for the ‘target distance × cognitive demand’ interaction (F 2,34 = 4.17, p = 0.03, ƞp2 = 0.20), whereas the remainder of the interactions were not statistically significant (p ≥ 0.08 in all cases). Post‐hoc analyses did not show statistically significant differences for the different comparisons between both supplement and cognitive demand conditions at the three distances tested (Figure 2).

The whiskers represent the standard deviations; horizontal lines indicate the median value and filled circles the average value.

Accommodative variability

For the variability of the accommodative response, statistically significant differences were found for the three main effects, namely ‘supplement’ (F 1,17 = 9.33, p = 0.007, ƞp2 = 0.35), ‘cognitive load’ (F 1,17 = 4.75, p = 0.04, ƞp2 = 0.22) and ‘target distance’ (F 2,34 = 42.44, p < 0.001, ƞp2 = 0.71). Overall, there was lower variability of accommodation in the RR compared with the placebo condition, as well as after watching the video versus performing the Stroop task. Regarding the target distance, there was a higher variability of accommodation at closer distances. The interaction effect of ‘target distance × supplement’ also reached statistical significance (F 2,34 = 4.82, p = 0.04, ƞp2 = 0.35), but the rest of interactions were not statistically significant (p > 0.06 for all cases).

To assess the influence of target distance on the supplement effects, separate ANOVAs were performed for each distance. For the 500 cm distance, there were no statistically significant differences for any of the two main effects or the interaction (p > 0.40). When considering the 40 cm distance, the main effects of ‘supplement’ and ‘cognitive demand’ exhibited statistically significant differences (F 1,17 = 23.14, p < 0.001, ƞp2 = 0.58; and F 1,17 = 7.20, p = 0.02, ƞp2 = 0.30, respectively); however, the interaction ‘supplement × cognitive demand’ was not significant (F 1,17 = 0.01, p = 0.94). Lastly, analysis of the results obtained at 20 cm showed statistically significant differences for the main effect of ‘supplement’ (F 1,17 = 5.09, p = 0.04, ƞp2 = 0.23), but not for the effect of ‘cognitive load’ (F 1,17 = 3.02, p = 0.10) nor the interaction ‘supplement × cognitive demand’ (F 1,17 = 1.31, p = 0.27). DES symptoms

The effects of the type of supplement and cognitive task on DES symptoms are shown in Table 1. Overall, all symptoms were sensitive to the cognitive load manipulation (all p‐values <0.05), that is, participants experienced more symptoms after performing the Stroop test in comparison with the video condition. The impact of the supplement on DES symptoms was limited to the symptom ‘Blurred vision when looking into the distance at the end of the near task’, showing a lower level of this symptom after RR in comparison with the placebo supplementation (p = 0.009). The interaction effect was also statistically significant (p = 0.02), with the mediating effects of RR supplementation being exacerbated in the more mentally demanding condition.

DISCUSSION

The results of this triple‐blind, placebo‐controlled study indicate that RR supplementation led to a decrease in the variability of the accommodative response at 40 cm and 20 cm following a 30‐min visual task. However, the magnitude of these differences appears clinically modest (~0.10 D at 40 cm and ~ 0.30 D at 20 cm). Regarding DES symptoms, RR supplementation influenced only one of the 10 assessed symptoms, specifically reducing the perceived levels of ‘blurred vision when looking into the distance at the end of the near task’. These findings partially support a mediating effect of RR supplementation on the dynamics of the accommodative response and DES symptoms after prolonged near viewing, although the clinical relevance of these changes requires further investigation.

We partially failed to accept our initial hypothesis, as RR supplementation did not influence the lag of accommodation. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have examined the acute effects of RR on the magnitude of the accommodative response. This substance is classified as an adaptogen, as it supports stress adaptation and helps maintain nervous system balance. Research investigating stimulating compounds, such as caffeine, has reported mixed findings. For instance, Abokyi et al., 40 found that caffeine ingestion increased the amplitude of accommodation in non‐habitual coffee consumers, whereas Redondo et al. 41 observed ∼4 mg/kg of caffeine did not modulate the lag of accommodation in either low‐ or high‐caffeine consumers. It is important to note that while the amplitude of accommodation reflects the maximum focusing capacity of the eye, the lag of accommodation represents the accuracy of fixation at a given distance, 42 suggesting that the physiological mechanisms underlying these variables may differ. Although the mechanisms of action of RR and caffeine on the nervous system are different, 19 , 43 neither substance appears to cause significant changes in the magnitude of the accommodative response during near viewing.

Ocular accommodation generally fluctuates within a range of approximately ±0.5D to provide directional cues for dynamic focusing, which is termed the variability of the accommodative response. 44 The balance between the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system plays a crucial role in controlling ocular accommodation, 25 and factors influencing this balance could potentially affect accommodative dynamics. Notably, higher fluctuations of accommodation have proven to be an accurate and sensitive indicator of visual fatigue and stress. Here, it was found that RR supplementation was associated with more stable accommodation at near distances (i.e., 40 and 20 cm). Given that RR has been recognised for its adaptogenic properties, particularly in reducing mental fatigue and enhancing cognitive performance, it is plausible that its effects on accommodation stability may stem from an improved ability to sustain visual attention and reduce visual fatigue. The magnitude of these differences is similar to those observed in studies manipulating attention capabilities, 45 placebo and nocebo effects 46 or caffeine intake. 41 For example, Redondo et al., 45 found greater stability of accommodation using auditory feedback, with the magnitude of the effects being very similar to those obtained with RR supplementation (~0.10 D and ~ 0.30 D at 40 cm and 20 cm, respectively). These preliminary results require further investigation to determine whether different dosing regimens or long‐term supplementation could have a more pronounced effect on ocular accommodation dynamics. Interestingly, the reduced variability observed at 40 cm (Figure 3, panel B), when compared with the other distances, may be related to it being a more habitual viewing distance in daily life, potentially resulting in more stable accommodative responses due to familiarity and oculomotor adaptation. Nonetheless, this explanation remains speculative and further research is needed to clarify this observation.

The impact of RR supplementation on DES symptoms seems to align with the effects observed on accommodative dynamics. Participants reported a lower level of ‘Blurred vision when looking into the distance at the end of the near task’, which is related to accommodation and binocular function. According to Wolffsohn and colleagues, 6 DES symptoms may derive from two distinct mechanisms, namely those associated with accommodation and/or binocular function and ocular surface health. Blurred vision and refocusing difficulties after computer use are primarily linked with alterations in accommodation, whereas sensations of tired eyes, eye strain, discomfort, irritation and dryness are more closely related to ocular surface alterations and dry eye disease. 47 In the current study, symptoms and signs associated with the accommodative function have demonstrated to be sensitive to RR supplementation; however, symptoms related to ocular surface health were unaltered. Given the significant impact of dry eye symptoms (e.g., burning, irritation, ocular dryness or tearing) on DES, future investigations should explore the effectiveness of supplements specifically designed to manage dry eye in alleviating DES. 48

Scientific evidence suggests that RR contributes to autonomic nervous system stability. 17 This stabilisation of autonomic balance could be responsible for optimising ciliary muscle function and preventing stress‐induced accommodative fatigue, as observed in the current study. The current findings provide novel insights into the potential utility of RR supplementation for the management and prevention of accommodation‐related DES. However, there are several aspects that may limit the impact and validity of the current study and must be acknowledged. First, the magnitude of the observed effects must be carefully considered when interpreting the effects of RR supplementation on the accommodation dynamics and DES symptoms. Second, the experimental sample was formed of a cohort of healthy young adults, and further studies should compare the effects of RR supplementation on DES signs and symptoms between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. 49 , 50 Third, the physiological effects of nutritional supplements are known to be dependent on dosage, time of ingestion and individual tolerance, 51 and these factors deserve further investigation. Lastly, as DES is associated with changes associated with accommodation and/or binocular function and ocular surface health, it is hoped that future studies will assess the effectiveness of nutritional supplements targeted to mitigate dry eye signs and symptoms on the prevention and management of DES.

CONCLUSIONS

The consumption of RR (1200 mg per day for 4 days) caused greater stability of the accommodative response, when measured at 40 and 20 cm, after the execution of a 30‐min visual task. Also, a reduction in perceived levels of ‘blurred vision when looking into the distance at the end of the near task’ was observed after consuming RR in comparison with a placebo. However, the consumption of RR did not affect the magnitude of the accommodative response or any other visual symptoms. These findings suggest that RR supplementation may be considered as a potential strategy to mitigate DES, although the clinical impact of these effects requires further investigation. The effectiveness of different doses, timing of ingestion or combination with other supplements should be investigated in future studies.
