Genetic association analysis of grain yield-related traits in Aegilops tauschii under drought and non-stress conditions
Authors: Mehran Falaknaz, Ali Aalami, Ali Ashraf Mehrabi, Danial Kahrizi, Atefeh Sabouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAegilops tauschii, a wild relative of wheat, represents a rich and diverse gene source for new alleles that can effectively enhance tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses in wheat. This experiment aimed to identify markers associated with important morphological and physiological traits of Aegilops tauschii under drought stress conditions using association analysis. A total of 125 genotypes of Aegilops tauschii were examined over two years (2017 and 2018) under two conditions: non-stress and drought stress. Based on SSR, ISSR, and retrotransposon markers, the genetic structure of the population was divided into seven sub-populations (K = 7). Among these markers, Xgwm30, UBC891, and the SUKKULA-UBC834 combinations exhibited the highest PIC values, while Xgwm539, UBC826, and the LTR1061-UBC834 combinations displayed higher MI indices. In comparing the three types of markers, retrotransposon markers exhibited higher values for the diversity indices, indicating their superior efficiency. According to the results of the association analysis, markers significantly related to morphological traits over two years non-stress and drought stress conditions were identified. Under drought stress conditions, these markers were as follows: grain diameter—UBC811, Xgwm320, Xgwm271; grain number per spike—LTR1061-LTR2116; harvest index—UBC811; plant height—LTR1061-LTR2116, LTR1061; root length—LTR2116-UBC834, UBC840, UBC811; plant fresh weight and dry weight—Xgwm271. Additionally, two markers, UBC811 and UBC840, showed significant relationship with grain diameter and root length in two years, respectively. Notably, UBC811 was associated with three traits: root length, harvest index, and grain diameter. Furthermore, Xgwm271 demonstrated significant relationships with four yield-related traits in both years. The identification of common markers for different traits can indicate genomic hotspots with pleiotropic effects or linkage of genomic regions involved in these traits. Moreover, markers demonstrating significant relationship with traits across various years or environments can be utilized in wheat breeding projects following additional validation. Considering that Aegilops tauschii is a source of resistance genes and drought is a major challenge in modern agriculture, identifying markers associated with genes that enhance drought resistance is crucial. This study is the first to identify markers UBC811 and Xgwm271 with significant associations across multiple yield-related traits under drought stress over two years, revealing potential genomic hotspots with pleiotropic effects. Introduction

Climate change, including global warming and drought stress, has led to increased food security risks in arid regions, particularly across Africa, the mountainous areas of Asia, and South America. Drought stress can significantly reduce crop yields by impairing plant growth, development, and physiological processes1.

The economic importance of cereals, especially wheat, as a primary food source for humans has prompted extensive efforts to optimize their production, particularly under challenging climatic conditions. One effective strategy involves developing and introducing high-yield cultivars capable of withstanding end-of-season drought stress, supplemented by efficient water management practices2. However, from a plant breeding perspective, drought tolerance is a complex trait that cannot be directly measured, making it challenging to identify drought-tolerant genotypes. Moreover, crop species bred or engineered to adapt to climate change often suffer from reduced genetic diversity, limiting their capacity to acquire new alleles. In contrast, the use of wild relatives offers breeders access to a diverse and rich gene pool, facilitating the discovery of novel and desirable alleles3.

Aegilops tauschii Coss is the most prominent species in the Aegilops genus, playing a significant role in the evolution of bread wheat as the donor of the D genome. Genome D has a crucial role in many agronomic traits of wheat, including disease resistance, environmental stress tolerance, baking quality, and yield. Aegilops tauschii is a wild, self-pollinated, diploid species (2n = 2x = 14, DD) native to Central Asia, spanning from Turkey to China and across the Caspian Sea. The natural cross between this species and tetraploid wheat resulted in the production of Spelt wheat approximately 10,000 years ago. However, only a few Aegilops tauschii lines from a limited area were involved in the combination. Previous studies have demonstrated that the D genome of bread wheat harbors lower genetic diversity compared to the A and B genomes, likely due to its more recent evolutionary origin and narrower genetic base4. Therefore, examining the genetic diversity of Aegilops genotypes and identifying significant genomic regions effective for tolerance to environmental stresses, especially drought, can be very effective in wheat breeding programs.

Drought resistance is a quantitative trait with a complex phenotype that is strongly influenced by the plant’s developmental stage5. Numerous association analysis studies have been conducted to discover and validate Quantitative trait loci (QTLs) or genes associated with important traits and to map candidate genes in various crops, such as wheat, under various types of abiotic stress6. Recently, many QTLs for key agronomic traits under diverse environmental conditions have been introduced into wheat. This information can be used in marker-assisted selection (MAS) breeding programs or to isolate these QTLs by map-based cloning. However, very few studies have been conducted to identify QTLs for physiological traits and to examine their co-localization with yield-related effects under stress conditions7–9.

To effectively utilize the genetic diversity found in Aegilops tauschii, as a valuable source of genes for enhancing resistance traits in bread wheat, particularly tolerance to drought and heat stress, it is necessary to conduct thorough genomic analysis in parallel with detailed morphological investigations10. Numerous studies have investigated the genetic diversity of tauschii species, and several have explored their genome structure and identified resistance-related genes within it3,4,11–13. These studies have identified several markers and genes associated with various morphological and other traits. Recently, Mahjoob et al.4 reported 23 significant associations based on genome-wide association study of morpho-physiological traits in Aegilops tauschii. These findings can facilitate the identification of candidate genes controlling these traits in bread wheat, thereby supporting future breeding efforts.

Iran, along with other countries in the Fertile Crescent (Iraq, Syria, Turkey, Israel, and Lebanon), is recognized as a major center of origin for Aegilops and wild wheat, as highlighted by Kilian et al.14 in their study. The hypothesis is that these genotypes may harbor favorable alleles conferring tolerance to environmental stress, particularly drought. To investigate this hypothesis, we have collected and extensively analyzed a diverse set of 125 Aegilops tauschii genotypes, encompassing both Iranian and foreign genotypes. We assessed a range of molecular markers, including Simple Sequence Repeats (SSR), Inter Simple Sequence Repeats (ISSR), and retrotransposon markers, with two main objectives: (i) to analyze the genetic diversity of the collected genotypes, and (ii) to identify genetic markers associated with drought tolerance traits.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The plant materials for the present study were 125 Aegilops tauschii (2n = 2x = 14) genotypes from various regions of Iran and other countries listed in Supplementary Table 1. We hereby confirm that the plant materials (Aegilops tauschii) used in this study were identified and propagated by Dr. Ali Ashraf Mehrabi, one of the co-authors of this manuscript. These materials are maintained as accessions within the Cereal and Pulse Gene Bank of Ilam University. The accession codes cited throughout this study correspond directly to the codes assigned and utilized by this gene bank, where the materials are curated. Furthermore, we confirm that the necessary permissions for the collection and organization of the cereal and pulse genetic resources in western and northern regions of Iran, including the Aegilops tauschii accessions utilized in this research, were obtained. These activities were conducted under a national project titled “Collection and Organization of Cereal and Pulse Genetic Resources of Iran” which received authorization from the Iranian Ministry of Science, Research and Technology, as well as the Department of Environment of Iran. The accession codes used in this manuscript are the official codes assigned to these materials within the aforementioned gene bank at Ilam University, which houses the genetic resources collected under these permissions. The study was carried out in 2017 and 2018 at Razi University in Kermanshah province of Iran (34°0.31′N, 47°0.07′L, 1391 m ASL). In each crop year, the planting period lasted for 8 months, and each year planting was done in the middle of November and harvesting was done in the middle of July of the following year. Supplementary Table 2 contains the meteorological data for Kermanshah in 2017 and 2018.

Experimental design

Genotypes were cultivated under two non-stress and drought-stress conditions to perform phenotypic evaluations. Irrigation was used immediately after sowing for seed germination and uniform and simultaneous growth in normal and dry experiments. Normal (non-stress) irrigation was done according to the plant’s water requirements until the end of the growing season. For this site, two supplementary irrigations were done at the beginning of the stem elongation and grain-filling stages. In a stress site, irrigation was carried out until flowering based on the plant’s water requirement. After that, irrigation was stopped until the end of the growing season. Also, shelters were preemptively placed over the stress site to mitigate the potential effects of forecasted rainfall. This precautionary measure was implemented to ensure that the experimental conditions remained consistent and reflective of drought stress conditions. The analysis of variance results confirmed the significant effect of stress treatment on physiological traits in this experiment15. For morphological traits such as plant height, spike length, root length, and grain weight per spike, data were recorded from 10 randomly selected plants per genotype in each plot. For physiological traits, measurements were taken from five randomly chosen plants per genotype. The experiment was conducted as an augmented design with six control genotypes and three replications in the research field. Six control genotypes (C1: IUGB-00205, C2: IUGB-00222, C3: IUGB-00275, C4: IUGB-00381, C5: IUGB-00382, C6: IUGB-02076) were selected based on seed availability to enable replication in the augmented block design, supporting environmental effect estimation. A combined analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted exclusively on the six replicated check varieties across four environments (two years × two stress conditions). The ANOVA model included the main effects of genotype (check), year, stress, and all possible two- and three-way interactions among these factors.

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Evaluation of traits

Morphological traits

Following physiological maturity, defined as the stage of maximum grain dry weight accumulation, key morphological traits were assessed. These traits include Number of Grain Per Spike (NGS), Single Plant Fresh Weight (SPFW), Single Plant Dry Weight (SPDW), Single Plant Grain Yield (SPGY), Harvest Index (HI), Grain Diameter (GD), Spike Length (SL), Root Length (RL), and Plant Height (PH).

Carotenoids: To measure carotenoids, 0.5 g of fresh flag leaf samples were homogenized in 5 ml of acetone. Subsequently, 3 ml of ether was added, and the absorbance of the resulting specimens was measured at 470 nm using a spectrophotometer. Proline content: Proline content (µg/g FW: micrograms per gram of fresh weight) was measured using Bates et al. method21. Approximately 0.1 g of powdered fresh leaves was mixed with 10 ml of 3% sulfuric acid. Afterward, 2 ml of the resulting extract was combined with 2 ml of ninhydrin and 2 ml of acetic acid, and heated in a water bath at 100°C for 1 h. Following this, 4 ml of toluene was added and mixed vigorously for 20 s. The resulting mixture separated into two phases in the test tube: a pink organic phase on top and a clear blue, colorless phase at the bottom. The organic phase was then used for colorimetry in a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 520 nm. To establish a standard curve, pure proline at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, and 250 μm was used, with all procedures conducted on ice. Subsequently, the standard proline curve was plotted, and the amount of dissolved proline was determined using this curve.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4). To ensure the validity of the subsequent analysis, homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test at a significance level of 1%. Additionally, normality of the data was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test at a significance level of 5%. A mixed model analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then conducted using the Proc Mixed procedure. This approach is appropriate because it accounts for the random effects of year and environment (normal and stress augment experiments) while treating genotype as a fixed effect. The error term for testing the significance of mean squares for the experimental factors was based on six control genotypes with three replications.

Genetic evaluations

DNA was extracted from the leaf tissue of 15-day-old seedlings using a modified CTAB method22. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted in a 20 μl volume. The reaction mixture included 50 ng of template DNA, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.05 mM of each dNTP, 0.2 μmol of primer (including 30 SSR, 30 ISSR, and nine retrotransposon combinations, Supplementary Tables 5–7 provide information on the primers of SSR, ISSR, and retrotransposon combination), one unit of Taq DNA Polymerase enzyme, and 1 × reaction buffer (provided by CinnaGen, a biotechnology company based in Iran). The PCR products were then separated on a 4% agarose gel using 1% TBE reaction buffer and SafeView dye. Finally, the DNA bands were visualized using the Quantum ST4 Gel Document device.

Analysis of polymorphism

POPGENE32 software version 1.3123 was utilized to calculate polymorphism information content (PIC), Shannon index, and Nei variation index. Also, NTSYS-pc software24 was employed to group the genotypes.

Analysis of the genetic structure

The genetic structure analysis of the population using the Bayesian grouping technique was performed in STRUCTURE software25. This technique assigns genotypes to hypothetical subpopulations with equal probabilities so that the LD is kept at a minimum and the gamete phase balance is maximum for each subpopulation. The STRUCTURE software creates a Q matrix for every K value (number of subpopulations) including an estimation of the probability coefficient of each cultivar’s membership in each subpopulation. The actual number of subpopulations (K) was calculated based on LnP(D) (selection criteria in STRUCTURE v.2.2) and the technique proposed by Evanno et al.26. The length of the period of 10.000 and the number of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations of 100.000 were selected in this software. The cycles were iterated 10 times. This technique is based on the ΔK statistic which breaks the slope of the LnP(D) probability function at the point where the hypothetical K is the most probable.

Marker-trait association (MTA) analysis

General linear model (GLM) and mixed linear model (MLM) were employed to identify markers significantly associated with the evaluated traits. The MTA analysis was conducted using TASSEL software v.2.0.127.

To ensure the quality and reliability of the genotype data, several steps were taken before conducting the MTA analysis. First, low-quality alleles (amplified fragments) and genotypes with a high amount of missing data were excluded. In addition, the Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) for each marker locus was calculated in Excel. Any markers with a MAF of less than 5% were removed, as they may not yield reliable associations. After these exclusions, the genotypic data was tested for Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) using TASSEL.

To account for population stratification, the Q-matrix was extracted using STRUCTURE software and incorporated into the MTA analysis. The Kinship (K-matrix) was also computed using TASSEL. This K-matrix was then used as a covariate in the association model to consider the relatedness of the genotypes. Finally, the Mixed Linear Model (MLM) in TASSEL was used to identify the marker–trait associations (MTAs). This model takes into consideration the Q + K matrix, which helps to avoid false associations between trait markers.

Results

Analysis of variance in Aegilops tauschii genotypes

The analysis of variance results revealed a significant effect of the year on all morphological traits except plant height. Genotype variance for all traits was significant (P < 0.01), indicating substantial genetic variation among Aegilops genotypes and a significant difference between the two years of evaluation. Due to significant interaction effects between genotype, year, and stress for most traits, the genetic analysis was performed separately for each year instead of using the mean of the two years (Supplementary Tables 3–4). To avoid redundancy, detailed phenotypic assessments, including morphological characteristics, physiological parameters, and tolerance indices, are reported in earlier studies28,29.

Genetic diversity

The pattern of the bands resulting from separating the PCR products for 30 pairs SSR, 30 primers ISSR, and nine retrotransposon primer combinations were clear and scoreable (Supplementary Fig. 1–3). According to the results, the average PIC statistic for ISSR, retrotransposons, and SSR was 0.332, 0.311, and 0.271, respectively (Table 1). Detail of the genetic diversity statistics, including allele frequency, PIC, and the number of observed alleles, are presented in Supplementary Tables 5–7. The highest PIC in ISSR primers was observed for UBC824 and UBC 891 (0.383). The average of the Shannon’s index for the retrotransposons markers was estimated 0.345 while this genetic diversity statistic for SSR, and ISSR were calculated at 0.278 and 0.232, respectively. The highest MI (0.434) and Shannon index (0.457) were observed for the LTR1061-UBC834 primer combinations. In this study, the average of the Nei index for retrotransposons, ISSRs, and SSRs was estimated to be 0.334, 0.147, and 0.277, respectively. The highest number of polymorphic alleles (1.735) and number of effective alleles (1.577) in retrotransposon markers was observed in LTR1061-UBC834. The results show that the used markers, especially retrotransposons and ISSRs, can reveal genetic diversity among Aegilops tauschii genotypes.

The results revealed that out of a total of 125 Aegilops tauschii genotypes, they were categorized into seven subpopulations (K = 7) at a probability exceeding 70% as shown in Fig. 1. The distribution within the subpopulations is as follows: six genotypes (4.8%) fell under the first group (red), four genotypes (3.2%) were classified in the second group (green), six genotypes (4.8%) belonged to the third group (blue), 29 genotypes (23.2%) were part of the fourth group (yellow), 28 genotypes (22.4%) were attributed to the fifth group (purple), six genotypes (4.8%) were associated with the sixth group (cyan), and 46 genotypes (36.8%) were allocated to the seventh group (mustard) as depicted in Fig. 2.

The goal was to identify markers significantly associated with physiological and morphological traits. The GLM model considered the Q matrix, while the MLM model incorporated both the K matrix and Q + K matrices as dependencies. The results of the GLM model’s association analysis in both stress and non-stress conditions in 2017 revealed a significant relationship between 48 markers under stress and 38 markers under non-stress conditions with the studied traits. Specifically, seven markers at the 5% significance level and 41 markers at the 1% significance level were associated with the traits under stress conditions, while one marker at the 5% significance level and 37 markers at the 1% significance level were associated with the traits under non-stress conditions. Furthermore, the MLM association model was employed to enhance accuracy and minimize bias compared to the GLM in identifying markers associated with the traits. The results indicated that 46 markers were significant under stress conditions and 34 markers were significant under non-stress conditions at both the 5% and 1% significance levels. Subsequently, an association analysis was conducted using the data from the second-year (2018) experiment, employing GLM and MLM methods to assess the stability of the identified markers. The association analysis results for the morphological data under stress and non-stress conditions demonstrated significant associations between 37 markers under stress and 57 markers under non-stress conditions. Specifically, all 37 markers under stress conditions showed significance at the 1% level, while six markers were detected at the 5% level. Additionally, 51 markers were observed at the 1% level under non-stress conditions (Supplementary Tables 8–11). Moreover, the results from the MLM association model indicated that 37 and 45 markers were significant at the 5% and 1% levels under stress and non-stress conditions, respectively.

The association analysis results for physiological data under both stress and non-stress conditions, as indicated by the GLM, highlighted the significance of 57 and 59 markers under stress and non-stress conditions, respectively (Supplementary Tables 12, 13). Specifically, under stress conditions, eight and 49 markers were significant at the 5% and 1% levels, respectively. Similarly, under non-stress conditions, eight markers were found to be significant at the 5% level, while 51 markers showed significance at the 1% level. Additionally, the results from the MLM association method revealed that 54 and 51 markers were significant at the 5% and 1% levels under stress and non-stress conditions, respectively. According to the result, UBC809 and UBC808 (ISSRs) displayed the highest R2 values for drought stress in 2017 and 2018, respectively.

According to the results, when wheat is exposed to drought stress, certain SSR markers show strong associations with traits related to yield and stress tolerance. For example, the marker Xgwm271 on chromosome 5D was significantly linked to SPGY, SPFW, SPDW, and GD. On chromosome 2D, Xgwm320 was associated with GD, while Xgwm114 on 3D showed a link to HI, indicating its role in resource allocation under drought. Additionally, Xgwm609 on 4D, associated with SPGY. Under non-stress conditions, several SSR markers were associated with traits influencing yield and plant architecture. On chromosomes 1D and 7D, Xgwm106 and Xgwm350 were linked to PH. Xgwm325 on 6D was associated with SPGY, and Xgwm271 (5D) and Xgwm341 (3D) were consistently linked to SPFW and SPDW. The marker Xgwm539 on 2D was repeatedly associated with RL, a trait important for water and nutrient uptake.

Some markers were also associated with physiological traits under non-stress conditions. Xgwm383 on 3D was linked to RWC, and Xgwm44 on 7D showed association with ELWR. Xgwm325 on 6D was connected to C1, and Xgwm271 on 5D was associated with Pro.

Under drought stress, Xgwm174 (5D) and Xgwm30 (2D) were linked to MSI, which reflects cell membrane integrity under oxidative stress. Xgwm341 (3D) was associated with C1, and Xgwm44 (7D) showed links to both MSI and ELWR, suggesting its potential involvement in water conservation and membrane protection.

Overall, based on the results obtained under drought stress conditions across both years, RL showed significant associations with UBC840, LTR1061-UBC834, and UBC811. Additionally, GD was associated with Xgwm271, Xgwm320, and UBC811 in both years. Moreover, HI exhibited an association with UBC811 in both years, and SPGY was associated with Xgwm271 and LTR1061-UBC834 in both years. PH was associated with LTR1061-LTR2116 and LTR1061 in both years. Furthermore, Xgwm271 demonstrated significant relationships with four traits (SPGY, SPFW, SPDW, and GD) in both years. Similarly, LTR1061-UBC834 exhibited significant relationships with three traits (SPGY, SPDW, and RL). Lastly, UBC811 was associated with three traits: RL, HI, and GD (Table 2).

In non-stress conditions, NGS and PH showed an association with LTR1061-UBC834 in both years. GD, NGS, and SL exhibited a significant relationship with UBC811 in both years. Additionally, NGS and SL were associated with UBC812 in both years. UBC840, Xgwm539, LTR2116, and UBC824 were consistently linked to RL in both years. Furthermore, SPFW and SPDW demonstrated an association with UBC899 in both years, while SL showed a significant relationship with SUKKULA, UBC811, and UBC812 in both years (Table 3).

Tables 4 and 5 present a comprehensive overview of the relationships between SSR, ISSR, and retrotransposons and physiological as well as biochemical traits in Aegilops tauschii under drought stress and non-stress conditions, respectively. Specifically, significant associations were observed between RWC and Pro with LTR1061 and UBC834 under non-stress and drought stress conditions, respectively. Furthermore, UBC826 and Xgwm44 were linked to MSI and ELWR under both conditions. Moreover, UBC811 and UBC812 exhibited notable relationships with C1 and C2 under both non-stress and drought stress conditions, and RWC showed a significant association with LTR1061-SUKKULA.

Discussion

Genetic diversity

The results of the present study revealed that the diversity indices of SSR, ISSR, and retrotransposon markers were comparatively high, suggesting the effective utility of these three marker systems in assessing genetic diversity. The PIC index is one of the criteria used to evaluate the distinguishing power of the markers, which can be used to discover the value of a marker demonstrating polymorphism within a population30. Among the three marker types, ISSRs showed the highest PIC value (0.332), followed by retrotransposons (0.311) and SSRs (0.271), indicating that ISSRs were most effective in detecting polymorphism. However, based on the Shannon diversity index, retrotransposon markers were superior to ISSR and SSR markers, with values of 0.345, 0.278, and 0.232, respectively. The Shannon index is particularly valuable for evaluating genetic diversity in populations when using dominant markers, which cannot distinguish heterozygous individuals and may not accurately reflect Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium31.

On the other hand, Nei genetic diversity is preferable over the Shannon index for co-dominant markers. The present study found the Nei index to be 0.334, 0.147, and 0.277 for retrotransposons, ISSRs, and SSRs, respectively, indicating the superiority of the retrotransposon markers in displaying genetic diversity. This is mainly due to the high number of amplified bands and the abundance of retrotransposon sites in the genome of the studied species. A study was conducted to analyze the genetic diversity of Aegilops tauschii, tetraploid, and hexaploid wheat using retrotransposon-based markers. The findings revealed that the PIC, Shannon, and Nei diversity indices were higher for IRAP markers (retrotransposon + retrotransposon) compared to REMAP markers (retrotransposon + ISSR).32 These findings emphasize that targeted marker selection plays a more critical role in diversity estimation than simply increasing the number of markers. Therefore, it is more effective to use highly informative markers rather than relying on an untargeted expansion of marker sets. Furthermore, high diversity index values reflect not only the efficiency of the markers but also the adequate distribution and sample size used in the study33, which are essential for reliable diversity assessments.

Overall, the results the results suggest that retrotransposon and ISSR markers are particularly valuable for assessing genetic diversity in Aegilops tauschii. Moreover, it appears that the genotypes used in the present study had a high intra-species diversity, which can provide the possibility of finding useful alleles and genes for wheat breeders.

Population structure and association analysis

Important agronomic traits are generally controlled by multiple QTLs and genetic interactions between QTLs and environmental factors. Further studies showed that association analysis can be used to identify QTLs controlling complex traits at narrow genetic distances34,35. In this technique, the relationship between the phenotype and genotype is used to identify the regions controlling the traits on the chromosomes based on linkage disequilibrium in diverse natural plant populations36.

The main challenge of association mapping is distinguishing between the actual association between the markers and the false associations resulting from kinship and population structure; however, population structure is the primary source of false-positive results37. Accounting for population structure is therefore a critical prerequisite in association mapping, as it helps prevent spurious associations between traits and markers38. The studied population in association analysis should ideally be unstructured, since the population structure may render the results unreliable due to the identification of false associations between traits and the markers. Thus, the effect of these relativeness factors must be considered in association analysis to reduce the skewness of the results39. In the present study, these confounding factors were addressed prior to conducting association analysis. The 125 genotypes were classified into seven subpopulations, and the MLM approach was employed to account for both kinship and population structure. This model has proven effective in minimizing false-positive associations, as it integrates both sources of confounding40,41.

Zhang et al.42 reported that MLM methods are useful in controlling for population structure and relatedness within genome-wide association studies. Li et al.37 investigated population structure and linkage disequilibrium patterns in a tetraploid alfalfa breeding population using genome-wide SSR markers and identified markers related to yield and cell wall composition through association mapping.

The results of this research showed that certain markers are shared across both morphological and physiological traits. For instance, the LTR1061 marker from the retrotransposon marker system had significant associations with 12 traits (C1, C2, Chla, ChlT, RWC, GD, NGS, PH, SPDW, SL, SPFW, and SPGY). In comparison, UBC118 from ISSRs had significant associations with 10 traits (C1, C2, MSI, GD, NGS, HI, RH, SPDW, SL, and SPFW), representing the highest number of trait associations among all markers evaluated. The identification of multi-trait-associated markers is particularly valuable in plant breeding, as it enables the simultaneous selection for multiple traits, thereby enhancing breeding efficiency43. The presence of common markers controlling several traits can be due to pleiotropy or linkage of the genomic regions controlling traits.

The association of a single trait with multiple markers may reflect its polygenic nature, where each gene contributes a small proportion to the overall phenotypic variation. Hence, a relatively small R2 is calculated in association analysis, indicating the polygenic inheritance of complex traits. This also indicates that the environment can have significant impacts on these traits. Therefore, it is recommended to estimate environmental effects and separate them from genetic effects by conducting the experiments in several environments, or over several years to identify stable genomic loci in different environments. Thus, the present study used data from two years to determine the association between traits and markers.

The present study identified several common markers exhibiting significant associations with morphological traits under stress conditions across both years were as follows: GD: UBC811, Xgwm320, NGS: LTR1061-LTR2116, HI: UBC811, PH: LTR1061-LTR2116, LTR1061, RL: LTR2116-UBC834, UBC840 and UBC811, SPDW and SPFW: Xgwm271. These markers can be considered for resistance modification as stable markers independent from year or environment44. In the study of Khodadadi et al.45 there was a significant relationship between the Xgwm271 marker and carotenoid content in bread wheat and its wild relative45.

The Xgwm320 region on chromosome 2D is genetically linked to loci harboring LEA and WRKY genes, which are central to plant drought resistance through their protective and regulatory roles in stress adaptation46. The Xgwm271 region on chromosome 2B is genetically linked to the photoperiod-response gene Ppd2 and the disease resistance locus Yr7/Sr9g, as indicated by the mapping of QTL for tissue-culture response47.

These findings are supported by previous studies. Motawea et al.48 reported that UBC-811 and UBC-840 markers were associated with plant height and number of spikes/m2. In the present study, Xgwm271 demonstrated significant relationships with four traits (SPGY, SPFW, SPDW, and GD) in both years. Furthermore, the two markers, UBC811 and UBC840, were revealed to have significant relationships with GD and RL across both years, respectively. Notably, UBC811 was associated with three traits: RL, HI, and GD. Khaled et al.49 in their study of RAPD and ISSR markers’ association with yield components in bread wheat under drought stress reported a significant relationship (R2 = 0.24) between the UBC811 marker and spike length and some yield components.

In 2021, Wang et al.50 conducted a Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) on kernel traits in Aegilops tauschii, analyzing 223 genotypes using 6723 SNP markers. They identified 66 significant markers distributed across all seven chromosomes, explaining 4.82–13.36% of the phenotypic variations. Specifically, 15, 28, 22, 14, 21, and 13 SNPs were identified for kernel length (KL), kernel width (KW), kernel volume (KV), kernel surface area (KSA), kernel weight length (KWL), and hundred kernel weight (HKW), respectively. Additionally, six candidate genes potentially controlling these kernel traits were also identified (AET2Gv20774800, AET4Gv20799000, AET5Gv20005900, AET5Gv20084100, AET7Gv20644900, and AET5Gv21111700). The study evaluated six kernel traits over three years, revealing high diversity and medium to high heritability (0.74–0.87). Correlation and regression analyses indicated that HKW increased with KL, KW, and KV, indicating that kernel size traits play a critical role in determining kernel weight and overall yield potential.

Pour-Aboughadareh et al.51 conducted an association analysis of biochemical traits in 186 species of Aegilops and Triticum using 24 SSR markers. They found high levels of biochemical and molecular variability among wild relatives of wheat. The SSR marker system effectively identified significant MTAs within this germplasm.

Khan et al52 reviewed 158 wheat genotypes using 165 simple sequence repeat markers distributed across the A, B, and D genomes. Their study emphasized the importance of identifying relationships between agricultural traits and markers for wheat breeding programs aimed at enhancing resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses.

Etminan et al53. examined the genetic diversity in 221 germplasm accessions belonging to twelve species of Aegilops and Triticum in Iran using CBDP markers. Their findings indicated that CBDP markers effectively differentiated species-related groups based on genomic structure, suggesting their utility in inter-species relationship studies and genetic mapping when combined with SSR markers.

In rice, ISSR and SSR markers have shown significant associations with agro-morphological traits, explaining up to 49.3% of phenotypic variance54. LTR retrotransposons have been used as molecular markers, providing access to polymorphic, non-genic genome regions55. In bread wheat, SSR and ISSR markers were associated with root architecture and agro-morphological traits, with some markers linked to multiple traits, indicating possible pleiotropic effects56. These studies highlight the value of various marker systems in identifying genetic regions associated with important plant traits, thereby enhancing breeding programs.

Aegilops tauschii, the D-genome donor of bread wheat, is a valuable resource for wheat improvement due to its diverse genetic pool and adaptability to various environmental conditions. The MTA studies have been crucial in identifying genetic loci underlying agronomic and physiological traits in this species. These studies have provided insights into the genetic architecture of complex traits and facilitated the development of MAS strategies for wheat breeding. For instance, Huang et al.57 identified multiple MTAs associated with grain yield and related traits in a diverse panel of A. tauschii accessions, explaining a significant proportion of phenotypic variation. MTAs have also been identified for various physiological traits in A. tauschii, including drought tolerance, heat tolerance, and nutrient use efficiency. These traits are critical for wheat improvement as they contribute to yield stability and adaptation to adverse environmental conditions. For example, Wang et al.58 reported MTAs associated with root traits, such as root length and root volume, which are crucial for drought tolerance.

Advancements in sequencing technologies have enabled the development of high-density genetic maps for A. tauschii, facilitating fine-mapping of QTLs associated with drought tolerance traits. Luo et al.59 identified a major QTL for root length on chromosome 7D using a high-density genetic map, which can help identify candidate genes underlying drought tolerance.

The D genome of bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) plays a crucial role in conferring drought tolerance, a trait vital for global food security. Numerous studies have identified specific chromosomes and genes within the D genome that contribute to this complex trait. For instance, Osipova et al.60 demonstrated that substituting chromosome 2D from the drought-tolerant cultivar Saratovskaya 29 into other wheat genotypes improved tolerance to water deficit, suggesting the presence of genes on chromosome 2D that regulate key physiological processes under drought stress.

Chromosome 4D has also been implicated in drought tolerance. Liu et al.61 identified QTLs on chromosome 4D associated with root length, a trait closely linked to drought tolerance, indicating the presence of genes involved in root development and water uptake.

Chromosome 7D is another critical chromosome for drought tolerance. Several studies have reported QTLs on this chromosome associated with various drought-related traits, including grain yield, plant height, and leaf water potential. For example, Wang et al.62 identified a major QTL on chromosome 7D that controlled root length and water uptake.

In conclusion, chromosomes 2D, 4D, and 7D of the D genome are key players in conferring drought tolerance in bread wheat. Further research is needed to identify the specific genes underlying these QTLs and develop marker-assisted selection strategies for improving drought tolerance in wheat breeding programs. Liu et al.63 utilized a GWAS to identify SNPs associated with drought tolerance traits in A. tauschii, identifying several SNPs linked to traits such as relative water content, membrane stability index, and proline content, which can be used for developing molecular markers for MAS.

The notable and worthwhile point of the present study is the identification of significant diversity among the studied Aegilops tauschii genotypes, which can be purposefully used in long-term breeding programs. The results of the association analysis, relying on the identified significant markers provide researchers with valuable information to continue research. However, the identified markers associated with the studied traits must be validated in larger and different genetic backgrounds as well as various environments to ensure the identified association and increase the efficiency of these markers in wheat breeding programs such as marker-assisted selection. After necessary validations, the markers with the highest R2 can be used to identify the genes controlling the traits and saturate and sequence the respective genomic region.

In the present study, several MTAs exhibited relatively low coefficients of determination (R2), indicating that individual markers accounted for only a small fraction of the observed phenotypic variance. This outcome is consistent with the polygenic nature of complex agronomic and physiological traits such as grain yield, harvest index, and chlorophyll content, which are influenced by numerous loci with small additive effects64. Although low R2 values may limit the predictive accuracy of single markers, their repeated detection across diverse environments and years enhances their reliability and potential utility in MAS and genomic prediction. This is particularly relevant for Aegilops tauschii, a wild wheat progenitor with limited genomic resources, where such associations provide a valuable basis for future validation, fine mapping, and breeding applications65.

Conclusion

This study revealed extensive genotypic and phenotypic diversity within the Aegilops tauschii population, highlighting its potential as a valuable genetic resource for wheat improvement programs. The identified genotypes harbor a rich reservoir of genes potentially conferring stress tolerance, which is a critical breeding target. The study also demonstrated the effectiveness of molecular markers, particularly Xgwm30 (SSR), UBC891 (ISSR), and the SUKKULA-UBC834 combination (retrotransposon), for high polymorphism detection (PIC). Furthermore, markers Xgwm539 (SSR), UBC826 (ISSR), and LTR1061-UBC834 (retrotransposon) exhibited high efficiency in capturing genetic diversity (MI). Association analysis identified promising markers linked to grain yield-related traits under stress and non-stress conditions. Notably, SPFW and SPDW demonstrated an association with UBC899 in both years. Under non-stress conditions, LTR1061-SUKKULA (retrotransposon) and Xgwm271 (SSR) emerged as significant markers. Markers LTR2116, SUKKULA (retrotransposons), UBC811, UBC812, UBC824, and UBC840 (ISSRs), and Xgwm539 (SSR) demonstrated the most associations with various morphological traits across both years. Additionally, markers Xgwm271, LTR1061-UBC834, and UBC811 were significantly associated with multiple traits under drought stress across two years. Notably, these markers warrant further investigation for their potential application in marker-assisted selection programs. The study also identified markers associated with physiological traits, including LTR1061, LTR1061-SUKKULA, UBC808, UBC809, UBC811-815, UBC826, UBC834-835, UBC840, Xgwm383, and Xgwm44. These markers provide a valuable resource for future studies exploring the genetic basis of physiological responses to stress in Aegilops tauschii. While this study has identified promising markers, further validation in a broader range of genetic backgrounds and diverse environments is crucial to confirm their generalizability and effectiveness in breeding programs. Functional validation of the identified QTLs through gene discovery and characterization will be essential for developing functional markers for MAS applications. Additionally, integrating these markers with other relevant QTLs and exploring potential epistatic interactions will provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture of complex traits in Aegilops tauschii Overall, this study has laid a strong foundation for exploiting the genetic diversity of Aegilops tauschii for wheat improvement programs, particularly in developing stress-tolerant and high-yielding wheat cultivars.
