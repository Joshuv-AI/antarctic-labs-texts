Quantitative analysis of self-reporting and contact tracing in heterogeneous risk groups: a stochastic modeling study of the COVID-19 outbreak in Daegu, Korea
Authors: Jiwon Han, Eunok Jung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the early stages of a novel infectious disease outbreak, when vaccines, treatments, and herd immunity are lacking, non-pharmaceutical interventions—particularly self-reporting and contact tracing—play a critical role in suppressing transmission. During the first wave of COVID-19 in Korea, a large outbreak centered around a religious community in Daegu rapidly escalated, thus highlighting the transmission risks associated with a closed and low-reporting high-risk group. This study aimed to quantitatively assess the effectiveness of self-reporting and contact tracing strategies across heterogeneous risk groups.MethodsThe population of Daegu was stratified into two groups: a high-risk group characterized by high transmissibility and low reporting rate, and a low-risk group with lower transmissibility and higher reporting compliance. We developed a stochastic model and applied a modified Gillespie algorithm incorporating both Markovian and non-Markovian processes. Scenario-based simulations were conducted to evaluate the impact of changes in self-reporting rates and delays in contact tracing. We simulated each scenario 10,000 times to estimate the mean and 95% credible intervals for the number of infections.ResultsWhen the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group was lowered to 0.1, the total infections increased by approximately 22%, while unreported infections rose by 164% compared to the baseline. Conversely, increasing the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group to 0.8 reduced the total cases by approximately 21% and unreported infections by 86%. Notably, unreported infections in the low-risk group increased by approximately 416% when their reporting rate declined to 0.4, although this group had a lower transmission potential. Even a modest contact tracing delay of 4–7 d resulted in an 85% increase in unreported cases, with diminishing returns for longer delays, highlighting the critical importance of timely tracing in outbreak control.ConclusionsIn situations with heterogeneous risk groups, improving the self-reporting behavior of high-risk populations and maintaining high compliance in the low-risk group are essential for effective outbreak control. Contact tracing should be completed within 1–4 d to prevent further spread. Our study which accounts for behavioral heterogeneity, provides a scientific foundation for designing group-specific intervention strategies in future outbreaks of emerging infectious diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24862-y.Keywords: COVID-19, Stochastic modeling, Heterogeneous risk groups, Self-reporting, Contact-tracing, Non-pharmaceutical interventions

Background

When an infectious disease spreads to a previously unaffected community, the speed and scale of its transmission are shaped not only by the biological characteristics of the pathogen but also by the social interactions and individual behaviors [1, 2]. In the early phases of an epidemic, before vaccines or treatments are available, non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) including self-reporting, contact tracing, social distancing, and mask wearing, play a central role [3–5]. These interventions have contributed significantly to limiting the rapid spread of infectious diseases in real-world situations. However, their effectiveness largely depends on group-specific acceptance of public health policies and patterns of close contact, making it difficult to implement a uniform strategy across different populations [6].

The first wave of COVID-19 in the Republic of Korea was centered in Daegu and rapidly spread within a specific religious group. This event clearly demonstrated how the behavior of a single group could amplify transmission. The primary case associated with the group continued to attend worship services and small gatherings after the onset of symptoms [7]. As of March 23, which marked 34 d since the first confirmed case on February 18, Daegu had reported another 5,334 cases. Among them, 4,383 cases (82.2%) were associated with the group [8–10]. The group had over 240,000 members, with several thousand residing in Daegu. Regular in-person worship, enclosed worship spaces, and worship rules that prohibited face coverings created environmental conditions conducive to transmission [11]. The frequent close contact among members and presence of infected individuals in large gatherings, especially those with hundreds or more participants, further elevated the risk [12]. This event serves as a representative case for examining how close contact patterns and low self-reporting within a closed group can drive large-scale transmission [13]. In response, the Korean government implemented early interventions, including promotion of self-reporting, contact tracing, restrictions on group activities, and expansion of drive-through testing [14–16]. These efforts were partially effective in reducing spread and delaying community transmission. The interaction between individual behavior and public health interventions was a key driver of transmission suppression during the early phase of the outbreak, as demonstrated in [17].

Various mathematical and stochastic models have been used to understand the transmission dynamics of COVID-19 and evaluate the impact of interventions [18–22]. Several studies have stratified populations based on risk factors such as age, occupation, and social behavior to account for heterogeneity in transmission [23–26]. These approaches have contributed to explaining infection patterns and informing policy responses. For instance, Shim et al. quantified transmission rates and reproductive numbers during the early phase of the outbreak, demonstrating that the COVID-19 spread followed a sub-exponential growth pattern rather than a purely exponential one [27]. Kim et al. estimated the basic reproduction number () separately for a religious group and the general population during the first outbreak in Daegu [28]. Son et al. employed an individual-based simulation model to capture differences in contact structures between groups and project future transmission under school reopening scenarios [29].

Although these studies have advanced our understanding of epidemic progression under varying transmission conditions, only a few have quantitatively compared the effects of self-reporting and contact tracing across heterogeneous risk groups using scenario-based analyses [30, 31]. In practice, self-reporting and contact tracing were implemented as core NPIs during the initial outbreak in Daegu, and similar strategies were later applied in response to Mpox. Ko et al. developed a stochastic model to assess the impact of self-reporting and contact tracing on Mpox spread at a non-endemic setting [32]; their model treated the population as a single homogeneous group, as subgroup differentiation was deemed unnecessary for that context. This modeling approach served as a reference for our simulation structure, which explicitly incorporated risk-group heterogeneity. Nonetheless, simulation-based studies that quantitatively examine how behavior-driven NPIs operate differently across stratified risk groups remain limited in existing literature.

In this study, we developed a stochastic model based on the first COVID-19 outbreak in Daegu in 2020, dividing the population into high- and low-risk groups according to the transmission risk and behavioral characteristics. We designed scenarios by varying policy-related factors such as self-reporting rates and delays in contact tracing, and then evaluated changes in reported and unreported infections through simulations. We also incorporated differences in social contact patterns between risk groups to assess the effectiveness of targeted intervention strategies. The objective of our study is to provide practical guidance for policy decisions by quantitatively analyzing how early interventions operate under heterogeneous population structures.

Methods

Epidemiological data for COVID-19

This study was based on confirmed case data for COVID-19 reported in Daegu from February 18 to March 23, 2020. This period corresponded to the early phase of the first major outbreak in Korea, during which large-scale clusters of confirmed cases associated with a specific religious group were reported in the Daegu area. We used the data of daily new confirmed cases in Daegu based on the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC, now the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency) daily press briefings. “Shincheonji-related” cases were indicated in this reported data. The case definition for “Shincheonji-related” included individuals with direct epidemiological links to the religious group, such as congregation members, family contacts, and those with documented exposure through contact tracing [33]. Note that spline interpolation was used to supplement to fill in missing case reports on certain dates, such as weekends.

Stochastic model

We stratified the population into two groups to identify the differences in transmission risk. Individuals associated with the religious group were classified as a high-risk group owing to their greater transmission potential and lower self-reporting rates. In contrast, the general community population in Daegu, which was not affiliated with the group, was defined as a low-risk group owing to lower contact intensity and higher compliance with self-reporting. This classification, based on social behavior and contact frequency, served as a structural foundation for the model and provided a reference for evaluating intervention effectiveness.

We constructed a stochastic model to replicate the early COVID-19 outbreak in Daegu and examine differences in transmission patterns between risk groups. The model incorporated variations in transmission and reporting behavior between the high-risk group (religious group) and the low-risk group (general local community members). The complete structure of the model is presented in Fig. 1. The entire population of Daegu was categorized into high-risk (pink flowchart) and low-risk groups (blue flowchart); each risk group was further divided into six compartments, i.e., susceptible (), latent (), self-reported infectious (), non-self-reported infectious (), uninfected contactor (), and isolated contactor (). Isolation and recovery were managed in the same space regardless of the group; hence, only one isolated confirmed () and one recovered () compartment existed. The navy-blue dashed lines indicate the reactions caused by contact tracing. If an infected individual in the high-risk () self-reports and is isolated (), contact tracing occurs by tracking the latent (), infectious (), or uninfected contactor () within the high-risk group. Contact tracing duration () is defined as the time interval from identification of a confirmed case isolated through self-reporting to identification and isolation of those (exposed individuals , unreported infected individuals , or uninfected contacts ) who had contact with that case. High-risk contactors () and low-risk contactors () were subjected to contact tracing within the untracing period (), leading to the isolation of some contactors in or . After the isolation period, those individuals returned to their respective groups as susceptible people who could be infected. In Fig. 1, the solid and dashed arrow curves indicate a Markovian and a non-Markovian process, respectively.

Scenario design

Our study focused on the COVID-19 outbreak in Daegu from February 18 to March 23, 2020, before the implementation of enhanced social distancing policies, to assess the impact of self-reporting rates and delays in contact tracing on disease transmission. We implemented a scenario-based stochastic simulation using a modified Gillespie algorithm that incorporated both Markovian and non-Markovian processes. The self-reporting rate varied from 0.1 to 1.0. The contact tracing delay was divided into five intervals: 1–4, 4–7, 7–10, 10–13, and 13–16 d. The delays were uniformly distributed within each interval, reflecting variability in tracing initiation. Each scenario was simulated 10,000 times to calculate the mean, 95% credible intervals, and relative changes in both groups’ infected and unreported cases.

Two main scenarios were constructed. In the first scenario, the self-reporting rate of the high-risk group was fixed at 0.4, while that of the low-risk group was varied. In the second scenario, the self-reporting rate of the low-risk group was fixed at 0.8, and the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group was varied. Each setting considered five tracing delay intervals, and the sizes of infections and unreported cases were compared across various conditions. We designed these scenarios to quantitatively examine how variations in self-reporting and contact tracing affect transmission dynamics under heterogeneous conditions. Additionally, we examined the impact of time-varying self-reporting rates around the government’s crisis alert elevation. These temporal scenarios are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1.

Results

Baseline scenario

In the baseline scenario, we set a self-reporting rate of 0.4 for the high-risk group and 0.8 for the low-risk group. The delay in contact tracing was modeled as uniformly distributed between 1 and 4 d. Considering that the transmission dynamics likely shifted after the implementation of social distancing on February 29, we estimated separate transmission rates before and after this event. The simulations were conducted 10,000 times under identical conditions. For each outcome, we calculated the mean value and 95% credible intervals, including the number of confirmed cases and the cumulative number of infected cases, including unreported cases.

Figure 2A compares the simulated mean trajectory with the observed data, showing cumulative confirmed cases over time for both risk groups. The cumulative number of confirmed cases in the high-risk group was estimated at 4,417 cases (95% credible interval: 4,075–4,759), while the low-risk group was estimated at 2,337 cases (2,001–2,690). Figure 2B displays the estimated total number of infections (including confirmed and unreported cases) in each group. The results indicated that an average of 2,168 cases (628–3,693) of unreported infections likely occurred, representing approximately 24.3% of all infections. These group-specific results are consistent with previous Daegu outbreak studies. Son et al. (2020) reported similar cumulative confirmed cases in the hotspot and non-hotspot groups (4,394 and 2,322 cases, respectively), while Kim et al. (2021) analyzed the rapid transmission patterns within the religious community [28, 29]. Our results showed that 1,773 of these unreported cases were concentrated in the high-risk group, suggesting that a substantial number of infections were not detected within the specific religious group. These findings supported the possibility that a portion of infections was missed during self-reporting or contact tracing, leading to sustained undetected transmission in the community.

When the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group dropped to 0.1 (SH1), infections in that group increased by an average of 5%, with the largest increase reaching 15% in Fig. 3A. Under the same conditions, infections in the low-risk group also rose by up to 46%, indicating that insufficient reporting in the high-transmission group could lead to substantial spillover effects. While maintaining the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group at 0.1, unreported cases increased more sharply under this condition, as shown in Fig. 3B. Specifically, unreported cases in the high-risk group increased by 134%, with maximum increases reaching 171%, while in the low-risk group, the increase reached 63%. These results suggest that low self-reporting in closed transmission settings may increase undetected spread across broader populations.

Conversely, when the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group rose to 0.8 (SH3), which matched the level of the low-risk group, both infections and unreported cases declined notably. Infections in the high-risk group declined by an average of 6%, reaching up to 19% in some simulations. In the low-risk group, the average reduction was 8%, with a maximum of 36%. Under the same conditions, unreported cases dropped by an average of 80% in the high-risk group (up to 89%) and as much as 79% in the low-risk group. These findings are further supported by global sensitivity analysis using Sobol indices (Supplementary Fig. 2). As shown in the sensitivity analysis, infections in the high-risk group are most strongly influenced by the self-reporting rate of the high-risk group.

In Fig. 4A, when the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group dropped to 0.1, infections increased by 11% in U(4,7) and 12% in U(13,16). Figure 4D indicates that in the low-risk group, unreported cases rise by 92% in U(4,7) and 109% in U(13,16), emphasizing the heightened risk of silent transmission when delays extend beyond 4 d. By contrast, when the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group was increased to 0.8, aligning it with the low-risk group (Figs. 4A–D), the impact of contact tracing delays diminished. Under the U(4,7) condition, infections decreased by 5% in both the high- and low-risk groups. Unreported cases also decreased under the same condition, dropping by 77% in the high-risk group and 34% in the low-risk group. However, when contact tracing was delayed to 13–16 d, unreported cases still decreased by 74% in the high-risk group but showed no significant reduction in the low-risk group, suggesting a potential threshold beyond which delayed tracing undermined the benefits of high reporting rates.

When the self-reporting rate in the low-risk group dropped to 0.4, contact tracing delays intensified transmission, particularly in the low-risk group. Under a 4–7 d delay (U(4,7)), infections in the low-risk group increased by 34% on average, with the largest rise reaching 39% under a 13–16 d delay (U(13,16)). Unreported cases in the low-risk group increased substantially, averaging 370% in U(13,16). In the high-risk group, infections remained relatively stable, showing minimal variation across all delay intervals. However, unreported cases in the high-risk group increased, reaching a maximum of 42% in U(13,16).

When the self-reporting rate in the low-risk group increased to 1.0, the impact of contact tracing delays diminished significantly. In U(4,7), infections in the low-risk group increased slightly, averaging 7%. In U(13,16), the total infections in the low-risk group increased by 8%, while unreported cases reached up to 44%. Although all individuals in the low-risk group reported (1.0), unreported cases still rose under delay conditions, with increases of 71% in U(4,7) and 44% in U(13,16). In the high-risk group, infections remained relatively stable, but unreported cases increased slightly, with a maximum rise of 13% in U(13,16).

Discussion

We constructed a stochastic model to investigate how differences in behavioral characteristics and timing of interventions influenced infection dynamics between high- and low-risk groups. The findings highlight the effectiveness of NPIs when aimed at enhancing self-reporting in the high-transmission group and facilitating timely contact tracing. First, we observed a marked increase in cumulative infections and unreported cases when the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group decreased to 0.1. Specifically, the total infections increased by up to 1,954 cases (22%), while unreported cases rose up to 3,556 (164%). These results showed that when self-reporting was substantially reduced in high-transmission populations, the likelihood of widespread, undetected transmission increased significantly. Conversely, when the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group was increased to 0.8 similar to the level in the low-risk group, infections decreased by 22% and unreported cases dropped by 86%. This underscores the critical importance of early self-reporting in high-risk populations to mitigate rapid infection escalation. In contrast, decreasing the self-reporting rate in the low-risk group to 0.4, matching that of the high-risk group, resulted in a similarly significant increase in transmission. The total infections increased by 1,989 cases (22%), with unreported cases rising up to 2,405 (111%). This finding illustrates that even in groups with lower baseline transmission risk, a decline in self-reporting can amplify overall transmission complicating containment efforts.

The impact of delays in contact tracing was also substantial. The simulation results were analyzed in comparison with the baseline scenario (delays of 1 to 4 d). When the self-reporting rate in the high-risk group declined to 0.4 and remained low, even modest delays in contact tracing were associated with substantial increases in both total and unreported infections. Specifically, under a 4–7 d delay, total infections rose by 15%, and unreported cases increased by 85%. Extending the delay to 13–16 d further intensified this trend, with total infections rising by 17% and unreported cases increasing by 100%. These findings highlight the importance of rapid contact tracing in both groups, as sensitivity analysis indicates that contact tracing delays are also the most influential factor for infections in low-risk groups. These outcomes are consistent with previous studies that underscore the importance of prompt contact tracing and high self-reporting rates. For instance, Kretzschmar et al. demonstrated that delays in contact tracing significantly reduce its effectiveness in controlling COVID-19 spread [39]. While Ferretti et al. focused on instantaneous digital contact tracing, our findings demonstrate that manual contact tracing can also achieve substantial transmission reduction when implemented rapidly within 1–4 d [40], From a policy standpoint, our findings support that public health strategies focusing on early self-reporting and prompt contact tracing can mitigate extensive transmission during future outbreaks. Initiatives such as incentivizing self-reporting in high-risk populations or leveraging digital tools to expedite tracing may help contain emerging clusters more effectively. In particular, policy measures such as ensuring anonymity and offering financial incentives may help lower reporting barriers within high-risk groups.

Overall, all these results emphasize the critical interplay between self-reporting compliance and the timeliness of tracing. In densely populated settings, infections may rapidly spread beyond high-risk groups unless self-reporting is enhanced, and tracing delays are minimized. Therefore, implementing targeted interventions to increase self-reporting rates in high-transmission groups and expediting tracing procedures in low-risk populations may represent a strategy for comprehensive outbreak suppression. This study has limitations. First, contact tracing delays were modeled using uniform distributions across fixed intervals. This approach provides a conservative baseline for policy evaluation, while acknowledging that real-world tracing delays may show more complex patterns. Future studies incorporating more realistic delay distribution models, such as log-normal or gamma distributions based on empirical data, would enhance model realism. Second, individual patient-level data, including age information, were not available from KCDC, preventing age-stratified analysis. Future work could explore age-based modeling approaches with access to individual patient data by age group.

Conclusions

This study underscores the combined role of self-reporting and contact tracing as essential mechanisms for outbreak control, demonstrating that strategies focused on group-specific risks can effectively reduce infection spread. The proposed modeling approach identifies heterogeneous risk populations based on reporting behavior and response delays, offering practical recommendations for targeting early interventions in high-risk settings.

From a policy perspective, establishing accessible reporting channels and promoting early reporting in high-risk groups may enhance initial outbreak response. Further, clear guidelines that define reporting criteria and outline tracing procedures can mitigate the risk of silent transmission, particularly in densely populated areas. Simplifying reporting systems and ensuring consistent communication may also support timely detection and limit broader community spread. By coordinating intervention strategies with group-specific transmission dynamics, the study outcome provides a structured framework for refining response efforts during emerging outbreaks, contributing to more targeted and adaptive public health policies.
