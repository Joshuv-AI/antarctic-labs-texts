Clinician perspectives on preclinical radiology education: a qualitative study
Authors: Jia Jun Zen Teng, Vivek Perumal, Ranganath Vallabhajosyula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While there is increasing relevance of diagnostic and interventional radiology in clinical practice, there is insufficient focus on radiology education in preclinical years of the medical course. There is also a prevailing consensus amongst clinicians that the students are not sufficiently prepared for interpretation of radiological images in the clinics. While it is advocated that basic imaging topics should be included in preclinical learning, it is wise to hear what and how much to include from practicing clinicians who directly understand the demand for this knowledge. The objective of this qualitative study is to explore such clinicians’ perspectives to build the foundation for an effective preclinical radiology education. Radiologists and non-radiology clinical consultants were interviewed on a one-to-one basis, focusing on their own preclinical learning experiences and foundational knowledge in radiology, pedagogical approaches, associated challenges, and thereby reflecting on future directions to build strong radiology foundation in preclinical years. The interview transcripts were analyzed with a ground-theory approach. Inductive thematic analysis was conducted linking common themes and subthemes. Inductive thematic analysis of the interview data from nine participants yielded five main themes. Results confirmed that radiology bolsters anatomical visualization suggesting a greater grasp of radiological knowledge is mandatory before transitioning to clinical practice. It was advised to develop interactive pedagogical approaches and self-directed learning to learn, to overcome the time limitation prevailing in the preclinical years. A focus should be placed on developing the fundamental understanding of radiology and its modalities yet ensuring the development of critical clinical reasoning ability. Active engagement via small group teaching and clinical content can foster long-lasting connections that will build a solid foundation for future clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58147-0.

Introduction

With increasing demands for radiological imaging in healthcare even at a primary care and general practice level, it is inevitable to include essential imaging into undergraduate (MBBS) medical education. As diagnostic imaging is now deeply intertwined with clinical decision making, clinicians are expected to be familiar with at least the four main commonly used modalities namely x-rays, CT, MRI and ultrasound1. Yet, despite its importance, it is evident from the literature that insufficient focus is placed on radiology education2,3 in the MBBS curriculum4; and it has been disproportionately geared towards resident-level training5 with insufficient focus on undergraduate education. This lack of a tailor-made effective foundational radiology curriculum for undergraduate level results in limited engagement, motivation and during their clinical rotations5,6 as students are often ill-prepared to read even the most basic of scans5.

Irrespective of the curriculum, it is mandatory for the medical students to undergo clinical rotations where they are expected to review clinically relevant radiological images. Any knowledge on radiological interpretation basically stems from the students’ preclinical education, and if that is limited, the student might not be able to diagnose the patient at the bedside. This makes it essential to understand how much preclinical radiology education is immediately relevant to understand the disease process and patient status during clinical rounds. As the students are directly under supervision of clinical consultants6 during the clinical years of the MBBS course, it is appropriate to hear from them (radiologists and non-radiology clinicians who will be referred to as healthcare professionals in the rest of the report) on how much radiological knowledge a standard MBBS student should hold to complete the clinical rotations without adverse learning outcomes7.

There is existing literature on survey-based studies that captured subjective opinions from a variety of clinicians8, but qualitative approaches with the influence of subjective questions are limited.

The objective of the study is to capture the perceptions of the clinical faculty in healthcare (radiologists and non-radiologists) on the importance of preclinical radiology education, and their insights into content and the pedagogical approach to build a strong preclinical radiology foundation.

We expect that the consensus of healthcare professionals will serve as the foundation for the development of novel, integrated, case-based learning methods which can lay the path of a more responsive curriculum that equips future physicians with the necessary knowledge to offer optimal patient care.

Methodology

Study design

It is an interview based qualitative study.

Participant information

A combination of convenience and purposive sampling method was utilized to recruit participants for this study. Convenience sampling allowed the study team to identify the readily accessible faculty who are actively involved in teaching at the study site. The purposive sampling method was applied to ensure that the recruited participants possessed necessary expertise to provide deep insights into the curriculum. Further, we restricted the eligible participants to consultants and senior consultants who are directly involved in teaching the medical students during the clinical rotations.

Thus, for this study, it is aimed to recruit clinical faculty in healthcare (radiologists and non-radiology clinicians like surgeons and medical practitioners) from diverse specialties for an online one-to-one interview, at their time of convenience to better represent their perceptions from each field in clinical medicine. We have approached adjunct faculty at Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine (LKCMedicine), as they have a better understanding of the current preclinical curriculum and thus would be able to provide greater insight into the necessary improvements of the curriculum. Consultants and senior consultants were chosen as they had the clinical expertise and experience to justify their opinions.

Semi-structured interview questionnaire

Two faculty members (RV and VP) from anatomy with more than 20 years of teaching and research experience have initially curated the semi-structured questionnaire. Further each member of the study team (RV, VP and ZT [year 4 medical student]) read the questionnaire individually, made necessary corrections and aligned them to one of the four objectives. Further, all the members of the team (RV, VP and ZT) have discussed and the final version of the questionnaire aligned with the following objectives was derived (supplementary Table 1).

Objective 1: Clinicians’ perception of the current state and adequacy of radiology taught.Objective 2: Specific radiological topics they consider to be essential.Objective 3: Specific radiological knowledge/practical skills they consider essential to acquire, irrespective of eventual clinical specialty.Objective 4: Perceived challenges/barriers to effectively integrate essential clinically relevant radiology education into the existing preclinical curriculum.

Objective 1: Clinicians’ perception of the current state and adequacy of radiology taught.

Objective 2: Specific radiological topics they consider to be essential.

Objective 3: Specific radiological knowledge/practical skills they consider essential to acquire, irrespective of eventual clinical specialty.

Objective 4: Perceived challenges/barriers to effectively integrate essential clinically relevant radiology education into the existing preclinical curriculum.

Interview process

Volunteered participants were invited to participate for a one-to-one interview at their time of convenience. These interviews were conducted by one member of the study team (ZT), occasionally accompanied by another author RV. The interviews were conducted online via Zoom and each interview lasted for about an hour. Informed consent and demographic data were obtained from each of the participants before the commencement of the interview.

Data collection and analysis

Zoom audio recordings of the participant interviews were captured along with the cloud recording transcription that has generated the verbatim file. Inductive thematic analysis was done to identify the patterns of themes within each data set in a six-step process9. The thematic analysis was conducted following a grounded theory approach10.

Steps of inductive thematic analysis

Step 1: familiarization

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim via Zoom Audio Transcription for Cloud Recordings. These transcripts were reviewed independently by two authors (ZT, RV). During the interview itself, the author (ZT) also made notes on certain important recurring key points brought up by the participants.

Phase 2: Generating initial codes

After familiarization with the data, a list of codes was generated for each interview manually. Keywords (codes) were then assigned to different segments of the interview to better reflect context and content.

Phase 3: Identifying the sub themes

Notes taken for each of the transcripts were arranged in a numerical order of the interview questions. They were then placed side by side and compared for recurring sub-themes that could connect various key ideas. Patterns and themes were noted down to encapsulate the relationship between the codes.

Phase 4: Reviewing themes

Subthemes supported by larger number of participants were selected to aid in clear comprehension and theme identification.

Phase 5: Identifying the themes

These subthemes were then refined and connected into the main themes that can be observed in the result section below. The authors ensured that the codes and subthemes derived were evaluated to align with the themes.

Phase 6: Producing the report

Relevant quotes supporting each theme were extracted from the transcript and analyzed together with the themes. The chosen themes aimed to represent study findings and formulated into arguments for improving preclinical radiological education.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki was obtained from Nanyang Technological University – Institute Review Board (NTU-IRB) with reference number IRB-2025-644.

Results

Participant information

This study was under a six-week scholarly project in year 4 of the LKCMedicine curriculum. Due to the timely nature of the project, there were only 9 participants who indicated their interest in volunteering for the study; thus, data was captured from only the volunteered participants. However, the participants are consultants and senior consultants with rich experience in clinical and medical education. Their expertise insights would be highly valuable to justify the objective of the study. The participant information is summarized in Table 1. There were nine participants (2 female, 7 male) whose clinical experience ranged from 12 to 37 years (mean: 22.6 years). Informed consent was obtained from all the participants before the commencement of the interview. All were consultants in their respective fields, including diverse surgical specialties (n = 7), and radiologists (n = 2). Following the data analysis, the patterns were largely repeated after the ninth participant indicating the sufficiency of the information, as a provision to provide evidence in support of the themes.

Thematic analysis

The inductive thematic analysis of the interview data yielded 5 main themes such as (1) participant’s learning experience, (2) foundational radiology knowledge, (3) fundamentals of radiology and its role in anatomy teaching, (4) pedagogical approaches, and (5) time and its impact on radiology education.

Participants’ learning experience.

Participants’ learning experience.

Participants’ learning experience.

Majority of the participants (7/9) indicated that there was minimal to no exposure to radiology education during their own preclinical years, where learning was fragmented, unstructured, and ad-hoc. Their first exposure to radiology was observed to be during Year 3 (during the transition to clinical years); their radiological knowledge largely stemmed from clinical case discussions and self-directed learning that was usually earned on the job.

“Very minimal exposure to radiology, specifically like radiology related to anatomy or radiology related to any applied anatomy or clinical anatomy. So that was a bit of a gap” [P1].

“Very minimal exposure to radiology, specifically like radiology related to anatomy or radiology related to any applied anatomy or clinical anatomy. So that was a bit of a gap” [P1].

2.Foundational radiology knowledge.

2.Foundational radiology knowledge.

Foundational radiology knowledge.

Almost all participants (8/9) opined that preclinical students require “foundational radiology knowledge” and it would be ideal if they were able to translate their basic anatomy/ radiology knowledge to identify the life-threating conditions and common pathologies.

“I expect the ability to look at common pathologies. Gallbladder stones… pancreatic cancers… chest X-ray, pneumonia… at a gross level” [P6].

“I expect the ability to look at common pathologies. Gallbladder stones… pancreatic cancers… chest X-ray, pneumonia… at a gross level” [P6].

All the participants (9/9) agreed that foundational knowledge helps the students understand what doctors are teaching during clinical years. Furthermore, the participants felt that their job was getting “a bit easier” as the students can make sense of what they learnt.

“I think during clinical years, our job is a bit easier, because they have some foundational knowledge of what the CT scan looks like, what the MRI looks like.” [P5].

“I think during clinical years, our job is a bit easier, because they have some foundational knowledge of what the CT scan looks like, what the MRI looks like.” [P5].

3.Fundamentals of radiology and its role in anatomy teaching.

3.Fundamentals of radiology and its role in anatomy teaching.

Fundamentals of radiology and its role in anatomy teaching.

Most participants (8/9) believe that the basic principles and the common radiological modalities (X-Ray, Ultrasound, Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)) should be taught with comprehensive overview on basic physics and its relevance in identification of structures. According to the participants, the fundamentals were of the greatest importance, and concepts that were far too advanced were to be omitted. Likewise, lessons should be given on basic terminology, such as anatomical planes, opacity and density, to further reduce the learning gap for students while bolstering anatomical visualization.

“I think the foundation of what different basic radiological investigations that are available and the principles behind how they work is very important.” [P1].“Learning anatomy will be much easier if imaging comes into the anatomy curriculum itself.” [P5].

“I think the foundation of what different basic radiological investigations that are available and the principles behind how they work is very important.” [P1].

“Learning anatomy will be much easier if imaging comes into the anatomy curriculum itself.” [P5].

4.Pedagogical approaches.

4.Pedagogical approaches.

Pedagogical approaches.

All the participants (9/9) opined that case-based teaching and learning in a small group setting is an ideal pedagogical approach to teaching radiology.

“The small group is more effective, because we can target it. For big groups, we don’t know who is listening and who is not listening.” [P3].

“The small group is more effective, because we can target it. For big groups, we don’t know who is listening and who is not listening.” [P3].

While it is universal agreement that anatomy is also a useful tool for radiology education, the participants felt that tools for visualization such as 3D imaging software will help students make sense of the 2D and 3D radiological images, especially for visual learners. However, according to participant perceptive, cadaveric dissections are useful to learn surgical anatomy, but not radiology.

“I think using 3D models, saw-bone models can help to appreciate the anatomy and what it looks like on different views.” [P4].

“I think using 3D models, saw-bone models can help to appreciate the anatomy and what it looks like on different views.” [P4].

The participants also suggested that a library of compiled radiological scans should be made available to students. There should be a focus on normal scans and normal variation of human anatomy. Pathology of important conditions should also be available. Additional pathological scans should also be available as additional reading but should be non-examinable.

“You just need a library of clinically relevant radiological content… so students can put to practice what they’ve learned.” [P7].

“You just need a library of clinically relevant radiological content… so students can put to practice what they’ve learned.” [P7].

5.Time and its impact on radiology education.

5.Time and its impact on radiology education.

Time and its impact on radiology education.

It is identified that time was lauded as the most essential resource in preclinical education. All the participants (9/9) acknowledged that the power-packed preclinical curriculum often make students “overwhelmed”. However, as a multitude of subspecialties are covered, each clinician may have differing viewpoints on what should be taught in the preclinical curriculum within the given time.

“There’s only so much time in preclinical years, right? In an ideal world, they would know everything there is to know, right? But it’s not possible.” [P2].

“There’s only so much time in preclinical years, right? In an ideal world, they would know everything there is to know, right? But it’s not possible.” [P2].

The participants (8/9) indicated that in curricular reformation, redundancies, such as repeated information should be excluded, as it is necessary to use the curriculum wisely for better time management.

“I think it would be good to re-look at the curriculum to see whether there’s any redundancy that can be cut down so that more can be introduced without compromising, overstretching or overtaxing the medical students’ time, and at the same time, the faculty’s time” [P1].

“I think it would be good to re-look at the curriculum to see whether there’s any redundancy that can be cut down so that more can be introduced without compromising, overstretching or overtaxing the medical students’ time, and at the same time, the faculty’s time” [P1].

One of the important concerns raised by the participants was the need for content to be “bread-and-butter” and not too advanced to avoid overwhelming students.

“If you get a radiologist, or someone who’s a specialist in that area, they may put stuff which is a little bit too highbrow. It needs to be very much bread-and-butter, basic stuff.” [P9].

“If you get a radiologist, or someone who’s a specialist in that area, they may put stuff which is a little bit too highbrow. It needs to be very much bread-and-butter, basic stuff.” [P9].

According to the participants, it is challenging to identify the depth of radiological knowledge to be taught in pre-clinical years since not all the students will pursue a career in radiology, thus making it difficult to propose and incorporate the required content in the curriculum. Thus, the focus should be on foundational knowledge to support clinical diagnosis and clinical reasoning in the curriculum. The participants also opined that excess details should be excluded, that might overwhelm the students and if it does not add value to the context.

“Another problem besides time is that we are not sure which specialty students will be going into. If you teach too much in the preclinical stage, then it might not be worth time from somewhere else” [P8].

“Another problem besides time is that we are not sure which specialty students will be going into. If you teach too much in the preclinical stage, then it might not be worth time from somewhere else” [P8].

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to capture the perceptions of clinicians on the need for and improvement of radiological anatomy in a preclinical undergraduate medical curriculum. An inductive thematic analysis of their opinions revealed diverse insights into radiology’s current state, potential modifications and a suggestion about the simultaneous reduction of redundant topics to balance the learning load and better shape the competence of medical students.

Medical education has advanced considerably in the past decades from traditional core anatomy and dissections to introduction of clinical topics (pathology, clinical skills and radiology) in the preclinical years11. In the current integrated curricular model that is successfully adapted worldwide, clinical concepts are well integrated into basic sciences right from year 112. However, while curricular reformations usually indicate what concepts are to be included or removed, implementation of those changes often remain arbitrary. For example, there is a strong recommendation13 to include basic radiology in preclinical anatomy teaching, but it is very vague on what is considered basic, essential, and relevant. This is especially true among medical schools in different geographical locations across the world. That is the reason why despite effective curriculum designs, their efficiency is still debated.

Evolution of radiology learning experience

Radiology, if at all taught in detail, was traditionally delivered in the clinical years of medical school. That knowledge was limited to interpreting the images relevant to the case discussed, rather than following a systematic way like including the physics behind the images, including artefacts and what modality to choose for what condition etc. Consultants who teach clinical year students also agree with this, recalling their minimalistic preclinical exposure to the otherwise fragmented radiological anatomy learning and application in clinical practice6,14.

Today, undergraduate medical students seem to appreciate and value their anatomy learning integrated with radiology10. This can improve the future clinician’s ability to select the appropriate radiological modality, avoiding overutilization of radiology, leading to an overall improvement in rationalization of radiological resources12,21. However, our participants also suggest not overwhelming students with details and instead make the learning case-based and self-directed11, limiting the details to help students appreciate what is not normal.

Radiology or radiological anatomy? How much radiology is enough?

There is also a debate on what should be taught in radiological anatomy sessions. There is often confusion on what is relevant for undergraduate medical students the radiologically relevant anatomy (such as cross-sectional anatomy, 3-dimensional spatial relationships etc.), or radiology in terms of which modalities to choose for specific conditions and the physics behind them15. Some of our participants suggested that dissection can only improve surgical anatomy knowledge and not radiology. This might be a different thought as radiologically relevant anatomy (e.g. cross-sections and 3D spatial architecture) can be studied in dissections.

However, the technical aspect of radiology and its role in diagnosis need a different approach for proper education, and such inclusions should overwhelm pre-clinical education16,17. It would be overwhelming to teach pathological and even the full list of normal anatomical details, so a database of “need-to-know” and “good-to-know” radiographs could be included in the teaching and made available to the students for reference18,. When specialists in the field are involved in teaching, it is difficult for them to cap the details to the basic and minimal level. Therefore, clear criteria must be formulated to keep the learning content focused on preclinical levels relevant to general practice19. This is also important as not all students enter surgical or radiological specialties in the future. Probably more emphasis is needed on affordable and bedside imaging like x-rays and ultrasound because unlike CT and MRI, the undergraduate students should be able to read these images without a detailed radiologist report. Students should also focus on developing critical clinical reasoning skills revolving around non-radiological methodologies, because radiology is not the main mode of diagnosis20,21. While radiology is important, it is recognized that an equal focus must be placed on developing clinical reasoning skills22,23 for effective diagnosis to achieve successful outcomes24.

Pedagogical approach

“Case-based learning” was viewed by the participants as the most effective form of learning for radiology25. Small group discussions are shown to be beneficial to larger cohorts when it comes to classroom teaching26, while self-paced, self-directed learnings benefit students who learn in non-competitive modes20. The knowledge gap required for self-study should be lessened by crafting a specialized introductory lesson to radiology. The participants have commented that a lesson on specific radiological terminology, such as density and opacity, will be useful for students. In addition to supplemental information on the basic principles of different modalities, this will lay the groundwork for students to start off on the right foot.

Our clinicians suggested creation of a database that could be made available to student learning. Where the medical school’s preclinical teaching is independent of clinical rotations, access to detailed database of radiological material would be a limitation. In such situations, online learning material like open-source databases would be handy. However, unsupervised dependence on the widely accessible online radiology resources also has a downside, as any misinformation makes it difficult22 for students without the adequate foundations to discern their utility in their own country’s context.

There lays a dichotomy between anatomy and radiology within the preclinical curriculum. Radiology can serve as a form of visualization to help students refine their anatomical understanding27. In contrast, anatomy knowledge can also be used to teach clinical radiology. The participants have expressed that the resolution of this dichotomy lies in the direction of the curriculum. The radiologists, as well as some of the other clinicians, fervently believe that radiology should be used to teach students how to better visualize spatial relationships between different organs and anatomical structures. Hence, radiology allows the application of anatomy to be better appreciated, which strengthens the students’ foundation for clinicals28. We suggest a right balance of integration between radiological concepts and basic sciences. For example, anatomy could be coupled with cross-sectional images and 3D spatial relationships related to radiology. In addition, radiological tissue properties (e.g. appearance of fluid contract between T1 or T2 MRI, or effusion is white in x-rays, etc. effusion is white, etc.) could be discussed in pathology, and finally how the related organs are affected (compressed in space occupying lesions) could be included in the medicine and surgery subjects, thereby spreading the content evenly within pre-and clinical years.

Also, three-dimensional visualization tools and applications are regarded as useful in radiology learning29, especially in visual learners30. The sub-theme of “interactiveness” can be derived due to the participants’ beliefs that students should be actively engaging with content. As radiology is inherently self-directed, the onus is on students to refine their knowledge during their own time31 Tools that allow for greater interactivity such as anatomy visualization tools or short interactive radiology videos may play a greater role today29. With the advent of social media, the attention span of an average person has decreased drastically, fractionating study time. These interactive approaches32 will allow students to actively engage with radiological learning material, generating better learning outcomes.

Time management in preclinical education

Medical knowledge is suggested to double every 72 days33. With this ever-expanding information influx, it is difficult to integrate all evolving concepts into medical curriculum or update it every year. Attempting to include all clinical concepts into preclinical learning will only overwhelm the students. Thus, it is necessary to cut down outdated concepts for new inclusions like radiology. Similarly, only necessary radiological knowledge for “bread-and-butter” conditions34 should be prioritized in the preclinical curriculum and the specifics of radiology for each subspecialty should be left to the clinical stage. Furthermore, formative and/or summative assessment35,36 of radiological knowledge in preclinical years ensures that students are equipped with the radiological knowledge that clinicians expect them to have as new clinical students; assessment ensures that students will place a focus on radiology.

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides deep insights into the preclinical radiology curriculum, there were few identifiable limitations worth mentioning. The study was single centric focused in a specific institutional context and its aligned curriculum and pedagogy. Thus, the results may not be generalizable and may not capture diverse pedagogical approaches. While it is a relevant context for pre-clinical education for any curriculum and pedagogical approaches, multicentric designs involving wider demographics including students, junior doctors, and specialists would be useful to identify the patterns of the findings across different educational frameworks.

Despite using robust sampling methods like convenience and purposive sampling37,38 our study was limited by the sample size. While consultants from different specialties were invited for the study, the data was not stratified according to their clinical specialty due to smaller sample size. In future studies, capturing specialty specific perceptions would be useful to determine the discipline specific patterns in radiology curriculum.

Our participant responses also opened new avenues for discussions, in the form of teaching, would benefit better from smaller groups of the cohort, where individualized attention could be provided, and certain overwhelming topics can be included, but not mandated (non-examinable). Further discussion of these statements is noteworthy but is beyond the scope of the current work.

Conclusion

This study has provided valuable perspectives of experienced clinicians on how and how much of the radiology could be integrated into the pre-clinical curriculum. The consensus is to introduce the radiology concepts early in the curriculum for a better and smoother transition from pre-clinical to clinical years, benefiting both learners and supervising clinicians. It is recommended to incorporate synthesized, interactive approaches and case-based discussions for better engagement, thereby promoting active participation and meaningful learning to forge long-lasting connections, while also ensuring that students are not overwhelmed with excessive information.
