Identification of serum metabolites associated with polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) exposure in papillary thyroid carcinoma: a case–control study
Authors: Jiayi Song, Yuan Liu, Jinling Peng, Yousheng Jiang, Xiaoshi Lin, Jianqing Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs the most common endocrine cancer, thyroid cancer (TC) has sharply increased globally over the past three decades. The growing incidence of TC might be counted by genetics, radiation, iodine, autoimmune disease, and exposure to environmental endocrine–disrupting chemicals (EDCs). Polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs), being typical EDCs, have been widely utilized in plastics, electronics, furniture, and textiles as flame retardants since the 1980s, and research has indicated a significant correlation between their exposure and the risk of TC. Even so, PBDEs exposure impact on the metabolic signature for TC remains unexplored. In this study, eight congeners of PBDEs were determined in serum from 111 patents with papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) and 111 healthy participants based on case–control epidemiology using gas chromatography–atmospheric pressure chemical ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (GC-APCI–MS/MS). Based on the tertile distribution of total PBDEs concentrations in 59 participants, metabolomics analysis was further performed by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled to hybrid quadrupole–Orbitrap MS. In the partial correlation analysis, the 29 identified metabolites were correlated with PBDEs exposure (P < 0.05). In addition, PBDEs disrupted the metabolism of glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, taurine, and hypotaurine, indicating that neurotransmitters, oxidative stress, and inflammation are the vulnerable pathways affected in PTC. Furthermore, (±)-octopamine and 5-hydroxyindole, both of which modulate the actions of neurotransmitters, emerged as potential disturbed metabolite markers for TC following exposure to PBDEs. This study analyzed the impact of PBDEs on PTC in terms of the metabolic changes and further explored possible biomarkers, which helped us have a deep understanding of the possible mechanism of the effects of PBDEs on TC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10653-024-02158-1.Keywords: PBDEs, Thyroid carcinoma, Metabolomics, Biomarker

Introduction

As persistent, bioaccumulative, endocrine-disrupting chemicals with a long half-life, PBDEs are widely used in electronic products, high-impact plastics, foam, and textile manufacturing. Although the production of PBDEs ceased at the end of 2004, people can still be exposed to PBDEs through usage of manufactured products, food intake, and indoor dust inhalation (Hou et al., 2021). Consequently, human exposure to PBDEs has aroused increasing concern regarding their deleterious effects on human health such as neurotoxicity, thyrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity (Chen & Ma, 2021; Sheikh & Beg, 2020). Some studies on the relationship between PBDEs exposure and risk of TC have been conducted in recent years. A nested case–control study (consisting of 70 cases and 70 controls) conducted in the United States showed that BDE-209 in house dust statistically increased the risk of PTC (Hoffman et al., 2017). Another nested case–control study, which recruited 186 patients with thyroid disease and 186 healthy participants, found that PBDEs in serum were significantly and positively associated with the risk of thyroid disease (Han et al., 2019).

As the most prevalent endocrine cancer, there were 586,202 new cases of thyroid cancer (TC) in 2020 worldwide (Sung et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2022). This is attributed to an increased incidence of papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), which presents a high health and disease burden to human society, with medical costs estimated to be approximately $3.5 billion per year by 2030 in the United States (Maniakas et al., 2018). Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy is the current effective preoperative screening method used for TC, although approximately 15–30% of thyroid nodules cannot be cytologically differentiated as malignant or benign (Baloch et al., 2008; Tee et al., 2007). Radiation exposure, iodine intake, autoimmune disease, and endocrine disruptors might be responsible for the development of TC (Fiore et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2020). However, the critical driving factors and molecular determinants underlying TC development and progression are not well understood.

As metabolic change is one of the important markers of tumors, metabolomics has been widely applied to the study of TC in the past few years (Abooshahab et al., 2022). A recent study of two dependent cohorts of 651 subjects with PTC, benign thyroid nodule (BTN), and healthy controls found that a metabolic biomarker panel was able to discriminate PTC from BTN and had greater accuracy compared with ultrasound (Yu et al., 2022). In addition, a serum metabolomics study of PTC showed that aspartic acid metabolism, glutamic acid metabolism, the urea cycle, and the tricarboxylic acid cycle are involved in the mechanism of PTC (Du et al., 2021). Similarly, changes in amino acid metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism after BDE-47 exposure have been shown in SK-N-SH human neuroblastoma cells (Tang et al., 2019). However, the physiological effects and mechanisms of carcinogenicity in TC related to PBDEs exposure remain largely unknown.

In this study, PBDEs levels in serum from 111 PTC cases and 111 healthy participants were measured by gas chromatography–atmospheric pressure chemical ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (GC-APCI-MS/MS). In addition, the differentiated, characterized, disturbed signaling pathways and sensitive biomarkers for PTC based on the tertile distribution of serum PBDEs concentration were identified in 29 PTC cases and 30 healthy participants. The findings of this study provide valuable scientific evidence on the physiopathology and potential mechanisms of PTC related to exogenous environmental pollutant exposure, and may facilitate the application of sensitive biomarkers for the early diagnosis of TC in the future.

Methods

Study population and design

This was a case–control study matched by gender and age (± 2 years) that included 111 newly diagnosed PTC cases and 111 healthy participants who were recruited from eight community health centers between September 2018 and 2019 in Shenzhen, China. PTC was diagnosed referencing the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, whereas the controls were physical examination participants who were examined during the same period as the patients and tested for five thyroid hormone indicators, two antibodies, and B-ultrasound, for those with normal thyroid function and ultrasound were defined as the control group. In addition, participants in both the case and control groups were eligible if they met the following criteria: 18–75 years of age, and living in Shenzhen for over 6 months. The exclusion criteria for both the case and control groups were those who received radiation or chemotherapy; suffered from serious heart, liver, or kidney disease; had a history of thyroid disease; were pregnant and lactating; or had recently taken iodine or other antithyroid drugs, contraceptives, or estrogen. The Human Ethical Committee of Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention inspected, reviewed, and approved the study protocol (No. [2019]012A). Each of the participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the purpose and potential risks of the study. Information on demographic characteristics, life and behavioral habits, and disease history was obtained with face-to-face interviews. Non-anticoagulant fasting 12 h peripheral blood and urine were collected from the control and case groups. Serum and urine were obtained and divided into Eppendorf (EP) tubes, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

PBDEs quantitation and urine iodine measurement

Three hundred microliters of serum was balanced overnight at 4 °C and spiked with eight 13C-labeled PBDEs internal standards including 10 μL of 1 ng 13C12-BDE-209; and 0.5 ng each of 13C12-BDE-28, 13C12-BDE-47, 13C12-BDE-99, 13C12-BDE-100, 13C12-BDE-153, 13C12-BDE-154, and 13C12-BDE-183. After treatment with a mixture of formic acid and ultra-pure water, the serum was processed with activated HLB columns (60 mg/3 mL; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and washed with 1 mL ultra-pure water. Thereafter, the PBDEs analyte was eluted with 7 mL n-hexane:dichloromethane (V:V = 1:1) and concentrated to near dryness under nitrogen. Then, it was loaded onto a custom-packed acid silica column and eluted with n-hexane, after which the eluate was collected and dried with nitrogen. The residue was further reconstituted in 20 μL of 13C-labeled injection standard before GC-APCI-MS/MS analysis. Then 1 μL extract was injected into the Agilent DB-5HT (15 m × 0.25 mm × 0.10 μm) gas chromatography system (Palo Alto, CA, USA) coupled with an atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) source. MS analysis conditions were as follows: the ion source was set in APCI mode; a constant flow mode (4 mL/min) was adopted with helium as a carrier gas; the APCI corona discharge pin was operated in a constant current mode at 1.6 μA; multiple reaction monitoring mode was used to monitor the area of the target compound; and the monitoring time was 9–46 ms.

The total concentration of eight PBDEs congeners (including BDE-28, BDE-47, BDE-99, BDE-100, BDE-153, BDE-154, BDE-183 and BDE-209) were corrected by total lipid in serum, which was calculated by triglycerides and total cholesterol in serum as follows: total lipid (g/L) = 1.12 × total cholesterol + 1.33 × triglycerides + 1.48 (Ma et al., 2011). Total serum levels of triglycerides and total cholesterol were analyzed with a fully automatic chemiluminescence instrument (Snibe Co., Ltd, Shenzhen, China). In order to further investigate PBDEs-related metabolome alterations, the exposure level was grouped into tri-sectional quantiles from the lowest to the highest ∑PBDEs concentration. According to the tri-sectional quantile distribution, samples in the frist tertile were selected as the high- PBDEs exposure group, samples in the second tertile were defined as medium-PBDEs exposure group and samples in the lowest tri-sectional quartile were chosen as the low-PBDEs exposure group for the following metabolomics study.

The concentration of urine iodine was measured by inductively coupled plasma MS (Agilent 7700×; Agilent Technologies), which was detailed in our previous study (Liu et al., 2021). According to World Health Organization standards, iodine nutrition levels were divided into deficiency (< 100 μg/L), adequate (100–200 μg/L), excess (200–300 μg/L), and overdose (≥ 300 μg/L).

Metabolic profiling analysis

After controlling for sex, age, body mass index (BMI) index, smoking status, alcohol use, education level, and iodine nutrition, the metabolic profile in the serum of 29 PTC cases and 30 healthy participants was further measured. To prepare samples for metabolic profiling, 100 μL serum and 400 μL cold methanol (including internal standard) were added to 1.5 mL EP tubes on ice (Table S1). The mixture was scrolled and oscillated for 1 min and placed at room temperature for 10 min. After centrifuging at 14,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was freeze-dried and redissolved with 100 μL 20% methanol/water for positive and negative ion mode analysis; the blank sample was also redissolved in 20% methanol/water. By using the same sample processing and data collection method as the experimental samples, the quality control (QC) samples made by pooling samples into two groups were detected. After the instrument was stabilized, one blank sample and two QC samples were analyzed. Thereafter, both the QC and blank samples were analyzed at the beginning and end of each batch, and one QC sample was collected with every 10 actual samples.

Metabolic profiling analysis of the serum samples was performed using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with the Q Exactive Focus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 column (1.7 μm particles, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters) and the ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm particles, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters). The column temperature was set at 50 °C and the sample injection volume was 5 μL; 0.1% formic acid/water and 0.1% formic acid/acetonitrile were used as mobile phase A and B, respectively. The gradient elution program was applied as 5% (B) held for 0–1 min, 1.1–11 min from 5% (B) to 100% (B), 11.1–13 min at 100% (B), and 13.1–15 min back to 2% (B). Regarding the MS parameters, a full scan mode ranging from 70 to 1050 (m/z) with a resolution of 70,000 and data-dependent acquisition mode resolution of 17,500 were applied. The TopN of 5, normalized collision energy (NCE)/stepped NCE of 20, 40 and spray voltage of 3.8 kV for positive ionization mode and TopN of 8, NCE/stepped NCE of 25, 35, 45 and spray voltage of −3.0 kV for negative ionization mode were set. The flow rates of sheath gas and aux gas were 35 Arb and 8 Arb, respectively. Capillary and aux gas heater temperatures were set at 320 °C and 350 °C.

Serum metabolomics and differentiated sensitive biomarker identification for PTC

Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to identify the metabolic differences among the three PBDEs exposure groups, as well as between the PTC and control groups. The differential metabolites were selected based on the following criteria: (1) variable importance (VIP) in projection scores in OPLS-DA models > 1; (2) fold change (FC) > 1.2 or < 0.8; and (3) P < 0.05 in the Mann–Whitney U test between any two of the three PBDEs exposure groups. The potential biomarkers were structurally identified by matching MS/MS fragments with databases including the METLIN (http://metlin.scripps.edu/) and Human Metabolome Database (http://www.hmdb.ca/). Likewise, the genes related to the differentially expressed metabolites were identified using MetaboAnalyst according to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database (www. genome.jp/kegg/).

Statistical analyses

SPSS Statistics 23.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R 3.6.3 (Lucent Technologies, Reston, VA, USA) were applied for all statistical analyses. Continuous variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations or the median and interquartile range. The Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparisons between the cases and controls. The chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were used to estimate the significant differences in categorical variables as counts and percentages (%). One-way analysis of variance, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to determine differences among two or three groups. The chi-squared tests and Fisher’s exact test were used to estimate the significant differences in categorical variables as counts and percentages (%). As candidates for further identification, the features with at least one significant difference in the comparison of different groups (high vs. medium PBDEs, medium vs. low PBDEs, high vs. low PBDEs) were considered. After adjusting for sex, age, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, and iodine nutrition, partial correlation analysis was applied to investigate the relationship between serum metabolites and PBDEs levels. Stepwise binary logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to evaluate potential diagnostic biomarkers, and the area under the curve (AUC) was applied to calculate the predictive ability of significant metabolites in TC. Graphs for descriptive analysis were depicted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 statistical software (La Jolla, CA, USA). P < 0.05 was statistically significant (two-sided).

Results

Demographic characteristics and body burden of PBDEs of the participants

A total of 222 people was included in this study, including 111 cases each in the case group and control group, as shown in Table 1. The mean age of the study participants was 40.96 ± 9.96 years. There were 40 males and 71 females in each group, accounting for about 36.0% and 64.0% of the study population, respectively, and the ratio of men to women was about 1:2. In addition, BMI was lower in the case group than in the control group, and the proportion of drinkers was higher in the case group than in the control group (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between the case and control populations in terms of education level and smoking status (P > 0.05). The concentrations of serum PBDEs in this study population are shown in Table 2. The detection rates of BDE-47, BDE-153, BDE-183, and BDE-209 were 90.5%, 99.5%, 77.0%, and 66.2%, respectively; and the detection rates of BDE-28, BDE-99, BDE-100, and BDE-154 were all lower than 7%. The median concentrations of BDE-47, BDE-153, BDE-183, BDE-209, and ∑PBDEs in the total population of the study were 0.6, 2.43, 0.47, 1.80, and 9.74 ng/g lipid, respectively. Serum levels of BDE-153, BDE-183, and BDE-209 were higher in the case group than the control group, whereas BDE-47 levels were lower in the case group than the control group (P < 0.05).

Demographic characteristics of the population based on different PBDEs levels

Because all subjects were exposed to a mixture of PBDEs, ∑PBDEs were used to represent the overall exposure level of PBDEs. Furthermore, 29 PTC cases and 30 healthy participants were selected to explore the effect of PBDEs on the serum metabolic profile in PTC, and the demographic characteristics are shown in Table S2. Based on the tri-sectional quantiles of serum ∑PBDEs concentration, 40 participants were distributed in the low- and medium-PBDEs exposure groups, and 19 participants were distributed in the high-PBDEs exposure group (Table 3). The first tertile concentration of serum ∑PBDEs was 3.23 ng/g lipid in the range of 1.20–5.45 ng/g lipid, the second tertile concentration was 9.80 ng/g lipid in the range of 5.69–14.00 ng/g lipid, and the third tertile concentration was 24.48 ng/g lipid in the range of 14.75–111.11 ng/g lipid. The difference in concentration of ∑PBDEs among the three groups was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Moreover, the mean age of the participants was 39.05 ± 8.48, 42.50 ± 6.70, and 40.89 ± 8.33 years, with 70%, 40%, and 52.6% in females in the low-, medium-, and high-PBDEs exposure groups, respectively. There was no significant difference in sex, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, and education level among low-, medium-, and high-PBDEs exposure groups (P > 0.05). Regarding the iodine level among the three groups, 30%, 40%, and 47.4% were at the iodine adequate (100–200 μg/L) level, and 20%, 20%, and 15.8% were at the iodine excess (200–300 μg/L) level. The proportion at overdose (≥ 300 μg/L) was only 5% in the medium-PBDEs exposure group, and no significant differences were found in iodine nutrition among the low-, medium-, and high-PBDEs exposure groups (P > 0.05).

Serum metabolomics and potential biomarkers related to PBDEs exposure

The potential changes in serum metabolites associated with PBDEs exposure were further investigated with nontargeted serum metabolism analysis in 29 PTC cases and 30 healthy participants. Data analyses of the low-, medium-, and high-PBDEs exposure levels with OPLS-DA models and score scatter plots showed that the samples were clearly separated in the positive ionization modes, showing that serum metabolic disturbances were significantly different in the three groups (Fig. S1). In further comparisons, samples based on the low- versus medium-PBDEs exposure group, low- versus high-PBDEs exposure group, and medium- versus high-PBDEs exposure group were also significantly different. The numbers of differential metabolites among the three groups are presented in a heat map (Figs. S1–S2); they were further screened based on the aforementioned criteria noted in the Methods. A total of 43 differentiated metabolites were initially identified based on the three different levels of PBDEs exposure, and their association with the serum level of PBDEs was investigated by partial correlation analysis after adjusting for sex, age, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, and iodine nutrition (Table S3). Eventually, 29 of the 43 metabolites were found to be significantly related to serum PBDEs levels and thus were considered to be potential metabolic biomarkers (Fig. 1). The comparisons on the 29 metabolites related to the three different PBDEs exposure levels are shown in the box charts (Fig. S3). Finally, KEGG analysis showed that nine pathways were enriched in response to PBDEs exposure. The results showed that the potential biomarkers upon high PBDEs exposure were highly associated with the perturbations of glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, propanoate metabolism, galactose metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Fig. 2).

Screening for diagnostic markers in PTC upon different PBDEs exposure

To explore the effects of PBDEs on downstream metabolic changes in the occurrence and progression of TC, the distribution of 29 identified metabolites associated with PBDEs exposure levels was further investigated in the case and control groups. The results of the Mann–Whitney U tests showed statistically significant differences in 20 of 29 PBDEs-related metabolites between the cases and controls (Fig. 3). Aminomalonate, D-methionine, 5-hydroxyindole, (±)-octopamine, TG (58:1), Cer-BDS(d55:5), SM(d43:4), stigmastentriol, and TG (16:0/18:0/16:0) were significantly reduced in the case group compared to the control group (P < 0.05). Compared with the control group, 2-Hydroxybutanoic acid, D-mannose, LysoPC (16:0), LysoPC(16:1), LysoPC(18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)), LysoPC(22:5), LysoPE(20:4), PC(16:1e), phaseolic acid, taurocholate, and trans-Vaccenic acid were significantly increased in the case group (P < 0.05). The diagnostic potential of 20 differentially metabolite biomarkers for TC was further investigated by stepwise binary logistic regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, and BMI (Table S4). The ( ±)-octopamine was significantly associated with PTC in model 1 (P < 0.05), and both 5-hydroxyindole and (±)-octopamine were correlated with an increased risk of PTC in model 2 (P < 0.05). Furthermore, ROC analyses with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of (±)-octopamine and 5-hydroxyindole were performed to quantify their diagnostic performance for PTC (Fig. 4). The AUC of (±)-octopamine and 5-hydroxyindole was 0.953 and 0.703, respectively, which indicates their potential to serve as an effective diagnostic biomarker for PTC upon high PBDEs exposure.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nontargeted metabolomics study on the relationship between PBDEs exposure and TC in a case–control study. The metabolites were identified to be associated with PBDEs exposure, which are involved in the metabolism of glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, taurine and hypotaurine, fructose and mannose, propanoate, galactose, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar, tryptophan, and primary bile acid. Moreover, we found that (±)-octopamine and 5-hydroxyoxindole can be used as potential independent diagnostic biomarkers for PTC upon different PBDEs exposure levels in the future.

In this study, the median concentration of PBDEs was 9.74 ng/g lipid in the total population of recruited participants, suggesting that the study population was exposed to PBDEs in the local area. Serum PBDEs concentrations in the present study were notably lower than those in residents from e-waste recycling sites and flame retardant production areas in China (Shantou, ΣPBDEs median: 73.91 ng/g lipid and Shandong ΣPBDEs median: 64.5 ng/g lipid weight), but were relatively higher than those in residents living in metropolis in China (Hong Kong, ΣPBDEs GM: 4.45 ng/g lipid weight and Shanghai, ΣPBDEs mean: 2.162 ng/g lipid weight), respectively (Ho et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2021). A study from the United States Department of Defense cohort showed that serum levels of BDE-28 were positively correlated with the risk of PTC (consisted of 742 cases and 742 controls) between 2000 and 2013 (Huang et al., 2020a, 2020b). Zhang et al. (2021) reported that a high body burden of PBDEs increased the risk of TC in a case–control study from China, indicating that the higher PBDEs body burden of the participants in this study increased the risk of TC. To reduce the influence of lifestyle and genetic background, 29 PTC cases and 30 healthy participants were grouped in the tertiles of PBDEs distribution and analyzed in metabolic pathways in TC after controlling for sex, age, BMI, smoking status, alcohol use, education level, and iodine nutrition.

In this study, 20 of 29 metabolites and nine affected pathways were identified and further analyzed in this PTC case–control study, and pathways including glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and taurine and hypotaurine metabolism were shown to be involved. Glycerophospholipid metabolism was the most affected pathway, which plays essential roles in cell growth and is characterized by an increase in the lipid content of cell membranes in carcinogenic pathways. The glycerophospholipid metabolite PC and phosphoethanolamine (PE) account for 50% of glycerophospholipids in cell membranes and are the precursors of lipid mediators in signal transduction. LysoPC is a class of lipid biomolecules derived by the cleaving of PC, and is linked to tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and inflammation in tumorigenesis (Liu et al., 2020). In this study, LPCs including PC(16:1e), LysoPC(16:1), LysoPC(16:0), LysoPC(22:5), LysoPC(18:3(6Z,9Z,12Z)), and LysoPE(20:4) were significantly increased in the case group, in accordance with the results of previous studies showing an increased demand for the membrane biosynthesis of tumor cells, which is thought to be potentially relevant to the biological behavior of TC (Liu et al., 2020). Apart from affecting glycerophospholipids, PBDEs exposure might also lead to an increased risk of TC by altering sphingolipid metabolites, which are structural component of all eukaryotic cell layers. As representative sphingolipid metabolites, ceramide and sphingosine modulate cell growth arrest and apoptosis, whereas sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) has a multitude of effects on regulating cellular proliferation and migration, neurite retraction, and cardiac embryogenesis (Hannun & Obeid, 2008). Sphingosine kinase (SphK) is the catalytic enzyme of S1P. It has been found that the overexpression of SphK1 correlates with high-grade tumors, resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and a poor prognosis and survival rate in a variety of cancers such as breast, lung, colorectal, gastric cancers, and glioma (Gupta et al., 2021). Moreover, the upregulation of SphK1 accompanied by the up-synthesis of S1P accounts for cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, apoptosis, metastasis, and microenvironmental inflammation in tumors (Huang et al., 2020a, 2020b; Maiti et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2018).

In this study, the value of identified differentiated metabolites in PTC and control was further estimated using the ROC curve, resulting in a diagnostic performance of ( ±)-octopamine and 5-hydroxyindole of AUC = 0.953 and AUC = 0.703, which were first reported to be biomarkers in PTC and might play a regulatory role in oxidative stress, inflammation, and glucose and lipid metabolism. Octopamine (OA), originally found as a natural component extracted from the posterior salivary glands of octopus vulgaris, is an endogenous and natural β3-adrenergic agonist produced from tyramine in invertebrates and vertebrates. As a neurotransmitter, octopamine induces the expression of the lipase gene lips-6 via its receptor SER-3 in the intestine of Caenorhabditis elegans to mediate lipid hydrolysis (Tao et al., 2016). In vitro, octopamine can enhance the lipopolysis of mouse adipocytes through the β3-adrenergic receptor and promote the glucose absorption of human adipocytes through the oxidation of amine oxidase (Bour et al., 2003). These observations in humans were further supported by the finding that octopamine has important physiological effects on the treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity, and has been added to many diet pills on the market (Wang et al., 2018). Therefore, the decrease in (±)-octopamine mediated by PBDEs might be a toxification mechanism for the pathogenesis of TC via disrupting glucolipid metabolism. Another potential involvement of octopamine in TC is oxidative stress (Wu et al., 2022). Octopamine has higher scavenging ability on the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl free radical, superoxide anion free radical, and hydroxyl free radical than its tyramine precursor (Bour et al., 2003). Octopamine activates the Gq-coupled octopamine receptor SER-3 and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway, which promotes glutathione biosynthesis and protects nematodes from mating-induced ROS (Tsai et al., 2023). Conversely, PBDEs affect thyroid function and oxidative stress, which are key contributors for the pathogenesis of TC (Zhou et al., 2021).

Meanwhile, we found that with an AUC of 0.703, 5-hydroxyoxindole was significantly decreased in the case group and high PBDEs exposure group. Given its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, 5-hydroxyoxindole may be implicated in the development of thyroid cancer, though its direct predictive role in this context remains to be elucidated. It is well known that 5-hydroxyoxindole is a metabolite of indole, which can be transformed from tryptophan via tryptophanase in indole-producing bacteria of human gut and act as a neurotransmitter that is involved in learning, memory, and central nervous system regulation (Huang et al., 2021). Previous studies have reported that 5-hydroxyoxindole exhibits radical scavenging activity, hydrogen peroxide-induced intracellular oxidative stress, and anti-proliferative activity by decreasing extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 (ERK1) and ERK2 isoforms in several cancer cells (Cane et al., 2000; Crumeyrolle-Arias et al., 2004; Yasuda et al., 2013). The increase in inflammation is closely correlated with cancer progression (Azizi et al., 2022; You et al., 2023). The gene expression of inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha, interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), and IL-6, are significantly upregulated after PBDEs exposure, indicating increased inflammation, which might contribute to the growth of thyroid tumors (Wei et al., 2020). However, the 5-hydroxyoxindole significantly inhibits lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory effects and induces expression of the transcription factor, nuclear factor erythroid-2 related factor 2, which is typically ubiquitinated by Keap1, an adaptor component of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex (Niino et al., 2018). It can be inferred that 5-hydroxyoxindole may play a role in thyroid cancer by reducing inflammation-induced cellular damage, inhibiting inflammation-induced cell proliferation, promoting apoptosis, and regulating immune responses. Although the specific mechanisms and effects of octopamine and 5-hydroxyoxindole in thyroid cancer still require further research and verification, the discovery of its predictive role in thyroid cancer still provides a new scientific basis for early disease diagnosis and the development of therapeutic drugs.

Conclusions

This study is the first metabolomics finding on PTC associated with PBDEs exposure. A variety of dysregulated metabolites associated with PBDEs were identified, mainly involved in the metabolic pathways of glycerophospholipid, sphingolipid, taurine, and hypotaurine. Our findings showed that PBDEs exposure decreased important metabolites such as octopamine and 5-hydroxyoxindole, which protect cells from oxidative stress, inflammation, and glucose and lipid metabolism disorders. Still, this study had some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small; thus, the results obtained from this study need further validation in the future. Second, the different pathological types and severity of PTC were not involved in this case–control study, resulting in it being difficult to detect differences in the metabolites of diseases of different severity. In addition to verifying the findings of this study in larger-scale population studies, future in vitro and in vivo studies should also explore the mechanisms underlying potential biomarkers. The strength of this study is that it highlights the link between PBDEs exposure and risk of TC. Furthermore, our findings provide a better understanding of the molecular and metabolic mechanisms induced by PBDEs exposure in TC, and may provide potential effective biomarkers for discriminating malignant from benign lesions and facilitating an early diagnosis.
