Developments and food safety considerations concerning genetically engineered microbial crop protection agents and biostimulants
Authors: Jan Pieter van der Berg, Gijs Spaans, Mark Sturme, Gijs Kleter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Genetically modified micro-organisms (GMMs) can be used in agriculture as crop protection agents against plant pests and diseases or as biostimulants intended to enhance plant nutrition. If GMMs are to be utilized in agriculture, they should conform with safety and regulatory requirements. Both academic and non-academic literature were evaluated for developments and commercial status of experimental GMMs intended as crop protection agents and biostimulants. The review also considers regulatory data requirements for GMMs that are relevant for food safety, as recommended by international organizations and authorities in the EU and USA. Experimental GMMs reported in literature and patents are mainly intended for biocontrol of insect pests and phytopathogenic fungi using specialized strains of bacteria (e.g., Bacillus spp.), fungi (e.g., Trichoderma spp.), and baculoviruses. GMMs with biostimulant or biofertilizer activity include microbes with plant nutrition-enhancing and enhanced nitrogen fixation traits. Food safety data requirements for GMMs, as mandated by EU and US regulations, are similar. By replacing high-risk chemical pesticides, GMMs could help achieve policies towards greater sustainability of agriculture.

1. Introduction

There is a renewed interest in genetically modified micro-organisms (GMMs) developed for use in agriculture. The potential agricultural applications of such GMMs include their use in biological pest control, as well as plant growth promoting agents.1) Benefits of the utilization of GMMs, as well as non-GM biopesticides instead of chemical agents are a shift towards decreased use of chemical pesticides, which are linked to a variety of adverse effects such as resistant pests and environmental and public health risks.2) This shift towards biological alternatives for chemical pesticides is accelerated in the EU by the European Green Deal, which aims to halve the chemical pesticide use in Europe by 2030.

Advances in biotechnology have allowed for the improvement of microbes to fulfil specific functions in agriculture. GMMs for use in agriculture have been explored since the 1980s. For instance, the commercial use of a genetically engineered Agrobacterium radiobacter strain K1026 as a biocontrol agent for crown gall disease of stone fruits and roses has been permitted since 1989 in New South Wales and since 1993 in all of Australia. This strain is sold under the name ‘NoGall®’3) and has also been approved for commercial use in the USA since 1999. Biotechnological techniques for the development of GMMs can include genetic modification techniques such as mutagenesis and transgenesis for the alteration of the hosts’ DNA. The recent development of gene or genome editing technologies, in particular CRISPR/Cas, has revolutionized biotechnology. It allows for efficient and precise alteration of genetic material. Although gene editing can help to introduce relatively large DNA fragments (e.g., transgenes), it is often used to generate small genetic alterations. In order to control plant pests, experimental GMMs often are engineered to produce specific biomolecules against pathogens, such as bactericidal toxins or enzymes that degrade fungal cell walls (section 2.4, Table 1).4,5) Besides bacteria like A. radiobacter K1026, also plant and insect viruses can be modified and used as crop protection agents. Examples of plant viruses include hypovirulent alphaflexivirus strains, which can be used to immunize greenhouse-grown vegetable plants against infection with virulent pepino mosaic viruses.6,7) Although mutagenesis and other forms of genetic engineering would facilitate the creation of mild plant virus isolates, the current commercial mild isolates originate from within the natural biodiversity range.6) Also, a variety of insect baculoviruses can be genetically modified with genes coding for insecticidal or insect hormone-disrupting compounds for more efficient protection of trees, fruits, and tea plants against, for example, lepidopteran insects.5,8) Historically, a number of genetically engineered baculoviruses were field-tested and received regulatory approval but never commercialized, whilst renewed interest has arisen recently.9)

Moreover, plant microbiome-associated micro-organisms are interesting targets to improve plant growth and plant health. For such purposes, a number of commercial products consisting of micro-organisms with biostimulant or biofertilizer activity have been developed.10) Also in this field, genetic engineering can be utilized to confer beneficial traits on target microbes. For example, commercial products developed by the company Pivot Bio are engineered with improved nitrogen-fixing capacity, which provides crops with additional biologically fixed nitrogen.11) In addition, the biostimulant product PROVEN 40 (Pivot Bio) consists of a mixture of gene-edited Klebsiella variicola and Kosakonia sacchari with enhanced biological nitrogen fixation and ammonium excretion. These activities allow microbial biostimulants to function as an alternative to chemical nitrogen sources, and these products thus may partially replace synthetic fertilizers.12)

All in all, genetically engineered micro-organisms are increasingly being developed for use as crop protection agent or biostimulant in agriculture to accommodate a shift towards decreased chemical pesticide and chemical fertilizer use. As these micro-organisms are to be deliberately released in the environment and can be applied on food crops, the utilization of such GMMs may raise both environmental and food safety concerns. Therefore, it is important to gain an overview of the extent at which such GMM products are already being commercialized worldwide. Furthermore, this information can help policymakers stay informed on new developments concerning agricultural GMM products and related food safety and regulatory implications. Thus, the aim of this study is to, by means of a literature analysis (Supplementary Information A), (1) obtain an overview of the developments, both scientifically and commercially, regarding GMMs for use in agriculture, and (2) to assess safety and regulatory implications of these GMMs with a focus on food safety aspects.

2. Development of GMMs for use in agriculture

2.1. GMM crop protection agents and their target pests and pathogens

Historically, GMMs for use in agriculture have extensively been explored in academia. Many studies describe bacterial species that are genetically engineered for biocontrol purposes in agriculture, such as strains of Pseudomonas and Bacillus spp., and to a lesser extent Agrobacterium spp. (Supplementary Information Table S3). The main target organisms of microbial crop protection agents’ activity are insect pests (35 of 59 studies, see Table S3,), but fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, and viral diseases are targeted as well. In many cases, the genetically engineered trait that confers biocontrol of insect pests is the gene encoding Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) endotoxin.4,13–16) On the other hand, the control of microbial pests (fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, viruses) seems to be more diverse, and may involve the production of specific antimicrobial metabolites (e.g., phenazine)17) competitive utilization of exotic carbon sources such as salicylate,18) but can also rely on fitness enhancement or inhibition of genetic mechanisms in the pathogen.19)

Besides GM bacterial crop protection agents, recent studies also described the development of engineered fungal hosts for the biocontrol of insect pests. For example, an engineered Metarhizium acridum was developed with increased virulence towards locusts.20) Due to the overexpression of transcription factor Som1 gene, which is part of the cAMP/PKA pathway, many downstream pathways contributing to increased fungal virulence were augmented, such as adhesion ability, appressorium penetration, and accelerated growth in host hemolymph.

Another recent study focused on the introduction of miRNAs in the fungus Beauveria bassiana, which have been shown to suppress pathways involved in insect immune response.21) Expression of such miRNAs modulates the insect immune response and allows the GM fungal spores to penetrate the insect, which leads to mycosis and death.

Other studies described the utilization of GM strains of Isaria, Lecapicillium, Paecilomyces, or Trichoderma spp. (Supplementary Information Table S3). In a few cases, also pathogenic fungi were the target of biocontrol activity, such as Rhizoctonia solani, a fungus responsible for a variety of plant diseases (e,g., in potato and soybean), and Gaeumannomyces tritici, causing take-all disease in wheat and barley.22)

The development of GM yeasts as biocontrol agents was described in a limited number of studies. Examples of these are the use of RNAi expressing GM Saccharomyces spp. for the control of the fruit fly Drosophila suzukii,23) or Pichia spp. producing cell wall degrading enzymes for the control of plant pathogenic fungi.24)

Literature indicates that GM viral crop protection agents are currently solely developed as biocontrol agents against insect pests, and mainly make use of baculoviruses. The baculovirus host most often engineered for controlling insect pests is the Autographa californica Multiple Nucleopolyhedrovirus,25–27) while genetically engineered Anticarsia gemmatalis-, Bombyx mori-, and Helicoverpa armigera multiple nucleopolyhedroviruses have been described in scientific literature as well.28–30) These baculoviruses can be genetically engineered to control a wide range of different insect pests, and exert pest control by means of different mechanisms, including production of toxins, immunosuppressive proteins or cell wall-degrading enzymes (Table S3).

2.2. GMM biostimulants

GMMs with an intended use as biostimulant or with plant growth promoting characteristics include bacteria from a variety of genera (Rhizobium, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella and Kosakonia), cyanobacteria belonging to the genus Synechocystis, and filamentous fungi belonging to the Trichoderma genus (Table S4). Introduced genetic elements are often involved in nitrogen fixation,31) the excretion of factors that promote plant growth and nutrient uptake,32–35) but also other characteristics are mentioned such as the control of certain plant diseases.32,33)

2.3. Biotechnologies for the development of GMM crop protection agents and biostimulants

Technologies available for use in the development of GMMs for agricultural application vary from mutagenesis techniques, such as UV-C irradiation or chemical mutagens,17,36) to transgenesis, RNA interference, and genome editing. Many of the studies on GMM crop protection agents with an introduced transgene concerned genetic elements encoding natural toxins, for example: scorpion toxin,37) spider toxin,38) or Bacillus endotoxin.4) Also, several studies focused on the expression of antimicrobial metabolites,39–41) cell wall degrading enzymes,28) immunosuppressive proteins,26) or metabolites improving the stress resistance or infectivity of the microbial control agent.27,42)

Gene silencing through RNA interference has been investigated for the control of insect pests with the use of different GMMs, such as different fungal species, baculoviruses, and the baker’s yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Several studies describe suppression of the insect immune system using GMM fungi,43–45) as well as the disruption of an important functional insect hormone by means of a baculovirus,25) both achieved through RNA-based gene silencing. Moreover, since 2014, S. cerevisiae has been studied as a microbial host for gene silencing in other organisms through RNA interference,46) and in a study by Murphy et al, the yeast was engineered to alter gene expression in the fruit crop pest Drosophila suzukii.23)

Recently, studies were also published on the utilization of CRISPR/Cas-based genome editing for improving fungal microbial crop protection agents.47,48) Making use of the Cas9 enzyme, Kreuter et al.49) developed a method in which multiple genes are simultaneously altered, to bring about biocontrol properties in Aureobasidium sp. fungi. In addition, some studies focus on the recombinant production of enzymes and metabolites with biocontrol properties from engineered microbes, such as the cell wall degrading enzyme aspartic protease24) and the antibiotic surfactin50) from modified Trichoderma harzianum and Bacillus subtilis, respectively.

Since 2015, the number of filed patents on GMM crop protection agents and biostimulants shows an increasing trend (Fig. 1). In the period of 2014–2024, the majority of patented inventions using GMMs involved biostimulants (61 in total), and all of these comprise the use of engineered bacteria (Supplementary Information Table S5). On the other hand, the patented inventions on GM crop protection agents for biological control of agricultural pests (16 in total) involved a variety of host organisms, including fungi, viruses, and bacteria. In Raven®, it has been transformed with a recombinant plasmid present in high copy numbers and expressing a cry3Bb gene, resulting in a 3–4 fold increase in crystal protein levels of Cry3Bb.52)

Another example of GM bacteria expressing cry genes are the MVP and M-Trak products previously sold by Mycogen, after MVP had become the first GMM crop protection agent to receive field-testing approval from the US EPA in the early 1990s. Both are based on inactivated GM Pseudomonas fluorescens cells expressing B. thuringiensis delta-endotoxins affording resistance against lepidopteran and coleopteran insect pests, respectively.53,54)

Notably, a number of non-transgenic, commercial B. thuringiensis products are in fact “transconjugants”, having received DNA plasmids encoding particular Cry proteins of interest from other B. thuringiensis strains. The reason for creating such transconjugants are the same as for recombinant DNA (GM) strains, namely the fact that certain Cry proteins will act synergistically against the target insects if used together. Not all plasmids carrying cry genes are amenable to transfer through conjugation between B. thuringiensis strains, though. In addition, plasmids carrying cry genes are commonly very large, also harbouring genes encoding inferior toxins, for instance. Farmers around the world, including Europe and the USA, use crop protection agents based on such strains to protect their crops. For example, the Agree® crop protection agents currently sold by Certis contains B. thuringiensis var. aizawai which has obtained a plasmid with the cry1 gene originally from a B. kurstaki strain.52)

3. Data requirements for the regulatory pre-market food safety assessment of GMMs

The legislation in many countries requires the authorization of plant protection agents, GM organisms, and other potentially hazardous agents before they can be lawfully marketed. GMMs used as biocontrol agents or bio-stimulants may require at least two separate authorizations, both as microbiological control agent or bio-stimulant per se (for GMMs and non-GM microbes alike); and as GM products. Whereas the precise laws may differ from one country to another, there has been, to some extent, international harmonization of the data required for the pre-market safety assessment. Key international organizations which foster the harmonization of such data requirements are the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO). The majority of (pre)commercial GMMs identified in this study, identified in patent applications and marketed products, was developed or commercialized in the EU and the USA. Therefore, the regulatory context in which these safety data have to be provided in these two countries/regions is described in more detail.

3.1. Microbial crop protection agents

3.1.1. International harmonization

FAO and WHO have jointly published guidelines for the data needed for registration of microbial crop protection agents for crop protection and public health uses.70) Regulatory agencies involved in drafting these guidelines acknowledged, for example, that microbial crop protection agents differ by nature from synthetic ones and that therefore different data may be needed. In addition, simplified procedures may apply to certain microbial species with a long history of use in agriculture without any major adverse impact.70)

The guidelines distinguish between the “active substance” and the “product”. The active substance is the part of the product that is responsible for the pesticidal action. This could be living or dead micro-organisms, and/or secondary metabolites formed by them. The product is the so-called “formulation”, that is, the mixture of the active substance with, e.g., stabilizers, emulsifiers, or synergists, as registered and applied to the crop in practice.70)

Technical data that applicants typically provide as “dossiers” for the regulatory approval procedure, which could also be relevant for food safety (although not specifically referred to), include70):

･Data on the nature, identity, biological characteristics, and analysis of the active substance and product, including the microbial strain used, its host range, any antibiotics it may form, its production process, the composition of the active substance and the product, and methods for its detection.

･Data on the nature, identity, biological characteristics, and analysis of the active substance and product, including the microbial strain used, its host range, any antibiotics it may form, its production process, the composition of the active substance and the product, and methods for its detection.

･Human health data, including toxicological studies in laboratory animals and cell-based models, such as genotoxicity of secondary metabolites, sensitization studies, acute toxicity testing, including checks for pathogenicity and invasiveness, as well as higher-tier toxicity studies, such as sub-chronic studies, although usually not necessary. A trigger for such testing may be the presence of potentially toxic secondary metabolites or ingredients in the product.

Supplementing these FAO/WHO guidelines with more detailed technical documents, the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) has developed guidelines on the environmental safety assessment and specific technical aspects (e.g., contamination of microbial preparations), reviews of regulatory requirements in different countries (e.g., USA, Canada, EU, Japan, Australia), data formats that can be filled in by companies filing application dossiers or governments summarizing them, and informative events. It treats micro-organisms as a subcategory of “biological pesticides”, which also includes other agents of biological origins, such as pheromones.71) Notably, its guidance for registration requirements for microbial crop protection agents notes that for mutant micro-organisms or GMMs, their differences with the wild-type host strain should be indicated, besides the many other details for their characterization as required under FAO/WHO guidance.72)

The EU, US, and other agencies base their own assessments, working procedures, and guidelines on these internationally harmonized documents, as discussed in more detail below.

3.1.2. European Union

Within the EU, Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009 regulates “plant protection products”. This covers a broad range of active substances including biological agents (organisms) and natural products, such as bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and viruses.73) The EU distinguishes between active agents and formulations. The approval and pre-market safety assessment of active agents (or active substance, active ingredient) takes place at the community level. The formulations will be approved by member state authorities following an additional assessment, taking stock of the EFSA experts’ opinion on the safety of the active ingredient. Many biological active substances may be designated “low risk”. Based on their favourable safety profile, these “low-risk” active substances are exempt from the full risk assessment typically carried out for their synthetic counterparts. Criteria for classification of microbes as low-risk include absence of certain forms of toxicity and not being a priority microbiological agent. Under the additional criteria introduced by Regulation (EU) 2022/1438, bacteria can be categorized as low-risk if they are sensitive to at least two categories of antimicrobials, whilst baculoviruses and plant viruses should not have adverse effects on non-target insects and plants, respectively. This determination of low-risk status is only done at a very late stage of the regulatory approval procedure, though.

The European Commission in 2022 published guidelines for data requirements for microbial crop protection agents, as well as substances/metabolites produced by microbes. For example, Regulation (EU) 2022/1439 provided guidance on what data would be needed for chemical substances produced by micro-organisms, including direct, or indirect and delayed effects, behaviour of the compounds in the environment, their toxicity, and exposure in the environment and through food, as well as that of workers and bystanders during application. The performance of animal studies is discouraged, though. For microbes themselves being the plant protection agent, the safety considerations include, for example, the absence or presence of possible factors of virulence, pathogenicity, production of metabolites of concern (e.g., toxins), and antimicrobial resistance factors and their possible transfer. Regulation (EU) 2022/1440 and 1441 delve more generally into the various data needed on the characteristics of formulations, e.g., technical composition of the product, the way of applying, its behaviour in the environment, and safety for human and animal health (including possible residues in food).

3.1.3. USA

Plant protection agents are within the regulatory oversight of the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Three laws may basically apply, namely the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA), the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetics Act (FFDCA), and the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA).54) Under FIFRA, both non-GM and GM crop protection agents will be assessed, and additional information on the genetic modification will therefore be required for the GM ones.

Before it can grant marketing permission under FIFRA, the US EPA will consider the potential impacts of the micro-organisms on non-target organisms, their environmental behaviour and their possible harm to humans and animals.

As regards human safety, three factors are to be considered: infectivity, pathogenicity, and toxicity. Usually, acute toxicity/pathogenicity studies are considered sufficient. Although residue data and a residue safety evaluation would ordinarily be required for pesticides, microbial crop protection agents are commonly exempted from this requirement under FFDCA as they are considered to have a favourable, benign toxicity profile. TSCA acts as a safety net, covering those micro-organisms that are not captured by the other two statutes.54)

For enforcement of pesticide residues in foods, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) commonly relies on the residue limits, or “tolerances”, set by the US EPA. In some cases, though, the EPA may grant “tolerance exemptions” based on the safety profile of the pesticide and the expected exposure of the consumer to the residues. In such cases, no tolerance is established.74) The applicant can request such an exemption through a “petition”. This has been the case for many “plant-incorporated protectants” (introduced into GM crops) and other GM and non-GM crop protection agents. This also means that the applicant for the particular microbial crop protection agent approval does not have to provide an analytical method to the US EPA.

3.2. Biostimulants

3.2.1. International harmonization

At the moment of writing, there is no internationally harmonized definition or data requirement for plant biostimulants. The term “biostimulant” was first coined in online media in 1997 and in scientific literature in 2007. Whereas initially the focus was on the stimulation of plant growth, the scope of the definition in literature expanded over time. It currently covers three types of effect: increased plant growth (e.g., nutrient utilization efficiency), stress tolerance, and product quality.75) In some jurisdictions, biostimulants may be treated the same as plant growth regulators, which commonly fall under the same legislation as plant protection agents

3.2.2. European Union

The Fertilizing Product Regulation (FPR), i.e., Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, specifically mentions “plant biostimulants” as the 6th of 7 distinct product categories. It distinguishes both microbial and non-microbial plant biostimulants. They are defined as a fertilizing product of which the function is to “stimulate plant nutrition processes independently of the product’s nutrient content”, with the aim of improving certain characteristics of the plant or its rhizosphere. These include nutrient use efficiency, abiotic stress tolerances, quality characteristics, and availability of nutrients in the rhizosphere. Any growth-regulating product not covered by this definition or not solely containing nutrients would still fall under the definition of a plant growth regulator and hence fits within the scope of pesticide legislation.

Applicants wishing to market a plant biostimulant should complete a “conformity assessment” of their product with criteria laid out in the Annex to the FPR. Following a positive assessment, the plant biostimulant can carry the CE marking, which indicates its compliance with EU standards for health, safety, and environmental sustainability. The Regulation also specifies a number of quality criteria, including limits for the levels of heavy metals. For microbial biostimulants, limits apply to their levels of pathogenic micro-organisms (Salmonella, Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Vibrio spp., Shigella spp., Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcaceae), plus aerobic and yeast/fungal plate counts, if applicable. In non-microbial plant biostimulants, this list is limited to Salmonella and E. coli/Enterococcaceae. Moreover, the microbial products can only contain one or more out of 4 categories of micro-organisms, alive or dead, namely Azotobacter, mycorrhizal fungi, Rhizobium spp., and Azospirillum spp. Other information that the applicant has to provide under the FPR include the nature of product (e.g., microbial strains) and the acclaimed effect. The European Committee for Standardization has published detailed guidelines for the characterization of these specifications.76)

3.2.3. USA

In the USA, no official legal definition of plant biostimulants exists yet. USDA has submitted two alternative definitions to the US Congress, one of which closely resembles that of the EU FPR. Plant regulators fall under the definitions of a pesticide under FIFRA. Yet plant inoculants, consisting of micro-organisms applied to a plant or soil with the only purpose of increasing nutrient availability are excluded from this pesticide definition of a plant growth regulator. This also has implications for the claims that can be made for the product.77)

3.3. GMMs

3.3.1. International harmonization

For the food safety assessment of GMMs, the FAO/WHO Codex Alimentarius has established internationally harmonized guidelines. However, these are focused on the use of micro-organisms for food production.78) which is different from the scenario of, e.g., residues of GMMs on harvested food crops. Within these guidelines, the focus is on the potential toxicity and pathogenicity of the newly introduced traits, the viability and residence of the GMM in the human gastrointestinal tract as well as the potential for horizontal gene transfer and antibiotic production.78)

As regards horizontal transfer, the guidelines particularly focus on antibiotic resistance genes, including the background presence of antibiotic resistance genes in the host and their introduction via genetic modification. Intrinsic resistance against specific antibiotics may occur naturally in host microorganisms. This presence need not invariably lead to preclusion of their hosts from genetic modification. Concerns are raised if the genetic information encoding this intrinsic resistance resides on transferable mobile genetic elements as this would facilitate its transfer to other microorganisms. The safety assessment should therefore address such presence on transferable elements. Moreover, antibiotic resistance genes may be combined with genes of interest as so-called “marker genes” to facilitate selection of microbial cells successfully transformed with both genes. Since transfer of genes and plasmids commonly occurs amongst microorganisms, the possible consequences of such a transfer of the GM material of the GMM to gastrointestinal microorganisms should be assessed. Moreover, in the design of a GMM, choices can be made so as to reduce the likelihood of transfer and/or adverse consequence thereof, such as by avoiding genetic elements that would afford a selective advantage to possible recipient microorganisms in the gastrointestinal tract.78)

The OECD Working Party for the Harmonization of Regulatory Oversight in Biology (WP-HROB) has published various consensus documents on the biology of four species of micro-organisms, which include biocontrol agents (Pseudomonas, baculovirus, Acinetobacter, Acidothiobacillus). It has also published transversal documents on horizontal gene transfer, taxonomy, and detection methods.79) Whilst these documents support the environmental risk assessment of GMMs by authorities worldwide, they also contain data that are relevant to food safety assessment.

3.3.2. European Union

In the EU, genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are defined by Directive 2001/18/EC as organisms in which the genetic material has been “altered in a way that does not occur naturally by mating and/or natural recombination”. This definition not just covers techniques such as recombinant DNA but also techniques of mutagenesis that cause man-made mutations in DNA. Yet random mutagenesis techniques such as chemical and radiation mutagenesis are deliberately exempted from the requirements for authorization as GMOs based on the history of application of these techniques. However, this exemption does not apply to organisms obtained with more recent, targeted mutagenesis techniques such as CRISPR/Cas9 and other nucleases given the lack of history of use. Current discussions within the EU focus on the introduction of new legislation granting a special regulatory status to plants obtained with gene editing and other “new genomic techniques”. Yet it is still unclear if and to what extent the same will be done for gene-edited micro-organisms.

An important consideration in the EU is whether a food has been produced “from” or “with” a GMM. The latter applies, for example, to food processing aids, such as certain food enzymes which are used in the production process but do not occur in the final food product consumed. It may also apply to food additives. For GMMs in food, an important criterion is whether there are materials from the GMM, such as transgenic DNA, still present in the food product.80) This also shows that in the EU, the presence of GM DNA, including those derived from biological crop protection agents, would trigger a safety assessment of the GM material regardless of whether the GMM from which it is derived is still viable or not.

3.3.3. USA

The USA has a record of decades of experimental testing with a broad range of GMMs tested for their pest management capacity, of which several have made it to the market. Besides the US EPA, the US FDA and the US Department of Agriculture’s Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (USDA-APHIS) are two other key federal agencies overseeing the safety of GMOs in the USA.81) Recent discussions within these agencies and with stakeholders have also focused on the regulation and data requirements for gene-edited products. For example, US EPA would exempt gene-edited “plant-incorporated protectants” from the requirement of review if already occurring in a sexually compatible plant.82) Yet these discussions have so far focused on gene-edited plants, hence the prospects for possible changes in data requirements for gene-edited micro-organisms are still unclear.

Besides data considered for all biological crop protection agents under FIFRA as explained in section 3.1.3, the US EPA will also consider stability of the transgene in GMM crop protection agents over generations of the production organisms.54)

Under the Plant Protection Act, USDA APHIS oversees the environmental releases (field trials) of GMMs used as biocontrol agents if these are “regulated products” because they may act as pathogens or other pests to plants. One of the factors considered by USDA-APHIS which is also relevant for food safety is the potential for horizontal gene transfer of the transgene and whether it would confer a fitness advantage to the recipient.83)

The US FDA regulates the food and pet food uses of GMMs under the Federal Food, Drugs, and Cosmetics Act. Contrary to the other agencies, it follows a “product-based” or actually “risk-based” approach. The most important criterion for whether a GMM should be “regulated” is the characteristics of the product ultimately consumed by the human or animal consumer. Unless these can be declared “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS), they have to undergo a full-fledged safety assessment prior to commercialization.84) For pesticide residues in foods, the US FDA commonly relies on the residue limits, or “tolerances”, set by the US EPA. For many “plant-incorporated protectants” and other biological crop protection agents, though, the US EPA has granted “tolerance exemptions” based on their safety profile and expected consumer exposure.74) When no tolerance is established then, neither has the applicant to provide an analytical method to the US EPA.

4. Discussion and conclusions

In this study, an overview of the developments regarding GMMs for use in agriculture was generated, based on academic and grey literature analyses. We identified that, to date, the majority of experimental GMMs in development for agricultural applications often consisted of GM bacteria, GM fungi, and GM baculoviruses for pest control, as well as several GMMs for use as biostimulant. The described GMMs for the biocontrol of unwanted organisms reportedly could serve against many different insect pests and plant pathogenic fungi, but also the control of some bacteria, viruses, and oomycetes. For example, experimental GM bacteria such as Pseudomonas spp. and Bacillus spp. were reported in scientific literature with potential activity against, among others, plant pathogenic bacteria,17) insects,4) or oomycetes.18) Experimental GM fungi, mainly Metarhizium spp. and Trichoderma spp., were mostly developed for the control of insect pests,37) as well as against plant pathogenic fungi.85) Among the GM baculoviruses, Autographa californica Nucleopolyhedrovirus can be utilized for control of insect pests by gene silencing through RNA interference in insect pests.25)

Furthermore, rather than to control pests, microbes can also be engineered to improve plant health. Such GMM biostimulants can for example influence plant growth by altering mechanisms involved in nitrogen fixation,86) as well as excretion of factors that are beneficial for the plant.34)

Different fields of legislation converge for GMMs, including those for crop protection agents, biostimulants (EU), and genetic modification. Microbial agents used as crop protection agent or biostimulant may be regarded a substance of low concern, such as “low-risk” pesticides in the EU under pesticide legislation. Also for biostimulants, a regime of reduced data requirements may apply as compared to the package of data that typically has to be handed in for synthetic pesticides. This may also account for the fact that there are no explicit data requirements on the food safety of residues of GMM crop protection agents on foodstuffs. Besides metabolites, also the presence of mutated or transgenic DNA is of interest, because this plays a decisive role in the EU whether or not the safety of the GM component needs to be assessed prior to authorization. Part of this should consider the potential consequence of horizontal gene transfer, which is a common feature of the environmental risk assessment of GMOs. Extensive research on the deliberate release of GMMs revealed that the environmental impact of GMMs is highly similar to their natural counterparts.87) Despite the large amount of data on the impact of GMMs on the environment, questions remain on potential safety issues, such as horizontal gene transfer. However, the large amount of available knowledge may support existing guidelines for risk assessment of GMM products but challenges still remain for a complete environmental risk assessment of the environmental release of GMMs.88)

Researchers and product developers can alter a variety of mechanisms while engineering GMMs for agricultural use. For example, in this study the identified modes of actions for pest control within GMMs include insect toxins, antibiotics, degradative enzymes that break down the pathogen cell wall, and suppression of the pathogen immune system. Such genome alterations are achieved with the use of several genetic engineering technologies. These include transgenesis, genome editing, and RNA interference. In addition, conventional random mutagenesis techniques, such as UV irradiation and chemical mutagens, are still employed as well. Synthetic biology and low-key, high-precision tools such as nucleases used for gene-editing of target genes and other sequences could accelerate the optimization of microbial strains for use as crop protection agent or biostimulant.

Patent applications for GMM crop protection agents and biostimulants have been granted worldwide in different regions. Nevertheless, only a limited number of products have been commercialized in several countries, such as in the USA, China, and Australia.

Taken together, biological plant protection products have gained renewed interest, and especially GMM alternatives for chemical pesticides synergize well with global Green Initiatives. Past studies have focused on the environmental impact of the release of GMM plant protection products, but knowledge gaps remain on potential food safety considerations (e.g., residues of GMMs and their DNA on food products). Regulatory frameworks on the authorization of GMMs in agriculture are currently not internationally harmonized, and divergences exist between countries/regions. In several regions such as the USA and Australia, various GMM crop protection agents and biostimulants have already undergone commercialization. On the other hand, in the EU and other regions, these product types have not yet been authorized for this purpose. Despite these differences in the regulatory landscape, our review shows that both EU and USA’s data requirements generally converge with regard to, e.g., requirements for microbial crop protection agents, biostimulants and recombinant DNA organisms. A notable exception is the mere presence of (remnants of) DNA linked to a genetic modification, which is treated under EU legislation as a GM product but not so in the USA, which is more concerned with the presence of viable GM cells. It would therefore be recommendable that parties agree on at least the actual risk scenarios linked to such presence of GM DNA before this may become a point of contention if the use of GMMs in agriculture were to become more widespread.

On a positive note, GMMs for use in agricultural crop protection may in the future aid Green Initiatives and support a shift away from high-risk chemical agents. Care should be taken, though, to ensure that stakeholders are duly informed by clarifying the distinction between chemical and biotechnological crop protection agents as these might easily be confused with each other, particularly by those with negative attitudes towards chemical crop protection.89)
