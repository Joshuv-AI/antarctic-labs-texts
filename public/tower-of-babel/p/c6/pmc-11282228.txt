Gallic acid exerts antibiofilm activity by inhibiting methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus adhesion
Authors: He Sang, Han Jin, Peng Song, Wei Xu, Fei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a serious threat to patients with nosocomial infections, and infection is strongly associated with biofilm formation. Gallic acid (GA) is a natural bioactive compound found in traditional Chinese medicines that exerts potent antimicrobial activity. However, the anti-MRSA biofilm efficacy of GA remained to be determined. This study investigated the antimicrobial activities of GA against MRSA and the mechanisms involved. The results revealed the significant antibacterial and antibiofilm activities of GA. The minimal inhibitory concentration of GA against MRSA was 32 μg/mL and a growth curve assay confirmed the significant inhibitory effect of GA on planktonic MRSA. Crystal violet and XTT assays showed that 8 µg/mL GA effectively inhibited the formation of new biofilms and disrupted existing biofilms by reducing both biofilm biomass and metabolic activities. Alkaline phosphatase and β-galactosidase leakage assays and live/dead staining provided evidence that GA disrupted the integrity of bacterial cell walls and membranes within the biofilm. Scanning electron microscopy observations showed that GA significantly inhibited bacterial adhesion and aggregation, affecting the overall structure of the biofilm. Bacterial adhesion, polysaccharide intercellular adhesion (PIA) production and real-time quantitative PCR assay confirmed that GA inhibited bacterial adhesion, PIA synthesis, and the expression of icaAD and sarA. These results suggested that GA inhibited biofilm formation by inhibiting the expression of sarA, then downregulating the expression of icaA and icaD, thereby reducing the synthesis of PIA to attenuate the adhesion capacity of MRSA. GA is therefore a promising candidate for development as a pharmaceutical agent for the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections caused by MRSA.

Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a major Gram-positive bacterium that causes nosocomial infections, leading to various diseases such as skin and soft tissue infections, septicaemia and pneumonia, with a high mortality rate1. In recent years, the overuse of antimicrobial drugs has led to MRSA developing resistance to most antibiotics, including β-lactams, making pneumonia and septicaemia more difficult to treat. This has positioned MRSA as one of the greatest threats in modern medicine2. According to the CHINET 2022 bacterial resistance monitoring report, the average detection rate of MRSA in China has exceeded 30% for the past five years3. And recent studies have shown that MRSA infections are increasing every year4–6. Moreover, in cases of prevalent hospital-acquired bacterial infections, the mortality rate for individuals with MRSA infection substantially exceeds that associated with infections caused by non-resistant bacteria. With the widespread outbreak of COVID-19 in 2019, the detection rate of MRSA has been continuously rising. The advent of secondary mixed infections resistant to multiple antibiotics has caused a sharp increase in MRSA bacteraemia cases, consequently leading to a higher mortality rate. These developments mark MRSA as an increasingly serious health concern7,8.

The formation of biofilms is an important factor contributing to bacterial resistance and serves as the first line of defence for bacteria. Statistics show that at least 80% of bacterial infectious diseases involve biofilm formation, and most patients undergoing antibiotic treatment may experience long-term recurrent infections due to biofilm formation9. Bacteria embedded in biofilms exhibit significantly improved resistance to antimicrobial agents compared with planktonic bacteria10. Studies have found that clinical isolates of MRSA are often strong biofilm producers, and biofilm formation further exacerbates the resistance of MRSA, reducing the sensitivity to antibiotics11. To date, MRSA biofilm infections have led to persistent and difficult-to-treat conditions, increasing the burden on healthcare systems. Therefore, the development of new drugs targeting MRSA biofilms is urgently needed.

Gallic acid (GA, 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid, C7H6O5) is a naturally occurring bioactive compound that is considered safe for humans12. It is found in traditional Chinese medicines, including gallnuts, propolis, Polygonum multiflorum and Dodartia orientalis. In recent decades, GA has gained attention for its antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, and is now widely used in the medical, biological and chemical industries, positioning it as a potent natural antimicrobial agent13,14. Nonetheless, recent research has largely focused on the application of GA in polymer material science and nanoscience, such as acylated pectin with GA, GA liposomes and GA conjugated with gold nanoparticles15–17. These studies concentrate on phenotype and preparation process improvement to enhance antioxidative, antimicrobial and antitumor activities rather than the antibacterial mechanisms of GA. In our previous work, we found Chinese propolis with significant anti-MRSA activity. And the major components of propolis were analysed for anti-MRSA activity and GA was found to be the most effective anti-MRSA compound. To date, there has been a lack of evaluation of the efficacy and explicit inhibitory mechanisms of GA against MRSA biofilm. Therefore, this study comprehensively evaluated the anti-MRSA biofilm efficacy of GA and analysed its mechanisms of action with the aim of providing theoretical insights and experimental data for the development of novel anti-MRSA drugs.

Materials and methods

Materials and bacterial strain

Gallic acid (GA, HPLC ≥ 98%) was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The MRSA strain (ATCC 43300) was stored at −80 °C in trypticase soy broth (TSB) with 20% glycerol (v/v) for long-term preservation, and at 4 °C in trypticase soy agar (TSA) for short-term storage.

Antibacterial activity under planktonic conditions

Determination of the minimal inhibitory concentration

The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of GA against MRSA was determined by the broth microdilution method according to a previously reported procedure18. Briefly, the cells of MRSA were cultured in TSB overnight and the bacterial suspension concentration was adjusted to 105 CFU/mL using TSB. Various concentrations of 100 μL of GA were then mixed with the equal volume of bacterial suspension in a 96-well microtitre plate, resulting in final GA concentrations ranging from 8 to 512 μg/mL. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, each well was treated with 20 μL of a 1 mg/mL resazurin sodium solution. The plate was then incubated for a further 3 h at 37 °C in the dark. Vancomycin hydrochloride (50 μg/mL) was used as a positive control. The MIC was determined as the lowest concentration of GA that prevented the solution from changing colour from purple to orange.

Determination of the minimal bactericidal concentration

The suspensions that exhibited no apparent bacterial growth in the aforementioned wells were transferred to TSA plates and subsequently incubated at 37 °C for an additional 24 h. The minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) was defined as the lowest concentration of GA at which no colonies were observed after subculture on TSA plates.

Growth curve

The impact of GA on the growth of MRSA was evaluated following the methodology outlined in a previous study by Yuan et al.19 with a few modifications. A bacterial suspension of 100 μL (105 CFU/mL) was inoculated into a 96-well microtitre plate. Different concentrations of GA were then added to the bacterial suspension, with final GA concentrations ranging from 8 ug/mL to 64 ug/mL. The absorbance was measured at 600 nm every 4 h using a microplate reader (FlexA-200, Aosheng, China) during a 24 h incubation period at 37 °C.

Antibiofilm assays

Inhibitory effect of GA on MRSA biofilm formation

The inhibitory effect of GA on biofilm formation was evaluated according to previously established protocols20,21. Briefly, MRSA cells were cultured overnight and the logarithmic phase cells of MRSA were harvested with the bacterial suspension concentration adjusted to 107 CFU/mL. Subsequently, the prepared bacterial suspension was exposed to different concentrations of GA (control, 8, 16, 32 and 64 μg/mL) in a 96-well microplate. A 1% sucrose solution was added to each well and the microplate was placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the planktonic bacterial cells were aspirated, and each well was subsequently rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). MRSA biofilm biomass and cell viability were assessed using crystal violet (CV) staining and XTT assays, respectively, according to established methods22.

Inhibitory effect of GA on mature MRSA biofilm

The inhibitory effect of GA on mature biofilm was assessed following the methodology reported in previous studies20,21. In brief, 200 μL of bacterial suspension (107 CFU/mL) containing 1% sucrose was added to the wells of a 96-well microplate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow the formation of mature MRSA biofilms. The planktonic cells were then removed and all wells were washed three times. Next, 200 μL of fresh TSB containing different concentrations of GA (control, 8, 16, 32 and 64 μg/mL) was added to the wells and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h again. At the same time, each treated well of the plate was supplemented with 1% sucrose. The biomass and cellular activity of the MRSA biofilm were also evaluated using CV and XTT assays in accordance with previous literature22.

Leakage of alkaline phosphatase of MRSA within the biofilm

The release of alkaline phosphatase (AKP) from biofilm-forming MRSA into the bacterial suspension was assessed using a previously reported method23 with some modifications. Briefly, the concentration of the bacterial suspension was adjusted to 107 CFU/mL after overnight cultivation of MRSA in TSB. 100 μL of bacterial suspension and 100 μL of TSB were combined in the wells of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow biofilm formation, with a final sucrose concentration of 1%. The bacterial suspension was then removed and the wells were washed. Subsequently, 200 µL of different concentrations of GA solution (control, 8, 16, 32 and 64 μg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for a further 24 h at 37 °C. The suspension was collected for AKP detection. The untreated bacterial suspension was used as a control. The leakage of AKP into the suspension was tested according to the instructions of the AKP kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

Leakage of β-galactosidase of MRSA within the biofilm

The release of intracellular β-galactosidase from biofilm-forming MRSA into the bacterial suspension was measured using a previously described method24 with several modifications. MRSA biofilms were pre-incubated and established as described in Sect. “Leakage of alkaline phosphatase of MRSA within the biofilm”. After removal of planktonic bacterial cells, all wells were washed three times. Subsequently, 200 μL of various concentrations of GA solution (control, 8, 16, 32 and 64 μg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for a further 24 h at 37 °C. The suspension was then collected and the β-galactosidase content was determined using a β-galactosidase activity test kit (Solarbio).

Live/dead bacteria staining

To visualise changes in MRSA cell membrane integrity within the biofilm, a live/dead bacteria staining assay was used18. Briefly, pre-cultured bacterial biofilms were exposed to various concentrations of GA ranging from 8 to 64 μg/mL, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C. After removal of the planktonic cells, the biofilm was washed and subjected to staining using the Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit for 15 min in the dark. Finally, the stained cells were transferred to a dish for observation under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, FV1200, Olympus, Japan).

Scanning electron microscopy observation of biofilm

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the effect of GA on the structure of bacterial biofilms according to a previously reported method25. Sterile coverslips were placed on the bottom of a six-well plate and overnight bacterial cultures (107 CFU/mL) with a final sucrose concentration of 1% were added to the wells and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to form biofilms. After removing the bacterial suspension, different concentrations of GA solution (control, 16 and 32 μg/mL) were added to each well and incubated for a further 24 h at 37 °C. Then, the coverslips were removed, rinsed and immersed in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution overnight at 4 °C for fixation. Following this, the MRSA biofilm on the coverslips was dehydrated using varying concentrations of ethanol, then tertiary butyl alcohol. Subsequently, the samples were freeze-dried, sputter-coated with gold and subjected to microscopic analysis using a scanning electron microscope (S-4800, Hitachi, Japan).

Determination of bacterial adhesion

The effect of GA on MRSA adhesion was assessed using a previously described method26. A 100 μL volume of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) was added to the wells of a 96-well microplate. Equal volumes of TSB with GA and sucrose were then added to the wells. The final concentration of GA ranged from 8 to 64 μg/mL, with a final sucrose concentration of 1%. TSB with 1% sucrose was used as a control. After allowing bacterial cells to attach for different periods of time (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 h) at 37 °C, the planktonic cells were removed and all wells were washed three times with PBS. Then, 200 μL of TSB was added to the wells and subjected to ultrasound for 5 min and the OD600 nm of the wells was measured using a microplate reader (FlexA-200).

Polysaccharide intercellular adhesion production analysis

The effect of GA on polysaccharide intercellular adhesion (PIA) production by MRSA was evaluated according to a previously reported procedure20 with some modifications. Briefly, MRSA suspension (108 CFU/mL) was exposed to different concentrations of GA ranging from 8 μg/mL to 64 μg/mL. After 24 h incubation at 37 °C, the combined suspensions were centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 30 min. The supernatant was then discarded and the resulting precipitate was reconstituted with sterile water. This suspension was then subjected to a further round of centrifugation to isolate the water-soluble PIA present in the supernatant.

The precipitates harvested in the final step of the above assay were resuspended with 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and a triple volume of 95% ethanol was added to the combined supernatants, which were then stored at 4 °C overnight. The sample was then centrifuged to obtain an alkali-soluble PIA precipitate. The quantification of PIA content was performed using the phenol/sulfuric acid method as described previously27.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis to determine gene expression levels

PIA synthesis-related genes were subjected to quantitative analysis by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The pre-cultured MRSA suspension (108 CFU/mL) was supplemented with 1% sucrose and 32 μg/mL GA at 37 °C for 12 h. The strain incubated in TSB supplemented with 1% sucrose and no GA served as a control. The bacterial suspensions were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C and the precipitates were collected. Total RNA was extracted using the flying shark plus bacteria RNA Kit (Nobelab, Jinan, China) and then converted to cDNA using ReScript IV RT SuperMix for qPCR (Nobelab) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-PCR using cDNA templates was performed with 2 × SYBR Premix UrTaqT II (Nobelab) using QuantStudio applied biosystems. The cycle threshold (CT) values of the genes analysed were standardised using 16S cDNA as a reference gene, and gene expression levels were calculated by the 2(−△△CT) method23,28. Statistical analysis

All assays were repeated at least three times independently. Data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 18.0 software with the Student’s t-test and ANOVA. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effect of GA on mature biofilm

To assess the effects of GA on the mature biofilm of MRSA, we also measured biofilm biomass and biofilm activity. As depicted in (Fig. 2C,D) clear disparities in biofilm biomass and activity between the treatment groups and the control group were observed. A significant reduction in biofilm biomass and activity was observed after treatment with 8 µg/mL GA, and the degree of reduction increased with increasing GA compared with the control. Taken together, it is clear that GA has a significant inhibitory effect on both the formation of new biofilms and the maturation of existing biofilms at concentrations below the 8 µg/mL.

Effect of GA on bacterial cell integrity within biofilms

AKP is located inside the bacterial cell, sandwiched between the cell wall and the cell membrane, making it undetectable in the extracellular environment unless the cell wall has been compromised by antibacterial agents22. Therefore, AKP levels in the bacterial suspension serve as an indicator of bacterial cell wall integrity. The results of this work are shown in (Fig. 3A). When the MRSA cells were treated with different concentrations of GA, the concentration of AKP in all of the suspensions increased significantly. Notably, the AKP content in the bacterial suspension was significantly increased in the 8 µg/mL GA treatment group compared with the control group, demonstrating the efficient inhibitory effect of GA on MRSA biofilm. β-Galactosidase is widely distributed in microbial cells and plays a role in lactose hydrolysis and transglycosylation. When the permeability of the bacterial cell membrane is increased, β-galactosidase can leak into the extracellular suspension. Thus, the activity of β-galactosidase in bacterial suspension can reflect the extent of damage to the cell membrane of bacteria in biofilms. As shown in Fig. 3B, β-galactosidase activity was significantly higher in the bacterial suspension of the treatment group compared with the control group, and the activity increased along with GA concentration. Similarly, the β-galactosidase content in the bacterial suspension was significantly increased in the 8 µg/mL GA treatment group compared with the control group. This result indicated that the permeability of the bacterial cell membrane within the biofilm was impaired. Combined with the results of the AKP assay experiments, these findings showed that low concentrations of GA could destroy both the integrity of the bacterial cell wall within the biofilm and the cell membrane, suggesting that GA has an effective inhibitory effect on MRSA biofilms.

The live and dead bacterial cells of the biofilm were stained using the Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit. This kit contains two stains, SYTO 9 and propidium iodide, which have different cell membrane penetration abilities and fluorescence characteristics. SYTO 9 can penetrate both living and dead bacterial cell membranes and emits green fluorescence, whereas propidium iodide can only penetrate cells with compromised membranes and emits red fluorescence. The yellow colour observed represents the superimposition of images of living and dead bacteria. Figure 3C shows that the red fluorescence increased significantly after treatment with 8 µg/mL GA and that the red fluorescence became more pronounced as the GA concentration increased, indicating greater damage to the bacterial cell membranes. The merged images also showed a more pronounced yellow hue with increasing GA concentrations. These results demonstrated that GA significantly compromises the cell membrane integrity of MRSA within the biofilm.

Effect of GA on bacterial adhesion

Biofilm formation is a dynamic, cyclical process that is generally divided into four stages: adhesion, aggregation and proliferation, maturation, and shedding and diffusion29. Adhesion is the initial and crucial stage that relies on the adhesive capacity of bacteria. Therefore, of bacteria to adhere can indirectly indicate their potential to form a biofilm. The key factor influencing the formation and robustness of biofilms is the bacterial capacity to adhere and aggregate. As depicted in Fig. 5A, the rate of adhesion inhibition exhibited a significant increase post-treatment with GA at or above 8 µg/mL during the critical phase of bacterial adhesion. Additionally, the inhibition rate correlated with the duration of culture. It was observed that GA not only hindered bacterial growth but also impacted their ability to adhere to solid surfaces. Effect of GA on PIA production

The major component of the staphylococcal extracellular polysaccharide is PIA, an intercellular poly-N-acetylglucosaminoglucose. Among the PIAs, alkali-soluble PIA plays a pivotal role in the process of biofilm formation and accumulation. Alkali-soluble PIA is an extracellular glucan that adheres to the surface of cells and contributes significantly to the development and aggregation of biofilms, whereas water-soluble PIA can provide a nutrient source for colonising pathogens30,31. As depicted in (Fig. 5B,C) a significant exponential decrease in the levels of both alkali-soluble and water-soluble PIA was observed after treatment with GA within the concentration range of 8 to 64 µg/mL. Furthermore, it was observed that the extent of the decrease in either alkali-soluble or water-soluble PIA content showed a positive correlation with the concentration of the sample. These results strongly suggest that GA significantly inhibits the production of PIAs by MRSA.

Effect of GA on expression of the icaAD and sarA genes

The significant effect of GA on the production of PIAs, as discussed above, has been established. The enzymes encoded by the ica operon are responsible for the synthesis of PIA32. To confirm the influence of GA on expression of the icaA and icaD genes, the major coding genes icaAD in the ica operon and its regulatory gene sarA were analysed in MRSA treated with 32 µg/mL GA, using the 16S rDNA gene as a reference gene. The results presented in (Fig. 5D–F) show that, compared with the control group, GA suppressed expression of the icaA, icaD and sarA genes. These results indicated that GA reduces the expression of the icaAD genes by downregulating the activity of the sarA gene, thereby significantly reducing the synthesis of PIAs.

Discussion

In recent years, the misuse of antimicrobial drugs has resulted in MRSA becoming resistant to the majority of antibiotics, making pneumonia and sepsis more difficult to treat and leading to MRSA being considered one of the greatest threats to modern medicine. Studies have shown a marked increase in mortality from MRSA infections year on year, indicating a growing epidemic33. The high detection and mortality rates of MRSA are of increasing concern. In addition, it has been shown that MRSA-induced malignant infections are mainly associated with biofilms, which enable MRSA to attach to the host for long-term proliferation, protect against antimicrobial agents and evade attack by the human immune system, resulting in diseases such as pneumonia, endocarditis and sepsis34,35. Biofilm formation further enhances the drug resistance of MRSA and reduces the effectiveness of antimicrobial drugs, resulting in severely limited therapeutic options. The development of novel, highly effective, low-toxicity anti-MRSA drugs is therefore urgently needed. GA is a natural small molecule compound found in a wide range of natural products. Previous studies confirmed that GA exerts effective inhibitory activity on multiple pathogens, including S. aureus, Escherichia coli and Shigella flexneri36–39. Blessing et al. reported the significant inhibitory effect of GA-rich methanol crude extracts of Ligustrum lucidum on MRSA40. However, the significant inhibitory effect of GA on MRSA biofilms has not been reported and the mechanism by which GA inhibits biofilm formation remains unclear. In the current study, we demonstrated the remarkable antibacterial and antibiofilm properties of GA against MRSA. Our findings indicate that GA not only effectively inhibits the formation of new biofilms but also disrupts established biofilms. GA had been shown to penetrate biofilms and disrupt the structural integrity of the bacterial cells within, leading to their demise. In addition, SEM analysis revealed significant changes in the overall structure of biofilms treated with GA. The disruption of the biofilm structure facilitated the penetration of GA into the biofilm matrix, enhancing its bactericidal effect on the embedded bacteria. These findings highlight GA as a promising candidate for the prevention and treatment of MRSA-associated diseases.

Biofilms are complex communities of bacteria that adhere to surfaces and are enveloped in a self-produced matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and humic substances41. Biofilm formation is a dynamic process that typically progresses through four stages: adhesion, aggregation and proliferation, maturation, and shedding and dissemination. Adhesion and aggregation serve as essential prerequisites for biofilm development, facilitating the establishment of new biofilms and the reinforcement of mature biofilms41,42. Given the importance of adhesion in biofilm formation, we investigated the effect of GA on MRSA adhesion. Our results showed the significant inhibitory effect of GA on MRSA adhesion. The ability of MRSA to adhere is closely related to the production of extracellular polysaccharides, particularly polysaccharide PIAs43. Our study showed that GA effectively suppressed the production of both alkali-soluble and water-soluble PIA, suggesting that this mechanism may underlie the inhibition of MRSA adhesion by GA.

It has been reported that the icaA and icaD genes partially overlap in sequence and together mediate the synthesis of N-acetylglucosamine in vitro using UDP-acetylglucosamine as a substrate, which forms β-1,6-linked homoglycan to synthesise PIA44. The expression of the icaA and icaD genes is under the control of the ica manipulator. The ica manipulator contains four functional genes, icaA, icaD, icaB and icaC, and a deterrent gene, icaR. The IcaA protein locates in the cell membrane and exerts N-acetylamino glucosyltransferase activity, which is highly active only when co-expressed with the IcaD protein; IcaB is a secreted protein; IcaC has a cytosolic integration region and expresses membrane proteins that contribute to the synthesis of full-length PIA molecules; and icaR is transcribed in the opposite direction to icaADBC and its encoded proteins have a negative regulatory effect on icaADBC32. In addition, the expression of icaAD is regulated by the Staphylococcal accessory regulator A (SarA). The sarA gene encodes the DNA-binding protein SarA and is essential for ica manipulator transcription, PIA generation and biofilm formation, and is an important global regulator of a variety of staphylococcal virulence factors45. SarA binds to the ica manipulator promoter to upregulate the expression of the icaAD functional gene, and is a positive regulator of ica manipulator functional genes. In our current study, we observed that GA significantly decreased the expression levels of the icaAD and sarA genes. Consequently, GA exerts its inhibitory effect on icaAD expression by suppressing sarA expression, resulting in a reduction in MRSA PIA production and ultimately a decrease in adhesion. This mechanism is likely to underlie the ability of GA to inhibit MRSA biofilms. Overall, the potent inhibitory effects of GA on MRSA and its biofilms suggest that GA holds great promise for the prevention and treatment of bacterial infections caused by MRSA. However, this study only used the standard MRSA strain (ATCC 43300) as the single-species biofilm model, and we will need to further confirm the antibiofilm activity of GA on the clinical isolates of MRSA, both in vitro and in vivo.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that GA has excellent antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against MRSA. The significant antibiofilm activity of GA was reflected by the reduction of biofilm activity and biomass, the disruption of bacterial cell integrity within the biofilm and the overall degradation of biofilm structure. The antibiofilm mechanism of GA against MRSA was elucidated by analysing bacterial adhesion, the production of PIAs and the expression of relevant regulatory genes. We found that GA could inhibit biofilm formation by inhibiting the expression of sarA and then downregulating the expression of icaA and icaD, thereby reducing the synthesis of PIA and consequently decreasing the adhesion capacity of MRSA. As a result, GA has great potential for use in the prevention and treatment of diseases caused by MRSA.
