Acute Urticaria as the Sole Manifestation of Dengue Fever: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Md Osman Goni, Mohammad Shahangir Biswas, Md Moyen Uddin PK, Md Ebrahim Khalil, Maksudul Haque, Mst Ayesha Siddika, Md Matiar Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUrticaria is a very uncommon dermatological manifestation of infection by the dengue virus. It can hinder diagnosis since some of its features overlap with allergic conditions, such as maculopapular and petechial rashes. We present a case of a 32‐year‐old male, resident of a dengue‐endemic area, who presented with an acute onset of generalized pruritic wheals and angioedema in the absence of any systemic symptoms, including fever. Laboratory tests showed leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated liver enzymes. Dengue serology confirmed primary infection (NS1 antigen and IgM positive). The patient responded to antihistamines and supportive care without complications. This case highlights the need to consider dengue in the differential diagnosis of acute urticaria in endemic areas, even without typical systemic symptoms.Keywords: acute urticaria, atypical presentation, dengue symptoms, dengue virus infection, viral exanthem

Introduction

Dengue virus infection is one of the most rapidly spreading mosquito‐borne viral diseases worldwide, posing a serious public health threat in more than 100 countries, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions [1]. It is caused by the Dengue virus (DENV), a flavivirus transmitted mainly by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes. It is estimated that there are approximately 390 million dengue infections worldwide each year, of which approximately 96 million are clinically manifest [2].

Clinically, dengue ranges from an asymptomatic or mild febrile illness to severe forms such as severe bleeding, plasma leakage leading to shock, or severe organ impairment. Typical symptoms include high fever, severe headache, retroorbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, and thrombocytopenia [3, 4]. However, in recent years, atypical manifestations involving the liver, kidneys, nervous system, and skin have been increasingly observed [5].

Dermatologic manifestations are common in dengue and often aid in clinical diagnosis. The most commonly reported cutaneous manifestations include maculopapular rashes, morbilliform rashes, petechiae, and purpura [6, 7]. In contrast, acute urticaria, a transient, pruritic rash characterized by wheals and angioedema, is rarely observed and remains underreported as an initial or sole symptom [8]. Dengue‐associated urticaria may result from immune responses, including mast cell degranulation and the release of histamine and cytokines, mimicking allergic reactions [9]. This case report highlights an atypical presentation of dengue fever in which acute urticaria occurred as the only initial symptom without fever or constitutional symptoms, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical vigilance and early laboratory confirmation in endemic regions.

Urticaria is a prevalent mast cell disease characterized by wheals, angioedema, or both [10]. Urticaria develops in the superficial dermis and is caused by vascular dilatation and fluid leaking into the skin, which is activated by histamine and vasodilator mediators released by mast cells and basophils [11].

Histamine and other mediators, including platelet‐activating factor (PAF) and cytokines released by activated mast cells, cause sensory nerve activation, vasodilation, plasma extravasation, and cell recruitment to urticarial lesions. The mast‐cell activation signals in urticaria are unclear and likely to be different. Wheals are histologically distinguished by upper and mid‐dermis edema and dilatation of the upper dermis postcapillary venules and lymphatic veins. Similar alterations are observed in angioedema's lower dermis and subcutis [10]. Skin affected by wheals almost always has increased endothelial cell adhesion molecules and a mixed inflammatory perivascular infiltrate of varying intensity, consisting of neutrophils and/or eosinophils, macrophages, and T cells, but no vessel‐wall necrosis, which is a characteristic of urticaria vasculitis [11].

This case is notable for the rarity of acute urticaria as a primary sign of dengue fever, which is rarely reported in the medical literature. Urticaria, a transitory, pruritic, and edematous skin eruption, is infrequently reported as the significant dermatological manifestation of dengue [12]. Urticaria is usually linked to allergic reactions, autoimmune illnesses, or viral infections such as hepatitis, Epstein–Barr virus, and cytomegalovirus [13]. In this case, the appearance of urticaria as the first symptom is quite unusual and may lead to a mistake. Since dengue fever can lead to serious complications such as dengue hemorrhagic fever and shock syndrome, early detection of atypical symptoms is critical for appropriate intervention [14]. One of the most notable elements of this case is that urticaria emerged before other common dengue symptoms, including fever and myalgia. This uncommon appearance poses a diagnostic problem since clinicians often do not link febrile urticaria with dengue virus infection. Without laboratory confirmation, such cases may be mistaken for an allergic reaction or another viral exanthem, delaying necessary treatment [15]. This case underscores the importance of including dengue in the differential diagnosis of acute urticaria, particularly when laboratory findings suggest viral etiology. Early recognition can prevent misdiagnosis and ensure timely management.

Case History

A 32‐year‐old male, with no significant medical history, presented to the outpatient department with complaints of generalized pruritic wheals and angioedema. The patient denied having fever, respiratory distress, gastrointestinal symptoms, or systemic complaints. The urticarial lesions developed suddenly and progressively worsened over a few hours before seeking medical attention. He had no prior history of allergic reactions, drug hypersensitivity, food allergies, or recent insect bites. On physical examination, the patient was afebrile (body temperature: 36.8°C), vital signs were stable with blood pressure: 120/80 mmHg, pulse rate: 76 beats per minute, respiratory rate: 18 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation: 98% on room air. Dermatological examination showed diffuse erythematous wheals with angioedema over the trunk and extremities. There was no mucosal involvement, petechiae, purpura, or hemorrhagic manifestations. Systemic examinations were unremarkable. Laboratory tests were performed to investigate the cause of acute urticaria. A complete blood count (CBC) showed mild leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, raising suspicion of a viral etiology. However, total serum IgE levels and absolute eosinophil count were within normal ranges, ruling out an allergic or parasitic cause. ALT and AST were elevated, renal function tests were standard, and coagulation parameters were unremarkable. Given the absence of an identifiable allergen and the patient's residence in a dengue‐endemic area, serological testing for dengue virus was conducted. The results showed NS1 antigen positive, IgM positive, and IgG negative, confirming a primary dengue virus infection. Other potential viral infections, including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, Epstein–Barr virus, and cytomegalovirus, were excluded through negative serological tests.

Methods: Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Based on the clinical presentation and biochemical findings, including Hematocrit (HCT), Alanine transaminase (ALT), and Aspartate transaminase (AST), which were 54%, 113 U/L, and 83 U/L, respectively (Table 1), the patient was diagnosed with dengue fever presenting with acute urticaria. Since there were no severe dengue manifestations such as severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, difficulty in breathing, and bleeding from the nose or gums in endemic countries, we highlight the necessity for doctors to consider acute urticaria as an atypical dengue presentation by focusing on an uncommon dermatological presentation of dengue fever.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

The HCT, ALT, and AST levels were normalized during the patient's follow‐up and the patient's symptoms fully recovered after treatment.

Discussion

Although maculopapular, petechial, or purpuric rashes are frequently linked to dengue fever, urticaria as the first symptom is still unusual and rarely documented in medical literature [16, 17]. Kulkarni et al. [18] documented a series of atypical dengue fever cases during an epidemic that manifested with urticaria and other unusual rashes and mentioned dengue cases with urticaria as one of the numerous symptoms, not as the primary or sole symptom. However, our patient did not have the common concomitant fever symptoms that were common in these situations and presented only with urticaria and angioedema. Thus, making a clinical diagnosis at that time is even more difficult. The differentials of acute urticaria (AU) include allergic reactions, drug hypersensitivity, autoimmune diseases, and parasitic infection. If no associated fever or allergen exposure is documented in a case of urticaria, then viral causes may be overlooked, specifically dengue. This is when thrombocytopenia and leukopenia become essential to raise suspicion for a viral infection and prompt timely serological testing. Infections are the leading cause of AU in adults, followed by medication hypersensitization. Upper respiratory tract infections were the most reported cause of infection in prior investigations [19]. Numerous studies have reported different dengue cutaneous symptoms; however, rather than urticaria, the majority of reports describe morbilliform eruptions, petechiae, or purpura [7, 20].

This patient's clinical presentation was unusual since they had angioedema and widespread pruritic wheals and subsequent fever, which is different from the typical dengue. Similar urticarial lesions in a dengue patient were documented by Kulkarni et al. [18]; however, in contrast to this case, fever and systemic symptoms were present. The first diagnostic challenge resulted from our patient's lack of feverish illness, highlighting the significance of taking dengue into account when urticaria remains unexplained, particularly in endemic locations [21]. Additionally, previous reports describe afebrile dengue infections, indicating that dengue can occasionally occur without fever, and such atypical presentations may delay diagnosis if clinicians are not aware of this possibility [22].

This case is remarkable because urticaria is the only symptom of dengue fever, which could cause an allergic reaction to be misdiagnosed. It is possible that dengue‐induced immunological dysregulation played a role in the cutaneous symptoms because dengue virus infection can cause mast cell activation and histamine release [9]. Therefore, delayed detection may raise the risk of consequences for patients with undiagnosed dengue; it is imperative to identify such atypical cases for early diagnosis and proper care including monitoring, reassurance, outpatient observation, and supportive therapy such as adequate hydration and symptomatic relief [2].

From a clinical perspective, this case emphasizes the necessity of treating patients with unexplained acute urticaria in endemic areas with heightened suspicion of dengue virus infection. In these situations, routine serological testing for dengue should be considered to guarantee prompt diagnosis and avoid sequelae [4]. Furthermore, this case highlights the importance of a conservative approach in dengue cases with mild symptoms, as our patient fully recovered with antihistamines and supportive treatment. This finding cannot be applied to all dengue patients who present with urticaria, as with any one case report. More research involving bigger patient cohorts is required to correlate dengue virus infection to urticaria and identify its precise pathophysiological pathways [23]. Future research should use molecular diagnostics like RT‐PCR to determine viral etiology with higher specificity, even if our patient had a positive IgM test for dengue [24, 25]. To better understand the broader processes of viral exanthem, more research should concentrate on the immunological pathways implicated in dengue‐associated urticaria. Large‐scale epidemiological research may also ascertain the actual prevalence of urticaria in dengue cases, which would improve clinical management and diagnostic techniques.

Conclusion

This finding shows that dengue virus infection can only appear with acute urticaria, stressing the necessity of detecting unusual dermatological signs. Early recognition of dengue in patients with unexplained urticaria may result in a rapid diagnosis and effective care, eventually improving patient outcomes.
