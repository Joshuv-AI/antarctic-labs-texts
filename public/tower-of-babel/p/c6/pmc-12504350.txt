Metabolic Reprogramming in Heart Failure: From Energy Starvation to Therapeutic Targets
Authors: Mariam Meddeb, Joseph R Goldenberg, Sydney C Jenkin, Virginia S Hahn
Published: 2025

Opinion Statement

Promising therapies that induce metabolic reprogramming include those that augment substrate availability, alter substrate utilization, and improve mitochondrial quality and function. Of note, good old exercise and lifestyle modification achieve many of these aims, which should be stressed to patients with heart failure. Therapies that augment fatty acid utilization appear to yield significant benefits in myocardial performance despite concerns about oxygen demand. Other strategies include ketone supplementation, epigenetic targeting of genes that augment metabolic reprogramming, and therapies to improve mitochondrial biogenesis and dynamics. Several of these therapies are currently being studied in human trials. Furthermore, several current therapies lead to metabolic reprogramming, including SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, and device-based therapies such as cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and left ventricular assist devices (LVADs). How exactly these therapies induce metabolic reprogramming deserves further study.

Introduction

While much attention has been devoted to the maladaptive aspects of metabolic remodeling, a growing body of human evidence suggests that the heart is capable of adaptive metabolic reprogramming that can enhance energy efficiency, preserve contractile performance, and even reverse pathological remodeling. This review aims to summarize the literature regarding abnormalities in cardiac metabolism in heart failure (both preserved ejection fraction and reduced ejection fraction) and propose solutions for metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 1). In the healthy adult heart, fatty acids (FAs) are the predominant energy substrate, while glucose, lactate, ketones, and amino acids provide metabolic flexibility. In HF, this balance is altered. Traditionally, shifts in substrate utilization have been viewed as maladaptive; however, newer studies show that the heart retains metabolic flexibility and metabolic shifts may compensate for energetic deficits and enhance myocardial performance. Key to this beneficial reprogramming is matching substrate supply to mitochondrial oxidative capacity, and supporting efficient ATP generation without overwhelming intermediary metabolic pathways. Strategies for metabolic reprogramming include augmentation of substrate availability, optimization of substrate utilization, and improvements in mitochondrial quality and function. Furthermore, several current therapies lead to metabolic reprogramming, including device-based therapies such as cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and left ventricular assist devices (LVADs).

Abnormalities in Cardiac Substrate and Energy Metabolism in the Failing Heart

The heart has a high energetic demand to sustain continuous contraction-relaxation cycles, consuming about 15–20 times its own weight in ATP daily [1]. Intracellular ATP concentration is low, ~ 5–10 mmol/g wet weight, and cardiac ATP must be continuously renewed. End-stage heart failure (HF) is associated with lower concentrations of adenylate purine nucleotides [2] and ~ 30% reduction in total ATP content [3]. ATP is replenished either through high-energy phosphate transfer from phosphocreatine (PCr) or by de novo synthesis through substrate-level oxidation. However, PCr content drops by ~ 50% in the failing heart [4], resulting in a reduced PCr/ATP ratio - a key indicator of diminished myocardial energy reserves. This energy-deficient state is reflected by a consistently lowered PCr/ATP ratio in both HFrEF [5, 6], and HFpEF [7, 8].

In addition to lessened energy reserves, several studies demonstrate de novo ATP production via oxidative metabolism is also impaired in the failing heart. For substrate level ATP production, the healthy heart mostly relies on oxidative metabolism with > 70–80% coming from carbon-rich fatty acids, and a minority coming from glucose, ketones, amino acids, and lactate [9, 10]. Chronic HF is marked by a shift in substrates used for energy production. In HFrEF, analysis of coronary sinus blood sampling and myocardial tissue suggest a shift away from the oxidation of fatty acids toward increased glycolysis, albeit uncoupled from complete glucose oxidation. This is accompanied by reduced pyruvate oxidation, enhanced anaplerotic flux to replenish TCA cycle intermediates, and increased myocardial uptake of lactate and ketone bodies [9, 11, 12]. Although there was a relative reduction in fatty acid utilization in HFrEF, fatty acids remained the predominant source of ATP generation [9]. This suggests therapeutic potential for augmentation of fatty acid oxidation. Fatty acids yield more ATP per mole than glucose, though at a higher oxygen cost (lower ratio of ATP molecules produced per molecular oxygen consumed), which is manageable for a healthy heart with normal oxygenation [13]. The shift toward glucose and ketone metabolism in HFrEF may initially be adaptive to preserve oxygen consumption but results in approximately 3.5-fold less ATP per mole of substrate oxidized [13].

Comparable studies of substrate utilization in human HFpEF have historically been limited and metabolic alterations in animal models of HFpEF appear to be more heterogeneous, likely due to differences between models and experimental techniques [14]. Systemic metabolic dysfunction in patients with HFpEF, particularly due to obesity and diabetes, are now commonly part of the HFpEF phenotype and may alter myocardial substrate utilization [15]. Cardiac PET studies in patients without heart failure showed increased fatty acid uptake and usage along with decreased glucose utilization in obese subjects without diabetes versus non-obese subjects [16] and increased fatty acid uptake and oxidation in patients with Type 2 diabetes and obesity versus obese subjects without diabetes [17]. However, there have been several recent advances in our understanding of cardiac substrate metabolism in HFpEF due to increased availability of myocardial tissue and coronary sinus sampling. In obese HFpEF, recent analyses of metabolomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics from myocardial tissue suggest defects in glycolysis and oxidation of fatty acids, glucose, and branched-chain amino acids, along with lower expression of enzymes responsible for oxidative phosphorylation [18–20]. Coronary sinus sampling in HFpEF supported a decline in cardiac uptake of free fatty acids, glucose, and TCA cycle intermediates; an increase in uptake of lactate and pyruvate; and an absence of compensatory extraction of ketones from the circulation [21]. Together these data suggest there are multiple metabolic deficits in HFpEF ripe for therapeutic targeting.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in HF disrupts oxidative phosphorylation, impairs ATP synthesis, alters substrate oxidation pathways, and contributes to the overall myocardial energetic deficit. Recent human tissue electron microscopy studies showed deranged mitochondria ultrastructure appears to be a common phenotype across etiologies of HFrEF [22] and in obesity-related HFpEF [23]. Mitochondrial dynamics also appear to be disordered, with changes in expression of proteins that regulate fission, fusion, and mitophagy leading to abnormal mitochondrial function [24, 25]. Additionally, expression of PGC1-α, a master transcriptional regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, is reduced in HFrEF and fails to keep up with need for new mitochondria [24]. Dysfunctional mitochondria produce more reactive oxygen species, which further exacerbate mitochondrial and cellular abnormalities by promoting lipid peroxidation, damaging mitochondrial DNA, disrupting electron transport chain respiration, and impairing calcium handling [26–28]. Reduced sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium storage and mitochondrial Ca2+ overload compromise redox balance and ATP generation, ultimately impairing excitation–contraction coupling and contractility [28]. Mitochondrial reprogramming also affects energy transfer. While at rest mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation contributes to > 95% of ATP production, ATP synthesis from PCr catalyzed by the creatine kinase (CK) enzyme is about 10x faster providing rapid energy during increased myocardial demand [29]. CK also forms the creatine shuttle which facilitates rapid ATP transport out of the mitochondria to sites of utilization, namely myofibrils [30]. However, the creatine shuttle is impaired in HFrEF patients from lower total creatine stores and reduced activity of the mitochondrial isoform of CK [31]. In a prospective study of NICM patients, impaired cardiac CK enzyme flux was independently predictive of all-cause mortality and HF related events [32]. Similarly, a recent retrospective study of HFrEF patients found those with relatively better cardiac CK flux were more likely to have LV reverse remodeling and improved EF along with lower rates of cardiovascular death or need for advanced heart failure therapies (transplant or left ventricular assist device) [33].

Muscle changes in HF are not limited to cardiac muscle and there is increasing appreciation for a skeletal muscle myopathy in HF, particularly in HFpEF [34]. Skeletal muscle abnormalities occur independently of cardiac output, highlighting an intrinsic skeletal muscle myopathy in HF [34]. Both HFpEF and HFrEF patients exhibit reduced mitochondrial content in skeletal muscle, along with impaired mitochondrial quality control, including decreased expression of mitochondrial fusion proteins [35, 36]. In addition to compromised cardiac output, oxygen availability may be lower in HF patients from reduced skeletal muscle capillary density [37, 38]. A shift in muscle fiber composition from oxidative Type I fibers to glycolytic Type IIb fibers has been observed in both HF phenotypes, further limiting oxidative metabolism [36, 37]. Collectively, these changes contribute to reduced skeletal muscle mitochondrial oxidative capacity in HFrEF and HFpEF [35, 36, 39]. As a consequence, during exercise to fatigue, energy stores in skeletal muscle of HFrEF and HFpEF patients decline faster, recover more slowly after exercise, and are closely linked with exercise intolerance [39]. Given the retained plasticity and regenerative potential of skeletal muscle, targeting these peripheral abnormalities represents a promising yet underexplored therapeutic strategy in HF management [40].

Studying metabolism in HF, including substrate usage and energy transfer, particularly in humans, relies on both noninvasive imaging and invasive sampling techniques. Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) can be used to noninvasively measure fatty acid and glucose metabolism with radiopharmaceuticals; however, its poor temporal and spatial resolution limits quantitation [41]. Positron emission tomography (PET) remains a cornerstone for quantifying myocardial uptake of glucose, fatty acids, and other substrates, using radiolabeled tracers. PET has good temporal resolution allowing for quantification of substrate uptake and utilization, but remains limited due to high cost, complicated image quantification schemes, and need for specialized expertise particularly when utilizing radiopharmaceuticals other than 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) [41]. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) provides noninvasive, ionizing radiation-free molecular quantification using nuclei such as 1H, 13C, 31P, and 23Na [42]. MRS enables assessment of high-energy phosphates (e.g., ATP, phosphocreatine), substrate metabolism (e.g., glucose, lipids, ketones), enzyme flux (e.g., creatine kinase, TCA cycle), and intracellular lipid content [43]. However, MRS is technically demanding, time-intensive, and may require multi-nuclear coils not widely available [44]. Additionally, 13C MRS requires exogenous tracers, though hyperpolarized 13C MRS is emerging as a promising method to overcome signal-to-noise limitations [45]. A major strength of non-invasive imaging-based studies is the evaluation of dynamic cardiac and skeletal muscle metabolic responses during exercise in HF [8, 39].

Invasive techniques such as coronary sinus sampling allow for direct measurement of myocardial arteriovenous metabolite gradients, enabling estimation of relative substrate uptake and oxidation [9, 21]. Myocardial tissue sampling provides additional insight into cardiac metabolism and supports high-throughput “-omics” analyses, which are especially valuable in HFpEF, where animal models have limited translation to human disease [18, 46]. Despite their unique value, direct coronary sinus sampling and tissue biopsies are limited by invasiveness, impracticality of serial measurements, and are static snapshots rather than dynamic flux assessments. Peripheral blood metabolomics are more accessible but poorly reflect myocardial metabolism due to poor correlations between circulating and tissue-level metabolite usage [47]. When necessary, animal models can bridge the gap and allow for more invasive approaches, including radionuclide tracing and serial biopsies, enabling detailed mapping of metabolic pathways. Methodological challenges inherent to studying metabolism, which is fundamentally dynamic, underscore the need for complimentary noninvasive and invasive methodologies to bridge the gap between systemic and myocardial metabolism in both research and clinical practice.

Treatment Options

Therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic reprogramming in heart failure (HF) span three major mechanistic domains: restoration of substrate utilization, regulation of mitochondrial redox state and quality control, and transcriptional activation of genes that promote metabolic remodeling. Furthermore, there are several therapies currently in use that contribute to metabolic reprogramming.

Restoration of Substrate Utilization

Restoration of substrate utilization is a key therapeutic goal in heart failure, aiming at optimizing cardiac energy yield. Early efforts at increasing glucose oxidation, either directly by activating pyruvate dehydrogenase [48] or by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation [49, 50] had initially shown promise in improving cardiac efficiency and function in preclinical models of heart failure, improving cardiac function and contractile reserve. However, while these findings were robust in animal models, data supporting translation to clinical benefit in humans remains controversial. For instance, trimetazidine (a 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase inhibitor) and perhexiline (a CPT-1 inhibitor) are two pharmacologic inhibitors of fatty acid oxidation that shift metabolism towards glucose oxidation. Small studies have shown improvements in ejection fraction, exercise tolerance, and a reduction in HF hospitalizations with trimetazine [51, 52], and an improvement in exercise capacity and cardiac energetics (as measured by the phosphocreatine/ATP ratio and peak VO2 max) with perhexiline [53, 54]. However large heart failure clinical trial outcome data are lacking, and hepatic and neurologic side effects limit their use.

More recently, increasing interest has focused on promoting alternative substrate utilization, particularly ketones bodies. Ketone bodies, such as β-hydroxybutyrate, not only act as high-yield mitochondrial fuels but also exert signaling functions that promote vasodilation and angiogenesis, reduce fibrosis, and modulate gene expression [55]. Ketones are efficiently oxidized by the failing heart and generate more ATP per unit of oxygen consumed compared to fatty acids. Clinical and preclinical studies demonstrate that in HFrEF, myocardial uptake and oxidation of ketones are upregulated, likely as a compensatory adaptation. However, in obesity-related human HFpEF, cardiac ketolysis appears to be impaired, limiting this adaptive response [18]. Recent clinical studies have further underscored the therapeutic potential of ketones. Two weeks of ketone ester (KE) supplementation improved resting and exercise hemodynamics and increased LVEF in patients with HFrEF [56] KE supplementation similarly improved rest and exercise hemodynamics in patients with type 2 diabetes and HFpEF [57]. Unfortunately, KE supplementation did not significantly improve exercise capacity in the Keto-HFpEF trial [58]. Large-scale randomized controlled trials with clinical endpoints are still needed to determine efficacy and safety across HF phenotypes.

Additionally, short-chain fatty acids and medium-chain triglycerides, which bypass carnitine-dependent mitochondrial entry, allowing more rapid oxidation and ATP production, are being investigated in nutritional strategies for HF [59]. A recent landmark study provided strong human evidence that enhancing fatty acid availability via intralipid infusion improved myocardial energetics and function in nonischemic HFrEF patients [60]. Surprisingly, increasing fatty acid oxidation, typically thought to be detrimental due to oxygen inefficiency, actually increased PCr/ATP ratios and stroke work, with no evidence of lactate accumulation or ischemia [61]. Other researchers had previously shown that enhanced fatty acid oxidation such as achieved with overexpression of ACSL1 [62] or silencing of ACC2 [63] improved mitochondrial oxidative capacity and myocardial energetics. These findings challenge the assumption that glucose is the preferred substrate in HF due to limited oxygen availability, underscoring the importance of optimizing substrate utilization.

The Interplay Between Mitochondrial Quality Control and Metabolic Reprogramming

One key aspect of adaptive metabolic reprogramming is the role of mitochondrial quality control, particularly under stress conditions such as heart failure [64]. Blunted mitophagy, specifically in the context of metabolic stress, leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced phosphocreatine/ATP ratio, and increased ROS [65, 66]. While high-fat diet (HFD) alone induces mitophagy and preserves energetics in an animal model, the combination of HFD + L-NAME does not stimulate this protective response and leads to HFpEF. Using human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and mouse models, investigators showed that enhancing fatty acid oxidation via deletion of acetyl-CoA carboxylase 2 reactivates mitophagy, reduces lipid accumulation, and improves cardiac function [66]. Human studies also support the notion that preserved mitochondrial quality control correlates with better mitochondrial structure and function [65]. Overall, targeting mitochondrial quality control pathways may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to reprogram failing myocardium toward improved energetics and function.

Mitochondrial enhancers and regulators are pivotal for cardiac metabolic reprogramming, given altered mitochondrial quality and excess ROS in HF [64]. Elamipretide, a mitochondrially targeted tetrapeptide that binds cardiolipin on the inner mitochondrial membrane, stabilizing the cardiolipin-cytochrome C complex and enhancing electron transfer, was a promising mitochondrial enhancer that showed promising data in preclinical studies [67], but unfortunately failed to translate to significant improvements in LV geometry in a small human trial [68].

Conversely, mitochondrial redox and quality control regulation continues to hold promise. Specifically, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) repletion activates sirtuins, which deacetylate and reactivate mitochondrial enzymes like long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (LCAD), enhancing antioxidant capacity, and promoting mitochondrial quality control and DNA repair pathways through the activation of polyADP-ribose polymerases (PARP) [69]. Such effects are also mimicked by lifestyle interventions, such as aerobic exercise and caloric restriction, which stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis, improve mitochondrial dynamics and elevate NAD⁺ levels, activating sirtuins in the heart and the skeletal muscle [70]. Small clinical trials have demonstrated that oral supplementation of nicotinamide ribose, an orally bioavailable nicotinamide precursor, increases blood NAD⁺ levels in a dose-dependent manner and modulates metabolic pathways in circulating mononuclear cells and skeletal muscle. Notably, nicotinamide ribose also decreased blood pressure and inflammatory cytokines in older adults, though changes in glucose metabolism or muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics were inconsistent across trials [71]. Despite promising preclinical evidence [72] and early safety clinical data, NAD⁺ repletion remains an investigational therapy in heart failure as randomized controlled trials are lacking. As of July 2025, 2 active HF clinical trials are registered on Clinicaltrials.gov testing nicotinamide ribose. One early phase 1 clinical trial has completed enrollment to test the effect of nicotinamide ribose on mitochondrial function, protein and epigenetic modifications in patients with end-stage HF receiving LVAD (NCT04528004) and another phase 2 study is actively enrolling to test the utility of nicotinamide ribose as part of a multiagent strategy to enhance exercise tolerance in patients with HFpEF (NCT04913805). Mitochondrial enhancers have also been an appealing area of research.

Transcriptional Activation of Genes Promoting Metabolic Reprogramming

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) is a transcriptional coactivator and master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism [73]. In HF, PGC-1α is often suppressed, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and energy deficiency. Pharmacologic activation of upstream pathways such as AMPK (such as with metformin), sirtuins (such as with NAD⁺ boosters), and PPARα/δ can upregulate PGC-1α activity. Nuclear receptors like thyroid hormone receptors and estrogen-related receptors (ERRα/γ) also promote mitochondrial gene transcription and are emerging therapeutic targets. In preclinical HF models, small molecules that activate ERR enhance fatty acid metabolism and overall mitochondrial function [74]. Lifestyle interventions are key, as repeated aerobic training increases PGC-1α expression and its beneficial downstream effects [75]. It is unknown if single transcriptional activators are sufficient in human heart failure, or if simultaneous targeting of multiple transcriptional nodes is necessary to achieve sustained metabolic reprogramming, such as through epigenetic modifications. Epigenetic modification is increasingly understood as both a driver and a therapeutic target in HF. Histone deacetylases (HDAC) inhibitors are the most widely studied metabolic epigenetic regulators in cardiovascular research. HDAC inhibitors have been shown to regulate substrate utilization and restore oxidative gene expression and mitochondrial enzyme function [76]. These effects may also be reinforced by exercise-induced epigenetic remodeling, which dynamically alters histone acetylation and DNA methylation patterns in the failing heart [77]. Additionally, Zhang et al. recently showed that METTL4-mediated N6-methyladenine accumulation in mitochondrial DNA is significantly increased in animal models of HF, interfering with the initiation of transcriptional complex assembly, mitochondrial DNA transcriptional stalling, and ultimately mitochondrial dysfunction, with all these deleterious effects being fully mitigated by specific silencing of METTL4 [78]. These data are consistent with the idea that epigenetic changes of mitochondrial targets play a key role in the long-term metabolic reprogramming needed to sustain mitochondrial recovery. Nonetheless, there are no published trials demonstrating the efficacy or safety of HDAC inhibitors or other epigenetic modulators for metabolic reprogramming in human heart failure.

Approved Therapies Targeting Metabolic Reprogramming in Heart Failure

Originally developed for type 2 diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitors have emerged as cornerstone therapies for both HFrEF and HFpEF due to consistent cardiovascular and renal benefits [79]. Mechanistically, SGLT2 inhibitors promote a metabolic shift toward ketone and fatty acid oxidation, enhance mitochondrial efficiency, and reduce glucotoxicity. They activate AMPK, SIRT1/3, and PGC1α pathways, improving autophagy, oxidative balance, and mitochondrial biogenesis [80]. However, the exact mechanisms of the associated metabolic reprogramming are unknown, as many of the above changes are also associated with myocardial recovery.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP1a) induce metabolic reprogramming primarily through indirect effects on cardiac and peripheral metabolism that accompany significant weight loss. GLP1a reduce body weight, improve glycemic control, lower blood pressure, decrease systemic and epicardial adiposity, and reduce inflammation and oxidative stress. These actions collectively improve insulin sensitivity, decrease circulating free fatty acids, and reduce lipotoxicity, which can enhance myocardial substrate utilization and efficiency [81]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that GLP-1a indirectly increase myocardial glucose uptake and oxidation, thereby enhancing cardiac energy efficiency and mitochondrial function [82]. Clinical and translational studies further support that GLP1a improve myocardial energy metabolism, reduce cardiac oxidative stress, and attenuate adverse remodeling, including reductions in left ventricular mass and left atrial volume, especially in obese HFpEF [81]. Additionally, GLP1a agents may improve endothelial function and microvascular blood flow [83], thereby improving fuel and oxygen delivery to cardiac and skeletal muscle. GLP1a and combined GLP1 / GIP agonists have shown a benefit in HFpEF clinical trials, while trials in HFrEF are needed.

Cardiac resynchronization therapy, which restores synchronized ventricular contraction in patients with dyssynchrony due to conduction delay, not only improves mechanical efficiency but also induces favorable metabolic remodeling. In a recent study, investigators used direct coronary flow and arteriovenous measurements (including from the coronary sinus) to ascertain the acute metabolic impact of CRT and its impact on reverse remodeling [61]. First, participants were either given insulin/glucose or intralipid infusion, then baseline and post-CRT measurements were obtained. In the insulin/glucose infusion group, glucose was the predominant substrate taken up by the heart, while free fatty acid uptake increased acutely after CRT. In the intralipid infusion group, free fatty acid uptake was already higher than the insulin/glucose group, consistent with other studies of retained metabolic flexibility of the failing heart. In the intralipid infusion group, CRT enhanced myocardial uptake of ketone bodies, providing another alternative fuel source. These changes correlated with future echocardiographic and hemodynamic improvements, suggesting that metabolic recovery may precede reverse remodeling.

Left ventricular assist device (LVAD) therapy, used as a bridge to transplantation or destination therapy in end-stage heart failure, is increasingly recognized for its capacity to induce beneficial cardiac remodeling, potentially halting or even reversing disease progression, enabling device decommissioning in select cases [84]. Histological and transcriptomic analysis of myocardium pre- and post-LVAD support shows partial normalization of mitochondrial structure, increased oxidative phosphorylation gene expression, and upregulation of fatty acid oxidation enzymes [85]. However, HF-associated changes in DNA methylation are minimally reversed with LVAD implantation, which may be an epigenetic barrier that limits full metabolic reprogramming [86]. Recently, using quantitative proteomic and metabolomic analyses of human HFrEF and LVAD myocardial tissue coupled with coronary sinus sampling, investigators observed normalization of key pathways involved in energy metabolism, mitochondrial function, and redox homeostasis after LVAD therapy, along with reduced excretion of unused acylcarnitines with LVAD, suggesting increased FA oxidation in hearts unloaded with LVADs [87]. These molecular findings support the hypothesis that chronic mechanical unloading creates a myocardial environment permissive to reverse metabolic remodeling. Importantly, while full recovery allowing for device explantation remains rare, insights from these recent studies support the idea that even partial molecular and structural recovery may significantly enhance myocardial function.

Conclusions

In conclusion, recognition that metabolic reprogramming in HF is not universally detrimental, but may be adaptive, opens new avenues for therapy. Far from being a passive response to injury, it may represent a strategic, reversible adjustment that can be harnessed therapeutically to restore cardiac function. The failure of agents forcing substrate shifts in an already metabolically impaired heart highlights the need for more nuanced metabolic interventions. Furthermore, strategies are needed to improve metabolic flexibility and overall mitochondrial health. Future research should focus on identifying patient subsets most likely to benefit from specific metabolic interventions, using metabolic imaging, myocardial tissue studies, and circulating metabolomic signatures. A precision-medicine approach, targeting the right metabolic pathway in the right patient, may yield the greatest therapeutic gains.
