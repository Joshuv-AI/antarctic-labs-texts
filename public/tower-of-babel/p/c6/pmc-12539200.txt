Development and validation of a nomogram for predicting PICC catheter-related bloodstream infection among patients with hematologic malignancies
Authors: Qian Guo, Yuting Liu, Xiaoli Zhu, Yanting Gao, You Zhou, Xiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo identify risk factors for PICC-related bloodstream infections (PICC-CRBSI) in patients with hematologic malignancies and to develop and validate a predictive nomogram for clinical risk assessment.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 764 patients with hematologic malignancy who had PICC from a tertiary center (2021–2024). LASSO regression identified key predictors after addressing class imbalance via SMOTE-NC in the training set (n = 534). A multivariate logistic regression was used to construct a predictive nomogram, subsequently validated in a validation set (n = 230). Model performance was assessed by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves with bootstrap resampling, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsIn our cohort, the occurrence of PICC-CRBSI infections was 6.02% (46/764) and Gram-negative bacteria was the major causative pathogen. Multivariate regression analysis showed that History of diabetes, Age, Time of PICC placement, PICC insertion attempts, Catheterized Diameter, Catheterized vein, ANC, ALC and D-dimer were independent risk factors associated with PICC-CRBSI in hematologic malignancy patients. Our nomogram model demonstrated a good calibration and discrimination in both training and validation sets, with AUC values of 0.883 and 0.822, The DCA suggested potential clinical utility.ConclusionThis validated nomogram integrates patient, catheter, and biomarker-specific factors to individualize PICC-CRBSI risk stratification in hematologic malignancies, potentially guiding targeted prevention strategies.

Introduction

Patients with hematologic malignancies, encompassing conditions such as leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma, face complex and often protracted treatment regimens involving intensive chemotherapy, stem cell transplantation, and prolonged supportive care [1, 2]. A reliable and durable venous access option is indispensable for the successful delivery of these therapies, including chemotherapy, blood products, antibiotics, and nutritional support [3]. Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) have emerged as a cornerstone for long-term venous access in this population. PICCs offer significant advantages over conventional central venous catheters, including reduced insertion-related risks (such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage), extended indwelling duration, cost-effectiveness, and the safe administration of vesicant chemotherapeutics, hyperosmolar solutions, and other cytotoxic agents, thereby minimizing the risk of extravasation-related vascular injury and tissue necrosis [1, 4–7]. However, the benefits of PICCs use are counterbalanced by the significant risk of complications, among which catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) stands out as one of the most frequent and devastating [8].

CRBSI is microbiologically defined as concordant bacteremia or fungemia from peripheral and catheter-tip cultures, occurring during catheterization or ≤ 48 h post-removal, with no other identifiable infection source [9–11]. The burden of CRBSI is particularly severe in patients with hematologic malignancies, reaching an incidence of 20.3–22.0 episodes/1000 neutropenic days [12–14], significantly higher than rates observed in solid tumors. CRBSI poses a grave threat in this population, frequently necessitating unplanned catheter removal, disrupting critical anti-neoplastic treatment schedules, precipitating sepsis and septic shock, prolonging hospital stays, substantially escalating healthcare costs, and significantly contributing to morbidity and mortality [15–20]. This unique susceptibility stems fundamentally from the underlying pathophysiology of hematologic malignancies and their treatments, characterized by severe and sustained immunosuppression and neutropenia, frequent breaches in mucosal barriers, and the necessity for repeated transfusions and chemotherapy infusions-collectively creating an environment highly conducive to bloodstream infections [17–20]. This substantial risk underscores the imperative for rigorous infection control protocols to optimize the safe utility of PICCs in this exceptionally vulnerable patient group.

Although several risk prediction models for CRBSI have been developed, the majority focus on general inpatients, critically ill, hemodialysis, and solid tumors populations [21–24]. However, these models are poorly suited for patients with hematologic malignancies due to several critical limitations. For instance, Erica Herc et al. [25] constructed the MPC model to identify risk factors for PICC- CLABSI(Central-line-associated bloodstream infection); however, this model showed only moderate discriminatory power (AUC: 0.67–0.77) and ignored demographic statistical characteristics (e.g., Age) and other idiosyncratic factors that may significantly influence clinical outcomes. Similarly, the nomograms construct of Wang et al. [26] and Tang et al. [27], although incorporating certain biomarkers (e.g., albumin < 30 g/L, platelets<30 × 10⁹/L, total bilirubin>34.2 µmol/L), did not incorporate crucial catheter-specific parameters (e.g., Time of PICC placement, Catheterized vein). Critically, existing models neglect the unique pathophysiology of hematologic malignancies, where synergistic effects of cytotoxic therapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and prolonged cytopenias create a distinct, high-risk milieu for CRBSI [28]. Consequently, there is a compelling need for a validated, clinically practical tool designed specifically to stratify the individual risk of PICC-CRBSI in patients with hematologic malignancies.

To address this gap, this study aimed to develop and validate a comprehensive nomogram for predicting the risk of PICC-CRBSI specifically among patients with hematologic malignancies.

Materials and methods

Participants

We retrospectively included patients with hematologic malignancies undergoing PICC catheterization at a tertiary medical center in Hangzhou, China, from January 2021 to December 2024. Data related to patient demographics, diseases characteristics and treatments were extracted from electronic health records (Hospital Information System, HIS). The inclusion criteria of the study were: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Hospitalized patients diagnosed with hematological malignancies and treated with chemotherapy; (3) Complete electronic medical record including demographic profile, treatment regimens, and laboratory parameters; (4) PICC placement time was ≥ 48 h. The exclusion criteria were: (1) PICC-CRBSI secondary to other sites, such as pulmonary infection, abdominal infection, etc.; (2) Hematopoietic stem cell donors.

In this study, patients with central venous catheters (CVCs) were not included. In clinical practice, PICCs are generally preferred in patients with hematologic malignancies due to their lower platelet count requirements, longer dwell times, and greater feasibility for chemotherapy administration. By contrast, CVCs often require a platelet threshold >50 × 10⁹/L, are associated with higher bleeding and thrombosis risks, and are less suitable for long-term use [29–31]. Furthermore, cervical lymphadenopathy in lymphoma patients frequently complicates CVC insertion. Based on these considerations, our study focused specifically on PICCs rather than CVCs.

PICC insertion and maintenance

The PICCs used in this study were manufactured by Bard (Covington, GA) and were primarily a model 4-French single-lumen catheter (PowerPICC® Solo² Catheter; No.7617405) and a model 5-French dual-lumen catheter (PowerPICC® Dual-Lumen Catheter; No.3275118). All PICC placement and maintenance was performed in strict accordance with evidence-based best practice guidelines [32].

Study endpoints and definitions

The data collection period began 48 h post-PICC placement. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of PICC-CRBSI, defined as the number of days from PICC placement to catheter removal or study cutoff in uninfected individuals. Censored events were defined as PICC removal for non-CRBSI reasons, loss to follow-up, or failure to complete the data collection period.

Vascular catheter-related infections included localized vascular catheter infections and CRBSI, with the former presenting only inflammatory manifestations such as redness, swelling, heat, pain, and exudation, and CRBSI presenting systemic manifestations of infection in addition to local manifestations. To diagnose ‘PICC-CRBSI,’ the same microorganism must be isolated from both the peripheral blood culture and the catheter tip, or there must be a differential time to positivity [33].The time to positivity was defined by a central line peripheral blood culture turning positive at least 2 h before the peripheral blood culture drawn at the same time [34, 35].

Data collection

All data were extracted from the HIS. A comprehensive literature review and group meetings with physicians and nurses were conducted before we decided on potential risk variables [28, 33, 36]. Three independent researchers (GQ, ZXL, and ZX) extracted, reviewed, and validated data from electronic medical records. The following data were collected (1) general patient information (Gender, Age, Body mass index [BMI], Type of disease, Whether hematopoietic stem cell transplantation[HSCT], History of diabetes, History of hypertension, and Carrying Hepatitis B Virus); (2) information on PICC placement (History of PICC placement, Catheter diameter, PICC insertion attempts, Catheterized Vein, and Time of PICC placement); and (3) laboratory examination (48 hours prior to placement of White Blood Cells [WBC], Platelets [PLT], Absolute neutrophil count [ANC], Absolute lymphocyte count [ALC] and D-dimer).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (Zhejiang Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine) (ref.2025-KLS-109-01). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and observational nature of the study. The study was conducted according to the standards set by the Declaration of Helsinki, and patient information was anonymized and de-identified before analysis.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) and R (version 4.5.1) software. Categorical variables were expressed as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test when the cell expected frequency was ≥ 5 and the Fisher exact test if the cell expected frequency was<5. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables that followed a normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test; continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution were reported as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

To address class imbalance (PICC-CRBSI prevalence: 6.02%), we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique for Nominal and Continuous features (SMOTE-NC) exclusively to the training cohort (n = 534), while preserving the natural outcome distribution in the validation set (n = 230). This strict segregation prevents data leakage and aligns with TRIPOD guidelines [37]. SMOTE-NC—an advanced resampling method—generates synthetic minority-class samples by interpolating between nearest neighbors using modified Euclidean distance metrics, effectively handling datasets with mixed categorical and continuous variables [38–41]. We selected this approach over cost-sensitive learning or ensemble methods due to its validated efficacy in medical prediction studies [40, 41]and suitability for our feature typology.

LASSO regression was selected for identifying potential characteristics of PICC-CRBSI occurrence in hematological oncology patients and the optimal λ value was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. Subsequently, the potential predictors identified through lasso regression techniques were used to build a predictive model using multivariate stepwise logistic regression, which eliminated highly correlated factors. We further used the statistically significant predictors obtained from stepwise logistic regression to construct a nomogram, which was created using the R package ‘regplot’, which can directly generate logistic regression-based results. The discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility of the model were assessed using subject work characteristics (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).

As a result, a total of 764 patients with hematologic neoplasms met the inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Incidence of PICC-CRBSI among hematologic malignancies

Among 46 patients diagnosed with PICC-CRBSI, a total of 98 microbial isolates were identified (Table 2). Gram-negative bacteria predominated (57.14%, 56/98), with Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12.24%) and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (12.24%) being the most frequently isolated species. Gram-positive bacteria accounted for 24.49% (24/98) of the isolates, primarily represented by Staphylococcus hominis (4.08%) and Enterococcus faecium (4.08%). In contrast, fungal infections (6.12%,6/98) and unclassified pathogens (12.24%,12/98) were less prevalent (Fig. 2A). Monomicrobial infections involving a single pathogen were observed in 19 patients. In contrast, polymicrobial infections (i.e., co-infections with two or more pathogens) were detected in 27 cases, including one case with eight distinct bacterial species (Fig. 2B).

Results of LASSO regression and multivariate analyses

A cross-validation error plot was generated for the LASSO regression (Fig. 3A). As shown by the vertical dashed line in Fig. 3B, nine candidate variables were retained when the cross-validation error reached one standard error; these candidates were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. The final multivariate analysis identified nine independent predictors of PICC-CRBSI (Table 3): History of diabetes (OR = 8.204, 95% CI: 5.115–13.520, P < 0.001), Age (per year increase: OR = 0.977, 95% CI: 0.965–0.988, P < 0.001), Time of PICC placement > 60 days (OR = 6.213, 95% CI: 3.668–10.933, P < 0.001), ≥ 2 PICC insertion attempts (OR = 2.917, 95% CI: 1.803–4.778, P < 0.001), Dual-lumen catheter (OR = 2.833, 95% CI: 1.901–4.270, P < 0.001), Catheterized vein — Cephalic vein (vs. Basilic vein) (OR = 7.288, 95% CI: 3.168–18.533, P < 0.001; Brachial vein vs. Basilic vein was not significant: OR = 0.874, 95% CI: 0.601–1.266, P = 0.478), ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹/L (OR = 1.864, 95% CI: 1.294–2.701, P < 0.001), ALC < 1.0 × 10⁹/L (OR = 5.476, 95% CI: 3.619–8.458, P < 0.001), and D-dimer ≥ 0.5 mg/L (OR = 7.029, 95% CI: 4.515–11.203, P < 0.001). These results indicate that both patient-level factors (e.g., diabetes, immune cell counts, coagulation markers) and catheter-related factors (e.g., dwell time, insertion attempts, lumen number, insertion site) independently contribute to PICC-CRBSI risk in patients with hematologic malignancies (Table 3).

Development of the nomogram

Based on the nine independent predictors identified in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, a nomogram was constructed to estimate the individualized risk of PICC-CRBSI in patients with hematologic malignancies. The predictors incorporated into the model were: History of diabetes (yes/no), Age (Continuous), Time of PICC placement (< 60 days vs. ≥60 days), PICC insertion attempts (1attempt vs. ≥2 attempts), Catheter diameter (single-lumen vs. dual-lumen), Catheterized vein (Basilic vein, Brachial vein, or Cephalic vein), ANC (< 1.5 × 10⁹/L vs. ≥1.5 × 10⁹/L), ALC (< 1.0 × 10⁹/L vs. ≥1.0 × 10⁹/L), and D-dimer (< 0.5 mg/L vs. ≥0.5 mg/L).As shown in Fig. 4A, each variable was assigned a corresponding point value according to its regression coefficient, and the sum of these points generated a total risk score, which was then mapped to the predicted probability of developing PICC-CRBSI. To facilitate clinical application, the assigned point values for each predictor are detailed in Table 4. The higher the total score, the greater the likelihood of PICC-CRBSI. An illustrative example of nomogram application is provided in Fig. 4B, demonstrating how individual patient profiles can be translated into risk probabilities for clinical decision-making.

Performance of the CRBSI prediction nomogram

The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.883 (95% CI: 0.863–0.903) in the training cohort (Fig. 5A) and 0.822 (95% CI: 0.719–0.924) in the validation cohort (Fig. 5B). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between nomogram-predicted probabilities and observed outcomes across both cohorts (Figs. 5C, D). The DCA revealed potential for clinical utility, with the net benefit curve exceeding the extreme lines over a threshold probability range of 0–1 in the derivation and validation datasets (Figs. 5E, F).

Discussion

This study developed and validated a novel nomogram integrating nine independent predictors: History of diabetes, Age, Time of PICC placement, PICC insertion attempts, Catheter diameter, Catheterized vein, ANC, ALC, and D-dimer—to stratify the risk of PICC-CRBSI among patients with hematological malignancies. The model demonstrated strong discrimination and calibration in both training and validation cohorts, with an AUC of 0.883 and 0.822, respectively. Importantly, our nomogram addresses key limitations of prior CRBSI prediction tools by incorporating catheter-specific parameters, hematology-relevant biomarkers, and host-specific immune vulnerabilities, thereby providing a more tailored risk assessment for this unique patient population.

Microbial epidemiology

The microbial spectrum observed in our cohort underscores the distinctive vulnerability of hematologic malignancy patients. Although Gram-positive bacteria have historically dominated CRBSI etiology [42], we found a predominance of Gram-negative bacteria (57.1%), with Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia as the most frequent isolates. This finding is consistent with recent evidence indicating a rising incidence of Gram-negative bacteria CRBSI (10~36%) in immunocompromised populations [43–45]. Alarmingly, Multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens may constitute 50% of these cases [46], substantially compromising therapeutic efficacy and clinical outcomes [47, 48]. As demonstrated by Calò et al. [49], both timely catheter removal and adequate empiric antimicrobial therapy are protective against mortality, reinforcing the clinical imperative for early recognition, environmental infection control, and antimicrobial stewardship in hematology units.

Mechanistic insights into predictors

In multivariable analysis, nine factors were independently associated with PICC-CRBSI: history of diabetes (OR = 8.204), younger age (per-year OR = 0.977), time of PICC placement ≥ 60 days (OR = 6.213), ≥ 2 PICC insertion attempts (OR = 2.917), dual-lumen catheter (OR = 2.833), cephalic vein insertion (vs. basilic; OR = 7.288), ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹/L (OR = 1.864), ALC < 1.0 × 10⁹/L (OR = 5.476), and D-dimer ≥ 0.5 mg/L (OR = 7.029). These predictors can be broadly categorized into device- and procedure-related factors, host immune competence, and thrombo-inflammatory status.

First, device and procedure-related parameters emerged as major contributors to infection risk. Prolonged dwell time was one of the strongest predictors, consistent with reports that more than 70% of exit-site infections occur beyond 30 days post-insertion, highlighting that risk accrues primarily from long-term maintenance rather than the insertion procedure itself [50].Extended indwelling duration also acts synergistically with repeated insertion attempts, both of which cause endothelial disruption, fibrin sheath formation, and vascular injury, thereby creating a favorable substrate for microbial adherence and colonization [51]. Device configuration further influenced outcomes: dual-lumen catheters were associated with nearly a threefold higher risk of CRBSI, plausibly due to increased internal surface area and more frequent hub manipulation, both of which are established entry points for microbial contamination. Vein selection also proved critical; Cephalic vein cannulation carried a markedly higher risk compared with the Basilic vein, consistent with its smaller caliber, tortuous course, and more challenging catheter advancement, which predispose to suboptimal tip positioning and local thrombosis. By contrast, the Basilic vein offers anatomical advantages—including larger diameter, straighter trajectory, and greater distance from neurovascular structures—that are associated with higher procedural success and lower complication rates [50, 52–56].

Second, Host-level vulnerabilities significantly potentiated catheter-related risks. Diabetes mellitus conferred the greatest single effect (OR = 8.204), reflecting its multifactorial impact on infection susceptibility, including impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytosis, diminished microvascular perfusion, and hyperglycemia-induced microbial overgrowth and skin colonization [57–60]. Hematologic immunosuppression was also central: both severe lymphocytopenia (ALC < 1.0 × 10⁹/L) and neutropenia (ANC < 1.5 × 10⁹/L) independently increased PICC-CRBSI risk, underscoring the essential roles of both adaptive and innate immune pathways in preventing microbial persistence.

Third, abnormalities in coagulation and systemic inflammation were closely linked to infection risk, with elevated D-dimer (≥ 0.5 mg/L) emerging as a strong independent predictor; as an established marker of hypercoagulability and subclinical inflammation, heightened D-dimer signifies the potential for nascent catheter-associated thrombus formation, likely reflecting underlying catheter-related thrombus formation and fibrin sheath development, which promote microbial adherence and shield biofilms from host defenses and antimicrobial agents [51, 61, 62].

Finally, the inverse association between age and CRBSI (per-year OR < 1) requires cautious interpretation. Rather than reflecting a true protective effect of older age, this relationship may be confounded by treatment intensity: younger patients are more likely to undergo aggressive induction chemotherapy, allogeneic transplantation, or salvage regimens that induce profound cytopenias and necessitate prolonged or repeated central venous access. Larger multicenter studies with stratified analyses are warranted to clarify this relationship.

Overall, these mechanistic insights highlight the interplay of vascular injury, immune suppression, and thrombosis in driving PICC-CRBSI risk and suggest actionable targets for prevention.

Disease type and transplantation-related considerations

Clinically. Our findings elucidate hematologic malignancy-specific vulnerabilities absent in general ICU or solid tumor CRBSI models [21–24]. In theory, acute leukemias, which are typically managed with more intensive chemotherapy regimens, confer deeper and more sustained marrow suppression compared with lymphomas, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection. Similarly, among transplant recipients, the degree of immunosuppression varies by modality: allogeneic HSCT generally entails the most profound immune dysfunction (due to conditioning intensity and graft-versus-host disease prophylaxis), followed by autologous HSCT, with CAR-T therapy generally resulting in comparatively shorter-term immune suppression (primarily lymphodepletion with fludarabine/cyclophosphamide). Physiologically, these gradations would be expected to translate into differential CRBSI risk.

In our cohort, however, no statistically significant association was observed between disease type (e.g., leukemia vs. lymphoma) or HSCT and the incidence of PICC-CRBSI. This is likely attributable to the limited sample size within these subgroups, as well as overlapping risk pathways already captured by our retained predictors (e.g., ANC, ALC, D-dimer). Nevertheless, prior studies have demonstrated higher infection risks in allo-HSCT recipients compared to auto-HSCT, largely due to more intensive conditioning regimens and graft-versus-host disease–related immunosuppression [63, 64]. Similarly, acute leukemias undergoing induction chemotherapy have been consistently associated with profound and prolonged neutropenia, predisposing to invasive infections [28]. While our model did not retain these variables, their well-established biological relevance suggests that future large-scale, multi-center studies should explicitly examine the incremental prognostic value of disease subtype and transplantation modality.

Our findings hold direct implications for clinical practice. First, preferential use of the basilic vein for PICC placement should be emphasized to minimize infection risk [50, 56]. Second, patients with diabetes and severe cytopenias warrant heightened surveillance, with routine monitoring of biomarkers such as D-dimer to guide prophylactic interventions (e.g., early thrombus detection, anticoagulation strategies). Third, training programs for nurses on long-term catheter care have been shown to significantly reduce infection rates [65] and should be integrated into standard care. Ultimately, the developed nomogram will provide clinicians with an evidence-based, individualized risk assessment at the bedside, facilitating targeted preventive interventions such as enhanced catheter care protocols, intensified monitoring, or even alternative access strategies for those at highest risk.

Limitations

Despite its robust validation, this study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design may restrict generalizability, and external validation across diverse healthcare settings is warranted to confirm broader applicability. In addition, our prediction model was specifically developed and validated for patients with Hematologic Malignancies receiving PICCs, using Bard (Covington, GA) 4-French single-lumen PowerPICC® Solo² Catheters (No. 7617405) and 5-French dual-lumen PowerPICC® Dual-Lumen Catheters (No. 3275118), with placement and maintenance performed according to evidence-based best practice guidelines. Therefore, the applicability of our model to other types of central venous access devices, such as tunneled catheters or Powerlines, cannot be assumed. Second, biomarker measurements (e.g., ALC, D-dimer) were obtained pre-insertion and may not reflect dynamic changes during catheter dwell time. Third, unmeasured confounders—such as detailed chemotherapy regimens, antibiotic prophylaxis, or neutrophil recovery kinetics-were not incorporated, potentially omitting influential variables. Moreover, our study was unable to comprehensively assess the depth and duration of neutropenia as independent risk factors for PICC-CRBSI due to retrospective data constraints. Clarifying these effects requires more granular longitudinal monitoring in future investigations. Fourth, due to the heterogeneity of chemotherapy regimens and frequent adjustments based on individual patient conditions, the impact of specific chemotherapy agents on PICC-CRBSI risk could not be assessed. Certain agents may exert greater mucosal or tissue toxicity and thus influence infection risk, but this level of detail was beyond the scope of the current retrospective study and warrants further investigation in future research. Fifth, the study did not separately analyze patients receiving immunotherapies. As immunotherapies are generally less cytotoxic than conventional chemotherapy, their impact on PICC-CRBSI risk may differ and warrants further investigation in future studies. Sixth, the study did not assess the antimicrobial resistance patterns of Gram-negative pathogens, including multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains, which have been increasingly recognized as emerging causes of PICC-CRBSI. Finally, prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the nomogram’s real-world utility and confirm its impact on clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This study established and validated a practical nomogram integrating patient comorbidities (History of diabetes), demographic factors (Age), catheter-related variables (Time of PICC placement, PICC insertion attempts, Catheter Diameter, Catheterized vein), and disease-specific biomarkers (ANC, ALC and D-dimer) to predict PICC-CRBSI in hematologic malignancy patients. Its methodological rigor, leveraging SMOTE-NC to address class imbalance and LASSO regression for feature selection, ensures robustness for this high-risk population. This tool may provide clinicians with a practical means to stratify patients with hematologic malignancies into differential risk categories, guiding personalized surveillance and preemptive strategies (e.g., intensified catheter care protocols, early diagnostic evaluation) to mitigate PICC-CRBSI-associated morbidity and mortality in hematologic oncology practice.
