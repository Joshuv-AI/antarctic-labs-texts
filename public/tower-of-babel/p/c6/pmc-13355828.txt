Disturbance intensity shapes universal and context-dependent functional traits in anaerobic microbiomes
Authors: Abeed F Mohidin, Soheil A Neshat, Ezequiel Santillan, Stefan Wuertz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrait-based frameworks, notably Grime's competitor–stress-tolerant–ruderal theory, offer a powerful lens for predicting how environmental fluctuations govern community structure. Yet, classical ecological models assume environments combining extreme stress and intense disturbance are non-viable for sustained colonisation, leaving a critical bottleneck in our ability to predict how microbial systems withstand compounded operational pressures. This gap severely hinders the predictive management of engineered microbiomes critical for global waste-to-energy conversion. Here we extend the application of classic ecological frameworks by demonstrating that anaerobic digester microbiomes deploy distinct, predictable life-history strategies across a 182-day compounded gradient of biomass turnover and organic loading. High-intensity single-event disturbances drive severe volatile fatty acid accumulation (propionate reaching 2,955 mg L−1), selectively shifting the microbiome toward stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal strategies. Traits associated with ribosome function, molecular chaperones, and enzymatic reactive oxygen species detoxification were particularly enriched under highly disturbed conditions. Conversely, intermediate regimes were associated with ruderal strategies that prioritise rapid growth over resource-uptake efficiency, dropping total chemical oxygen demand removal to 41%. Cross-system comparisons encompassing anaerobic digestion, activated sludge, and soil ecosystems, revealed both universal and context-dependent ecological traits. Survival-associated traits linked to cell maintenance and repair, protective mechanisms, and cell motility were universally associated with stress-tolerant or ruderal strategies across ecosystems, whereas nutrient transport and metabolic traits exhibited greater context dependency. These insights establish a gene-resolved framework that reconciles microbial trait selection with ecological theory, providing a roadmap to engineer microbiome resilience against process failures.Keywords: Community structure, Grime's CSR life-history strategies, Trait-based approach, Metagenome-assembled genomes, Disturbance intensity, Stress, Anaerobic digestion

Introduction

Ecological studies pivoting on functional attributes have gained traction as a complementary approach to taxonomy-based methods, emphasising functional traits that drive ecological processes and provide a better understanding of ecological strategies in response to environmental changes. Functional traits, defined as morphological, physiological, or phenotypic attributes intrinsic to each species that influence its growth, survival, or reproduction [1,2], form the basis of trait-based frameworks such as Grime's competitor–stress-tolerant–ruderal (CSR) theory [3]. The CSR framework classifies organisms into three primary ecological strategies: competitors (C)—organisms that maximise resource acquisition in undisturbed, productive environments; stress-tolerants (S)—organisms that survive in persistently adverse environments; and ruderals (R)—organisms that thrive in frequently disturbed environments. Since the CSR framework is structured around gradients of stress and disturbance, it is important to distinguish these concepts. Stress refers to persistent adverse environmental conditions that constrain microbial function [[4], [5], [6]], whereas a disturbance is a temporal event that reshapes community structure by altering resources, substrate availability, or the physical environment [7,8]. Although CSR was originally developed for plant communities, its underlying emphasis on traits related to competition, stress-tolerance, and disturbance response has motivated recent efforts to explore its relevance to microbial systems. However, the theory does not explicitly make predictions for environments that combine high stress and high disturbance, which are commonly considered non-viable for plants [9]. This rationale may not hold for microorganisms, which can persist under extreme conditions. The CSR framework also offers an alternative to the two-dimensional r/K selection theory, which primarily focuses on reproductive strategies in animals experiencing stable and unstable environments [6,10]. Subsequent work introduced an additional ‘A’ (adversity) strategist category to this framework, broadly analogous to CSR's stress-tolerant strategy [11], indicating that r/K theory can be extended to account for high-stress, low-disturbance conditions.

CSR theory has been applied in microbial ecology, predominantly through observational studies and meta-analyses of soil microbiomes [[12], [13], [14]]. However, the scarcity of manipulative experiments testing its validity limits our ability to establish causal relationships between specific disturbance regimes and microbial ecological strategies, with few exceptions, such as studies examining the effects of cadmium toxicity on the soil microbiome [15]. To address this, controlled, manipulative studies have investigated the effects of varied disturbance frequencies on wastewater-treatment microbiomes in replicated activated-sludge [6,16] and anaerobic bioreactors [17]. These studies employed community-aggregated traits (CATs), a trait-based approach that quantifies microbial community function by measuring the weighted mean of functional traits across taxa, independent of individual taxonomic classifications [18]. By capturing community-wide trait distributions, CATs highlight the collective functional potential of microbial communities, in which interactions among species drive ecosystem functions that may not be evident from individual species' traits alone [7]. The findings of these earlier studies demonstrated consistent patterns in community structure across different disturbance types, with undisturbed, press-disturbed (prolonged), and intermediately disturbed levels corresponding to competitive, stress-tolerant, and ruderal traits, respectively. While they provided valuable insights into the applicability of CSR in engineered systems, different types and intensities of disturbance may exert varying microbial community responses, potentially shaping distinct life-history strategies [7].

Anaerobic digesters are engineered systems that biologically degrade organic waste to produce methane as a renewable energy source [19]. The intensity of substrate addition (feeding) and biomass removal (discharge) plays a crucial role in maintaining process stability [20]. The substrate for anaerobic digesters is typically added daily in small volumes (low-intensity feeding) to ensure efficient degradation of organic matter. Infrequent feeding with a large substrate load (high-intensity feeding) may disrupt anaerobic digestion (AD) and overwhelm microbial processing capacity, resulting in volatile fatty acid (VFA) accumulation [20,21]. Such abrupt loading events may occur in full-scale anaerobic digesters due to operational interruptions, equipment malfunction, human error, or batch-based substrate addition, resulting in increased biomass turnover and substrate pulses that can impose strong selective pressures on anaerobic microbial communities. These events can transiently elevate instantaneous loading and/or promote biomass washout, closely resembling the disturbance mechanisms examined in this study. VFA build-up can subsequently lower the pH in digesters and inhibit methanogenic activity, leading to a cascade of undesirable process-related outcomes, including reduced chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal, incomplete solids stabilisation, elevated carbon dioxide (CO2) and reduced methane (CH4) production [20,22]. While the CSR framework has previously been applied to AD under disturbance in the form of biomass removal [17], a key question is whether certain functional traits consistently link to specific CSR strategies regardless of disturbance type, or whether their abundance is uniquely affected by the nature of each disturbance. The answer will help clarify the predictability of microbial community responses, enabling more customised mediation strategies in the event of process disruptions.

This study aims to investigate the applicability of Grime's CSR theory by evaluating how a compounded disturbance, characterised by varying intensities of biomass removal (discharge) and substrate addition (feeding), influences the selection of microbial life-history strategies in anaerobic digestion systems. We hypothesise that microbial communities will adopt stress-tolerant strategies in digesters subjected to high discharge and feeding intensities, conditions that promote VFA accumulation and process instability, characterised by traits focused on maintenance, survival, and protective mechanisms against harsh conditions. In contrast, undisturbed digesters operated with low discharge and feeding intensities, which maintain process stability by reducing VFA buildup, will favour competitor strategies with traits specialised for efficient resource acquisition and utilisation. To test this hypothesis, six groups of triplicate digesters were subjected to varied intensities of biomass removal and substrate addition, creating a disturbance gradient from least (low intensity) to most disturbed (high intensity). To evaluate microbial ecological responses, we used CATs calculated from Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) annotations of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), which were subsequently classified into C-S-R strategies using the MicroEcoTools R package [23]. Next, we compared the functional traits and ecological strategies encountered with those reported in previous studies on activated sludge, AD, and soil ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

We operated 18 anaerobic digesters (labelled A1–A18) with a working volume of 3 L at 35 ± 1 °C. Seed sludge was sourced from an anaerobic digester operating at an average solids retention time (SRT) of 24 ± 9 days, which treated waste sludge at a municipal wastewater treatment plant in Singapore. The substrate consisted of a 1:1 (by volume) mixture of thickened primary sludge and thickened waste activated sludge, both obtained from the same treatment plant. The mixture contained 55.9 ± 15.0 g L−1 total solids, 48.5 ± 51.4 g L−1 volatile solids, and 81.5 ± 12.0 g L−1 total COD (TCOD). Acetate, propionate, butyrate, and valerate concentrations were 1,295 ± 400.2, 1,125 ± 454.9, 548.2 ± 316.4, and 229.2 ± 88.4 mg L−1, respectively.

All 18 digesters were operated at a 10-day SRT to ensure stable performance. Prior to the commencement of the study, the digester contents were pooled, thoroughly mixed, and redistributed equally among the 18 digesters; they were then subjected to a short acclimation period of 10 days at an SRT of 10 days. This procedure minimised divergence among microbial communities across replicate digesters while ensuring that all digesters maintained stable performance and comparable baselines. Digesters were divided into six groups (Groups A–F), each comprising three replicate digesters, to represent increasing disturbance levels (Fig. 1). Each disturbance event consisted of withdrawing a volume of well-mixed digestate and replacing it with an equal volume of substrate; thus, disturbance intensity was represented by the per-event biomass turnover. Group A underwent a single daily discharge-feeding event in which 300 mL of digester effluent was withdrawn and replaced with 300 mL of substrate. Groups B–F were operated under progressively increasing disturbance intensities by reducing the frequency of discharge-feeding events (from five times per week to once per week) while proportionally increasing the per-event discharge and feeding volumes, ranging from 420 mL per event applied five days a week (Group B) to 2,100 mL applied once weekly (Group F). The SRT was maintained at 10 days across all groups throughout the study, and the organic loading rate (56,884 ± 8,449 mg COD L−1 week−1) naturally varied with the characteristics of the substrate obtained from the treatment plant. Physicochemical parameters were analysed thrice a week prior to feeding events. Sludge samples for molecular analysis were collected prior to feeding events and preserved at −80 °C until DNA extraction. The physicochemical and molecular data collected at the end of the study period (Day 182) were used to test the CSR theory.

Analytical methods

Total solids (TS) and volatile solids (VS) concentrations were determined in accordance with standard methods [24]. Well-mixed digestate and feed samples were dried at 105 °C to constant weight to quantify TS; the dried residue was then ignited at 550 °C and reweighed to determine VS. Samples were cooled in a desiccator prior to each weighing. TCOD and soluble COD (SCOD) were analysed with HACH COD High Range digestion vials (Loveland, CO, USA). For soluble-phase measurements, digester and feed samples were centrifuged, and the supernatants were used for subsequent SCOD, total ammonia nitrogen, and VFA analyses. Biogas volume was recorded daily using a μFlow and Gas Endeavour flow meters (Bioprocess Control AB, Sweden). Biogas composition (CH4, CO2, H2S) was measured using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a TCD detector. VFAs were quantified using a gas chromatograph (GC-2010 Plus, Shimadzu, Japan) fitted with TCD and FID detectors and a DB-FFAP capillary column supplied by Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Genomic DNA extraction and sequencing

The DNA extraction protocol was optimised using the ZymoBIOMICS™ MagBead DNA/RNA kit, including a mechanical homogenisation step (three bead-beating cycles) to ensure complete lysis of bacterial cell walls [17]. A Tecan M200 Evo (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) liquid handler was used for the subsequent nucleic acid purification step, with an additional ethanol purification step to enhance contaminant removal, as per the protocol described by Neshat et al. [17]. Quantitative and qualitative analyses of the extracted and purified DNA samples were conducted using a Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and an Agilent Tapestation (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) with genomic DNA screen tape, respectively. DNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Nano library preparation kit (Illumina, CA, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X sequencing platform (Illumina, CA, USA).

Reads processing and statistical analysis

Reads processing was conducted based on a previously described pipeline [25], with minor modifications (see Supplementary Text S1 for further details). Briefly, raw reads were quality-trimmed, and reads from triplicate digesters within each group were co-assembled and binned into MAGs, which were then filtered to retain medium-quality MAGs (completeness >50%, contamination <10%) [26] for downstream analysis. All computational work for read processing was performed on the AMD-based Cray EX supercomputer at the National Supercomputing Centre, Singapore, with Red Hat Enterprise Linux 8 operating system (https://www.nscc.sg). Both microbial community relative abundances and community-aggregated functional trait data for COG categories were used for C-S-R classifications, with experimental groups (A–F) representing an operational disturbance gradient. For each genus and COG category, differential abundance across experimental groups was assessed using the Welch's ANOVA function pairwise_welch(·) implemented in the MicroEcoTools R package [23]. Based on these results, taxa and COG categories significantly enriched in low-disturbance groups were classified as competitors (C); those enriched under sustained physicochemical constraints as stress-tolerants (S); and those enriched in intermediately disturbed groups as ruderals (R). These classifications were performed using the function CSR_assign(·), which applies a decision-tree framework to translate enrichment patterns and disturbance gradients into C-S-R strategies. Meanwhile, microbial community relative abundance or functional traits that were significantly abundant in any two of these disturbance groups; (i) low and intermediately disturbed groups, (ii) low and extremely disturbed groups, (iii) intermediately and extremely disturbed groups, were classified as competitor-ruderal (CR), competitor-stress-tolerant (CS), and stress-tolerant-ruderal (SR), respectively. Microbial community relative abundance or functional traits that were not significantly abundant in any experimental group were categorised as CSR.

Results and discussion

We investigated the effect of a compounded disturbance, involving varied intensities of biomass removal and substrate addition, across six groups of triplicate anaerobic digesters (Fig. 1) on community structure, ecosystem functions (process performance), and functional traits in the context of Grime's CSR life-history strategies.

Disturbance-driven shifts in microbial community dynamics and ecosystem function

We examined microbial communities exposed to different disturbance levels at the end of the study period (day 182) using canonical analysis of principal coordinates (CAP) based on a Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix derived from Hellinger-transformed relative abundances (Fig. 2), following the workflow outlined by Santillan et al. [6]. The plot revealed distinct group separation, with Group A clustering closely with Groups B–D, indicating minimal divergence in microbial community composition at lower disturbance levels. In contrast, Groups E and F were clustered further apart, reflecting a more pronounced shift in microbial community structure with increasing disturbance intensity. These clustering patterns reflect an underlying divergence in ecological strategies adopted by the communities, consistent with Grime's CSR theory, in which Groups A–D, E, and F represent communities exhibiting competitive, ruderal, and stress-tolerant strategies, respectively.

Colours and shapes denote increasing disturbance intensities from Group A (undisturbed) to Group F (most disturbed). The numbers following each group designation (e.g., A1–A3, B1–B3) represent the three biological replicate digesters within each disturbance level. Statistical testing showed a significant difference in multivariate location among groups (PERMANOVA, P-value = 0.0001), whereas multivariate dispersion was not significant (PERMDISP, P-value = 0.655), indicating that the observed differences were entirely attributable to multivariate location.

We evaluated ecosystem function responses to disturbances by classifying physicochemical parameters into life-history strategies. Here we distinguish organism-level functional traits [2] from physicochemical variables that represent ecosystem function responses (process-level indicators). These physicochemical parameters were treated as indicators of ecosystem functional responses. Their classifications (Supplementary Table S1) therefore reflect a life-history interpretation of ecosystem functions, rather than organism-level functional traits as defined by Violle et al. [2]. Key ecosystem parameters such as TCOD, SCOD, and VFA concentrations (propionate, butyrate, valerate) were classified as stress-tolerant (Supplementary Table S1), conforming with the overlaid Pearson correlation vectors (Fig. 2) of ecosystem functions, where these parameters correlated strongly with the extremely disturbed Group F. The fitted vectors for these significant variables were oriented largely along CAP1, indicating that these associations were primarily captured by the first canonical axis. The elevated TCOD (57,200 mg L−1) and SCOD (5,410 mg L−1) levels in Group F suggest that the microbial communities experienced stress due to organic overload and incomplete substrate degradation under these conditions (Supplementary Table S2). Although the total weekly discharge and feeding volume were conserved across all groups (Fig. 1), concentrating this exchange into fewer, larger events (Group F) resulted in high per-event microbial community washout and organic loading, causing an imbalance between organic matter degradation and the subsequent consumption of intermediate fermentative products. In contrast, distributing the same weekly discharge and feeding volume into smaller, more frequent discharge/feeding events (Groups A–D) reduced per-event biomass turnover, allowing more time for the conversion of intermediate products between events. Consistent with this interpretation, Group F exhibited high propionate concentrations (Supplementary Table S2; 2,955 mg L−1), coinciding with the absence of key syntrophic propionate-oxidising bacteria such as Candidatus Syntrophosphaera and DMER64 [27,28]. By contrast, Ca. Syntrophosphaera was more prevalent in Groups A and C, whereas DMER64 was more prevalent in Group E. Meanwhile, the low acetate levels (Supplementary Table S2; 50.6 mg L−1) in Group F suggest a possible shift toward syntrophic acetate oxidation coupled with hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis, replacing acetoclastic methanogenesis as the primary methane-producing pathway. This is supported by the higher prevalence of hydrogenotrophic methanogens, such as UBA412 (Supplementary Fig. S1), and the substantial methane production observed in Group F (Supplementary Table S2; methane concentration and yield of 56% and 0.2 L per g VS fed, respectively). Conversely, TCOD removal and propionate removal, classified as CR (Supplementary Table S1), were highly correlated with the low and moderately disturbed groups (Fig. 2), reflecting a microbial community capable of degrading excess substrate, either due to the emergence of specific taxa or because there were enough cells (biomass) to consume the VFAs.

To complement these functional observations, the genus-level heatmap of the top 30 genera (Fig. 3) offers a more detailed view of the taxonomic distribution across disturbance levels. The clustering pattern observed in the genus-level heatmap was similar to that found in the CAP analysis, reinforcing the consistency of community shifts across a disturbance gradient. Here, disturbance was operationally imposed through feeding and discharge, thereby increasing per-event biomass turnover in Groups A–F. Disturbance in microbial systems is inherently multidimensional, being characterised by attributes such as type, frequency, intensity, incidence, and extent, each of which can differentially shape community dynamics [7]. Following Rykiel's [29] framing, stress is interpreted as a condition in which disturbance effects persist as sustained physicochemical constraints that prevent community function from returning to comparable pre-disturbance dynamics. Alternative stress–disturbance distinctions have been proposed that are particularly suited to gradients of disturbance frequency [6,7,16,17], where stress emerges when frequent disturbances prevent recovery between events [4]. By contrast, the present experiment was structured around increasing per-event disturbance intensity rather than a monotonic frequency gradient. Group F experienced the highest per-event biomass removal and substrate addition (i.e., the highest per-event disturbance intensity) and correspondingly exhibited the strongest and most persistent stress, as indicated by sustained elevation of volatile fatty acids (Supplementary Fig. S2), with mean propionate levels of 3,971 ± 1,157 mg L−1 prior to day 182. Grime's original CSR framework treats the combination of simultaneously high stress and high disturbance as an unlikely or poorly supported habitat for plant communities; however, microbial communities can persist under such compounded pressures, and we therefore interpret the CSR axes here as heuristic rather than absolute. Accordingly, Group F is interpreted as stress-tolerant, yet remains the most disturbed group under our operational definition of per-event disturbance intensity. Meanwhile, Group E is intermediately disturbed within this operational gradient and, although propionate levels were elevated early in the study, they declined from 3,389 ± 1,096 mg L−1 (days 2–119) to 1,192 ± 747 mg L−1 (days 121–182), remaining substantially lower than in Group F (Supplementary Fig. S2). Group E is therefore interpreted as exhibiting ruderal strategies under comparatively lower stress. We use the terms low-, intermediate-, and most (or extremely) disturbed throughout the manuscript to refer to Groups A–D, E, and F, respectively, based on the imposed per-event disturbance intensity gradient.

Notably, the arbitrarily assigned disturbance levels may not fully reflect how the microbial community perceives environmental pressures [30] and should therefore be interpreted as heuristic categories to guide trait-based analysis rather than as rigid classifications. In this study, grouping A–D as low-disturbance levels is not intended to imply absolute uniformity among replicates, but rather to capture their shared pattern of minimal divergence relative to Groups E and F. Although disturbances were applied to Groups B–D, most replicates remained closer in composition to Group A than to Groups E and F, suggesting that the applied intensity was insufficient to induce a substantial shift in community composition or that the communities recovered rapidly between disturbance events. One exception was D3, which deviated from D1 and D2, likely reflecting stochastic assembly processes despite identical operating conditions [31,32]. Taken together, these observations provide a rationale for assigning Groups A–D, E, and F as competitive, ruderal, and stress-tolerant communities, respectively, under the CSR framework, which was subsequently used to guide trait-based analyses.

Functional traits selection under disturbance

Functional traits provide valuable insights into the potential roles and activities of microbial communities, particularly in response to environmental disturbances. While the present study mainly focused on Grime's CSR theory, alternative frameworks tailored specifically for microbial communities have also been proposed, such as the yield–acquisition–stress-tolerance (YAS) and competitor–stress-tolerator–opportunist (CSO) theories [9,33]. In YAS, microbial strategies are described along three axes: high growth yield (Y), resource acquisition (A), and stress tolerance (S), with the acquisition and yield axes corresponding to the competitor and ruderal dimensions of CSR, respectively. This reframing stems from the notion that microbial competition often centres on resource capture in nutrient-limited environments. Meanwhile, the CSO framework links stress tolerance to survival in resource-constrained environments [9]. These perspectives might not be true for all environments. In our anaerobic digesters, soluble COD remained high in all groups (Supplementary Table S2; 750–5,410 mg L−1), suggesting that substrate was not limiting. Under such nutrient-rich conditions, competition is more likely to reflect efficient resource utilisation and niche exploitation. Meanwhile, stress tolerance may not primarily arise from constrained resource use but rather from persistence under chronic physicochemical constraints that impose high maintenance demands. We therefore consider Grime's CSR definitions of competitors (maximising resource acquisition in undisturbed, productive environments) and stress-tolerant strategies (persisting under prolonged adverse conditions) to be appropriate for interpreting microbial ecological strategies in AD. Building on this framework, the following subsections explore COG categories and genes within the CSR framework to determine which life-history strategies were most strongly associated with increasing disturbance levels in AD.

Competitor traits

Competition in microbial communities primarily centres on resources such as nutrients and space [34]. In this study, no COG categories were classified exclusively as competitor traits, likely because the relatively high disturbance intensities favoured the selection of stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal strategies over purely competitive ones, which is further elaborated in Section 3.3.2. Among the traits associated with competitive strategies, genome size has been suggested as one of the key predictors of microbial life-history strategies [13,14,35]. Larger genomes are believed to encode genes associated with increased metabolic activity, diverse functional attributes and adaptability to different environmental conditions, while smaller genomes primarily contain essential genes required for cell maintenance and survival [[36], [37], [38]]. In our study, no clear trends in genome size were observed, with both competitor and stress-tolerant genera exhibiting a wide range of genome sizes based on MAGs, from 0.8 to 5.1 Mb for competitor genera and 1.1 to 4.6 Mb for stress-tolerant genera. Others reported similar findings, with no consistent association observed in genome size between microbes in nutrient-scarce and nutrient-rich environment [16,39,40]. While large and small genomes have often been linked to competitive and stress-tolerant traits, respectively [13,14], the inconsistencies observed across studies, including ours, highlight the complexity of genome size as a predictor of ecological strategies. Genome size could be shaped not only by ecological strategies, but also by evolutionary histories [41,42]. Furthermore, the variation in genome size estimation methods across studies may also contribute to the observed discrepancies [39]. Genome size estimates from MAGs also have inherent limitations, since MAGs reported as 100% complete by tools such as CheckM may still lack certain genomic fragments, as completeness is assessed solely by the presence of marker genes [16,43].

Stress-tolerant traits

Grime's CSR theory suggests that organisms exhibit stress-tolerant traits when exposed to high-stress, less-competitive environments [3]. Traits linked to biofilm formation, molecular chaperones, and DNA damage repair are often characterised as stress-tolerant traits [12,13,33,35,16,44], consistent with the patterns observed in this study.

COG categories such as ‘translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis,’ ‘cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis,’ ‘posttranslational modification, protein turnover, chaperones’ (COG category J, M, and O, respectively) refer to the SR trait (Table 1) and reflect the functional strategies of microbial communities to cope with disturbances in Groups E and F. Ribosome biosynthesis, one of the most energy-intensive cellular processes, may be more efficiently conducted by recycling existing ribosomes, thereby conserving energy and maintaining protein synthesis efficiency in highly disturbed environments [[45], [46], [47]]. A study investigating disturbance in the form of increasing organic overloading in an activated sludge system also classified the ribosome-related COG category J as a stress-tolerant trait, supporting its role in enabling microbial survival under persistent environmental stress [16]. Accordingly, genes related to ribosome recycling (Supplementary Table S3; COG0233) were significantly more abundant in the most disturbed group (Group F), reflecting an adaptive strategy to minimise new ribosome synthesis and optimise resource utilisation in a continuously stressed environment. Meanwhile, chaperones aid in protein folding and prevention of protein aggregation under suboptimal conditions [48]. Chaperones could play a crucial role in maintaining protein stability in digesters experiencing oxidative stress due to VFA accumulation. Elevated VFA levels can impose osmotic stress due to increased concentrations of dissolved solutes in the digester environment, which disrupt intracellular osmotic pressure and promote the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby triggering oxidative stress-related mechanisms in microbial cells [49]. Supporting this, COG genes (Supplementary Table S3) such as molecular chaperone GrpE (COG0576) and redox-regulated molecular chaperone (COG1281) were significantly more abundant in the most disturbed group (Group F) and the DnaJ domain protein (COG1076) dominated in both the intermediately and most disturbed Groups E and F. Molecular chaperones such as GrpE and DnaJ are known to facilitate protein folding and prevent protein aggregation associated with acid or oxidative stress [50,51], highlighting their importance in microbial adaptation in a highly disturbed environment.

Furthermore, the classification of the ‘cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis’ trait as SR likely reflects the need to maintain cell integrity in a highly disturbed environment. A key component is exopolysaccharides, which promote biofilm formation [52] and serve as protective mechanisms against environmental stressors, such as osmotic and acid stress [53,54]. Meanwhile, capsular polysaccharide (CPS) protects bacterial cells from external stressors by forming a tightly bound extracellular polysaccharide layer on the cell surface [55,56]. In this study, the osmotic pressure due to propionate accumulation may have influenced the abundance of COG genes (Supplementary Table S3) related to exopolysaccharide biosynthesis, including COG3206 (exopolysaccharide export protein) and COG3932 (exopolysaccharide synthesis protein), which were significantly more abundant in the intermediately and most disturbed group (Groups E and F), with COG3932 being particularly enriched in the most disturbed group. Likewise, CPS-related COG genes such as COG4421 (CPS biosynthesis protein) in intermediately disturbed (Group E), and COG2148 (gene related to colanic acid synthesis, a key exopolysaccharide that defines capsular structure [56]) were also more prevalent in the most disturbed group (Group F). The protective role of exopolysaccharides and CPS under these conditions may stem from their ability to mitigate osmotic stress; exopolysaccharides retain water in the extracellular matrix to prevent dehydration [54,57,58], while CPS increases expression of colanic acid-related genes, which buffers cells against osmotic pressure [59,60].

COG categories such as ‘transcription’ (COG category K) and ‘signal transduction and mechanism’ (COG category T) are also classified as stress-tolerant traits. COG genes (Supplementary Table S3) associated with both COG categories K and T, such as COG1974 (SOS-response transcriptional repressor LexA (RecA-mediated autopeptidase)) and COG genes associated specifically with COG category T such as COG0589 (Nucleotide-binding universal stress protein, UspA family), were significantly more prevalent in both the intermediately and extremely disturbed groups (Group E and F for COG1974) as well as the most disturbed Group F (for COG0589). LexA is a key regulator of the SOS response, activating DNA repair pathways through RecA-mediated self-cleavage under genotoxic stress [[61], [62], [63]]. Meanwhile, UspA, a universal stress protein, is essential for protecting cells from DNA damage [64,65] and contributes to bacterial adaptation to various disturbances, including oxidative stress [66], heat shock [67], and acid stress [68]. The high abundance of these genes in intermediately and extremely disturbed groups underscores the importance of stress-response mechanisms for microbial adaptation in a disturbed environment. Taken together, the selection of traits related to ribosome structure, protein stability, and cell maintenance and repair in intermediately and extremely disturbed environments aligns with the CSR framework. Microbial communities under these conditions prioritise survival through repair and adaptive mechanisms, supporting their classification as stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal strategists.

Ruderal traits

Ruderals are defined as organisms that thrive in frequently disturbed, low-stress environments [3], characterised by rapid growth, high reproductive rates, cell motility, and spore formation [35,44]. Their high growth rates are often associated with inefficient nutrient uptake [69], as observed by Santillan et al. [6], who reported lower COD removal in moderately disturbed groups with 3-CA toxicity. The classification is based on the notion that in temporarily resource-rich environments, ruderals tend to prioritise growth over resource uptake. In AD, parameters such as VS removal, TCOD and SCOD removal, and acetate and propionate removal may be used to assess digester efficiency and nutrient uptake. Similarly, the intermediately disturbed ruderal group (Group E) showed lower nutrient uptake and digester efficiency compared to the lower disturbance competitor groups (Groups A–D) in our study (Supplementary Table S2). Specifically, Group E showed reduced removal of VS (31%), TCOD (41%), SCOD (75%), and acetate (93%) relative to Groups C and D (32.5–33.4%, 46–47%, 89% (for Group D), 98–99%, respectively). In contrast to ruderals, competitors tend to develop more efficient metabolic pathways for substrate uptake and conversion, ensuring greater digester efficiency under stable conditions.

As for functional traits, cell motility (COG category N) was classified as SR. Our findings align well with other studies, including those on engineered systems, where cell motility has been consistently classified as either stress-tolerant [6] or ruderal [13,17,44], highlighting the consistency of the trait's C-S-R classification across various ecosystems despite differing environmental conditions. Overall, our findings emphasise the adaptive strategies of ruderal microbial communities in AD. While these communities exhibit lower nutrient uptake efficiency, their functional traits associated with cell motility likely confer a competitive advantage in responding to environmental stressors.

Comparison of key ecological strategies for different disturbance types and intensities

Understanding microbial ecological strategies is crucial for predicting community responses to environmental changes. Previous studies have shown that undisturbed, intermediately disturbed, and highly disturbed conditions often align with competitive, ruderal, and stress-tolerant strategies, respectively. Here, trait classifications from the present study are contrasted with those from the aforementioned studies and from ecosystems such as soil to explore whether the observed classifications reflect universal microbial strategies or are influenced by the applied disturbances.

Convergent trait selection despite different disturbance types in activated sludge and anaerobic digestion systems

Traits such as ‘amino acid, carbohydrate and lipid transport and metabolism’ (COG categories E, G and I), ‘coenzyme and inorganic ion transport and metabolism’ (COG categories H and P), and ‘intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport’ (COG category U) were classified as stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal traits (Table 1). In a study on 3-chloroaniline (3-CA) toxicity in aerobic activated sludge bioreactors, Santillan et al. [6] similarly classified COG categories H, P, and U as stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal, coinciding with the findings of the present study, while amino acid and carbohydrate transport and metabolism were classified differently as competitor and ruderal traits, respectively. Traits pertaining to cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport could possibly be selected because microbial communities need to maintain cell integrity and eliminate toxic compounds. In the study by Santillan et al. [6], this selection was likely caused by 3-CA toxicity, while in the current study, the inhibitory effect of VFA accumulation (propionate, butyrate, and valerate) may have led to oxidative stress for the AD microbiome due to the increased production of ROS, which compromise cell membrane permeability and cause DNA damage [49,70]. VFA accumulation also imposes hypertonic conditions that challenge microbial cells to maintain osmotic balance. Cells express high-affinity potassium transport systems, such as the Kdp-ATPase complex, which help restore intracellular turgor pressure under osmotic stress [71]. Consistent with this, several COG genes related to these systems (Supplementary Table S3; COG2216 and COG2156) were enriched in the intermediately and most disturbed Groups E and F, suggesting that microbial cells relied on osmotic stress response mechanisms to survive under VFA-induced stress conditions.

Apart from the VFA accumulation, the large amount of substrate added on a single day in the most disturbed group (Group F) may also have increased oxygen intrusion, further aggravating oxidative stress. Elevated ROS levels may, in turn, trigger ROS-scavenging mechanisms [49,70], with traits related to cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport playing a crucial role in maintaining cell integrity under such conditions. Enzymes such as copper-zinc superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase are essential for cellular defences against ROS [[72], [73], [74], [75]]. In line with this, several COG genes associated with enzymatic ROS detoxification (Supplementary Table S3; COG2032, COG3546, COG0753, COG2128, and COG2837—under COG category P) were significantly more prevalent in either the intermediately and most disturbed group (Group E and F; for COG2032) or exclusively in the most disturbed group (for COG3546, COG0753, COG2128, and COG2837). Additionally, antioxidants such as ubiquinone (Coenzyme Q) also play a crucial role in protecting microbial cells from oxidative stress [76,77]. In this study, several COG genes involved in ubiquinone biosynthesis (Supplementary Table S3; COG0661, COG2226, COG2960, COG3154, and COG5590—related to COG category H) were more prevalent in either the intermediately and most disturbed group (Groups E and F) or solely in the most disturbed Group F. The prevalence of ROS-detoxifying and antioxidant-related genes suggests that microbial communities in highly disturbed environments employ broad oxidative stress mitigation strategies to reduce VFA accumulation and oxygen exposure.

Nutrient metabolism-related traits (amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid transport and metabolism—COG categories E, G, and I, respectively) were classified as stress-tolerant in the present study due to the need to degrade excess substrate resulting from the substrate overload in the most disturbed group (Group F). Carbohydrate and lipid metabolism facilitated energy production, membrane stability, and substrate utilisation [78,79], while amino acid metabolism may have played a dual role in degrading excess substrates and supporting pH homeostasis [80]. Elevated propionate levels in Group F (Supplementary Table S2; 2,955 mg L−1) may have imposed weak acid toxicity, as undissociated propionic acid can diffuse into membranes and disrupt intracellular pH [81]. This is reflected by the detection of acid tolerance genes (Supplementary Table S3), including COG2235 (arginine deiminase gene) in Group F and COG0076 (gene linked to glutamate decarboxylase) in Groups E and F. Although pH data were unavailable for Day 182, earlier measurements (Days 72, 108 and 144) showed lower pH in Group E (6.8 ± 0.2) and F (5.6 ± 0.4) compared to the undisturbed Group A (7.3 ± 0.2), possibly due to the repeated low-pH substrate influx (pH around 5.5) during feeding events. Such recurrent exposure to acidic conditions may have sustained the enrichment of acid-tolerance genes, allowing the communities in Groups E and F to withstand and anticipate recurring low-pH episodes even when the digester pH returned to near-neutral between feeding events.

Santillan et al. [6] found that excess substrate and acid stress were not major environmental pressures; amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism primarily functioned as part of routine nutrient metabolism rather than stress adaptation, leading to their classification as competitor or ruderal traits. In contrast, the high substrate influx observed in the present study led to both undigested substrate accumulation and potential acid stress, underscoring the importance of nutrient-metabolism-related traits for microbial survival and adaptation. This suggests that the classification of nutrient metabolism traits (COG categories E, G, and I), as well as those associated with cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport (COG categories H, P, and U), is strongly shaped by the specific type and intensity of the imposed disturbance. Despite differences in experimental design between the present study and Santillan et al. [6]— including closed versus open systems, aerobic versus anaerobic conditions, different type of disturbance, and small (20 mL) versus large (3 L) scales—the analogous functional traits related to cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport in both studies substantiate the predictability and broad applicability of CSR theory in different environments.

Divergent trait classifications under varied disturbance intensities in anaerobic digestion systems

To assess whether similar AD systems yield comparable ecological strategies, we examined the study by Neshat et al. [17], which used the same substrate type, reactor scale (4 L versus 3 L), and open-anaerobic configuration as the present work. To ensure consistency, we adopted their C-S-R classification approach, including only individual COG categories and not separating multiple COG categories into their component categories. Despite their operational and methodological similarities, we found little resemblance in how functional traits were classified by the MicroEcoTools [23] under Grime's CSR theory. Neshat et al. [17] imposed disturbance via biomass removal by reducing the SRT from 10 to 5 days, with a constant organic loading rate and constant disturbance events. Although both studies applied disturbance via biomass removal, the disturbances differed in intensity: in the most disturbed group of Neshat et al. [17], approximately 90% of the biomass was displaced after 10 consecutive days of SRT reduction. In the present study, the most disturbed group experienced comparable biomass removal, with 80% of biomass removed during each disturbance event. However, this removal was compounded by simultaneous increases in substrate addition, raising the organic loading and imposing a higher disturbance intensity than that in Neshat et al. [17]. This, coupled with the relatively low amount of influent (400 mL day−1 at a 10-day SRT) during the post-disturbance period in Neshat et al. [17], likely created a more stable environment with reduced microbial community turnover compared to the disturbance period at a 5-day SRT, causing the AD microbiome to re-establish competitive dominance, rapidly grow, and efficiently utilise the available substrate, thereby favouring competitor and ruderal traits over stress-tolerant ones. Consequently, nutrient metabolism traits (COG categories E and G), traits related to cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport (COG categories H, P, and U), and traits related to cell maintenance and repair (COG categories M and O) were classified as competitor–ruderal or ruderal in Neshat et al. [17], supporting competition and cell growth.

In contrast, disturbance was sustained through both high biomass turnover and increased substrate addition in the present study, creating persistently stressed conditions, as observed in Group F where the VFA levels remained high even after the disturbance ended (Supplementary Table S2: propionate, butyrate, and valerate concentrations of 2,955, 13, and 187 mg L−1, respectively), leading the same traits to be classified as stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal. Although the intermediately disturbed Group E exhibited relatively low VFA concentrations on day 182 (Supplementary Table S2; 49.4 mg L−1), the elevated propionate concentrations during the disturbance period (average of 3,030 mg L−1 from day 14 to day 133) may have contributed to the persistence of stress-tolerant–ruderal traits during the post-disturbance period (day 182). Overall, these findings emphasise that the selection of ecological strategies is influenced not only by the type of disturbance but also by its intensity. Similar functional traits can shift between competitive and stress-tolerant classifications depending on whether the post-disturbance environment stabilises, as in Neshat et al. [17], or remains stressed, as in the present study.

Emerging patterns in microbial trait selection

Based on the insights derived from this study and comparisons with previous works, we identified key patterns in ecological trait selection for various environmental conditions (Fig. 4). First, traits associated with cell motility were commonly classified as stress-tolerant or ruderal in multiple studies, as were traits linked to protective mechanisms such as exopolysaccharide production [13,33,35,44] and cellular maintenance and repair (e.g., traits related to protein stability, cell wall and DNA damage repair). These traits reflect the fundamental requirements for microbial survival and adaptation to environmental changes, irrespective of ecosystem type. However, Neshat et al. [17] deviated from these universal classifications, classifying traits related to cell maintenance and repair as CR, likely due to a lower disturbance intensity than in the present work, as discussed in Section 3.3.2. Under such comparatively lower intensities, cell wall synthesis was probably favoured to support new cell growth, while chaperones likely aided the proper folding of newly synthesised proteins [82], thereby facilitating microbial growth in competitive and ruderal environments. These discrepancies highlight the inherent complexity of deciphering patterns in microbial communities and the influence of both environmental conditions and disturbance dynamics on trait selection. Despite these variations, general patterns can still be observed across multiple studies, reinforcing the adaptability and applicability of the CSR framework in understanding microbial ecological strategies (Fig. 4).

Conceptual framework of microbial ecological strategies under the competitor–stress-tolerant–ruderal theory. Functional traits are categorised as universal or context-dependent. Universal traits, such as those related to cell motility, protective mechanisms, and cellular maintenance and repair, are commonly classified as ruderal or stress-tolerant across various ecosystems. Context-dependent traits, including those related to nutrient metabolism, cellular homeostasis, and metabolite transport, vary with the type and intensity of disturbance. Lower disturbance intensity promotes the selection of competitor–ruderal or ruderal traits, while stronger disturbance intensity shifts the classification of the same traits toward stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal.

Second, traits related to nutrient metabolism, cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport are highly context-dependent and influenced by the type and intensity of the disturbance. In the present study, nutrient metabolism traits were classified as stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal, likely driven by substrate overload. In contrast, amino acid metabolism was classified as competitor–ruderal in an activated sludge system subjected to the same disturbance type [16]. Meanwhile, toxicity due to high 3-CA and high levels of VFAs selected for traits related to cellular homeostasis and metabolite transport was classified as stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal in both Santillan et al. [6] and the present study. This combination of divergence (different classifications under similar disturbance types) and convergence (similar classifications under different disturbance types) underscores the multidimensional nature of disturbance effects. Even when disturbance types appear similar, as in the present study and Santillan et al. [16], differences in operational context, microbial community dynamics, and system-specific stress responses can lead to different trait classifications. Conversely, when the imposed disturbance creates a similar selective pressure, such as maintaining cellular integrity under toxicant exposure in the present study and in Santillan et al. [6], systems with different configurations can have similar trait classifications. Additionally, the intensity of disturbance significantly influenced trait classification, with higher disturbance intensity favouring stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal traits, while lower disturbance intensity resulted in the same traits being classified as competitor–ruderal or ruderal in the present study and Neshat et al. [17], respectively.

Collectively, these findings suggest that trait classification within the CSR framework can either be universal or context-dependent. Variation in context-dependent trait classification is driven by the multidimensional nature of disturbance, in which factors such as frequency, intensity, extent, and type may shape microbial responses in distinct ways [7]. By comparing the present study with previous studies [6,17,16], we established how different disturbance types and intensities influence trait classification. More well-replicated empirical studies are needed to distinguish traits that consistently align with certain strategies from those that vary across disturbance regimes. Moreover, future studies may explore the effects of compounded disturbances (e.g., disturbance regimes with abrupt shifts in multiple variables, such as temperature and pH) to evaluate whether CSR trait assignments remain consistent across multiple disturbance types and to better understand how combined disturbances shape trait selection. From an application perspective, trait-based insights provide a framework for linking operational disturbance regimes to microbial community functions, offering a basis for predictive and adaptive management of engineered systems. Recognising that disturbance regimes may differ across multiple dimensions, explicitly relating disturbance intensity to microbial life-history strategies can inform operational decisions beyond empirical operations. For instance, promoting ruderal-associated traits may be advantageous during reactor start-up to accelerate community establishment and enhance resilience, whereas favouring competitor-associated traits may support stable operation with efficient substrate conversion and minimal accumulation of intermediate products under steady-state conditions.

Study limitations

While this study provides insights into microbial life-history strategies under different disturbance regimes, several caveats should be acknowledged. First, classifying Groups A–D as a low-disturbance group may raise concerns due to an unbalanced grouping: Groups A–D collectively contain 12 digesters, whereas Groups E and F each contain only 3. Although some COG categories shifted in their C-S-R assignments under an alternative classification (with Group A as undisturbed, Group F as most disturbed, and Groups B–E as intermediately disturbed), the broader ecological interpretation remained consistent (Supplementary Table S4). With the latter approach, additional COG categories (C, D, F, I, K, Q, S, and Z) were classified as CSR, while COG category B were classified from CSR to R. COG categories such as G, J, P, U, M, N, and O remained consistently classified as either S or SR, suggesting that their classification was not influenced by the grouping chosen, and the overall interpretation of traits selection patterns remained unchanged between the two grouping arrangements. These findings indicate that most R-related classifications originated primarily from Group E rather than from Groups B–D. The low weighted abundance of these COG categories in Groups B–D suggests that they did not exhibit strong ruderal characteristics but instead contributed to greater variation in C-S-R classifications, resulting in an increased number of COG categories classified as CSR.

Conversely, when Groups B–D were designated as part of the low-disturbance group along with Group A, the nearly similar trait abundances in Groups A–D reduced classification variability, leading to fewer COG categories being classified as CSR, while the classifications for R-related traits remained largely unaffected. These observations suggest that the numerically unbalanced grouping did not substantially bias the overall trait classification outcomes. The consistency in trait classification reinforces the reliability of our findings, demonstrating that the observed patterns are not artefacts of the grouping approach but rather reflect genuine ecological strategies driven by disturbance conditions.

Second, CATs derived from metagenomes represent community-level functional potential and may include DNA originating from dormant, inactive, or lysed cells [18,83]. To minimise this uncertainty, CATs in the present study were derived from MAGs of at least medium quality, which may reduce the contribution of highly fragmented DNA from extensively lysed or dead cells because such DNA is less likely to assemble into sufficiently complete genomes and would be excluded by MAG quality thresholds (e.g., completeness/contamination filters). Nevertheless, MAG quality filtering cannot fully exclude DNA from dormant or inactive organisms; therefore, our CAT-based classifications should be interpreted as reflecting functional potential rather than confirmed activity. Moreover, metagenome-based CAT estimation is constrained by annotation coverage, as a considerable fraction of genes in environmental metagenomes remain of unknown or uncertain function [18,84]. Future work could address these limitations by combining genome-resolved metagenomics with complementary annotation frameworks (e.g., KEGG, EggNOG, or Pfam) and/or gene activity-based methods (e.g., metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, or stable isotope probing) to better link inferred traits with expressed functions under disturbance.

Conclusion

Understanding microbial life-history strategies through a trait-based approach offers a powerful framework for predicting community responses to disturbances. This study advances CSR theory by providing mechanistic insights into how microbial communities balance survival and growth across various disturbance conditions, namely:

•Disturbances imposed through biomass removal and substrate addition selected for traits related to ribosome function, protein stability, and cellular repair in intermediately and highly disturbed digesters. These traits reflected stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal strategies, highlighting microbial survival and adaptation under stronger disturbance conditions.

Disturbances imposed through biomass removal and substrate addition selected for traits related to ribosome function, protein stability, and cellular repair in intermediately and highly disturbed digesters. These traits reflected stress-tolerant or stress-tolerant–ruderal strategies, highlighting microbial survival and adaptation under stronger disturbance conditions.

•Disturbance type strongly influences the selection of functional traits related to nutrient metabolism, cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport, with consistent trait classifications despite substantial differences in design, scale and operational parameters across studies. Furthermore, increasing disturbance intensity consistently favoured stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal traits over competitor–ruderal and purely ruderal strategies, highlighting the adaptive shift in microbial strategies in response to escalating environmental stress.

Disturbance type strongly influences the selection of functional traits related to nutrient metabolism, cellular homeostasis, secretion, and metabolite transport, with consistent trait classifications despite substantial differences in design, scale and operational parameters across studies. Furthermore, increasing disturbance intensity consistently favoured stress-tolerant and stress-tolerant–ruderal traits over competitor–ruderal and purely ruderal strategies, highlighting the adaptive shift in microbial strategies in response to escalating environmental stress.

•Consistent identification of universal stress-adaptive and ruderal traits, such as protective mechanisms, cell maintenance and repair, and cell motility, underscores their fundamental role in microbial survival and adaptation across disturbed environments. In contrast, traits related to nutrient metabolism and cellular homeostasis were context-dependent, varying with the type and intensity of disturbance.

Consistent identification of universal stress-adaptive and ruderal traits, such as protective mechanisms, cell maintenance and repair, and cell motility, underscores their fundamental role in microbial survival and adaptation across disturbed environments. In contrast, traits related to nutrient metabolism and cellular homeostasis were context-dependent, varying with the type and intensity of disturbance.

•This study establishes a strong empirical link between functional gene abundance and microbial ecological strategies, advancing our ability to predict and manage community responses to disturbances in natural and engineered ecosystems. Trait-based approaches may aid the optimisation of microbial ecosystem services across scales and disturbance regimes. Identifying stress-tolerant and ruderal traits under specific disturbances may enable priming of communities with desired functions, enhancing resilience and stability in wastewater treatment, and informing management strategies in other systems.
