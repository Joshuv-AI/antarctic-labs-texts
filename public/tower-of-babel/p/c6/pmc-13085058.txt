Risk and prognosis of infection and colonisation by carbapenemase-producing Citrobacter freundii in the North Denmark Region during 2012–2023: a matched case–control and cohort study
Authors: Lukas Holm Saarup, Kasper Vium Andersen, Kirstine Kobberøe Søgaard, Regitze Gyldenholm Skals, Kristoffer Koch, Nina Ank, Samuel Azuz, Paw Jensen, Mogens Tornby Stender, Anette M. Hammerum, Henrik Hasman, Hans Linde Nielsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSince October 2012, New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM)-1–producing Citrobacter freundii has caused a prolonged outbreak at Aalborg University Hospital, Denmark. Despite infection control efforts, transmission persisted in haematology and abdominal surgery wards. This study aimed to identify risk factors for infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii and to assess all-cause mortality.MethodsWe conducted a matched case–control and cohort study (2012–2023). Cases were patients with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii detected in clinical or screening specimens. For each case, eight controls without C. freundii were selected from the same ward using risk-set sampling and matched on specimen date. Conditional logistic regression was used to identify risk factors, and Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for all-cause mortality.ResultsSixty-nine cases and 552 controls were included. The strongest risk factors were prolonged hospital stay (>14 vs ≤7 days; odds ratio [OR]=6.4, 95% confidence interval [CI]= 3.6–11.3), peritoneal disease (OR=4.4, 95% CI= 1.5–13.3), pancreatobiliary disorders (OR=5.5, 95% CI= 3.0–10.3), biliary tract surgery (OR=4.6, 95% CI= 2.7–7.8), pancreatic surgery (OR=4.4, 95% CI= 2.2–8.6), and in-hospital exposure to vancomycin (OR=13.9, 95% CI= 6.8–28.4), beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors (OR=8.8, 95% CI= 4.2–18.2), fluoroquinolones (OR=8.2, 95% CI= 4.6–14.9), or systemic antifungals (OR=8.6, 95% CI= 4.7–15.6). Thirty-day mortality was higher among cases (adjusted HR=2.2, 95% CI= 1.1–4.3), while 90- and 365-day mortality did not differ.ConclusionInfection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii was associated with prolonged hospitalisation, abdominal infections, invasive surgical procedures, broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure, and higher short-term mortality, likely reflecting underlying disease severity.

Introduction

Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPEs) are a major public health concern due to their ability to hydrolyse carbapenems and other β-lactam antibiotics, thereby making these critical treatments ineffective [1]. Among carbapenemases, New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase (NDM) is of particular concern due to its global spread [2].

Citrobacter freundii is a Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic bacterium that is part of the normal gut microbiota of animals and humans but is also found in soil, sewage, and water [3]. While typically commensal, C. freundii can act opportunistically, causing infections such as urinary tract infections, intra-abdominal infections, and bloodstream infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [4].

In Denmark, carbapenem use is restricted to the treatment of severe infections [[5], [6], [7]]. Despite this, an ongoing outbreak of NDM-1-producing C. freundii multi-locus sequence type (MLST) 18 was first detected in October 2012 at Aalborg University Hospital in the North Denmark Region [8]. The source remained unidentified, and despite infection control efforts, the number of infected or colonised patients increased over the following decade. Environmental investigations revealed a high burden of these strains in toilets on the haematology and abdominal surgery wards, the only wards with persistent contamination and transmission [9]. At the national level, surveillance data have documented the occurrence of ST18 and other carbapenemase-producing C. freundii lineages across all five Danish regions, likely reflecting inter-regional patient movement [7].

The aim of this study was to identify hospital-associated risk factors for infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii through a matched case–control study and to compare all-cause mortality at 30, 90, and 365 days between patients with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii and controls using a matched cohort design.

Material and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective, observational study with two components: 1) a matched case–control study to identify clinical factors associated with infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii and 2) a matched cohort study to assess all-cause mortality among patients positive for ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii compared with a matched cohort without C. freundii. The study included patients admitted to Aalborg University Hospital between October 2012 and August 2023, the largest in the North Denmark Region, serving a population of approximately 590,000 during the study period. In Denmark, healthcare is provided by the Danish National Health Service and is free of charge to all residents. Since 1968, each resident has been assigned a unique civil registration number, either at birth or upon immigration, through the Danish Civil Registration System [10]. This identifier allows for individual-level linkage to nationwide registries, including demographic and clinical data.

ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii cases

All patients with a first-time detection of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii in clinical or screening samples were identified through the regional microbiology database (wwBakt, Autonik AB, Sweden). Data retrieved included the date of testing, the submitting clinical ward (most commonly haematology or abdominal surgery), and sample type (clinical samples such as blood culture, urine, or abdominal drainage or screening samples such as rectal swabs).

Microbiological diagnostics

C. freundii was identified using the MALDI Biotyper (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) and tested for antimicrobial susceptibility by EUCAST disc diffusion (https://www.eucast.org/). Screening samples were enriched in Brain Heart Infusion broth and cultured on CHROMID® CARBA agar (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France). Isolates with reduced carbapenem susceptibility were confirmed using the ROSCO KPC/MBL and OXA-48 Confirm Kit (ROSCO Diagnostica, Albertslund, Denmark) and/or the NG-TEST® CARBA-5 assay (NG Biotech, Guipry, France). All carbapenemase-producing C. freundii isolates, whether from clinical or screening samples, were referred to the National Reference Laboratory for Antimicrobial Resistance at Statens Serum Institut, Copenhagen. Whole-genome sequencing was performed to confirm the presence of the blaNDM-1 gene and other resistance determinants, plasmid replicon type, and MLST [8]. Clonal comparison was done using SeqSphere+ v10.0.5 (Ridom, GmbH, Germany).

Contact precautions

Upon detecting carbapenemase-producing C. freundii, patients were promptly isolated within the ward, and contact precautions were implemented under the supervision of the local department of infection control. Relevant contact tracing measures were initiated, including screening for CPE using rectal swabs from other patients in the same ward [6]. CPE screening practices evolved over time and were primarily risk-based, in accordance with national guidelines [6]. On the abdominal surgery ward, from July 2017 to February 2021, drainage fluid samples from all hospitalised patients with abdominal drains were collected weekly for culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. From February 2021 onwards, systematic weekly rectal screening was implemented for all patients in the abdominal surgery ward with an expected hospital stay of more than three days.

Controls

We used regional hospitalisation data and risk-set sampling to select eight controls per case from patients hospitalised in the same ward and alive on the case’s index date. This ensured that controls were at risk of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii infection or colonisation at the same time as the corresponding case. None of the controls later tested positive for NDM-1-producing C. freundii, minimising misclassification of case/control status. Cases and controls were not matched on sex, age, or other demographic characteristics.

Data variables

We collected data to assess risk factors for infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii and to compare all-cause mortality between cases and controls. Demographic data (age and sex) and comorbidities were extracted from medical records, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [11] was calculated at admission. Medication use during the 180 days before admission was obtained from the Shared Medication Record (Fælles Medicinkort, FMK), and in-hospital medications were extracted from the hospital medication module. Pre-admission and in-hospital medication exposures were analysed separately. We included systemic antibiotics (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical [ATC] codes J01-J06), corticosteroids (H02), antineoplastic agents (L01), and other immunosuppressive treatments (L04). Hospital data included ward of admission, time from admission to the positive test (for cases) or index date (for controls), and length of hospital stay (LOS). Diagnostic (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10]) and procedure codes were used to identify relevant surgical and invasive procedures, including abdominal surgery, drainage procedures, gastrointestinal endoscopy, nephrostomy or urinary tract catheterisation, and central venous catheter insertion. These procedures were selected based on clinical relevance to abdominal surgery patients. Date of death was obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and categorical variables as counts and proportions. Univariable conditional logistic regression analysis was applied to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between each predefined risk factor and infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii. Risk factors included CCI score, LOS, concurrent diagnoses, in-hospital procedures, and prior medication use. The CCI was grouped as 0 (reference), 1–2, and ≥3 and LOS as ≤7 (reference), 8–14, and >14 days. Procedures (e.g. biliary, pancreatic, and endoscopy) were analysed as binary variables (yes/no). Medications were classified by ATC codes and included systemic antibiotics (ATC codes: J01–J06), antifungal agents (J02A), antivirals (J05), corticosteroids (H02), antineoplastic agents (L01), and immunosuppressants (L04). Exposure was defined separately for in-hospital exposure and for exposure within 180 days before admission as at least one administration of a drug from the respective ATC class and coded as a binary variable (yes/no). For the matched cohort study, follow-up extended from the index date to death or 31st August 2023, whichever occurred first. Time-to-event analyses accounted for the matched cohort and for right-censoring. Survival probabilities were estimated with Kaplan–Meier curves. As proportional hazards were violated for some covariates, Cox regression models were stratified into three intervals (0–30, 31–90, and 91–365 days) and adjusted for age, LOS, and CCI. Analyses were performed in STATA 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii in the North Denmark Region during 2012–2023

During the study period, 72 patients tested positive for ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii (Supplementary Figure 1). A total of 40 patients (55.6%) were identified through clinical samples (urine, n=17; abdominal drainage, n=10; blood, n=6; abdominal abscess, n=4; and other, n=3). The remaining 32 patients (44.4%) were identified through screening. Nineteen patients tested positive during admission to the haematological ward and 50 during admission to the abdominal surgery ward. The remaining three patients tested positive in outpatient settings without recent hospitalisation (within 30 days), although all had prior contact with the haematological ward. These three cases were excluded from the case–control study due to uncertainty regarding the timing of C. freundii acquisition and the inability to identify suitable matched controls.

Baseline characteristics of cases and controls

A total of 69 cases of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii and 552 matched control patients were included (Table I). The median age was similar between cases and controls (67 years [IQR= 60–77] vs 68 years [IQR= 56–77]), but a higher proportion of cases were males (78.3% vs 54.9%). The CCI distribution was comparable, though slightly more cases had a score ≥3 (33.3% vs 26.4%) (Supplementary Figure 2). Comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, and peripheral vascular disease were more frequent among cases. The prevalence of haematological malignancies, including leukaemia and lymphoma, were similar across the two groups, reflecting the number of patients sampled from the haematology ward in both groups. Medication use during the 180 days before admission showed no major differences. Approximately one-third of patients in each group had received systemic antibiotics, most commonly penicillins (ATC code: J01C). Admission data

The median time from admission to the date of positive results was nine days (IQR= 2–20) for cases, compared with three days (IQR= 1–7) from admission to the matched index date for controls. The median LOS was 21 days (IQR= 7–38) for cases and seven days (IQR= 3–14) for controls (Table II). A hospital stay of more than 14 days was associated with significantly increased odds of C. freundii positivity (OR = 6.4, 95% CI= 3.6–11.3; Supplementary Figure 3). Of the controls, 116 (21.0%) underwent CPE screening, primarily via rectal swabs, with abdominal drainage samples analysed in a subset. All tested negative. Concurrent haematological cancers and gastrointestinal malignancies were not significantly associated with an increased risk of ST18 C. freundii positivity. However, several acute intra-abdominal infections showed a strong association. Notably, diseases of the peritoneum (ICD-10 K65–K67; including peritonitis and abdominal abscesses) and disorders of the gallbladder, biliary tract, and pancreas (ICD-10 K80–K87) were associated with ORs of 4.4 (95% CI= 1.5–13.3) and 5.5 (95% CI= 3.0–10.3), respectively. At the single-diagnosis level, acute peritonitis (K65), cholangitis (K83), and pancreatitis (K85) were also significantly associated with an increased risk of C. freundii positivity, with ORs of 4.7 (95% CI= 1.5–14.5), 6.1 (95% CI= 2.2–16.7), and 4.3 (95% CI= 2.1–8.9), respectively. Surgical and interventional procedures were likewise strongly associated with C. freundii positivity. The most notable associations were observed for biliary tract operations and pancreatic surgery with ORs of 4.6 (95% CI= 2.7–7.8) and 4.4 (95% CI= 2.2–8.6), respectively. Additionally, minor gastroenterological procedures (KTJ) and endoscopies of the digestive tract (KUJ) were associated with ORs of 3.8 (95% CI= 2.1–6.8) and 1.8 (95% CI= 1.1–2.9), respectively (Supplementary Figure 4). Antibiotic treatment during admission

Targeted antimicrobial therapy for C. freundii infection was initiated in 18 cases, most commonly involving intravenous tigecycline (n = 6) and/or colistin (n = 17). In-hospital exposure to antibiotics, antifungals, antivirals, and immunosuppressive agents during the 180 days preceding the positive C. freundii test (or the matched index date for controls) was analysed. Prior exposure to several antimicrobial classes was significantly associated with increased odds of C. freundii. The strongest associations were observed for systemic vancomycin (J01XA01; OR = 13.9, 95% CI= 6.8–28.4), penicillins with beta-lactamase inhibitors (J01CR; OR = 8.8, 95% CI= 4.2–18.2), fluoroquinolones (OR=8.2, 95% CI= 4.6–14.9), and systemic antifungal agents (J02A; OR = 8.6, 95% CI= 4.7–15.6) (Figure 1). All-cause mortality

In the matched cohort study, all-cause mortality was higher among cases than among controls at 30, 90, and 365 days (Figure 2). However, after adjusting for age, LOS, and CCI, a significant association was only observed for 30-day mortality (adjusted hazard ratio = 2.2, 95% CI= 1.1–4.3). Discussion

This study describes an 11-year outbreak of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii at Aalborg University Hospital, the largest verified CPE outbreak in Denmark [[7], [8], [9]]. Key risk factors included prolonged hospitalisation, intra-abdominal infections, and invasive hepatopancreatobiliary procedures. Prior in-hospital exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics, particularly vancomycin, beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, fluoroquinolones, and systemic antifungals, was also significantly associated with positivity. Patients testing positive had higher 30-day all-cause mortality, even after adjusting for confounders.

Worldwide, most CPE outbreaks are caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae or Escherichia coli [[12], [13], [14]]. However, NDM-positive C. freundii outbreaks have also been reported internationally [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]], and European studies show that Citrobacter species are emerging as important carbapenemase carriers and reservoirs for multi-drug resistance genes [8,[17], [18], [19], [20]]. A German multi-centre study (2017—2019) reported increasing detection of Citrobacter, predominantly C. freundii, carrying diverse carbapenemases including NDM, OXA-48-like, and Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) [17]. In Poland, a genomic study of 118 metallo-β-lactamase-producing Citrobacter isolates showed C. freundii predominance with high ST diversity, including ST18, ST8, and ST22 [18]. In France, three major STs circulated, with OXA-48 being the most common carbapenemase [19]. Finnish data identified C. freundii as the third most common CPE species, with ST18 persisting in clinical and environmental samples [20]. Similarly, Danish national surveillance data have reported C. freundii ST18 and other STs (ST22, ST65, ST91, and ST116) involved in verified outbreaks across the Danish administrative regions, with diverse carbapenemase types including NDM-1, OXA-48, and OXA-232 [7]. Taken together, these findings highlight that C. freundii and related species are emerging as significant drivers of carbapenemase dissemination in European healthcare settings.

Patients tested positive primarily during admission to the haematology or abdominal surgery wards. Local environmental surveillance data demonstrated persistent contamination in both departments, supporting nosocomial transmission. This is further supported by a recent study from the same wards, in which NDM-1-producing Klebsiella oxytoca was traced to toilet environments as a likely source of patient transmission [9]. In the haematology ward, extensive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and immunosuppressive therapies may have facilitated intestinal colonisation, whereas in the abdominal surgery ward, frequent use of antibiotics, invasive procedures, and prolonged hospital stays likely contributed to ongoing transmission. Complete eradication appears unlikely, consistent with other European outbreaks [20].

The impact of comorbidities on CPE acquisition remains debated [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. No significant differences in age or CCI were found, though more cases had a CCI score ≥3. Specific comorbidities were slightly more common among cases which may suggest increased vulnerability, though power limitations and unmeasured confounders remain.

We identified specific admitting diagnoses as strong risk factors for ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii, particularly peritoneal diseases and disorders of the gallbladder, biliary tract, and pancreas. These conditions are often severe and involve frequent use of antibiotics, likely explaining the increased risk observed. A retrospective study by Yang et al. [25] of patients with acute pancreatitis found that prior abdominal surgery and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography were independent risk factors for CRE infection, most commonly K. pneumoniae (35%), E. coli (33%), Enterobacter cloacae (25%), and C. freundii (7%). Consistent with these findings, our study showed that gastrointestinal procedures, especially biliary and pancreatic surgery, were strongly associated with C. freundii positivity, aligning with reports linking invasive procedures to carbapenem-resistant C. freundii [16] and CPE-related intra-abdominal infections [26].

The median LOS was considerably longer among cases than among controls (21 vs 7 days), and an LOS >14 days was associated with a more than sixfold increase in risk. The median time from admission to the first positive test among cases was nine days, indicating that most patients likely acquired the strain during hospitalisation. These findings are consistent with previous reports linking prolonged hospitalisation to CPE acquisition [[26], [27], [28]] and support LOS-based screening strategies in high-risk wards to improve early detection and strengthen infection control.

No significant association was found for medications used before hospitalisation, whereas several in-hospital antibiotic classes were strongly associated with an increased risk, consistent with studies identifying broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure as a major driver of CPE infection and colonisation [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]]. In our study, systemic vancomycin, penicillins with beta-lactamase inhibitors (e.g. piperacillin/tazobactam), fluoroquinolones, and antifungals showed the strongest associations. Vancomycin is used for severe Gram-positive infections, while piperacillin/tazobactam is widely prescribed in haematology patients and for severe intra-abdominal infections. Notably, penicillins with beta-lactamase inhibitors account for approximately one-quarter of all antibiotic use in Danish hospitals [7], consistent with earlier work linking these classes to an increased CPE risk, including in patients with intra-abdominal infections [26]. Although carbapenems are rarely used in Denmark due to restrictive national antimicrobial stewardship policies [[5], [6], [7]], their use was also associated with C. freundii acquisition. Similar associations have been reported elsewhere [26,31]. Even limited carbapenem exposure may therefore contribute to the selection and persistence of CPE in vulnerable patients. The association between systemic antifungal agents, particularly echinocandins and fluconazole, and C. freundii likely reflects the clinical complexity of patients requiring prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Previous studies of critically ill and surgical patients have shown that cumulative exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals is a key risk factor for CPE colonisation [26,28,29,31]. Glucocorticoid use was also associated with increased odds of C. freundii acquisition, though weaker than for antibiotics, while other immunosuppressive agents showed no clear associations.

The survival analysis showed a twofold increase in 30-day mortality among cases compared with controls, while differences at 90 and 365 days were not significant after adjustment. This aligns with a meta-analysis by Soontaros et al., reporting a pooled adjusted OR of 2.85 for mortality among patients with CPE infections [32]. As both infected and colonised patients were included, separating their individual contributions to mortality was not possible. Nonetheless, previous studies indicate that CPE colonisation alone may confer a mortality risk comparable with active infection [27], underscoring the importance of identifying and managing both groups. However, in the absence of detailed measures of disease severity, infection or colonisation with ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii in our setting may primarily act as a marker of severe underlying illness rather than an independent driver of short-term mortality.

A key strength of this study is its comprehensive 11-year dataset, covering all confirmed cases of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii at Aalborg University Hospital. The use of risk set sampling ensured that controls were hospitalised at the same time and in the same ward as cases, minimising selection bias and yielding OR estimates that approximate relative risks. Another strength is the integration of detailed microbiological and clinical data, enabling a robust assessment of risk factors, treatments, and outcomes.

However, the study also has limitations. First, the study included two distinct patient populations, haematology and abdominal surgery patients, with different clinical characteristics. While this reflects the real-world distribution of ST18 C. freundii, it complicates interpretation of certain associations. For instance, haematological malignancies were not associated with an increased risk, likely due to high baseline prevalence in both groups, whereas significant associations (e.g. intra-abdominal infections and hepatobiliary procedures) were primarily observed among surgical patients. Second, both infected and colonised patients were included, but the limited sample size precluded stratified analyses, restricting evaluation of their individual contributions to risk and mortality. Third, although screening intensity increased from 2021 onwards in the abdominal surgery ward, CPE screening was not systematic throughout the entire study period, and some asymptomatic carriers among controls may therefore have been missed, potentially underestimating certain associations. Fourth, to limit dataset complexity, we did not quantify antimicrobial duration or cumulative exposure, although both likely increased risk of acquisition. Fifth, due to the exploratory nature of this study, we identified individual predefined risk factors only and did not account for potential interactions between them. It is highly possible that some risk factors may influence each other and that their combined effect could be greater or smaller than the individual effects observed. This should be investigated in future studies with greater statistical power. Sixth, objective measures of acute disease severity such as validated severity-of-illness scores or information on intensive care unit admission were not included, limiting our ability to fully account for differences in clinical severity between cases and controls. Finally, transmission pathways beyond the known reservoir in ward toilets could not be determined. Future studies should include larger, ward-specific analyses to clarify subgroup differences and transmission dynamics.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into a prolonged outbreak of ST18 NDM-1-producing C. freundii, identifying intra-abdominal infections, hepatobiliary procedures, prolonged hospitalisation, and prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals as key risk factors. Although C. freundii remains less common than K. pneumoniae or E. coli, its persistence in hospital environments poses a major infection control challenge. Colonisation or infection was associated with increased 30-day mortality; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as it may reflect underlying disease severity rather than an independent causal effect. These results highlight the importance of targeted screening, antimicrobial stewardship, and environmental control measures to prevent transmission and identify high-risk patients.
