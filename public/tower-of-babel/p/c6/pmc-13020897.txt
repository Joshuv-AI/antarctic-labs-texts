Integrating multimodal management and molecular profiling in a patient with BRAF V600E–positive melanoma and brain metastases
Authors: David Gritsch, Riley M Fadden, Maura K Mahar, Melin J Khandsekar, Kevin S Oh, Daniel P Cahil, Gavin P Dunn, Ryan J Sullivan, Priscilla K Brastianos
Published: 2026

CASE OVERVIEW

A man in his late 20s was diagnosed with BRAF V600E‐mutated cutaneous melanoma of the left knee (T3a) and underwent wide local excision. One year later, he had a solitary local recurrence resected and received 12 months of adjuvant dabrafenib and trametinib. Seven months after completion of adjuvant therapy, he developed headaches and a right visual field deficit. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a solitary left occipital enhancing lesion. Subtotal resection confirmed melanoma brain metastasis (MBM), and he initiated adjuvant ipilimumab/nivolumab, complicated by Clostridiodies difficile colitis and subsequent immune‐related colitis requiring corticosteroids and vedolizumab. Postoperative radiotherapy (RT) was completed. Six months later, he underwent stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) to a new left putamen lesion (hereinafter referred to as basal ganglia lesion). Nivolumab was resumed but discontinued because of severe inflammatory arthritis requiring immunosuppression.

The patient presented to our institution 6 months later. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography identified a new right thigh metastasis. MRI revealed apparent enlargement of the left occipital and basal ganglia lesions (Figure 1). Ipilimumab/nivolumab treatment was reinitiated but was complicated 3 months later by worsening right hemiparesis and hemianopia in the setting of hemorrhagic changes in both lesions. Stereotactic biopsy of the occipital lesion confirmed radiation necrosis. He subsequently developed acute worsening of right hemiparesis, and a lacunar infarct, attributed to mass effect on the lenticulostriate vessels, was identified. Given his biopsy‐proven radiation necrosis and complete response of the right thigh metastasis, with no other systemic disease identified at the time, immunotherapy was discontinued (the last dose was received 3–4 weeks before biopsy).

Three months after the biopsy, resection of the enlarging occipital lesion was performed in the setting of worsening hemianopia and again confirmed radiation necrosis. One month later, the basal ganglia lesion showed further hemorrhagic progression and enlarging nodular enhancement with worsening edema, headaches, and aphasia. Surgical re‐resection was not recommended. Multidisciplinary review concluded that, although radiation necrosis could not be excluded, a focal nodular region of enhancement raised concern for active tumor progression. Given diagnostic uncertainty and the need for rapid intracranial disease control, the team initiated encorafenib/binimetinib as the systemic therapy most likely to produce a prompt response and help distinguish tumor progression from radiation necrosis. Imaging 4 weeks later revealed reduced lesion size and edema. Over the following months, he was doing well clinically with only mild symptom burden and was able to work full time. After 7 months on targeted therapy, MRI revealed asymptomatic progression in the basal ganglia and a new occipital lesion, which was treated with hypofractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT; 3800 centigrays in 10 fractions). During this course of radiation, he developed worsening headaches, and high‐dose corticosteroids were initiated for symptomatic management.

Four months after the patient completed radiation, radiographic basal ganglia progression was again noted. At that time, he remained on high‐dose steroids and was overall feeling fairly well; although, by this time, he had stopped working because of fatigue and neurocognitive difficulties. Laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) of the basal ganglia lesion was performed for suspected tumor progression with symptomatic improvement in executive function, aphasia, and abulia. Three months later, systemic relapse was identified on positron emission tomography/computed tomography, which demonstrated a new, fluorodeoxyglucose‐avid, left lower‐lobe pulmonary nodule, a left retroperitoneal nodule, and focal uptake in the right tibia. Encorafenib/binimetinib was continued, and nivolumab/relatlimab was added after new cerebral enhancement and biopsy‐confirmed retroperitoneal disease were established. This combination—dual mitogen‐activated protein kinase inhibition together with dual immune checkpoint blockade—is not standard practice because of a lack of prospective evidence for improved efficacy and the potential for increased toxicity. 1 , 2 , 3 After multidisciplinary discussion reflecting the patient's rapidly evolving disease, limited remaining therapeutic options, and motivation, the decision was made to proceed with treatment despite the anticipated risks and uncertain benefit. At the time of initiating nivolumab/relatlimab, he had successfully tapered dexamethasone to 2 mg daily. Imaging revealed reduced edema and lesion size on steroid taper.

Approximately 4 months later, new intralesional hemorrhage occurred in the left basal ganglia lesion, temporally associated with the use of nonsteroidal noninflammatory drugs. Short‐interval MRI demonstrated further lesion enlargement with worsening hemorrhage, edema, and midline shift. At the time, he suffered from worsening right hemiparesis as well as insomnia, fatigue, generalized malaise, and nausea/vomiting. Given continued clinical and radiographic progression despite maximal therapy, he was transitioned to hospice care and died approximately 3 years and 4 months after his initial diagnosis of intracranial disease (Figure 2).

NEURO‐ONCOLOGY PERSPECTIVE

Despite representing only approximately 1% of all cancers, melanoma is the third most common cause of brain metastases after lung and breast cancers. 4 , 5 , 6 Brain metastases are a major contributor to morbidity and mortality in advanced melanoma and often define the disease course in affected individuals. 7 , 8 Although central nervous system (CNS) involvement is most often observed in the context of widespread metastatic disease, up to 20% of patients may present with isolated brain lesions. 9 , 10 The interval from primary diagnosis to detection of brain metastases varies widely, with some cases occurring many years later. 11 , 12 Neurologic complications are common and often severe in patients with metastatic melanoma. Clinical manifestations are dictated by lesion location and may include headache, seizures, focal neurologic deficits, cognitive decline, and, in some patients, acute deterioration because of hemorrhage or edema. 13 , 14 , 15 On a molecular level, MBMs frequently harbor activating BRAF mutations (approximately 50% of patients), with NRAS mutations identified in 15%–20%. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 These alterations drive constitutive activation of the MAPK pathway, and co‐alterations in PTEN can activate parallel PI3K/AKT signaling. 17 , 18 Importantly, BRAF mutations are typically concordant between brain and extracranial metastases, enabling systemic therapy selection from extracranial tissue or plasma. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 However, it has been demonstrated that brain metastases may acquire additional molecular alterations over time, reflecting branched evolutionary trajectories distinct from the primary tumor and systemic disease. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Preclinical models and human data also suggest that MBMs exhibit increased oxidative phosphorylation, potentially contributing to CNS tropism and therapeutic resistance. 24 Given the high rate of concordance but potential for brain‐specific evolution, molecular testing of the brain metastasis should be considered when resection is otherwise clinically indicated. 26 Emerging liquid biopsy approaches using cerebrospinal fluid or plasma circulating free DNA may complement tissue‐based molecular profiling in the future but are not yet standard. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Historically, the prognosis after a diagnosis of brain metastases was dismal, with median survival of 4–6 months. 13 , 31 , 32 , 33 However, the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). 7 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 BRAF/MEK inhibitors in BRAF‐mutant disease, and SRS has significantly improved outcomes in selected patients. 7 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37

Radiation necrosis and steroid management

Although systemic therapy is foundational in the treatment of patients who have advanced melanoma with asymptomatic MBMs, local modalities like SRS remain essential for achieving rapid control of symptomatic or progressive intracranial disease. 34 However, SRS carries a risk of radiation necrosis, which is a delayed complication involving inflammatory and ischemic injury to previously irradiated brain tissue. Incidence estimates range from 0% to 30%, reflecting variability in diagnostic criteria and imaging definitions. 7 , 38 Distinguishing radiation necrosis from tumor progression is challenging because both can appear as enhancing lesions with surrounding edema on MRI. Advanced modalities, such as magnetic resonance spectroscopy and perfusion imaging, may aid in differentiation but lack standardized validation. Consequently, clinical assessment combined with serial MRI remains the diagnostic mainstay, with biopsy or resection reserved for cases in which management hinges on diagnostic clarity. 7 , 38 Our patient exemplified this diagnostic dilemma, with recurrent left occipital and basal ganglia lesions demonstrating hemorrhagic and mass effect features concerning for either progression or radiation necrosis. Initial occipital biopsy confirmed necrosis without viable tumor, sparing him from premature escalation of systemic therapy. However, subsequent lesion growth, edema, and neurologic decline despite corticosteroids ultimately necessitated LITT. Management of symptomatic radiation necrosis typically begins with dexamethasone. Whereas prolonged or high‐dose therapy (≥16 mg daily) may be necessary, it may blunt the efficacy of ICIs. Tapering should follow clinical and radiographic improvement, generally reducing the dose every 3–7 days. Our patient remained steroid‐dependent for prolonged intervals, requiring endocrine evaluation for adrenal suppression. When indicated, transitioning to physiologic prednisone may facilitate safer tapering, and endocrinology input is recommended for complex cases. Bevacizumab, a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor, has shown benefit in radiation necrosis, with a 65.5% response rate in a randomized trial and universal benefit in a small brain metastasis cohort. 39 , 40 Although, in this patient, the risk of intracranial bleeding from hemorrhagic brain metastases precluded its use, it remains a key steroid‐sparing option for refractory edema. In contrast, small‐molecule VEGF/VEGF receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors have not been systematically studied for the treatment of radiation necrosis and thus are not standard for this indication. Because these agents also inhibit the VEGF pathway and have been associated with bleeding and thromboembolism in patients who have cancer, similar caution generally applies, particularly in tumors with hemorrhagic propensity or in the setting of recent intralesional hemorrhage. 41 , 42 , 43

Intracranial hemorrhage

Intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a well recognized complication of MBM. Melanoma, along with renal cell carcinoma and choriocarcinoma, has one of the highest hemorrhagic propensities among brain tumors. 44 , 45 Classic clinicopathologic series have documented that up to approximately 50% of metastatic melanoma lesions are hemorrhagic. 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 In our patient, multiple episodes of hemorrhage occurred within the left basal ganglia lesion, contributing to neurologic decline and complicating both diagnostic assessment and therapeutic planning. In practical terms, clinicians should assume that any MBM may hemorrhage during its course, and sudden neurologic deterioration in these patients should trigger emergent workup for ICH. Anticoagulation is an important risk factor for ICH in the setting of MBM. Recent data indicate that long‐term anticoagulation is associated with higher likelihood of hemorrhagic stroke in patients with MBM. 44 Reported ICH rates with therapeutic anticoagulation range from approximately 3% to 16% in patients who have MBM, with variation depending on the study population and follow‐up duration. 50 , 51 Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) appear to demonstrate comparable or slightly lower ICH rates than low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH) in this population. Recent meta‐analysis and cohort studies suggest that DOACs may be associated with a lower overall ICH risk compared with LMWH. 50 , 52 It should be noted however, that patients with actively bleeding or large hemorrhagic metastases are typically excluded from cohort studies, limiting generalizability to these highest risk populations. Therefore, decisions regarding the initiation or resumption of anticoagulation in patients with hemorrhagic MBMs remain highly individualized, requiring assessment of thrombotic risk, confirmation of hematoma stability on short‐interval imaging, and multidisciplinary input from neuro‐oncology, neurosurgery, and hematology. Notably, after a diagnosis of bilateral pulmonary emboli in our patient, anticoagulation was deferred because of the elevated risk of ICH, underlining the need for individualized, multidisciplinary evaluation to balance the thrombotic and hemorrhagic risks in this population. For small, asymptomatic hemorrhages, conservative management is typically sufficient. This includes close observation with serial imaging, tight blood pressure control, and medical therapy, such as corticosteroids, to reduce vasogenic edema and mass effect. If the hemorrhage is large or causes significant neurologic deficits, surgical intervention is often considered because this can simultaneously remove the tumor and evacuate the hematoma, relieving pressure on the brain. Resumption of anticoagulants, if needed at all, is delayed until the acute phase passes. Each decision is case‐by‐case, factoring in the patient's thrombotic risk versus neurologic risk.

Venous thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a significant complication in patients with brain metastases, with an incidence rate of up to 30%. 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 Patients with MBM face a dual complication: an elevated risk of VTE and a concurrent risk of ICH from the metastases themselves. 57 In a recent study of 228 patients with melanoma who were receiving ICIs, 20.6% developed thromboembolism over a median follow‐up of approximately 2 years. 58 A 2020 study derived a risk‐prediction model (the PICOS [thrombogenic primary, immobilization, chemotherapy, obesity, steroid] score) for patients with brain metastasis, identifying five independent predictors of VTE. 59 These included thrombogenic primary tumor type (including melanoma), immobilization, chemotherapy, obesity, and dexamethasone use. 59 Our patient developed bilateral pulmonary emboli after prolonged corticosteroid use and limited mobility, both of which are recognized VTE risk factors. The treatment of VTE in patients with brain tumors typically involves anticoagulation for at least 6 months and/or inferior vena cava filter placement. 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 The use of LMWH has been favored over warfarin for the treatment of VTE in patients who have cancer given evidence for higher effectiveness and comparable bleeding risk. 64 , 66 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 More recently, DOACs have been used increasingly in patients with systemic cancer because of studies demonstrating safety and efficacy comparable to those of LMWH, although data on patients with brain metastases remain limited. 64 , 74 Our case exemplifies the clinical dilemma in MBM: the need to balance thrombotic risk against the significant danger of intracranial hemorrhage. Decisions around anticoagulation in this setting must be individualized, weighing lesion stability, bleeding history, and overall prognosis. If a metastasis has recently bled or is large/edematous, clinicians may delay anticoagulation or use reduced doses until the acute hemorrhagic risk abates. Consultation with neurosurgery or hematology can help stratify ICH risk. Conversely, if a VTE is extensive or life‐threatening (e.g., massive pulmonary embolism), full‐dose anticoagulation may be indicated despite brain metastases, possibly after urgent interventions (e.g., thrombectomy, inferior vena cava filter placement).

Tumor‐related seizures

Melanoma as a primary tumor type is an independent risk factor for developing seizures in patients who have brain metastasis. 75 In a population‐based analysis, MBMs were associated with an approximately 1.4‐fold higher hazard of seizure compared with nonsmall cell lung cancer metastases. 75 Beyond histology, the burden and location of metastatic lesions are important. Patients with multiple metastases (more than four lesions) have a higher risk of seizures; and lesions in high‐risk locations, such as the frontal and temporal lobes, are particularly epileptogenic. 75 Local therapy (resection or radiosurgery) can contribute to improved seizure control; however, some modern treatments, such as SRS and immunotherapies (which can cause inflammation or edema), are suspected to increase late seizure risk in otherwise stable patients. 75 Pharmacologic seizure prophylaxis with antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) is generally indicated in patients who have MBMs and tumor‐related seizures. 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 Prophylactic AED therapy in patients with brain tumors who have not yet developed seizures is currently not recommended because clinical studies have failed to demonstrate a clinical benefit for this approach. 80 , 81 , 82 Postoperative seizure prophylaxis with AEDs, however, is commonly used in clinical practice after neurosurgical interventions. 83 , 84 There is currently no clear evidence from clinical studies that would support the choice of one AED over another. However, AEDs that do not interfere with the hepatic drug metabolism, such as levetiracetam, topiramate, or lacosamide, are generally preferred given the decreased risk of drug–drug interactions. 80 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 From a cancer neuroscience perspective, there is growing interest in glutamatergic mechanisms as contributors to tumor‐related hyperexcitability and possibly tumor progression, particularly in gliomas, for which peritumoral glutamate release and AMPA (α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid)‐receptor–mediated network hyperexcitability have been implicated in tumor‐related seizures. 89 , 90 , 91 Therefore, perampanel, a selective, noncompetitive AMPA receptor antagonist, is an appealing option conceptually, and small series in glioma‐associated epilepsy suggest it can be an effective and generally well tolerated add‐on agent in refractory patients. 92 , 93 However, clinical data remain limited, and there is currently no evidence that selecting perampanel over more commonly used agents, such as levetiracetam, improves oncologic outcomes or modifies brain metastasis biology.

Cerebrovascular accident

Cerebrovascular accident (CVA) is another rare but important complication in patients with brain metastases. Observational studies demonstrate that roughly 1.4% of all patients with cancer will experience a cerebrovascular event (ischemic or hemorrhagic) during the first year after cancer diagnosis. 94 , 95 , 96 Cancer‐related CVAs often have different underlying causes than typical atherosclerotic CVAs. 97 , 98 , 99 Tumors can provoke a hypercoagulable state through secretion of procoagulant molecules (like tissue factor, cancer procoagulant) and through systemic inflammation. 97 , 98 , 99 Apart from circulating emboli, a tumor can directly cause CVA through local vessel compression or invasion, as in our patient. 100 , 101 , 102 Furthermore, both chemotherapy and radiation therapy have been linked to accelerated atherosclerosis and vascular injury. 97 , 98 , 99 The management of acute ischemic CVA in patients with brain metastases requires individualized consideration. Intravenous thrombolysis is generally contraindicated in patients with known intracranial intra‐axial neoplasms because of the heightened risk of intracerebral hemorrhage, according to American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines. 103 After the acute phase, secondary CVA prevention should be tailored to the patient's overall risk profile. In our patient, antiplatelet therapy was deferred because of concurrent hemorrhagic risk. Importantly, standard measures like controlling blood pressure, optimizing diabetes and cholesterol, and smoking cessation should not be overlooked; these help reduce typical CVA risk and are certainly applicable to patients with cancer as well. 104

NEUROSURGERY PERSPECTIVE

Surgical resection remains a cornerstone of local therapy for select patients with MBMs, particularly in cases of solitary or oligometastatic disease, mass effect, or diagnostic uncertainty. 34 Surgery provides immediate relief of elevated intracranial pressure and can be critical in reducing steroid dependency and improving neurologic function. 105 , 106 , 107 It also enables histopathologic confirmation, which can help differentiate viable tumor from treatment‐related changes, such as radiation necrosis. 34 Early studies established that surgical resection of brain metastases can improve both survival and quality of life. 105 , 106 , 107 In the modern era, the role of neurosurgery remains vital but must be integrated with recent advancements in focal radiation techniques and systemic treatments. Surgical decision‐making occurs in a multidisciplinary framework, taking into account tumor genetics, radiographic features, the availability of systemic treatment options, and the anticipated risk profile given a tumor's specific location. 34 For example, our patient's case highlights how surgery was used at multiple junctures: an occipital metastasis was resected to alleviate visual symptoms and obtain tissue; a deep hemorrhagic basal ganglia lesion, however, was deemed too high‐risk for open resection and was managed with alternate strategies, targeted therapy, and, later, laser ablation. As in our patient, the integration of surgery with other modalities is often sequential and patient‐specific. 34 In our patient, for instance, resection of the occipital lesion provided symptom relief and allowed the initiation of immunotherapy with less immediate risk of treatment‐related edema. Conversely, when brain metastases are small and asymptomatic, it may be reasonable to start systemic therapy first and monitor, especially in the setting of highly active agents, such as MAP kinase inhibitor combinations (BRAF/MEK inhibitors) or combination immunotherapy. However, it is important to note that targeted therapy generally demonstrates less durable intracranial efficacy compared with extracranial disease. 34 Multidisciplinary tumor board input is critical in sequencing these therapies optimally. Current guidelines emphasize that all symptomatic brain metastases warrant prompt local therapy (surgery and/or radiation) regardless of the systemic treatment planned. 34 Importantly, patients with uncontrolled widespread systemic disease or multiple (four or more) brain metastases are less likely to benefit from an aggressive surgical approach. In such patients, surgery is usually reserved for palliation of a dominant symptomatic lesion or when needed to avert acute neurologic catastrophe (e.g., hemorrhage or hydrocephalus). For most other patients, less invasive options like SRS are preferred for managing multiple small metastases. 34 The presence of active extracranial disease should always be weighed: if a patient's overall prognosis is only a few months because of progressive visceral metastases, a major brain operation may not be appropriate. Conversely, if systemic disease is reasonably controlled, treating brain metastases aggressively can significantly improve both quantity and quality of life. 34 , 106 If a brain lesion is the first manifestation of cancer or if there is any doubt that a lesion is metastatic melanoma, surgery (or at least a stereotactic biopsy) can help to establish a histologic diagnosis. Likewise, in patients with prior radiation, distinguishing tumor recurrence from radiation necrosis can be challenging on imaging alone, and a surgical biopsy/resection often provides a definitive answer in these cases. 34 , 108 This was exemplified in our patient, in whom a biopsy of the enlarging occipital lesion revealed only treatment‐related necrosis, sparing him from a potentially unnecessary change in systemic therapy. However, as illustrated in our patient, even biopsy‐confirmed radiation necrosis may later demonstrate radiographic progression, underscoring the ongoing diagnostic complexity and the limitations of imaging and tissue sampling in isolation. Finally, resection of metastases can also yield tissue for comprehensive molecular analysis, which may reveal actionable mutations or resistance mechanisms. 26 , 34

Laser interstitial thermal therapy

One emerging, minimally invasive option is LITT, which uses MRI‐guided laser fiber ablation to destroy tumor tissue or radiation necrosis in situ. LITT has shown particular promise for lesions not easily accessible by open surgery (e.g., deep‐seated metastases in the basal ganglia or thalamus) and for treating radionecrosis if resection is undesired. 34 , 109 , 110 , 111 In a prospective, multicenter trial (Laser Ablation After Stereotactic Radiosurgery [LAASR]; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01651078), LITT was performed in patients who had progressive lesions after prior SRS (including both recurrent tumors and radiation necrosis). 112 The results were encouraging: at 26 weeks (6 months) postprocedure, the local progression‐free survival (PFS) rate was 74% and the overall survival (OS) rate was 72%. 112 Approximately 30% of patients were able to taper off of high‐dose corticosteroids after LITT, reflecting an effective reduction of edema. 112 Importantly, LITT stabilized or improved neurologic function and quality of life in the majority of patients, with no significant differences in complication rates between patients who were treated for active tumor versus those who were treated for necrosis. 112 In our patient, LITT was pursued as a salvage, minimally invasive, local therapy for a deep hemorrhagic basal ganglia lesion that had previously been treated with SRS and later fractionated re‐irradiation, for which open resection was not recommended, and the residual/recurrent tumor versus treatment effect remained uncertain. The laser ablation produced initial clinical improvement and reduction in lesion size, supporting its role as a salvage therapy in a challenging location. It should be noted that LITT is not without risks; potential adverse events include intracerebral hemorrhage, edema from the ablation zone, or thermal damage to adjacent fiber tracts; however, in experienced hands, it can be performed safely with a shorter hospital stay than a typical craniotomy (often 1–3 days). 34 , 109 , 110 , 111 Ongoing studies are examining the combination of LITT with immunotherapy because the thermal ablation might release tumor antigens and enhance immune response, a strategy that is intriguing for melanoma, although not yet proven. 113

Postoperative management and multidisciplinary care

The neurosurgery perspective would not be complete without emphasizing the importance of coordinated postoperative care. Surgery is rarely curative on its own; instead, it sets the stage for adjuvant therapies. After resection of a metastasis, we aim to initiate radiation to the resection cavity typically within 3–4 weeks (after the wound has sufficiently healed). Systemic therapy is typically resumed as soon as feasible postsurgery. Targeted therapies (BRAF/MEK inhibitors) are usually restarted once the patient is eating and medically stable because they have relatively low impact on wound healing. Immunotherapy can also be initiated or resumed postoperatively, although immunotherapy must be managed in the context of perioperative steroids. High‐dose corticosteroids given for cerebral edema (common around surgery) can dampen the efficacy of ICIs. 34 Therefore, a common practice is to perform surgery—which itself helps reduce edema—and then taper steroids as quickly as clinically safe, before or while starting immunotherapy. Our patient's journey reflects these challenges: after his initial surgery, adjuvant ipilimumab plus nivolumab was started once he recovered, and radiation therapy was administered to the resection cavity. Unfortunately, he developed severe immune‐related colitis, which was managed by the medical oncology and gastroenterology teams. This exemplifies the close coordination needed between neurosurgery, neuro‐oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, and other specialties.

MEDICAL ONCOLOGY PERSPECTIVE

The introduction of molecularly targeted therapy and immune checkpoint blockade has dramatically reshaped the management of MBMs, offering the potential for durable intracranial control in a disease historically associated with dismal outcomes. Early systemic therapies demonstrated limited CNS efficacy, and patients who had brain metastases were often excluded from registration trials. However, accumulating prospective data now support the safety and efficacy of systemic treatment for intracranial disease (Table 1).

In this patient, molecular profiling revealed a BRAF V600E mutation, an actionable alteration found in nearly one half of cutaneous melanomas and often concordant between primary tumors and brain metastases. 19 , 114 , 115 This finding informed systemic treatment selection across multiple disease stages. After the development of immunotherapy‐related toxicity and intracranial disease progression, encorafenib/binimetinib was initiated to achieve rapid intracranial control. Immunotherapy was later reintroduced in combination with targeted therapy in response to further disease progression. It is important to note that the concurrent use of BRAF/MEK inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade falls outside standard treatment paradigms. Existing data do not demonstrate improved outcomes with this approach, and toxicity can be substantial. In this patient, the combination was pursued only after multidisciplinary review in the context of aggressive intracranial and systemic disease and limited remaining therapeutic options. The presence of a BRAF mutation enabled flexible sequencing and integration of targeted and immune‐based modalities, allowing treatment to be tailored to disease kinetics, steroid exposure, and evolving intracranial and systemic burden.

Systemic chemotherapy

The role of chemotherapy in MBMs remains limited in modern practice because of modest response rates and minimal survival benefit. Agents like cisplatin, temozolomide, and fotemustine have demonstrated variable intracranial response rates ranging from 5% to 25%, but responses are often nondurable. 116 , 117 , 118 , 119 , 120 , 121 , 122 , 123 , 124 , 125 Dacarbazine, historically the only US Food and Drug Administration‐approved cytotoxic agent for melanoma, offers even less intracranial activity because of poor CNS penetration and has largely fallen out of use. 126 In carefully selected patients with refractory symptomatic disease, who have exhausted ICIs and targeted therapies, oral temozolomide may be considered as a palliative option, as it is better tolerated than dacarbazine and achieves higher CNS exposure, although overall efficacy remains limited. 122 , 123 , 126 , 127 When temozolomide is administered, particularly in patients receiving concurrent corticosteroids, monitoring for lymphopenia and considering Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis is recommended.

Targeted therapy: BRAF/MEK inhibitors

Combination BRAF and MEK inhibition has become a cornerstone in the management of BRAF V600‐mutant MBM. The COMBI‐MB trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02039947), a nonrandomized, open‐label, phase 2 study, evaluated dabrafenib plus trametinib across four patient cohorts with BRAF V600E/K–mutant MBMs stratified by prior treatment, symptom status, and performance status. 115 In cohort A (n = 76; asymptomatic patients with no prior local therapy and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1), the intracranial overall response rate was 58%, with a median PFS of 5.6 months and a median OS of 10.8 months. 115 Cohorts B–D (patients with prior local therapy, symptomatic disease, or BRAF V600K mutations) demonstrated lower response rates (intracranial overall response rate, 44%–59%) and shorter median PFS (3.5–5.5 months), highlighting the impact of baseline disease burden and performance status on therapeutic outcomes. 115 The intracranial disease control rate exceeded 75% in all cohorts, though responses were generally less durable than those observed extracranially. 115 Vemurafenib combined with cobimetinib has also demonstrated intracranial activity, though data remain more limited. 128 In the TRICOTEL phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03625141), 65 patients with BRAF V600–mutant melanoma and active brain metastases received vemurafenib (720 mg twice daily), cobimetinib (60 mg daily, 21 of 28 days), and atezolizumab (840 mg intravenously every 2 weeks). 129 Among the 53 evaluable patients, the intracranial objective response rate was 42%, with a median duration of response of 7.4 months. 129 Grade 3 or greater adverse events occurred in 68% of patients, although there were no treatment‐related deaths. 129 The combination demonstrated manageable toxicity and clinically meaningful intracranial activity in a population historically excluded from immunotherapy trials. Encorafenib plus binimetinib has demonstrated encouraging results in more limited studies, including previously treated populations. A retrospective analysis of heavily pretreated patients with MBMs reported an intracranial overall response rate of 33%, suggesting utility as a salvage strategy. 130 The POLARIS trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT03911869), a multicenter, phase 2 study, evaluated this regimen in treatment‐naive patients who had BRAF V600–mutant melanoma and asymptomatic MBMs. In the safety lead‐in cohort (n = 10), the brain metastasis response rate was 60%, with one complete response and five partial responses. 130 In the subsequent dose‐expansion phase, two of three patients achieved partial responses, yielding a brain metastasis response rate of 67%. 130 Although the study was terminated early because of accrual challenges, the regimen demonstrated favorable intracranial activity and tolerability. Collectively, these data support the role of BRAF/MEK inhibitor combinations in selected patients with BRAF V600–mutant MBMs and underscore the importance of prospective studies to optimize sequencing and integration with local therapies.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors: PD‐1 and CTLA‐4 blockade

Immune checkpoint blockade has fundamentally altered the therapeutic landscape of advanced melanoma, including for patients with brain metastases. Ipilimumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte–associated protein 4, was among the earliest agents evaluated in this setting. In a phase 2 trial of 72 patients with MBMs who received ipilimumab at a dose of 10 mg/kg (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT00623766), intracranial disease control (complete response, partial response, or stable disease at 12 weeks) was achieved in 24% of asymptomatic patients who did not require corticosteroids and in 10% of symptomatic patients who received steroids. 131 The NIBIT‐M1 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01654629) further evaluated ipilimumab in combination with the alkylating agent fotemustine in 20 patients with asymptomatic MBMs, reporting an intracranial response rate of 50%, including durable responses in a subset of patients. 132 Anti‐programmed cell death protein 1 (anti–PD‐1) monotherapy has also demonstrated efficacy in MBMs. A single‐arm phase 2 study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02085070) evaluated pembrolizumab (10 mg/kg every 2 weeks) in patients who had 18 melanoma with untreated MBMs, yielding four intracranial responses (22%) with a median follow‐up of 7 months. 133 Neurologic adverse events occurred in 17%, including seizures and cognitive dysfunction, although the overall safety profile was acceptable. 133

Dual immune checkpoint blockade using nivolumab (anti–PD‐1) and ipilimumab (anti–CTLA‐4) has demonstrated superior intracranial activity compared with monotherapy. 134 In the phase 2 CheckMate 204 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02320058), 94 patients with asymptomatic, nonirradiated MBMs received combination nivolumab (1 mg/kg) and ipilimumab (3 mg/kg) every 3 weeks for four doses, followed by nivolumab (3 mg/kg every 2 weeks). 134 The confirmed intracranial response rate was 57%, and 26% of patients achieved complete responses. 134 The median PFS was 5.3 months, and the median OS was not reached at 20 months of follow‐up. 134 Treatment‐related grade 3–4 adverse events occurred in 55% of patients, including CNS events in 7%, reinforcing the need for close monitoring. 134 In parallel, the ABC trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02374242) randomized 79 immunotherapy‐naive patients with untreated MBMs to combination nivolumab (1 mg/kg) plus ipilimumab (3 mg/kg) or nivolumab monotherapy (3 mg/kg every 2 weeks). 135 Among 35 patients in the combination arm, the intracranial response rate was 46%, compared with 20% in the monotherapy arm. 135 Notably, all patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1 and were off corticosteroids, emphasizing the importance of patient selection for combination immunotherapy. The combination of nivolumab and relatlimab (an LAG‐3–blocking antibody) demonstrated promising intracranial activity in treating MBMs, especially in patients who have progressed on prior PD‐1–directed therapy, according to a post‐hoc analysis of the RELATIVITY‐020 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT01968109). Among 27 heavily pretreated patients, the confirmed intracranial overall response rate was 22%, and the clinical benefit rate was 63%, with a median duration of intracranial response not reached. Of 14 evaluable patients who had target intracranial lesions, 6 experienced ≥30% reduction, and 63% remained free of intracranial progression for >3 years, highlighting the potential for durable intracranial responses in this refractory population. 136 These studies established dual checkpoint inhibition as the most effective systemic strategy for asymptomatic MBMs to date.

Tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte therapy

Adoptive cellular therapy using tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) has emerged as an important option for patients with metastatic melanoma who have progressed on ICIs and targeted therapies. Lifileucel (Amtagvi; IOVANCE Biotherapeutics, Inc.), an autologous TIL therapy, received accelerated US Food and Drug Administration approval in February 2024 for unresectable or metastatic melanoma previously treated with anti–PD‐1 therapy and, if BRAF V600–mutant, a BRAF inhibitor with or without an MEK inhibitor, based on phase 2 data demonstrating meaningful objective response rates (range, approximately 31%–36%) and durable responses in heavily pretreated patients. 137 , 138 , 139 This represents the first cellular therapy approval in melanoma and expands options beyond checkpoint blockade and targeted agents in the refractory setting.

Patients with active or symptomatic brain metastases were excluded from the registrational studies that led to the approval of lifileucel. 137 A small pilot trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT05640193) is currently underway to evaluate lifileucel specifically in patients with MBMs that may clarify its safety and efficacy in this population. 140 While larger confirmatory studies are awaited, TIL therapy represents a promising emerging approach for patients with ICI‐resistant and targeted therapy‐resistant melanoma, including potential applicability in those with CNS involvement.

Treatment selection considerations

In patients with BRAF V600–mutant MBMs, the choice between upfront ICI and BRAF/MEK‐targeted therapy remains a key clinical decision and is driven primarily by intracranial disease burden, symptom status, corticosteroid requirement, and urgency for response (Figure 3). Symptomatic disease requiring corticosteroids generally predicts poorer outcomes with immunotherapy and may favor initial targeted therapy in patients with BRAF‐mutant disease. 7 , 34 Asymptomatic patients with low steroid requirements derive the greatest benefit from upfront combination checkpoint blockade. BRAF status remains crucial because BRAF/MEK inhibitors offer rapid intracranial responses, which are useful in patients with mass effect, hemorrhage risk, or need for urgent disease control. 34 Our patient ultimately received both approaches, with BRAF/MEK inhibition used when rapid intracranial control was needed because of mass effect and hemorrhage. Prior treatment exposures, overall disease burden, and patient comorbidities must also inform therapeutic sequencing. Although next‐generation sequencing was not performed in this patient, genomic profiling of brain metastases is an area of active investigation. Currently, sampling brain metastases solely for genomic analysis is not recommended in clinical practice because the therapeutic benefit of targeting actionable, intracranial‐specific alterations remains uncertain. Studies across melanoma, lung, and breast cancers suggest that, whereas many established driver mutations are typically concordant between brain and extracranial sites, brain metastasis‐specific alterations occur in approximately 15%–30% of patients and may contribute to intracranial adaptation and resistance. 21 , 22 , 26 , 141 , 142 , 143 , 144 , 145 , 146 , 147 The greatest utility of molecular profiling currently lies in identifying patients for genomically matched clinical trials, particularly those exploring targets or pathways that may be uniquely relevant to brain metastases biology. 38 , 148 , 149 , 150 Therefore, genomically guided trials evaluating targeted agents may offer personalized therapeutic avenues once standard treatments are exhausted. Despite significant advances in systemic therapy, local treatment with neurosurgical resection and/or SRS remains the standard of care for patients with symptomatic, large, or rapidly progressing MBMs. 34 Integration of local modalities with systemic treatment must be tailored to disease kinetics, performance status, and treatment urgency. Early multidisciplinary evaluation is essential to optimize outcomes in this clinically heterogeneous population.

Symptomatic versus asymptomatic brain metastases

A critical and often underappreciated distinction in the management of MBMs is whether patients are symptomatic at presentation. Symptomatic brain metastases are variously defined across clinical trials, most commonly referring to patients with neurologic deficits attributable to intracranial disease and/or requiring corticosteroids for symptom control. 34 , 129 , 151 , 152 This heterogeneity in definition has posed challenges for trial design and interpretation; notably, the TRICOTEL study required protocol amendments related to symptom status and steroid use, underscoring the complexity of this population. 129 , 153

Across multiple prospective studies, symptomatic brain metastases have consistently been associated with inferior outcomes compared with asymptomatic disease. 151 , 152 In ICI trials, including CheckMate 204 (nivolumab plus ipilimumab), the ABC trial, and earlier ipilimumab monotherapy studies, intracranial response rates and survival outcomes were substantially lower in symptomatic or steroid‐requiring patients than in asymptomatic cohorts. 151 , 152 , 154 Similarly, outcomes with targeted therapy appear attenuated in symptomatic patients, despite the rapid intracranial responses often observed with BRAF/MEK inhibition. 115 , 155 , 156

These observations have important therapeutic implications. Asymptomatic patients with minimal or no steroid requirements derive the greatest benefit from upfront combination immune checkpoint blockade, which offers the potential for durable intracranial control. In contrast, symptomatic patients, particularly those with mass effect, hemorrhage, or steroid dependence, often require initial local therapy and/or BRAF/MEK inhibition to achieve rapid disease control and neurologic stabilization. In this setting, targeted therapy may serve as a bridge to subsequent immunotherapy once symptoms improve and corticosteroids can be tapered. Our patient's clinical course exemplifies this paradigm, with treatment selection driven by evolving symptom burden, steroid dependence, and urgency of intracranial disease control.

RADIATION ONCOLOGY PERSPECTIVE

Despite the advances in systemic therapies with intracranial activity, radiation remains a mainstay of treatment for patients with brain metastases. Our patient's experience described above highlights both the benefits and potential harms associated with these treatments.

Stereotactic radiosurgery

For patients with limited intracranial disease, whole‐brain radiotherapy (WBRT) has largely been supplanted by focal therapy with SRS. Initial studies of patients with less than four metastases compared the use of SRS with or without WBRT. 148 , 157 , 158 , 159 Those studies demonstrated that WBRT was not associated with improved survival and was associated with significant neurocognitive toxicity, and they established SRS alone as a standard of care for patients with limited brain metastases. More recently, emerging data from a randomized trial of patients with <20 brain metastases demonstrated that focal treatment of lesions with SRS was associated with better brain tumor‐specific quality‐of‐life measures compared with hippocampal‐avoidant WBRT. Furthermore, this approach was not associated with inferior survival, suggesting that SRS is a reasonable approach for those with who have from one to 20 brain metastases. 160 Over time, there has been greater recognition of the negative consequences of WBRT, which can result in cognitive deficits, especially in domains of verbal memory and executive function. 161 These neurocognitive deficits are associated with impaired quality of life and are a source of distress for patients and their families. Although neurocognitive‐sparing strategies (e.g., memantine, hippocampal‐avoidant WBRT) can mitigate some toxicity, WBRT is generally avoided for MBMs and is reserved only for rare patients with extensive, symptomatic intracranial burden for whom SRS is not feasible. In the modern era, WBRT provides minimal clinical benefit for MBMs because most patients who are eligible for WBRT have aggressive, diffuse intracranial disease and limited survival. Given these limitations, contemporary management strongly favors SRS whenever possible.

The main drawback of SRS is the risk of radionecrosis, which can occur any time from a few months after SRS to years later. In general, the risks of radionecrosis are related to the volume of brain being treated as well as the dose and how that dose is divided (termed fractionation). Initial studies established single‐fraction SRS doses that resulted in acceptable risks of radionecrosis based on lesion size. 162 Over the ensuing decades, stereotactic techniques were adapted to deliver focused radiation over more fractions (termed hypofractionated SRT), which can reduce the risk of radionecrosis for larger lesions of those in more eloquent areas while providing good local control . 163 In our patient, the combination of eloquent location and prior radiation therapy led to the choice of a hyperfractionated SRT regimen to balance local control and the risk of radionecrosis.

Postsurgical radiation

In our patient, his initial presentation with a single brain metastasis strongly favored surgical resection for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Randomized data indicate that the rate of local recurrence of disease in the resection cavity without postoperative radiation therapy is approximately 41%–57%. 164 , 165 For patients with limited brain metastases, the use of SRS to the resection bed was associated with better neurocognitive outcomes compared with WBRT without negatively effecting survival. 166 For our patient, surgery was followed by postoperative radiation to the cavity. Although single‐fraction SRS (typically up to 24 grays) and fractionated SRS (typically, five or less fractions up to 35 grays) are commonly used in the postoperative setting, our patient received 38 grays in 10 fractions. This more protracted fractionation scheme reflects an institutional practice intended to reduce the risk of radionecrosis, particularly for larger resection cavities. 167

Radiation toxicity and complications

Despite these considerations, our case highlights the impact of radionecrosis on the management of patients with brain metastases. As noted above, radionecrosis occurs because of injury to the underlying brain tissue and vasculature. It can occur as early as a few months after SRS but can also arise later, even more than a decade after treatment. Thus as more patients are treated with SRS and live longer because of better systemic therapies, this toxicity is taking on increased importance. Furthermore, there are data suggesting that some immunotherapies 168 and other systemic therapies, such as antibody–drug conjugates, 169 can be associated with increased rates of radionecrosis after SRS. Further confounding the picture is the difficulty in diagnosing radionecrosis based on imaging as well as the potential for a mixed picture of necrosis and recurrent tumor. In our patient, initial biopsies were consistent with necrosis but later responded to BRAF/MEK inhibition, suggesting that some of the increased enhancement and edema was related to disease progression in addition to necrosis. In these patients for whom initial SRS has been used, various salvage therapies, including surgical debulking, re‐irradiation, or LITT, are often considered. However, all of these treatments carry significant risk, and careful multidisciplinary consideration of the appropriate sequence of interventions is mandatory.

Timing of systemic therapy relative to radiation

Coordination of systemic therapy around radiation is a critical consideration in the management of MBMs. For patients receiving SRS, immune checkpoint inhibitors are commonly continued or resumed shortly after treatment because available data suggest that concurrent or closely sequenced therapy is generally well tolerated and may enhance intracranial control. 170 , 171 , 172 , 173 However, caution is warranted in patients requiring high‐dose corticosteroids or with recent hemorrhage, for whom the risk of toxicity and reduced immunotherapy efficacy must be weighed. In contrast, targeted therapies, particularly BRAF and MEK inhibitors, are often held briefly before and after SRS because of concerns regarding radiation‐associated toxicity, including radiation necrosis, hemorrhage, and dermatologic reactions. 174 , 175 , 176 Although institutional practices vary, many centers pause BRAF/MEK inhibition several days before SRS and resume treatment after acute radiation effects have subsided. For patients undergoing WBRT, systemic therapy is more commonly interrupted during treatment, given the limited evidence supporting concurrent administration and the potential for compounded toxicity.

In our patient, after a disease recurrence post‐SRS, initial treatment with systemic therapy followed by fractionated re‐irradiation, LITT, and additional systemic therapies were used to try to maintain the patient's quality of life and neurologic function for as long as possible. However, ultimately, our patient's experience demonstrates that additional therapies are needed both for the management of this disease and for the management of treatment‐related toxicities.

NURSING/ADVANCED PRACTICE PROVIDER PERSPECTIVE

Advanced practice providers (APPs) occupy a central role in the care of patients with MBMs, navigating between complex oncologic treatments and the lived realities of patients and families. This case illustrates how APPs contribute to symptom management, patient and caregiver education, care coordination, and psychosocial support across the disease trajectory.

Role of the APP in managing patients with brain metastases

APPs provide longitudinal oversight in both inpatient and outpatient settings, often serving as the consistent point of contact for patients whose care spans multiple disciplines. Routine neurologic assessments, careful monitoring for new or worsening deficits, and early triage of complications, such as headache, intracranial hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, or seizures, are essential responsibilities. By maintaining close clinical surveillance, APPs ensure that urgent needs are addressed promptly while supporting continuity across treatment transitions.

Symptom management and supportive care

MBMs carry a high symptom burden, compounded by treatment‐related complications, such as seizure activity, radiation necrosis, prolonged corticosteroid use, and immune‐related adverse events. APPs are central in monitoring steroid dependence, titrating doses to balance neurologic stability with systemic side effects, and managing endocrinopathies, such as adrenal suppression. In this case, the patient's prolonged steroid dependence required repeated dose adjustments to manage cerebral edema while also mitigating systemic complications, and seizure management became a persistent challenge after ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. Beyond pharmacology, APPs serve as the primary care coordinator between multitudes of teams, including primary oncology, physical and occupational therapy, speech and language pathology, neurocognitive assessments, social work, and palliative care.

Patient education and counseling

Given the complexity of multimodal therapy, APPs provide clear, accessible education to patients and caregivers. This includes preparing families for potential side effects and reinforcing when new or worsening symptoms warrant urgent evaluation. Repeated counseling fosters health literacy, reduces decisional conflict, and empowers patients to engage in care planning despite the uncertainty of disease progression.

Care transitions and coordination

The management of MBMs demands seamless collaboration and communication across complex, multidisciplinary teams. APPs often function as the anchor provider, synthesizing evolving treatment plans and ensuring communication and education of specific neuro‐oncology concerns across specialties. Coordinating urgent visits, facilitating smooth transitions between hospital and rehabilitation, and guiding goals‐of‐care discussions are frequent and critical tasks that help patients and families feel supported within an otherwise fragmented system.

Psychosocial and quality‐of‐life considerations

APPs are uniquely positioned to address the psychosocial toll of MBMs given their sustained relationships with patients and families. Regular screening for mood changes, caregiver strain, and existential distress allows early intervention through psycho‐oncology, social work, and supportive care. Caregiver burden and distress are important considerations in patients with brain metastases, especially considering the associated changes of brain metastases on patients functioning and mood. 177 Specifically for patients who have brain metastases, our understanding of the effect on caregivers remains limited, and the downstream impact on patient outcomes has yet to be characterized. 178 , 179 , 180 Ongoing research is needed to fully characterize the unique needs of patients with brain metastases and their caregivers to develop tailored interventions to improve their quality of life. For our patient, APPs played a large role in timing/safety around (international) travel for life events, such as weddings and seeing immediate family, that required built‐in safety plans because of neurologic function and active pulmonary embolism. Often, APPs are also the team members discussing family planning before, during, and after treatment. In this patient, repeated hospitalizations, neurologic decline, and therapy‐related complications required ongoing recalibration of goals. When the patient developed worsening aphasia and functional decline from basal ganglia progression, APPs supported the family in reframing hope toward comfort, meaningful time at home, and dignity in his final weeks. APPs play a critical role in helping patients and families hold hope while also preparing for the realities of advanced disease. Holding dual realities involves validating the patients desire for recovery and emphasizing the possibility of meaningful time and improved quality of life, even as we acknowledge that time may be limited. By framing hope as multidimensional, APPs can honor what patients value most without fostering unrealistic expectations. When goals shift toward comfort measures, APPs are often instrumental in facilitating this transition and supporting both the family and the hospice team.

Reflections and lessons learned

From the APP perspective, several lessons emerge. Vigilance in symptom recognition can meaningfully alter the clinical course. Equally important is relational continuity: knowing the patient over years and through each stage of illness builds trust during increasingly stressful moments and can help facilitate critical decision‐making. Finally, APP involvement in multidisciplinary tumor boards ensures that patient‐centered perspectives inform therapeutic decisions. APPs are often the first members of the treatment team in whom patients and their families confide when discussing changes in goals of care, treatment fatigue, or readiness for transitions such as hospice. They play a critical role in facilitating communication and ensuring that the entire care team remains aligned and informed about evolving goals of care. Even when disease‐directed options are limited, APPs remain critical in advocating for dignity, comfort, and quality of life.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the complexity of managing MBMs in the era of multimodal therapy and molecularly targeted treatment. The patient's clinical course was marked by recurrent intracranial disease, treatment‐related toxicity, immune complications, and neurologic events, including hemorrhage, seizure, and stroke, necessitating repeated multidisciplinary deliberation. Integration of neurosurgical resection, SRS, systemic therapies (including BRAF/MEK inhibitors and checkpoint blockade), and novel interventions like LITT was essential to balancing symptom control with durable disease management.

Management decisions were guided by lesion location, symptom burden, prior treatment history, and evolving radiographic findings. The case also highlights the importance of distinguishing tumor progression from radiation necrosis, an increasingly common diagnostic challenge in patients receiving immunotherapy and SRS. The patient's longitudinal care required close coordination between neuro‐oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, neurosurgery, neurology, and supportive care teams.

This case also highlights the growing importance and practical challenges of multidisciplinary care models for patients with brain metastases. Although integrated decision‐making is increasingly recognized as essential, coordinating care across neuro‐oncology, medical oncology, radiation oncology, neurosurgery, neuroradiology, and supportive services remains difficult even at academic centers and may be particularly challenging in community practice. In response, several institutions have developed dedicated multidisciplinary brain metastasis clinics or tumor boards designed to streamline complex decision‐making, improve treatment sequencing, and reduce fragmentation of care. Although such models require substantial logistical coordination, protected time, and institutional support, they may facilitate more timely and cohesive management for this challenging patient population.

As systemic options expand, the role of multidisciplinary tumor boards in tailoring individualized treatment strategies remains central. Timely access to molecular profiling, expert imaging interpretation, and the ability to leverage subspecialty expertise, ranging from specialty management of immune and targeted treatment toxicities to nuanced decisions about anticoagulation, are critical to optimizing outcomes. This case underscores the need for continued refinement of treatment algorithms, prospective clinical trials in patients with active brain metastases, and broader access to comprehensive, team‐based care.
