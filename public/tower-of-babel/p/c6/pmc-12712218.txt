Effect of lactoferrin on biofilm formation of bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus species
Authors: Kumala SERAPHINA, Rin FURUKIDO, Naoki ISOBE, Takahiro NII, Yuzo KUROKAWA, Naoki SUZUKI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The most common bovine mastitis pathogen is the Staphylococcus species, consisting of Staphylococcus aureus and non-aureus staphylococci (NAS). Lactoferrin (Lf) is an iron-binding protein with antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties. Lf has a metal-free form (apo-Lf) and a natural form (native-Lf), and their differences were reported to affect their activity against bacteria. However, its effects on bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp. remain unclear. Fifteen S. aureus and 49 NAS strains were isolated from bovine mastitis cases, and their growth and biofilm-forming abilities were observed. Bacterial growth and biofilm formation were observed by culturing each strain with/without bovine milk apo-/native-lactoferrin (200 μg/mL). Without Lf treatment, the growth and biofilm formation abilities of S. aureus were significantly higher and lower, respectively, than those of NAS. The growth of S. aureus and NAS significantly decreased during apo-Lf treatment and significantly reduced the total amount of biofilm produced by S. aureus whereas native-LF treatment did not affect the growth and biofilm formation of Staphylococcus species. These results confirmed the ability of Lf to act as an antimicrobial and antibiofilm substance against mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp., although various responses from each strain were observed. Additionally, the iron-binding state of Lf affected growth but did not affect the biofilm formation ability. Differences in the responses of Staphylococcus strains to Lf may help explain their pathogenicity, requiring further research.

INTRODUCTION

Mastitis is the most common disease caused by a broad spectrum of pathogen invasions, leading to inflammation of the mammary tissue in dairy animals [9, 12, 46], reduced milk production, and increased treatment cost [36, 46]. Staphylococcus spp. is the most prevalent mastitis-causing pathogen [5, 26]. According to the availability of coagulase enzyme—a virulence strategy to convert fibrinogen into fibrin—the Staphylococcus spp. are divided into Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci, also called non-aureus staphylococci (NAS) [6, 43].

S. aureus is often isolated from bovine clinical mastitis milk and is a contagious pathogen that is easily transmitted from one cow to another, as it is often found in the surroundings and milking equipment of the cow [45]. S. aureus is also known for its diverse virulence factors that attach, persist, and modulate the immune response in the mammary gland through Toll-like receptor 2 activation, leading to highly damaged mammary tissues and high somatic cell count in milk [5, 8, 26, 43]. NAS is highly persistent in the environment of a dairy cow (floor, bedding, etc.) [6, 33, 35, 46], provoking inflammatory responses, and increasing somatic cell count, although it is lower than S. aureus [4, 6, 14]. A total of 27.9% of NAS were isolated from the milk of bovine mastitis cases [45]. It has been reported that S. simulans and S. chromogens could colonize the mammary gland and were associated with persistent intramammary infection [14].

The ability of isolated bovine mastitis-causing bacteria to form biofilms is a mechanism for surviving stressful conditions [19, 34]. Both S. aureus and NAS were able to produce an exopolysaccharide matrix, such as slime, to shield themselves, supply nutrients, and facilitate the dissemination of antibiotic resistance gene transfer [24, 34, 41]. S. aureus strains isolated from subclinical mastitis have been reported to have various biofilm-forming abilities characteristics (strong, 16.66%; moderate, 58.33%; weak, 25%) [46] whereas they have also been reported in NAS with negative (14.9%) and various (weak, 36.4%; moderate, 25.5%; and strong, 23.1%) [44]. The exopolysaccharide layer protects the pathogen from the opsonization and phagocytosis mechanisms of the innate immune system or from treatment by obstructing antibiotic penetration, thus supporting pathogen survival and reducing mastitis treatment efficiency in cows [11, 27, 34].

Several antimicrobial drugs are used to treat mastitis in dairy cow [42]. Nonetheless, the overuse of antibiotics, especially in livestock, has been widely reported and may increase the risk of antibiotic resistance in some bacteria [24]. Endogenous antimicrobial proteins are positively charged substances that naturally interact with negatively charged bacterial surfaces, leading to the extermination of pathogens [41, 47]. Lactoferrin (Lf), a naturally secreted milk antimicrobial protein from the mammary epithelium and neutrophils, is a globular iron-binding glycoprotein that inhibits bacterial adherence, growth, and biofilm formation via iron sequestration [7, 17, 21, 37]. Based on its tertiary structure, Lf exists in metal-free (apo-Lf), natural (native-Lf), and iron-saturated forms (holo-Lf), which affect its configuration and interactions with bacteria and their biofilms [3, 21,22,23]. Despite the important role of Lf in the bovine innate immune system, its activity against S. aureus and NAS remains unclear. Hence, this study aimed to compare and investigate each mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp. growth and biofilm-forming ability in response to bovine milk apo- and native-Lf treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial strains

A total of 64 strains of bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp., consisting of 15 strains of S. aureus and 49 strains of NAS, were obtained from a previous study [39]. This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines for animal experiments issued by Hiroshima University and was approved by the Animal Research Committee of Hiroshima University (E19-3).

Lactoferrin preparation

Bovine milk Lf (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) was used as a native-Lf, whereas Apo-Lf was prepared using methods previously reported [22, 23]. Briefly, 100 mg/mL native-Lf was dialyzed against 0.1 M citric acid monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) (pH 4, 24 hr, 4°C). It was dialyzed again against distilled water (24 hr, 4°C) to remove unbound metals and then solidified using a centrifugal concentrator (CC-105, TOMY SEIKO, Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Bacterial growth quantification

Bacterial growth was measured using previously reported methods [15, 27, 38]. Briefly, each strain was spread on tryptic soy agar (Eiken Chemical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and incubated at 37°C overnight, which was then adjusted to 0.5 McFarland Standard (bioMérieux SA, Métropole de Lyon, France) in Mueller Hinton Broth (Becton Dickinson and Co., Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) (approximately 108 cfu/mL). Subsequently, 200 μL of bacteria (107 cfu/mL), with or without 200 μg/mL of apo-/native-Lf treatment, were incubated in a 96-well flatbottom plate (Sumitomo Bakelite, Tokyo, Japan) (24 hr, at 37°C, static conditions), triplicated, then optical density (OD) of 600 nm (OD600) were observed using an absorptiometer (Multiskan SkyHigh Absorption Microplate Reader, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Biofilm quantification

Crystal violet assays were conducted to quantify mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp. biofilm formation using a previously reported method [15, 28, 46]. After measuring the OD600, the planktonic cells and culture medium were removed and washed with phosphate-buffered saline. Dried adherent biofilms were fixed with methanol for 15 min at room temperature (RT), then removed, dried, and stained with 0.1% Gentian Violet (w/v) (Katayama Chemical Industries Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) (30 min, RT). After washing, the stained biofilm was resolubilized with 150 μL of acetic acid (33% vol/vol) with continuous shaking (5 min at 700 rpm, RT) using an orbital microplate shaker (IKA-Werke GmbH & Co., KG., Staufen, Germany) and OD was measured using an absorptiometer at 570 nm (OD570). Reproducibility was confirmed through triplication for each strain. Reproducibility was confirmed through triplication, independently three times, on each strain. The biofilm-forming ability (biofilm index) was measured as the ratio of the total amount of biofilm produced normalized to bacterial growth (OD570/OD600) [20]. The biofilm-producing ability of the strain was determined using the cutoff optical density (ODco) [30, 31] which was calculated as three standard deviations above the mean of the blank results of OD570. Each strain was then classified as a non- (OD570 <ODco), weak (ODco <OD570 <2ODco), moderate (2ODco <OD570 <4ODco), or strong biofilm producer (4ODco <OD570).

Broth microdilution method of Lf

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of Lf was determined using a broth microdilution method [2, 13, 16]. Briefly, each strain was suspended in 25% Mueller-Hinton Broth (Becton Dickinson and Co.) adjusted to 0.5 McFarland Standard (approximately 108 cfu/mL). After dilution, each strain as 104 cfu/mL final concentration was incubated with/without various levels of native- or apo-Lf (31.25 to 4,000 μg/mL as final concentration) inside the round-bottom microplate (Watson Co., Ltd., Kobe, Japan), overnight at 37°C. The endpoint was identified as the lowest Lf treatment concentration, with no visible growth observed using a magnified mirror. The time-fold of visible growth was calculated as the ratio between the apo-Lf and native-Lf MIC results.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using generalized linear mixed model analysis, and Tukey’s range test was performed using JMP Pro 17 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Each OD was set as a dependent variable, and species or treatment effects (exposure to Lf or not) were set as fixed effects, and each strain was included as a random effect. Descriptive analysis was used to interpret broth microdilutions of Lf.

DISCUSSION

Staphylococcus species are the major pathogens responsible for causing mastitis in bovines and triggering their immune response, resulting in a high somatic cell count in milk [4, 8]. The biofilm-forming ability of S. aureus and NAS were reported to be important to support their survivability [10, 27, 34, 46]. Under the same in-vitro circumstances, our results suggest that S. aureus has a robust growth ability compared to NAS (Fig. 1), even after native (Fig. 4) and apo-Lf (Fig. 5) treatment. Most of our isolated Staphylococcus spp. strains were classified as non- and weak biofilm producers, and NAS strains with a strong and moderate biofilm-producing ability were found. Among the major Staphylococcal species isolated in this study, the most notable growth and biofilm formation trends were observed in S. aureus and S. simulans. Our findings suggested that S. aureus focuses more on growth, whereas the NAS, particularly S. simulans, relies on biofilm formation.

Approximately 200 μg/mL Lf has been reported in healthy dairy cow milk [40]. Thus, 200 μg/mL native-Lf and apo-Lf were used to observe the growth and biofilm production alteration of S. aureus and NAS in this study. The results showed that Lf treatment, especially apo-Lf, was effective against isolated bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp. observed on their growth and biofilm reduction (Fig. 3), particularly on S. aureus, S. epidermidis and S. simulans (Fig. 6). The difference in native- and apo-Lf antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity might be due to its iron saturation level; thus, native-Lf was unable to chelate as much iron as apo-Lf to inhibit Staphylococcus spp. growth and biofilm formation through an iron-dependent mechanism [21].

The isolated Staphylococcus spp. also showed various growth and biofilm production trends to Lf treatment. S. haemolyticus was unaffected by Lf treatment, whereas S. simulans increased its growth while lowering its biofilm production in the presence of Lf. Thus, bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus spp., especially NAS, showed variable growth and biofilm production in response to Lf treatments.

To confirm the antibacterial activity of Lf, the broth microdilution method was used. S. aureus was able to survive higher Lf native- and apo-Lf concentrations than NAS (Fig. 7A and 7B). The milk Lf concentration in dairy cows with clinical and subclinical mastitis was reported between 318 to 2,270 μg/mL and 199 to 1,229 μg/mL, respectively [18]. Thus, it was suspected that the amount of Lf naturally present in milk can be used to control the mastitis cases. Apo-Lf might exert stronger antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus spp. than native-Lf, however, a resistancy in apo-Lf treatment was also observed (Fig. 7C), especially in S. aureus, which might be due to their survivability in the iron-restricted environment. A previous study has reported that under extreme iron deprivation, bacteria may start to build iron chelation systems to provide iron for cellular metabolism [29]. S. aureus and S. epidermidis have been reported to possess an iron uptake mechanism induced in a low iron content environment, resulting in growth restoration [1, 32], thus further study is needed.

Despite being classified as non-biofilm producers, S. aureus strains were still able to grow at higher concentrations of both native and apo-Lf than NAS, mostly categorized as weak biofilm producers, which may explain its pathogenicity. In contrast, the MIC of the isolated strain classified as a strong biofilm producer (S. saprophyticus) was far lower than S. aureus. A previous study reported that 98% of S. aureus mastitis-causing isolates possessed biofilm-producing genes (icaA and icaD) despite some being classified as weak or non-producing biofilm [25]. Therefore, it is suspected that the amount of produced biofilm is not a key factor in bovine mastitis-causing S. aureus survivability. Moreover, the role of biofilms in bovine mastitis-causing NAS pathogenicity is still not fully understood; hence, further studies are required. In conclusion, bovine milk Lf is a potential antibacterial and antibiofilm agent against most bovine mastitis-causing Staphylococcus species.
