Validating claims-based algorithms for a systemic lupus erythematosus diagnosis in Medicare data for informed use of the Lupus Index: a tool for geospatial research
Authors: Candace Feldman, Jeffrey R Curtis, Jinoos Yazdany, Peter Izmirly, Jim C Oates
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to validate claims-based algorithms for identifying SLE and lupus nephritis (LN) in Medicare data, enhancing the use of the Lupus Index for geospatial research on SLE prevalence and outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated the performance of rule-based algorithms using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes to identify SLE and LN in a well-defined prospective longitudinal cohort of patients with and without SLE from a South Carolina registry and rheumatology outpatient clinics. The analysis included comparison of algorithms based on Medicare fee-for-service claims data to these rigorously phenotyped populations. The primary classification for SLE cases was based on the American College of Rheumatology and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus International Collaborating Clinics criteria for SLE and LN. Algorithms were based on the number of ICD-10 codes with and without a 30-day separation in the observation period, including all of 2016–2018.ResultsThe algorithm using two ICD-10 codes for SLE, with or without a 30-day separation, showed the best overall performance. For LN, specific ICD-10 codes outperformed combinations of SLE and renal/proteinuria codes that were found in ICD-9.ConclusionsThe findings of this study highlight the performance of specific ICD-10 code algorithms in identifying SLE and LN cases within Medicare data, providing a valuable tool for informing use of the Lupus Index. This index allows for improved geographical targeting of clinical resources, health disparity studies and clinical trial site selection. The study underscores the importance of algorithm selection based on research objectives, recommending more specific algorithms for precise tasks like clinical trial site identification and less specific ones for broader applications such as health disparities research.

Introduction

Models to predict outcomes of SLE treatments using biological variables alone are imperfect, suggesting that social and/or environmental factors are also important in driving outcomes.1 Therefore, to reduce health inequities and target resources to populations at elevated risk for adverse outcomes, geographical variables must be considered in predicting outcomes. To develop such models, large data sets containing geographical information from individuals with SLE need to be accessible to stakeholders with validated algorithms to define SLE.

The Lupus Index is a geographical information system-based data set that was developed by the Lupus Research Alliance in collaboration with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and the National Minority Quality Forum. This resource includes diagnosis codes, as well as geographical, demographic and provider information. Reporting variables include prevalence, mortality and hospital admission rates and emergency room admission rates from fee-for-service Medicare patients in the years 2014–2016. Data are presented more granularly in a tabular form and visually in colour-coded maps (info@lupusresearch.org). This resource was designed to study health disparities, target clinical resources and inform advocacy efforts, and determine the ideal locations for clinical trial sites. The performance of billing claim diagnosis code-based algorithms using CMS data must be evaluated to find which algorithms, based on their sensitivity and specificity, are more suited for specific use cases. For instance, one might use more specific algorithms for study site selection and research and less specific algorithms for advocacy. This study was designed to evaluate the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code-based algorithm performance within CMS claims data in a well clinically phenotyped population of individuals with and without SLE in a both a longitudinal registry and the rheumatology clinics at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC), described further in online supplemental table 1.

Methods

General study protocol overview

Algorithms based on Medicare fee-for-service claims data from both dual eligible patients <65 and those ≥65 years of age were linked to SLE classification data from two separate cohorts of patients seen in the rheumatology clinics at the MUSC. The first cohort consisted of research participants with and without SLE who were prospectively enrolled from 2003 to 2022 and classified by the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria for SLE as part of a National Institutes of Health (NIH)-funded registry.2 3 The second cohort, selected for enrichment of patients without SLE, was derived from a random sample of non-overlapping patients seen in the rheumatology clinics at the MUSC independent of billing diagnosis. The labelling of SLE classification and the number of ACR criteria for each patient in this random sample were determined by manual chart review (JCO). Only those with four or more ACR criteria or two criteria, including a renal biopsy consistent with SLE, were classified as SLE. From these two cohorts, those who had any Medicare claims during the study period were selected. For each patient, the number of claims with the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code M32.* and the time interval between these codes and all encounter codes were determined. Algorithms using different numbers of SLE diagnosis codes and those using different numbers of diagnosis codes separated by 30-day intervals4 were evaluated for their performance in predicting SLE in these well-defined cohorts, as described in the following section.

Data sources

CMS data linkage to MUSC clinical registry

Extracting identified data from the Lupus Index for validation is not possible due to the agreement with the CMS to only release aggregate data on populations of 10 or more in a geographical region; we therefore created a synthetic CMS population. All claims for all patients in the linkage CMS data from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2018 who had one or more claims for any type of claim submitted by a limited set of National Provider Identifiers (NPIs) were extracted. The NPIs used were those of physician or advanced practitioner providers at the MUSC who practised rheumatology during this period. The search in the CMS data included both outpatient and inpatient encounter diagnosis codes. Data were limited to patients between 18 and 95 years of age. An institutionally approved honest broker with access to all MUSC clinical warehouse data limited this data set to participants in the MUSC rheumatology longitudinal registry using previously published methods of linkage.5

MUSC longitudinal registry

The NIH-funded P30 Core Center for Clinical Research in the Division of Rheumatology and Immunology at the MUSC supports a longitudinal registry of SLE and healthy control volunteers that spans the years 2003 to present. Patients with suspected SLE were recruited in standard of care visits and characterised during subsequent standard of care visits with laboratory and disease activity and damage measures. Rheumatology attending providers classified patients according to the ACR 1997 classification criteria.6 Those who met at least four ACR criteria or by a modification of the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus International Collaborating Clinics criteria to require two ACR criteria with a diagnosis of lupus nephritis (LN) by histopathology were classified as having SLE. Classification of a patient as having LN required a renal histopathological diagnosis of LN by the International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society classification, or a combination of four ACR SLE criteria plus Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) attribution of proteinuria (new ≥0.5 g/g protein/creatinine attributed to SLE or rise in protein/creatinine by ≥0.5 g/g since the last measure also attributed to SLE).7 All SLE, undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD; at least one immunological and one clinical criterion) and healthy control individuals (screened with and having no positive responses on the Connective Tissue Disease Screening Questionnaire8) in this database with Medicare-billed visits between 2016 and 2018 were included in the analysis. LN was diagnosed either by biopsy9 or the ACR criterion for renal disorder.10

CMS data linkage to the MUSC Research Data Warehouse

A separate cohort was generated from a search of a random 5% sample for all CMS patients in the MUSC Research Data Warehouse (RDW) who were seen by a rheumatology billing provider. This percentage was chosen to generate a limited data set of adequate size for a second random selection for manual chart review (detailed in the following section).

The RDW is maintained by the Biomedical Informatics Center at the MUSC and contains Epic electronic health record data from 17 May 2012 to present. This population was narrowed to those with one or more Medicare parts A, B and/or D claims of any type submitted by any of the above NPIs from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2018.

Analysis cohort creation

Cohorts from the MUSC were initially selected as being either enriched for high likelihood of SLE or LN (inclusion in the lupus registry) or lower likelihood of having SLE or LN (general rheumatology population from a limited data set in the RDW). The RDW and registry cohorts were combined into a single data set for analysis, and duplicate entries for four patients who were represented in both cohorts were removed.

Manual chart review to confirm cases and controls

Because very few controls in the longitudinal registry were Medicare patients, a data extraction of all Medicare patients seen in the MUSC rheumatology clinics (using the same providers by NPI as the referring providers for the longitudinal cohort) was performed. A randomly selected set of 2081 Medicare patients with visits by MUSC providers were available for chart review. A random number sequence was created in Microsoft Excel, and manual chart review was performed on a random subset of 94 patients (2%) from the entire data extract population. The charts from these patients were reviewed to confirm SLE classification at any time (as the longitudinal cohort classification was also not confined to the study interval). The chart review process was accelerated by text search of notes, labs and procedures for evidence of criteria for lupus or rheumatology provider diagnosis of SLE. The search terms selected from the ACR criteria and from a previously published set of unique identifiers used for classification are found in online supplemental figure 1.11 Notes from rheumatology providers were also reviewed for a diagnosis of SLE and the number of ACR criteria present. The gold standard definitions of SLE and LN classification from the longitudinal cohort were also employed for this population (review by JCO).

Data organisation and covariable inclusion

For each cohort, claims-level data were sorted in date order to include the following data elements: date of birth, date of service, sex, diagnosis/diagnoses on that claim, procedures (if done), NPI, file type (carrier line, inpatient, outpatient), tax ID of the billing entity (eg, MUSC), cohort identifier variable (longitudinal cohort vs 5% random sample) and a derived variable for the number of months in the target 3-year window that the patient had part A+B coverage, for a total of 36 months. Electronic Health Record (EHR) data were provided in the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) common data model format.

Rationale for combined data sources

To include both highly probable and highly negative individuals as well as a prevalence population, we combined three groups of patients into a single data set. This was performed as a modification of the PheValuator method as described12 13 to increase the generalisability of the findings by mimicking the process one might use case validation in the absence of a manual chart review. While designed to validate algorithms when manual chart review is not available, PheValuator was chosen as a validated rationale for pooling smaller data sets of different populations to test algorithm performance. This allowed us to limit to a manageable number the charts required for manual review when the population of interest is a small fraction of the total population. In brief, this method is designed to evaluate phenotype algorithms using only administrative data and with no chart review. Three populations are recommended. The first is a highly specific population with five or more ICD codes for a disease. The second is a sensitive population of all individuals in a setting (in this case, the rheumatology clinic) that may have a substantial portion of patients of the phenotype of interest. This set is subdivided to create a ‘noisy negative’ labelled population of individuals with no billing for the ICD codes of interest. The substitute for the highly specific population in this study was a well-phenotyped registry population. The substitute for the ‘noisy negative’ population was the registry individuals without connective tissue disease who screened negative with the Connective Tissue Disease Screening Questionnaire in place of individuals with no ICD codes for SLE. The equivalent of the sensitive population was the random selection of CMS patients in the clinic population that contained non-SLE, SLE and other autoimmune populations. We improved the validity of the algorithm performance testing by performing manual chart reviews on all patients used in the study.

Classification algorithm performance

Using the combined longitudinal cohort and random clinic population set data, we evaluated the performance of different ICD-10 code algorithms in identifying SLE as classified as above. The algorithm scheme was based on earlier algorithms based on ICD-9 codes.14 The first set of algorithms assessed had different numbers of SLE ICD-10 encounter diagnosis codes with no requirement for a minimum interval between them. The second set of algorithms evaluated one to four SLE diagnosis codes (M32.* excluding M32.0) separated by 0, 30, 60 and 90 days, respectively.

Similarly, algorithms were evaluated for their performance in the classification of LN. Previous classification of LN was based on lupus and renal ICD-9 codes,15 and this study was designed to evaluate the performance of ICD-10 codes. First, LN-specific codes (M32.14 and M32.15) were evaluated for performance by the number of codes (1–4) and the number of codes versus separated by 0, 30, 60 and 90 days. Similar to previous publications, we assessed the combination of two SLE diagnosis codes and either proteinuria or nephritis codes (R80.*, N04.*, N05.*, N06.* or N12.*).15

Statistical analysis

Model performance was described by area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (ROC AUC) with the asymptotic significance (null hypothesis being a true area=0.5) and 95% CIs reported. The sensitivity and specificity at each coordinate of the ROC were reported, as well as the positive predictive value (PPV) and F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall).

Results

Populations

The MUSC longitudinal cohort enrolled 1474 individuals with and without SLE from 2003 to 2022. Of 857 patients with suspected lupus (32 of whom had overlap syndromes with scleroderma, myositis or Sjögren syndrome, and 4 who were enrolled as controls but later were diagnosed with SLE), 701 were confirmed by classification criteria. 596 out of 617 suspected controls were confirmed as having no features of connective tissue disease. Of these populations, 185 confirmed patients with SLE and 5 confirmed patients without SLE linked to the CMS data set. From the 13 008 patients with outpatient visits to the MUSC rheumatology clinics between 2016 and 2018, 4749 had billing to Medicare. Of these, 94 were randomly selected for manual chart review to approximate equal numbers of patients with and without SLE in the analysis set. Of this random selection, duplicates were removed, and 8 patients were confirmed as having SLE, while 86 were confirmed as not having SLE. After combining the cohorts, 193 were classified as SLE by either criterion in the longitudinal cohort (185) or by manual chart review criteria count in the randomly selected cohort (8). 155 were confirmed as patients without SLE (73 with no ACR criteria and multiple with less than four ACR criteria and/or with alternative diagnoses: 27 UCTD, 5 cutaneous lupus, 4 inflammatory arthritis, 4 mixed connective tissue disease, 4 Sjögren syndrome, 3 systemic sclerosis, 2 drug-induced lupus, 2 sarcoidosis, 1 multiple sclerosis, 1 IgA nephropathy and 1 juvenile idiopathic arthritis) (figure 1).

The SLE cases within this Medicare fee-for-service population tended to be younger and more often female compared with the controls. Black patients made up the majority of patients with SLE, while white patients were more represented in the controls (table 1). SLE algorithm performance

The performance of algorithms was evaluated in the combined registry and chart review groups. The overall receiving operator characteristics curve was good for the algorithms that did and did not require a 30-day separation between codes (0.877 (0.838–0.917, p<0.001) vs 0.881 (0.842–0.921, p<0.001), respectively). The curves were matching except at the two-diagnosis code cut point for both algorithms. The specificity was numerically but not significantly greater at two diagnosis codes if the codes were separated by 30 days or more (0.768 vs 0.761). As expected, the sensitivity and specificity of the algorithms varied by the number of codes used as a threshold (figure 2). Sensitivity and specificity in both algorithms were greater than 0.8 with three diagnosis codes (table 2). Of note, algorithm performance differed by race. Specificity was lower in black patients when lower numbers of ICD-10 codes were used. Conversely, specificity was lower in white patients when higher numbers of codes were used (online supplemental table 2).

SLE algorithm characteristics in a national Medicare population

The algorithm performance is expected to be different in a well-validated local population compared with a less well-validated national population. Therefore, we compared the percentage of patients classified as having lupus in each algorithm within the national and local populations. The reduction in percentage of patients classified as SLE from one to two ICD-10 codes was greater in the national than the well-validated population, as expected (35% vs 15%, respectively). However, the reduction in the percentage of patients classified as SLE when increasing the number of codes above two reduced similarly between the two groups (online supplemental table 3). Sensitivity and specificity could be determined in the national cohort without validation.

LN algorithm performance

The performance of LN-specific and combined SLE and proteinuria/nephritis ICD-10 codes was assessed for the combined groups. Even single LN-specific codes were highly specific for LN classification in this population (ROC AUC 0.891 (0.837–0.944), p<0.001). Requiring a 30-day interval between codes reduced the sensitivity for classification precipitously without significantly affecting specificity (ROC AUC 0.787 (0.716–0.857), p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 2). The combination of SLE codes and either nephritis or proteinuria codes with or without 30-day separations had a similar specificity but a much lower sensitivity, and the ROC AUCs for two codes were not significantly better than chance (0.573 (0.486–0.659, p=0.1) and 0.567 (0.489–0.654, p=0.13), respectively) (table 3). Combining LN-specific and SLE+renal codes did not improve the model (ROC AUC 0.881 (0.826–0.936), p<0.001).

To explore potential reasons for the lower sensitivity of LN codes compared with SLE codes, data were reviewed to determine the LN disease activity among those with missing codes. Of 83 patients with a known history of LN in the longitudinal cohort, 18 had no diagnosis codes of LN during the data extract interval. Of those with missing diagnosis codes who had biopsies performed (eight patients), all biopsies were performed before 2016. Eleven of the LN patients without LN diagnosis codes had no proteinuria captured on SLEDAI while enrolled in the cohort. Four had completely inactive disease by SLEDAI during the entire enrolment period, suggesting that we may have captured actively treated rather than remotely diagnosed and inactive LN with this algorithm. In other words, providers seemed to have defaulted to a general SLE code rather than the specific LN code when the LN diagnosis is more remote or not active. To support this notion, of those with kidney biopsies, the average interval between the biopsy and the study start date was 10 years (median 5).

Discussion

In this study, we describe the performance of different ICD-10-based algorithms for classifying SLE and LN in a well-described South Carolina Medicare cohort to mimic the performance that might be expected in the national Lupus Index Medicare population. As expected, we report that more ICD codes increased specificity while reducing the sensitivity for classification of SLE. We also demonstrate that a single LN code does not maximise sensitivity but provides robust specificity.

Other studies have described the performance of ICD-9 and ICD-10 algorithms for SLE and LN classification.14 15 To our knowledge, this is the first study to use ICD-10 code algorithms alone to classify Medicare patients with SLE and LN in the rheumatology office setting. Barnado et al14 evaluated algorithms using both ICD-9 codes and either laboratory testing (positive ANA) or medication (disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) ever use) variables. In this study, a similar algorithm of two ICD-9 codes resulted in a validated PPV of 65% with a sensitivity of 86%. Addition of positive ANA testing and DMARD ever use and exclusion of diagnosis codes for systemic sclerosis or dermatomyositis improved the PPV to 91% in a validation cohort. This type of algorithm provides superior PPV at the expense of sensitivity. It also requires laboratory and medication data that are not available in the Lupus Index. In addition, the use of medications or lab tests in the algorithm may result in a cohort that excludes patients with disease who are receiving either lower quality care or have less access to subspecialty care, limiting the ability to conduct health disparities research.

There are several aspects of the study design that may influence algorithm performance relative to non-Medicare data outside of academic institutions. Assessment of the portability of the ICD code-based algorithm published by Barnado et al14 produced lower performance characteristics in an external academic hospital-based cohort.16 This may have occurred because the original study used coding by rheumatology providers, who may have more frequent and SLE-focused encounters, whereas chart documentation of non-rheumatology encounters may have included less detailed information on SLE criteria and therefore more challenging to be considered an SLE case. Differences in billing and coding practices across institutions may also influence the portability of rule-based algorithms. In addition, the current study contains only Medicare patients. Medicare patients with SLE under the age of 65 are often prescreened for SLE by objective criteria and consultant evaluation as an entry criterion for disability.17 Model performance may differ in an inception cohort versus this prevalent cohort. Specifically, our analysis would suggest that the reduced sensitivity of the LN codes resulted from lack of LN-specific coding in individuals with SLE who have long-standing, prevalent and likely inactive LN. We found a comparative loss of specificity when classifying black patients and sensitivity when classifying white patients when higher numbers of ICD-10 codes were used. These reflect the findings of Barnado et al18 in which patients from three different academic centres from which different percentages of black patients were included. This study included both ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, however. Variations in access to care as well as possible provider implicit bias in diagnosis and coding may have contributed to these observed differences.

Swerdel et al13 evaluated the performance characteristics of administrative data from the IBM MarketScan Medicare database to define predicted SLE. This study used the PheValuator method of predicting disease as a ‘probabilistic gold standard’ rather than chart review or criteria-driven cohort-defined disease.12 That study used a correction for the incidence data based on diagnosis codes for early symptoms of SLE. The PPV for SLE-predicted diagnosis using a two-code algorithm in the Swerdel et al13 study was higher (95%) yet the sensitivity was lower (47%) than the current study. Direct comparisons with the current study cannot be made due to differences in algorithms (correction for index date) and case definitions (criteria-defined vs predicted).

Chibnik et al15 have determined the performance of ICD-9-based algorithms to define LN in a Medicaid population using the same case definition of LN. Recall that ICD-9 lacked a specific code for LN. The highest performing algorithm returned an 88% PPV, like that of the combined SLE and nephritis/proteinuria codes (86%) in this study but lower than the LN-specific ICD-10 code-based (M32.14 or M32.15) algorithm (97%). This comparison highlights the potential effect of the more specific ICD-10 coding that may be used more frequently by specialists. We hypothesise that the sensitivity for LN may be higher in an LN inception cohort rather than a prevalence cohort, as the LN-specific codes appear to be missed in individuals with a more remote LN diagnosis and likely inactive disease. This is supported by the lack of active proteinuria by SLEDAI or renal biopsy during the data extract interval in patients with LN but lacking an LN-specific code. In addition, 26% of those with LN but lacking LN codes were in end-stage kidney disease. A more recent study using two or more ICD-10 codes for LN in two well-defined SLE population found similarly low sensitivities (43%–55%) and good specificities (95%–96%).19

This study did not require continuous enrolment in Medicare throughout the years studied and could bias the algorithm performance based on comorbidity and healthcare utilisation. However, the method of inclusion used in this study does reflect expected algorithm performance in the context of the Lupus Index. Prospective, longitudinal cohorts such as ours have smaller numbers of cases and controls for algorithm validation. Algorithm performance in this rheumatology-based cohort may not reflect performance in the general population with non-specialist coding.

A strength of this study was its use of well-characterised SLE, LN and control populations. The similar sensitivities noted in this and the Barnado et al study14 and the similar PPV to the Chibnik et al study15 suggest that some generalisation between academic centres is possible. However, this approach could falsely alter the algorithm performance when compared with a population of patients not evaluated by rheumatologists. However, confirmation of the SLE classification in these patients is likely less precise because non-rheumatologists may not purposefully document elements of classification criteria in progress notes. Another strength of this study is the comparison of ICD-9-like (general SLE and renal disease codes) and ICD-10 LN-specific codes for LN. This comparison demonstrated superiority of the LN-specific ICD-10 codes, yet the ability to detect a more remote history of LN rather than actively managed LN was still limited.

The Lupus Index provides geospatial data for multiple potential uses, including but not limited to advocacy, resource allocation, clinical trial site selection, social determinants of health and studies of environmental risk factors for SLE and SLE-associated hospital events and mortality among Medicare patients. However, algorithm choice depends on the research purpose. For instance, higher sensitivity, lower specificity algorithms may be used for spatial studies of healthcare inequities or advocacy, while more specific algorithms may be used for clinical trial site selection. This study provides crucial performance data for code-based algorithms in the Lupus Index, guiding their use in future analyses in the Lupus Index and other Medicare SLE and LN data sources.
