Structural determination of small proteins by cryo-EM using a coiled coil module strategy
Authors: Samson Camille, Dossou Irène, Steinmetz Anke, Kumar Anand, Mathieu Magali, Rak Alexey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electron cryo-microscopy (Cryo-EM) has traditionally been used for structural determination of proteins larger than 50 kDa. Recently, various approaches, such as fusion to a scaffold or the use of DARPins-cages, have been developed to extend its application to smaller proteins. In this study, we determined the structure of the small protein target kRasG12C by fusing it to the coiled-coil motif APH2, which is targeted by several nanobodies. This method enabled us to achieve a structure with atomic details at a resolution of 3.7 Å. The kRasG12C structure was bound to the inhibitor drug MRTX849 and GDP, both clearly visible in the density map. This method is advantageous due to its ease of setup and applicability to other targets. Additionally, we investigated several other techniques that can be applied to small proteins, regardless of the presence of a terminal helix. These advancements demonstrate the potential of cryo-EM for detailed structural analysis of a wide range of protein targets, extending cryo-EM application for drug discovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20608-3.

Introduction

Single-particle electron cryo-microscopy (cryo-EM) has emerged as a competitive method to x-ray crystallography for determining protein structures at atomic resolution enabling structure based rational drug discovery approaches. However, resolving small protein targets remains challenging. Large molecules are more easily found in images with low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), providing sufficient contrast and features to determine their orientation and position for alignment. In contrast, small particles are hard to detect in low-SNR images and lack the signal strength required for accurate alignment. Theoretical limits suggest that proteins around 38 kDa are the smallest suitable targets for high-resolution cryo-EM reconstructions1. Nevertheless, many targets of suboptimal size have been characterized by cryo-EM2. Notably, the 3.7 Å structure of the 40 kDa S-adenosylmethionine-IV riboswitch RNA is the smallest target resolved without a molecular scaffold3. However, these small protein structures currently represent less than 1% of all cryo-EM reconstructions deposited in the Electron Microscopy Data Bank (EMDB). Despite these challenges, many small proteins remain of significant interest, as half of all known proteins are under 50 kDa, and the majority are below 100 kDa, including many potent drug targets.

One approach for studying small protein structures with cryo-EM involves phase contrast techniques, such as using Volta phase plates (VPPs)4,5. While VPPs can enhance image contrast, they come with technical challenges like precise focusing requirements, which complicate and slow down data acquisition4. Consequently, the most common strategy is to increase the effective size of the protein either by fusing it with another protein or incorporating a binding partner. This approach presents two main challenges: first, achieving a rigid attachment of proteins to scaffolds, and second, developing a modular system that avoids the need for extensive molecular engineering, optimization, and time-consuming cryo-EM testing for each new target molecule, meeting aggressive timelines in drug discovery.

Various scaffolds have been explored for the fusion approach. For example, proteins can be fused to an oligomeric scaffold like glutamine synthetase, a dodecamer with six-fold dihedral (D6) symmetry6, or to a protein that is targeted by a known Fab, such as BRIL7. Another promising approach involves using binding partners such as nanobodies. Nanobodies offer several advantages for cryo-EM applications: they are easily produced in microorganisms, highly stable, and able to access cavities or clefts on protein surfaces. Additionally, nanobodies bind conformational epitopes on folded proteins with high affinity. However, their small size (12–15 kDa) limits their effectiveness in cryo-EM8. This challenge can potentially be addressed by increasing their size through the rigid attachment of a large scaffold, either by inserting a scaffold into a nanobody loop to create a “megabody”9 or by using the “Legobody” construct10.

Recently, an innovative strategy was developed to resolve the structure of oncogenic kRas at 3 Å resolution using cryo-EM, employing designed ankyrin repeat proteins (DARPins) as stabilizing scaffolds11. DARPins are engineered helical proteins recognized for their versatility as binding platforms, capable of tightly interacting with diverse target molecules through customized amino acid sequences in their loop regions12–14. Their predominantly alpha-helical structure allows for seamless fusion with other proteins via continuous helical linkages, making them ideal for structural biology applications. In this approach, DARPins were organized into a symmetric protein cage, surrounding and stabilizing kRas. Such DARPin-based cages have proven effective in enhancing the stability of small or flexible proteins in cryo-EM, creating a rigid, symmetric environment conducive to high-resolution imaging15–17. Encapsulating kRas within this DARPin-cage structure enabled high-resolution cryo-EM imaging, effectively addressing the challenges posed by the low mass and flexibility of small proteins.

While DARPin cages have effectively stabilized kRas in cryo-EM, their engineered complex requires precise optimization of each repeat unit for solubility, stability, and binding specificity, which can be time-consuming and technically challenging18. Additionally, in this approach, kRas is bound to DARPin, which inhibits interactions with some partners, such as c-Raf. This complex formation could also hinder the study of kRas bound to small molecules, as the DARPin might prevent certain interactions. Therefore, it would be beneficial to have a more accessible, both time- and cost-effective, approach that does not rely on a binding partner. In this paper, we present four different strategies we explored to elucidate the structures of small proteins using user-friendly methods that require minimal molecular engineering for each target. Initially, we focused on three distinct methods applicable to proteins with terminal helices, specifically targeting kRasG12C. Concurrently, we aimed to develop one technique for proteins without terminal helices using TEAD2 human therapeutic target protein as a model. However, the significant flexibility introduced by our method prevented us from achieving a high-resolution structure.

kRas and TEAD2 are medically significant proteins, central to cancer and developmental biology research. kRas, a 19 kDa GTPase, contains flexible loops such as the Switch I (residues 30–40) and Switch II (residues 60–76) regions19, and a C-terminal helix suitable for stable fusion to a scaffold after deletion of hyper variable C-Terminal region including prenylation site. It functions as a molecular Switch, cycling between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states. The G12C mutation in kRas leads to permanent activation, causing uncontrolled cell growth20. This mutation has enabled the development of inhibitors like MRTX849, which specifically blocks downstream signaling pathways21,22. In its active state, kRas interacts with effector proteins such as NF123 and c-Raf24. Structural analysis of the kRas(GMppNp)-NF1(GRD)-SPRED1 complex (PDB 6V65) shows interactions between the GAP-related domain and Switch II region25, while c-Raf interacts with both Switch I and II regions26. TEAD2, a key regulator in the Hippo signaling pathway, controls organ size and inhibits tumor growth by modulating cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis27. It interacts with YAP1 and WWTR1/TAZ, influencing cell migration and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)28.

The C-terminal helix of kRasG12C was fused to three different scaffolds: an artificial trimeric protein scaffold (HR00C3_2)29 that can bind three kRas molecules, a helical peptide (the huntingtin peptide) recognized by a known human variable light chain domain30, or a coiled-coil motif that forms a kRas dimer recognized by nanobodies31. This last approach enabled us to achieve a well-resolved structure of kRasG12C, with the MRTX849 compound and GDP clearly visible in the final cryo-EM map. The principle of our technique involved fusing kRasG12C to the coiled-coil (CC) motif APH2, which is known from protein origami applications. APH2 is one of twelve CC dimer-forming modules that self-assemble into a tetrahedral polypeptide chain cage, referred to as TET12SN32. These modules were also utilized to generate a nanobody library through phage display31, identifying four high-affinity nanobodies (Nb26, Nb28, Nb30, and Nb49) for the APH2 motif. The X-ray structures of APH2 bound to Nb26, Nb49, and a combined Nb28/Nb30 complex have been resolved (PDBs 7A50, 7A48, and 7A4D, respectively). We fused kRasG12C to the APH2 motif using a continuous alpha-helical fusion design and employed cryo-EM to determine the structures of two distinct complexes of kRasG12C fused to APH2 in complex with nanobodies.

Materials and methods

Methods to design the different kRas constructs

Fusion to the cyclic homo-oligomer scaffold HR00C3_2

Three strategies were employed to design scaffolds with a longer N-terminal helix. In the first strategy, the N-terminal helix (residues 1–7) of HRC00C3_2 was superimposed on a helix made of 16 alanine residues using Coot (version 0.9.4.1)33. Subsequently, the computational design software Maestro (Schrödinger Release 2024-4) was used to propose a single mutation for each alanine in the helix to achieve a more rigid structure. This construct was named aH1.

In the second approach, one turn of the N-terminal helix of HRC00C3_2 was superimposed on one turn of a helix (residues 143–172) from the granulocyte colony-stimulating factor34 (PDB: 1RHG), which is characterized as rigid and ideal for helical fusion35. Using Maestro, different sets of mutations were proposed to stabilize the fusion: R22 and M26 for the first construct, R22, Q25, and W26 for the second construct, and R22, R23, Q25, and M26 for the third construct. This resulted in three constructs named aH2, aH3, and aH4.

In the third strategy, one turn of the N-terminal helix of HRC00C3_2 was superimposed on one turn of a stable single-alpha helix (SAH) domain, from residue K22 to E33, described as stable and already used for helical fusion to a scaffold for cryo-EM structure determination (PDB: 6HR1)36. Again, using Maestro, different sets of mutations were proposed to stabilize the fusion: R11 and M12 for the first construct, and Y8, R11, and M12 for the second construct. This resulted in two constructs named aH5 and aH6.

Out of the six constructs we purified, only the aH6 construct was fully soluble and produced in high quantities. Consequently, kRasG12C was fused to aH6 (refer to the sequence). To connect kRas to the aH6 scaffold, one turn of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C was superimposed on one turn of the aH6 N-terminal helix using Coot. In the superimposed region, amino acids were selected from either of the two natural sequences to stabilize the shared helix.

Fusion to the helical huntingtin peptide

The sequence for the helical huntingtin peptide is derived from PDB 3LRH30. All constructs were developed using a “shared helix method”35. This technique involves superimposing one or two terminal alpha-helical turns of the interacting proteins using a molecular graphics program to form a stable connecting helix.

Construct 1: Two turns of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C were superimposed on two turns of the huntingtin peptide.

Construct 2: Three turns of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C were superimposed on three turns of the huntingtin peptide.

Construct 3: One turn of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C was superimposed on one turn of the huntingtin peptide.

Construct 4: Two residues of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C were superimposed on the two first residues of the huntingtin peptide.

In the superimposed region, kRasG12C residues were chosen for positions pointing to its core, and huntingtin peptide residues were chosen for positions pointing to its core (refer to the sequence). All superimpositions were performed using Coot (version 0.9.4.1)33.

Alpha helix fusion methods: fusion to the APH2 motif

Three distinct kRasG12C constructs fused with the APH2 motif, labeled CC1, CC2, and CC3, were developed using different design approaches.

CC1 was created by substituting the last five residues (165–169) of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C with the first five residues of the APH2 motif (see Supplementary Fig. 3).

CC2 was designed using again a “shared helix method”35. Two turns of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C were superimposed on two turns of the N-terminal helix of the APH2 motif in Coot. In this superimposition, residues His166 and Lys167 of kRasG12C faced its core, so these residues were retained in positions 2 and 3 of the fusion helix. From the APH2 motif, Leu1, Glu4, and Leu5 faced the core of the APH2 dimerization interface, so they were incorporated into positions 1, 4, and 5 of the fusion helix (see Fig. 1).

CC3 employed another strategy based on connecting through a helical linker to construct an alternative fusion. Here, kRasG12C was linked to the APH2 scaffold using the N-terminal residues 5–18 of the Htt protein as a helical peptide linker30. Using a similar residue-selection approach as for CC2, one turn of the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C was superimposed on one turn of the Htt motif, while two turns of the APH2 motif were superimposed on one two turns of the Htt linker. Residues pointing toward the core of either kRasG12C or the APH2 scaffold were retained in the final design (see Fig. 1).

Additionally, CC3 WT (without the G12C mutation) was also produced.

Cloning, expression and purification

For all kRas constructs—including the six HR00C3 constructs, kRas fused to HRC00C3, the four kRas fused to huntingtin constructs, and CC1, CC2, and CC3 (G12C and WT)—along with the nanobodies (N26, Nb28, Nb30, Nb49, and the anti-BC2 nanobody) and NF1, DNA sequences encoding each protein were cloned into proprietary (Sanofi) expression vector with a C-terminal hexa-histidine. In contrast, the TEAD2, c-Raf-CR1, and VL constructs were also cloned into the same expression vector but included a stop codon to eliminate the His-Tag introduced by the plasmid.

All the designed recombinant proteins were produced in Escherichia coli BL21 DE3 Star cells (Thermo Fisher, 10328512) cultured in Luria Broth (LB) auto-inducible medium (Merck Millipore, 71757-5), supplemented with kanamycin (30 mg/L) (Merck Millipore, K0129). The bacteria were grown at 28 °C, and cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4,000× rpm for 20 min. Cell lysis was achieved through sonication using a lysis buffer composed of 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) (Merck Millipore, 93363), 500 mM NaCl (Merck Millipore, S7653), 1 mM TCEP (Merck Millipore, C4706), and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck Millipore, 11836145001). Following lysis, cell debris were removed by ultracentrifugation at 20,000 × rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was then subjected to a second centrifugation at the same speed before being loaded onto a 5 mL HisTrap FF crude column (Cytiva, 17528601). Protein elution was performed by gradually increasing imidazole concentrations from 0 to 500 mM using buffer B (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 500 mM imidazole (Merck Millipore, I202)). The purest fractions were pooled and concentrated to 20 mg using a 30 kDa cut-off Vivaspin concentrator (Merck Millipore). Final purification was performed using size-exclusion chromatography either on a Superdex 75 or 200 HiLoad GE column (Cytiva), depending on the protein size. The column was pre-equilibrated with buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 1 mM MgCl2 (Merck Millipore, 13152)) prior to use. The purified protein was then concentrated to 10 mg/mL and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at − 80 °C.

For the TEAD2 construct, an additional purification step was incorporated between the HisTrap and gel filtration processes. The purest fractions obtained from the HisTrap elution were diluted fivefold with buffer R (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5 (Merck Millipore, H3375), and 1 mM TCEP) before being injected into a ResourceQ column (Cytiva). The protein was eluted using a high-salt elution buffer and subsequently subjected to gel filtration.

For the c-Raf CR1 construct, an additional purification step was introduced following the HisTrap process. The MBP tag was cleaved from the purest fractions obtained after HisTrap by incubating with TEV protease for 10 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was reloaded onto Ni-NTA beads to capture any remaining tagged proteins. After recovering the flow-through, the sample was then subjected to gel filtration for further purification.

DSF thermal stability measurement

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) was employed to assess the thermal stability of the CC1 and CC2 constructs. SYPRO™ Orange dye stock (5000X, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used, and samples were prepared in a 96-well plate. Each well contained 10 µL of protein at 0.5 mg/mL, with SYPRO ORANGE dye added at a final concentration of 20X. Measurements were conducted using a CFX96TM rT-PCR System (Bio-Rad), recording fluorescence at 500 and 610 nm across a temperature range from 20 °C to 90 °C, with a temperature increase rate of 0.12 °C/min. Data were processed using Bio-Rad CFX Maestro software (version 2.2).

Preparation of CC2-MRXT849 and CC3-MRXT849 complexes

Purified recombinant CC2 and CC3 proteins, in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 1 mM MgCl2, were concentrated to 10 mg/mL. These proteins were then supplemented with 5 mM MgCl2 and a fivefold molar excess of MRXT849 compound, dissolved in DMSO. The resulting ligand-protein complexes were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Prior to further analysis, the complexes were spin-filtered.

Nucleotide exchange protocol using (NH4)2SO4/alkaline phosphatase

The exchange buffer was prepared with 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and adjusted to pH 8.0. CC3 was diluted to a concentration of 130 µM and combined with a solution containing 600 µM Gpp(NH)p (Jena Bioscience, NU-401-50), 600 µM AP (equivalent to 11 U/mg of KRAS), 200 mM ammonium sulfate (Merck Millipore), and 100 µM ZnCl₂ (Merck Millipore), with the necessary volume of exchange buffer added to complete the mixture. The solution was incubated for 1 h at 23 °C, during which the tube was inverted once. After incubation, MgCl₂ was added to achieve a final concentration of 100 mM, and the mixture was centrifuged at 21,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting solution was concentrated using centrifugal filter units (e.g., Amicon® Ultra-2 Ultracel-3 membrane, 3 kDa MWCO). Finally, the sample was applied to a HiLoad Superdex 75 PG column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated with buffer Y (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM TCEP, pH 7.5) to ensure efficient buffer exchange and complete removal of AP.

High-performance liquid chromatography

Protein complexes were loaded onto a S200 10/300 column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 2 mM MgCl2. The complexes were injected into an HPLC system (Agilent 1260 Infinity) and eluted over 50 min at a flow rate of 0.05 mL/min. As a control, each protein was injected individually. Detection was performed at 280 nm.

For all cryo-EM analyses, 55 µL of protein complexes were prepared at a concentration of 70 µM for the targets or partners, 1.2X for the anti-nanobody Fab, and 1.5X for all nanobodies. These samples were then injected into the HPLC system and collected in 30µL fractions.

Cryogenic electron microscopy and structure analyses

Grid freezing

For all kRasG12C strategies, purified complexes at a concentration of 0.7 mg/mL, in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, and 1 mM MgCl2 were applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh gold grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH). In the case of TEAD2, purified complexes at the same concentration were applied either to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 300 mesh gold grids or UltraAuFoil R0.6/1.0 Au grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH). All grids were vitrified using a Vitrobot Mark IV system (FEI) at 4 °C and subsequently stored in liquid nitrogen.

Data collection and processing

Cryo-EM data for all the complexes were collected on a Glacios electron microscope equipped with a Falcon4 direct electron detector (ThermoFisher Scientific). The microscope was operated at 200 kV, and data were acquired in counting mode using EPU software (version 2.9), with a defocus range of − 0.8 to − 2.2 μm and a pixel size of 0.58 Å. For the CC3-Nb28-Nb30 complex, each movie consisted of 54 frames (representing 162 electron-event exposure fractions), with a dose per frame of 1.14 electrons/Å² and a total exposure time of 4.72 s.

Data processing was carried out using cryoSPARC (version 4.5.3). Full-frame motion correction and contrast-transfer function (CTF) estimation were performed using the built-in patch-motion correction and patch-CTF estimation routines. A total of 9,509 movies were selected from which 4,222,834 particles were automatically picked. From this set, 269,284 particles were further processed with Topaz for particle picking, leading to a total of 2,534,908 picked particles. After 2–3 rounds of 2D classification, 159,026 particles were used for a second round of particle picking with Topaz and for generating an ab initio model with two classes.

Heterogeneous refinement was performed using 885,752 particles from the second Topaz picking across four different volumes. This refinement step yielded 252,503 particles, which were then subjected to 3D non-uniform (NU) refinement and a final local refinement. The resulting map achieved a resolution of 3.55 Å, with locally higher resolution (see Fig. 2).

Structure analysis

The 3D structures of the three nanobodies bound to the APH2 motif were retrieved from the PDB (PDBs 7A50 for Nb26, and 7A4D for Nb28 and Nb30)32. The 3D structure of kRasG12C bound to GDP and MRXT849 was also available in the PDB (PDB 6UT0)21. These models were fitted into the cryo-EM density using rigid body fitting in UCSF CHIMERA (version 1.17.3), followed by manual adjustments in Coot33. All models were real-space refined in PHENIX (version 1.19.2)37, and their quality was evaluated using MolProbity. The final MolProbity score for the CC2-Nb26 complex was 2.22, with 95.34% of the amino acids in Ramachandran favored conformations (see Table S1, PDB ID 9I5E, EMD-52628).

Results

Different approaches were implemented to determine the structure of kRasG12C using cryo-EM

First, kRasG12C was fused to the trimeric scaffold HR00C3_2, designed in silico and consisting entirely of alpha helices, as confirmed by its x-ray structure (PDB 5K7V)29. This scaffold allows the fusion of three kRas molecules simultaneously, resulting in a final size of 150 KDa. To facilitate fusion with the C-terminal helix of kRas, the N-terminal helix of the scaffold was extended. To achieve this, three different helices were used. Initially, a poly-alanine helix was employed. Subsequently, two rigid helix segments were tested: an ideal helix from the 1RHG structure (amino acids 143–172) from the PDB database34, previously described for use in shared alpha helix connections35, and a single alpha-helical (SAH) domain previously described as a rigid linker36. Finally, computational design was utilized to identify various mutations and stabilize each N-terminal extension (see Materials and Methods). This approach led to the creation of six constructs, one of which was confirmed to be a soluble trimer. We then attempted to determine its structure using X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM. Unfortunately, even when we obtained crystals for X-ray crystallography, they diffracted at around 6 Å. For cryo-EM, issues with preferred orientation and high flexibility prevented us from resolving the cryo-EM map for both the scaffold alone and when fused to kRasG12C (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In a second approach, the helical huntingtin peptide, which is recognized by a known human variable light (VL) chain domain (PDB code 3LRH)30 was used. This method aimed to achieve a final complex size of 50 KDa, and additionally, we planned to use the megabody technique9 to enlarge the VL domain, resulting in a final complex size of 110 KDa. The huntingtin peptide was fused to kRasG12C, resulting in four distinct constructs (described in Materials and Methods), all of which were soluble and successfully recognized by the human VL domain (Supplementary Fig. 2). In parallel, the VL domain was fused to two bacterial proteins using the megabody technique9; however, this approach was unsuccessful as the fused proteins could not be produced as soluble.

Lastly, the antiparallel homodimeric APH2 CC motif31,32 was employed as a scaffold to design three kRasG12C-APH2 constructs. A helical fusion strategy was employed, utilizing the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C and the N-terminal helix of APH2 (Fig. 1). The first two constructs, named CC1 and CC2, directly fused the C-terminal helix of kRasG12C to the N-terminal helix of the APH2 motif. For CC1, residues from both kRas and the APH2 motif were retained without modification. In contrast, for CC2, residues from the fusion helix were selected following the “shared helix method”35, meaning residues of kRas were chosen for amino acid positions pointing to its core and APH2 residues were chosen for positions pointing to its core, as described in Materials and Methods. The third construct, named CC3, fused kRasG12C to the scaffold using a helical peptide linker, consisting of the N-terminal residues 5–18 of Htt30 (Fig. 1B), also using the “shared helix method”. This construct has a longer helix length than the other two constructs.

The three constructs were produced and purified. They formed dimers of approximately 40 KDa due to the APH2 motif. Since CC1 and CC2 had similar helical lengths, their stability was compared to determine which construct to use for the remainder of the study. Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) experiments revealed that CC2 was significantly more thermally stable than CC1, with a melting temperature of approximately 60 °C compared to 40 °C for CC1 (Supplementary Fig. 4). In consequence, only CC2 and CC3 were used to form complexes with different nanobodies. All four nanobodies successfully bound to both constructs individually. Additionally, two nanobodies, Nb28 and Nb30, were able to bind to CC3 simultaneously, but not to CC2 (Supplementary Figs. 5). Since the dimeric CC3 reaches a size of 40 KDa, the ability of two nanobodies to bind each monomer of CC3 simultaneously is advantageous, as the full complex would have a size of 100 KDa instead of 70 KDa with only one bound nanobody. Therefore, CC3, rather than CC2, was complexed with Nb28 and Nb30 to be used for initial cryo-EM datasets. To enhance the stability of kRasG12C prior to nanobody complexation, kRasG12C was incubated with the inhibitor MRTX84921.

The final 3D reconstruction achieved an overall resolution of 3.55 Å. A single kRas molecule, along with the full APH2 motif and the two nanobodies, Nb28 and Nb30 were visible in the final map. The second kRas molecule was not resolved in the density. Notably, the scaffold core—comprising the APH2 motif and nanobodies Nb28 and Nb30—was resolved at a significantly higher resolution than the kRasG12C component. The X-ray structure of kRasG12C bound to MRTX849 (PDB code 6UT0)21 was fitted into the map density, but key structural features of the protein were not well defined (Fig. 2). Neither MRTX849 nor the GDP molecule were clearly visible in the density.

CC3 binds two GTP-bound partners: c-Raf and NF1

To assess the functionality of our CC3 construct, we formed a complex with c-Raf in its GTP-bound state. Additionally, we produced and activated the CC3 WT construct (lacking the G12C mutation) to form a complex with NF1. After producing and purifying CC3 G12C and WT, the CR1 domain of c-Raf (consisting of the Ras-binding domain and the cysteine-rich domain, from amino acid 52 to 188) and the GRD domain of NF1 (from amino acid 1203 to 1551), we created a complex with activated CC3 (G12C and WT) using a protonated γ-guanylyl imidodiphosphate (γ-GppNHp) group (see Material and Methods). HPLC confirmed interactions between CC3 G12C/GTP and CR1 and CC3 WT/GTP and the GRD domain of NF1. To facilitate structural resolution via cryo-EM, we incorporated the nanobodies Nb28 and Nb30 into the final complexes. Images were collected for both complexes; however, while CR1 bound to kRasG12C/GTP was observed in the 2D classes, a well-defined 3D density map could not be obtained. In contrast, the APH2 motif and the nanobodies in the CC3-Nb28-Nb30-GRD complex were distinctly defined, while the kRasWT/GTP-GRD particles displayed only weak densities. Finally, a density map with a resolution of 3.16 Å was obtained, revealing only APH2 and the nanobodies, with no visible secondary structures for the kRasWT/GTP-GRD complex (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Cryo-EM structures of the oncogenic KRAS protein bound to MRTX849 and GDP

To enhance kRasG12C stability, we chose to use the shorter fusion helix length of the CC2 construct instead of CC3. As previously mentioned, due to steric hindrance, Nb28 and Nb30 were unable to bind simultaneously to CC2. A complex of CC2 bound to a single nanobody would have a molecular weight of approximately 70 kDa. In order to increase the final size of the complex, an anti-Nb Fab (Sanofi proprietary) was added. Following binding experiments, a 120 kDa complex consisting of CC2, Nb26, and the anti-Nb Fab was prepared for cryo-EM studies.

Two distinct complexes were observed in the 2D classes: one composed of CC2, Nb26, and the anti-Nb Fab, and the other composed of only CC2 and Nb26. We initially attempted to refine the full complex, including the anti-Nb Fab (Fig. 3). After several rounds of heterogeneous refinement, the CC2 dimer was successfully fitted into the map, but only one anti-Nb Fab bound to Nb26 was aligned on the coiled-coil structure. To improve the density, a local refinement was performed after non-uniform (NU) refinement. The mask used for local refinement focused on the dimeric CC2, a single Nb26, and the variable region of a single anti-Nb Fab (Supplementary Fig. 7). Although it was possible to fit models of anti-Nb Fab, Nb26, and CC2 into the final cryo-EM density, key structural features of each protein remained unclear.

In a second attempt, we focused on refining the smaller complex, which consisted of CC2 and Nb26, resulting in a 70 KDa assembly. The final 3D reconstruction achieved an overall resolution of 3.7 Å, with the scaffold core, composed of the APH2 motif and nanobody 26, resolved at a higher resolution than kRas (Fig. 3 and Table S1, PDB ID 9I5E, EMD-52628).

The X-ray structures of kRasG12C bound to MRTX849 (PDB code 6UT0)21 and nanobody 26 bound to the APH2 motif (PDB code 7A50)31,32 were initially fitted into the map density. The final model of kRasG12C shows a good fit to the map, with well-defined chain tracing across most of the structure. In the higher resolution regions, the side chain density corresponds well with the model. However, some regions were not clearly visible in density, including residues 61–76, 104–108, 114–127, and 143–155. Chain tracing was observed for the kRas Switch-I region, but no side chain density was evident there. As previously noted in the literature19, kRas Switch-II is not well-stabilized in this structure.

The X-ray structure of kRasG12C bound to MRTX849 fits well into the cryo-EM map. The ligand occupies the same pocket observed in the X-ray crystallography structure and is positioned identically. Additionally, the covalent bond between Cys12 and MRTX849 is clearly visible in the map. The GDP molecule is also clearly defined in the density (Fig. 4).

Tag-Based approach for structural determination of small proteins lacking terminal alpha-helices: a cryo-EM study of TEAD2

The methods tested on kRas relied on a target with a terminal helix to facilitate fusion on the scaffold. In a subsequent attempt, we aimed to develop a tool for determining the structure of small proteins without a terminal helix, using TEAD2 as a target.

To solve the structure of TEAD2 (from residue 217 to the end) by cryo-EM, we employed a tag-fusion strategy using the BC2 Tag38, which is recognized by a known anti-BC2 Nb with an established X-ray structure (PDB code: 5IVN). We designed a single construct, TEAD2-BC2, with a direct fusion to minimize flexibility. Specifically, the last residue of TEAD2 was directly replaced by the first residue of the BC2 Tag. The construct was successfully produced and purified. Concurrently, we generated the anti-BC2 Nb38 and formed a complex with the TEAD2 construct.

The final complex reached approximately 50 kDa and was further stabilized by adding an anti-Nb Fab (Sanofi proprietary), resulting in a total size of 100 kDa. Initially, we collected 12,291 movies on gold grids, resulting in a final map at 3.97 Å; however, only the Fab and nanobody were visible in the 2D classes and the map (Supplementary Fig. 8). Subsequently, we collected 9,050 movies on carbon grids, where the Fab and nanobody were clearly defined, but TEAD2 proteins exhibited weak densities. Ultimately, the proteins were not discernible in the final cryo-EM map, indicating that the fusion of TEAD2 to the tag introduced excessive flexibility, making this method ineffective for resolving the structure of TEAD2.

Discussion

In this study, we developed three distinct methods to elucidate the structure of a target protein, kRasG12C, using cryo-EM, each providing a unique advantage. The first method involved fusing kRas to a helical trimeric scaffold, the HR00C3_2 protein29, which is beneficial due to its larger size that avoids the need for complex formation with additional proteins like nanobodies or Fabs. The second approach, which involved fusing kRas to a small helical peptide, was advantageous for its simplicity, requiring minimal protein engineering since it involved only a small motif rather than a larger scaffold. Future investigations should focus on generating larger VL domains30 using the megabody technique9. The third system, which involved fusing kRasG12C to the coiled-coil motif APH2, demonstrates a viable strategy for determining the structure of kRasG12C and similar small therapeutic targets with helices at the N- or C-terminus using cryo-EM.

This coiled-coil system marks a significant improvement over existing methods, as it is specifically designed to enhance the effective size of small proteins while ensuring strong stability and achieving C2 symmetry with minimal modifications to the target protein. Furthermore, this system can be expanded with four publicly available nanobodies that bind specifically to the APH2 motif, increasing its versatility in various applications. Using this method, we successfully determined the structure of kRasG12C linked to the APH2 motif and Nb26 at a global resolution of 3.7 Å.

All these methods could be further optimized, particularly considering the observed flexibility, by using the synthetic cross-linker linker EY-CBS39, which selectively stabilizes a-helices by forming cross-links between cysteines at positions i and i + 11. However, using our last method, the observed flexibility is also due to the target itself, notably because of the Switch regions, which remain flexible unless constrained in crystal contacts19.

In our investigation, we explored a novel strategy for proteins lacking helices at the N- or C-terminus, by fusing the BC2 tag to the TEAD2 protein38. This fusion aimed to facilitate the formation of a complex with an anti-BC2 nanobody and a known anti-Nb Fab. However, this approach resulted in excessive flexibility, even with the tag directly attached to the last b-strand of TEAD2. To address this flexibility, one idea could be to introduce a peptide within the structure of the target that can be recognized by a nanobody and/or a Fab, thereby increasing the final size. Recently, the nanobody 6 (Nb6) was shown to bind the inactive-state intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) of the kappa opioid receptor (KOR), even when this loop was grafted onto a wide range of receptors40. The ICL3 loop is known to be flexible, but the binding of Nb6 appears to rigidify the loop. The use of Nb6 bound to this intracellular loop successfully resolved several GPCR structures, including the neurotensin 1 receptor bound to an antagonist, the µ-opioid receptor with a clinical antagonist, and the somatostatin receptor 2 in its apo state40. One possible approach could be to use the ICL3 loop by inserting it within the structure of the small target to solve its structure by cryo-EM.

Additionally, an alternative approach for proteins missing N or C-terminal helices could involve using our coiled-coil system indirectly. This can be achieved by targeting proteins with terminal helices, such as DARpins or Alpharep41, which can then bind to proteins without terminal helices. This strategy would facilitate the formation of a dimeric complex where the fused helical scaffold is attached to the small target protein. However, this method introduces complexity, as it requires the development of target-specific scaffolds for each new target.

Future studies encompassing a broader range of protein targets will be crucial to fully demonstrate the versatility and imaging advantages of our scaffolding system using the APH2 motif. The current coiled-coil approach and its possible variations should make cryo-EM structure determination of small proteins a routine method. This approach stands out as a significant advancement, serving as a scalable and adaptable platform for structural studies of small, therapeutically relevant proteins with terminal alpha-helices. This paves the way for future advances in structural biology.
