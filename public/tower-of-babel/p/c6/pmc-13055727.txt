Development and Validation of a Prognostic Nomogram for Post–Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Heart Failure Hospitalization in Patients With Concurrent Symptomatic Aortic Stenosis and Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Jun Wang, Shoupeng Duan, Hui Li, Shili Wu, Peng Zhao, Tongjian Zhu, Shengxing Tang, Jiajun Zhu, Bi Tang, Jinjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting risk stratification models are insufficient in identifying patients at high risk for heart failure (HF) hospitalization, particularly among those presenting with symptomatic aortic stenosis and the HF with preserved ejection fraction phenotype.MethodsThis multicenter cohort study enrolled 321 patients diagnosed with severe aortic stenosis and HF with preserved ejection fraction who underwent transcatheter aortic valve replacement between January 2017 and February 2024. Various predictive modeling techniques were used, including random forest, XGBoost, SuperPC, plsRcox, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–Cox, Gradient Boosting Machine, Coxboost, and Cox regression analysis, at multiple time points.ResultsPatients were divided into a derivation cohort (n=191) and an external validation cohort (n=130) based on institutional affiliation, with a median follow‐up of 20 months. Feature selection using the Boruta algorithm and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, combined with variance inflation factor analysis to assess multicollinearity, identified 6 independent predictors. Among 8 prediction models evaluated, the Cox regression–based nomogram demonstrated superior performance in external validation, achieving time‐dependent area under the curve values of 0.824 (95% CI, 0.693–0.956) at 12 months and 0.818 (95% CI, 0.715–0.920) at 20 months. The nomogram exhibited excellent calibration and substantial clinical utility across both time points, consistently outperforming the European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation in discrimination and reclassification analyses. An interactive web‐based clinical decision support tool was developed to facilitate point‐of‐care implementation.ConclusionsThis nomogram, based on machine learning and incorporating metabolic biomarkers, exhibits high predictive accuracy for HF hospitalization in patients with symptomatic aortic stenosis and high‐risk HF with preserved ejection fraction phenotype following transcatheter aortic valve replacement.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: ChiCTR2400092655.

This is the first externally validated prognostic nomogram specifically designed to predict post–transcatheter aortic valve replacement heart failure hospitalization risk in patients with concurrent heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and severe aortic stenosis, addressing a critical gap in postprocedural risk stratification for this complex, high‐risk population. Heart failure (HF) and aortic stenosis (AS) frequently coexist, creating a significant clinical challenge due to their shared pathophysiological mechanisms and substantial morbidity and death. 1 , 2 Despite transformative advances in transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), HF decompensation remains the leading cause of cardiac rehospitalization and a key factor influencing death in patients with AS, before and after the procedure. 1 , 2 Notably, the prevalence of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) remains high following TAVR. However, focusing solely on the postprocedural valvular hemodynamics does not adequately address the broader range of cardiovascular risks that persist beyond the immediate periprocedural period. 3 , 4

Historically, AS and HFpEF have been conceptualized as distinct clinical entities within research paradigms and practice guidelines, categorizing them as valvular heart disease and myocardial dysfunction, respectively. 5 , 6 This artificial dichotomy has led to fragmented clinical management strategies that prioritize valve‐centric outcomes while neglecting systematic HFpEF assessment and treatment optimization in post‐TAVR care. This is particularly concerning given the pathophysiological overlap between these conditions. Both share essential mechanisms of diastolic dysfunction, which include impaired ventricular relaxation and increased myocardial stiffness. 1 , 3 In AS, chronic pressure overload leads to compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy, which progressively impairs diastolic function, similar to the primary mechanisms behind HFpEF. 1 , 3 Consequently, these overlapping mechanisms often lead to similar clinical presentations, such as exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and exercise intolerance, posing challenges to traditional diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Contemporary echocardiographic parameters often fail to capture the nuanced pathophysiology of HFpEF, particularly in the context of concurrent valvular disease. This can lead to delays in recognition, suboptimal treatment strategies, and negative clinical outcomes. 1 , 3 Identifying subclinical left ventricular decompensation early, through novel biomarkers and integrated multimodal assessments, presents a crucial opportunity for intervention before overt systolic dysfunction occurs. The translation of comprehensive clinical phenotyping, incorporating comorbidity status, biochemical, and imaging parameters, into actionable risk stratification tools could fundamentally transform post‐TAVR care by enabling timely, targeted interventions that mitigate HF progression.

Electronic health records (EHRs) provide an unprecedented opportunity for longitudinal risk assessment through systematic capture of multidimensional clinical data. 7 , 8 , 9 When combined with machine learning algorithms, EHRs facilitate the development of sophisticated predictive models for automated risk stratification. 7 , 8 , 9 Although our previous work established a machine learning framework for predicting 1‐year composite cardiovascular outcomes (cardiac death, revascularization, myocardial infarction, and stroke) in patients with severe AS having HFpEF undergoing TAVR, there are still critical gaps. 4 Notably, long‐term HF‐specific rehospitalization outcomes derived from EHR data have not been systematically evaluated. Additionally, current machine learning approaches in cardiovascular medicine often neglect the temporal dynamics of risk evolution, which is a significant limitation considering the progressive nature of HF pathophysiology.

To address these limitations, this study incorporates time‐varying risk assessment into the development of machine learning models, using comprehensive EHR data to evaluate multiple algorithms for predicting long‐term HF rehospitalization in patients with severe AS and HFpEF phenotype following TAVR. This approach aimed to provide clinically actionable risk stratification that reflects the dynamic nature of cardiovascular risk after TAVR, ultimately informing personalized surveillance strategies and preventive interventions.

Methods

Data Sharing Agreement

Individual participant data that underlie the results reported in this article, after deidentification, can be obtained from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the local research ethics committees of the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University (2024 KY065), the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (K202308‐26), the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College (2024 [130]), and Xiangyang Central Hospital (2024–169). Given the retrospective design of the study, the need to obtain informed consent from eligible patients was waived by the ethics committees.

Participants

This multicenter, retrospective cohort study analyzed consecutive patients aged ≥65 years with severe AS and HFpEF. Patient data were systematically collected from 4 tertiary cardiovascular centers across geographically diverse regions spanning January 2017 through February 2024. To minimize systematic differences and ensure data consistency, all participating centers implemented standardized protocols for TAVR planning, procedural execution, and postprocedural care. 6 Specifically, standardized indicator‐based methodologies were uniformly applied across centers for TAVR planning, including consistent preprocedural risk assessment, standardized echocardiographic and computed tomography imaging protocols, and harmonized patient selection criteria according to current international guidelines. Regarding enrollment periods, while the overall study spanned 7 years, each center contributed patients throughout the majority of this time frame, ensuring balanced temporal representation across institutions. This design minimizes potential confounding from evolving TAVR technologies or changing clinical practices over time. All participating centers were established high‐volume TAVR programs with experienced operators who had completed their institutional learning curves before study initiation, thereby reducing operator‐related variability. Procedural workflows were standardized through regular intercenter meetings and adherence to unified protocols for valve selection, deployment techniques, and postprocedural monitoring. Comprehensive inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Table S1, with patient enrollment methodology illustrated in Figure 1. The study protocol received approval from the institutional review boards of all participating centers and was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, which used deidentified clinical data, informed consent requirements were waived in line with institutional guidelines. Our work is reported in line with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines. 10

EHR Data

Comprehensive EHR data were systematically extracted for all enrolled patients using standardized data collection protocols. To ensure methodological rigor and minimize selection bias, all clinical variables were obtained exclusively from routine standard‐of‐care assessments performed during the index hospitalization and are fully described in Supporting Methods S1. This approach eliminated concerns about selective data availability or measurement inconsistencies across participating centers. Laboratory specimens were collected following standardized protocols: Venous blood samples were obtained during fasting conditions on the morning following hospital admission and before TAVR procedures. All biochemical analyses adhered to certified laboratory methods in accordance with institutional quality assurance standards. Inflammatory and metabolic biomarkers were calculated using established formulas 11 , 12 : triglyceride–glucose index (TyG), TyG–body mass index (TyG‐BMI), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and AIP‐BMI index.

European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation

The European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) is a detailed tool for assessing perioperative risk, specifically designed and validated for patients undergoing cardiac surgery. 13 For each patient in our study, we meticulously collected clinical data based on the predefined variables and scoring criteria outlined in the original EuroSCORE publications. All calculations were carried out by trained clinical researchers with subsequent verification through double data entry to minimize computational errors.

Clinical End Point

Study participants underwent systematic clinical monitoring with a median follow‐up duration of 20 months. This was achieved through structured outpatient visits, supplemented by standardized telephone interviews when in‐person assessments were not possible. Follow‐up evaluations were conducted according to a prespecified protocol at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 20 months after TAVR, with subsequent annual evaluations thereafter.

The primary end point of the study was the time to the first unplanned hospitalization for HF. This was defined as an unplanned hospital admission with a primary diagnosis of acute decompensated HF, necessitating intravenous diuretic therapy or vasoactive support. HF hospitalizations were adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee comprising 2 cardiologists blinded to baseline risk stratification data. Any discrepancies in their assessments were resolved through a consensus review.

Feature Selection

Feature selection used a comprehensive 2‐stage machine learning approach aimed at optimizing predictive performance while ensuring that the models remain interpretable and generalizable. We began with a candidate set of EHRs that included demographic information, comorbidity profiles, anthropometric measurements, laboratory results, and echocardiographic imaging findings. This selection was based on established clinical relevance and the availability of complete data. To identify the most informative and stable predictors, we implemented a dual‐algorithm selection strategy.

In the feature selection phase, we used the Boruta algorithm and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression as complementary methodologies, recognizing that each approach captures distinct statistical properties of predictor–outcome relationships in the derivation cohort. The Boruta algorithm is a wrapper‐based approach that uses random forest importance rankings, iteratively comparing the importance scores of original features against permuted shadow attributes to distinguish genuinely informative variables from noise artifacts. This method excels at detecting complex nonlinear relationships and variable interactions that may not conform to linear modeling assumptions. 14 Concurrently, LASSO regression applies L1 regularization penalties to coefficient estimation, systematically shrinking noncontributory parameters toward 0 while promoting sparse model architectures that minimize overfitting risk. 15 LASSO is particularly effective at identifying variables with strong linear predictive associations while penalizing redundant or weakly contributing features. For λ optimization, we used 10‐fold cross‐validation to identify λ.min, which represents a widely adopted approach for optimizing predictive performance in penalized regression frameworks. We selected λ.min, defined as the regularization parameter value that produced the minimum mean cross‐validated binomial deviance across all 10 folds. This dual‐selection framework enhances the robustness of feature selection by requiring agreement between fundamentally different selection mechanisms: importance‐based ranking and penalty‐based shrinkage.

Variables that met selection criteria across both algorithms were evaluated for multicollinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis. 16 A stringent VIF threshold of <5 was enforced to ensure acceptable predictor independence, consistent with established best practices for high‐dimensional biomedical modeling. This cautious approach helps minimize the risk of unstable coefficient estimates and improves model interpretability by removing redundant predictive pathways. The final set of features, which met algorithmic selection criteria and multicollinearity requirements, was then used in comprehensive model development and validation workflows.

Model Validation and Evaluation

The feature variable selection and model construction were performed exclusively on the derivation cohort using internal cross‐validation approaches. The independent external validation cohort was strictly held out during the entire model development process and was used only for final performance evaluation. This institutional‐based stratification strategy ensures complete prevention of data leakage while providing robust external validation across different health care settings, patient populations, and clinical practices. We implemented a comprehensive suite of 8 machine learning algorithms specifically selected for their proven effectiveness in survival analysis and clinical risk prediction. These include random forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting Machine, LASSO‐Cox, Coxboost, SuperPC, plsRcox, and Cox proportional hazards regression. The specific parameter settings have been documented in Table S2.

This diversity of algorithms allows for a robust comparison of models across various methodological paradigms, ranging from tree‐based ensemble methods optimized for nonlinear interactions to regularized regression techniques suited for high‐dimensional data, as well as specialized survival models that incorporate latent variable frameworks. The performance of the model was evaluated using a comprehensive assessment framework that included measures of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. For discrimination assessment, we used the bootstrap resampling methodology with 500 iterations to obtain bias‐corrected estimates of the concordance index. Discriminative performance was also assessed using time‐dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve calculations at the 12‐ and 20‐month follow‐up time points. Calibration accuracy was evaluated by comparing predicted versus observed HF hospitalization probabilities across these time intervals. Clinical utility was analyzed through decision curve analysis at the 12‐ and 20‐month marks, assessing the net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities to evaluate the real‐world applicability of risk‐based intervention strategies.

The derivation and external validation cohorts were stratified into low‐risk and high‐risk groups on the basis of risk scores derived from a nomogram. Median‐based dichotomization was used to ensure balanced group sizes in both the derivation and validation cohorts, thereby facilitating robust statistical comparisons. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed to assess time to first HF hospitalization across risk strata, with statistical significance evaluated using the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression to quantify the magnitude of risk differences between groups.

We evaluated the effectiveness of the nomogram and the overall ability of the EuroSCORE assessment in predicting HF hospitalization. This was done by using various methods to enhance discrimination, including increasing the area under the curve (AUC), achieving a category‐free continuous net reclassification improvement >0, and improving integrated discrimination improvement. 17

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of quantile–quantile plots. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as means±SDs and compared between derivation and validation cohorts using the independent samples Student's t‐test. Nonnormally distributed variables are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and proportions (%) and compared using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were <5. All statistical tests were 2‐sided, with statistical significance defined as P<0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

This multicenter retrospective cohort study included 321 patients with concurrent HFpEF and severe AS undergoing TAVR. Patient data were systematically collected from 4 tertiary cardiovascular centers across geographically diverse regions spanning January 2017 through February 2024. This approach ensured representation of varied clinical practice patterns and patient demographics. To optimize model development and validation, patients were stratified by institutional affiliation into temporally and geographically distinct cohorts. The derivation cohort consisted of 191 patients (59.5%) from the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. The independent external validation cohort included 130 patients (40.5%) from the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical College and Xiangyang Central Hospital. Baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory test, and imaging characteristics demonstrated overall comparability between derivation and validation cohorts, supporting the validity of our external validation approach. However, we observed statistically significant differences in several variables, including platelet count, indirect bilirubin, EuroSCORE, cystatin C, total bilirubin, platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, platelet distribution width, alanine aminotransferase, and fibrinogen (P<0.05), as detailed in the Table1. These differences reflect the natural variability across different patient populations and health care settings, which enhances the generalizability of our predictive models.

Model Construction and Variable Selection

The Boruta algorithm is a robust wrapper‐based feature selection method that evaluates the predictive importance of original variables by comparing them to permuted shadow attributes generated through random shuffling. This process helps distinguish genuinely informative features from spurious correlations that often arise in high‐dimensional data sets. Through iterative random forest–based importance scoring over 500 iterations, Boruta identified 13 statistically significant predictors that outperformed their randomized counterparts. These predictors are age, type 2 diabetes, neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, TyG index, TyG‐BMI, AIP, AIP‐BMI, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and γ‐glutamyl transferase (Figure 2A and 2B).

Systematic feature selection was conducted using the Boruta algorithm, a wrapper‐based method that identifies all relevant predictors by comparing feature importance against randomly permuted shadow features through iterative random forest modeling.

This approach provides robust feature selection by maintaining statistical control over the selection process while avoiding arbitrary importance thresholds, ensuring comprehensive identification of clinically relevant predictors associated with heart failure hospitalization risk in patients with symptomatic AS and HFpEF following TAVR. Complementary feature selection was conducted using LASSO regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation to identify the most parsimonious predictive model while reducing the risk of overfitting (Figure 3A and 3B). LASSO applies L1 regularization to the binomial deviance loss function, systematically shrinking the coefficients of less significant variables toward 0 through progressive penalization. The optimal regularization parameter (λ.min=0.060) was determined using the 1‐SE rule, resulting in a sparse model that included 9 features from the original candidate set of 54 variables: type 2 diabetes, current smoking, NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, AST, and total protein. The combination of both selection methodologies produced 7 consensus predictors that exhibited exceptional robustness across fundamentally different algorithmic approaches: type 2 diabetes, NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG index, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, and AST. This dual‐algorithm consensus strategy enhances feature stability by requiring simultaneous selection through importance‐based ranking (Boruta) and penalty‐based regularization (LASSO), thereby maximizing the likelihood of identifying truly predictive biomarkers while minimizing false discovery rates. Subsequently, the 7 consensus predictors were subjected to stepwise backward multivariable Cox regression analysis, which identified 6 independent predictors that were significantly associated with an increased risk of HF hospitalization: type 2 diabetes (adjusted HR, 2.915 [95% CI, 1.244–6.833]; P=0.014), NT‐proBNP (HR, 1.001 [95% CI, 1.001–1.001]; P<0.001), fasting blood glucose (HR, 1.218 [95% CI, 1.011–1.469]; P=0.038), TyG‐BMI (HR, 1.015 [95% CI, 1.005–1.025]; P=0.005), AIP‐BMI (HR, 1.073 [95% CI, 1.019–1.130]; P=0.008), and AST (HR, 1.012 [95% CI, 1.001–1.024]; P=0.039) (Figure S1). The VIF analysis indicated no evidence of significant multicollinearity among the selected predictors (Table S3). All variables demonstrated VIF values well below the conservative threshold of 5: type 2 diabetes (VIF, 1.80), NT‐proBNP (VIF, 1.14), fasting blood glucose (VIF, 1.84), TyG‐BMI (VIF, 1.77), AIP‐BMI (VIF, 1.55), and AST (VIF, 1.28). These results suggest that each variable contributes unique, nonredundant information to the predictive model, supporting their inclusion in the final risk stratification algorithm. Following the multicollinearity assessment, 6 independent predictors were retained for the construction of the machine learning model: type 2 diabetes, NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, and AST.

Cox Regression Model Diagnostic Assessment

Comprehensive assessment of the proportional hazards assumption was conducted using Schoenfeld residuals tests (Figure S2). The global test revealed significant departure from the proportional hazards assumption (P=2.46×10−5), indicating time‐varying effects for several covariates. Individual variable testing identified specific violations: diabetes (P=0.0112), NT‐proBNP (P=0.0455), TyG‐BMI (P=0.0083), and AST (P=0.01) all demonstrated significant time‐varying effects. In contrast, fasting blood glucose (P=0.1069) and AIP‐BMI (P=0.111) satisfied the proportional hazards assumption. Schoenfeld residual plots (Figure S2) confirmed these findings, showing temporal coefficient variations for diabetes, NT‐proBNP, TyG‐BMI, and AST, while fasting blood glucose and AIP‐BMI exhibited stable coefficients throughout the follow‐up period.

Deviance residuals analysis revealed acceptable model fit without extreme influential observations. The residual distribution demonstrated symmetric clustering around 0 with no observations exceeding the critical threshold of |deviance residual| >3 (Figures S3 and S4). The fitted trend line showed minimal systematic deviation from 0, and CI bands remained stable across the observation range, indicating robust model performance without significant bias from individual cases.

Martingale residuals analysis revealed nonlinear associations for several continuous predictors, including NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG‐BMI, and AST (Figure S5). Despite violations of the proportional hazards assumption for several variables, DFBETA residuals analysis confirmed model stability with no single observation exerting disproportionate influence on coefficient estimates across all variables. The residual patterns supported overall model robustness and reliability of the final predictive model.

Model Validation and Evaluation

A comprehensive ensemble of 8 advanced machine learning algorithms was developed and rigorously optimized to predict the risk of HF hospitalizations in patients with HFpEF and severe AS following TAVR. The algorithms include random forest, XGBoost, SuperPC, plsRcox, LASSO–Cox, Gradient Boosting Machine, CoxBoost, and Cox proportional hazards regression. These algorithms represent diverse methodological approaches spanning tree‐based ensemble methods, regularized regression techniques, dimensionality reduction strategies, and classical survival analysis frameworks. All models were trained on the complete derivation cohort (n=191) using identical feature sets and were subsequently subjected to rigorous testing on an external validation cohort (n=130) to ensure that they generalize well across various institutional settings and patient populations.

Model performance was evaluated comprehensively using established metrics for survival prediction. Discrimination was assessed by calculating AUC at 12‐ and 20‐month time points, while calibration plots and clinical utility were determined through decision curve analysis quantifying net clinical benefit across probability thresholds.

Among all the evaluated algorithms, the Cox proportional hazards regression model demonstrated the best discriminative performance during external validation, achieving time‐dependent AUCs of 0.824 (95% CI, 0.693–0.956) at 12 months and 0.818 (95% CI, 0.715–0.920) at 20 months for HF hospitalization prediction (Figure 4A through 4D). Moreover, the Cox model additionally exhibited optimal calibration characteristics, demonstrating a strong agreement between predicted probabilities (Figure S6A through S6P) and observed event rates. It also exhibited favorable clinical utility across relevant decision thresholds (Figure S7A through S7D). On the basis of comprehensive evaluation of discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability, we selected the Cox regression model as the final algorithm for developing a nomogram and for clinical implementation. Consequently, we constructed this clinically applicable prognostic nomogram model, which incorporates 6 validated predictors to estimate individualized probabilities of remaining free from HF hospitalization at 12‐ and 20‐month intervals following TAVR in patients with concurrent severe AS and HFpEF (Figure 5).

Diagnostic Performance of the Nomogram

The nomogram exhibited excellent discriminative accuracy in the derivation cohort, with a bias‐corrected concordance index of 0.920 (95% CI, 0.895–0.945) based on 500 bootstrap replicates, and retained strong discriminative ability in the external validation cohort, yielding a bias‐corrected concordance index of 0.796 (95% CI, 0.702–0.890). This indicates consistent performance across independent patient populations and health care settings (Figure S8A,B). In the derivation cohort, the nomogram achieved outstanding discrimination at 12 months (AUC, 0.957 [95% CI, 0.930–0.984]) with good performance maintained at 20 months (AUC, 0.958 [95% CI, 0.932–0.985]; Figure S8A). The external validation cohort confirmed clinically meaningful discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.824 (95% CI, 0.693–0.956) at 12 months and 0.818 (95% CI, 0.715–0.920) at 20 months (Figure S8B). This demonstrates the successful transportability of the model across different institutional cohorts.

Calibration assessments using bootstrap‐corrected calibration plots (with 500 iterations) demonstrated excellent alignment between predicted probabilities and observed HF hospitalization rates at 12 and 20 months in both derivation and validation cohorts (Figure S9A‐S9B). The calibration curves demonstrated minimal deviation from the 45‐degree line of perfect agreement, indicating that predicted probabilities accurately reflect true event rates without systematic over‐ or underestimation bias.

Decision curve analysis evaluated the clinical utility of the nomogram by assessing the net benefit across various probability thresholds, comparing it to standard strategies of treating all patients or none. In the derivation cohort, the nomogram demonstrated a positive net benefit across threshold probability ranges of 1% to 72% for 12‐month predictions and 1% to 92% for 20‐month predictions (Figure S10A,S10B). External validation confirmed sustained clinical utility with ranges of 7% to 95% at 12 months and 7% to 55% at 20 months (Figures S10C,S10D) through, indicating that the nomogram yielded positive net benefit across 12 and 20 months. The net benefit rate of the prediction nomogram was found to be higher than that of separate models run for each of the 6 variables (type 2 diabetes, NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, and AST) for 12‐ and 20‐month HF hospitalization risk in both the derivation and external validation cohorts (Figure S11A through S11D).

Risk stratification utility was evaluated by dichotomizing patients into high‐ and low‐risk groups using the median nomogram‐derived risk score. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant risk stratification efficacy in both cohorts, with high‐risk patients experiencing substantially elevated HF hospitalization rates compared with low‐risk patients (derivation cohort: log‐rank P<0.001; validation cohort: log‐rank P<0.001; Figure S12A and S12B).

Incremental Predictive Value Assessment: Nomogram Versus EuroSCORE

To assess the incremental predictive value of our novel nomogram compared with the established EuroSCORE risk assessment tool, we conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis using various discrimination and reclassification metrics in the derivation and validation cohorts (Tables S4 through S7). Discriminative performance comparison revealed substantial superiority of the nomogram over EuroSCORE across both cohorts. In the derivation cohort, the nomogram achieved a concordance index of 0.920 (95% CI, 0.895–0.945) compared with the EuroSCORE's concordance index of 0.536 (95% CI, 0.471–0.601). This difference represents a clinically meaningful improvement in discrimination (Δconcordance index = 0.385, P<0.001). External validation confirmed this advantage, with the nomogram retaining a concordance index of 0.796 (95% CI, 0.702–0.890) versus EuroSCORE's concordance index of 0.551 (95% CI, 0.461–0.641). This resulted in a significant improvement in discrimination (Δconcordance index = 0.245, P<0.001).

Advanced reclassification analysis using category‐free continuous net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement metrics quantified the nomogram's incremental predictive utility. In the derivation cohort, the nomogram exhibited outstanding reclassification performance with a category‐free continuous net reclassification improvement of 1.552 (95% CI, 1.289–1.815; P<0.001) and integrated discrimination improvement of 0.528 (95% CI, 0.445–0.611; P<0.001; Tables S4 and S6). External validation confirmed robust reclassification benefits with category‐free continuous net reclassification improvement of 1.577 (95% CI, 1.234–1.920; P<0.001) and integrated discrimination improvement of 0.341 (95% CI, 0.223–0.459; P<0.001), demonstrating consistent incremental value across independent patient populations (Tables S5 and S7). A comprehensive performance evaluation using time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis revealed that the validated nomogram demonstrated a superior ability to discriminate risk compared with the EuroSCORE across all examined time points, in the derivation and external validation cohorts (Figure S13A and S13B).

Clinical Implementation and Digital Health Integration

To promote widespread clinical adoption and improve accessibility at the point of care, we developed CycleDream, an interactive web‐based clinical decision support tool (https://cycledream.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/). This tool transforms our validated nomogram into a user‐friendly digital platform that enables real‐time assessment of HF hospitalization risk (Figure 6). The CycleDream platform features a comprehensive risk calculator interface that systematically collects 6 validated predictor variables (type 2 diabetes status, NT‐proBNP levels, fasting blood glucose, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, and AST) through intuitive input fields with built‐in data validation and range checking. Upon variable entry, the application performs real‐time Cox regression calculations using our validated model coefficients. This generates individualized survival probabilities at 12 and 20 months, along with corresponding 95% CI. Additionally, the platform automatically categorizes patients into distinct risk groups using an empirically validated median cut point, facilitating quick clinical interpretation and decision making.

Discussion

This comprehensive multicenter study presents the first validated prognostic nomogram specifically designed to predict the risk of HF hospitalization in patients with HFpEF and severe AS undergoing TAVR. Through rigorous machine learning–driven feature selection and comprehensive algorithm comparison, we identified 6 independent predictors: type 2 diabetes, NT‐proBNP, fasting blood glucose, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI, and AST. Together, these predictors provide superior risk stratification compared with established perioperative assessment tools. The resulting nomogram demonstrates excellent discriminative performance (concordance index: 0.920 in derivation and 0.796 in validation). It exhibits robust calibration and substantial clinical utility across relevant decision thresholds, representing a significant advancement in personalized cardiovascular risk assessment for this high‐risk population.

The contemporary management of patients with HFpEF and severe AS represents a significant clinical challenge that persists even after successful TAVR. 1 , 3 , 4 Despite achieving excellent procedural outcomes and favorable hemodynamic results, many patients continue to experience HF hospitalizations in the months following intervention. This disconnect between procedural success and clinical outcomes suggests fundamental gaps in our understanding and management of this complex patient population.

Current clinical practice tends to emphasize the technical aspects of valve replacement while inadvertently overlooking the comprehensive management of underlying HFpEF. 18 , 19 , 20 This valve‐centric approach becomes particularly problematic when patients present with mildly to moderately elevated natriuretic peptide levels in the context of preserved left ventricular systolic function. Clinicians may mistakenly attribute these biomarker elevations solely to the valvular pathology, assuming they will normalize after valve replacement. However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that HFpEF and severe AS are not independent entities but rather share common pathophysiological substrates including metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and microvascular disease. 4 , 19 , 20 Their coexistence likely represents a synergistic amplification of cardiovascular risk rather than a simple additive effect. Recognizing this pathophysiological overlap is essential for developing effective post‐TAVR management strategies.

The systematic exploitation of clinical data through sophisticated analytical frameworks can reveal previously hidden risk patterns and prognostic relationships that inform precision medicine approaches. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Modern EHR systems contain vast repositories of longitudinal clinical data, providing a holistic view of patient phenotypes and risk trajectories. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 To address this knowledge gap, we leveraged EHR data to develop a practical prediction model for HF hospitalization following TAVR in patients with HFpEF. Our comprehensive data set encompassed laboratory values, medication records, comorbidity profiles, and imaging findings that captured the full spectrum of patient phenotypes. Using machine learning–based feature selection followed by rigorous external validation, we identified 6 readily available clinical and metabolic parameters that reliably predict post‐TAVR HF risk across diverse patient populations.

An important methodological finding from our study is that Cox proportional hazards regression outperformed more complex machine learning algorithms in external validation, yielding concordance index values of 0.824 (95% CI, 0.693–0.956) at 12 months and 0.818 (95% CI, 0.715–0.920) at 20 months. This represents a more stable performance profile, with only a 13.4% relative decrease from derivation to validation compared with a 20% to 22% decreases for complex algorithms. This observation reinforces several important principles for clinical prediction research. First, algorithmic complexity does not guarantee superior real‐world performance, particularly when sample sizes are moderate and relationships between predictors and outcomes are approximately linear. Second, the interpretability of Cox regression, through explicit HRs and intuitive nomogram visualization, addresses practical barriers to clinical adoption that limit complex “black‐box” algorithms regardless of their theoretical flexibility. 25 , 26 Third, appropriate feature engineering, such as using composite metabolic indices that capture biological interactions, can enable simpler statistical methods to achieve robust performance while maintaining clinical transparency. 25 , 26 Our experience suggests that model selection should balance predictive accuracy with data characteristics and implementation requirements rather than defaulting to algorithmic sophistication. 25 , 26

The elevated levels of NT‐proBNP observed after technically successful TAVR in patients with HFpEF act as a clinical indicator for ongoing progression of this condition. However, this biomarker is often misinterpreted or overlooked in the context of a “successful” valve replacement. 27 This oversight represents a missed opportunity for early intervention and targeted management strategies for HFpEF, which could help prevent future hospitalizations due to HF. Our study's identification of NT‐proBNP as a continuous predictor of HF hospitalization risk provides empirical support for systematic biomarker‐guided management approaches that extend beyond the immediate postprocedural period.

Perhaps the most striking finding is the dominant role of metabolic dysfunction in predicting HF risk. Type 2 diabetes (HR, 2.915) and fasting blood glucose (HR, 1.218) demonstrated independent associations with HF hospitalization, reflecting their dual role as contributors to HFpEF pathophysiology through mechanisms of diabetic cardiomyopathy and as markers of systemic metabolic dysregulation. 28 Beyond diabetes status, 2 composite metabolic indices, TyG‐BMI and AIP‐BMI, emerged as independent predictors alongside fasting glucose. These composite indices, easily calculated from routine laboratory data, capture complex metabolic interactions that individual parameters evaluated separately cannot adequately represent. 11 , 12 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 TyG‐BMI integrates insulin resistance with adiposity, reflecting myocardial lipotoxicity and systemic inflammation, 11 , 30 , 31 while AIP‐BMI combines atherogenic dyslipidemia with body composition, capturing amplified cardiovascular risk when dyslipidemia coexists with obesity. 12 Our dual‐algorithm feature selection process consistently identified these composite indices in preference to their individual components modeled separately, and VIF analysis confirmed they provided nonredundant prognostic information.

The metabolic emphasis in our prediction model aligns with emerging paradigms that conceptualize HFpEF as a systemic metabolic disorder with cardiac manifestations rather than a primary cardiomyopathy. 5 This perspective has important therapeutic implications. Evidence‐directed medical therapy for HFpEF focuses primarily on diuretics for congestion management and, more recently, sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors, which have demonstrated particular efficacy in patients with metabolically complex HFpEF. 33 Our findings suggest that comprehensive metabolic optimization, including aggressive diabetes management, lipid control, and weight management, may represent underused therapeutic opportunities for reducing HF risk in this population. However, prospective intervention trials are needed to establish whether targeting these metabolic abnormalities improves clinical outcomes.

The inclusion of AST in our final model warrants discussion. While statistically significant, AST demonstrated a modest per‐unit effect (HR, 1.012), suggesting that it functions primarily within the context of comprehensive multivariable risk assessment rather than as a stand‐alone clinical indicator. Its retention through rigorous feature selection likely reflects unique information about cardiohepatic interactions and subclinical hepatic congestion, a pathophysiological dimension distinct from myocardial stress (NT‐proBNP) or metabolic dysfunction (glucose, TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI). In patients with combined aortic stenosis and HFpEF, elevated left atrial pressure transmits backward through the pulmonary circulation to the right heart and systemic venous system, potentially causing hepatic congestion that manifests as subtle transaminase elevations even before clinically apparent hepatic dysfunction. 34 Importantly, most patients in our cohort had AST values within or near normal reference ranges, suggesting these subtle elevations may represent early hemodynamic derangements. This finding supports comprehensive metabolic and hepatic assessment in patients with HFpEF undergoing TAVR, acknowledging the systemic nature of this syndrome. Furthermore, serial AST monitoring may be particularly valuable in identifying patients who would benefit from early intervention with guideline‐directed medical therapy optimization or closer surveillance for emerging HF symptoms. The relatively modest cost and wide availability of AST testing make it a practical biomarker for risk stratification in routine clinical practice, especially when integrated with our validated nomogram for comprehensive risk assessment.

The superior performance of our nomogram compared with generic perioperative risk scores like EuroSCORE, along with significant improvements in reclassification and integrated discrimination, provides strong evidence for disease‐specific prognostic tools that capture the unique pathophysiology of concurrent HFpEF and AS. Generic surgical risk scores, developed primarily for mortality prediction in heterogeneous cardiac surgical populations, lack the granularity to identify patients at specific risk for HF progression in this context. Our nomogram addresses this gap by incorporating metabolic phenotyping alongside traditional cardiac biomarkers.

To facilitate clinical implementation, we developed a web‐based calculator (CycleDream platform) that incorporates input validation features to prevent user errors. The calculator flags implausible values for continuous variables and provides immediate feedback on data entry errors. The CycleDream platform (https://cycledream.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/) has been developed and optimized for compatibility across major modern web browsers, including Chrome (version 90+), Firefox (version 88+), and Microsoft Edge (version 90+). The application uses responsive web design principles and is fully functional on mobile devices, including smartphones and tablets running iOS (version 13+) and Android (version 8+) operating systems. The interface automatically adapts to different screen sizes while maintaining optimal usability for data input and result interpretation, enabling point‐of‐care risk assessment in various clinical settings including bedside consultations, outpatient clinics, and mobile health care environments. During pre‐TAVR evaluation, this tool enables individualized risk estimation for postprocedural HF hospitalization, potentially identifying high‐risk patients who warrant intensified metabolic optimization, closer postdischarge monitoring, or enrollment in clinical trials evaluating emerging HFpEF therapies. The immediate availability of risk estimates facilitates structured discussions with patients and families about realistic post‐TAVR expectations. Additionally, our study demonstrates that readily available clinical and metabolic parameters can effectively stratify HF hospitalization risk following TAVR in patients with HFpEF. The dominant role of metabolic dysfunction, encompassing diabetes, insulin resistance, adiposity, and atherogenic dyslipidemia, suggests that comprehensive metabolic management represents an important but underused therapeutic target in this population. These findings support a paradigm shift from valve‐centric to patient‐centric management, recognizing HFpEF as a systemic metabolic disorder requiring multifaceted intervention beyond procedural success. However, we emphasize that this tool provides risk prediction to inform clinical judgment rather than prescriptive treatment algorithms. Therapeutic decisions must be individualized on the basis of comprehensive clinical evaluation, patient preferences, and other factors beyond the nomogram's scope.

Study Limitations

It is important to recognize several limitations when interpreting our findings. First, the retrospective multicenter design may introduce selection bias, which could limit the generalizability of our results to broader populations, especially those with different baseline characteristics or comorbidities. Second, the relatively modest sample size may limit the precision of effect estimates and ability to identify predictors with smaller effect sizes, despite rigorous internal cross‐validation and external validation demonstrating acceptable model stability. Third, future prospective studies with larger multicenter cohorts and extended enrollment periods are needed to validate our findings across diverse populations, identify additional predictive variables, and refine risk stratification thresholds with greater precision. Fourth, the dynamic nature of HFpEF phenotypes and potential changes over time in predictor relationships indicate the need for periodic model recalibration and prospective updates. Fifth, our model was limited to readily available clinical and laboratory parameters and did not incorporate advanced imaging parameters, emerging biomarkers, or genomic information, which may further enhance risk stratification and provide deeper insights into HFpEF pathophysiology. Investigating model‐guided therapeutic interventions through randomized controlled trials will be crucial to demonstrating improved patient outcomes and cost effectiveness. Sixth, our model used single baseline measurements of NT‐proBNP and other biomarkers, which cannot capture the dynamic evolution of neurohormonal activation following valve intervention or the heterogeneous patterns of biomarker trajectories across individuals. Seventh, the geographic and institutional specificity of our cohorts may limit applicability to different health care systems, practice patterns, and patient populations with varying comorbidity profiles. To establish global applicability, validation in diverse international cohorts with varying baseline characteristics, comorbidity patterns, and treatment protocols is essential. Eighth, while our Cox regression analysis reveals violations of the proportional hazards assumption, this is justified by several key factors. The time‐varying effects observed for diabetes and metabolic parameters are clinically meaningful and biologically plausible in patients with HFpEF following TAVR. Despite these violations, our Cox model demonstrated superior discriminative performance compared with machine learning algorithms and maintained strong external validation performance (concordance index, 0.796 [95% CI, 0.702–0.890]). The violations are moderate rather than extreme, and the model provides interpretable HRs essential for clinical decision making while maintaining excellent calibration and generalizability across different patient populations. Finally, while our model incorporates composite metabolic indices (TyG‐BMI, AIP‐BMI) that demonstrated superior predictive performance over individual components during rigorous feature selection, we did not formally compare model performance against a simplified model using only base metabolic components (BMI, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol as separate predictors). Such comparative analysis would help quantify the incremental clinical utility of calculating composite scores and represents an important direction for future validation studies.

Conclusions

The validated nomogram serves as an evidence‐based tool for clinicians to perform personalized risk assessments in patients with concurrent HFpEF and severe AS undergoing TAVR. Implementing systematic risk stratification can enhance clinical decision making by identifying high‐risk patients who require intensified monitoring, early intervention, and specialized HF management. The model's focus on metabolic predictors encourages a comprehensive assessment approach that goes beyond traditional cardiac evaluations to include diabetes management, lipid optimization, and monitoring of liver function.
