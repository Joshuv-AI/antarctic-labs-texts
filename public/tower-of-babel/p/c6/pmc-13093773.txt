Heavy Metals in Soils: An Investigation of the Contaminated Floodplains Around the Sinu River, Colombia
Authors: Diana Di Luccio, Aimé Lay‐Ekuakille, Sabino Maggi, Néstor Hernando Campos, Maurizio Palmisano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHeavy metal contamination in rivers mostly originates from anthropogenic sources such as industrial activities, mining, agriculture, and urban runoff. These metals accumulate in river sediments and impact living organisms and the surrounding ecosystem. Historically, some rivers in Colombia have faced issues related to heavy metal contamination, in particular from gold mining activities, which can lead to the release of heavy metals into waterways. To assess the current status of heavy metal presence in the Sinú River, one of the most important rivers in northern Colombia, we analyzed sediment samples collected in a section of the river between the towns of Santa Isabel and Montería. ICP‐MS analysis found concentrations of Hg, Cd and Ni up to five times larger than the baseline concentrations, while concentrations of Pb, Zn and Cr were consistently below the baseline. The measured concentrations varied with both geographical location and sampling depth. During the dry season heavy metals accumulate near the surface, while rains spread them below the surface and into the groundwater, and across the surrounding land. These abnormal concentrations of potentially toxic elements are probably related to the widespread use of Hg in illegal gold mining activities, which are prevalent in the region, while Cd and Ni contamination probably derives from mining, as well as from agricultural practices and industrial discharges. The results point to the necessity to continuously monitor the state of the river and develop management strategies to reduce heavy metal contamination, protecting the health of the ecosystem and of the neighboring communities.

Introduction

Heavy metal pollution is a significant environmental problem that affects all aquatic ecosystems and has serious adverse effects on human health (Briffa et al., 2020; Das et al., 2023; Shakoor et al., 2013). This type of pollution is mainly characterized by the presence of potentially toxic elements (PTEs), such as the heavy metals lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic and chromium (for simplicity and because the term is widely used in the literature, we will usually refer to them as heavy metals throughout the paper, even though this label is broader and less precise).

Heavy metals originate from either natural sources, such as soil erosion and runoff processes, or anthropogenic sources, among which are industrial or agricultural activities, urban runoff, and the exploitation of mineral resources (Ferguson, 1990; Serpaud et al., 1994). Industrial activities, such as manufacturing and processing facilities, can release heavy metals into soil and rivers through improper waste disposal or accidental spills. Intensive agricultural practices involving the use of pesticides and fertilizers further contribute to the contamination of soil and water with heavy metals (Marrugo‐Negrete et al., 2017). These heavy metals tend to accumulate in sediments, altering the natural balance of the ecosystem and posing a threat to aquatic life. Once absorbed by aquatic organisms, heavy metals can enter the food chain and bioaccumulate in tissues when uptake exceeds the rate of metabolism or excretion, posing health risks to animals and humans who consume contaminated fish (Islam et al., 2015).

Colombia, as many other countries in the world, faces problems relating to heavy metal contamination across its environments. Mining is a major source of pollution, releasing heavy metals such as mercury, cadmium and lead into the environment. For example, the gold extraction process requires large amounts of mercury for amalgamation with gold; after use, this mercury is often discharged directly into the surrounding environment, resulting in severe contamination.

In particular, the Department of Cordoba, in the Colombian Caribbean region, faces intensive mining activities that are a major source of heavy soil contamination. Currently there are 106 mining concessions for the extraction of coal, nickel, copper, silver, gold and platinum, that cover more than 124,000 ha, corresponding approximately to 5% of the area of the Department (Agencia Nacional de Minería, 2017, 2020). In addition, there are around 220 temporary mines, mainly dedicated to the extraction of high‐value metals, as well as a considerable number of illegal mines. The latter are the main source of heavy metal pollution.

The main river in the Department of Córdoba is the Sinú River, whose basin is critical to the economy and biodiversity of the region. Its alluvial plain spans an area of 13,700 km2, and due to the river's frequent flooding, the land along its course is extremely fertile. The river is an essential resource for local communities, supplying drinking water across the entire region, providing irrigation for agriculture and supporting industrial activities. The river also serves as an important transportation route for goods and people throughout the region.

The areas surrounding the Sinú River are well‐known for their abundant metal deposits, particularly gold, iron and nickel. In 2019, the extraction of these resources accounted for 5% of the country's GDP (Castaño et al., 2020). However, this activity is frequently carried out illegally, producing vast amounts of excavation waste and leading to the contamination of soil and water with high levels of nitric acid, cadmium and mercury that is discharged into the surrounding environment without any control, leading to serious environmental damage around the river.

Despite the crucial role of the Sinú River in the socio‐economic development and ecological balance of north–western Colombia, very few studies have examined the presence of heavy metals in its floodplain sediments (see Section 2).

In 2021, we carried out a thorough study to evaluate the concentration of heavy metals by sampling the soils along the riverbanks of a specific region of the Sinú River, between the towns of Santa Isabel and Montería, the latter being the capital of the Department of Córdoba and the fourth largest city in Colombia (Arenas et al., 2022). We focused on Hg, Cd, Ni, Pb, Zn, and Cr because they are recognised as PTEs of high environmental and public health concern. Previous studies in the Sinú River basin have generally examined these elements individually and without considering seasonal variability, depth profiles, or relationships with the physico‐chemical properties of the sediments. To address these limitations, we measured how the concentration of heavy metals change between the two dominant climatic phases in north–western Colombia, the rainy season and the subsequent dry season, and across two different depths.

Compared to the original work (Arenas et al., 2022), the present paper includes a more in‐depth analysis of the measurements and an expanded discussion of the results. To provide a more comprehensive geochemical characterization of the study area, we also report here the main physico‐chemical characteristics of the sediments, such as pH and electrical conductivity (EC), as well as the amount of organic matter (OM) and cation exchange capacity (CEC), because these parameters can affect the precipitation, dissolution and solubilization of heavy metals (Nuralykyzy et al., 2021).

No efforts were made to formally assess the environmental and human health implications of the results, as such an evaluation would require additional expertise and measurements. This study also complements earlier work that examined potential environmental contamination from mud volcanoes in Colombia's northern Caribbean coastal area (Di Luccio et al., 2021; Palmisano et al., 2024), providing a more comprehensive picture of environmental risks across the nation's inland and coastal ecosystems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides a brief literature overview of soil contamination by PTEs, focusing on the limited studies carried out in Colombia and, in particular, within the Sinú River basin. Section 3 describes the main characteristics of the study area. The methodology used during the in situ survey is reported in Section 4, together with details about the chemical analyses of the river sediments. The results are described and discussed in Section 5. Conclusions and future directions are reported in Section 6.

State of the Art

Soil contamination by heavy metals has become a major research focus and has received considerable attention in recent years due to its extensive environmental and health implications. The presence of heavy metals in soils can adversely affect plant growth, disrupt ecosystems and pose serious risks to human health via the food chain. Several studies have examined the sources, pathways and impacts of heavy metal contamination (Angon et al., 2024; Bian et al., 2024; He et al., 2019; Hofer et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2011; Proust et al., 2013), and emphasized the need to develop effective management and remediation techniques (Alessandrino et al., 2022; J. Chen et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024; Kravchenko et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024). For a comprehensive overview of this pressing issue, readers can refer to the recent review by Li et al. (2019), which examines the main sources and effects of heavy metals contamination in soils, together with the latest remediation techniques.

In addition to global studies, investigations of heavy metal contamination at the regional (Liu et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2017, and references therein) and local scale (Acosta et al., 2011; Anda et al., 2022; Bhuyan et al., 2017; Boszke et al., 2008; Gimeno‐García et al., 1996b; Giouri et al., 2010; Gosar et al., 1997; Hahladakis et al., 2013; Jaskuła & Sojka, 2022) can be especially significant, as they can inform policymakers to develop effective strategies for pollution management and remediation, thereby supporting sustainable environmental policies and practices. Due to its explosive economic growth, China is one of the most extensively studied regions globally regarding heavy metal contamination and its associated impacts (H. Wang, Wang, et al., 2015; J. Wang, Liu, et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2020; Xiao et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022; Adnan et al., 2024).

In the context of Colombia and in particular the area surrounding the Sinú River, several studies have examined heavy metal contamination in river sediments and surface soils along its banks, drawing attention to the presence of anomalous concentrations of potentially toxic metals. Feria et al. (2010) found no significant variations in metal concentrations across different seasons of the year. However, they observed differences based on the location of the sampling sites, likely attributable to the use of fertilizers in agricultural practices as well as to erosion processes occurring along the riverbanks.

Marrugo‐Negrete et al. (2017) suggested that soil contamination was primarily linked to agricultural practices, with the exception of mercury, which was likely originated from atmospheric deposition and river flow transport associated with upstream gold mining. Furthermore, the elevated concentrations of nickel suggested a pollution source related to both agricultural activities and ferronickel mining.

More recently (Lans‐Ceballos et al., 2023), re‐evaluated the water quality of the Sinú River, revealing the presence of heavy metal contamination. However, despite the observed toxicity levels, as indicated by the Water Quality Index which showed the lowest quality levels during the rainy season, their findings did not indicate a significant hazard to human health.

Study Area

The Department of Córdoba is located in the northwestern part of Colombia and is traversed from south to north by the Sinú River (Rio Sinú), which which serves as one of the primary sources of fresh water for the department (Figure 1). The Sinú River originates in the northern part of the Andes mountain range in the Antioquia Department and flows for about 400 km before reaching the Caribbean Sea.

Study area and location of the sampling sites: (a) map of Colombia and the Caribbean Region, (b) sampling sites along the riverbanks of the Sinú River.

The river is subject to intense fluvial dynamics that, together with intense rainfall that often increases the river flow above 400 m3/s, enhances erosion processes and produce displacement of huge sediment volumes (Corporación Autónoma regional de los Valles del Sinú y del San Jorge, 2024). In the middle Sinú region, where our study area is located, the erosion rate is close to 90%, and reaches nearly 100% in many deforested areas scattered across the region, as reported by Colombia's National Environmental Licensing Authority (Castaño et al., 2020).

The high fluvial dynamics favors the predominance of coarser sandy material, while finer particles, that have the greatest capacity to retain heavy metals, are washed away more easily, leading to periodic mobilization of heavy metal contaminants within the river system. Periodic flooding of the Sinú River inundates the surrounding land and releases substantial amounts of heavy metal‐rich sediment into the soil, potentially impacting agricultural land, groundwater quality, and local ecosystems.

The rainfall regime in the Department of Córdoba is basically characterized by the alternation of two seasons, a rainy season from April to September and a dry season from December to March. Annual rainfall varies significantly across the region, ranging from about 1,500 mm per year in the coastal area to over 4,000 mm per year in the southern mountainous region. The average number of rainy days per year ranges between 50 and 100, but in the southern part of the Department it is possible to have up to 200 rainy days per year.

This work focuses on the central region of the Sinú River (Figure 1b), located south of Montería, the capital of Colombia's Department of Córdoba. Montería, with a population of around half a million, ranks as the fourth‐largest city in the country. The importance of the area is further amplified by the presence of extensive nearby metal mining operations. Despite this, there is no water treatment plant and the river water, which is used for both local water supply and irrigation throughout the region, undergoes no purification treatment.

The convergence of rapid urban development and intensive mining activities highlights the urgent need for a thorough evaluation of their potential environmental impacts on the ecosystem and natural resources of the region.

Materials and Methods

Two sets of sediment samples were collected during the rainy season of 2021 along the riverbanks of the Sinú River, at 25 different sampling sites between the towns of Santa Isabel and Montería (Figure 1b). The first set consisted of samples collected at the surface, approximately ∼0.1 m below the ground level, while the other set comprised samples collected at a depth of 0.5 m. The sample collection was repeated in the same conditions during the dry season of the same year. In the rest of the paper we will use the terms “surface” or “deep” samples to refer to these two different sets.

Sampling a few centimeters below the soil surface is standard practice to minimize the inclusion of surface debris that may be affected by atmospheric contamination and surface runoff, while the samples collected at 0.5 m help to evaluate the mobility of the PTEs, providing insights into possible effects on groundwater quality and plant growth. To avoid metal contamination, no metallic tools were used during sampling, storage, and transport (Figure 2). However, due to the nature of the soil, this requirement prevented the collection and analysis of samples from deeper soil layers.

Soil sampling along the Sinú River floodplain during the rainy season. To prevent metal contamination, no metal tools were used during sampling, storage and transport.

The spatial distance between two consecutive sampling sites was approximately 0.5 km, resulting in a total surveyed length of about 12 km. The sampling distance represents a compromise between covering a wide geographical area and keeping a manageable number of sampling sites.

The sampling strategy was designed to maximize data collection within a reasonable timeframe, while ensuring thorough coverage of the study area and addressing some challenging logistical and security constraints, such as the need to access sites on foot while carrying all necessary heavy equipment through difficult and often muddy terrain and limiting the duration of stays at each location. These constraints mandated that each sample be collected from a single point rather than from multiple locations within a site, which would have provided a more representative characterization of the surrounding environment.

Samples were immediately sealed in polyethylene bags, stored in a refrigerated container at 5°C and transported to the laboratory for analysis. There, the soil samples were air‐dried at room temperature, gently disaggregated by hand without grinding to preserve their natural aggregates and avoid crosss‐contamination, and sieved through a 2 mm nylon mesh to remove coarse debris. Each sample was then homogenized and sieved using a polyethylene sieve with a mesh size of 60 μm. Reducing and sorting the sediments to this grain size is critical, because heavy metals are concentrated in the silty clay fraction.

After sieving, each sample was divided into two subsamples, the first subsample was used to measure the concentration of mercury, while the second subsample was used to determine the concentration of all other heavy metals. Due to the high volatility of mercury, the subsamples used for the determination its concentration were dried at room temperature by placing them under a fume hood for 5 days (Ackermann, 1980), while the remaining subsamples were heated in an oven at 75°C for 4 days.

A selective method using acid attack by dissolution with aqua regia was used for the determination of the concentration of the remaining heavy metals (Hass & Fine, 2010). The acid attack was prolonged until the solution changed color. The observed color change was likely related to the dissolution of OM, although minor dissolution of mineral phases cannot be excluded. The samples were then heated until total dehydration, allowed to cool to room temperature and washed with distilled water.

Afterward, in accordance with the EPA protocol, 5 ml of pre‐heated HCl, kept just below its boiling point, was added to each sample, prior to filtration and cooling (Edgell, 1989; USEPA, 1996).

The solid residues were carefully rinsed with distilled water and the resulting washes were combined with the main extract prior to analysis to ensure full recovery of the analytes. The resulting solutions were analyzed by using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (PerkinElmer NexION 5000 Multi‐Quadrupole ICP Mass Spectrometer).

For the physico‐chemical characterization, pH and EC were measured with a multi‐parameter probe (Hanna HI9829), while OM was determined according to the Walkley‐Black method, using a conversion factor of %C = 1.72. Cation exchange capacity was determined through extraction with ammonium acetate (Chapman, 1965). Lastly, a laser granulometer (Malvern Mastersizer Hydro 2,000 MU) was used to determine the grain size of the sediments.

Most distributions are unimodal, featuring well distinct single peaks, with only a few instances of bimodal distributions, for example, Cd in Figure 4 or Hg, Cd and, to a lesser extent Zn, in Figure 5. In Figure 4 the box‐plots for the two sampling depths are generally well separated, with clearly different median values for most metals. In contrast, the box‐plots in Figure 5 exhibit a significant overlap, indicating that seasonal differences are not readily apparent when the measurements from the two depths are combined.

However, when the surface and depth measurements are analyzed separately, the seasonal differences between the dry and rainy periods become far more evident (Figures S1–S2 in Supporting Information S1). This is supported by paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests comparing concentrations measured at the same depth across the two seasons. All of these tests consistently produce very small p‐values, on the order of 10−4−10−5, for all metals except “deep” Ni (p‐value ∼0.7), indicating significant changes in concentration between seasons.

No clear relationships between pair of metals are apparent from the scatterplots of Figures 4 and 5. The sole exception is Zn, which shows a visually clear relation with Cr, supported by a significant correlation coefficient (p‐value <0.001) for the “surface” samples (Figure 4) as well as for the samples collected in the two seasonal conditions (Figure 5).

The weak correlation found among the concentrations of different heavy metals is further supported by a more detailed correlation analysis using Pearson's method (Figures S3–S5 in Supporting Information S1).

When considering all measurements, correlation between measured concentrations of heavy metals is weak, with the exception of Zn which, as noted above, shows a strong correlation with Cr, suggesting a potential shared source for these two elements in the analyzed samples (Figure S3 in Supporting Information S1). A moderate correlation between Zn and Ni is also found, as well as between Cr and Ni. In these two cases, the degree of correlation remains largely unchanged when considering only sample collected from the surface (Figure S4 in Supporting Information S1), while it appears significantly weaker for samples collected 0.5 m below the surface (Figure S5 in Supporting Information S1).

Evaluation of Concentrations Relative to the Baseline Values

Absolute concentration values alone provide limited information regarding the safety of heavy metal content in the soil for humans and the environment. To obtain a clearer understanding of the contamination levels in the sampled soils, we normalized the concentration data against IAEA‐405 baseline concentration for each metal. The distribution of the normalized concentrations in the rainy and dry seasons at the two sampling levels is shown in Figure 6, while the same data for each site are plotted in the heatmap of Figure 7, where the green color indicates a concentration below the baseline, while progressively darker shades of orange and red indicate increasingly concerning concentration levels.

Heatmap showing the concentration of heavy metals at each site, differentiating between the measurements taken from samples collected at the two sampling levels and in the dry and rainy seasons. The measurements are normalized to the IAEA‐405 baseline concentrations (Wyse et al., 2004).

Overall, the measured concentrations of Pb, Zn, and Cr are consistently well within the baseline concentration at all sampled sites, whereas those of Hg, Cd, and Ni can significantly exceed the baseline (Figures 6 and 7). Specifically, the measured Cd and Ni concentrations can be up to 2.5−3.0 times the baseline, while Hg is of particular concern since it can reach levels close to 5 times the baseline at most sampled sites, in particular during the dry season (Figure 7).

The statistical comparison of surface samples versus samples taken at a depth of 0.5 m revealed element‐specific concentration patterns (Figure 6). Paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests (one‐tailed, α=0.05), indicated that Ni, Cr, and Zn concentrations were significantly higher in surface samples than in deep samples during both the dry and rainy seasons (p<0.01 for all six comparisons). In contrast, Hg and Pb show no significant surface versus deep differences in either season (p>0.1), while Cd is significantly higher in surface sediments during the rainy season at the conventional 5% level (p=0.03), while in the dry season the difference is significant only at the 10% level (p=0.07).

Overall, these differences are stronger in the dry season, probably because the minimum amount of precipitation during the dry season reduces surface runoff and produces heavy metal accumulation zones that are not leached by rainfall and thus tend to concentrate at the surface (Giridharan et al., 2008). This causes an accumulation of heavy metals in areas characterized by low kinetic energy of the river, where the action of deposition of sediments prevails over erosion. During the heavy rainfall season typical of the area, soil pollutants are dispersed by surface runoff into the floodplains of the Sinú River may promote downward migration of metals into deeper soil layers, potentially influencing shallow groundwater and causing further widespread harm to the surrounding ecosystem.

Table 2 shows the measured physico‐chemical parameters of the sediments collected during the dry and wet seasons, which show marked differences across all sites and measured parameters. As the variation in the values of pH and EC (and consequently of OM and CEC) between surface and deep samples was negligible, we reported only the averaged values for simplicity.

The pH can influence the solubility of the metal species; in general, a lower pH increases the solubility of the metals, thus increasing their mobility (B. Alloway, 1995; B. J. In Alloway, 2013). The measured pH was systematically higher (i.e., more alkaline) in the rainy season, between 6.9 and 8.3, decreasing to values between 5.6 and 6.9 (i.e., more acidic), in the dry season (Table 2).

The analyzed elements include both cationic species (e.g., Hg, Cd, Ni, Zn, Pb) as well as an oxyanion (Cr). Cationic metals are typically more mobile under acidic conditions, whereas oxyanions may become more soluble at circumneutral to alkaline pH. This contrasting behavior is consistent with our results, which show no clear relationship between pH and heavy‐metal concentrations, even when stratifying the data by depth or season. The rather wide variability of the measured pH is representative of a composite soil, where conditions of low infiltration and percolation of water alternate with alternating levels of phosphorus and free carbonates (Ryan et al., 2001).

Organic matter levels are moderate, ranging from 5.8 to 6.7% during the rainy season and increasing to 7.2 to 9.8% in the dry season. This difference is likely due to the greater accumulation of biomass, such as fallen leaves and animal matter, on the soil during the dry season. Although OM can influence trace metal dynamics through complexation processes, no consistent relationship was found between OM levels and heavy metal concentration. Organic matter content showed only a weak correlation with heavy‐metal concentrations, even after partitioning data by depth or season (with absolute values of the correlation coefficient r∼0.2−0.3, at the p<0.05 level).

Higher EC values were also measured during the dry season, when the leaching effect of rainfall is absent. Ranging between 2.7 and 3.8 mS/cm, these values indicate a moderate degree of salinization, which is probably due to the intensive use of fertilisers (Hu & Schmidhalter, 2005). Lastly, the CEC measured in our sediments is generally high, with values ranging from 32 to 68 meq/100 g where, as for OM and EC, CEC values are lower in the rainy season and increase in the dry season. Also EC and CEC did not exhibit a statistically significant correlation with measured concentrations, even when data were partitioned by depth or season.

Another key factor impacting the mobility and bioaccumulation of heavy metals is sediment texture. Mobilized metals in the environment can adsorb onto sediment particles, and this adsorption capacity is closely related to grain size. Finer sediments, with smaller grain sizes, typically have a higher adsorption capacity, allowing them to retain larger quantities of metals under the appropriate environmental conditions (De Vivo et al., 2004; Liang et al., 2019). Adsorption is also influenced by the chemical properties of the sorbent, which determine its affinity for particular metal species.

Heavy metals exhibit a wide range of aqueous chemistries. Some, such as Cd2+ and Pb2+, are present mainly as free cations, whereas others exist chiefly as oxyanions (e.g., Cr, which forms the chromate oxyanion CrO42− or, under more oxidising conditions, the dichromate ion Cr2O72−). These different speciation patterns lead to markedly different behaviors in terms of transport and bioaccumulation potential. Once mobilized in anions, cations, and other ionic complexes, these elements tend to be adsorbed and incorporated into inorganic and organic solid phases. Clay minerals are important sorbents for heavy metals, but organic phases such as humic substances and dissolved OM also play a crucial role in sorption and complexation processes.

A Pearson correlation analysis showed a moderate, statistically significant relationship between grain size and the concentrations of Cr, Ni, and Zn (r∼±0.5,p=0.001), while the concentrations of Cd, Hg and Pb did not show any meaningful correlation with grain size. Because the data do not necessarily follow a normal distribution, we also performed a Spearman rank‐correlation analysis, which produced very similar results.

Another key parameter is water temperature, measured in situ with a multiparameter probe. During the course of the measurements, the recorded water temperature varied within a relatively narrow range, between 26°C and 30°C depending on the season, but did not display marked spatial differences. It can be estimated that this range of temperatures affected the solubility of metals by up to 10% (Van der Sloot et al., 1991). However, no clear relationship was observed between temperature and the concentration of heavy metals. This suggests that, under the studied conditions, the effect of temperature on heavy metal mobility could be considered negligible.

Overall, none of the measured physico‐chemical properties (pH, EC, OM, CEC, water temperature) nor the textural components (clay, silt, sand) showed a statistically significant association with the concentrations of any of the measured heavy metals. The only identifiable pattern is an inverse seasonal relationship between pH and the other three variables, OM, EC and CEC, as illustrated (Figure S6 in Supporting Information S1).

This lack of detectable correlations does not mean that these factors are irrelevant; rather, it probably reflects the fact that the spatial pattern of metal concentrations is dominated by the heterogeneous distribution of mining and industrial sources than by soil chemistry, a factor not captured by our measurements. Moreover, sampling at only two depths and in two seasons may miss transient processes (e.g., episodic leaching, seasonal redox fluctuations) that influence metal speciation. Sampling a single point per site also increases the risk that the chosen location is not fully representative of the surrounding environment. As explained in Section 4, this compromise was unavoidable given the challenging local logistical and security constraints.

Nevertheless, the results confirm that the study area exhibits spatial‐ and seasonal‐dependent metal variability, even if the physico‐chemical controlling mechanisms remain still unresolved.

Possible Sources of Heavy Metal Contamination

Analyzing the aggregated concentration data in more detail (Table S2 in Supporting Information S1), we note that the concentration of nickel ranges from a minimum of 21.5 mg/kg to a maximum of 72.5 mg/kg, with an average value of 48.2 mg/kg in sediments collected from the surface during the dry season and 41.7 mg/kg in sediments collected from the surface during the rainy season, while for the sediments taken at 0.5 m depth, the average concentration of Ni was 34.3 mg/kg in the dry season and 35.2 mg/kg in those collected during the rainy season. The largest Ni concentrations are found in the surface sediments, with a significantly large variation between the two seasonal periods (p<0.01). At 0.5 m below the surface the concentration of Ni is lower, with no statistically significant variability between the two seasons. In all cases, most measured Ni concentrations are above the limit for uncontaminated sediments, that for Ni is 32.5 mg/kg (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1).

The high concentration of Ni in these soils could be related to the presence, a few tens of kilometers from the sampling area, of the Cerro Matoso mine, which is the largest iron and nickel mine in Latin America, as well as of smaller unregulated mining plants scattered around the sampling area. An additional possible source of Ni pollution could be related to the presence of extensive farming areas, where the use of large amounts of ultra‐low‐cost pesticides and fertilizers based on copper and iron sulfates, which contain relatively large amounts of heavy metals among which Ni, leads to a further increase of Ni concentration in the sampled soils (Gimeno‐García et al., 1996a). Irrigating crops with water from the Sinú River can also contribute to Ni pollution, as the river receives significant amounts of polluted water from its tributaries along its course (Kim et al., 2014).

It is worth noting that elevated levels of Ni in the environment pose significant health risks; the accumulation of Ni and its compounds in the human body can be responsible for various diseases, such as pulmonary fibrosis, cancer, lung and cardiovascular diseases (Seilkop & Oller, 2003).

As for Cd, the highest cadmium content is 1.94 mg/kg, well above the allowed limit of 0.73 mg/kg, and is found in the samples collected at the surface in the dry season, while the lowest concentration of 0.35 mg/kg is recorded in the samples collected in rainy season 0.5 m below the surface. The average concentration of Cd is either 0.87 mg/kg or 1.12 mg/kg for the surface samples in the rainy and dry seasons, respectively, while for samples taken at 0.5 m, it is 0.73 mg/kg or 0.96 mg/kg in the rainy and dry seasons, respectively. Most measured values are two or three times larger than the allowed values (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1).

Such high concentrations of Cd may arise from multiple sources, such as mining, atmospheric deposition, use of phosphate fertilizers and residual polluted sludge, or polluted groundwater or river water (Kim et al., 2014). In aquatic systems under oxic to suboxic conditions, Cd occurs as Cd2 and forms chloro‐, carbonato‐ and organic complexes, which confer relatively high mobility. Under reducing, sulfidic conditions, Cd precipitates as cadmium sulfide. This precipitation markedly decreases the dissolved cadmium concentration, causing the metal to become immobilized and trapped within river sediments. Remobilization can occur upon oxidation/acidification or through complexation (Rodríguez et al., 2009).

High concentrations of Cd dispersed in the environment are dangerous to organisms, and in humans they can produce high respiratory damage, while intake of Cd‐contaminated water and food induces severe gastrointestinal effects (Järup et al., 1998; Nordberg, 2004).

The concentration of mercury in the sampled sediments ranged from 0.47 mg/kg in the rainy season to 3.82 mg/kg in the dry season. At a depth of 0.5 m, Hg concentration ranged from 0.47 mg/kg to 3.9 mg/kg, where again lower concentrations of Hg were observed in the rainy season and higher concentrations in the dry season. More specifically, Hg concentration at the surface ranged from 0.47 mg/kg to 3.12 mg/kg in the rainy season and from 0.93 to 3.82 mg/kg in the dry season, with an average of 1.99 and 2.59 mg/kg in the rainy and dry seasons, respectively. Measurements at a depth of 0.5 m below the surface show a very similar variability as they range from 0.47 mg/kg to 3.04 mg/kg in the rainy season and from 1.22 to 3.90 mg/kg in the dry season, with averages of 1.75 and 2.58 mg/kg, respectively. These fluctuations could be attributed to preferential deposition phenomena due to runoff and surface leaching processes by the frequent and intense rainfall.

In all cases, the measured Hg concentrations are up to 5 times higher than the IAEA baseline of 0.81 mg/kg (Wyse et al., 2004), as well as well above the limits stated by WHO. The presence of such high concentrations of Hg is probably due to an anthropogenic contribution. In fact, the exponential growth of alluvial gold mining operations in the surrounding area, many of which are illegal, has had an extremely high environmental impact over time due to the indiscriminate use of high levels of Hg in the process of gold amalgamation (Pinedo‐Hernández et al., 2015).

Mercury contamination is one of the most significant environmental issues due to its high toxicity and the ability of living organisms to accumulate this metal in their tissues over time. This process, known as biomagnification, involves the entire trophic chain, ultimately reaching humans (Córdoba‐Tovar et al., 2022). In the environment, Hg is transformed into methylmercury, a highly toxic organic compound that is absorbed much more rapidly by organisms. Hg contamination is responsible for serious diseases at the level of the renal and neurological systems. Chronic exposure to even low concentrations of Hg is responsible for toxic effects on the reproductive system, affecting growth, normal development and learning ability in both animals and humans (Boerleider et al., 2017).

The other heavy metals found in the sediments studied, namely Cr, Pb, and Zn, always show concentration values well below the permissible limits (Wyse et al., 2004). Their presence is attributable to the numerous livestock farms and intensive crops present in the study area, all activities conducted with an high use of pesticides and fertilizers characterized by high contents of Pb and Zn.

Conclusions

Heavy metals are dangerous for humans and the environment as these elements have a high chemical stability with respect to biodegradation processes. Small concentrations of heavy metals are naturally present in the components of the Earth's crust, in soils and in plants. However, natural processes such as leaching and soil erosion, combined with human activities, can significantly influence the presence and distribution of these elements. Some of these elements, notably Zn and Cu, are indispensable micronutrients in trace amounts, others such as Pb and Cd have no known physiological role and are toxic even at low concentrations.

This study examined six heavy metals present in the floodplain sediments of the Sinú River, between the towns of Montería and Santa Isabel in the Department of Cordóba, Colombia. The results of the analysis show an anomalous concentration of Ni, Cd and above all Hg both in surface and deep samples, while the concentration of Pb, Zn and Cr are always well below baseline. Surface samples show higher concentrations of metals than samples taken at a depth of 0.5 m, particularly during the dry season. This might be due to the minimal amount of precipitation during this season, which reduces runoff and creates zones of heavy metal accumulation at the soil surface.

The main limitation of this work is that each site was represented by a single sampling point and only two depths (surface and 0.5 m) were examined in the dry and rainy seasons. The sampling scheme was designed to maximize data collection within a realistic time frame and to achieve broad spatial coverage while also addressing the challenging local logistical and security constraints. Consequently, it may have missed fine‐scale spatial heterogeneity and transient processes (e.g., episodic leaching, redox spikes) that could affect metal speciation and mobility. Nevertheless, despite these limitations, the work provides a detailed characterization of heavy‐metal contamination in an area of the Sinú River that has not been adequately studied until now.

The lack of a clear relationship between metal concentrations and measured physicochemical parameters (pH, EC, OM, CEC, grain‐size fractions) indicates that source heterogeneity, rather than soil chemistry, dominates the observed concentration patterns. Seasonal variations are driven principally by hydrological factors (e.g., runoff, dilution) rather than by changes in pH or redox conditions.

Overall, the anomalous presence of Ni in the study area could be linked to the release from natural sources and intensive agricultural practices, while the high levels of Cd and Hg are probably associated with anthropogenic activities, in particular mining operations in the regions adjacent to the sampling area. In these areas, illegal mining activities generate significant amounts of excavation waste that is left unprotected and stored. During the heavy rains typical of the region, these residues are washed away and accumulate in the floodplains of the Sinú River. Also, the process of extracting gold from alluvial sediments involves roasting them to separate their valuable contents. This process takes place in the open air, resulting in the dispersal of toxic fumes that fall to the ground with rain, depositing large amounts of Hg in the sediments.

The documented contamination of the riverbanks of the Sinú river, together with the identified anthropogenic sources, underscores the need to implement a systematic, long‐term monitoring activity aimed at reducing contaminant inputs and protecting the environmental and human health of the Sinú River basin. Future work should increase the spatial density of the sampled sites and incorporate multiple points per site, extend depth profiling to capture deeper sediment horizons, integrate source‐tracking techniques to discriminate mining‐versus agricultural contributions, and evaluate remediation options tailored to the dominant contaminants. Implementing these measures would provide the quantitative basis needed to protect the river ecosystem and the health of the surrounding communities.

It is also crucial to raise public awareness through campaigns that educate local communities about the risks and encourage safe practices, especially regarding the consumption of potentially contaminated water or fish.

Addressing heavy metal contamination requires a long‐term, coordinated effort involving government agencies, industry, and local communities. This collaboration is essential to ensure the protection of the environment and public health through sustainable practices, effective regulations, and community engagement.
