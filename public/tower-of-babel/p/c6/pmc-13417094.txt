Magnetic fingerprint of naphthalenediimide-based radical crystals
Authors: Xiandong He, Qi Chen, Shaohua Tong, Hanlin Gan, Qinglin Jiang, Yuguang Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While π-conjugated radicals are essential for developing high-temperature organic ferromagnets, the interplay between their spin density distribution, packing modes, and resulting magnetic order remains poorly understood. This study focuses on planar conjugated naphthalene diimide (NDI) anion radical systems characterized by robust π–π interactions. By synergistically modulating NDI substituents and reductive crystallization conditions, we successfully constructed a series of single-crystals exhibiting distinct aggregation modes. Utilizing a combination of electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry, and DFT calculations, we carried out a deep analysis of how geometric parameters of molecular packing—including slip displacement, interplanar spacing, and twist angles—modulate the magnetic exchange coupling constant (J), and constructed a structure–magnetism correlation map. These crystals exhibit pronounced Mott insulator characteristics and low-dimensional localized magnetism, with magnetic coupling strengths governed by orbital phase overlap integrals, demonstrating high tunability ranging from −397 cm−1 to −69 cm−1. Crucially, our theoretical investigations identify an “accidental orbital orthogonality” phenomenon induced by specific slippage along the short axis, which effectively quenches the kinetic antiferromagnetic term and reveals potential ferromagnetic fingerprint regions. This work establishes clear physical models and molecular precursors for the rational design of functional materials with ferromagnetic coupling through precision crystal engineering.

Introduction

Organic magnets, which utilize unpaired electrons in p orbitals to generate magnetism, represent a field of profound academic significance with broad potential for applications complementary to inorganic magnets.1,2 Through the precise modulation of molecular electronic and packing structures, ferromagnetism has been successively observed in a variety of organic radical crystals, including nitroxide,3,4 Blatter,5,6 verdazyl,7,8 dithiadiazolyl,9,10 dithiazolyl11–13 and pyrazine-fused furazan14 radicals. As the field evolved from localized radicals stabilized by sterically hindered groups to highly planar, conjugated delocalized systems, the driving forces for molecular assembly have transitioned from weak interactions—such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic forces, and van der Waals interactions15,16—to robust π–π stacking and inter-radical pancake bonding.17,18 Consequently, the spin exchange mechanism has shifted from weak magnetic dipole–dipole interactions to direct SOMO–SOMO orbital overlap, leading to orders-of-magnitude increases in coupling strength and a corresponding rise in magnetic ordering temperatures from 0.65 K to 35.5 K.19

Against this backdrop, the construction of planar, large π-conjugated radical crystals is considered a pivotal structural strategy for achieving high-transition-temperature organic magnets.20,21 Recently, our group observed room-temperature ferromagnetism in microcrystalline powders of perylene diimide (PDI)22 and NDI anion radicals23 reduced by hydrazine hydrate, and further developed ferromagnetic thin films exhibiting enhanced saturation magnetization through optimizing molecular assembly.24 Although X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) has confirmed that the magnetism originates from unpaired π-electrons of radicals,25,26 the lack of precise crystallographic and compositional information—both of which are known to be critical determinants of magnetic properties27—has hindered further understanding of their magnetic behavior. Thus, a comprehensive elucidation of the structure–property relationships in imide-based systems is essential for achieving a breakthrough in room-temperature ferromagnetism.

It should be emphasized that the exceptionally strong magnetic interactions in these systems do not simply arise from exchange within isolated molecular dimers or limited nearest-neighbor clusters.28 Instead, they are mediated by the partial delocalization of radical electrons along the π-stacking direction, which facilitates the long-range transmission of magnetic exchange.29 This mechanism fundamentally represents a collective magnetic behavior of strongly correlated electrons, where the physical description aligns more closely with the band–spin coupling models of low-dimensional correlated systems than with conventional localized molecular magnetism.30 While traditional charge-transfer complexes, such as those based on TTF radicals, have established rich magnetic phase diagrams through diverse crystal and electronic structures, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports of π-radical-based ferromagnetism in those systems.31,32 Driven by this understanding, we aim to further elucidate the intrinsic physical correlation between crystal structure, the degree of electronic delocalization, and macroscopic magnetic order in fused-ring imide radical systems, providing generalizable physical principles for the rational design of high-temperature organic magnets.

In this work, we selected the highly planar NDI molecule as a model system. By systematically tuning the imide-position substituents, reducing agents, and crystallization conditions, we successfully constructed a series of stable, prominently π-stacked anion radical single crystals. Due to subtle variations in molecular configurations and intermolecular interactions, these radicals exhibit diverse aggregation and stacking modes. The relative geometric parameters of adjacent molecules (such as horizontal slip displacement, interplanar spacing, and twist angles) evolve systematically across the different crystals, providing an ideal platform to explore the internal relationships between crystal structure, electronic structure, and magnetic interactions. Utilizing electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) and superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry, we performed a detailed characterization of the microscopic spin states and macroscopic magnetic order in these crystals. Combined with theoretical calculations, we constructed a comprehensive structure–property correlation map for NDI-based radicals. Our experimental and theoretical results not only rationalize the modulation of magnetic coupling strength by structural parameters but also reveal potential structural regimes for ferromagnetic coupling. This provides essential experimental evidence and theoretical criteria for surmounting the limitations of conventional localized exchange models and achieving the rational design of high-temperature organic magnets based on delocalized radicals.

Results and discussion

Synthesis

In a glovebox, neutral NDI was mixed and heated with an equivalent amount of reducing agent under stirring to obtain the corresponding anion radical solution. The anionic crystals were precipitated and isolated via diffusion of a poor solvent or slow evaporation. A total of 9 anionic crystals are reported in this work, with their identifiers shown in Scheme 1a; detailed synthetic procedures are provided in the SI. These crystals are thermodynamically stable under an inert atmosphere but unstable under ambient conditions due to air oxidation. For the NDI molecules constituting these crystals, the primary structural distinction lies in the substituents at the imide positions. However, since the solution absorption spectra (Fig. S1), electrochemical behavior (Fig. S2 and Table S1), and EPR hyperfine coupling patterns (Fig. S3, S4 and Table S2) indicate a high degree of similarity in their monomeric electronic structures, our investigation focuses predominantly on their divergent arrangements within the crystalline state.

Crystal structure

The structures of the obtained crystals were characterized by single crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD). The corresponding crystallographic data are provided in Tables S8–S10 in the SI. Two reductants—cobaltocene (CoCp2) and tetrakis(dimethylamino)ethylene (TDAE)—were employed in this study. Influenced by the modulation of counterions and substituents, along with the π–π interactions between NDI anions, different crystalline aggregation forms were identified, namely finite multimers (dimers) or infinite one-dimensional chains (π-stacks). Analysis of the structural distortions in the CoCp2 (Table S3) and TDAE (Table S4) molecules confirms that their unpaired electrons have been almost entirely transferred to the NDI moieties. Consequently, we focus on the characteristic integration of the NDI molecular packing structure in this section. The magnetic coupling is primarily related to the relative positions and orientations of the radical molecules. Here, we define the relevant geometric variables in Scheme 1b: the interplanar spacing dz, the transverse slip distance dx along the molecular long axis, the longitudinal slip distance dy along the molecular short axis, and the rotation angle θ. These geometric variables hold universal value for establishing magneto-structural correlations in radical systems.33

Multimer

In contrast to the previously used decamethylcobaltocene with its large steric bulk,34 the reductants employed in this work are relatively small in size. Their limited volume prevents them from dominating the lattice packing. Instead, the π–π interactions between the NDI radicals drive the initial face-to-face assembly. However, constrained by their own molecular structures and competition of other intermolecular interactions, the stacking pattern in some systems extends along other directions, thereby terminating the infinite propagation of π-stacking and leading to the formation of finite aggregates. Their crystal structures are shown in Fig. 1.

The crystal structure of 4a has been reported in our earlier studies in detail. Briefly, the radicals exist as isolated dimers. The small steric hindrance of the amino substituent allows the molecules to adopt an eclipsed geometry with maximized overlap, providing favorable conditions for strong SOMO–SOMO interactions. This is further reinforced by hydrogen-bonding interactions between the amino group and carbonyl groups, which cooperatively distort the molecular plane and confine the π–π interactions within the dimeric unit. The corresponding geometric parameters are dx = 0.5 Å, dz = 3.204 Å, dy = 0, and θ = 0°. Similar to 4a, the substituent in 5a is a hydrogen atom. The small steric hindrance favors the formation of a slipped stacking geometry. However, along the molecular long axis, hydrogen atoms interact with carbonyl groups on adjacent molecules to form a hydrogen-bonding network, promoting the formation of a one-dimensional zigzag chain along the c-axis. This lateral interaction suppresses the intra-dimer interaction, making it weaker than that in 4a. The corresponding geometric parameters are dx = 0.5 Å, dz = 3.28 Å, dy = 0, and θ = 0° (Fig. 1b).

The formation of crystal 3b belongs to a special case within the family of aromatic cyclic substituents (detailed discussion follows). As shown in Fig. 1c, face-to-face stacking of NDI molecules assembles only up to a pentamer in this crystal, which is then interrupted by TDAE cations. The pentamers are alternately arranged perpendicular to the oab plane and parallel to the c-axis, while TDAE cations are located within the four planes of oac and obc, two in each. The stoichiometric ratio of NDI to TDAE in the crystal is 5 : 2. Considering that TDAE is oxidized to its dicationic state (see Fig. S4 and Table S4), this corresponds to five NDI molecules sharing four electrons. Within the repeating pentameric unit, three distinct configuration types of NDI molecules are present. Based on variations of carbonyl group and the conjugated backbone bond lengths, two are inferred to be in the charged anionic state, while the third is in the neutral state.35 Both anionic NDIs are positioned on either side of the neutral NDI molecule. In other words, the pentamer contains two radical dimeric pairs separated by a neutral NDI molecule, a feature reflected in its spin properties. The corresponding geometric parameters are dx = 0, dy = 0, dz = 3.273 Å, and θ = 60.81°.

One-dimensional chain

When the substituent at the imide position is a pyridine ring, benzene ring, or p-aminophenyl ring, steric competition arises between the ortho-hydrogen atoms of the aromatic ring and the carbonyl oxygen atoms. To minimize van der Waals repulsion, the substituent ring rotates around the N–C bond, adopting a nearly orthogonal orientation relative to the NDI core. This conformation also facilitates intermolecular interactions along other dimensions. In this geometry, direct face-to-face overlap between NDI molecules is sterically prohibited, necessitating either longitudinal slip or rotation to avoid a steric clash (rotation is commonly observed in the practical crystals). This allows adjacent molecules to maintain π–π interactions without inducing excessive molecular distortion. Effective π-orbital overlap can occur both above and below the molecular plane, thereby preserving translational symmetry and enabling the formation of infinitely extended one-dimensional chains.

The radicals exhibit a strong assembly drive along the direction perpendicular to the molecular plane (all related single crystals are needle-shaped). In the plane-parallel dimension, however, assembly relies primarily on alternately weak ion–pair interactions between donors and acceptors, with C–H⋯π interactions between aromatic substituents and adjacent NDI cores. As a result, each NDI column lacks direct orbital overlap and remains magnetically isolated. Furthermore, similar driving forces lead to a common structural motif in these crystals: they can be described as two interpenetrating sublattices—one consisting of NDI chains and the other of counterion clusters. The two sublattices nest within each other, with counterions occupying the cavities at the center of every four NDI columns, and the NDI columns residing in the cavities formed by every four counterion clusters.

While the above features represent common structural themes in this series, the specific combinations of substituents and reductants yield variations in crystal composition, crystal system, and symmetry. Therefore, the following discussion will focus on introducing and comparing individual crystals from the perspectives of relative NDI molecule positions and their electronic states.

The charge-transfer crystals obtained using cobaltocene as the reductant are discussed first. For 1a (Fig. 2a), there are two distinct NDI configurations within the repeating unit: one with D2 symmetry and the other reduced to C2h symmetry. However, both exhibit identical carbonyl bond lengths. Given a donor-to-acceptor stoichiometry of 1 : 1, each molecule is considered to carry one charge. These molecules stack alternately in a face-to-face staggered arrangement along the direction parallel to the line connecting the molecular center. The corresponding geometric parameters are dx = 0, dy = 0, dz = 3.262 Å, and θ = 60.00°.

From left to right: the top view along the π–π stacking direction, NDI molecular interlayer spacing, and molecular relative orientation diagrams. Replacing the pyridine substituent with phenyl leads to a significant change in crystallization behavior. The stoichiometry of the crystalline product is governed by the initial molar ratio of the reactants. When an excess of cobaltocene is used, a 1 : 1 adduct crystallizes, denoted as 2a-1, whose structure is shown in Fig. 2b. This likely results from the kinetic predominance of preformed ion pairs in a concentrated solution.36 This crystal belongs to the triclinic system with relatively compact packing. The NDI molecules adopt three structures close to the ionic state, arranged in an ordered …ABACABAC… repeating sequence. The interplanar spacings and rotation angles between different conformers vary slightly, with geometric parameters of dx = 0, dy = 0, dz1 = 3.332 Å, θ1 = 60.04° and dz2 = 3.319 Å, θ2 = 62.26°. Conversely, when an equimolar ratio is employed, a crystal phase with an NDI : CoCp2 ratio of 2 : 1 is obtained, denoted as 2a-2 (Fig. 2c), which likely represents a thermodynamically preferred packing motif. Since cobaltocene loses only one electron to achieve a stable 18-electron configuration,37 two NDI molecules share one electron. The two NDI molecules, however, exhibit distinct conformations: their bond lengths are shorter than those of the neutral and ionic states, respectively. Their pairing is reminiscent of mixed-valence π-pimers,38 but the interlayer spacings within and between pimers are identical, indicating an alternating, equidistant stacking of NDI anions with different charge densities, analogous to a charge-ordered insulator. The geometric parameters are dx = 0, dy = 0, dz = 3.32 Å, and θ = 70.95°.

When the substituent is changed to a p-aminophenyl group, the crystal structure of 3a differs again. Despite their similar compositions and stacking patterns, the presence of the amino group likely introduces directional interactions, leading to higher crystal symmetry. As shown in Fig. 2d, only one type of NDI conformer is present, forming a one-dimensional chain through staggered, quasi-equidistant stacking (the difference of 3.274 Å and 3.275 Å is within the error range of SC-XRD resolution). The electronic structure corresponds to a uniform ¼-filling. The geometric parameters for NDI are dx = 0, dy = 0, dz = 3.275 Å, and θ = 76.83°. The larger rotation angle likely arises from the increased steric demand of the p-aminophenyl group.

Another powerful reductant is TDAE; the presence of multiple electron-rich amino groups and the formation of a stable conjugated structure upon oxidation make it highly prone to losing two electrons.39 However, likely due to the lack of specific interactions, both the pyridine- and phenyl-substituted NDI molecules crystallize in the orthorhombic system when combined with TDAE, and their packing patterns are largely similar. For the former, the counterion moiety could not be resolved due to a high degree of disorder. For the latter (2b), unambiguous structural determination reveals an NDI : TDAE stoichiometric ratio of 2 : 1, indicating that each NDI carries one negative charge. Based on the similarity in crystal structure and powder X-ray diffraction patterns, 1b is expected to share the same composition. Nevertheless, their specific chain-packing structures exhibit significant differences: NDI-Py adopts an equidistant one-dimensional stack with higher symmetry, whereas NDI-Ph displays a periodically modulated, dimerized structure characterized by alternating π–π stacking distances, likely resulting from the competition between electronic energy and lattice energy.40 Accordingly, the relative geometric parameters for the NDI molecules in the two crystals are dx = 0, dy = 0, dz = 3.42 Å, θ = 55.47° and dx = 0, dy = 0, dz1 = 3.357 Å, dz2 = 3.386 Å, θ = 55.47°, respectively.

Physical properties

A comprehensive understanding of the electronic structure is a prerequisite for discussing the magnetic behavior of these crystals, as the packing mode serves both as the structural origin of the electronic phase and the external manifestation of charge distribution.41 Apparently, 1b and 3a represent 1/2 filled and 1/4 filled systems with uniform charge distribution, respectively, while 2b undergoes an additional dimerization distortion relative to the former. In contrast, the electronic states of 1a, 2a-1, 2a-2, and 3b are considerably more complex due to the presence of multiple NDI molecular configurations. Consequently, we employed periodic model calculations on the unit cells of these four crystals to elucidate their specific charge distributions. The results indicate that in 1a and 2a-1, likely due to the equal stoichiometric ratio of donors to acceptors, the charge on each distinct NDI conformation remains within −1 ± 0.1e, showing only marginal variance. Conversely, 2a-2 and 3b exhibit pronounced charge disproportionation, where different NDI conformations possess charges approaching 1e and 0e, consistent with qualitative inferences derived from molecular bond lengths (detailed computational results are provided in Table S5). These distinct electronic configurations lead to variations in the relative magnitudes of the transfer integral (t) and the on-site (U) or inter-site (V) Coulomb repulsion energies, thereby modulating the charge transfer characteristics.30 This, in turn, influences the electronic absorption spectra and charge transport properties within the one-dimensional chains, reflecting their underlying delocalization features (see Fig. S6 and S7 for a detailed discussion).

Although unpaired π-electrons are expected to delocalize between molecules as charge carriers via the overlap of NDI radical π-orbitals, the room-temperature conductivity of these one-dimensional chain crystals is restricted to the range of 10−3 to 10−1 S cm−1 due to various insulation mechanisms. The temperature-dependent conductivity consistently exhibits semiconducting behavior, suggesting that a significant portion of the π-electrons remain localized on the acceptor molecules. These electrons act as localized magnetic moments arranged on a low-dimensional molecular lattice, forming a low-dimensional magnetic system. Based on this, the magnetic susceptibilities of the polycrystalline radical samples described above were measured using a SQUID magnetometer over a temperature range of 2–400 K under an applied field of 0.5 T to investigate their magnetic behaviors. PXRD confirmed that the bulk crystalline material adopts the same structure as the single crystals (Fig. S5). Considering the characteristic molecular packing, the variable-temperature magnetic susceptibilities of the different crystals were fitted to corresponding Heisenberg exchange models, with necessary corrections for temperature-independent susceptibility (χ0) and Curie impurity contributions.

Magnetic interactions in discrete multimer systems

For crystals exhibiting dimeric stacking characteristics, π-interactions are largely confined within the dimeric unit, while interactions between dimers occur only indirectly via counterions. Consequently, their magnetism arises from the spatial coupling of two spins. The zero-field splitting features observed in EPR further confirm the strong coupling between two S = 1/2 spins.42 The variable-temperature magnetic susceptibility is well reproduced by the Bleaney–Bowers model.43 As shown in Fig. 3a and b, the decrease in effective magnetic moment upon cooling indicates a spin-paired singlet ground state. The magnetic coupling strength shows a significant positive correlation with the overlap area within the dimer. For 4a, the shortest interplanar distance and minimal slip give rise to the strongest antiferromagnetic coupling, with J = −397 cm−1. In contrast, the J value for the dimer in 3b is significantly attenuated to −203 cm−1 due to a larger separation and greater rotation angle (Table 1). This trend aligns with the results derived from variable-temperature EPR data, while the J value for 5a falls between these two. Furthermore, 4a exhibits larger zero-field splitting, broader peak-to-peak linewidth, and a more ideal Gaussian lineshape, corresponding to stronger intermolecular dipole–dipole interactions (Fig. 3c and d).44

Magnetic interactions in one-dimensional chain systems

For crystals exhibiting one-dimensional stacking characteristics, the spin magnetic moments are regularly distributed within the π-stacked chains, while the chains themselves remain magnetically isolated from each other with weak interchain coupling. The absence of a sharp cusp at low temperatures rules out the establishment of three-dimensional long-range magnetic order. Consequently, their magnetic behavior can be described using a one-dimensional Heisenberg model.45 For 1a in Fig. 4a, the magnetic susceptibility increases with temperature above 150 K, corresponding to the thermal disruption of antiferromagnetic spin pairing. However, likely due to either excessively strong antiferromagnetic coupling or thermal decomposition of the sample leading to an anomalous surge in susceptibility, no clear antiferromagnetic-to-paramagnetic transition was observed within the measured temperature range. Therefore, only a rough fit using the Bonner–Fisher model was possible, precluding the extraction of a precise magnetic coupling constant (J). Fitting the data at 300 K yields reasonable correlation, giving an estimated coupling constant of J ∼ −300 cm−1, along with a minor fraction (0.86%) of paramagnetic defects. The results for 2a-1 in Fig. 4b are similar: a higher proportion of paramagnetic defects (8.69%), superimposed on the low antiferromagnetic susceptibility, masks the expected rise in susceptibility at elevated temperatures. Nonetheless, a coupling constant of −264 cm−1 can be obtained from the fit. Such strong antiferromagnetic interactions in both cases likely originate from close interplanar distances and extensive π-orbital overlap of molecules with high spin density.

In contrast to the two cases described above, typical signatures of one-dimensional antiferromagnetic ordering are observed in 2a-2, 3a, and 1b. Their magnetic susceptibilities exhibit broad maxima centered at approximately 270 K, 130 K, and 230 K, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4c, d and e. Using the relationship |2J|/kB ≈ Tmax/0.641 (K) for a one-dimensional chain, the corresponding antiferromagnetic coupling constants are estimated to be −145 cm−1, −69 cm−1, and −119 cm−1. Compared to 1a and 2a-1, both 2a-2 and 3a belong to the cobaltocene system, yet the halving of the NDI charge per formula unit significantly reduces the intrachain antiferromagnetic coupling strength. The rotation angle also plays a critical role: the large rotation angle in 3a causes the SOMOs to miss optimal overlap, resulting in the weakest magnetic coupling. 2b presents a slightly more special case. Homogeneous molecules are arranged with alternating interplanar distances, implying the presence of two distinct magnetic exchange interactions. Indeed, compared to models assuming a single exchange constant (uniform 1D antiferromagnetic chain or simple dimer), the best fit across the temperature range of 100–400 K is achieved with a one-dimensional alternating antiferromagnetic (Hatfield) model,46 yielding J1 = −172 cm−1 and J2/J1 = 0.93. The two exchange interactions are not significantly different, consistent with the structural feature of two similar π–π stacking distances.

Mathematical fitting of the antiferromagnetic chains not only allows for an approximation of the intrachain magnetic coupling strength but also enables the quantification of the spin concentration within the system. Using a 100% S = 1/2 contribution as the theoretical benchmark, our one-dimensional chain samples consistently fall below the ideal value. This suggests that a portion of the π-electrons are delocalized and thus do not participate in magnetic coupling. Further comparative analysis reveals that, with the exception of 2a-1, the spin concentration is negatively correlated with room-temperature conductivity. This trend corresponds to the fact that systems with lower spin concentrations exhibit a higher degree of electronic delocalization, leading to a greater number of charge carriers and, consequently, higher conductivity. The anomaly observed in 2a-1 may be attributed to its complex crystalline environment.

As shown in Fig. S11 and S12, the EPR characteristics of the one-dimensional chain crystals are more complex, exhibiting overall anisotropic features. However, differences in molecular configuration and crystal field effects among the various crystals influence the strength of spin–orbit coupling along different directions, leading to variations in the relative magnitudes of g⊥ and g∥. The linewidth and lineshape of the EPR spectra also provide information about magnetic exchange interactions and electronic transport properties in the crystals. Compared to BEDT-TTF-based Mott-insulating charge-transfer salts, which typically show room-temperature ΔHpp values around 10 Gs,47 our crystals exhibit much narrower linewidths of only 1–2 Gs. This narrowing is likely a result of strong exchange interactions (exchange narrowing).48 The nearly temperature-independent linewidth further supports the dominance of this mechanism. The relatively low electrical conductivity indicates a limited contribution from spin–lattice relaxation to the linewidth. The lineshape is not purely Lorentzian but is better described by a Voigt profile—a convolution of Lorentzian and Gaussian components—reflecting the combined effects of homogeneous broadening due to exchange interactions and inhomogeneous broadening from dipole–dipole interactions.49 Furthermore, the exchange coupling constants estimated from fitting the temperature-dependent EPR-derived susceptibility are in reasonable agreement with those obtained from SQUID magnetometry (see the SI). This consistency indicates that the spin states in these systems are still predominantly localized magnetic moments and validates the applicability of the chosen magnetic models.

Theoretical calculations

The experimental results presented above have successfully established a qualitative correlation between magnetic coupling strength and crystal structure. However, due to the inherent complexity of intermolecular interactions, modifying the molecular formula often triggers a cascade of structural changes, preventing the relative packing geometry between molecules from varying in a single, continuous manner. To address this limitation, we have constructed NDI-based dimer models based on the experimental findings. Using two sets of variables—namely dx and dy, or dz and θ—we aim to quantitatively establish magneto-structural correlations.

To achieve this objective, the selection of a reliable electronic structure method is of paramount importance. Accordingly, we extracted dimer clusters from the 4a crystal structure and evaluated various density functional theory (DFT) methods (Table S6) by comparing calculated magnetic coupling constants against experimental benchmarks. Based on this validation, the LC-PBE/def2-TZVP level of theory was identified as providing the most accurate description of exchange interactions for the current NDI system, and all subsequent calculations were maintained at this consistent level. Using this protocol, the unsubstituted NDI-H anion radical was first optimized to its energetic minimum; this monomeric geometry was then held rigid to construct a series of dimer models. Specifically, the optimized monomer was fixed in the xy-plane, and a second monomer was positioned as a rigid replica by systematically applying transverse and longitudinal slips (dx, dy), varying the π–π stacking distance (dz), and introducing a rotation angle (θ), as illustrated in Scheme 1b. The theoretical J values for the resulting ensemble of dimers were evaluated to establish a quantitative magneto-structural correlation.

Given that the primary packing variations in the experimental one-dimensional chain crystals reside in the interplanar distances and twist angles, we initially performed calculations and derivations of the exchange coupling constant (J) based on these two variables: dz and θ. In the experimental crystals, the π–π distances of NDI range from a minimum of 3.204 Å to a maximum of 3.408 Å. Consequently, the dz range was set from 3.2 Å to 3.5 Å with a step size of 0.05 Å, while the twist angle θ spanned from 0° to 180° with a 5° increment. This ensemble of 511 dimers not only covers the established packing structures (55°–75°) but also explores potential configurations with small or large twist angles currently unreached in experiments.

The calculated results, shown in Fig. 5a, reveal that the angle-dependent magnetism is axisymmetric about 90°, while the magnetic coupling strength decreases monotonically with increasing π–π distance. Therefore, we focused on the variation of J from 0° to 90° at dz = 3.2 Å (see Fig. S15a for a more intuitive curve). In the initial state (0°), the antiferromagnetic (AFM) coupling is at its maximum due to the direct face-to-face alignment and maximal overlap of the SOMO orbitals between the two molecules.50 However, as the angle increases, the |J| value first decreases and then increases, reaching a local minimum at 35° and a local maximum at 60°, before further decreasing to a sign reversal at 90°. At this point, a narrow ferromagnetic (FM) region emerges with J = +13.5 cm−1.

The thick blue line represents the trajectory dy = 0.6dx, illustrating the transition in magnetic coupling behavior.

To understand this trend, we calculated the overlap integrals for dimers at several representative coordinates (Fig. S16a). The absolute values of these integrals correlate positively with the AFM coupling strength and approach zero within the FM region at 90°. According to the Hubbard model,51,52 the AFM coupling strength is proportional to 4t2/U, where the hopping integral t depends on the SOMO–SOMO overlap integral S. This indicates that a 35° rotation precisely induces a mismatch between positive and negative phase overlaps, a phenomenon intrinsically linked to the nodal distribution of the radical π-orbitals. Furthermore, the 55°–75° range, which corresponds to the rotation angles in the actual crystals, also exhibits significantly stronger AFM coupling.

To further explore potential ferromagnetic (FM) coupling regimes currently unobserved experimentally, and to delineate the trends in coupling type and strength within the dimer as a function of horizontal slip, we expanded our computational scan based on the parameters of θ = 0° and dz = 3.30 Å. Considering that the perpendicular distances from the NDI molecular center along the transverse and longitudinal axes to the peripheral hydrogen atoms are approximately 4.5 Å and 3.4 Å, respectively, we established a scan range of 0–5 Å for dx and 0–4 Å for dy, both with a step size of 0.1 Å. This comprehensive ensemble of 2091 dimers encompasses configurations ranging from perfectly cofacial to completely separated.

The calculated results, presented in Fig. 5b, reveal that the dependence of J on slip is considerably more complex than its angular or distance dependence, owing to the mutual interference between the dx and dy components. Nonetheless, clear patterns are discernible. Specifically, the transition between magnetic regimes is predominantly governed by the longitudinal slip (dy). Robust ferromagnetic coupling is maintained within irregular windows defined approximately by 0.8 < dy < 1.3 Å and 2.8 < dy < 3.6 Å (demarcated by thick black curves in the figure), although FM coupling can degrade back to AFM coupling for specific dx values.

This behavior is intrinsically linked to the rapid phase transformations and steep nodal gradients of the SOMO along the short axis (see Fig. S13 for details).34 Specific longitudinal slips can position the molecular orbitals of one molecule directly above the nodes of the other, effectively reducing the overlap integral. When superimposed with appropriate transverse slips (dx), these configurations are expected to further cancel out positive and negative phase overlaps, thereby triggering accidental orthogonality. Within these orthogonal stacking windows, the kinetic antiferromagnetic contribution (typically proportional to −t2/U) is effectively quenched, allowing the potential exchange term to stabilize the ferromagnetic ground state. Conversely, the coupling type exhibits significantly lower sensitivity to transverse slip (dx). This arises because the orbital nodal planes run parallel to the long axis, meaning pure longitudinal slip cannot escape the constraints of significant orbital overlap. For a detailed description of the variation in J with dy under different dx values, please refer to Fig. S15b.

The black circles centered around the coordinates (dx, dy) = (1.5 Å, 0.9 Å) in Fig. 5b represent the regime of the strongest FM coupling within our calculated ensemble, reaching a maximum J value of +58.1 cm−1. Notably, a vector extending from the origin (0, 0) through the point (1.5 Å, 0.9 Å) traverses a sequence of magnetic transitions: AFM to FM to AFM to FM. We specifically examined the evolution of the overlap integrals for a series of dimers along this trajectory, as shown in Fig. S16b. Consistent with the above observations, J and |S| exhibit a negative correlation; however, the FM coupling strength does not increase indefinitely as the overlap integral decreases. This is because the reduction in overlap is achieved at the expense of increasing the center-to-center molecular distance, which simultaneously leads to a decay in the potential exchange integral that drives ferromagnetism.50

Finally, to validate the reliability of the aforementioned computational models, we compared the experimental J values derived from the one-dimensional chain crystals with the calculated J values at corresponding coordinates in the dimer models. The sensitivities of both datasets toward dz and θ show excellent qualitative agreement (see Table S7). This consistency reinforces the physical reality of the FM regions identified in our calculations. Nevertheless, the experimental crystals invariably fall within the AFM regions. This preference is likely dictated by the low binding-energy states associated with these configurations; indeed, similar rotational stacking motifs are frequently encountered across various NDI radical molecular frameworks.53,54

Conclusions

In summary, this study significantly expands the phase diversity of fused-ring imide-based molecules in the field of organic solids, successfully closing the structure–property loop from molecular architecture to crystal packing and, ultimately, to magnetoelectronic functionality. Characterization of charge transport and spin properties demonstrates that the current charge-transfer NDI anion radical crystals fall within the regime of typical Mott insulators. Their strongly correlated electronic behavior governs the emergence of low-dimensional localized magnetic systems, accompanied by a discernible degree of electronic delocalization. Depending on the relative positions, spatial orientations, and charge densities of adjacent molecules, the magnetic coupling strength exhibits remarkable tunability, with J values spanning a wide range from −397 cm−1 to −69 cm−1. We further constructed a radical dimer model to establish a comprehensive magneto-structural correlation map. The dependence of the theoretically calculated J values on intermolecular distances and twist angles is in excellent agreement with experimental data. Moreover, the variations in magnetic exchange can be rationally explained by orbital overlap integrals, validating the applicability of the Hubbard model in describing magnetic interactions within large π-conjugated molecular systems. Crucially, the identification of potential ferromagnetic fingerprint regions reveals that achieving ferromagnetic coupling hinges on precise longitudinal (short-axis) slippage between NDI molecules. Future research will focus on locking these idealized packing modes through targeted chemical modifications.
