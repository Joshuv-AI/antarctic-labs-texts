Teaching bioinformatics with generative AI: judgment, uncertainty, and responsibility
Authors: Ashley I. Teufel, David A. Liberles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenerative artificial intelligence (AI) tools are rapidly reshaping how students write code, analyze data, and articulate scientific reasoning. In bioinformatics education, where computational workflows intersect with biological interpretation, these tools present both substantial opportunities and serious pedagogical risks. Rather than attempting to prohibit or police AI use, which is increasingly impractical, instructors must reconsider what it means to teach bioinformatics when routine coding, data wrangling, and even statistical analysis can be automated. This is not a temporary disruption; students will build careers in an automated world. A central challenge is the relationship between expertise and effective AI use. Generative AI is most productive in the hands of users who can evaluate its outputs, yet students are precisely the population still developing that capacity. This perspective reflects on teaching undergraduate and graduate bioinformatics courses during the early generative AI era, focusing on how AI tools alter student behavior, assessment design, and instructor responsibilities. When integrated deliberately into pedagogy, AI can lower technical barriers and accelerate learning. When students engage with AI without adequate scaffolding, it risks masking misunderstandings and short-circuiting the development of foundational skills.

PERSPECTIVE

Bioinformatics has always been shaped by technological change. From the introduction of high-throughput sequencing to the widespread adoption of statistical programming languages, the field has repeatedly adapted to new tools that transformed what it means to analyze biological data. Generative artificial intelligence (AI) represents a qualitatively different shift. Unlike prior technologies that primarily automated computation, large language models can generate an executable code, construct analysis pipelines, interpret results, and explain concepts in natural language (1, 2).

For educators, this raises an immediate and uncomfortable question: what exactly are we teaching when students can ask an AI system to write a complete bioinformatics pipeline in seconds?

Early discussions of AI in undergraduate education often focus on cheating, plagiarism, or academic misconduct (3). While these concerns are understandable, they obscure a deeper issue that is relevant to both undergraduate and graduate students. In bioinformatics, producing a syntactically correct code has never been the primary learning objective. The real goals are understanding biological systems, selecting appropriate analytical frameworks, interpreting uncertainty, and reasoning about models and data limitations. Generative AI challenges instructors to design courses that foreground these goals rather than superficial technical outputs (4).

This perspective draws on experience teaching undergraduate and graduate bioinformatics and biostatistics courses; the same principles apply across levels, although undergraduate courses often require more structured scaffolding while graduate courses can place greater responsibility on students to justify open-ended analytical decisions. The aim is not to offer a universal prescription but to articulate principles that can help instructors navigate this transition thoughtfully and responsibly.

In this discussion, we are using bioinformatics in a wide sense, rather than distinguishing it from computational biology. Within this broad area, there is one set of tasks that is technical in nature and another set of tasks that is more analytical. This distinction drives the discussion of modern training for bioinformatics students.

WHAT BIOINFORMATICS EDUCATION ACTUALLY TEACHES

Although bioinformatics curricula vary widely, most courses target three overlapping categories of learning outcomes. First, students must develop computational fluency, including familiarity with programming languages, file formats, data structures, and software tools. Second, they must learn statistical reasoning and, in some cases, probability theory and stochastic processes, including model assumptions, sources of variability, uncertainty, and the consequences of analytical choices. Third, and most importantly, students must develop biological interpretation skills. They must be able to ask whether an analytical result makes sense in the context of evolution, molecular function (including biochemistry and molecular biophysics), ecology, or physiology (5).

In practice, assessment often relies on computational artifacts because they are easy to grade. Code either runs or it does not. Figures either render or they do not. Generative AI decouples these artifacts from understanding. A student can now submit a polished analysis without being able to explain why any step was performed or what limitations apply. This shift exposes a long-standing weakness in bioinformatics education. Too often, technical execution is treated as a proxy for conceptual mastery. Generative AI forces instructors to confront this distinction directly.

STUDENT BEHAVIOR IN THE PRESENCE OF GENERATIVE AI

Students vary widely in how they use AI tools. Some employ them cautiously as debugging aids or exploratory tutors. Others rely on them to generate entire assignments. Most fall somewhere in between. Importantly, AI does not create problematic behavior so much as amplify existing habits. Strong students tend to use AI to accelerate tasks they already understand. They ask focused questions, evaluate outputs, and adapt responses thoughtfully. Struggling students are more likely to use AI outputs uncritically, copying code or explanations that they do not fully comprehend. In these cases, AI functions less as a scaffold and more as a mask (6, 7).

Attempts to ban AI rarely solve this problem. Students continue using it covertly, and instructors expend energy policing behavior rather than teaching. More importantly, prohibition fails to prepare students for the reality that AI-assisted workflows are rapidly becoming standard in research and industry (4, 8).

EXPERTISE, EVALUATION, AND THE CHALLENGE OF PRODUCTIVE USE

One reason generative AI feels simultaneously powerful and unsettling is that its productive use depends substantially on the user’s existing expertise. In academic research practice, AI tools are effective because domain experts already know how to perform the analyses they ask these tools to generate. When an output is wrong, they notice. When assumptions are inappropriate, they recognize the mismatch. When an explanation is fluent but misleading, prior knowledge supplies the friction needed to catch the error before it propagates. In this sense, what makes AI useful is not the tool itself but the ability to supervise it.

This observation is not unique to bioinformatics. Recent research from Microsoft has found that some workers expend more cognitive effort when working with AI in evaluation tasks, not less, precisely because they take on the burden of assessment that less experienced users cannot perform (9). The tool rewards vigilance; it does not generate it.

There is, however, something quietly disheartening about this arrangement. Generative AI excels at doing things experts already know how to do. It writes code in languages they spent years learning, drafts analyses they could implement themselves, and produces explanations whose correctness they can judge only because they already understand the underlying concepts. The expert’s role becomes one of refinement. This is undeniably useful, but it is also revealing. The tool amplifies existing competence; it does not extend it in any epistemically safe way.

Critically, experts cannot rely on the same system to teach them something genuinely new. When they move outside their domain of expertise, the very property that makes AI productive becomes a liability. Without the ability to independently evaluate correctness, they lose the capacity to distinguish insight from fabrication. The tool does not know when it is wrong, and they no longer know how to tell. This creates a tension at the heart of AI-assisted learning: the moment one most needs help is the moment when the help becomes unreliable.

West and colleagues have formalized a related phenomenon they call the generative AI paradox, the observation that large language models can produce expert-like outputs that exceed what they actually understand (10). Unlike humans, for whom basic comprehension almost always precedes the ability to generate sophisticated work, generative models acquire fluent output capabilities without the corresponding depth of understanding. For educators, this inversion is consequential. Students encountering AI-generated explanations may receive confident, polished prose that contains fundamental errors, errors that require the very expertise students are still developing to detect.

This asymmetry scales across levels of training. The tool is most productive in the hands of experts who need it least for learning and least reliable for the students who stand to benefit most from good instruction. Generative AI is, in this sense, epistemically parasitic on prior knowledge. Where that foundation is absent, fluent AI output risks substituting for understanding rather than supporting it (11).

For education, this creates a genuine pedagogical challenge, not because anyone is asking students to uncritically accept AI outputs, but because the natural dynamics of tool use tend in that direction. Students working under time pressure, facing unfamiliar material, or lacking confidence in their own abilities will gravitate toward the path of least resistance. If AI provides an answer that looks reasonable and runs without errors, the incentive to scrutinize it diminishes. The problem is not malicious intent but ordinary behavior.

Seen this way, the central pedagogical challenge of generative AI is not academic dishonesty nor the speed at which assignments can be completed but the gap between fluency and understanding. We have developed remarkably powerful tools for accelerating execution, yet ones whose outputs require precisely the expertise that students are still building to evaluate. That tension is real but can be partially addressed through intentional course design.

The instructional goal, then, is to structure learning environments where critical evaluation is built into the workflow and where students develop the capacity to assess AI outputs as part of the learning process itself rather than as a separate skill to be acquired later. This reframes AI not as a shortcut to be policed or a tutor to be trusted but as a collaborator whose contributions require the same scrutiny we would apply to any other source. For bioinformatics, this redoubles the importance of understanding biology.

REFRAMING ASSIGNMENTS AROUND INTERPRETATION AND JUDGMENT

A productive response is to reframe assignments so that AI use is permitted but insufficient on its own to succeed. Rather than evaluating whether students can produce code, instructors can evaluate whether students can explain, critique, and contextualize analyses (4).

For example, instead of asking students to construct a basic pipeline, an assignment might ask them to compare two AI-generated pipelines, identify their assumptions, and argue which is more appropriate for a given biological question. Students might also be asked to interpret deliberately flawed AI outputs. Generative models frequently make subtle but consequential errors, such as inappropriate statistical tests or biologically implausible interpretations. Diagnosing these errors requires genuine understanding and builds exactly the skills students need.

Reflective components such as short written explanations, oral check-ins, or in-class discussions further surface student reasoning. These elements are difficult to outsource because they depend on course-specific contexts and shared discussions. They also create opportunities for instructors to model the kind of critical engagement with AI outputs that characterizes expert use (5). In these designs, the student remains the epistemic agent. AI becomes a source of material to be evaluated rather than an authority to be deferred to.

A core part of this instruction is for the students to have explicit expectations for results that they would observe. These expectations qualitatively would come from a critical understanding of core concepts in interdisciplinary biology. More quantitatively, it involves skills in the use of process-based mechanistic models (12). That is, there is still a need for traditional, non-AI modeling skills. When results between this type of modeling and AI differ, it is either flawed AI output or a misunderstanding in our appreciation of process or mechanism. The latter would reflect a gain in knowledge but is only achievable when expectations from our current state of knowledge are made explicit. In a pedagogic context, assignments using toy models and AI can illustrate this approach, and both outcomes explain a divergence in findings.

One concrete way to operationalize this shift is to design assignments that make student thinking visible before, during, or after AI use. This is partly a return to an old goal of scientific education: teaching students how to think, not merely how to produce an answer. In some assignments, AI may be permitted during production, but students are later asked to explain, defend, and compare their work. In others, students first commit to their own expectations or arguments before comparing them with an AI-generated response. Still others ask students to build process-based models that generate formal expectations before those expectations are compared with data or automated outputs. Box 1 presents three examples across this range: an AI-permitted lab with live code review, a reasoning-first writing assignment built around competing interpretations of probability, and a multiweek bioinformatics modeling sequence that moves students from simulation to mechanistic interpretation. Across these examples, AI is not treated as a replacement for student thinking but as a material that students must interpret, question, and refine.

Structuring AI use around explanation, expectations, and judgment

These examples illustrate several ways to incorporate AI into student work without making AI-generated output the endpoint of the assignment. The examples differ in where AI enters the process. In some cases, AI may be used during production, and accountability comes through explanation and peer comparison. In others, students reason first and use AI next. In more process-based assignments, students build models that force them to make explicit expectations about the relationship between biological mechanisms and resulting data. In all cases, the instructional goal is to move students beyond producing an answer toward explaining, evaluating, comparing, and revising.

Example 1: AI-permitted lab with live code review. In a statistics or bioinformatics lab, students work through a guided R analysis using a biological data set. AI tools may be used during this portion of the lab, but students are responsible for understanding the code they submit. At the end of the lab, several students are randomly selected to walk through their analysis for the class. In one implementation, each student is assigned a number, and selection is made using a 24-sided die, making the process transparent, routine, and mildly playful rather than punitive. The selected students’ code is projected on the board for a live peer code review. Each student explains the research question, how assumptions were checked, what analysis was run, what results came back, and what the result means biologically. A short comparison discussion then asks whether students used the same approach, transformed the data differently, or reached the same biological conclusion. All students then submit their work along with a brief reflection, identifying what they did differently, what they would change in their own code, and what remains confusing. In this structure, AI may help students produce code, but the assessment centers on whether they can understand, defend, and interpret that code.

Example 2: Reasoning from competing hypotheses. Students are given two alternative hypotheses about probability. One hypothesis is that probability accounts for uncertainty in observations and nothing more. The other is that probability is a fundamental property of the universe, such that different outcomes remain possible even if all observable conditions are controlled. Students first write a short thesis taking one side of the argument without using AI. They then use their own phrasing of the problem and their choice of AI tool to ask AI to write a thesis on the same question. Finally, students compare the two pieces of text, assess the strengths and weaknesses of each, and revise their original response. In this structure, students commit to their own reasoning before consulting AI, making the comparison between human and AI-generated arguments explicit.

Example 3: Process-based bioinformatics modeling. A more technical version of the same principle asks students to build models that generate explicit expectations about biological data. For example, students might begin with count data from 100 patients, each potentially infected with one or more of 20 pathogens. For each pathogen, each patient may show only a background environmental signal, a non-disease-causing level of infection, or a disease-causing level of infection. Students are asked to develop a mixture-modeling approach that assigns each patient-pathogen combination to one of these categories and to interpret what that assignment means biologically.

A related multiweek assignment can move further toward process-based modeling. Students first write a forward-time Wright-Fisher-like simulator for a diploid population with an explicit data structure, mimicking aspects of gene regulation. They then extend the simulator to include mutations that affect gene expression, specify regulatory regions and their mutational effects, connect expression to organismal fitness, and eventually simulate expression evolution across a small phylogeny. In a final extension, students model cell type-specific expression as a stochastic outcome of transcription factor concentrations and regulatory architecture. These assignments require students to formalize assumptions, choose data structures and distributions, and explain how biological mechanisms generate the observed or simulated data.

Together, these examples show that AI can enter the learning process in different ways. Sometimes, it is allowed during production and followed by explanation, peer comparison, and reflection. Sometimes, it is introduced only after students have committed to their own line of reasoning. Sometimes, the emphasis is on mechanistic or statistical models that make expectations explicit before interpretation occurs. What matters is not the exact sequence of AI use but whether the assignment requires students to explain, evaluate, compare, and revise rather than simply accept a polished output.

ASSESSMENT UNDER AI CONDITIONS

Assessment practices must evolve alongside instruction. Traditional take-home assignments are particularly vulnerable to unreflective AI use. In response, many instructors are returning to controlled assessments such as in-class exams, oral defenses, or time-limited coding exercises (8). These formats help ensure baseline fluency but should not dominate the course.

Bioinformatics is inherently iterative and open-ended. Overreliance on proctored assessment risks narrowing the curriculum to what can be tested under constraint. A balanced approach combines AI-permitted assignments with AI-constrained assessments. For example, open-ended projects may explicitly allow AI use, paired with in-class evaluations focused on interpretation and reasoning (4, 5).

Rubrics in these assignments should, therefore, shift from evaluating technical completion to evaluating interpretive reasoning. Criteria might include whether students can state the biological question, justify the statistical or computational approach, identify relevant assumptions, interpret uncertainty, explain the biological meaning of the result, and recognize limitations or alternative explanations.

Additionally, clear communication is essential. Students should know when AI use is acceptable, expected, or restricted and, crucially, why. Explaining the pedagogical rationale behind AI policies helps students understand that restrictions are not arbitrary obstacles but intentional scaffolding designed to build skills they will eventually need to use AI productively (3).

TEACHING AI LITERACY AS PART OF BIOINFORMATICS

AI literacy is becoming as essential as statistical literacy in bioinformatics. Students must understand that generative models do not reason biologically, do not understand experimental design, and do not evaluate correctness autonomously (10). One effective instructional strategy is to expose students to AI failure modes directly. Demonstrating incorrect AI-generated analyses in class and working through the process of identifying what went wrong quickly teach that confident language does not imply correctness. These exercises transform abstract warnings into concrete experience. Students who have personally caught an AI making a plausible-sounding but incorrect claim develop intuitions that persist.

Teaching students how to evaluate outputs, check assumptions, and perform sanity checks should be viewed as legitimate bioinformatics skills rather than ancillary ones. In a field where computational tools have always mediated between researchers and data, the ability to critically assess automated outputs has always mattered. Generative AI simply raises the stakes.

Framed this way, AI use becomes less about short-cutting work and more about developing judgment. Students learn not merely to use AI but to collaborate with it productively, recognizing when to rely on it cautiously and when to set it aside entirely.

ACCESS CONSIDERATIONS AND BRINGING OUT THE BEST IN EVERYONE

Generative AI has genuine potential to lower barriers for students with limited computational backgrounds (1, 5). A student who struggles with syntax errors can use AI to get working code faster, freeing cognitive resources for the biological questions that are the actual learning objective. At the same time, unequal access to paid tools and uneven familiarity with effective usage can create differences in opportunity to advance (11). Students who have been using AI tools since high school arrive with prompting skills and calibrated expectations that their peers lack. Instructors should be mindful of generating assignments that are accessible to students without access to premium tools or prior AI experience. At the same time, instructors should also work to engage all students at an appropriate level, including those who are already advanced to enable their further advancement.

Where possible, institutions should provide access to approved platforms and training for both students and faculty. Treating AI as an individual advantage rather than an institutional resource risks widening existing gaps. It is important to be mindful of not only who has access to these tools but also who has the scaffolding necessary to use them productively (3, 11).

FACULTY IDENTITY AND THE CHANGING ROLE OF THE INSTRUCTOR

For instructors trained prior to the generative AI era, there can be a sense of loss. Many spent years developing computational fluency only to watch machines replicate those skills instantly. However, the role of the instructor is not becoming obsolete. It is shifting. Faculty expertise now lies less in writing every line of code and more in guiding judgment, framing questions, and modeling how experts evaluate automated outputs.

In this sense, AI brings instruction closer to how scientific work actually occurs, where the challenge is not writing code from scratch but rather evaluating analytical choices, interpreting ambiguous results, and knowing when something does not look right. The instructor’s role is to make that tacit expertise visible and to show students what expert evaluation looks like in practice, not just tell them it matters. It is getting back to teaching people to be scientists, to think critically, rather than to simply learn technical skills.

PREPARING STUDENTS FOR FUTURE SCIENTIFIC PRACTICE

Students entering data-driven biological fields will encounter AI routinely. Shielding them from these tools does them a disservice. Instead, bioinformatics courses should prepare students to work with AI productively, understanding both its capabilities and its limitations (4).

This includes developing confidence to question outputs and humility to recognize one’s own blind spots. It means understanding that critical evaluation is not a burden imposed by skeptical instructors but a core professional skill. These are durable competencies that will persist even as the specific tools evolve. The goal is not to produce students who can use the current generation of AI tools but students who understand what productive collaboration with any computational tool requires because they have internalized what it means to know something rather than merely to have been given an answer.

CONCLUSIONS

Generative AI represents a structural shift in how bioinformatics is practiced and taught. Education in this field cannot remain anchored to assessments that prioritize code production over reasoning. Instead, instruction must focus on interpretation, judgment, and biological insight.

AI can accelerate learning and lower barriers when integrated deliberately into pedagogy, when instructors scaffold its use, build evaluation into assignments, and model the critical engagement that characterizes expert practice. When students engage with AI without that scaffolding, it risks obscuring misunderstandings and short-circuiting the development of foundational skills. The solution is not resistance or prohibition, but conceptual stewardship, a commitment to preserving the epistemic core of what it means to be trained in this field.

Teaching bioinformatics with AI is not about surrendering rigor. It is about clarifying what rigor means in a field where automation is inevitable. The future of bioinformatics education lies in helping students develop the judgment to use these tools well, which means first helping them understand what “well” requires. It means emphasizing the BIO in bioinformatics.
