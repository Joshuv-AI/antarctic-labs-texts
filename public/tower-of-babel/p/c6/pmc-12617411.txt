Single-cell mapping of the glycocalyx in the adult human and mouse ocular surface
Authors: Rafael Martínez-Carrasco, Pablo Argüeso, Dennis Discher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe glycocalyx is a critical but often underappreciated modulator of cellular behavior. Its diversity across cell types within tissues remains poorly understood, but recent advances in single-cell profiling now enable more precise analysis of cell surface composition. Here, we applied single-cell glycan and RNA sequencing to profile glycocalyx diversity across human and mouse ocular surface cell types. Glycocalyx patterns effectively distinguished epithelial subtypes, with corneal epithelial cells enriched in complex and high-mannose N-glycans, conjunctival cells in fucosylated structures, and goblet cells in O-glycans. We also observed dynamic changes during epithelial maturation, marked by regulated shifts in sialic acid structures. In the mouse ocular surface, glycocalyx patterns distinguished major cell types, but the glycan profiles differed from those in humans, pointing to species-specific features. These findings demonstrate that glycocalyx composition is closely linked to cell identity and maturation and provide a foundation for exploring its roles in tissue organization and disease.Keywords: cell differentiation, glycocalyx, lectin, ocular surface, single-cell sequencing

This study introduces the first high-resolution atlas of the ocular surface glycocalyx, generated using single-cell glycan and RNA sequencing. Our findings reveal distinct glycan signatures across epithelial cell types that correspond to cellular identity, differentiation state, and species-specific traits. The work offers a framework for integrating glycan composition with gene expression in intact epithelia and underscores the dynamic remodeling of the glycocalyx during cellular maturation.

Introduction

All cells are covered by a dense and complex layer of carbohydrates known as the glycocalyx, which comprises a diverse array of glycoproteins, glycolipids, and proteoglycans extending from the plasma membrane into the extracellular space. Once regarded as a passive protective barrier, the glycocalyx is now understood to be a dynamic structure that plays essential roles in biological processes, including cellular protection, adhesion, signaling, and differentiation (1, 2). Its structural diversity is driven by the underlying expression and activity of glycosylation enzymes, giving rise to cell-type-specific glycan architectures adapted to diverse physiological demands. The mammalian glycome is estimated to encompass more than 7,000 distinct glycan structures, assembled from just 10 monosaccharides through the coordinated action of ∼700 proteins, including nearly 200 glycosyltransferases that extend glycan chains using nucleotide- or lipid-linked donor sugars (3). Aberrant glycosylation has been implicated in a wide range of pathological processes, including cancer progression, immune evasion, and vascular dysfunction, positioning the glycocalyx as both a central regulator of cell–environment interactions and a promising source of diagnostic and therapeutic targets (4–6).

A significant challenge in glycocalyx research lies in resolving its compositional heterogeneity at the level of individual cell types within complex tissues. Although high-throughput platforms such as lectin microarrays and mass spectrometry have enabled broad glycan profiling, these techniques typically lack the spatial resolution required to attribute glycan features to specific cell populations. Single-cell transcriptomics provides an indirect approach to infer glycan composition by analyzing the expression of glycosylation-related genes, or glycogenes (7, 8). However, the relationship between transcript abundance and glycan structures is influenced by numerous factors, including substrate availability, enzyme competition, post-transcriptional regulation, and the local microenvironment complicating direct predictions of glycan output from gene expression alone (7, 9, 10). Recent advances in single-cell glycomics have enabled direct measurement of glycan diversity at single-cell resolution. Among these methods, single-cell glycan and RNA sequencing (scGR-seq) combines lectin-based glycan profiling with transcriptomic analysis, allowing the simultaneous characterization of glycan composition and gene expression in individual cells (11). This approach has revealed cell-state-specific glycan signatures in cultured systems and biological fluids, including human pluripotent stem cells and immune cell populations (12–15). However, scGR-seq has not yet been broadly applied to map glycocalyx diversity across the cellular landscape of intact solid tissues.

The cornea, conjunctiva, and limbus together form the anterior surface of the eye and are essential for maintaining vision and protecting the ocular surface (16). These anatomically contiguous but functionally distinct tissues consist of specialized epithelial, stromal, and immune cells, among others. The cornea enables light refraction and serves as a protective barrier, the conjunctiva contributes to immune surveillance and tear film stability, and the limbus harbors stem cells required for corneal epithelial renewal. Components of the epithelial glycocalyx in these compartments have been shown to support critical functions, including barrier integrity (17), wound healing (18), pathogen defense (19, 20), and limbal stem cell biology (21, 22). Despite its importance, the degree of glycocalyx heterogeneity across the ocular surface remains poorly defined. Here, we applied scGR-seq to map the glycocalyx at single-cell resolution in human and mouse ocular surface tissues. Our findings reveal distinct glycan signatures linked to cell identity and differentiation, demonstrating the utility of single-cell glycomics for characterizing glycocalyx diversity in solid tissues and providing a foundation for future studies of its roles in spatial cell organization and disease.

Results

Single-cell profiling reveals an association between glycocalyx signatures and ocular surface cell identity

Plant lectins have long served as molecular tools for detecting specific carbohydrate structures on glycoproteins, glycolipids and cells, with binding influenced by the structural context and accessibility of the glycan epitopes (23). To systematically profile glycan features at single-cell resolution, we conjugated DNA oligonucleotides to a panel of plant lectins with defined glycan specificities, including those that recognize Gal(β1-3)GalNAc epitopes commonly found on O-glycans, as well as lectins specific for N-glycans, N-acetyl-lactosamine, fucosylated motifs, and terminal residues containing N-acetylglucosamine and sialic acid (Fig. 1A). Consequently, single-cell suspensions were prepared from healthy human donor tissue, labeled with the lectin panel, and analyzed by single-cell RNA sequencing. After quality control filtering, 22,042 cells were retained for downstream analysis. Unsupervised clustering of transcriptomic profiles identified 23 distinct clusters, which were subsequently consolidated into ten major cell types based on the differential expression of established marker genes (Fig. S1, Dataset S1). These included limbal progenitor cells, transient amplifying cells, limbal suprabasal epithelial cells, corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, goblet cells, melanocytes, fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, and immune cells (Fig. 1B).

To determine whether ocular surface cell identity is associated with specific glycocalyx signatures, we performed unsupervised reclustering based solely on lectin-binding profiles. This analysis resolved five distinct glycocalyx-defined clusters (lectin clusters [LCs]), each enriched for specific cell populations and distinguished by unique glycan patterns (Fig. 1C and D). LC1 was predominantly composed of conjunctival epithelial cells; LC2 included limbal progenitor, transient amplifying, suprabasal, melanocyte, vascular, and immune populations; LC3 was enriched for corneal epithelial cells; LC4 comprised a heterogeneous mix of cell types; and LC5 was strongly associated with goblet cells. These glycocalyx profiles were consistent across the three donors analyzed and unaffected by interindividual variation (Fig. S2).

To further determine whether the lectin clusters reflected established ocular surface epithelial phenotypes, we performed differential gene expression analysis for each cluster, revealing distinct enrichment of canonical epithelial cell markers (Fig. 1E, Dataset S2). LC1 was defined by high expression of conjunctival epithelial markers, including PIGR, CEACAM6, CEACAM7, KRT4, LCN2, and AQP5, among the top 15 differentially expressed genes. LC2 exhibited elevated expression of basal/progenitor-associated genes, such as KRT14, ITGA6, and S100A2. LC3 showed selective enrichment for corneal epithelial markers, including MAL, KRT12, and KRT24. While LC4 lacked a distinct transcriptional signature corresponding to a specific epithelial subtype, LC5 demonstrated upregulation of goblet cell–associated genes, including MUC5AC, TFF1, and TFF3. These findings support a strong correspondence between lectin-binding signatures and epithelial cell identity.

We also examined the differential expression of endogenous lectins within the single-cell RNA-seq dataset to complement the exogenous lectin-binding profiles. Endogenous lectins also showed cell-type-specific expression patterns across the ocular surface (Fig. S3). Galectins such as LGALS3 and LGALS7B were broadly detected in epithelial populations, while trefoil factors and ZG16B were enriched in goblet cells. Several C-type lectins, including CLEC7A and COLEC12, were restricted to immune and vascular compartments. These patterns suggest that endogenous lectins could provide an additional regulatory layer through their interactions with glycan structures within the cell.

Lectin binding intensity defines distinct ocular surface epithelial cell types

We next asked whether the binding intensity of individual exogenous lectins could distinguish among epithelial cell types. To address this, we identified lectins with statistically significant differential binding to the glycocalyx of each epithelial population (Fig. 2A). Limbal progenitor cells exhibited strong binding to SNA I and MAA I, consistent with enrichment of α2-6- and α2-3-sialylated residues, respectively, and showed minimal reactivity to WGA. Transient amplifying and limbal suprabasal cells displayed a similar MAA I and WGA binding pattern but showed increased reactivity to the N-glycan-specific lectin ConA. Corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells demonstrated opposing lectin-binding profiles, with ConA, DSA, MAA I, WGA, and Aleuria aurantia lectin (AAL) showing inverse reactivities between the two populations. Notably, goblet cells exhibited a distinct profile marked by strong enrichment of Gal(β1-3)GalNAc epitopes, as detected by PNA and MPA. These findings were supported by lectin histochemistry, which revealed intense DSA staining in the corneal epithelium relative to the conjunctiva, and PNA reactivity restricted to a subset of MUC5AC-positive goblet cells (Fig. 2B and C).

The observed differences in lectin binding among ocular surface epithelial cell types prompted us to investigate whether these patterns reflected underlying variation in glycosylation pathways. For each cell, we calculated pathway activity scores corresponding to key biosynthetic processes involved in nucleotide-sugar metabolism, glycosyl-substrate/donor transport, glycan biosynthesis and degradation (Table S1). Correlation analysis between lectin binding and pathway scores revealed generally weak associations, with Spearman correlation coefficients below 0.4 across comparisons (Fig. 2D). Despite these low overall correlations, the O-glycan-binding lectins MPA and PNA showed consistent associations with O-glycosylation activity, with MPA also correlating with sialylation-related pathways, particularly sialyl T-antigen biosynthesis. N-glycan-binding lectins, including DSA and ConA, were linked to pathways involved in the assembly of the N-glycan oligosaccharide precursor and its transfer to nascent polypeptides via the oligosaccharyltransferase complex. The N-acetyl-lactosamine (LacNAc)-binding lectin ECA was associated with genes involved in N-glycan branching and diacetyllactosediamine (LacDiNAc) synthesis. Several correlations did not align with expected biosynthetic processes, underscoring the limitations of inferring glycocalyx composition from transcriptomic data alone and highlighting the need for direct glycan profiling to resolve cellular glycosylation states.

Corneal epithelial differentiation is associated with distinct glycocalyx profiles

Analysis of glycocalyx composition across ocular surface epithelial cell types suggested that glycosylation patterns may reflect stages of cellular differentiation. To test whether the glycocalyx serves as a marker of epithelial maturation, we focused on the limbal-to-corneal epithelial lineage by isolating the limbal progenitor, transient amplifying, limbal suprabasal, and corneal epithelial cell clusters for further analysis. These populations were reclustered based on lectin-binding profiles, and trajectory analysis was used to infer differentiation paths defined by glycocalyx composition (Fig. 3A).

Using a principal graph–based approach, we assessed differential gene expression and lectin binding along the inferred differentiation trajectory, revealing significant changes in gene expression—including established markers of epithelial maturation—and shifts in lectin binding patterns across pseudotime (Fig. S4A and B). Notably, SNA I binding was enriched at early pseudotime points, mirroring the expression of KRT14, a canonical marker of epithelial progenitor cells (Fig. 3B). As differentiation progressed, binding of the N-glycan-specific lectin DSA increased, paralleling the expression of KRT3, a marker of suprabasal corneal epithelial cells. Toward the terminal stages, WGA binding rose markedly, aligning with increased expression of KRT24, a marker of superficial corneal epithelium. To validate the lectin-inferred trajectory, we performed pseudotime analysis using transcriptomic data from the same limbal–corneal populations (Fig. S5A–C). Both approaches showed early enrichment of progenitor markers (KRT14, KRT15, CXCL14, and S100A2) and later upregulation of differentiation-associated genes, including LGALS3, MAL, CLDN7, KRT12, and KRT24. The differential expression of lectins was further supported by histological analysis, which showed SNA I reactivity concentrated in basal epithelial layers of the human limbus, while WGA staining was strongest in superficial layers (Fig. 3C).

The analysis also revealed progressive changes in glycogene expression along the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. S4C). Genes upregulated at later pseudotime points were predominantly associated with sialylation, including those involved in sialic acid biosynthesis (GNE and CMAS) and sialyltransferases (ST3GAL4, ST6GALNAC1, and ST6GALNAC6). Notably, GNE, which encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of N-acetylneuraminic acid—the most common form of sialic acid in humans—showed increased expression in differentiated cells. This bifunctional enzyme, possessing both epimerase and kinase activity, is highly expressed in tissues with elevated sialylation demands (24). In situ hybridization confirmed elevated GNE expression in superficial limbal and corneal cells (Fig. 3E), consistent with a progressive increase in sialylation during corneal epithelial differentiation.

Ocular surface glycocalyx composition varies across species

Given the relevance of mouse models for studying ocular surface diseases, we applied the lectin-based single-cell profiling approach to mouse ocular surface tissue. Following quality control filtering, 16,947 cells were retained for downstream analysis. Unsupervised clustering of transcriptomic profiles identified ten major cell types based on the expression of canonical marker genes (Fig. S6, Dataset S3). These included limbal progenitor cells, transient amplifying cells, corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, goblet cells, keratocytes, fibroblasts, myofibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, and immune cells (Fig. 4A).

Reclustering based on lectin-binding profiles identified four distinct lectin clusters, each representing glycan signatures that were distributed across a broader range of cell types compared to their human counterparts (Fig. 4B and C). LC1 was predominantly composed of limbal progenitor cells, transient amplifying cells, corneal epithelial cells, and basal conjunctival epithelial cells, and was marked by strong PNA, DSA, and AAL binding. Differential gene expression analysis of this cluster revealed enrichment of canonical epithelial markers consistent with a basal or undifferentiated profile (Fig. S7, Dataset S4). LC2 was enriched for apical corneal and conjunctival epithelial cells, goblet cells, and most vascular endothelial cells, and was defined by elevated binding of ECA, SNA I, and WGA. LC3 was primarily composed of fibroblasts and was characterized by high MPA and low PNA binding. LC4 comprised a heterogeneous mixture of cells from multiple lineages and was marked by strong ConA and MAA I binding.

To evaluate interspecies differences in ocular surface glycocalyx composition, we compared normalized lectin-binding intensities across corresponding human and mouse cell types (Fig. 4D). DSA binding was largely conserved between species; however, most lectin-binding patterns revealed substantial divergence. Human goblet cells showed strong MPA and PNA binding, consistent with abundant O-glycans, whereas these signals were markedly reduced in mouse goblet cells, which instead exhibited elevated ConA binding, indicative of high-mannose enrichment. WGA and AAL binding were broadly elevated in human samples, particularly in the conjunctival epithelium, while ECA binding was more prominent in mouse cells, especially in limbal and corneal epithelial cells.

Observed glycocalyx differences in WGA staining between human and mouse cornea were further confirmed by lectin histochemistry (Fig. 4E). In human tissue, WGA staining was strongest in differentiated corneal epithelium, whereas staining in mouse cornea was minimal, consistent with the scGR-seq profiles. Conversely, SNA I staining was more prominent in the mouse corneal epithelium than in human, also in agreement with the scGR-seq data. Elevated ConA binding in mouse conjunctival goblet cells was likewise confirmed by lectin histochemistry, in stark contrast to PNA, which was broadly detected across the conjunctival epithelium (Fig. 4F). Together, these findings underscore pronounced interspecies variation in the distribution and abundance of glycocalyx features across ocular surface cell types.

Discussion

Once regarded as a passive surface coating, the glycocalyx is now recognized as a dynamic and complex structure that reflects and regulates cell identity, tissue function, and disease progression (25). This complexity arises from the underlying glycosylation machinery, which is tightly regulated within individual cells and gives rise to highly cell-type-specific glycan patterns. Yet, conventional glycomics approaches often mask this diversity by averaging signals across mixed populations. Emerging single-cell methods now allow glycosylation profiles to be resolved at the level of individual cells (7). In this study, we applied scGR-seq to the human ocular surface and found that glycocalyx signatures alone can distinguish cell types and maturation states. Moreover, interspecies comparisons revealed striking differences in glycocalyx composition, highlighting the importance of species context when evaluating glycan function in animal models.

Previous studies sought to characterize glycan structures at the ocular surface using techniques such as liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry (26). While informative, these bulk approaches failed to resolve cell-type-specific contributions and profiled a mixture of mature glycans and intracellular precursors. Lectin histochemistry has addressed this limitation in part by revealing differential glycan features between ocular surface epithelia. For instance, these analyses have shown that high-mannose and complex N-glycans are enriched in the central cornea and detected at lower levels in the conjunctival epithelium (27). However, this method lacks single-cell resolution within heterogeneous tissues, offers limited capacity for multiplexed glycan profiling, and provides minimal insight into glycocalyx composition when intracellular staining is present. Moreover, it does not permit inference of glycan biosynthetic pathways through glycogene expression. To address these limitations, we applied scGR-seq, a single-cell approach that integrates glycan-binding profiles with transcriptomic data, to map the glycocalyx composition and infer underlying biosynthetic pathways at the human and mouse ocular surface.

A key observation consistent with prior findings was the presence of distinct lectin-binding patterns that differentiate corneal from conjunctival epithelial cell lineages. In particular, corneal epithelial cells exhibited an enrichment of N-glycans within their glycocalyx. Although overall associations between lectin binding and glycosylation pathway activity scores in our study were weak, N-glycan abundance in the cornea correlated with higher expression of the oligosaccharyltransferase complex, a central enzyme in N-glycosylation. While the biological implications of this glycosylation pattern remain to be fully defined, studies in C. elegans have shown that the oligosaccharyltransferase complex is essential for maintaining endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis and regulating host defense against pathogenic bacteria (28)—functions that may similarly contribute to preserving corneal clarity. Another notable observation concerned goblet cells, a specialized secretory population within the conjunctival epithelium responsible for producing soluble mucins that maintain ocular surface hydration. We identified a distinctive glycocalyx signature that distinguishes goblet cells from other ocular surface epithelial types, characterized by a high abundance of Gal(β1-3)GalNAc epitopes on their surface. While previous studies reported PNA staining localized to goblet cell mucous granules (29), our findings demonstrate that this glycan structure is also a prominent component of the goblet cell surface glycocalyx.

Dynamic remodeling of glycosylation during corneal epithelial differentiation is reflected not only in the cell-type-specific expression of glycosyltransferases between basal and apical layers (30) but also in the reduced fucosylation of the limbal stem cell glycocalyx, a feature critical for preserving replicative potential (21). Here, we further demonstrate that the glycocalyx serves as an indicator of differentiation status in the cornea. Limbal progenitor cells exhibited strong binding of SNA I, consistent with its use as a stemness marker in other tissues (31). SNA I recognizes α2,6-sialylated LacNAc structures (32), a modification predominantly catalyzed on N-glycans by ST6GAL1 and ST6GAL2 (33) and associated with pluripotent and progenitor states (34, 35). Although low overall glycogene expression in limbal progenitor cells and the limited sequencing depth likely precluded definitive detection of these two enzymes, pseudotime analysis identified B4GALT1 as differentially expressed along the differentiation trajectory. While B4GALT1 does not directly catalyze α2,6-sialylation, it is essential for generating the LacNAc substrate that is subsequently modified and recognized by SNA I (36). As differentiation progressed, WGA binding increased—reflecting greater presentation of terminal N-acetylglucosamine and sialic acid—along with upregulation of GNE, a key enzyme in sialic acid biosynthesis. Pseudotime analysis also revealed increased expression of ST6GALNAC1 and ST6GALNAC6 in differentiated cells, suggesting a shift toward α2,6-sialylation of O-glycans, a modification highly abundant on membrane-bound mucins expressed by superficial epithelial layers (37). These findings underscore the dynamic nature of the glycocalyx during corneal epithelial differentiation, highlighting regulated changes in sialic acid structures and supporting its utility both as a biomarker of cell state and a target for investigating limbal stem cell biology.

Glycans display remarkable structural diversity across species, shaped by evolutionary pressures that influence tissue-specific functions; however, the full extent of glycan diversity in nature remains poorly understood (38). This variation is particularly evident at mucosal surfaces, such as the eye, where the glycocalyx plays a critical role in barrier function and epithelial homeostasis. Comparative glycomic analyses of ocular mucins from humans, dogs, and rabbits further underscore this divergence, showing that human mucins are predominantly sialylated, whereas those from rabbits and dogs are enriched in neutral or fucosylated glycans (39). More recently, lectin microarray profiling revealed species-specific glycan repertoires in mouse and human ocular surfaces, although this approach lacked the resolution to distinguish individual cell types (40). Extending these findings, our single-cell analysis uncovered additional interspecies divergence in glycocalyx organization at the cellular level. While DSA binding appeared largely conserved, most lectins exhibited species-specific patterns. Notably, human goblet cells displayed strong reactivity with MPA and PNA, consistent with an abundance of core 1 O-glycans, whereas these signals were markedly reduced in mouse goblet cells, which instead showed elevated ConA binding, indicative of high-mannose N-glycans. WGA and AAL binding were broadly increased in human samples, particularly within the conjunctival epithelium, suggesting enhanced presentation of terminal N-acetylglucosamine, sialic acid, and fucose. By contrast, ECA binding was more pronounced in mouse cells, reflecting potential differences in Galβ1-4GlcNAc structures and terminal galactosylation.

In conclusion, this study defines the diversity of the ocular surface glycocalyx and identifies cell-type-specific glycan signatures linked to epithelial identity and differentiation. These findings provide a foundation for future studies on the functional roles of the glycocalyx in health and disease.

Materials and methods

Human tissue

All procedures involving human tissue were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Health Sciences Institutional Review Board at Tufts University. Human donor eye globes containing bulbar conjunctiva were obtained from Saving Sight (Kansas City, MO). All specimens were fully deidentified and collected by Saving Sight under protocols that obtained informed consent. All donors tested negative for standard infectious disease serologies. The donor cohort included two females and one male with a mean age of 64.3 ± 4.7 years (range, 59–68 years). The average interval from death to tissue preservation was 5.2 ± 3.5 h, and tissues were maintained in organ culture for an average of 2.6 ± 0.6 days.

The bulbar conjunctiva was gently grasped with forceps and excised from the globe using scissors. The anterior segment was removed followed by careful dissection of the iris and ciliary body with a scalpel, after which the corneal–limbal region was excised. Corneal–limbal and conjunctival tissues were incubated separately at 37 °C for 2 h in DMEM/F12 containing 2.4 IU/mL Dispase II (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The epithelia were scraped, collected, and further digested with Gibco TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 5 min. The remaining stromal and conjunctival fragments were cut into small pieces, placed each in a MACS C tube (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) containing 4 mg/mL collagenase A (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in DMEM, and digested for 1 h at 37 °C using a gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). After digestion, single cells from all individual compartments were pooled, filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (Miltenyi Biotec), washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich), and counted. Cells were resuspended in cold 0.04% BSA–PBS, and cold methanol was added dropwise to a final concentration of 80%. Samples were incubated at −20 °C for 30 minutes before storage at −80 °C.

Mouse tissue

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (12–15 weeks old; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used in accordance with institutional guidelines and the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Following euthanasia, the conjunctiva and cornea were excised. The conjunctiva (bulbar and fornicial) was isolated by pinching with forceps and making an incision from the distal bulbar region to the palpebral conjunctiva using microscissors. The cornea, including the limbus, was dissected at its junction with the conjunctiva. Residual tissues were removed by peeling off the iris and scraping with a blade. Corneal tissues were then sectioned into smaller fragments to aid digestion.

Conjunctival and corneal tissues were digested separately in DMEM containing 2 mg/mL collagenase A and 10 mg/mL Dispase II at 37 °C for 1 h. During digestion, tissues were gently triturated by passing them through a 20G syringe needle five times every 20 min. Following enzymatic digestion, samples were rinsed with DMEM and incubated in TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 10 min at 37 °C. Cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer, washed in 0.04% BSA-PBS, and counted. Cells were resuspended in cold 0.04% BSA–PBS, and cold methanol was added dropwise to a final concentration of 80%. Samples were incubated at −20 °C for 30 min before storage at −80 °C.

Lectin-DNA conjugation

We developed a panel of DNA-barcoded lectins by adapting a previously established protocol (11). Lectins were conjugated to DNA oligonucleotides, each carrying a unique barcode sequence for precise identification and a capture sequence compatible with the 10x Genomics Next GEM kit. Unconjugated lectins—including AAL, Maclura pomifera agglutinin (MPA), Maackia amurensis agglutinin I (MAA I), Concanavalin A (ConA), Datura stramonium agglutinin (DSA), wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), peanut agglutinin (PNA), and Erythrina cristagalli agglutinin (ECA)—as well as biotinylated Sambucus nigra agglutinin I (SNA I), were obtained from Vector Laboratories (Newark, CA, USA). DNA oligonucleotides for lectin conjugation were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA). Each oligonucleotide was designed according to 10x Genomics specifications to generate custom conjugates containing the following elements: a “Capture sequence 1” (GCTTTAAGGCCGGTCCTAGCAA), a “Feature Barcode” (selected from the 10x whitelist), ten random nucleotides, and a “TruSeq Read 2” sequence (GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT). All DNA oligos were capped at the 5′ end with an azide group (Fig. S8A).

Lectin conjugation to oligonucleotides was performed as previously described (11). For each lectin, 50 μg of protein (1 mg/mL in PBS) was incubated with a 5-fold molar excess of dibenzocyclooctyne–polyethylene glycol-4-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester (DBCO-PEG4-NHS ester; Click Chemistry Tools, Scottsdale, AZ, USA) at room temperature for 1 h. Excess DBCO-PEG4-NHS ester was quenched by adding 10 μL of 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0). To remove excess linker, lectins were dialyzed against PBS using Tube-O-DIALYZER Medi with an 8 kDa molecular weight cutoff (St. Louis, MO, USA) for 2 h at room temperature followed by overnight dialysis at 4 °C. Lectins were then concentrated using 10 kDa Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter tubes (Sigma-Aldrich). A 3-fold molar excess of 5′-azide-modified DNA oligonucleotide was added to 50 μL of DBCO-modified lectin and incubated overnight at 4 °C to facilitate conjugation via click chemistry. To remove unbound oligonucleotides and preserve sugar-binding activity, DNA-barcoded lectins were purified by affinity chromatography using columns specific to each lectin. AAL was purified using fucose-immobilized Separopore 4B-CL resin (Bioworld, Dublin, OH, USA) prepared as described in Odaka et al. (11). MPA, MAA I, ConA, DSA, and WGA were purified using commercially available fetuin-immobilized agarose beads (Sigma-Aldrich). PNA and ECA were purified using asialofetuin-immobilized agarose beads prepared with AminoLink Plus Coupling Resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

The beads were loaded into Micro Bio-Spin chromatography columns (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Conjugated lectins were dissolved in up to 200 µL of Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and added to the preloaded columns. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature with occasional gentle mixing to enhance binding efficiency. After incubation, the columns were opened, and the flow-through was collected. Beads were then washed 10 times with 1 mL of DPBS. To elute the bound lectin–oligonucleotide conjugates, the columns were transferred to 2 mL collection tubes and eluted four times using a sugar solution specific to each lectin. Elution buffers were prepared in DPBS and included: 0.5 M galactose (MPA, PNA), 0.5 M lactose (ECA, MAA I), 0.2 M fucose (AAL), 0.2 M mannose (ConA), and a 1:1 (v/v) dilution of chitin hydrolysate (Vector Laboratories) for DSA and WGA.

Eluted lectins were subsequently dialyzed and concentrated using Tube-O-DIALYZER Medi and Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters, respectively. The purified DNA-barcoded lectins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE (Fig. S8B). Protein and DNA concentrations were determined using the Quant-iT Protein Assay Kit and the Quant-iT OliGreen ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively.

Flow cytometry

To assess potential steric hindrance among selected lectins, we performed flow cytometry using a telomerase-immortalized human corneal limbal epithelial (HCLE) cell line (41). Cells were cultured in keratinocyte serum-free medium (KSFM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 25 μg/mL bovine pituitary extract, 0.2 ng/mL EGF, and 0.4 mM CaCl2. Upon reaching ∼80% confluency, cells were detached with TrypLE (10 min, 37 °C), washed, and fixed on ice by gradually adding cold methanol to a final concentration of 80% for 10 min. Fixed cells were washed three times with PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Fluorescein-conjugated and unconjugated AAL, ConA, and DSA were obtained from Vector Laboratories. Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with increasing concentrations of individual fluorescent lectins to determine optimal labeling conditions, or with a single concentration of a mixture containing both unconjugated and fluorescent lectins, as specified (Fig. S9A and B). After washing with 1% BSA–PBS, samples were analyzed on a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and data were processed using FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR). Steric hindrance was evaluated by comparing staining intensities between lectins used individually and in combination; comparable signals indicated minimal interference under these conditions.

Single-cell glycan and RNA sequencing

Single-cell suspensions (1 mL, ∼106 cells/mL) were thawed and washed in wash-resuspension buffer containing 3× SSC, 1 mM DTT, 0.04% ultrapure BSA, and 0.2 U/μL RNase inhibitor. Cells were incubated on ice for 30 min with a mixture of DNA-labeled lectins and biotinylated SNA I, each at 0.5 μg/mL (equivalent to 0.05 μg of lectin per million cells). After three washes in wash-resuspension buffer, cells were incubated with TotalSeq-B0975 Streptavidin (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) at 0.5 μg/mL (0.05 μg per million cells) for 30 min on ice. Cells were then washed three times, counted, and adjusted to a final concentration of ∼1,000 cells/μL.

Single cells were captured using the Chromium Controller, and single-cell RNA sequencing libraries were prepared with the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ v3.1 Kit and the 3′ Feature Barcode Kit (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions to generate both the gene expression and cell surface feature libraries. For sequencing, gene expression and cell surface feature libraries from each sample were pooled at a 4:1 ratio and sequenced using either the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 or NovaSeq X Plus platforms, targeting 20,000 reads per cell.

Human data processing and cell annotation

Raw sequencing data were aligned, filtered, and normalized using Cell Ranger (v7.2.0, 10x Genomics). The resulting read count matrix was imported into R (v4.4.1) and converted to a Seurat object using the Seurat package (v5.1). Cells with fewer than 300 detected genes, fewer than 1,000 total reads, or more than 10% mitochondrial reads were excluded. Doublets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder package (v2.0.4). Datasets from individual samples were then merged, and gene expression was normalized using the LogNormalize method. The top 2,000 variable genes were selected using the FindVariableFeatures function and scaled using ScaleData.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these variable genes, and datasets were integrated using Harmony to minimize batch effects across samples. The number of principal components (PCs) used for downstream analysis was determined using the ElbowPlot function. A k-nearest neighbor graph was constructed using the FindNeighbors function based on the first 40 PCs. Cluster stability across different resolutions was assessed using the clustree package to guide resolution selection. Cell clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 1.0, resulting in 23 clusters. Cell populations were visualized by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) using the RunUMAP function on the first 40 PCs.

Differentially expressed genes between clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes expressed in at least 25% of the cells, with an average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) ≥ 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant markers (Dataset S1). Clusters were annotated based on the presence of cell-type-specific markers previously reported (42–46).

Clusters 8 and 9 both expressed the limbal marker KRT15, but cluster 8 additionally expressed KRT14, TP63, MMP10, and SLC6A6, and showed higher levels of basal cell markers LAMA3 and S100A2, supporting its annotation as limbal progenitor cells (LPC). In contrast, cluster 9 showed lower expression of LAMA3 and S100A2 and was classified as limbal suprabasal (Limb. supra).

Cluster 14 expressed proliferation-associated genes (MKI67, BIRC5, UBE2C, and TOP2A) and was identified as a proliferative population. It was further subclustered into three groups (Fig. S1B). Two subclusters expressed KRT4, KRT7, and KRT13, consistent with a conjunctival proliferative identity. The third subcluster expressed GJB6, KRT3, and KRT12, and was classified as corneal transient amplifying cells (TAC).

Clusters 5, 10, and 11 expressed GJB6, KRT3, and KRT12, with cluster 10 additionally expressing the corneal superficial marker KRT24; all were annotated as corneal epithelium (Cornea ep.). Clusters 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12, and 13 expressed conjunctival markers (KRT13, KRT4, KRT7, AQP5, LCN2, and CEACAM7) and were classified as conjunctival epithelium (Conj. Ep), together with the conjunctival proliferative cells.

Cluster 16 expressed MUC5AC, SPDEF, ATP2C2, TFF1, and TFF3, and was annotated as goblet cells (Goblet). Cluster 15 expressed melanocyte-associated genes (TYRP1, PMEL, MLANA, TYR, and DCT) and was annotated as melanocytes (Mela). Cluster 18 expressed FBLN1, COL1A1, DCN, LUM, and TWIST2, consistent with fibroblast identity (Fibro).

Clusters 20 and 22 expressed markers of blood vessels (ACKR1, POSTN, and SELE) and lymphatic vessels (CCL21 and LYVE1), respectively, and both shared vascular endothelial markers (PECAM1, MMRN1, and CLDN5); accordingly, they were categorized as vascular (Vasc). Clusters 19 and 21 expressed immune cell markers, including CCL3 (monocytes/macrophages) and CD3D (T cells), respectively. Both also expressed PTPRC, CYTIP, and FYB1, supporting their classification as immune cells (Immune).

Cluster 7 did not express any established ocular surface markers. Its top five differentially expressed genes were mitochondrial (MT-CO1, MT-CO2, MT-CO3, MT-ATP6, and MT-ND4), suggesting that it represented a population of damaged or stressed cells. This cluster was excluded from further analysis.

Mouse data processing and cell annotation

The processing of mouse single-cell RNA sequencing data followed the same approach as described above for human samples. Filtering criteria were adjusted for mouse samples, with cells excluded if they had fewer than 300–500 detected genes (depending on the sample), fewer than 700–1,000 total reads, more than 50,000 total reads, or more than 2–5% mitochondrial reads. The more stringent mitochondrial threshold applied to mouse samples (2–5%) compared to human samples (10%) reflects the fact that the average mitochondrial proportion in single-cell RNA sequencing data is significantly higher in human tissues than in mouse tissues (47). Doublets were identified and removed using the DoubletFinder package. Gene expression was normalized, and the top 3,000 variable genes were selected using the FindVariableFeatures function.

PCA was performed on the variable genes, and datasets were integrated using Harmony to minimize batch effects. A k-nearest neighbor graph was constructed using the FindNeighbors function based on the first 40 PCs. Cell clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 0.8, resulting in 22 clusters. Cell populations were visualized by UMAP using the RunUMAP function on the first 40 PCs.

Differentially expressed genes between clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes expressed in at least 25% of the cells, with an average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) ≥ 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant markers (Dataset S2). Clusters were annotated based on the presence of cell-type-specific markers previously reported (48–50).

Clusters 2 and 1 both expressed the limbal progenitor markers Krt15, Krt14, Trp63, and Gpha2, supporting its annotation as limbal progenitor cells (LPC). Clusters 5 and 12 expressed proliferation-associated genes (Mki67, Top2a, Pcna, Dut, and Hells) and was identified as corneal transient amplifying cells (TAC).

Clusters 0, 16, 4, and 8 expressed Krt12 and Slurp1, with clusters 4 and 8 additionally expressing the corneal suprabasal markers Cdkn1a and Dsg1a; all were annotated as corneal epithelium (Cornea ep.). Clusters 19, 10, 7, 3, 11, 13, and 21 expressed conjunctival markers (Krt13, Krt4, Krt19, Muc4, and Muc20) and were classified as conjunctival epithelium (Conj. Ep).

Cluster 14 expressed Muc5ac, Muc5b, and Spdef, and was annotated as goblet cells (Goblet). Clusters 6, 9, and 18 expressed fibroblast-associated genes (Vim, Dcn, and Lum). Cluster 9 expressed Kera and was annotated as keratocytes (Kera). Cluster 18 expressed Col8a1, Tagln, and Acta2, and was identified as myofibroblasts (Myofibro), leaving cluster 6 annotated as fibroblasts (Fibro).

Cluster 17 expressed markers of vascular endothelial cells (Vim, Pecam1) and was categorized as vascular (Vasc). Clusters 15 and 20 expressed immune cell markers, including Cd74, and H2-Aa (monocytes/macrophages) and Ccl5, Cd3g, and Cd69 (T cells), respectively. Both also expressed Ptprc supporting their classification as immune cells (Immune).

Lectin-based clustering

To recluster cells based on DNA-conjugated lectin profiles, unbiased clustering was performed using Seurat, setting “ADT” as the default assay. Data were normalized using the centered log-ratio (CLR) method (NormalizeData, method = “CLR,” margin = 2). The data were then scaled using the ScaleData function, and variable features were identified. Linear dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch correction with Harmony, grouping by original sample identity. UMAP was applied to the first seven PCs for visualization. Cell–cell relationships were computed using the FindNeighbors function using the first seven PCs, and clustering was performed using the FindClusters function at a resolution of 0.2.

To determine how annotated cell types were distributed across lectin-based clusters, the frequency and proportion of each cell type within each lectin-based cluster were calculated. A Sankey diagram was generated using the ggalluvial and ggplot2 packages to visualize the flow between transcriptome-based and lectin-based clusters. To assess which cell types predominated within each lectin cluster, differentially expressed genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes were filtered to include those expressed in at least 50% of cells per cluster and with an average log2 fold change (avg_log2FC) ≥ 0.5. For visualization purposes, the top 15 genes per cluster, ranked by adjusted P-value, were retained for display.

Lectin binding and correlation with glycosylation pathways

To assess differences in lectin binding across epithelial cell clusters, we subset the dataset to include only epithelial populations: LPC, TAC, Limb. supra, Cornea ep, Conj. ep, and Goblet. Differentially expressed lectin features within these clusters were identified using the FindAllMarkers function on the “ADT” assay, considering lectins with an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and a log2 fold change > 0.25 as significant. The relationship between lectin binding and glycosylation pathways was investigated by calculating module scores for glycosylation pathways using the AddModuleScore function, with pathway gene sets adapted from the GlycoEnzOnto pathway enrichment set (51). Only pathways with well-defined structural outputs relevant to our lectin panel were retained to ensure meaningful interpretation, while categories not recognized by the lectins (e.g. glycosaminoglycan pathways) or overly broad terms (e.g. “glycan biosynthetic pathway”) were replaced with more specific pathways (e.g. O_glycosylation and N_glycan_initiation). Spearman's rank correlation was then performed between pathway module scores and normalized “ADT” expression levels, with significant correlations defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute correlation coefficient (|ρ|) >0.1.

Pseudotime analysis of the corneal–limbal lineage

Cells from corneal–limbal clusters (LPC, TAC, Limb. supra, and Corneal ep) were subsetted and reclustered using either DNA-conjugated lectin features or gene expression to explore glycocalyx and transcriptomic variation, respectively, along the differentiation trajectory. For lectin-based analysis, data were normalized using the NormalizeData function with the “CLR” method and scaled using ScaleData, following the same procedure applied to the full dataset. For gene expression-based analysis, the standard Seurat log-normalization pipeline was applied. In both cases, linear dimensional reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch correction and integration with Harmony, grouping cells by their original sample identity to account for potential batch-related variation. UMAP was then applied using the first seven PCs.

Pseudotime analysis was conducted using the Monocle3 package (v1.3.7). A cell data set was generated from the Seurat object using the as.cell_data_set function from the SeuratWrappers package (v0.3.5). Size factors were estimated with estimate_size_factors to normalize expression values and account for differences in sequencing depth across cells. UMAP coordinates from the Seurat object were retained and assigned to the Monocle3 cell data set. Cells were clustered and partitioned using cluster_cells, and a trajectory graph was constructed using learn_graph. Root nodes were selected to order cells along pseudotime.

To identify genes and glycocalyx features that varied significantly along pseudotime, the graph_test function was used. This function fits a generalized additive model (GAM) to each feature, using pseudotime as the independent variable, and tests whether expression changes significantly along the trajectory. Features with a q-value < 0.05 were considered significantly associated with pseudotime progression.

RNAscope

The RNAscope HiPlex Assay (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA) was performed according to the ACD Bio protocol. Human corneas were freshly embedded in Tissue-Plus O.C.T. Compound (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), frozen, and sectioned at 10 μm thickness using a CM3050 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA). Sections were stored at −80 °C and used within a maximum of 3 months. Prior to hybridization, frozen sections were fixed in freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature and dehydrated through sequential 5-min incubations in 50, 70, and 100% ethanol. Sections were then treated with Protease IV for 30 min at room temperature.

Target-specific mRNA probes were hybridized for 2 h at 40 °C. Consecutive sections were incubated with either positive control probes targeting human housekeeping genes or negative control probes, as recommended by the manufacturer. Signal amplification was performed for 30 minutes at 40 °C, followed by fluorophore labeling for 15 min at 40 °C. Sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted using Fluoromount-G Aqueous Mounting Medium (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were acquired using a Leica THUNDER Imager (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA).

Lectin histochemistry

Ocular tissues were embedded in O.C.T. Compound, frozen, and sectioned at 10 μm thickness. For lectin fluorescence staining, tissue sections were blocked in 5% BSA in DPBS for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with either fluorescein-conjugated DSA (2 µg/mL), biotinylated SNA (1 µg/mL), rhodamine-conjugated WGA (5 µg/mL) (Vector Laboratories), fluorescein-conjugated ConA (1 µg/mL), or biotinylated PNA (1 µg/mL). When biotinylated lectins were used, sections were washed three times with DPBS and incubated for 1 h with Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated streptavidin. PNA and anti-MUC5AC co-staining, sections were similarly blocked in 5% BSA in DPBS for 1 h, followed by incubation with biotinylated PNA (1 µg/mL; Vector Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature. After washes with DPBS, sections were permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 for 20 min and incubated with anti-MUC5AC antibody (clone 45M1; 1:500 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sections were then washed three times with DPBS and incubated for 1 h with species-appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 and with fluorescein-conjugated streptavidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After staining, slides were washed in DPBS and mounted with FluoroMount-G containing DAPI (Invitrogen). Imaging was performed using a THUNDER Imager. For comparative analysis between human and mouse tissues, lectin staining was performed in parallel using matched protocols, and imaging was acquired under identical intensity and exposure time settings.
