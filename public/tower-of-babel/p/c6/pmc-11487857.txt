Guardians under pressure, a spotlight on hypertension among healthcare workers in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of Congo: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rodrigue Khonde, Aline Labat, Joël Konde, Guillaume Kiyombo, Yves Coppieters
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is one of the main risk factors for cardiovascular disease impacting over a billion people worldwide. Work environment factors could adversely affect workers’ cardiovascular health, including contributing to hypertension. Healthcare workers who treat patients are also affected. In the Democratic Republic of Congo, limited studies explored the work environment factors associated with hypertension. This study aimed to examine hypertension prevalence and determine the associated risk factors among healthcare workers in Kinshasa.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Kinshasa’s healthcare facilities from December 2023 to January 2024 among healthcare workers selected by multistage stratified random sampling. Data was collected through a structured questionnaire using a modified WHO STEPwise approach and Karasek questionnaire. Anthropometric parameters, blood pressure, and fasting blood sugar were measured. The prevalence of hypertension was assessed. All sociodemographic, occupational, and lifestyle variables associated with hypertension were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis at the 5% significance level.ResultsThe study encompassed 614 participants, comprising 55.2% females and 44.8% males. The mean age of participants was 38.8 ± 10.4 years, ranging from 20 to 78 years. The prevalence of hypertension was 22.6% and over half of those with hypertension (56.1%) were unaware of their condition. Of the known hypertensive participants before the study, 60.7% had uncontrolled blood pressure. In the multivariable analysis, identified risk factors for hypertension were age ≥ 40 years (aOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 1.64–4.61), seniority ≥ 10 years (aOR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.54–4.58), multiple job holding (aOR = 3.11, 95% CI: 1.81–5.34), job stress (aOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.17–2.89), physical inactivity (aOR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.03–2.68), overweight (aOR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.06–2.90) and obesity (aOR = 3.75, 95% CI: 2.10–6.70).ConclusionOur results underline an important prevalence of hypertension among healthcare workers in Kinshasa, despite their medical knowledge of the causes and the risks. A healthy lifestyle among healthcare workers is fundamental for ensuring the efficiency and productivity of the healthcare system. Regulating multiple job holding and implementing a hypertension prevention intervention encompassing their holistic support is crucial for reducing job stress and promoting well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11727-0.Keywords: Hypertension, Healthcare workers, Occupational health, Cross-sectional study, Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of Congo

Background

Hypertension (HTN) is one of the main modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease [1]. Worldwide, HTN is estimated to affect more than a billion people between the ages of 30 and 79 and contribute to more than 10 million avoidable premature deaths every year, with two-thirds living in low- and middle-income countries [2, 3]. According to projections, by 2025, the number of adults suffering from HTN will reach a total of 1.56 billion [4]. Although the prevalence of HTN has declined in high-income countries, we are facing an increase in low- and middle-income countries [5]. Another challenge is that a large proportion of the population suffering from that disease is undiagnosed, untreated, or inappropriately treated, contributing to increase the burden of HTN in low- and middle-income countries [6].

The known risk factors for HTN can be divided into two main traditional categories: modifiable or behavioral and non-modifiable. The modifiable factors include an unhealthy diet (with insufficient consumption of vegetables and fruits), a sedentary lifestyle, tobacco, and excessive alcohol consumption [3, 7]. Non-modifiable risk factors include sex, age, race/ethnicity, and genetic factors (family history) [5, 8]. In addition to these traditional factors, other occupational environmental factors are increasingly mentioned by several authors, including noise [9], job stress [10], and shift work [11].

Epidemiological studies of HTN in Africa have been conducted over the years to estimate the burden of HTN in this zone and have shown different prevalence rates in the general population and within specific groups of population [12]. Regarding the specific group of workers, this prevalence varies from one sector of activity to another. For example, in the industrial sector in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), 32.5% were reported [13], 34.5% in the banking sector in Congo [14], and 18.6% in the administration sector in Benin [15]. Within the health sector, 30.4% were reported in DRC [16] and 22.1% in Nigeria [17]. Such high prevalences within a sector well aware of the causes and consequences of HTN is questionable.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are all people who deliver care and services focused on improving health [18]. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), healthcare services are provided through both the public sector, covering 60%, and the private sector, accounting for 40% [19]. However, an undocumented proportion of public sector HCWs combine working both in public and private healthcare facilities, termed “dual practice” or “multiple job holding” [20]. Public sector employment is usually the “main” position to which HCWs should devote most of their working time. Regarding this dual practice, studies have predominantly focused on the healthcare system’s mechanisms to retain HCWs in the public sector and to enhance population access to healthcare, especially with universal health coverage [21]. Nonetheless, limited studies have investigated the adverse effects on the health of HCWs who are engaged in multiple workplaces with extended working hours, lacking a policy or comprehensive regulation overseeing this practice.

Studies estimating the prevalence of HTN among workers in DRC were mainly in non-healthcare sectors, such as industry [13], administration [22], and education [23]. To our knowledge, a limited number of these studies have assessed the burden of HTN among HCWs, with all known studies conducted at a single healthcare facility, especially in the southeast of the DRC [16, 24]. As reported in several studies, work environment factors are associated with hypertension, with specific factors varying by sector according to the conditions workers encounter, such as noise and job stress. Workers’ health has a major influence on workplace productivity. Identifying and characterizing modifiable risk factors for HTN is important for clinical medicine and public health in the primary prevention framework [25].

For HCWs to perform in their role, they need to be healthy. The well-being of HCWs and the need for healthy working conditions for their professional and social development align with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 3 & 8) embraced by countries, including the DRC. Having comprehensive data on prevalence and risk factors among HCWs would enable the planning and implementation of targeted interventions aimed at preventing HTN within this specific group. Thus, the study aimed to estimate HTN prevalence and determine the associated risk factors among HCWs of Kinshasa in DRC.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted among selected HCWs in Kinshasa’s General Referral Hospitals (GRH) from December 2023 to January 2024.

Sampling methods and participants

Kinshasa has 35 health zones distributed across its 24 municipalities, each served by a GRH. These municipalities are further divided into four administrative districts (Fig. 1). The sample size was allocated proportionally across these four districts. The study participants included physicians, nurses, and midwives, selected through a multistage stratified random sampling approach. Initially, all 35 GRHs in Kinshasa’s 24 municipalities were identified and classified into the respective administrative districts. Subsequently, three municipalities were chosen randomly from each district. Within each selected municipality, the GRH was selected when there was only one or randomly chosen if there were two. Using comprehensive lists of HCWs from each healthcare facility, participants were randomly chosen through a systematic polling process.

Map of the study area in Kinshasa, DRC, 2024

The sample size was calculated based on an estimated prevalence (p) of 41.9% [22], a confidence level (t) set at 95% (standard value of 1.96), a 5% margin of error (m), a design effect of 1.5, with an additional 10% accounting for non-response. Inclusion criteria encompassed any HCW (physician, nurse, or midwife) involved in direct healthcare delivery, employed at a designated Kinshasa GRH who consented to participate. Exclusion criteria were any HCW unable to answer the questions due to disability and any HCW working solely in a private healthcare facility. The ratio was one physician to one nurse or midwife. In total, 614 HCWs from 12 GRHs across 12 municipalities of Kinshasa were included in the study.

Data collection and study variables

Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews using an electronic questionnaire adapted from the WHO STEPwise approach [26] and Karasek questionnaire [27], integrated into smartphones with the Open Data Kit (ODK) Collect application for direct data entry by trained interviewers (Supplementary material). By employing random sampling, recruitment bias was minimized, while non-response rates were reduced by the study’s interest, acceptable interview duration, and participants’ freedom to choose an appropriate survey time. Training sessions for interviewers and a pre-test for the validity and reliability of data collection tools helped reduce measurement bias. A threshold of 10% (60 HCWs) was chosen for conducting the pre-test. This pre-test was carried out among HCWs from two GRHs not involved in the main study.

The questionnaire comprised three distinct sections: the first included sociodemographic, occupational, and lifestyle information; the second and the third were dedicated to measures: anthropometric and blood pressure (BP) as well as fasting blood sugar (FBS).

The dependent variable was HTN. The independent variables were the following:

- Sociodemographic information included sex, age, educational level, and marital status (unmarried, comprising single, divorced and widowed; and living with a partner, including married and cohabiting).

- Occupational information covered occupational category (physician, nurse, or midwife), employment contract, seniority, night shifts in GRH per month, dual practice or multiple job holding, and job stress.

BP control was defined as normal BP at the time of measurement among hypertensive participants who were under treatment. The BP was measured three times at 2 to 3-minute intervals on the same arm with a digital BP monitor (OMRON X2 Basic), with the arm supported at heart level and the feet flat on the floor, after sitting at rest for at least 10 min. The SBP and DBP measurements (in mmHg) were the mean of three readings [29].

Statistical analysis

The software Stata version 17 was used to perform statistical analyses. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test. Continuous variables were reported using mean with standard deviation and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to explore the associated variables with HTN. The final model included all the sociodemographic, occupational, and lifestyle variables associated with HTN in the bivariate analyses. The best-fitting model selection criterion was a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test. The problem of multicollinearity was examined by testing the variance inflation factor (VIF). A VIF over 5 indicated the existence of multicollinearity. The odds ratio (OR) with a corresponding 95% confidence interval was reported to quantify the strength of the association. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

The cross-sectional version of the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist was employed for the study presentation [35].

Results

Participant characteristics and prevalence of hypertension

Initially, 641 HCWs were selected to participate in the study, including 288 physicians, 310 nurses, and 43 midwives. Of these, 21 did not consent and 6 were excluded due to missing data. Finally, a total of 614 HCWs were included in the study, of whom 279 were physicians, 296 nurses, and 39 midwives (Fig. 2).

Flow chart of participants included in the study, Kinshasa, DRC 2024 (n = 614)

Of 614 HCWs enrolled in the study, 55.2% were female and 44.8% were male. Three-fifths of participants (60.0%) were under 40 years, and the mean age was 38.8 ± 10.4 years ranging from 20 to 78 years. The majority of participants (93.3%) had a university degree, and living with a partner (56.3%) accounted for over half of all respondents.

Physicians (45.4%) and nurses (48.2%) were the main occupational categories, with the majority being hospital employees (permanent) (87.8%), and three-quarters having less than 10 years of seniority (73.6%). Over a third of them had multiple jobs (37.3%) ranging from 1 to 6 healthcare facilities. Almost half had at least four night shifts at the GRH per month (47.9%), and job stress was found in 36.2% of HCWs. Discussion

This study has examined the prevalence of HTN and its associated factors among HCWs in Kinshasa. The prevalence of HTN was 22.6%, and the sociodemographic, occupational, and lifestyle variables associated with HTN were age, seniority, multiple job holding, job stress, physical activity, overweight, and obesity.

Prevalence of hypertension

The prevalence of HTN in our study was close to those reported in some studies conducted among HCWs [17, 36]. Nevertheless, some studies have found a lower prevalence than ours [28, 37]. On the other hand, some authors reported a higher prevalence than what we found among HCWs [38, 39]. Various factors, including geographical location, study populations, and working conditions may explain these differences. In our study, the population can be qualified as young, only 40% had an age ≥ 40 years, among whom the prevalence of HTN was 39.2% (96/245).

Therefore, the prevalence of HTN might have been even higher if we had an older population. Such findings are alarming, especially in light of evidence suggesting that the most affected age group falls between 30 and 79 years [3]. One similarity between these two studies, which had a higher prevalence compared to ours, is that the healthcare workers were older, with mean ages of 45.2 ± 11.3 years in South Africa [38] and 42.3 ± 11 years in Nigeria [39].

Another study carried out among workers of the Multimodal Freight Management Office of the City of Kinshasa (DRC) reported a prevalence of 41.9%. However, the mean age of these workers was 45 ± 8.9 years [22].

More than half of HCWs were unaware they had HTN. This finding was higher than that reported in another study conducted among HCWs [38]. HTN is usually asymptomatic, sets in insidiously, and is frequently discovered at the complication stage. Without regular screening and early detection, the proportion of HCWs who don’t know they have HTN is unable to be reduced, and so prevention is not possible. Only a small proportion of HCWs, despite being aware of their hypertensive status, had a BP control. This result is close to a study carried out in South Africa [38]. It is really concerning to discover they don’t give big attention to the follow-up of their own health, given that HCWs are the major actors involved in preventive measures through the advice they provide to their patients, this is a worrying situation. This also means that if we have a high proportion of HCWs who are unaware that they have HTN, or who don’t have BP control, while they know HTN, there is a real threat of a higher proportion in the general population.

Association of hypertension with sociodemographic factors

In this study, the chance of being hypertensive was associated with older age (≥ 40 years). Similar findings have been reported elsewhere among HCWs in Brazil [36], mostly physicians and nurses in Angola [40], and among nurses in South Africa [38]. The risk of HTN was 2.75 times higher for ages ≥ 40 years. Older adults are physiologically more at risk of HTN due to progressive changes in their cardiovascular system (vascular remodeling, endothelial dysfunction, and arterial stiffness) [41]. BP is known to increase with age after midlife, contributing to the development of HTN in middle-aged and older adults [42]. Therefore, this age-related rise in BP is reported both among workers in other sectors as well as in the general population.

Association of hypertension with occupational factors

The seniority was associated with HTN. Findings show that HCWs ≥ 10 years of seniority were more likely to have HTN (2.65 times), compared to those less than 10 years. Seniority was also reported elsewhere as associated with HTN [43]. Possible explanations include that older people have also more seniority in the profession. However, after adjusting for age, this association still showed statistical significance. There is also the argument that they have been exposed for longer to working conditions that could adversely affect their cardiovascular health [10]. A study carried out among workers of an industrial group revealed that 90% of people with HTN had never changed jobs since they were hired [43].

The results showed that HCWs with job stress had an increased risk of HTN. This finding is consistent with several studies that have reported an association between job stress and the chance of developing HTN, especially among this specific group of HCWs [24, 44]. Frequent exposure to workplace stressors is believed to elevate neuroendocrine activity in the sympathetic nervous system (catecholamines) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (glucocorticoids). Consequently, prolonged exposure to job stress, combined with other risk factors, may induce endothelial dysfunction, vasoconstriction, cell proliferation, and inflammation, leading to arterial stiffness, HTN, and subsequent cardiovascular events [45]. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that individuals develop coping strategies when faced with stressors or situations perceived as stressful. Lazarus and Folkman describe coping as a set of cognitive and behavioral efforts individuals engage in to manage, reduce, or endure internal or external demands that exceed their resources [46]. However, this ability varies among individuals depending on their respective vulnerability levels.

HCWs with multiple job holding or dual practice were also more likely to be hypertensive. There is an increasing number of studies addressing multiple job holding among HCWs. These studies highlight the negative effects of practicing in different healthcare facilities at the same period, resulting in absenteeism, lack of time, and reduced interest in patient care in public healthcare facilities, in favor of private clinics where they earn more money [20]. Conversely, other studies have highlighted the potential benefits of this practice, not for the workers’ health or well-being but for the health system. Multiple job holding broadens the range of healthcare services available to the population and contributes to maintain underpaid workers in the public sector as they can supplement their income in the private sector [21].

While in small towns and villages, HCWs are less likely to have a dual practice [47], in big cities like Kinshasa, many HCWs are in dual practice to increase income and support their family’s needs, as mentioned elsewhere [48]. Hence, besides public healthcare facilities, HCWs perform their clinical activities in many private clinics. Within our study, we found HCWs practicing in up to six healthcare facilities. For general practitioners or nurses, this frequently leads to more night shifts in private clinics. In contrast, specialist physicians might perform medical consultations or surgery in two or three healthcare facilities in the same day. This leads to an increase in the number of workdays and work hours. Several studies have highlighted the higher risk of developing HTN among workers who have long working hours and short sleep duration [49, 50]. Moreover, Kinshasa, boasting 24 municipalities, presents a high urban density and traffic jams. To reach these multiple healthcare facilities, HCWs often have to commute long distances, exacerbating stress levels, a factor associated with HTN through the release of catecholamines.

The bivariate analysis revealed no difference between HCWs with a higher frequency of night shifts and those with fewer night shifts at the GRH. However, studies report that night shift workers, like HCWs, have a disrupted circadian rhythm and are known to have short sleep durations, as well as high consumption of coffee, hypnotic drugs, alcohol, and a sedentary lifestyle. These factors are associated with HTN [36]. Nonetheless, in the context of Kinshasa, general practitioners or nurses commonly opt to trade or monetize their night shifts at public healthcare facilities, which provide lower compensation, for shifts at private clinics, where compensation is higher or based on a fee-for-service model. As a result, HCWs may undertake fewer night shifts in a public healthcare facility while having a substantially higher number in a private clinic. This could explain the absence of difference in the chance of developing HTN between those with high and low monthly night shifts in public healthcare facilities.

Association of hypertension with lifestyle factors

Physical inactivity was associated with HTN. Strong evidence indicates a dose-response inverse relationship between physical activity and HTN in adults with normal BP. Physical activity reduces BP in adults with normal BP and HTN [51]. In the healthcare sector, several studies carried out, especially among nurses, reported that they spent a long duration of shifts sitting, a situation more pronounced during night shift work [52, 53]. Despite their knowledge and high level of awareness of the risk of a sedentary lifestyle, HCWs often fail to practice behaviors that promote and maintain good health. However, this is not only a problem of knowledge, the healthcare facilities as the workplace of the HCWs do not often emphasize activities to promote well-being at work.

In our study, the risk of HTN was 1.75 times higher in overweight and 3.75 times higher in obese individuals compared to those with normal weight. Several studies reported similar findings among HCWs [28, 36] and other occupational groups [5, 13]. Half of the HCWs had a high BMI (overweight or obese), which is likely attributed to an unhealthy lifestyle. Furthermore, studies have highlighted that insufficient sleep prolongs exposure to stress, increases appetite, raises serum total cholesterol levels, and promotes obesity, factors strongly associated with the development of HTN [36]. Additionally, BMI tends to rise with age [28]. Due to job constraints, job stress, and night shifts, HCWs tend to have irregular eating habits and engage in less physical activity.

The legal framework in the DRC requires companies to organize an occupational health service. When this is not feasible, they are required to form agreements with healthcare facilities for the treatment, screening, and prevention of diseases [54]. Although the DRC labor code refers to disease prevention for workers, there is a lack of occupational health services in public healthcare facilities. The very few healthcare facilities providing such services are private clinics. Healthcare facilities cannot promote healthy lifestyles and well-being in the workplace to their contracted companies as long as these aspects are not applied to HCWs as employees.

The health system in the DRC, as in many low- and middle-income countries, leans heavily towards curative care, primarily targeting infectious diseases. Given the ongoing epidemiological, demographic, and nutritional transitions in low- and middle-income countries, there is a growing need to shift focus towards non-communicable disease prevention and health promotion, particularly in the workplace where the most affected age group spends a substantial time. Evidence suggests a correlation between the health-promoting behaviors of HCWs and the preventive measures embraced by their patients [55, 56]. Consequently, preventing HTN among HCWs could serve as a key strategy for fostering a healthy lifestyle within the workplace and among the wider population.

Limitations

This study has some limitations worth noting. First, this study focused solely on physicians, nurses, and midwives, thus limiting the generalizability of the findings to all HCWs. Second, it only included HCWs who were present at the healthcare facilities (workplace) during the survey, which may introduce healthy worker bias and result in a lower prevalence of HTN. Third, HTN was diagnosed only by three times repeated measurements and diabetes was diagnosed based on a single FBS measurement, without the use of glycated hemoglobin, potentially leading to an underestimation or overestimation of HTN and/or diabetes cases in our study population. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the inference of causality.

Strengths

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable information that should be mentioned. First, this is the first study in Kinshasa conducted on a substantial sample of HCWs to assess the prevalence of HTN and its risk factors with a strong focus on occupational factors. Second, this study underscores its relevance by highlighting the lack of active health promotion strategies for workers, particularly for HCWs in the DRC within the workplace, leaving them vulnerable to non-communicable diseases. Furthermore, the dearth of data on this matter complicates the formulation of effective initiatives by policymakers. Consequently, this study presents baseline data that may serve to conduct a broader study among workers, especially HCWs, in the DRC.

Conclusion

Our study revealed a significant prevalence of HTN among HCWs in Kinshasa. A high proportion of them were unaware they had HTN. HTN was associated with various sociodemographic, occupational, and lifestyle factors, including age, seniority, job stress, multiple job holding, physical inactivity, overweight, and obesity.

A healthy lifestyle among HCWs is fundamental for ensuring the efficiency and productivity of the healthcare system. Implementing an HTN prevention intervention that encompasses holistic support for HCWs is crucial for reducing job stress and enhancing the well-being of HCWs in healthcare facilities. Regulating dual practice and improving government salaries for HCWs is imperative to mitigate the adverse effects of this phenomenon on their health.

Instituting a policy aimed to promote a healthy lifestyle and regular HTN screening among HCWs through occupational health services within healthcare facilities, with a preventive focus, can yield positive impacts on their health and the lifestyle of their patients.
