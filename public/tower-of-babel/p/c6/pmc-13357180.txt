ALK-positive large B-cell lymphoma presenting in the nasal cavity with CLTC::ALK fusion: a case report of alectinib-integrated therapy followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Qiong Yi, Xiuwen Chen, Lulu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive large B-cell lymphoma (ALK+ LBCL) is a rare and aggressive lymphoma for which no standard frontline treatment has been established. Presentation in the nasal cavity is particularly uncommon. We report the case of a 39-year-old man who presented with progressive left-sided nasal obstruction, epistaxis, and bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. Imaging revealed a left sinonasal/nasopharyngeal mass, bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy, diffuse skeletal hypermetabolism, markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and focal bone marrow involvement. Histopathologic examination showed a plasmablastic neoplasm with strong granular cytoplasmic ALK expression. The tumor cells were positive for CD138, MUM1, EMA, and kappa light chain, but negative for CD20, CD19, CD79a, CD3, CD30, CD56, and EBER. Fluorescence in situ hybridization confirmed ALK rearrangement, and RNA sequencing together with RT-PCR identified a CLTC::ALK fusion (CLTC exon 31::ALK exon 20). The patient was diagnosed with Ann Arbor stage IVB ALK+ LBCL. Disease progression after initial CVAD chemotherapy and persistent disease after CVAD-E prompted the addition of alectinib from cycle 3, with complete metabolic response on PET-CT after cycle 4. Consolidation with Hyper-CVAD plus concurrent alectinib was followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto-HSCT) and post-transplant alectinib maintenance. The patient remains in complete remission 10 months after diagnosis. This case illustrates the diagnostic value of integrated morphologic and molecular assessment in CD20-negative plasmablastic lymphoid neoplasms and suggests that incorporating ALK inhibition into multimodal therapy may be a reasonable strategy for selected patients with ALK+ LBCL.

Introduction

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive large B-cell lymphoma (ALK+ LBCL) is a rare and aggressive mature B-cell neoplasm recognized as a distinct entity in the current classification of lymphoid tumors (1, 2). It accounts for far less than 1% of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas and typically exhibits immunoblastic or plasmablastic morphology with a characteristic immunophenotype (3, 4), including expression of ALK, CD138, and MUM1, but frequent lack of conventional B-cell markers such as CD20 and CD79a (3, 5). The most common genetic abnormality is the CLTC::ALK fusion generated by t (2;17)(p23;q23), which is usually associated with a granular cytoplasmic ALK staining pattern (6). ALK+ LBCL most commonly involves lymph nodes, especially mediastinal and cervical nodal sites, whereas sinonasal presentation is distinctly uncommon (5, 7). Clinically, the disease is characterized by an aggressive course and historically poor outcomes with conventional chemotherapy alone (8). More recently, ALK-directed therapies have emerged as a rational treatment option in selected patients (9, 10), although the optimal timing, combination strategy, and duration of ALK inhibition remain undefined. Here, we report a case of ALK+ LBCL presenting with prominent sinonasal symptoms and harboring a molecularly confirmed CLTC exon 31::ALK exon 20 fusion. The patient showed an inadequate response to initial chemotherapy but subsequently achieved a complete metabolic response after incorporation of alectinib, followed by consolidation with intensive chemotherapy, auto-HSCT, and post-transplant alectinib maintenance. This case underscores the importance of integrated morphologic and molecular diagnosis and highlights the potential clinical relevance of ALK inhibitor-integrated therapy in this rare lymphoma.

Case presentation

A 39-year-old Chinese man presented in April 2025 with progressive left-sided nasal obstruction and intermittent blood-tinged nasal discharge of approximately 2 months’ duration. He also reported reduced olfaction on the left side. Approximately 20 days before admission, he noticed bilateral cervical masses associated with mild tenderness. His medical history was notable for psoriasis of more than 20 years’ duration, with no family history of malignancy. He denied fever and significant weight loss but reported night sweats. His Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status was 0. Physical examination revealed leftward deviation of the nasal septum and an exophytic mass in the posterior left nasal cavity extending into the nasopharynx. Multiple bilateral cervical lymph nodes were palpable, firm, poorly mobile, and ill defined.

Paranasal sinus computed tomography showed an irregular soft-tissue mass in the left nasal cavity protruding into the nasopharynx. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated scattered lesions involving the left maxillary sinus, nasal cavity, nasopharynx, and bilateral ethmoid sinuses, with the largest lesion measuring approximately 3.2 × 2.0 cm and showing marked heterogeneous enhancement. Multiple enlarged bilateral cervical lymph nodes were also identified, the largest measuring 3.4 × 2.2 cm (Figures 1A–C). Baseline PET-CT revealed hypermetabolic lesions in the bilateral ethmoid sinuses, left sphenoid sinus, left maxillary sinus, and posterior left nasal cavity (SUVmax 13.1–33.6), together with multiple bilateral cervical lymph nodes (SUVmax 2.1–28.3) and diffuse skeletal involvement affecting the bilateral humeri, clavicles, scapulae, sternum, ribs, vertebral bodies, pelvic bones, and bilateral femora (SUVmax 4.6–34.5) (Figures 1D–F). Serum lactate dehydrogenase was markedly elevated to 1619.5 U/L. Bone marrow trephine biopsy demonstrated focal infiltration by abnormal lymphoid cells.

Histologic examination of the left nasal cavity biopsy specimen showed diffuse infiltration by large atypical lymphoid cells with abundant basophilic cytoplasm, prominent nucleoli, and frequent mitotic figures (Figures 2A, B). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated strong granular cytoplasmic ALK expression (Figure 2C). The tumor cells were also positive for the plasmacytic markers CD138, MUM1, and EMA (Figures 2D–F), with kappa light-chain restriction (lambda negative). Ki-67 showed a high proliferation index of approximately 85% (Figure 2G). Pan-B-cell markers CD20 (Figure 2H), CD19, and CD79a were negative, as were CD3, CD30, and CD56. Epstein-Barr virus–encoded RNA in situ hybridization was negative (Figure 2I). ALK break-apart fluorescence in situ hybridization confirmed ALK rearrangement in 71% of tumor cells (Figure 2J). RNA sequencing and RT-PCR identified a CLTC::ALK fusion involving CLTC exon 31 and ALK exon 20, consistent with t (2;17)(p23;q23) (Figure 2K). RNA sequencing also generated low-confidence candidate IGHA1::FOXP1 and CD44::IGHA1 fusion calls; these lacked robust split-read or discordant-mate support and were not confirmed by targeted RT-PCR, and were therefore interpreted as unvalidated RNA-level findings rather than pathogenic fusion events. Based on the morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular findings, the patient was diagnosed with ALK+ large B-cell lymphoma, Ann Arbor stage IVB, with an International Prognostic Index score of 3, indicating high-intermediate risk.

The patient initially received CVAD chemotherapy consisting of cyclophosphamide, vindesine, liposomal doxorubicin, and dexamethasone. The clinical response was unsatisfactory, with progression of bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy 2 weeks after completion of cycle 1. Etoposide was therefore added in cycle 2 (CVAD-E), but palpable supraclavicular lymphadenopathy persisted, prompting the addition of alectinib (600 mg orally twice daily) from cycle 3 onward, which was continued throughout subsequent cycles. Although formal restaging imaging was not performed after cycle 3 and response could therefore not be formally classified according to Lugano criteria at that time point, supraclavicular lymphadenopathy remained palpable on clinical examination. Restaging PET-CT after cycle 4 demonstrated complete metabolic response (Deauville score 3), with marked shrinkage of the sinonasal lesion, substantial regression of cervical lymphadenopathy, and markedly decreased metabolic activity in previously involved skeletal sites (Figure 3). Repeat bone marrow aspiration and trephine biopsy performed at this time showed no morphologic or immunophenotypic evidence of residual lymphoma.

Representative axial fused PET/CT images demonstrate complete metabolic response (Deauville score 3) in (A) the sinonasal region, (B) bilateral cervical lymph nodes, and (C) a representative skeletal lesion.

After achieving complete metabolic response, the patient underwent consolidation with alternating Hyper-CVAD Part A and Part B, with alectinib continued at the same dose (600 mg twice daily) throughout consolidation. In October 2025, he proceeded to auto-HSCT after BEAM conditioning with melphalan, cytarabine, carmustine, and etoposide. Neutrophil engraftment was achieved on day +11. The only clinically significant treatment-related adverse event was grade 2 peripheral neuropathy during Hyper-CVAD Part A. Alectinib was held during the peri-transplant period and resumed as maintenance therapy at the same dose 3 months after auto-HSCT. At the latest follow-up on February 28th, 2026, approximately 10 months after diagnosis and 4 months after auto-HSCT, the patient remained in complete remission without clinical or radiologic evidence of recurrence. Discussion

ALK+ LBCL remains a diagnostically challenging and clinically aggressive lymphoma, particularly when it presents at unusual extranodal sites. In the sinonasal region, the absence of conventional B-cell markers together with plasmablastic differentiation may lead to diagnostic confusion with plasmablastic lymphoma, extramedullary plasmacytoma/plasma cell myeloma, or extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (5, 7). The nasal cavity case reported by Chen et al. (7) showed similar plasmablastic morphology, granular cytoplasmic ALK expression, plasma cell-associated marker expression, and loss of conventional B-cell markers. In contrast, our case showed more disseminated disease with skeletal and focal bone marrow involvement, exon-level CLTC::ALK confirmation, and treatment with an alectinib-integrated multimodal strategy followed by auto-HSCT and maintenance. In the present case, the combination of strong granular cytoplasmic ALK expression, CD138/MUM1/EMA positivity, kappa light-chain restriction, absence of pan-B-cell markers, and negative EBER supported the diagnosis of ALK+ LBCL. Meanwhile, EBER negativity was crucial in ruling out sinonasal NK/T-cell lymphoma, which is the most prevalent aggressive lymphoid malignancy at this anatomical site in Asian populations (11). These findings underscore the importance of incorporating ALK immunohistochemistry into the diagnostic evaluation of CD20-negative large B-cell or plasmablastic neoplasms arising at extranodal sites, especially when routine lineage markers are non-informative.

Beyond the histopathologic differential diagnosis, the anatomic assignment of primary site also warrants caution. The precise anatomic origin of the disease could not be established with certainty. Although the patient’s initial symptoms were sinonasal and imaging showed a prominent lesion in the nasal cavity/nasopharyngeal region, baseline PET-CT also demonstrated extensive skeletal disease, markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and focal bone marrow involvement, indicating disseminated high-burden disease at presentation. It is therefore more appropriate to regard the nasal cavity as the initial site of clinical manifestation rather than a definitively proven primary site. This interpretation is more consistent with the systemic biological behavior of ALK+ LBCL and avoids overstating the anatomic origin of the tumor.

A noteworthy aspect of this case is the complete molecular diagnostic chain from immunohistochemistry to exon-level fusion transcript identification. Initially, the distinctive granular cytoplasmic ALK staining pattern served as a critical morphologic clue to the presence of a CLTC::ALK fusion, effectively distinguishing it from the diffuse nucleocytoplasmic pattern characteristic of NPM::ALK fusions (4, 6). This impression was subsequently confirmed by FISH, RNA sequencing, and RT-PCR, which identified a CLTC exon 31::ALK exon 20 fusion transcript. Exon-level confirmation was important not only diagnostically but also therapeutically, as it demonstrated preservation of the ALK kinase domain and therefore provided a biologically plausible rationale for ALK-targeted therapy (12). At the mechanistic level, ALK fusion proteins are known to drive oncogenic signaling, including STAT3-dependent survival pathways, providing further biological support for ALK inhibition in this disease (12, 13).

From a therapeutic perspective, the initial use of CVAD should be viewed as an individualized institutional choice rather than a standard regimen for ALK+ LBCL. It was selected as a modified CHOP-like anthracycline-containing regimen for aggressive CD20-negative plasmablastic-type disease with high tumor burden, while CHOP-like or DA-EPOCH-like regimens remain more conventional backbones. The decision was also partly informed by dose-intensive strategies discussed for aggressive plasmablastic-type B-cell lymphomas, including plasmablastic lymphoma, although this extrapolation should be interpreted cautiously because ALK+ LBCL is a distinct entity. It should also be acknowledged that no frontline chemotherapy regimen has been demonstrated to be superior for ALK+ LBCL. In the multi-institutional series by Atallah-Yunes et al. (8), despite frontline anthracycline-based chemotherapy in nearly all patients and intensified regimens in nearly half, median event-free survival remained less than one year — underscoring that conventional approaches, whether standard or intensified, have yielded unsatisfactory results in this disease. Because upfront ALK inhibition was not guideline-established and alectinib use was off-label, requiring approval/access arrangements and drug procurement, cytoreductive chemotherapy was started first; alectinib was added from cycle 3 after progression/inadequate response. Published ALK inhibitor-treated cases are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 and suggest meaningful activity of next-generation ALK inhibitors mainly in refractory settings (9, 10). In the Atallah-Yunes cohort (8), among 12 patients who received ALK inhibitors, alectinib was associated with more durable responses than crizotinib, supporting cautious integration of next-generation agents while underscoring the need for additional evidence (8). Earlier incorporation could also have been reasonable, and the optimal timing remains undefined. Finally, the complete metabolic response cannot be attributed solely to alectinib, as etoposide was added in cycle 2 and the patient subsequently received Hyper-CVAD consolidation and auto-HSCT; it should therefore be interpreted as the result of sequential multimodal therapy.

The subsequent use of Hyper-CVAD consolidation, auto-HSCT, and post-transplant alectinib maintenance was selected as an individualized strategy to deepen remission and maintain ALK suppression. This strategy is broadly consistent with the report by Wang et al. of sustained remission after alectinib-containing chemotherapy, auto-HSCT, and maintenance therapy (14). In addition, isolated experience from other ALK-rearranged lymphomas (15), such as ALK+ anaplastic large cell lymphoma, suggests that post-transplant alectinib maintenance may be feasible in selected settings, but no intensive consolidation regimen or maintenance strategy has demonstrated proven superiority in ALK+ LBCL. Therefore, this approach should not be interpreted as a generalizable standard.

The regimen was tolerable in this patient, with grade 2 peripheral neuropathy as the only clinically significant adverse event. Nevertheless, the feasibility, durability, and optimal duration of post-transplant ALK inhibitor maintenance require longer follow-up and additional cases. Beyond treatment-related considerations, the patient’s long-standing psoriasis also merits brief comment. Although psoriasis has been epidemiologically associated with an increased risk of lymphohematologic malignancies, particularly cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, no evidence specifically links psoriasis to ALK+ LBCL. Therefore, no causal inference can be drawn from the present case (16, 17).

This report also has several limitations. First, the follow-up duration remains relatively short — ten months from diagnosis and four months after auto-HSCT — which is insufficient to draw meaningful conclusions regarding the long-term survival benefit of this intensified regimen or the durability of remission; continued follow-up is required before such assessments can be made. Second, comprehensive somatic mutation profiling by targeted DNA panel sequencing or whole-exome sequencing was not performed, which limited a more complete molecular interpretation of the treatment response. Accordingly, potentially relevant co-occurring alterations involving TP53, MYC, STAT3 pathway components, or other lymphoma-related genes could not be assessed, limiting interpretation of the molecular basis of chemotherapy resistance and treatment response. Third, no international consensus currently exists regarding the optimal duration of post-transplant alectinib maintenance, and the appropriate timing of discontinuation in this patient will need to be guided by ongoing molecular monitoring and long-term clinical follow-up.

In summary, we report a case of ALK+ LBCL presenting with prominent sinonasal symptoms, extensive skeletal and bone marrow involvement, and a molecularly confirmed CLTC::ALK fusion. After an inadequate response to initial chemotherapy, the patient achieved a complete metabolic response following the incorporation of alectinib and has remained in remission after auto-HSCT and post-transplant alectinib maintenance. This case highlights the diagnostic value of integrated morphologic and molecular assessment in ALK+ LBCL and supports further investigation of ALK inhibitor-integrated treatment strategies in this rare and aggressive lymphoma.
