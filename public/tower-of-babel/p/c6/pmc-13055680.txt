A New Era of Cardiothoracic Transplantation in Adults With Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Sofia Hu, Aravind Krishnan, Danielle M Mullis, Elbert Heng, Stefan Elde, Alyssa C Garrison, Daniel Alnasir, Alexander R Berg, Nataly M Vargas, Christiane Haeffele, George Lui, Y Joseph Woo, John W MacArthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing number of children with congenital heart disease survive to adulthood and require heart transplantation. This study evaluates the trends and outcomes of cardiothoracic transplantation for patients with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) and describes our institution's 3 decades of experience.MethodsWe identified all patients with ACHD who underwent first‐time cardiothoracic transplantation at our institution between December 1987 and December 2024. We compared characteristics and outcomes between a historical and contemporary cohort of transplant recipients.ResultsIn total, 196 patients with ACHD underwent cardiothoracic transplantation. The annual rate of cardiothoracic transplantation in patients with ACHD doubled after 2018. The first era of heart transplantation in patients with ACHD from 1987 to 2018 is defined predominantly by heart–lung transplantation, most commonly for isolated septal defect. The second era since 2018 is defined by sicker, more complex patients. Following the United Network for Organ Sharing heart allocation policy change, recipients were older, underwent more prior cardiac surgeries, were more likely to be hospitalized, and were more likely to have single‐ventricle anatomy with Glenn/Fontan palliation. Comparing the 2 eras, 1‐year survival rates for all patients with ACHD were comparable (81% versus 81%, P=0.939) and improved for heart‐only transplant recipients (72.5% versus 90.7%, P=0.066).ConclusionsThe number and complexity of patients with ACHD undergoing cardiothoracic transplantation has grown, with a significant increase in patients who previously underwent palliative surgeries. With increased use of medical and surgical adjuncts, excellent long‐term survival for transplant recipients is achievable.Keywords: adult congenital heart disease, heart transplantation, heart–lung transplantation, single ventricleSubject Categories: Congenital Heart Disease, Transplantation

An increasing number of children with congenital heart defects are surviving to adulthood. 1 Surgical and medical advances have significantly improved life expectancy for these patients, but many still experience long‐term complications related to their congenital conditions, including heart failure, arrhythmias, residual cyanosis, and valvular dysfunction. As a result, many adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) eventually require heart transplantation, 2 , 3 including multiorgan transplantation. 4 , 5

Heart transplantation in patients with ACHD presents unique challenges due to the complex nature of their underlying anatomy, prior surgical history, and presence of other comorbidities, such as pulmonary hypertension, high rates of liver and renal dysfunction, and high levels of antibody sensitization. 6 , 7 Multiple medical and surgical innovations have led to improved survival of patients with congenital heart defects into adulthood. This includes palliative surgical procedures such as the Glenn and Fontan operations, which have led to an increase in the number of palliated single ventricles. These patients are medically and surgically complex and require specialized, multidisciplinary care. Recognizing the unique status of patients with ACHD, the United Network for Organ Sharing designated a separate listing status to patients with ACHD on October 18, 2018. 8 , 9

The rapidly evolving landscape of heart and multiorgan transplantation in this cohort of patients has not been well described. We evaluate the trends and characteristics of patients with ACHD who underwent cardiothoracic transplantation at our institution.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review under Protocol Number 71797 on September 28, 2023. The need for individual patient consent was waived due to the minimal risk posed to patients. The anonymized data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Data Source

Patient‐level data were collected through manual chart review and included detailed medical history, history of congenital defects and associated surgical history, donor characteristics, and posttransplantation outcomes. We identified all adult (aged ≥18 years) first‐time heart transplant recipients with a diagnosis of ACHD at Stanford University Medical Center between December 1987 and December 2024. Patients undergoing multiorgan transplants, including heart–lung, heart–liver, and heart–kidney transplants, were also included. Patients are identified using the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network “PX_ID” and “TRR_ID” codes, which are unique identifier codes assigned to each patient and transplant event, respectively. Follow‐up survival data were obtained through linking patient codes with the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network Standard Transplant Analysis and Research database (December 2024 release), which captures all solid‐organ transplant recipients in the United States and records time‐to‐death data as a mandatory data entry field. Recipients were categorized into the first and second eras on the basis of their transplantation date occurring before or after the allocation policy change on October 18, 2018, respectively. Recipients were also classified into nonoverlapping congenital heart disease diagnostic categories as detailed in Table S1.

Outcome

The primary outcome of this study was a comparison of recipient and donor characteristics between patients in the first and second eras. Secondary outcomes included 30‐day and 1‐year posttransplant death and in‐hospital complications such as length of stay, need for additional procedures, and new dialysis requirement.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as frequencies (percentages) or median (interquartile range). Differences between first‐ and second‐era patients were analyzed via Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test or Pearson's χ2 test, where appropriate, for categorical variables. Posttransplant survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Patients who were alive at the end of the study period (either 30 days or 1 year) or lost to follow‐up were right censored at the date of their last known contact. Comparisons between the 2 era cohorts were performed using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify potential contributors to posttransplant death using a 2‐step process. First, we conducted a univariable analysis of a prespecified list of clinically relevant variables, including age, sex, time on waitlist, preoperative inotropic support and mechanical circulatory support (MCS), history of Glenn/Fontan palliation, diagnostic category, sternotomy number, and total ischemic time. The variables that had a P<0.20 on univariable analysis were included in the final multivariable model. All statistical analysis was performed using R 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Overall Trends in Transplantation

From 1987 to 2024, a total of 196 patients with ACHD underwent first‐time heart transplantation, including multiorgan transplantation, at our institution. The annual volume of heart transplantation for patients with ACHD is characterized by 2 main eras (Figure 1). The number and type of transplants performed changed significantly in 2018, coinciding with the United Network for Organ Sharing heart allocation policy change. We therefore grouped our cohort for analysis into those occurring before the policy change and those occurring after, referred to as the first and second eras, respectively (Figure 2). Following the first successful heart–lung transplantation performed in 1981 at our institution, 10 patients with ACHD undergoing transplantation from the 1980s to 2000s predominantly underwent heart–lung transplantation. Beginning in the mid‐2010s, an increasing number of patients with ACHD underwent heart‐only and heart–liver transplantation. On average, 4 heart transplants per year (including multiorgan transplants) were performed from 1990 to 2017, compared with 9 transplants per year from 2018 to 2024 (P=0.009).

The pretransplantation medical conditions of patients with ACHD also shifted between the 2 eras (χ2 test, P=0.008). The most common congenital heart disease diagnosis in the first era was an isolated septal defect, which accounted for 40% of patients in the first era and 20% of patients in the second era. The most common diagnosis in the second era was single‐ventricle anatomy, which increased in frequency from 22% in the first era to 30% in the second era. This increase is predominantly due to a rise in patients with single‐ventricle anatomy who underwent Glenn/Fontan palliation, from 6% in the first era to 19% in the second era.

The second era is characterized by a rise in patients with prior and increasingly complicated past congenital surgical history (Figure S1). As such, the median number of cardiac surgeries before transplant in recipients increased from the first to second era (1 versus 2, P<0.001). Similarly, the median number of pretransplantation sternotomies was 1 in the first era and 2 in the second era (P<0.001).

Granular characteristics of recipients' congenital defects and past surgical history are shown in Table S2. Of 196 patients, a total of 175 patients had sufficient medical records for determination of their medical and surgical history. The 21 patients excluded in this granular analysis were transplanted from 1987 to 1996 and include 1 heart and 21 heart–lung transplants. This analysis revealed that, while the rates of congenital diagnoses were stable between the 2 eras, the second era saw a marked increase in patients with prior cardiac surgeries, including palliative procedures. For example, the proportion of patients with tetralogy of Fallot was similar in the first and second eras (5.8% versus 9.3%, P=0.404). In contrast, the proportion of patients with Blalock–Taussig–Thomas shunts more than doubled in frequency (7% versus 26%, P<0.001). In addition, the second era saw patients with history of surgeries that had not previously been encountered in the first era, such as the Rastelli procedure and arterial switch (Rastelli: 0 versus 2 transplants (4%), P=0.035; arterial switch: 0 versus 3 transplants (6%), P=0.009).

Transplant and Waitlist Characteristics

While heart–lung transplantation accounted for 68% of all heart transplants in the first era, they represented 37% of transplants in the second era. The rate of heart‐only transplantation increased from 28% to 46%, and the rate of heart–liver transplantation increased from 4% to 17%.

The median duration of time spent on the transplant waitlist decreased from 159 days in the first era to 75 days in the second era (Table 2, P=0.008). Higher status corresponded to shorter waitlist duration. In the first era, median waitlist duration was 110 days and 368 days for patients who were status 1 and status 2 at time of transplant, respectively (P<0.001). In the second era, waitlist duration was 40 days and 118 days for patients listed as statuses 1 through 3 and status 4, respectively (P=0.023).

Compared with patients in the first era, patients in the second era were hospitalized at higher rates and treated with higher levels of medical support before transplantation. More patients in the second era were hospitalized in the ward or intensive care unit before transplantation (ward: 5% versus 33%; intensive care unit: 16% versus 19%; χ2 test, P<0.001). Reflecting the increasing complexity and past surgical history of patients, rates of additional reconstructions during transplantation increased in the second era. Pulmonary artery reconstruction was performed in 10% of patients in the first era and 20% of patients in the second era (P=0.103). Superior vena cava reconstruction was performed in 4% and 17%, respectively, in the first and second eras (P=0.009). Ascending aorta reconstruction was performed in 6% and 9%, respectively, in the first and second eras (P=0.507).

Despite the increase in surgical reconstruction, median cardiopulmonary bypass time was stable across both eras (208 versus 232 minutes, P=0.099). Notably, cross‐clamp time decreased from 131 minutes in the historical group to 118 minutes in the second era (P=0.005). Circulatory arrest was performed in 3% of operations in the first era and 13% in the second era (P=0.026). Temporary chest closure increased from 14% in the first era to 22% in the second era (P<0.008).

Mechanical circulatory support was used more frequently in the second era. Perioperative initiation of intra‐aortic balloon pumps increased from 7% to 24% (P<0.005). Rates of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation also increased, as it was initiated perioperatively in 7% of patients in the first era and 22% of patients in the second era (P<0.001), and preoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was continued in 4% of patients in the second era (P<0.003). New cannulation strategies were also used in patients in the second era, including venoarterial–pulmonary artery cannulation (Table S4).

Postoperative Outcomes

The median survival of all 196 patients in the cohort was 7.9 (95% CI, 6.7–11.5) years. The overall survival probability was 91% (95% CI, 86.9%–95.0%) at 30 days, 81% (95% CI, 75.6%–86.7%) at 1 year, and 61% (95% CI, 54.7–69.2%) at 5 years. Postoperative outcomes are shown in Table 5. Death at 30 days and 1 year was not significantly different between the 2 eras (30 days: 9.2% versus 9.3%, P=0.948; 1 year: 19.0% versus 18.5%, P=0.939). However, length of hospitalization during transplant admission was significantly longer in the second era. Patients were hospitalized in the intensive care unit following transplantation for a median of 6 days and 17 days, respectively, in the first and second eras (P<0.001). Patients were hospitalized following transplantation for a median of 16 days and 37 days, respectively, in the first and second eras (P<0.001). Patients in the second era received dialysis at higher rates (17% versus 50%, P<0.001) during transplant admission and underwent posttransplant procedures at higher rates (16% versus 50%, P<0.001), reflecting a progressive increase in patient complexity and intensity of medical therapy (Figure S2).

One‐year survival rate for heart‐only transplant was 80.0% (95% CI, 70.0%–90.1%) across both eras. While not statistically significant, 1‐year survival improved from 72.5% (95% CI, 59.9%–87.8%) in the first era to 90.7% (95% CI, 79.2%–100%) in the second era (P=0.066; Figure 3C). One‐year survival rate for heart–lung transplant was 82.7% (95% CI, 76.1%–89.9%) across both eras, and specifically 84.4% (95% CI, 77.4–92.0%) in the first era and 75.0% (95% CI, 58.2%–96.6%) in the second era (P=0.284; Figure 3D). There was no difference in survival following heart–lung transplant in the subset of patients who survived to discharge (P=0.924; Figure S3), suggesting that the survival difference may be driven by early death following transplantation during hospitalization in the second era.

Univariable and multivariable predictors of death at 30 days and 1 year were analyzed for patients with sufficient medical records and are listed in Table S5. Following heart‐only transplantation, a history of Glenn/Fontan palliation was associated with increased 30‐day and 1‐year death in univariate analysis (30 days: hazard ratio [HR], 16.06 [95% CI, 2.66–96.79]; P=0.002; 1 year: HR, 6.44 [95% CI, 1.95–21.26]; P=0.002). While multivariable analysis did not identify any covariates that were significantly associated with death following heart‐only transplant, sternotomy number was associated with increased 30‐day and 1‐year death following heart–lung transplant (30 days, HR, 1.85 [95% CI, 1.07–3.19]; P=0.028; 1 year: HR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.03–2.63]; P=0.036). Recipient age, sex, waitlist time, and preoperative support with inotropes or MCS were not significantly associated with death following heart‐only or heart–lung transplantation.

DISCUSSION

Over the past 3 decades at our institution, the number of heart transplants performed for patients with ACHD has grown. Our findings highlight significant shifts in patient demographics, clinical complexity, transplant strategies, and posttransplant outcomes, particularly following the 2018 United Network for Organ Sharing donor heart allocation change. By encompassing 196 patients from 1980 to 2024, this study captures the evolving landscape of management of heart failure in patients with ACHD. To our knowledge, this study is the largest single‐institution cardiac transplantation experience in patients with ACHD. Compared with other single‐institution studies of patients with ACHD who underwent heart transplantation, 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 our investigation provides granular insight on a significantly larger and more contemporary cohort and includes multiorgan transplantation.

Excellent Survival Following Cardiothoracic Transplantation in Patients With ACHD Is Achievable

We report a 1‐year survival rate of 90.7% (95% CI, 79.2%–100%) following heart‐only transplantation in our institutional cohort of patients with ACHD since 2018. This is comparable with the 1‐year survival rate of 87.5% (95% CI, 85%–90%) 10 following heart‐only transplantation for all patients nationally after the allocation policy change. When looking specifically at heart‐only transplant in patients with ACHD, our institutional survival rate is higher or similar to the national survival rate of 85% from 2000 to 2019 17 , 18 , 19 and to those reported in other single‐center studies (77% for 48 patients from 1997 to 2014, Lewis et al 11 ; 94% for 20 patients from 2010 to 2016, Menachem et al 12 ; 93% for 28 patients from 2017 to 2024, Robinson et al 13 ). These outcomes reflect the continuing trend of improving outcomes for patients with ACHD undergoing transplantation, which predates the allocation policy change. 20 , 21 , 22

Fewer data are available on survival following heart–lung transplantation. While not statistically significant, our 1‐year survival rate of 75.0% (95% CI, 58.2%–96.6%) is lower than our previously reported 1‐year survival rate of 87% (95% CI, 81%–93%) for all heart–lung recipients from 2001 to 2022. 10 Given the association of increased prior sternotomy number with death following heart–lung transplantation in patients with ACHD, we hypothesize that increased surgical complexity may contribute to this difference. Earlier consideration of transplantation should be explored to address the unique obstacles faced by this patient population.

Increased Complexity and Higher Level of Care for Transplant Recipients With ACHD

The transition from the first to the second era of heart transplantation in patients with ACHD is marked by an increase in the surgical complexity of patients. Patients with ACHD now survive into adulthood following multiple palliative and corrective procedures. As a result, we are encountering and performing transplantation in patients with novel anatomies. While this patient population remains very heterogeneous, we identify key shifts in this population, including an increasing proportion of patients with single‐ventricle anatomy and multiple prior cardiac surgeries. These variations in anatomy and physiology require complex problem solving in the operating room. A higher number of prior sternotomies increases the operative difficulty of transplant and may contribute to the increased duration of cardiopulmonary bypass time seen in the second era.

Our emerging experience indicates the need for medical and surgical adjuncts, such as reconstruction at time of transplant, mechanical circulatory support, multiorgan transplantation, and posttransplant dialysis to address the unique challenges faced by recipients with ACHD. The high level of resources and expertise needed to treat patients with ACHD undergoing transplantation supports the argument for regionalization of this care, in line with previous studies that identified improved outcomes at high‐volume centers. 23 , 24 This specialized care extends beyond surgical and perioperative capabilities to include multidisciplinary care in the pretransplant and intensive care settings. 22 , 23

Unique Risks of Transplantation in Patients With ACHD Are Not Reflected in the Current Allocation System

In addition, our findings indicate that patients with ACHD in the second era presented in greater medical acuity. Higher rates of hospitalization, increased use of mechanical circulatory support, and greater reliance on inotropic therapy suggest that these patients were in more advanced stages of heart failure at the time of transplantation. The 2018 United Network for Organ Sharing allocation change granted a unique designation for patients with ACHD and coincided with a reduction in waitlist duration. However, the current allocation system prioritizes transplantation for patients with left heart failure or need for inotropic support. This does not sufficiently account for patients with ACHD, who have nontraditional presentations and develop predominantly right‐sided heart failure symptoms. 25 , 26 , 27 Additionally, patients with ACHD are less frequently candidates for ventricular assist devices and other durable MCS compared with patients without ACHD, thereby disadvantaging them from priority listing. Many patients with ACHD have single‐ventricle physiology with well‐preserved systolic function, as seen in our cohort where the median systemic ejection fraction is 45%. 1 , 28 , 29 Importantly, even though many patients with single‐ventricle palliation are clinically stable before transplantation, we find that a history of Glenn/Fontan palliation is associated with a higher mortality rate after heart transplantation. The clinical stability of patients with ACHD before acute decompensations belie their complexity and increased risk.

The unique challenges faced by patients with ACHD merits further investigation. Specifically, future studies should examine whether earlier transplantation and prioritization based on ACH‐specific features can offer improved outcomes. While the number of patients with ACHD listed and transplanted has increased following the allocation policy change, 22 , 30 the proportion of patients with ACHD undergoing transplantation remains similar. Our study finds that a similar proportion of transplant recipients with ACHD were status 4 in the second era as the previous status 2 in the first era (51.9% versus 50.4%). While clinically stable, many of the current patients with ACHD listed as status 4 are medically complex, face high surgical risks, and are at higher risk of acute clinical decompensation. Patients with ACHD have unique features that may not be captured in the current allocation system, including cyanosis, multiorgan dysfunction, complex anatomy, and multiple sternotomies. Due to their higher sensitization rates, patients with ACHD often require exception status to avoid prolonged wait times for transplantation. These factors may contribute to the lower 1‐year survival rate after heart‐only transplantation seen for patients with ACHD compared with patients without ACHD nationally (85% versus 93%) 10 and are not considered under the current allocation system.

Given the increased complexity of contemporary heart transplant recipients with ACHD, it is notable that overall survival has remained stable between the 2 eras. Heart‐only transplant recipients demonstrated improved 1‐year survival in the second era compared with the first era, suggesting that excellent long‐term survival remains achievable. Despite their increasing medical and surgical risk, cardiac transplantation is a successful strategy for the management of heart failure in patients with ACHD.

Study Limitations

Our study is limited by its retrospective design and single‐center experience, which may not be fully generalizable to other institutions. There is also significant heterogeneity in this cohort, with regard to congenital diagnosis, surgical history, and transplant procedure. A small percentage of the cohort underwent heart–liver and heart–kidney transplants, which limits conclusions about post‐multiorgan transplant death. Additionally, the exclusion of early heart–lung transplant recipients from some analyses due to incomplete records may influence the results of long‐term trends.

Conclusions

Heart transplantation in patients with ACHD has grown tremendously over the past 3 decades. This study presents a single institution experience as we enter a new era defined by sicker and more complex patients with ACHD. Advancements in surgical expertise, MCS, and adjunctive medical therapies have enabled an increasingly complex patient population to reach and survive definitive treatment for ACHD. Our health systems and organ procurement organizations will need to prepare for the increasing numbers of these patients and the complex coordination of care they may require.
