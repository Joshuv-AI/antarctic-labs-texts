Population Pharmacokinetics of Intravenous Infusion of Hydromorphone in Critically Ill Patients Supported by Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation
Authors: Xuehui Gao, Lu Yao, Fang Zeng, Hongling Zhang, Yongran Wu, Yifei Huang, Zhou Hong, Huaqing Shu, Jiancheng Zhang, Xiaobo Yang, Xiaojing Zou, You Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe pharmacological properties of hydromorphone, including lower lipophilicity and reduced protein binding, suggest that it may have less sequestration in the circuit with more predictable pharmacokinetics (PK) during extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) compared to traditional analgesics. However, reports on its PK in ECMO patients are limited. The aim is to develop a population pharmacokinetic (PK) model for hydromorphone in ECMO patients, so as to provide a reference for further research and clinical treatment. 26 ECMO patients (12 veno‐venous, 14 veno‐arterial) received hydromorphone infusion (0.03 mg kg−1 h−1) for 72 h. Blood samples were collected during and after infusion. Pharmacokinetics of hydromorphone was determined from arterial plasma concentrations. A nonlinear mixed‐effects modeling approach was used to analyze the hydromorphone concentration‐time data. A total of 484 plasma concentrations from 26 subjects were included in the population PK analysis. A three‐compartment model with first‐order linear elimination in the central compartment best described hydromorphone PK in veno‐venous and veno‐arterial ECMO patients. In veno‐venous ECMO, central compartment clearance was 0.955 L min−1 and volume of distribution was 23.6 L; in veno‐arterial ECMO, these were lower at 0.657 L min−1 and 16.3 L, respectively. Patients with elevated liver enzymes (alanine/aspartate aminotransferase) exhibited substantially higher drug exposure, with geometric mean increases ranging from 9.59% to 19.8% observed across both ECMO modes. Veno‐arterial ECMO patients may require less hydromorphone than those on veno‐venous ECMO. Hepatic dysfunction was linked to increased drug exposure, suggesting the need for individualized dosing in ECMO patients. Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a life‐saving strategy for critically ill patients suffering from severe cardiac and respiratory failure caused by a potentially reversible condition [1, 2, 3, 4]. Analgesia is necessary to enhance patient tolerance and reduce stress responses in critically ill patients on ECMO support, and opioids are the first‐line analgesics in clinical practice [5, 6, 7]. However, traditional opioid analgesics, like fentanyl and sufentanil, are heavily sequestered by the ECMO circuit due to their high protein binding and lipophilicity, as demonstrated in ex vivo ECMO studies [8, 9]. Therefore, ECMO patients often require higher analgesic doses than non‐ECMO patients with similar disease severity.

Hydromorphone is a potent opioid analgesic [10]. Its physicochemical properties resemble morphine, but it is 5–10 times more potent [11]. Less lipophilic and protein‐bound than other opioids, hydromorphone may experience reduced sequestration in ECMO circuits, making it a potentially better choice for ECMO patients [12]. It shows good stability and high recycling rates in polyvinyl chloride (PVC) infusion sets [13], but its stability in ECMO circuits, with their large surface area and membrane oxygenator, remains uncertain. Currently, there is limited research on the pharmacokinetic (PK) changes of hydromorphone in ECMO. This study aimed to characterize the PK of hydromorphone in critically ill patients undergoing veno‐venous (V‐V) and veno‐arterial (V‐A) ECMO using a population PK modeling approach.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science & Technology, Wuhan, China, reference number 2021‐0875‐01 on November 1, 2021 and was registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05565495). Written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians.

Patients

Patients were recruited at the general ICU between September 2022 and August 2024. Patients between the ages of 18 and 80 and expected to undergo ECMO for more than 48 h were considered eligible. Exclusion criteria include known allergy to hydromorphone, performing continuous renal replacement therapy during ECMO, pregnancy, ileus, severe hepatic dysfunction (Child‐Pugh class C), and taking any medication that could potentially interact with hydromorphone or affect its concentrations (Table S1).

Dosing of Hydromorphone

The dosing of hydromorphone was based on patients' body weight, continuously infused for 72 h with a constant dosage of 0.03 mg kg−1 h−1 [7, 14], supplemented as needed with other analgesics.

ECMO System

The ECMO circuit included a centrifugal blood pump with a pump controller (Maquet, Germany), an air‐oxygen mixer (Maquet, Germany), conduit tubing (MicroPort, China), and heat exchanger (HICO‐ AQUATHERM 660, Germany). The priming volume of the ECMO system ranged between 400 and 500 mL.

Blood Sampling and Drug Assay

The study commenced promptly following ECMO initiation, with blood samples collected during ECMO support. Serial PK samples to measure hydromorphone concentrations were drawn from dwelling arterial lines at 0, 1–2, 3–5, 10–15, 30–40 min, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 30–40, 48, and 72 h after the start of infusion. Hydromorphone administration ceased after 72 h of continuous infusion. Subsequent PK samples were collected at 1–2, 5–10, 30–40 min, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h after the stop of hydromorphone infusion. Additionally, pre‐ and post‐oxygenator blood samples were taken at 0, 12, 24, 48, 60, and 72 h after the initiation of the administration to explore oxygenator effects on hydromorphone PK. Blood samples (0.5 mL) were collected into EDTA‐coated tubes, immediately placed on ice, centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were stored at −80°C until analysis.

The plasma concentrations of hydromorphone were analyzed using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method.

Structure Pharmacokinetic Model

A three‐compartment disposition model with first‐order elimination was selected as the structural model after comparing to 1‐ and 2‐compartment models. V‐A and V‐V ECMO differ in their circulatory consequences—V‐A bypasses both the cardiac and pulmonary circulations, whereas V‐V bypasses only the pulmonary circulation—so between‐mode differences in hydromorphone PK are likely to span multiple correlated parameters (CL, V1, V2, Q, V3) rather than be confined to one. A covariate approach on one or two parameters can compress such multi‐parameter differences onto the selected term(s) and absorb the remainder into inter‐individual variability or residual error; this concern is amplified in small samples, where secondary mode effects may fail to meet stepwise inclusion thresholds even when physiologically real. We therefore estimated mode‐specific typical values (fixed‐effect parameters) for V‐V and V‐A while sharing a single set of inter‐individual variability (random‐effect) estimates across both modes (Table 2), preserving statistical power for the variability components across the full 26‐patient dataset. This “separate fixed‐effects, shared random‐effects” strategy is a small‐sample variant of the fully‐separate‐model approach used in biosimilar PK comparability, where test and reference products are estimated independently rather than via a single covariate framework [15]. As an empirical check, a pooled model with ECMO mode as a categorical covariate was also developed (Supporting Information): MODE on V2 was retained (ΔOFV = −14.41, p < 0.001) but MODE on CL did not meet the inclusion threshold (ΔOFV = −5.02, p > 0.01), even though the separate‐mode analysis showed a 31.6% CL difference between modes—directly illustrating the single‐covariate framework's limitation in this setting. Covariate Analysis Development

Covariates including age (AGE), gender (GEND), baseline body weight (BW), body‐mass index (BMI), body surface area (BSA), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (CR), creatinine clearance (CRCL), temperature (Temp), total protein (TP), gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), total bilirubin (TBIL) were evaluated for their impacts on hydromorphone pharmacokinetic parameters in critically ill patients supported by V‐V and V‐A ECMO. Covariates were added to the structural model using stepwise covariate modeling approaches, including forward inclusion and backward elimination. A drop in the objective function value (OFV) of 3.84, indicating significance at p < 0.05 level for one degree of freedom was necessary for a covariate to be included in the model during the forward inclusion step. Conversely, during the backward elimination step, an increase of OFV smaller than 6.63 corresponding to p < 0.01 level for one degree of freedom was required for elimination from the model.

Model Evaluation

Models were assessed by evaluating their goodness of fit plots (GOFs). This included comparisons between individual and population‐predicted concentrations versus observed concentrations, as well as conditional weighted residual versus time and population‐predicted concentrations. To ensure the reliability and stability of the ultimate population pharmacokinetic model, a nonparametric bootstrap resampling approach was employed. By creating 1000 bootstrap datasets through random sampling with replacement from the original dataset, new datasets of equal patient numbers were formed. Median parameter values and nonparametric 95% confidence intervals were then obtained from the NONMEM fit using the 1000 bootstraps dataset and contrasted with the final parameter estimates [16]. Additionally, prediction corrected Visual predictive checks (pcVPCs) were conducted to investigate the final PK model's capacity for prediction [17]. The final model parameter estimates, along with demographic information of subjects, were utilized to simulate 1000 new virtual clinical trials. Simulated plasma concentrations, represented by a median and 95% prediction interval, were plotted and compared with observed plasma concentrations.

The Impact of Covariates on PK Parameters Evaluation

The hydromorphone concentration‐time profiles were simulated using the individual post hoc parameter estimates from the final population PK model. Hydromorphone exposure metrics included the concentration at the end of infusion (C72h) and the area under the plasma concentration‐time curve from 0 to 96 h (AUC0‐96 h); AUC0‐96 h was calculated using the linear trapezoidal approximation. The purpose of this simulation was to compare these exposures between body weight groups (≤ median vs. > median), age groups (≤ median vs. > median), renal function groups (CRCL ≤ median vs. > median), and hepatic function groups (TBIL ≤ median vs. > median, ALT ≤ median vs. > median, and AST ≤ median vs. > median). It is suggested that differences in drug exposure up to 20% are not clinically significant. Of note, this simulation accounts for potential correlations among covariates.

Considering the impact of delta‐V (defined as the difference between the cumulative volume of fluid intake and the cumulative volume of fluid output during the period of using hydromorphone.) and two ECMO‐related factors including rotational speed of pump and pump flow rate (Time‐weighted averages of rotational speed of pump and pump flow rate were used for statistical analysis.), might directly impact the volume distribution and inter‐compartment clearance, these factors were grouped by the median. The distribution of PK parameters, including CL, V1, and Q2, was then compared across the groups.

Sequestration of Drugs by Membrane Oxygenator

Sequential blood sampling pre‐ and post‐oxygenator, done as quickly as possible, allows the post‐/pre‐oxygenator drug concentration ratio to estimate oxygenator drug sequestration, despite minor sampling accuracy limitations. Plotting this ratio against time allows for a better understanding of drug sequestration by oxygenator.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as count (percentage), mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), unless specified. Hydromorphone concentrations over time were analyzed via non‐linear mixed‐effects modeling [18, 19, 20]. The analysis was conducted with NONMEM 7.5.0 software from ICON Development Solutions in Ellicott City, Maryland, USA, Perl Speaks NONMEM (PsN) Ver 5.2.6 from Uppsala University, Sweden, and R ver. 4.2.3. All models underwent fitting using the FOCE method with interaction to obtain results.

Population Pharmacokinetic Model

During the development of the base model, several elimination and disposition models were thoroughly evaluated, including linear and Michaelis–Menten elimination models, as well as 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐compartment models. After careful consideration, a three‐compartment model with first‐order linear elimination in the central compartment was determined to be the most suitable structural model for describing the PK of hydromorphone in critically ill patients receiving V‐V and V‐A ECMO support (Figure S1). Stepwise OFV comparison strongly supported this choice: the 1‐compartment, 2‐compartment, and 3‐compartment models yielded OFV values of 3034.94, 3009.94, and 2323.90, respectively, with the 3‐compartment model producing a ΔOFV of −686.04 versus the 2‐compartment model (p < 0.001 for 2 additional parameters; Table S3). Michaelis–Menten elimination was also tested but did not improve the fit. The 1‐ and 2‐compartment models additionally showed systematic bias in CWRES versus time during the late post‐infusion phase, which was resolved by the 3‐compartment structure. The population PK model parameters included central compartment clearance (CL), central compartment volume of distribution (V1), intercompartment clearance between central compartment and peripheral compartment 1 (Q2), peripheral compartment 1 volume of distribution (V2), intercompartment clearance between central compartment and peripheral compartment 2 (Q3), and peripheral compartment 2 volume of distribution (V3).

Covariates were tested using the stepwise forward and backward method in NONMEM. The final model results showed that no covariate had a significant impact on PK parameters of hydromorphone in critically ill V‐V and V‐A ECMO patients. A separate pooled analysis with ECMO mode as a categorical covariate was conducted as a sensitivity analysis (Table S4 and Figure S5); MODE on V2 was retained (ΔOFV = −14.41 vs. pooled base, p < 0.001 for 1 df), but the pooled‐model pcVPC (Figure S5) reveals a systematic bias in opposite directions between modes—the pooled typical CL (0.78 L min−1) is lower than the V‐V value (0.955 L min−1) and higher than the V‐A value (0.657 L min−1), so the predicted median sits above the observed median in V‐V and below it in V‐A. This confirms that a single MODE‐on‐V2 covariate cannot simultaneously absorb the mode‐specific CL and V1 differences captured by the separate‐mode primary analysis.

The Impact of Covariates on PK Parameters Evaluation

Although no significant covariates were identified for the PK model parameter, the limited statistical power due to a small sample size may explain the absence of significant findings, rather than a true lack of association. Based on clinical plausibility and prior literature indicating potential PK relevance, exploratory simulations included covariates such as albumin level, lactate level, and total bilirubin. These simulations were designed to assess the impact of inter‐individual variability on hydromorphone exposure in ECMO patients, rather than to confirm covariate effects. By incorporating these covariates, the simulations help visualize trends and generate hypotheses for future, larger‐scale studies.

The simulated concentration‐time profiles, shown in Figure 3, indicate a markedly higher drug concentration‐time curve for V‐A ECMO versus V‐V ECMO. Covariate effects on hydromorphone exposure are presented in the Tables S5–S11. Geometric mean exposure differences ranged from −6.45% to 6.89% across body weight and age groups. In V‐A ECMO, 7 patients with CRCL > median had up to 29.6% lower exposure than those with CRCL ≤ median; in V‐V ECMO, exposure was 1.18%–1.29% higher for CRCL > median. Patients with TBIL > median showed 6.20% higher exposure in V‐A ECMO and 6.90% lower in V‐V ECMO compared to TBIL ≤ median. Elevated ALT/AST levels were linked to higher exposure in both ECMO modes, with geometric mean differences of 9.59%–19.8%. Higher lactate levels correlated with increased exposure in both modes. V‐V ECMO with higher lactate had 35.6% higher concentration than patients with lower lactate.

Discussion

This study presents a population PK model of hydromorphone in ECMO patients, using data from 12 V‐V and 14 V‐A ECMO patients. A three‐compartment model with first‐order elimination best described hydromorphone PK in critically ill V‐V and V‐A ECMO patients. We assessed both one‐ and two‐compartment models also, but they were discarded due to the poorer fit. The selected model accurately characterized the pharmacokinetics of hydromorphone in the studied population, as evidenced by satisfactory goodness‐of‐fit plots.

V‐A ECMO exhibited a 31.6% lower CL and 30.9% lower V1 than V‐V ECMO, suggesting slower elimination. Given that hydromorphone has an intermediate hepatic extraction ratio of 0.51 in healthy volunteers [21], its clearance is likely influenced by hepatic blood flow and cardiac output. ECMO patients, particularly those on V‐A ECMO, often require high‐dose vasoactive drug therapy. Although cardiac output was not measured, hypotension data and vasopressor doses in V‐A ECMO patients indicated that there were hemodynamically related changes that might affect the pharmacokinetics of hydromorphone. Moreover, in our research population, V‐A ECMO patients exhibited significantly higher liver enzyme levels (e.g., ALT/AST) compared to V‐V ECMO patients.

Compared to historical ICU postoperative data (V1, 3.35 L; CL, 1.01 L min−1), the final model showed an increase in V1 (23.6 L for V‐V; 16.3 L for V‐A), but contrary to expectations, CL decreased (0.96 L min−1 for V‐V; 0.657 L min−1 for V‐A) [22]. Impaired hepatic function in our study cohort likely contributed to the reduced hydromorphone clearance observed compared to non‐ECMO ICU patients. Hydromorphone undergoes hepatic glucuronidation, forming hydromorphone‐3‐glucuronide and dihydroisomorphine glucuronide, with parent drug and metabolites excreted renally [10]. We evaluated covariate effects on PK exposure across renal (CRCL ≤ median vs. > median) and hepatic function groups (TBIL ≤ median vs. > median, ALT ≤ median vs. > median, and AST ≤ median vs. > median). In V‐A ECMO patients, those with CRCL > median had 29.6% lower concentrations than those with CRCL ≤ median. However, a strong association between renal and hepatic dysfunction was observed in this subgroup: among V‐A patients with CRCL ≤ median, 71% had ALT > median, compared to only 29% of those with CRCL > median. Given this collinearity, the apparent CRCL effect may be largely or entirely driven by concurrent hepatic impairment—especially as V‐A patients with ALT > median showed up to 19.8% higher exposure. Therefore, we cannot conclude that low CRCL independently predicts higher exposure, and no dose reduction based solely on CRCL is recommended. In contrast, V‐V ECMO patients showed minimal CRCL effects (1.18%–1.29% higher exposure with CRCL > median, clinically insignificant), and no significant collinearity between CRCL and ALT was detected in that group (data not shown). Patients with elevated ALT/AST exhibited higher exposure in both ECMO modes, suggesting that hepatic impairment may influence pharmacokinetics regardless of ECMO type. Due to the small sample size, none of these trends reached statistical significance; they are hypothesis‐generating and underscore the need for larger studies capable of separating the contributions of renal and hepatic dysfunction in ECMO populations.

Multiple factors contribute to changes in the volume of distribution and clearance of hydromorphone in critically ill ECMO patients [23]. Lactate is a key regulator of in vivo metabolism [24]. Patients with elevated lactate showed higher hydromorphone exposure in both V‐V and V‐A ECMO modes. Notably, V‐V ECMO patients with higher lactate had 35.6% greater concentrations than those with lower lactate, though this may lack clinical significance as lactate levels in all V‐V ECMO patients were normal. Further studies are needed, especially in patients with abnormal lactate levels.

A prior study on postoperative ICU patients found that hydromorphone clearance decreased with age, and body weight significantly affected PK [22]. We assessed covariate impacts on PK exposure across age (≤ median vs. > median) and body weight (≤ median vs. > median) groups. Heavier patients showed slightly higher exposure (AUC0–96h and C72h), but exposure differences across age and weight groups were−6.45% to 6.89%, deemed clinically insignificant. One reason for this finding may be that the range of age and body weight in our study population was narrow.

Complex pathophysiology in critically ill patients impacts drug PK during ECMO [25, 26, 27], alongside hemodilution and drug sequestration by PVC tubing or membrane oxygenators [28]. The level of interaction between the drug and the ECMO circuit is determined by the physicochemical properties of the drug, such as lipophilicity, protein binding, molecular size, and degree of ionization at physiological pH [29, 30, 31, 32]. Lipophilic drugs have high solubility in organic materials and can be sequestered in large quantities by the ECMO circuit, while hydrophilic drugs are less affected [29, 33]. Drugs with high protein binding rates are more easily sequestered in the ECMO circuit [29, 32]. In this study, post‐ to pre‐oxygenator hydromorphone concentration ratios consistently approximated 1.0, suggesting negligible oxygenator sequestration, consistent with hydromorphone's low protein binding (8%–19%) and lipophilicity (log P, 1.1–1.8) [11, 34, 35]. Importantly, the absence of a decreasing trend in the oxygenator‐associated concentration difference over time further suggests a lack of time‐dependent saturable sequestration, which would otherwise be expected if hydromorphone were sequestered by the circuit material. Given the absence of any temporal trend in the post‐/pre‐oxygenator ratio, a formal circuit‐sequestration sub‐model (e.g., a saturable binding compartment) would have been statistically unidentifiable from these data and was not pursued; ex vivo circuit studies with deliberate sequestration challenges remain valuable for future work, particularly for drugs whose lipophilicity or protein binding differs substantially from those of hydromorphone. Although oxygenators contribute less to sequestration than PVC tubing, these findings confirm low hydromorphone sequestration in ECMO circuits. Recent trials report lower morphine milligram equivalents and sedative use in ECMO patients receiving hydromorphone versus fentanyl, suggesting hydromorphone as a preferred long‐term analgesic for critically ill ECMO patients [35, 36, 37]. Nevertheless, to fully characterize hydromorphone pharmacokinetics during ECMO, further studies integrating circuit pharmacokinetic modeling and ex vivo assessments of circuit‐drug interaction would be beneficial.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is limited, yet it employs a population pharmacokinetic approach to optimize the utilization of the available data. Shekar et al. suggested that a sample size of 10–12 individuals is adequate for a population PK analysis in ECMO patients [38]. Second, the majority of ECMO patients in the early stages requiring deep sedation prevented us from exploring the pharmacodynamics of hydromorphone in terms of the level of sedation and analgesia. Third, active hydromorphone metabolites, potentially causing neuroexcitatory effects and accumulating in renal impairment, were not measured [10]. Fourth, cardiac output was not measured. Although ECMO flow rates, CRCL and lactate were recorded, they do not reliably reflect native cardiac output or total systemic circulation, limiting covariate analysis in the PK model. Future studies incorporating direct or estimated measures of cardiac output would be valuable in further elucidating its role in hydromorphone pharmacokinetics.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a three‐compartment linear PK model effectively characterized hydromorphone PK in critically ill patients on V‐V and V‐A ECMO. V‐A ECMO patients may require lower hydromorphone doses than V‐V ECMO patients. Those with impaired hepatic function showed higher drug exposure than those with normal function. Minimal hydromorphone sequestration by the membrane oxygenator was observed. We believe that these findings provide valuable insights for future clinical studies and applications of hydromorphone.
