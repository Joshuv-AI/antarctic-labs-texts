Grounded in culture: a participatory empowerment evaluation of the oskâyak (youth) power program for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) at pakitahaw sākahikan iýiniwak (sturgeon lake first nation)
Authors: Norma Rabbitskin, Clifford Ballantyne, Udoka Okpalauwaekwe, Scott Tunison, Vivian R Ramsden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Oskâyak (Youth) Power Program (YPP) at Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) was co-created by and with youth to strengthen miyo-ayāwin (wellness) among oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys). Using a participatory empowerment evaluation grounded in a braided framework of community-based participatory research (CBPR), transformative learning, and ethical space, guided conversations were held with 14 youth supported by Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers). Oskâyak (Youth) described how cultural teachings, land-based activities, and mentorship nurtured confidence, identity, and connection, cultivating hope, belonging, purpose, and meaning. The findings show how Nation-led, strength-based programs grounded in Indigenous knowledge can foster holistic miyo-ayāwin, agency, and community connectedness. The YPP illustrates the power of Indigenous youth-led, culturally grounded approaches to address health inequities while honoring self-determination and Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession (OCAP®) principles.Keywords: Youth Power Program, program evaluation, Indigenous youth engagement, grounded in culture, holistic wellness, Indigenous young men and boys

Plain language summary

This study evaluates the impact of the Youth Power Program (YPP), co–created with youth at Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) to enhance miyo–ayāwin (wellness) among oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys). Using a culturally grounded, community–based participatory research approach integrated with transformative learning and participatory empowerment evaluation, guided conversations were held with 14 youth participants aged 11–17. Findings showed increased confidence, new skill acquisition, and strengthened peer connections. Participants valued the inclusive, youth–driven environment and expressed interest in expanded programming for older age groups. YPP fostered identity, purpose, and belonging, highlighting the value of youth–led, culturally informed initiatives.

Background

Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation (SLFN)) is a signatory of Treaty 6 with an on–reserve population of over 1500 people and of which there are about 568 adolescents and young adults between the ages of 11 and 25 residing on–reserve [1]. SLFN’s vision for miyo–ayāwin (wellness) emphasizes balance of spirit, emotion, mind, and body prioritized through relationships, grounded in their customs, values, and guided by their Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) [2,3]. The Nation has a long history of co–creating authentically engaged, Nation–driven research projects and programs [2–6], which range from traditional health clinics to cultural camps [7]. One such program is the māmawōhkamātowin (working together), which blends traditional and Western miyo–ayāwin (wellness) approaches [7]. This focus on enhancing holistic health and miyo–ayāwin (wellness) led to the co–creation of the Oskâyak (Youth) Power Program (YPP) in SLFN.

The YPP was co–designed in 2017 by and with the oskâyak (youth) at Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) with the goal of enhancing miyo–ayāwin (wellness) by, for and with oskâyak (youth) in the Nation, by empowering and engaging oskâyak (youth) in activities grounded in Cree cultural teachings, ceremonies, and the miyo–ohpikihâwasowin [8]. Defined locally as mamāhtāwihiwēwin (meaning empowerment), SLFN characterizes empowerment as a journey to self–discovery using the innate gifts granted by the Creator [8]. As such, the YPP was created as an avenue to help oskâyak (youth) discover their path to self–discovery and mamāhtāwihiwēwin. (empowerment) [3,8]. The Oskâyak (Youth) Center (commonly known as the ACCESS Oskâyak (Youth) Center) serves as the primary hub for most YPP activities (See Figure 1). Collectively, the activities carried out with the oskâyak (youth) provided opportunities for them to connect with peers learning cultural and oskâyak (youth)-friendly activities with Kēhtēyak (Elders), Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers), peers, and their families.

The philosophy of the YPP was grounded in the culture of the Nation and focused on mentorship. It preserved a way of life framed within the nēhiyaw mamâwacikewin (First Nation world view) by weaving cultural teachings and practices together to preserve and protect the health and well–being of the oskâyak (youth). In the YPP, Kēhtēyak (Elders) and peer mentorship offered the opportunity to provide oskâyak (youth) with the tools for āhkamēyimowin (perseverance) by addressing the historical context of colonialism in Canada and by sharing traditional and cultural wisdom and practices [3,4]. Embodying mentorship and engaging oskâyak (youth) in co–creating programs have been shown to enhance health and miyo–ayāwin (wellness) by and with oskâyak (youth) [3]. This is because engaged oskâyak (youth) can form meaningful relationships with peers, and supportive adults [4,9]. These empowering processes allow for oskâyak (youth) to openly share and access knowledge, all the while nurturing a sense of hope, purpose, meaning and belonging [1]. As they cultivate these values, they find balance across the four holistic dimensions of health, which are physical, mental, emotional, and spitritual [10,11]. According to SLFN teachings, a person experiences miyo–ayāwin (wellness) when they have hope, know they belong in the community; understand that their life has meaning; and have a unique and specific purpose in life; while staying grounded in the culture and values of the nation (Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [4,12,13].

The YPP was co–designed by, with, and for oskâyak (youth) to ensure safe, culturally relevant spaces responsive to the unique needs and rites of passage for iskwêsis (girls), napêsisak (boys), and those journeying through questions of identity during puberty. While Western terms such as ‘Two–Spirit’ or ‘non–binary’ are often used in academic discourse about Indigenous peoples, Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation (SLFN)) prefers to ground these concepts in their own language and teachings, which are shared with youth at puberty as part of their cultural journey toward mamāhtāwihiwēwin (inherent and shared power stemming from self–discovery and wellness). In this way, the YPP honors both the sacred roles of oskinîkiwak (young men) and iskwêsis (girls), and womenthe inclusive, evolving pathways of identity, framed through the Nation teachings and cultural reclamation. The YPP for iskwêsis (girls) was called the Iskwêsis (Girl) Power Program [3,8], while the version for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) was known as the oskâyak (youth) Power Program for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys). The YPP for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) was particularly important in SLFN because it encouraged communal participation in teaching and mentoring of oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to effectively cultivate mamāhtāwihiwēwin (empowerment)(empowerment) through the co–construction of knowledge, agency, voice, vulnerability, and active listening, grounded in the culture of wāhkōhtowin (kinship), strength–based approaches, and reciprocity [7]. Building on nation knowledge and teachings, we worked with oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) at SLFN to learn from their stories, ways the YPP for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) had enhanced nation miyo–ayāwin (wellness) and impacted them on their individual and collective miyo–ayāwin (wellness) journeys while considering opportunities for transformation. Although this evaluation centers oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys), the broader initiative encompasses and supports the holistic wellness of all youth living in the Nation.

Methodology and methods

Relationships

Building relationships precedes any work carried out with First Nations (and Indigenous peoples) in Canada, as it ensures that the work is Nation–driven and honors self–determination (Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [12]. UO is physician and Indigenous ally who built upon established relationships within the Nation and has engaged over time with the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to foster these relationships. CB is a member of Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) and at the time of this work, the oskâyak (youth) coordinator at the ACCESS Oskâyak (Youth) Center. NR is the Senior Nurse Manager at the Sturgeon Lake Health Center (SLFC) and is recognized by the Nation as a Traditional Knowledge Keeper (Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw). ST is a researcher and educator. VRR is a nurse and strong ally with SLFN. VRR has collaborated on several Nation–driven projects with SLFN for more than two decades [2,3,5,9,14]. The authors of this paper continue to foster relationships with oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) at SLFN that are built on trust, respect, transparency, humility, honesty, and communication grounded in the culture of the Nation. This process reflects the principles of reciprocity and relational accountability, which are foundational to Indigenous methodologies and emphasize that knowledge is created and shared within ongoing, respectful relationships [15–17]. In this project, reciprocity was enacted through youth member–checking circles and interpretive dialogs with Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers), ensuring that findings remained accountable to community voices.

Study design

We employed an integrated framework of community–based participatory research (CBPR) [18,19], transformative learning [3], and participatory empowerment evaluation (PEE) [20,21], braided within a strength–based approach grounded in ethical space of engagement [22]. CBPR emphasizes equitable partnerships between researchers and members of the Nation, leveraging the strengths of all partners to translate knowledge into action and drive social change [18,19]. Transformative learning involves critical analysis, reflection, and action to address community issues [3]. PEE is a community–driven approach where participants co–create evaluation processes, fostering capacity building, self–determination, and continuous improvement [21]. In the context of our study, we use the term ‘​​​​​​participants’ broadly to include members pf the Nation (i.e. the oskâyak (youth) Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers)), program staff, and researchers. Given that the project was driven by the Nation, these participants functioned as equal partners, shaping the evaluation questions, processes, and interpretations in line with the Nation priorities and cultural teachings. As such, PEE in this study extended beyond mere participation to reflect reciprocal partnership grounded in mamāhtāwihiwēwin (the teaching that power is inherent and relational).

A strength–based research approach highlights the resilience and positive attributes of Indigenous communities, countering the prevalent deficit–based narratives; and ethical spaces of engagement create environments for meaningful, respectful, and equitable dialog between individuals with differing worldviews and values [22]. Ethical space, as described by Ermine [22], is not simply a common ground to accommodate different viewpoints but a mutual commitment to encounter and dialog across distinct knowledge systems without assimilation or dominance of one or the other [22]. The Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) hold specialized knowledge (kiskēýihtamowin) passed down orally from the ancestors which ground their knowledge systems and way of knowing and being. As illustrated in Figure 2, the Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) knowledge systems are grounded in oral traditions, customs, ceremony, and relational codes of conduct, while Western knowledge systems emphasize written texts, policies, and standardized methodologies. The overlap, or ‘ethical space,’ represents the respectful and intentional meeting point where both systems can enter into dialogue while preserving their integrity. In this space, cultural teachings are not subordinate to Western frameworks; rather, they are positioned as equally authoritative, guiding how meaning is made and how decisions are shaped [22]. For Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation), this meant privileging oral systems and relational accountability as the grounding for evaluation, with written methodologies (as described above) serving in a supportive role.

While this concept resonates with Etuaptmumk/Two–Eyed Seeing [23] in its valuing of multiple knowledge systems, the Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) at Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) emphasized that their teachings move beyond the two–eyed seeing framework (up to four–eyed seeing framing according to local teachings in some cases), and therefore, for this study, ethical space [22] provides the most appropriate framing for our evaluation (See Figure 2).

Within this braided methodological framework, the Nation partners planned, implemented, and evaluated their own programs, using co–designed concepts, and outcomes to foster improvements and honor self–determination [21]. Central to this methodology was the philosophy that this evaluation should serve as an instrument for authentic engagement and mamāhtāwihiwēwin (empowerment) rather than critique, placing an accent on self–determination and capacity strengthening [20,21]. While we refer to ‘empowerment evaluation’ to reflect the established approach, we aligned this concept with mamāhtāwihiwēwin (empowerment), the Nation’s teachings that power is inherent and relational. Accordingly, our evaluation centers shared power, self–determination, and capacity strengthening rather than power transfer. As illustrated in Figure 2, the Nation’s oral systems and cultural teachings ground and guide this interpretation, reaffirming that empowerment in this context reflects Indigenous knowledge systems specific to Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation).

SLFN believe that by using a braided methodology framework that reflects their cultural values and centers oskâyak (youth) voices–working with rather than on, would empower the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to critically appraise and refine the YPP, thereby promoting sustainable outcomes. By ‘braided,’ we mean the intentional weaving of CBPR, transformative learning, and PEE within an ethical space of engagement in ways defined by and with the Nation. This braid reflects the cultural and oral teachings in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) which emphasizes that research processes must remain grounded in their (i.e. the Nation’s) knowledge systems. While this metaphor resonates with (but is distinct from) other approaches such as Etuaptmumk/Two–Eyed Seeing [23] and Te Awa Whiria (Māori braided rivers) [24], our braid organizes participatory methods and roles to uphold the self–determination, relational accountability, and youth–defined values/outcomes in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation).

Participants

Oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) aged between 10 to 25 years were invited to participate in the conversations. Informed oral consent was obtained from all the participants and their parents or guardians.

Data collection methods and instruments

Data were collected jointly using conversational and individual interview sessions. Three sessions were carried out, and each lasted between 40 and 60 minutes. In these conversations, the purpose of the study was explained and the procedures for sharing information were reviewed. The conversation guide ensured consistency in audio–recorded conversations and field notes taken for data analysis. Accommodations were made for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) who preferred to converse individually or through other forms of communication (e.g. email). All data collected were returned to the Oskâyak (Youth) Coordinator (CB).

Data processing and analysis

We conducted reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) following Braun & Clarke’s six phases ([25]). Two of the co–authors (UO, CB) independently familiarized themselves with transcripts, generated initial codes, and maintained reflexive field notes. Through iterative meetings, we (UO, CB and NR) developed a shared codebook and reached consensus on code definitions and theme boundaries, explicitly centering the youth meaning and context over categorical parsimony (i.e. simplifying categories for sake of efficiency). Throughout these iterative processes, we (UO and CB) engaged in ongoing reflexive journaling and debriefing sessions to surface assumptions, to ensure our interpretations remained relationally accountable to the oskâyak (youth) and Nation’s context. To avoid extractive de–contextualization (i.e. reducing participants’ stories to abstract categories detached from their cultural and relational meaning) [15,16], we (i) preserved longer narrative segments before cross–case synthesis, (ii) retained Cree terms and local (contextual) categories, and (iii) conducted member–checking circles with the oskâyak (youth) and interpretive dialogs with Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers)to confirm interpretation, cultural emphasis, and cultural meaning. We did not compute inter–rater reliability (in keeping with RTA by Braun and Clarke), as our braided methodological approach centers relational consensus and reflexivity rather than coder interchangeability. The emergent themes from our analyzes were interpreted through the Nation’s miyo–ayāwin (wellness) Model [commonly referred to as miyo–pimâtisiwin (good life) or the cycle of life, which emphasizes balance across the four domains of wellbeing (i.e. physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual] and the concept of mamāhtāwihiwēwin (inherent/shared power); grounding them within the Nation’s cultural context as well as within broader Indigenous scholarship (e.g. ethical spaces of engagement). As such, our analytical decisions were guided by Indigenous methodologies [15–17,22] emphasizing story, place, kinship [15,17], relational accountability [16], all, within an ethical space of engagement [22].

Ethical considerations

Building on the relationship we had formed with the Nation, Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) granted permission to conduct this evaluation in accordance with the Nation’s Research Protocol [1]. Thereafter, this study was reviewed by the University of Saskatchewan’s Behavioral Research Ethics Board and received an Exemption from review as per Article 2.5 of the Tri–Council Policy Statement (TCPS): Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans, December 2022 (Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [12], which states that ‘​​​​​​Quality assurance and quality improvement studies, program evaluation activities, and performance reviews, or testing within normal educational requirements when used exclusively for assessment, management or improvement purposes, do not constitute research for the purposes of this Policy, and do not fall within the scope of REB review.’

Consent process with SLFN oskâyak (youth)

We conducted all aspects of this study in an ethical manner as guided by Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) and stipulated in TCPS2 (2022) (Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) [12]. For minors (i.e. Oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) less than 16 years, parent or guardian permission was obtained orally in the presence of the oskâyak (youth) coordinator (CB), consistent with the Nation’s Protocol and TCPS 2 Article 3.7. After parents/guardians gave permission, oskâyak (youth) were then invited to provide their own oral assent. Both processes were explained in plain language, ensuring that youth and guardians understood the study’s purpose, confidentiality measures, and voluntary nature. The consent/assent process was facilitated by the oskâyak (youth) coordinator from the ACCESS oskâyak (youth) program, with researchers present to answer questions. No names or signatures were recorded; instead, audio files and field notes were assigned study codes. All raw data were returned to the Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) as well as the oskâyak (youth) coordinator in accordance with OCAP® principles [26] and Nation data stewardship protocols [27].

Fifty–seven percent (8/14) of the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) reported that they lived with their parents. YPP activities and experiences

a) YPP activities

The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) identified several kinds of oskâyak (youth) activities that they participated in through the YPP. These activities were then mapped according to the Nation’s miyo–pimâtisiwin (good life), which emphasizes balance across the four domains of wellbeing (i.e. physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual) which also upholding the oskâyak (youth)-defined outcomes of hope, belonging, purpose, and meaning (see Figure 3a). The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) highlighted the activities they most enjoyed, which included land–based practices (canoeing, berry picking, rabbit snaring, pulling traps), cultural camps, fishing, hunting, archery, singing, and drumming. These activities were valued not only for skill development but also for strengthening connections to land, Kēhtēyak (Elders), and cultural teachings.

Some of the older oskâyak (youth) mentioned that they also participated in structured workshops and training sessions, such as mental health first aid, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), security training, and vehicle driving (see Figure 3b). While these were not traditional activities, they were still framed by the oskâyak (youth) and Kēhtēyak (Elders) as contributing to their sense of preparedness, confidence, and belonging in community life.

Together, these traditional and Western activities illustrate how the YPP weave cultural teachings with practical skill–building, reinforcing the holistic view of wellness held by the Nation.

Oskâyak (youth) experiences

Highlighting the experiences of oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) participating in oskâyak (youth) activities, the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) used words like enjoy, enjoyable, exploratory, learning experience, chilling, relaxational, and calming to describe their experiences. See quotation:

“I enjoy most of the things organized for us in the community such as knitting, archery, canoeing and others I cannot recall. One of the moments I recalled and enjoyed doing was cleaning some areas of the reserve. I enjoyed it because it was an outdoor activity, and it was beneficial to the community.”

Strengths and impact of the YPP activities

The sub–themes identified as part of the strengths and impact of the YPP for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) were, a) facilitating community relationships and building new connections, and b) fostering appreciation for culture and cultural identity.

a. Facilitating community relationships and building new connections

The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) described the strengths of the YPP activities as building real and beneficial connections with their peers and community. The following quotations highlight their strengths:

“I enjoy hanging out with my peeps[people] here at the youth center, playing video games, board games and teasing ourselves. We try to support and encourage one another when we are down, that’s why we have a group chat for just us boys.”

“there is a culture in the youth space that is very different from other places in the community…here we share everything we have, we share meals, we share stories, we support each other when it feels like no one else does.”

b. Fostering appreciation for culture and cultural identity

The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) explained that the YPP activities fostered feelings of community, self–esteem, and solidarity among one another. One participant provided an example from a culture camp, highlighting how the activities facilitated genuine relationships and deepened his understanding and appreciation of the gifts of friendship.

“I enjoyed the group activities hosted by Bob and Jill [pseudonyms used] because they helped me understand why I am proud of my identity…We learned how we have so much to be grateful about like the friends, brothers, aunties and uncles and cousins. We learned that we need to keep these teachings to rise above the challenges we face…”.

Another speaking about YPP activities done in the youth space:

“… what we do here is cultural because of the culture of the youth space…It keeps us grounded in our reality and shows us why we need to have each other’s backs at all times.”

Opportunities for change

The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) mentioned a few areas that could be improved, some of which were indirectly related to the YPP. Two young men mentioned expanding the oskâyak (youth) space to accommodate the growing number of oskâyak (youth) that use it:

“Sometimes it feels crowded, and when there are many folks using this space, I just want to leave, but I know that it is selfish to say. Maybe we could have our own space, and the little ones [could] have their own space…”

One oskâyak (youth) discussed the negative impact of poor adult role models, mentioning that they did not inspire oskâyak (youth) towards miyo–ayāwin (wellness). He noted that adults should set a better example to encourage oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to be responsible:

“… A lot of times we hear in our town meetings what the youth needs to do [get done], we want to do it, but we don’t see the old ones, our leaders, doing it. How do they expect us young men and boys to change? … How can we change when the ones that should show us the way are not modeling the right path…”

Discussion

This Nation–driven evaluation advances Indigenous Oskâyak (youth) program evaluation by operationalizing mamāhtāwihiwēwin(the teaching that power is inherent and relational) as a youth–defined value and outcome; by co–creating the evaluation questions and criteria (e.g. hope, belonging, purpose, meaning) with oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) with the guidance of their Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers); by braiding CBPR, ethical spaces of engagement (using transformative learning) with PEE so that decision–making authority remains with the Nation; and by engaging youth as partners/co–researchers in enhancing miyo–ayāwin (wellness) by, with and for themselves using community braided strategies that center their voices, their values and their culture (ways of knowing and being). Together, these strategies shift traditional evaluation from being merely extractive to, youth–led sense–making that is locally valid, context–sensitive, and relatively transferrable to other Nation–led Indigenous programs with similar contexts/structures.

Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) prides itself in building a healthy community by integrating an ethical space framework that embraces and engages different worldviews and systems with Indigenous ways of knowing and being. The oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) at Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) were engaged and gained knowledge from their stories and experiences how the Oskâyak (youth)Power Program for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) enhanced community miyo–ayāwin (wellness), fostered hope, nurtured meaning and purpose, and enhanced their knowledge of their cultural identity. These areas of strength and opportunities for change within the program and within the Nation were further explored. Our findings revealed that the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) appreciated oskâyak–focused activities both inside and outside the ACCESS Oskâyak (Youth) Center highlighting how the reciprocal relationships they fostered with their peers, families and community members were central to their miyo–ayāwin (wellness). The oskâyak (youth) reported feeling a renewed sense of pride in their cultural identity and a stronger connection to their peers, Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) emphasizinghow community values like respect, humility, and hope grounded them in their respected miyo–ayāwin (wellness) journeys even when they faced challenges.

The Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers) in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) describe the wellbeing of oskâyak (youth) as being integral to the overall wellbeing and sustainability of their community [28,29]. This is because, oskâyak (youth), especially their oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys), hold a unique place, as they symbolize the future health and well–being of their community. Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) recognizes that Oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) are at a pivotal stage in their development, a point when they transition from boyhood to young adulthood and start to define their individual roles as well as their roles within their families and the Nation. This was one of the reasons why they preferred to use the term oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to refer to their oskâyak (youth) who were between the ages of 10 to 25 years. This is also a rite of passage (miyo–ohpikihâwasowin) for Indigenous oskâyak (youth) [i.e. oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys)] as they create their own paths and find identity in themselves. The Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers)in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) teach that identity of the oskâyak (youth) in the Nation is grounded in their culture and is a determinant of health that connects the individual, their families, their communities, their ancestral spirits, and the environment [30–32]. As such, miyo–ayāwin (wellness) is achieved, supported, and enhanced through holistic interventions grounded in the culture of the Nation (which includes, Indigenous knowledge, Indigenous practices, Indigenous languages, and ways of knowing) [4,33,34]. In Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation), the connection to culture is carried out in these ways (grounded in culture) to provide support for growth and well–being during the transition from adolescence to adulthood; instilling confidence, pride and fostering a sense of identity, purpose, belonging and meaning for them [35–37]. These ideas were expressed in many ways in the evaluation of the YPP, where the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) talked about how they loved spending time with friends, family and in the community doing cultural and non–cultural activities, learning about their language, talking with Kēhtēyak (Elders) and engaging in school activities within the community. The value of community, building/nurturing relationships, and finding strength in support such as family members, friends, and the community is instilled in these oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) from a young age, which is ultimately expressed in their daily interactions with their peers, families, and community. For example, over 79% (11/14) of oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) perceived that their overall health was Good to Very Good. As such, the engaging and empowering processes implemented as a part of the Oskâyak (Youth) Power Program for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) encouraged them to engage with one another and access knowledge, all the while nurturing a sense of hope, purpose, meaning and belonging which in turn mitigates feelings of hopelessness and helplessness [38].

How then can hope, belonging, meaning, and purpose be facilitated in, with, by, and for Indigenous communities/Nations based on the findings of this study? Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) affirms that the miyo–ayāwin (wellness) of First Nation oskâyak (youth) [including oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys)] is shaped by self–determination, a strong sense of self, cultural identity (i.e. connections to culture, land, and community) and the ability to be in balance with the world around them [33]. This concept is further shaped by the systems they interact with to meet their basic needs (i.e. education, housing, health services, etc.) which are frequently considered social determinants of health in mainstream knowledge systems [38]. Many a time, our systems (which are largely based on Euro–Western worldviews and socio–economic determinants) fail to take into consideration Indigenous determinants of health and miyo–ayāwin (wellness) which are grounded in the culture of the Nation [30–33]. It is important to remember that long before colonization, original First Peoples had highly sophisticated systems and structures that provided for the basic needs of their peoples and communities [39,40]. Rooted in the miyo–ayāwin (wellness) teachings were Indigenous values and teachings of hope, belonging, meaning and purpose [38–41]. These four aspects of miyo–ayāwin (wellness) enriched and defined what some Indigenous peoples called the Medicine Wheel (comprised of the physical, mental, emotional and spiritual components) [41,42]. In Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation), they refer to this concept as miyo–ayāwin (wellness) or by most cultural teachings, miyo–pimâtisiwin (good life), which emphasizes balance across the four domains of wellbeing (i.e. physical, emotional, mental, and spiritual), taking into account the holistic nature of the person. Hope, as a fundamental aspect of miyo–ayāwin (wellness) was grounded in a sense of cultural identity, a sense of belonging to, and staying culturally connected with family and community; a sense of meaning intertwined with an understanding of how one’s life and those of their families and communities are part of creation and history; and a sense of purpose in one’s daily life. Hope, belonging, meaning, and purpose are demonstrated by Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) peoples in many ways such as pursuits for education, employment, cultural ways of being and doing [43,44]. Undergirding miyo–ayāwin (wellness) for Indigenous oskâyak (youth) [including oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys)] is the pursuit of hope, what the Kēhtēyak (Elders) and Kayāsi Kistēyihtamow Kanawēyihcikēw (Traditional Knowledge Keepers)in Pakitahaw Sākahikan Iýiniwak (Sturgeon Lake First Nation) termed āhkamēyimowin (perseverance) [3]. Therefore, in pursuit of enhancing miyo–ayāwin (wellness) with Indigenous communities’ consideration of Indigenous knowledge will guide the Nation towards transformative changes.

It is not uncommon for Indigenous oskâyak (youth) [including oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys)] to lose connection within themselves or their culture as they grow and gain more independence (e.g. moving out of community, move on to other jobs, etc.) causing an ‘unbalanced displacement’ or ‘disconnectedness’ in holistic miyo–ayāwin (wellness) [45]. This concept have been described in the literature [46]; Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC) [47], and explained as root–caused in intergenerational trauma (i.e. the transmission of emotional, psychological, and social effects of colonialism, Indigenous residential schools and systemic racism from one generation to the next; impacting the well–being and cultural identity of Indigenous peoples) [46]; Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC) [47]. Sadly, frequently research and programs involving Indigenous communities neglect or reject this idea and focus more on the deficits, challenges and struggles of Indigenous peoples. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRCC) was established to acknowledge and address the harm caused by the Residential School System and to promote healing, reconciliation, and renewed relationships between Indigenous and non–Indigenous peoples in Canada [48]; Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC) [49]. It is important to point out that strengths exist in Indigenous communities despite intergenerational trauma and should be identified by the community and celebrated [3,5,36,50,51]. Promoting strength–based rather than deficit–based approaches to systematically answer questions that are meaningful to the Nation is critical to enhancing health and well–being with the Nation.

Program–specific innovations and youth–led outcomes from this work

Beyond documenting the benefits (and opportunities for change) of the YPP for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys), this study also co–produced an evaluative process and tools with youth that centered mamāhtāwihiwēwin (empowerment)and youth–defined outcomes. Several program–specific innovations and youth–led outcomes emerged that extend the contribution of this work beyond a single program evaluation.

First, the oskâyak (youth) (including oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys)) reframed program ‘success’ around the outcomes of hope, belonging, purpose, and meaning. Rather than positioning evaluation criteria as externally imposed or output–focused, the oskâyak (youth) articulated hope, belonging, purpose, and meaning as the benchmarks and foundation of miyo–ayāwin (wellness) enhancements. This repositioning is significant as it demonstrates how Indigenous oskâyak (youth) can define what matters in evaluation, shifting the locus of authority to the community and away from deficit–based or external measures of evaluation.

Second, the oskâyak (youth) connected cultural teachings and land–based practices with concrete developmental gains based on the thematic findings. Their reflections have been used to generate a youth–defined program logic model (i.e. strength–based youth–engaged evaluation framework which is beyond this study’s focus) in which cultural and land–based activities provide relational mentorship that fosters identity, skill–building, confidence, and resilience. This cultural logic model represents an important methodological innovation, illustrating how evaluation can capture causal pathways rooted in First Nation worldviews, and beyond the conventional biomedical indicators.

Finally, and similar to the above, this evaluation process itself seeded other new (and re–vitalized) youth–led initiatives within the Nation. Offshoots of this work are already informing other programs for the oskâyak (youth), including explorations of how to embed youth–defined, strength–based evaluation frameworks in broader Nation–driven evaluations. In these ways, this YPP evaluation has contributed to miyo–ayāwin (wellness) enhancements, self–discovery, capacity strengthening and sustainability by equipping youth with tools (tools they resourced themselves) to shape not only the assessment of their own program but also the design and evaluation of future initiatives.

These program–specific innovations highlight this study’s unique contributions as it documented not only the immediate benefits of the YPP for the oskâyak (youth), but also demonstrated how First Nation oskâyak (youth) can co–create evaluative frameworks, and knowledge translation strategies that are culturally grounded, relational, and sustainable.

Study limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, not every oskâyak (youth) participated in the conversations. Thus, it is possible that the dominant voices among the Oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) may have overshadowed, confirmed, or conformed the contributions of others who did not speak during the sessions. We tried to mitigate these potential biases by: a) having the option for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) to participate in individual conversations or send their responses via other forms of communication (e.g. email), b) engaging in a conversation session to share and check–in with the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) on the findings and to see if the interpretations reflected the voices of the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) that participated. Also, it is important to note that the findings from this study are not generalizable to other Indigenous communities in Canada.

Conclusions

The evaluation of the YPP for oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) showed that the program had a positive impact in promoting positive oskâyak (youth) development in the Nation, facilitating knowledge grounded in culture, and fostering a sense of belonging and purpose for the oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) through nurtured relationships. Land–based activities, cultural camps, fishing, hunting, archery, singing, and drumming were common cultural activities the young men and boys enjoyed and engaged in. This study showed that engaging oskinîkiwak (young men) and napêsisak (boys) in oskâyak–led activities facilitated self–confidence, fostered hope, enhanced life skills, and encouraged peer–support.
