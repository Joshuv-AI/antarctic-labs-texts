S-nitrosylation of EMMPRIN influences the migration of HSCs and MMP activity in liver fibrosis
Authors: Xinyan Zhu, Zihui Tang, Wei Li, Xiaojuan Li, Yasuko Iwakiri, Fei Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The mechanism of extracellular matrix metalloproteinase inducer (EMMPRIN) in the regulation of liver fibrosis has not been clarified. This study aims to investigate the role of EMMPRIN S-nitrosylation (SNO) in the regulation of hepatic stellate cell (HSC) migration and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activities in liver fibrosis. The results from the tissue microarrays and rat/mouse liver tissues suggest that EMMPRIN mRNA and protein levels in the fibrotic livers are lower than those in the corresponding normal control livers, but higher SNO level of EMMPRIN in fibrotic liver area was shown by immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence staining, and biotin-switch assay conversely in vivo. Primary EMMPRIN comes from hepatocytes and liver sinus epithelial cells (LSECs) rather than quiescent HSCs. To mimic the uptake of extrinsic EMMPRIN, supernatants from mouse primary hepatocytes/293 cells transfected with EMMPRIN wild-type plasmids (WT) and EMMPRIN SNO site (cysteine 87) mutation plasmids (MUT) were collected and added to JS-1/LX2 cell medium. The MUT EMMPRIN diminishes SNO successfully, enhances the activities of MMP2 and MMP9, and subsequently increases HSC migration. In conclusion, SNO of EMMPRIN influences HSC migration and MMP activities in liver fibrosis. This finding may shed light on the possible regulatory mechanism of MMPs in ECM accumulation in liver fibrosis.

Introduction

Extracellular matrix metalloproteinase inducer (EMMPRIN) is a glycosylated transmembrane protein, also known as Basigin (BSG), CD147, and HAb18G [ 1, 2]. EMMPRIN was first found in lung cancer cell lines to stimulate adjacent fibroblasts to secrete collagenase (MMP1) [3]. It is known to be involved in several cell functions and diseases, such as lung injury, myocardial ischemic injury, atherosclerosis, SLE, GVHD, RA, MS, and pathogen invasion [4].

The classical theory of liver fibrosis holds that the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) into myofibroblasts (MFBs) is considered the core factor in liver fibrosis [5]. After the activation of HSCs, a large amount of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins is secreted. The ECM synthesis and degradation imbalance can eventually form pseudolobules [6]. EMMPRIN is reportedly involved in the regulation of liver fibrosis. EMMPRIN can directly activate HSCs after liver injury with high expression of MMP-2, -9, -13 and -14, which are directly involved in regulating ECM remodeling [7]. EMMPRIN can activate HSCs by TGF-β1 and upregulate the expression of MMP-2 [8], forming a positive feedback loop between EMMPRIN and TGF-β1 to enhance the activation of HSCs [9]. In addition, EMMPRIN promotes liver fibrosis via crosstalk between hepatocytes and liver endothelial cells (LSECs) mediated by the VEGF-A/VEGFR2 signaling pathway [10]. However, as the inducer of MMPs, the role of EMMPRIN in liver fibrosis has not been fully clarified thus far.

Several studies have focused on the role of posttranslational modification of EMMPRIN in the fibrotic liver. The glycosylation of EMMPRIN has been extensively studied. The glycosylation status of EMMPRIN (highly glycosylated or poorly glycosylated) determines its ability to induce MMPs [11]. Similarly, S-nitrosylation (SNO) is another common protein posttranslational modification, a covalent bond formed by the nitric oxide (NO) group and the thiol chain of cysteine. Abnormal NO levels cause protein S-nitrosylation and thus change its function, which plays a critical regulatory role in the physiological and pathological processes of various diseases [ 12, 13]. SNO widely influences liver diseases [14], such as hepatocellular carcinoma [ 15, 16], hepatic steatosis [17], and cholestasis [18]. Nevertheless, SNO also promotes fibrogenesis. SNO-JNK increases JNK pathway activity and accelerates cardiac fibrosis [19], but little is known about SNO in liver fibrosis. Our group applied organelle proteomics analysis and showed that EMMPRIN is S-nitrosylated at the Golgi apparatus of hepatocytes. Cysteine 87 (C87) was identified as its potential SNO site, located in the extracellular 1 (EC1) domain of EMMPRIN [20]. Therefore, it will be interesting to explore whether EMMPRIN can be S-nitrosylated and its potential role in liver fibrosis.

In this study, we established a rat liver fibrosis model and examined the presence of EMMPRIN SNO in normal and fibrotic livers. Then, we generated a mutant EMMPRIN plasmid that lacks the SNO site and evaluated the effects of S-nitrosylated EMMPRIN on the function of HSCs, including the migration, proliferation and activities of MMP-2 and MMP-9.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male Wistar rats (140‒150 g) and male C57BL/6J (6 weeks) were obtained from Naval Medical University (Shanghai, China). Animals were maintained on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle at 22°C, given water ad libitum, fed with standard laboratory chow, and allowed to acclimatize for a minimum of 1 week. All experiments were conducted with the approval of the Animal Research Committee of Tongji University (Shanghai, China).

Liver fibrosis modelling and sample collection

Rat liver fibrosis was induced by intraperitoneal injections of 50% CCl 4 (Sinopharm, Beijing, China) twice a week (5 μL/g for the first time and 3 μL/g after that). Mouse liver fibrosis was induced by intraperitoneal injections of 10% CCl 4 (Sinopharm) twice a week (5 μL/g) and bile duct ligation (BDL) was performed following a previously reported method [21]. The number of animals in both the control and experimental groups was three. Animals were sacrificed after 8 weeks. Liver specimens were collected and routinely fixed in 4% phosphate-buffered formaldehyde for 18 to 24 h and embedded in paraffin in 3 days. The remaining liver tissue was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at ‒80°C until use.

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) and immunohistochemistry (IHC)

A liver microarray with multiple pathological types (DP087Lv01) was purchased from Xi’an Bioaitech Co., Ltd (Xi’an, China). The samples were analyzed by IHC using an anti-EMMPRIN antibody (13287; CST, Danvers, USA) according to standard methods and microarray instructions. The results were obtained from 5‒10 randomly selected fields of view, and then the average was taken.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Rat or mouse liver tissues were fixed with 10% Neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and enbeded in paraffin. Paraffin sections (6 μm) were used for histological analysis and immunolabelling. After being blocked, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Secondary antibodies conjugated with AlexaFluor dyes (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) were used to detect proteins of interest. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermo Fisher). Images were captured with a confocal system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Primary antibodies used were SNOC (N5411; Sigma-Aldrich), EMMPRIN (sc-46700, sc-374101; Sant Cruz Biotech, Sant Cruz, USA), and α-SMA (19245; CST).

Immunohistochemistry

The paraffin sections were treated as described in Immunofluorescence microscopy experiment. After being blocked, the sections were incubated with anti-SNOC antibody (N5411; Sigma-Aldrich) at 4°C overnight. An EnVision Detection Rabbit/Mouse Kit (GK500710; GeneTech, Shanghai, China) was used for the next step. The staining was terminated by observation under a light microscope (CX31-LV320; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured by a Zeiss Image System (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Mouse liver primary cell isolation

Primary mouse liver cells were isolated from livers of C57BL/6J mice. The mouse livers were perfused automatically with digestive enzyme I (0.05% collagenase Type IV and 0.1% pronase; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, USA). After being separated from the body, the digested livers were stirred gently with Hank’s solution containing 0.05% collagenase Type IV and 0.02% pronase at 37°C for 20 min. After filtration, the cell suspension was separated into parenchymal (pellet) and nonparenchymal (supernatant) parts by centrifugation for 2 min at 50 g. Hepatocytes in the pellet were resuspended and centrifuged again. HSCs and LSECs in the nonparenchymal parts were centrifuged at 1400 g for 10 min at 4°C, and then resuspended. The cell suspension was centrifuged using Nycodenz (Sigma-Aldrich) or Percoll (Sigma-Aldrich) gradient medium for HSCs and LSECs, respectively. The interphase containing HSCs or LSECs was collected and washed with MEM-α (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA) or EBM2 medium (Gibco).

Cell culture experiment

LX2 cells, 293 cells, JS-1 cells, and T6 cells were obtained from Prof. Wenfen Xie’s laboratory (Naval Medical University, Shanghai, China). All cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO 2.

Plasmid construction

EMMPRIN WT and C87 mutant plasmids (NG_007468.1 RefSeqGene) were synthesized by Sangon (Shanghai, China). The transfection of plasmids was performed using lipo-2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

HSC proliferation, migration, and scratch assay

Conditioned medium (CM) was collected from the supernatant of 293 cells after transfection of EMMPRIN WT and C87 mutant plasmids. LX2 or T6 cells were treated with CM to perform HSC proliferation, migration and scratch assays. LX2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3×10 3 cells/well, and cell proliferation was measured on d0, d1, d2, d3 and d4 using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8; Dojindo, Tokyo, Japan). Cell migration assay was performed using Transwell chambers (BD Bioscience, Sussex, USA). Briefly, LX2 or T6 cells (4×10 4) with CM were seeded in the upper chamber. After 48 h of incubation, cells migrated to the lower side of the membrane were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet and 20% methanol. Then images were captured and the number of cells in 5 selected fields was counted using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, USA). Cell migration was also measured by scratch assay. LX2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5×10 5 cells/well, and incubated with CM. After 100% confluent, scrape the cell monolayer in a straight line by a pipet tip. Images were captured by using an inverted microscope (DMI3000B; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at days 0, 2, 4, and 6. Then the images were analyzed quantitatively by using Image J (NIH, Bethesda, USA).

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Takara, Tokyo, Japan) as described by the manufacturer, and cDNA was generated from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara). qPCR was performed using the TB Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara) in a reaction volume of 20 μL, as described in the product manual. The primers are shown in Table 1. PCRs were performed for 40 cycles at 95°C for 45 s, 60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s on a 7500HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (ABI, Foster City, USA). Threshold cycle (Ct) values of each sample were normalized to β-actin expression, and the fold change for each mRNA was calculated using the 2 ‒ΔΔCt method [22]. Western blot analysis

The proteins of the cell lysate were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes. After being blocked with 5% nonfat milk or bovine serum albumin in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After 3 times wash with PBST, membranes were incubated with IRDye-conjugated secondary antibodies (IRDye® 800CW donkey anti-rabbit IgG, IRDye® 800CW donkey anti-mouse IgG, or IRDye® 680RD goat anti-mouse IgG; Li-Cor) or HRP-linked secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse IgG; 7076; CST) or goat anti-rabbit IgG; 7074; CST) for 1 h at room temperature. After wash with PBST two times and with PBS once, protein bands were detected with a Li-Cor Odyssey system (Li-Cor) or using ECL reagent (12757; CST) with an imaging analysis system (Tanon 4100; Tanon Science and Technology, Shanghai, China). Quantification of bands was performed by using the Odyssey software (Image Studio Lite Ver 5.2; Li-Cor) or the ImageJ program (NIH). Primary antibodies include EMMPRIN (sc-46700, sc-374101; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA), HA (3724; CST), eNOS (ab76198; Abcam, Cambridge, USA), GAPDH (37985-2; Signalway Antibody, College Park, USA), β-actin (YM3028; Immunoway, Plano, USA), and HNF1α (89670; CST).

Zymography assay

The cell samples used for zymography assay were harvested from 6-well plates by using lysis buffer (P0013C; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The samples from the supernatant were obtained from the media with or without serum. After samples were mixed with 4×Laemmli buffer (KL-25-05103; Kanglang, Shanghai, China) without heating, SDS-PAGE was performed under nonreducing condition using an 8% separating gel containing 2 mg/mL gelatin. After electrophoresis, the gel was incubated in detergent buffer (2.5% Triton X-100) with gentle shaking at room temperature for 15 min twice to remove the SDS. Then, the gel was incubated in reaction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM CaCl 2, 0.05% Brij-35 solution) at 37°C for 24 h. Next, the gel was incubated in Coomassie blue solution consisting of 0.1% Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (Sigma-Aldrich), 25% methanol, and 15% acetic acid for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. MMP2 and MMP9 activities were detected as a clear band on the resulting blue background of undigested gelatin. Gel images were scanned with Odyssey Imager (LI-COR Biosciences; Li-Cor, Lincoln, USA) and quantified using the Odyssey software (Image Studio Lite Ver 5.2; Li-Cor).

Biotin-switch assay

Biotin-switch assay was performed as reported in literature [23]. Briefly, after blockage of free thiols by methylmethanethiosulfonate (MMTS; Sigma-Aldrich), the nitrosothiol bonds were selectively decomposed to thiol bonds by ascorbate. Then, the newly formed thiols were reacted with N-[6-(biotin amido)hexyl]-3′-(2′-pyridyldithio)propionamide (biotin-HPDP; Thermo Fisher). After purification by using immobilized streptavidin, the biotin-bound protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Statistical analysis

All results were expressed as the mean±SEM. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA). Unpaired Student’s t-test was used for comparison between two groups. One-way ANOVA with unpaired Student’s t-test was used for multiple comparisons. Differences with P<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

EMMPRIN expression level is decreased in fibrotic livers

To investigate the role of EMMPRIN in pathological progression in patients with fibrosis, we performed IHC staining of tissue chips containing normal liver tissue ( n=11) and liver tissue samples from fibrotic liver ( n=20) and liver cirrhosis ( n=9) patients (patient details are provided in Supplementary Table S1). The results demonstrated that the expression of EMMPRIN significantly declined in the fibrotic liver compared with that in the normal control ( Figure 1A). We further constructed hepatic fibrosis models to verify the expression of EMMPRIN. The fibrosis model was successfully established by intraperitoneal injection of CCl 4 for 8 weeks in rats/mice or BDL induction for 4 weeks in mice, and verified by RT-PCR and Sirius Red staining ( Supplementary Figure S1). The mRNA and protein levels of EMMPRIN were significantly downregulated in rat fibrotic liver tissue induced by CCl 4 injection compared with those in control liver tissue ( Figure 1B). Similarly, EMMPRIN declined considerably in fibrotic liver tissues in the fibrosis models ( Figure 1C,D). These data indicated that EMMPRIN expression level is decreased in fibrotic livers. EMMPRIN transfers SNO after fibrosis

We evaluated the overall S-nitrosylated protein levels in mouse livers using an antibody against S-nitrosocysteine (SNOC). Immunohistochemistry results showed that the S-nitrosylated proteins were present in the normal liver sporadically, but the SNOC-positive area was increased in the fibrotic liver, particularly high in the fibrotic bridging areas ( Figure 2A). Furthermore, we determined S-nitrosylated EMMPRIN by a biotin-switch assay, in which S-nitrosylated cysteine residues can be replaced by biotin [23], and found that S-nitrosylated EMMPRIN level was higher in the CCl 4 group than in the control group, as revealed by western blot analysis ( Figure 2B). In particular, immunofluorescence staining in mouse livers showed that SNOC level in fibrotic livers was much higher than those in normal livers and that SNOC was colocalized with EMMPRIN in cells ( Figure 2C), despite a significant decrease in EMMPRIN in the fibrotic liver ( Supplementary Figure S2). Furthermore, tri-protein immunofluorescence costaining of α-SMA, EMMPRIN, and SNOC in fibrotic liver tissue suggested that colocalization of EMMPRIN and SNOC occurs in the fibrotic liver ( Figure 2D). These results demonstrated that EMMPRIN transfers SNO after fibrosis. EMMPRIN is not expressed in quiescent HSCs

We evaluated EMMPRIN level in rat primary hepatocytes, HSCs, and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) isolated from mouse livers both in controls and in CCl 4-induced fibrosis by western blot analysis. EMMPRIN was highly expressed in hepatocytes but not in HSCs among normal liver tissues. However, EMMPRIN was expressed in both hepatocytes and HSCs among fibrotic liver tissues ( Figure 3A). Immunofluorescence staining in mouse livers clearly showed that colocalization of EMMPRIN and α-SMA, the marker of HSCs, existed only in fibrotic liver tissues, not in the control ones ( Figure 3B). In conclusion, EMMPRIN does not exist in quiescent HSCs but is expressed in activated HSCs. HSCs can take up EMMPRIN

We hypothesized that EMMPRIN may be S-nitrosylated in hepatocytes during fibrogenesis and then released to regulate HSC functions, leading to increased fibrosis. An organelle proteomic study on the Golgi apparatus revealed that EMMPRIN is S-nitrosylated at its cysteine residue 87 (C87) of the EC1 domain, one of the two extracellular domains in the N-terminus of EMMPRIN [20]. Therefore, we synthesized the SNO null mutant of EMMPRIN (EMP MUT; C87V) by substituting the cysteine residue with valine at its SNO site and used the wild-type control plasmid (EMP WT as follows) as a control. To facilitate the detection of these plasmids, we tagged the plasmids with HA (human influenza hemagglutinin) at the N-terminal.

Firstly, 293T cells were transfected with these EMMPRIN plasmids for 48 h to test the transfection effect of the plasmids. Transfected 293 cells showed similar mRNA expression of EMP WT and MUT plasmids but a slightly lower level of EMP MUT protein than WT protein, suggesting that mutation might have decreased EMMPRIN protein stability and that SNO may be required for EMMPRIN protein stability ( Figure 4A). To confirm whether cysteine 87 (C87) is the SNO site in EMMPRIN, we performed a biotin-switch assay in protein lysates of 293 cells transfected with EMP WT or MUT plasmids ( Figure 4B). The results showed that EMP WT was S-nitrosylated in response to treatment with SNAP (an NO donor) but not EMP MUT, suggesting that mutation of C87 leads to the loss of SNO of EMMPRIN, which confirmed that C87 is the SNO site in EMMPRIN. To further investigate the possible effects of secreted EMMPRIN on hepatocytes and HSCs, we transfected primary hepatocytes isolated from mice with these EMMPRIN plasmids, collected conditioned medium, and treated JS-1 cells with conditioned medium derived from EMP WT or EMP MUT for 48 hours ( Figure 4C). It has been shown that the EC1 domain of EMMPRIN is cleaved by MT1-MMP and released extracellularly [24]. Although HSCs do not express EMMPRIN, they may take up the released EMMPRIN domain and influence HSC function. To verify this hypothesis, we measured EMP WT and MUT levels in primary hepatocyte lysates, primary hepatocyte supernatants (conditioned medium), and JS-1 cell lysates (conditioned medium). HA was detected in all these samples ( Figure 4D), suggesting that WT and MUT EMPs released from primary hepatocytes were taken up by JS-1 cells. These results support the possibility that HSCs could take up EMMPRIN released from hepatocytes.

S-nitrosylated EMMPRIN decreases the migration of HSCs via the downregulation of MMPs

To determine the effects of EMP WT and EMP MUT on HSC functions, we treated human and rat HSC cell lines LX2 and T6, respectively. Transwell assay was performed in cells incubated with conditioned medium from 293 cell supernatants expressing WT or MUT EMP. Both cell lines showed significantly increased migration in the EMP MUT group compared to the EMP WT and control (no transfection) groups ( Figure 5A). Similarly, cell scratch assay also showed greater cell migration in the EMP MUT group than in the other two groups ( Figure 5B). Then, we determined the effects of SNO on cell proliferation in LX2 cells by CCK-8 assay and found no difference among all three groups, suggesting that SNO of EMMPRIN does not influence the proliferation of HSCs ( Figure 5 C). Discussion

ECM regulation is a very complex process during liver fibrosis. MMPs and TIMPs are considered critical regulators of ECM turnover in liver fibrosis [25]. MMP-2 and MMP-9 increase during the fibrogenic process in the liver [26]. A question arises as to why ECM can be produced and accumulate to form liver fibrosis, although MMPs increase in the fibrotic process. We are curious to know if some factors can influence the ability of MMPs to degrade ECM.

EMMPRIN has one SNO site at C87, which can be S-nitrosylated in liver cells. EMMPRIN SNO was notably increased in the aorta of cirrhotic rats [20]. This suggests that EMMPRIN SNO plays a role in MMP activities. In this study, we demonstrated that EMMPRIN altered the SNO form after liver fibrosis and that the inhibition of SNO could enhance the activities of MMPs. This result suggested that SNO can suppress the induction role of EMMPRIN on MMPs, although MMP expression reaches a high level in the fibrotic liver, which might be part of the reason why elevated MMPs in the fibrotic liver cannot degrade ECM.

In addition, abnormal NO levels can lead to changes in the protein SNO, thus changing its function and playing a critical regulatory role in the physiological and pathological processes of many diseases [13]. NO donors can release NO spontaneously to provide external NO and promote SNO. Therefore, NO donors are an ideal tool to study SNO. In our study, we chose SNAP to promote SNO in 293 cells. The difference between EMP WT and EMP MUT confirmed the SNO site in EMMPRIN and the possibility of EMMPRIN SNO under NO stress.

NO is a byproduct of the oxidation of L-arginine to citrulline and is regulated by NO synthases (NOSs). In mammals, NOSs can be divided into three types: neuron NOS (nNOS or NOS1), inducible NOS (iNOS or NOS2), and endothelial NOS (eNOS or NOS3). nNOS and eNOS are expressed stably and constitutively, in which eNOS can produce a small amount of NO to maintain hepatic homeostasis and play a protective role under pathological conditions. iNOS is not expressed in quiescent cells but can be induced by immune stimulation, such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and inflammatory cytokines, producing a large amount of NO in liver diseases, including fibrosis. The role of nNOS in liver biology is unclear [27]. iNOS and eNOS are the major participants in liver physiological alterations, and their expression levels are regulated by many signaling pathways and transcription factors, including NF-κB, STAT-1, IRF-1, AP-1, TCF-4, TCF-11, and STAT-3 [28]. Knocking down or knocking out iNOS can inhibit SNO [29], while eNOS derived from LSECs can form a high concentration NO “pool” nearby, thus promoting SNO [30]. Although EMMPRIN can be S-nitrosylated in liver fibrosis and deactivate MMPs, its process and regulatory mechanism are still relatively obscure. Further studies on the role of NOSs in EMMPRIN SNO are required to unveil the mechanism of MMP regulation in liver fibrosis.
