Comparing cup implantation errors in total hip arthroplasty following rotational acetabular osteotomy: computed tomography-based navigation versus robotic arm-assisted system
Authors: Yohei Matsushita, Hajime Sugiyama, Tetsuo Hayama, Ayano Amagami, Ryuichi Sato, Makoto Sato, Takuya Maeda, Mitsuru Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic arm-assisted total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been reported to achieve acetabular cup implantation that more accurately reproduces the preoperative plan than computed tomography (CT)-based navigation. However, whether this superiority applies in technically demanding cases, such as THA following pelvic osteotomy, remains unclear. Therefore, we evaluated the cup-positioning errors in THA performed with these technologies in patients who had previously undergone rotational acetabular osteotomy (RAO).MethodsTwenty-three patients (24 hips) underwent navigated THA between January 2017 and January 2021 (Navigation group), and 22 patients (24 hips) underwent robotic-assisted THA between February 2021 and February 2023 (Mako group). The error between preoperative planning and postoperative results was calculated and compared between the groups. The absolute values of errors in cup orientation (radiographic inclination and anteversion) and position (mediolateral, anteroposterior, and superoinferior axes) were assessed.ResultsAll absolute errors, except for the superoinferior axis in the Navigation group (3.4 ± 3.5 mm), were within 3° or 3 mm, indicating high accuracy. The absolute error in radiographic inclination was significantly lower in the Mako group than in the Navigation group (0.8 ± 0.8° versus 1.9 ± 1.3°; p = 0.005), whereas errors in radiographic anteversion and position did not differ significantly between the groups. No cup-related complications were noted in either group. From baseline to 2 years postoperatively, the Japanese Orthopaedic Association hip score improved significantly in the Navigation group (from 46.0 ± 9.3 to 89.7 ± 5.9) and the Mako group (from 50.0 ± 12.6 to 93.3 ± 5.8) (both p < 0.001), demonstrating satisfactory clinical outcomes.ConclusionsIn technically demanding THA after RAO, CT-based navigation and robotic-assisted systems demonstrated comparable acetabular cup placement errors and satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes. Although robotic-assisted THA was associated with a significantly smaller error in radiographic inclination, overall cup placement errors did not differ significantly between the two technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09508-3.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Robotic arm-assisted surgery, Computed tomography-based navigation surgery, Acetabular dysplasia, Rotational acetabular osteotomy

Introduction

How the acetabular cup is implanted in total hip arthroplasty (THA) has a substantial impact on postoperative outcomes [1]. In this study, accurate cup implantation refers to how precisely the postoperative cup position reproduces the preoperative plan. To improve this accuracy, systems such as the computed tomography (CT)-based navigation system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) and the Mako robotic arm-assisted system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) have been developed and have demonstrated satisfactory results in primary THA [2, 3]. Among these, the Mako system has been reported to provide superior accuracy, owing to robotic assistance, compared with the navigation system in primary THA without prior pelvic osteotomy (PO) [4].

Various types of PO have been developed to treat acetabular dysplasia (AD) and have shown favorable clinical outcomes [5, 6]. Of these procedures, rotational acetabular osteotomy (RAO) [7] is primarily performed in Japan. However, performing THA after PO remains technically challenging due to altered acetabular morphology, including changes in anteversion and the loss of bone stock, regardless of the osteotomy type [8, 9]. Specifically, difficulties in achieving the intended cup alignment have been reported [8], and postoperative cup placement tends to be positioned more superiorly and laterally in THA after PO than in THA performed in patients without prior PO [10].

To date, no study has directly compared the accuracy of cup placement between the navigation and Mako systems in patients undergoing THA after PO. We hypothesized that the Mako system would provide more accurate cup implantation than the navigation system in THA following PO, as observed in primary THA. In this study, we evaluated and compared the errors between the preoperative plan and the actual postoperative cup alignment and position when using these two technologies in THA after RAO, based on CT data.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study, which evaluated and compared the errors between the preoperative plan and the actual postoperative cup alignment and position in THA after RAO, was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital (approval number: krh-2023-013), and all patients provided informed consent for use of their data. This was a retrospective study based on the medical records of consecutive surgeries performed by a single hip specialist (H.S.) at our hospital (Kanagawa Rehabilitation Hospital, Kanagawa, Japan). All patients had previously undergone RAO and subsequently underwent THA using the posterolateral approach. Twenty-three patients (24 hips) who underwent THA using the CT-based navigation system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) between January 2017 and January 2021 (Navigation group) and 22 patients (24 hips) who underwent THA using the Mako robotic arm-assisted system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) between February 2021 and February 2023 (Mako group) were included in the study (Fig. 1). All patients were followed up for 2 years after surgery, and this period was used as the observation period.

Allocation of patients All patients had previously undergone rotational acetabular osteotomy (RAO) and subsequently underwent total hip arthroplasty (THA). Patients who underwent THA between January 2017 and January 2021 received surgery using a computed tomography -based navigation system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) (Navigation group; 23 patients, 24 hips), whereas those who underwent THA between February 2021 and February 2023 received surgery using the Mako system (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) (Mako group; 22 patients, 24 hips)

RAO surgical techniques and cases where THA was required after RAO

Using a modified Ollier transtrochanteric approach to expose the lateral aspect of the acetabulum, we performed the osteotomy with a curved osteotome and rotated the osteotomized fragment anterolaterally. The target lateral center-edge angle [11] was 30°. After filling the gap between the ilium and the rotated fragment with β-tricalcium phosphate blocks (OSferion; Olympus Terumo Biomaterials, Tokyo, Japan) instead of bone grafts, two or three absorbable screws (Super-Fixsorb; Teijin Medical Technology, Osaka, Japan), 60–70 mm in length and 4.5 mm in diameter, were used to fix the fragment to the ilium. These screws were composed of hydroxyapatite particles and poly-L-lactide. Finally, the greater trochanter was secured with two or three absorbable screws of the same type, each 6.5 mm in diameter (Fig. 2B). In patients who later required conversion to THA due to progression of osteoarthritis, the accuracy of cup implantation may be compromised by various acetabular abnormalities, including defects of the anterior or posterior acetabular walls (Fig. 2D) [9, 12].

Preoperative planning

The CT images (Revolution EVO; General Electric Healthcare, Boston, MA, USA) of 0.625-mm slice thickness from the iliac bone to the distal femur, which were obtained 1 month before the surgery, were imported into the three-dimensional (3D) planning system of the CT-based hip navigation (version 1.3; Stryker Leibinger, Freiburg Germany) in the Navigation group (Fig. 3A), and into the built-in 3D planning system of the Mako system in the Mako group (Fig. 4A). Then, preoperative plans were made, and the following process remained the same. A functional pelvic coordinate system was used for cup planning in both groups. The target cup inclination was radiographic inclination (RI) of 40° and radiographic anteversion (RA) of 20°. The target position was expressed by the center of rotation (COR) [13]. When determining the target position of the cup, the bottom edge of the cup was placed at the same level of the bottom edge of the teardrop; however, when the cup CE angle (cup-CE angle) [14] was < 0° or when a large bone defect was noted, the cup was moved upward or inward to ensure adequate press-fit fixation.

Implantation of the cup during surgery

In the Navigation group, the implanted cups included Tritanium (Stryker; n = 13), G7 (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA; n = 6), and Pinnacle Gription (DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA; n = 5). For each type of cup, a designated mount was created to allow the tracker of Stryker’s Navigation system to be attached to the shaft of the reamer and cup impactor, enabling reaming and cup insertion to be performed in the same manner as with Stryker’s cup. In the Mako group, Trident II Tritanium cups (Stryker) were used.

In both groups, the decision on whether to add screw fixation was made intraoperatively by the surgeon based on information on the condition of the acetabular host bone, extent of the bone defect, and strength of the press fit. Bulk bone grafting was not performed in this study.

Postoperative evaluation

The planning system of the Mako system does not have a function to verify the accuracy of implantation after surgery and is not compatible with other planning software. Therefore, to verify the accuracy of cup placement, it is necessary to use other software [2, 4, 15], as follows (Fig. 4). In the Navigation group, although CT-based hip navigation can verify the accuracy of implantation, the same verification procedure with the Mako group was adopted to unify the verification conditions (Fig. 3).

First, the preoperative CT data were imported into a 3D preoperative planning software (ZedHip; Lexi, Tokyo, Japan), and all reference points in the original preoperative plan, which were made in the CT-based hip navigation or Mako system, were manually copied using a functional pelvic coordinate system. Computer-aided design (CAD) models of the cups were manually placed onto their images according to the original preoperative plan (Figs. 3B and 4B). Second, postoperative CT data, which were obtained 4 weeks postoperatively, were imported into ZedHip, and all reference points were copied again. CAD models of the cups were manually superimposed onto the contour of the implanted cup (Figs. 3C and 4C).

Finally, the differences between those planned preoperatively and measured postoperatively were automatically calculated using ZedHip (Figs. 3D and 4D) [2]. This difference was expressed in terms of the RI and RA for orientation and each axis (mediolateral [ML], anteroposterior [AP], and superoinferior [SI] axes) for the COR (Fig. 5). Using the functional pelvic plane as a reference, the axes were oriented such that the anterior, lateral, and superior directions were defined as positive.

Measurement items of cup alignment and position Cup alignment was evaluated by measuring radiographic inclination (RI) and radiographic anteversion (RA). Cup position was assessed by determining the difference between the actual cup center of rotation (COR) and the COR in the preoperative plan along each axis (mediolateral [ML], anteroposterior [AP], and superoinferior [SI]). Using the functional pelvic plane as a reference, the axes were oriented such that the anterior, lateral, and superior directions were defined as positive

All procedures were performed independently by the same examiner (Y.M.) twice, with a two-month interval, and once by a fellow (T.M.) who had completed training at a hip joint specialist hospital for more than six months.

As a supplemental radiographic evaluation, we measured the cup-CE angle [14], as well as the vertical and horizontal distances of the cup. These measurement items were evaluated in the coronal view of the preoperative plan imported into ZedHip, using the slice in which the center of the femoral head was clearly visualized. The vertical distance, as described in a previous study [10], was defined as the perpendicular distance from the interteardrop line—which connects the inferior edges of both teardrops—to the center of the femoral head component. The horizontal distance was defined as the distance between the inferior edge of the teardrop and the point at which a perpendicular line drawn from the center of the femoral head component intersects the interteardrop line. If the interteardrop line was not clearly identifiable, a line connecting the lower margins of the ischial tuberosities was used as a supplemental reference. Additionally, cup loosening on the 2-year postoperative anteroposterior radiograph was evaluated according to the classification described by Tompkins et al. [16], based on radiographic findings such as radiolucent lines ≥ 2 mm or component migration.

Information on patient background characteristics, surgery, complications, and clinical scores before and 2 years after surgery was collected from medical records and assessed. Clinical scores were based on the Japanese Orthopaedic Association hip score (JOA score) [17]. The JOA score, which is widely used in Japan, consists of 40 points assigned for pain, 20 for range of motion, 20 for walking ability, and 20 for activities of daily living, with a maximum total score of 100 points.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise indicated. We compared the absolute values of the errors in cup orientation and position between the Navigation and Mako groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. We also evaluated the actual cup orientation and evaluated whether it fell within Lewinnek’s safe zone (RI: 30°–50°; RA: 5°–25°) [18]. Moreover, because three different acetabular cup implants were used in the Navigation group, we evaluated whether the errors differed among the three implant types. If the data were normally distributed, one-way analysis of variance was applied; if not, the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was used. Differences in demographic data and pre and 2-year postoperative JOA scores were analyzed. Comparisons were performed using the Fisher exact test for categorical variables, two-sample or paired t-test for continuous variables with normal distributions, and the Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test for nonparametric data. A power analysis (α = 0.05, power = 0.80) was performed to confirm that the sample size was sufficient. Based on effect sizes calculated from a previous study [4], detecting a clinically meaningful difference of 3° or 3 mm [15] required at least 8 hips per group. As the present study included 24 hips in each group, the sample size was considered sufficient. After calculating the errors, we also calculated the minimal detectable difference (MDD) to interpret the clinical relevance of our findings. Intra- and interobserver reliabilities were assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for these estimates. ICCs were classified using the method reported by Landis and Koch: 1–0.81, almost perfect agreement; 0.6–0.80, substantial agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; and 0.21–0.40, fair agreement [19]. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (version 30; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and statistical significance was set at a P value < 0.05.

Results

Comparison of patient background characteristics and surgical details

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, sex, body mass index, time from RAO to THA, blood loss, or operative time (Table 1). The frequency of screw insertion was significantly lower in the Mako group than in the Navigation group (42% versus [vs.] 71%; P = 0.039). In the evaluation of cup position relative to the pelvis—including the planned preoperative cup–CE angle and planned horizontal and vertical distances—no statistically significant differences were noted between the groups (Table 1). In the Navigation group, one case of femoral fracture occurred during stem implantation, but there were no complications regarding cup implantation in either group. From baseline to 2 years postoperatively, the JOA score improved significantly in both groups (Navigation group: from 46.0 ± 9.3 to 89.7 ± 5.9; Mako group: from 50.0 ± 12.6 to 93.3 ± 5.8; both P < 0.001). On the radiographs obtained 2 years postoperatively, all cups in both groups were evaluated as stable.

Comparison of the cup alignment errors

The measured RI and RA values were within Lewinnek’s safe zone (RI: 30°–50°; RA: 5°–25°) [18] for all cases in both groups (Fig. 6). In terms of the absolute value of the error in RI, the Mako group had a significantly smaller error than the Navigation group (0.8 ± 0.8 vs. 1.9 ± 1.3; P = 0.005) (Table 2). However, there was a trend toward fewer errors in RA in the Mako group than in the Navigation group, but there was no clear difference between the two groups. The ICCs for the measurement of cup orientation were > 0.6 for intra- and interobserver reliabilities (Table 3). The MDDs, calculated using inter-observer ICCs and standard deviations, were 2.1° for RI and 1.3° for RA. There were no significant differences in errors among the three implant types used in the Navigation group (Kruskal–Wallis, P > 0.05; Additional File 1).

Comparison of the cup position errors in each axis

In the evaluation of cup position accuracy, there was no significant difference in the COR error in each axis (ML, AP, and SI) between the two groups, but the mean error was smaller in the Mako group than in the Navigation group for all items (Table 2). The ICCs for the measurement of cup position were > 0.6 for intra- and inter-observer reliabilities (Table 3). The MDDs, calculated on the basis of inter-observer ICCs and standard deviations, were 2.0, 2.2, and 2.7 mm for ML, AP, and SI, respectively. No significant differences in errors were observed among the three implant types used in the navigation group (Kruskal–Wallis, p > 0.05; Additional File 1).

Discussion

This is the first study to measure and compare the errors of acetabular cup implantation using navigation and Mako robotic systems in THA following RAO, a procedure generally considered technically challenging. In both groups, all cups were positioned within Lewinnek’s safe zone, and the majority of absolute mean errors in cup implantation were < 3° or 3 mm, suggesting that these technologies can enable highly accurate implant placement. Although the Mako system was associated with a significantly smaller error in radiographic inclination, no significant differences were observed between the two systems in the other parameters of acetabular cup implantation.

Comparison of the cup alignment errors

The navigation and Mako robotic systems have been reported to provide more accurate cup implantation than conventional techniques in primary THA [20, 21] and in THA for osteoarthritis secondary to acetabular dysplasia [3, 15], which is generally considered technically demanding. In THA following PO, including RAO, accurate cup placement is particularly challenging due to factors such as acetabular wall bone defects, collapse of the osteotomized fragment, and altered acetabular version [8, 9, 12]. In fact, Osawa et al. reported that the frequency of cup orientation within Lewinnek’s safe zone was significantly lower in THA after RAO than in primary THA (69% vs. 84%, P = 0.038) [12].

However, the accuracy of cup implantation using the navigation and Mako systems in THA after PO has not yet been fully elucidated. In the present study, cup orientation was within Lewinnek’s safe zone in all cases, demonstrating that reproducible cup placement is feasible even after RAO when using these technologies.

Comparison of the cup position errors in each axis

With respect to cup position, previous reports in primary THA without prior PO have shown the following mean errors using the navigation and Mako systems, respectively: ML, 1.9 ± 1.6 mm and 1.2 ± 0.9 mm; AP, 3.1 ± 2.5 mm and 1.1 ± 0.9 mm; and SI, 3.7 ± 2.5 mm and 2.0 ± 1.3 mm [2, 4]. In most cases in our study, errors were within 3 mm, and the results were comparable to those of previous studies in primary THA. We believe that these favorable results can be attributed to the ability of both systems to provide real-time feedback on cup orientation and position during reaming and implantation.

Differences in errors and their clinical relevance

Although the groups in this study were not randomized, no significant differences were noted in baseline patient characteristics. Additionally, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in the planned preoperative cup–CE angle or planned horizontal and vertical distances. The error in RI was significantly smaller in the Mako group than in the Navigation group. For the other parameters, although the Mako group tended to show smaller errors, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Ando et al. reported smaller cup placement errors with the Mako system compared with navigation in primary THA without prior PO [4]. Our findings in post-RAO cases may be interpreted in a similar context. While navigation systems provide real-time visualization of implant orientation from reaming through cup implantation, the procedure still relies on manual control, which may introduce discrepancies between the preoperative plan and the actual cup position. Importantly, this type of discrepancy reflects the limitations of manual execution during navigation-assisted THA and should not be interpreted as an error inherent to the navigation system itself. In contrast, the Mako system provides robotic-assisted control during reaming and implantation, reducing manual variability and possibly contributing to the improved accuracy observed in our study. However, as the calculated MDDs were small, the differences between the two systems may not be clinically meaningful for experienced hip surgeons.

Issues related to compatibility and evaluation

The Mako system does not have a function for verifying the accuracy of implantation or compatibility with other software; therefore, manually transferring the preoperative plan to another software is necessary for accuracy verification [2, 4]. However, this is a subjective and complicated process, and there may be limitations in intra and interobserver reliabilities. In this study, the ICCs were > 0.6 in all cases and > 0.8 in approximately half of the cases; hence, we think that the ICCs were substantial to good and that a certain degree of reproducibility was guaranteed. Although the Mako system is useful, its poor compatibility with other systems hinders objective evaluation.

Limitations

First, although the Navigation group used a navigation system made by Stryker, not all cups were made by Stryker. We created a designated mount to use Stryker’s tracker and a navigation system for cup implantation made by other companies. Although the results of the Navigation group were good, they may not reflect the accuracy of the Stryker’s navigation system in a strict sense. Moreover, because the cups used by the two groups were different, we must consider the possibility that the performance of the cups affected the accuracy of implantation. Second, many Japanese patients with acetabular dysplasia are female [22]. Therefore, the proportion of women who underwent THA after RAO in this study was high. Thus, the data may be biased by sex. Third, the planned cup–CE angle and the decision regarding the use of supplemental screws were determined by the surgeon based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings and inevitably involved a degree of the surgeon’s subjective judgment. Indeed, the Mako group tended to have a smaller planned cup–CE angle than the Navigation group, although the difference was not statistically significant. Fourth, although the primary study aim was to compare the errors in cup alignment and position between the preoperative plan and the actual implantation using early postoperative CT data, it is also vital to evaluate how accurately the anatomical hip center was reconstructed and how reliably long-term biological fixation can be achieved. These aspects could not be assessed in the present study and should be considered a limitation. Finally, since RAO is a type of PO that is primarily performed in Japan, our findings may have limited generalizability when applied to the postoperative evaluation of THA following other types of PO.

Conclusions

In THA after RAO, CT-based navigation and robotic systems demonstrated cup placement errors that remained small and within clinically acceptable ranges. While robotic-assisted THA tended to provide smaller cup placement errors compared with CT-based navigation, a statistically significant difference was observed only for radiographic inclination.
