Histopathology‐based assessment of the tumour microenvironment in gastrointestinal cancers: practical approaches to prognostication and treatment stratification
Authors: Shelly Yunbi Ni, Howard Ho‐Wai Leung, Anthony Wing‐Hung Chan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tumour microenvironment (TME) is a key factor in carcinogenesis by affecting tumour growth, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry provide accessible and reproducible methodologies for TME evaluation, with growing evidence supporting their prognostic and predictive significance in gastrointestinal cancers. This commentary examines four papers published in the Journal of Pathology: Clinical Research that illustrate this methodology across various gastrointestinal cancers. The Tumour Microenvironment Score, Combined Immune Checkpoint Stromal Score, Immune Score for Gastric Cancer, and the multicomponent microenvironment and tumour inflammatory features panel have been developed to assess TME characteristics in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm. The reviewed studies demonstrate that basic morphological characteristics together with the expression of immune checkpoint markers and immune cell markers enable the development of effective clinical tools to enhance risk assessment and treatment planning. These practical, cost‐effective, and promising methods need to undergo extensive multicentre testing with established protocols, digital pathology, and molecular information integration.

The tumour microenvironment (TME) is a key factor in carcinogenesis by affecting tumour growth, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. Understanding the complex and dynamic interactions between tumour cells and their microenvironment, encompassing immune cells, stromal components, and inflammatory mediators, will help find biomarkers for predicting patient outcomes and formulating treatment strategies. The challenge of bringing TME research to clinical practice is developing a scientifically valid and simple evaluation method. While sophisticated molecular techniques offer detailed insights into the immune landscape, they have significant challenges, including high costs, lengthy processing times, and the need for specialised expertise. In contrast, assessing TME by conventional histology and immunohistochemistry methods may provide comparable prognostic and predictive capabilities. Gastrointestinal malignancies constitute a substantial global health challenge, accounting for 26% of cancer incidence and 35% of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1]. It is worth investigating whether TME evaluation using histology and immunohistochemistry is beneficial in gastrointestinal malignancies. The Tumour Microenvironment Score (TMS), Combined Immune Checkpoint Stromal Score (CICSS), Immune Score for Gastric Cancer (ISGC), and multicomponent microenvironment and tumour inflammatory features (MoTIFs) are four distinct histological TME scores formulated for various gastrointestinal cancers in these four studies.

Colorectal cancer is the commonest gastrointestinal malignancy, accounting for 38.8% and 26.0% of all gastrointestinal cancer cases and deaths, respectively [1]. Hatthakarnkul et al demonstrated how basic histopathological TME features can predict stages I–III colorectal cancer prognosis [2]. They developed a Tumour Microenvironment Score (TMS) by analysing 855 patients in the discovery cohort and confirming it with 671 patients in an independent validation cohort. The TMS system used three readily measurable histological features, which include Klintrup‐Mäkinen grade (KMG) (the extent of immune cells at the invasive front), tumour stroma percentage, and tumour budding assessment. It classified patients into four distinct groups with different clinical outcomes: TMS0 (high KMG), TMS1 (intermediate), TMS2 (high stromal content or high tumour budding), and TMS3 (high stromal content and high tumour budding). The significant difference in survival rates among these groups—TMS3 patients exhibiting 31–48% 5‐year survival versus 72–86% for TMS0—illustrated the efficacy of this simple TME evaluation. The prognostic significance in rectal cancer was particularly noticeable, as TMS3 patients demonstrated exceedingly poor outcomes (10–16% 5‐year survival). The TMS was shown to be an independent prognostic factor apart from those established prognostic factors, including staging, margin status, vascular invasion, and peritoneal involvement. The biological basis of these histopathological results became clearer through molecular studies that examine mutations and transcriptomes. The aggressive TMS3 tumours displayed a high rate of TP53 mutations and enhanced activity in epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, and immune suppression pathways. TMS3 patients may be vulnerable to therapeutic approaches targeting EMT‐related proteins. The authors demonstrated that the assessment of fundamental histopathological TME characteristics could not only forecast clinical outcomes but also delineate a subgroup of patients suitable for possible targeted therapies.

Al‐Badran et al evaluated the TME prognostic value among patients with stage I–III colorectal cancer, focusing on the expression of immune checkpoint proteins [3]. They examined the expression of PD‐1, PD‐L1, TIM‐3, and LAG‐3 in 773 patients by immunohistochemistry. High stromal immune cell expression of PD‐1, TIM‐3, and LAG‐3 was associated with better overall survival, whereas high tumoural expression of PD‐1 and LAG‐3 was associated with poorer overall survival. They developed a Combined Immune Checkpoint Stromal Score (CICSS) by combining the expression of PD‐1, TIM‐3, and LAG‐3 in stromal immune cells and stratified patients into three prognostically different groups: CICSS 3 (high expression of all three immune checkpoint proteins), CICSS 2 (high expression of any two immune checkpoint proteins), and CICSS 1 (other combinations). This combined score showed better prognostic power than individual protein expression but was not an independent prognostic factor. The CICSS 3 group showed the best overall survival and was associated with a higher tumour stroma percentage and intratumoural immune cell infiltrate, especially CD3+ T cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, and FoxP3+ Tregs. It also possessed a higher percentage of defective mismatch repair (28% compared to 12% in CICSS 1 and 2), further indicating immune checkpoint inhibitor susceptibility. This study offered significant insights into immune checkpoint expression in colorectal cancer; however, it would have been enhanced by a validation cohort to substantiate its findings, as well as mutational and transcriptomic data to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

As the second commonest gastrointestinal malignancy, stomach cancer accounts for 21.7% and 23.2% of all gastrointestinal cancer cases and deaths, respectively [1]. Zhao et al developed and validated the Immune Score for Gastric Cancer (ISGC) to predict neoadjuvant chemotherapy efficacy and overall survival in advanced gastric cancer patients, based on immunohistochemistry of the TME characteristics [4]. They examined paired pre‐ and post‐neoadjuvant treatment tumour samples from 104 patients with stage III/IV gastric adenocarcinoma and measured the densities of infiltrating immune cells. Elevated pre‐treatment levels of infiltrating CD3+ and CD8+ T cells, alongside CD68+ and CD11c+ macrophages (indicators of anti‐tumour immunity), were significantly linked to a positive response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, whereas increased FoxP3+ Tregs and CD163+ macrophages (markers of immunosuppression) were associated with therapeutic resistance. The ISGC, employing logistic regression that combined immune score‐T cells and immune score‐macrophages, had significant predictive capability for neoadjuvant chemotherapy response and served as an independent prognostic indicator for overall survival. The ISGC‐high group exhibited 95–100% complete or partial responses to the neoadjuvant therapy, in contrast to the ISGC‐low group, which demonstrated 11–28% complete or partial responses. The neoadjuvant chemotherapy remodelled the tumour microenvironment by enhancing anti‐tumour immune cell infiltration, reducing immunosuppressive populations, and upregulating PD‐L1 expression, particularly in treatment responders, indicating potential implications for subsequent immunotherapy. The rise in the PD‐L1 positive rate after neoadjuvant therapy was primarily noted in the ISGC‐high group. The ISGC could serve as a biomarker for the postoperative implementation of combination immunotherapy. This study established that the ISGC is a viable, clinically relevant biomarker for directing personalised treatment and enhancing outcomes in advanced gastric cancer. In contrast to this study, Hatthakarnkul et al and Al‐Badran et al excluded patients with colorectal cancers receiving neoadjuvant therapy [2, 3]. The alterations in the TME in colorectal cancer following neoadjuvant therapy merit further investigation.

Gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm (GEP‐NEN) is a rare gastrointestinal malignancy, but its incidence is increasing globally [5]. There exists a relatively small number of studies examining the TME in GEP‐NEN. Milione et al's study addressed a knowledge deficit in GEP‐NEN prognostication by integrating recognised histological criteria with TME‐derived markers [6]. The authors analysed 350 GEP‐NEN tissue samples through immunohistochemistry to detect markers associated with immune response, inflammation, angiogenesis, proliferation, and fibroblast activity. They discovered that the multicomponent microenvironment and tumour inflammatory features (MoTIFs) panel, which included tumoural COX‐2, stromal PD‐1, tumoural and stromal HLA‐I, as well as stromal HLA‐DR expression, functioned as an independent factor for predicting disease‐free survival. They created a nomogram that combined MoTIFs panel results with tumour morphology to produce better disease‐free survival predictions. They demonstrated that neuroendocrine neoplasms exhibit major differences in their TME between grade 1/2 neuroendocrine tumours and grade 3 neuroendocrine carcinomas. This grade‐related immune evolution was characterised by higher levels of adaptive immunity markers (CD3, PD‐1, PD‐L1), which were countered by immune escape mechanisms such as HLA class I downregulation and the activation of immunosuppressive pathways. They identified a specific subgroup of grade 3 neuroendocrine carcinomas that maintained HLA class I expression and displayed spontaneous adaptive immune system activation through CD3, CD4, CD8, PD‐1, and PD‐L1 co‐expression, which is potentially beneficial for immunotherapy. Their results supported current findings from GEP‐NEN immunotherapy clinical trials. The use of single‐agent immune checkpoint inhibitors produced minimal benefits for patients with grade 1/2 neuroendocrine tumours. Dual immune checkpoint inhibitor combinations and chemo‐immunotherapy regimens produced better treatment outcomes for patients with grade 3 neuroendocrine tumours and neuroendocrine carcinomas, while some patients achieved long‐lasting responses to the therapy [5].

These four studies demonstrate the accuracy of TME scoring systems to predict outcomes and guide treatments, but multiple issues need to be resolved before their adoption in standard clinical practice. First, the clinical applicability of TME scoring systems needs validation by large‐scale multi‐institutional studies. Second, the evaluation process requires standardised protocols and quality control methods to ensure reproducible results across different institutions and pathologists. The use of digital pathology instruments with artificial intelligence‐based scoring systems provides an objective and reliable method to reduce intra‐ and inter‐observer variability, which makes sophisticated scoring methods more accessible. The digital evaluation performed by QuPath software showed high agreement with manual pathologist assessments, according to Al‐Badran et al [3]. Third, the relationship between histopathology‐based TME scoring and established molecular classification systems remains poorly understood. The study by Hatthakarnkul et al showed that TMS and Consensus Molecular Subtypes share little similarity [2]. Future studies need to explore how molecular changes affect histological and immunohistochemical features in tumours. Finally, the current scoring methods fail to capture the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of the TME. The present scoring methodologies could be refined through correlation with more advanced spatial biology techniques, including multiplex immunohistochemistry and spatial transcriptomics [7]. The study by Zhao et al also showed how the TME evolves through time following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The monitoring of TME alterations may require patients to undergo multiple biopsy procedures during their treatment course. Multiplex immunohistochemistry, combined with multispectral imaging technology, facilitates the rapid and precise automated evaluation of various markers with a single pathology slide, as well as overcomes the constraints of limited biopsied tumour tissue.

In conclusion, the reviewed studies demonstrate that basic morphological characteristics together with immune checkpoint markers and immune cell markers enable the development of effective prognostic and predictive tools for different gastrointestinal malignancies. These promising methods require extensive multicentre testing with established protocols, digital pathology, and molecular information integration. The histopathology‐based TME scoring system is a scientifically valid, practical, and cost‐effective method to enhance risk assessment and treatment planning.
