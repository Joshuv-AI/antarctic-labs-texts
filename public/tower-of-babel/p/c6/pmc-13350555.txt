Dialysis and Pregnancy: Optimizing Therapy for Best Maternal and Fetal Outcomes
Authors: Shreepriya Mangalgi, Silvi Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPregnancy in women with end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) requiring dialysis remains high risk, with significant maternal and fetal complications. While fertility rates are markedly reduced in this population, advances in dialysis care and obstetric management have led to increasing reports of successful pregnancies. Important maternal adverse outcomes include early pregnancy loss, preeclampsia, polyhydramnios, and cesarean delivery, while common adverse fetal outcomes are preterm birth, low birth weight, and high neonatal intensive care utilization. Evidence consistently demonstrates that dialysis intensification—achieving longer treatment hours or targeting lower maternal blood urea nitrogen levels—correlates with improved gestational age and birth weight, thereby enhancing live birth rates. Optimizing therapy further requires individualized dialysis prescriptions, careful volume and blood pressure management, and timely adjustment of dialysate composition. A multidisciplinary approach involving nephrology, maternal‐fetal medicine, obstetrics, gynecology, and neonatology is critical for care coordination. Despite these advances, significant disparities persist, particularly in access to intensive dialysis and specialized care worldwide. Future directions include strengthening obstetric nephrology training, expanding research in peritoneal and home hemodialysis during pregnancy, and establishing integrated care models. With careful planning and optimization of renal replacement therapy, maternal and fetal outcomes in this high‐risk population can continue to improve. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on fertility, dialysis strategies, and maternal–fetal outcomes in pregnant women with ESKD on dialysis, while highlighting practical considerations for dialysis optimization and multidisciplinary care, thereby addressing gaps in consolidated guidance for clinicians managing this increasingly encountered high‐risk population.

Pregnancy on Dialysis: Epidemiology, Outcomes, and Fertility Challenges

Successful pregnancy in women with end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD), particularly those on dialysis is uncommon. However, with improved obstetric and dialysis care, pregnancy rates are increasing in the dialysis population. A retrospective cohort study based on the data from the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) studied pregnancy rates in women on dialysis aged 15–44 years between 2005 and 2013 [1]. Of the 47,555 women on dialysis, 2352 pregnancies were identified in 2008 women. Unadjusted pregnancy rate was noted to be 17.8 per thousand patient years (PTPY), with a significantly higher rate in women aged 20–24 years (40.9 PTPY). Women who were on peritoneal dialysis (PD) had a 53% lower likelihood of pregnancy than those receiving hemodialysis. Of the 1622 (69%) pregnancies with known outcomes, only 637 (39%) were live births [1]. Studies prior to this had shown much lower rates of pregnancy [2, 3]. In comparison, even though consistently declining over the past two decades, the overall pregnancy rate for women aged 15–44 in the United States was 83 pregnancies per 1000 women in the year 2020 [4].

Another USRDS based study looked at obstetric deliveries in women with ESKD aged 18–44 years from 2002 to 2015 [5]. They noted an increase in unadjusted obstetric delivery rate from 2.6 to 3.8 per thousand patient years among all women with ESKD (transplant and dialysis) from 2002 to 2015. While women on hemodialysis showed an increase in obstetric delivery rate from 2.1 to 3.6 per thousand patient years, there was no significant increase in delivery rate for women on PD [5].

A retrospective cohort study based on the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant (ANZDATA) registry linked to mandatory perinatal data sets from four Australian jurisdictions studied live birth rates (> 20 weeks gestation) in women on dialysis from 1991 to 2013 [6]. They reported 248 live births in 168 women receiving kidney replacement therapy, of only which 37 live births were noted in 31 mothers on any form of dialysis. The remaining 211 births were in women with kidney transplants. Women on dialysis had a live birth rate of 5.8 births/1000 women/year (95% CI 4.1–8.1), which was 91% lower compared to the general population (61.9 live births/1000 women/year, 95% CI 61.8–62.0) and 73% lower compared to women with kidney transplants (21.4 live births/1000 women/year, 95% CI 18.6–24.6). Women who conceived while on chronic dialysis had a live birth rate of 3.3/1000 women/year (95% CI 2.0–5.2) compared to 2.5 live births/1000 women/year (95% CI 1.4–4.1) for women who started chronic dialysis during that pregnancy. Live birth rate in women on PD was 50% lower compared to women on hemodialysis [6].

A recently published study based on the French REIN registry (Epidemiology and Information Network in Nephrology) reported pregnancy outcomes in 348 pregnancies in 240 women receiving chronic dialysis between 2006 and 2020 with an overall incidence of 11.5 cases per 1000 person years (95% CI 9.9–12.3). Of these, 174 (50%) resulted in abortions (< 22 weeks), of which 104 (29.9%) were elective abortions, 44 (12.6%) were miscarriages, 17 (4.9%) were therapeutic abortions, and the remaining 9 (2.65) were ectopic or molar pregnancies [7].

In the exclusively home dialysis population, a USRDS‐based study reported an unadjusted pregnancy rate of 8.6 per thousand patient years. However, they did note a significantly higher adjusted likelihood of pregnancy in the home hemodialysis population compared to the PD population (HR 2.34; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.79–3.05) [8].

Of note, the studies discussed above have all used different metrics for reporting pregnancy in women with end‐stage kidney disease on dialysis. Each of the terminologies has different meanings, including pregnancy rate, obstetric delivery rate, and live birth rate. As such, there are significant difficulties in comparing the different studies. However, undoubtedly women on dialysis face significant challenges in conceiving, carrying, and delivering offspring. One of the important causes is reduced fertility due to the dysregulation of the hypothalamus‐pituitary‐gonadal axis due to uremia [9, 10]. Reduction of cyclical GnRH secretion leads to an inadequate LH surge, which results in anovulatory cycles. Additionally, increased prolactin levels also contribute to GnRH secretion through negative feedback. Menstrual irregularities including irregular cycles, amenorrhea, menorrhagia, and premature menopause are seen frequently in women with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) including those on dialysis [11]. There is restoration of the hypothalamic gonadal axis after kidney transplantation with return of fertility. Additionally, women on dialysis also have increased risk of endometrial atrophy, which affects successful conception [12]. Women on dialysis also have significantly high levels of sexual dysfunction [13, 14]. Sexual activity in women on dialysis is significantly lower compared to age‐matched healthy controls, which could be attributed to fatigue, reduced libido, and altered body image [15]. Further lower rates of pregnancy in women on PD may be attributed to mechanical factors including intraperitoneal, fallopian, and ovarian fibrosis, toxicity of intraperitoneal solutions which may inhibit ovulation, and oocyte passage to the uterus [11, 16]. Above all, a high rate of maternal and fetal adverse outcomes, as discussed in detail in the following sections, remains a significant deterrent to pregnancy in women on dialysis.

Maternal and Fetal Outcomes of Pregnancy in Women on Dialysis

A successful pregnancy is characterized by favorable maternal and fetal outcomes, with the preservation of maternal health and the birth of a healthy infant being paramount while minimizing complications. Unfortunately, pregnant women with end‐stage kidney disease on dialysis have a high risk of maternal and fetal adverse outcomes. However, the frequency of such outcomes is difficult to assess given the significant heterogeneity of reported data. Maternal outcomes include early pregnancy loss, pre‐eclampsia, cesarean delivery, polyhydramnios, cervical incompetence, need for blood transfusions, HLA‐sensitization, and, most importantly, maternal mortality. Live birth, preterm birth, low birth weight, small for gestation age, need for neonatal intensive care, perinatal mortality, and congenital malformations are important fetal outcomes [17]. A 2015 systematic review of pregnancy outcomes in dialysis patients covering the period between 2000 and 2014 analyzed both case series as well as case reports. They reviewed a total of 681 pregnancies in 647 patients reported in 36 case series and 90 case reports [18]. The authors noted significant heterogeneity in the studies included, particularly in the definitions of maternal and fetal outcomes. A more recent systematic review published in 2023 included 14 studies published between 2010 and 2020 with data on 2754 pregnancies in 2364 women [19]. Unfortunately, data for pregnancy complications and fetal outcomes was available for only 402 pregnancies (13 studies), as the largest study [1] had a high number of unknown outcomes, thereby affecting the statistical significance of the analysis.

Despite significant heterogeneity across the studies, early pregnancy loss has been reported in 5%–50% of cases. Preeclampsia has been reported in 5%–20% of pregnancies in women on dialysis. A high rate of cesarean delivery has also been reported, ranging anywhere from 30% to 70%. Polyhydramnios has been reported in 5%–50% of patients. Cervical incompetence has been reported in around 15% of pregnancies [20]. Maternal mortality has been reported at less than 1% in published studies, although this figure must be interpreted with caution due to significant heterogeneity in the data. Need for blood transfusions due to worsening anemia has been reported in up to 30% of pregnancies. Unfortunately, none of the studies have looked at HLA sensitization post pregnancy, which could be important with regards to future kidney transplantation.

With respect to fetal outcomes, live births have been reported in 60%–90% of pregnancies. Preterm birth has been overwhelmingly the most common adverse fetal outcome, reported in 40%–90% of pregnancies. Correspondingly, low birth weight has been reported in 40%–100% of pregnancies whereas small gestational age has been reported in 30%–50% of pregnancies. Approximately 70% of neonates required admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), with lengths of stay varying widely. Perinatal mortality has been reported at around 5%–20%. Congenital malformations were not increased compared to the general population [18, 19]. However, there are very few reports of long‐term outcomes in children of women on dialysis [21, 22].

Studies have also examined pregnancy outcomes based on whether dialysis was initiated after conception or conception occurred while the patient was already on dialysis. A retrospective cohort study based on the ANZDATA registry assessed outcomes of 77 pregnancies in 73 women between 2001 and 2011. Of these, 53 pregnancies were in women who were already established on maintenance dialysis whereas in 24 pregnancies, dialysis was initiated during the pregnancy. The study reported significantly higher live birth rates in women who started dialysis after conception; however, birth weight and gestational age were not different between the two groups [23]. In another study—a single‐center series from Brazil that analyzed 93 pregnancies in 89 women between 2000 and 2017—nearly 50% of the participants were already receiving dialysis prior to conception. The study found no significant differences in fetal outcomes between women who conceived while on dialysis and those who initiated dialysis during pregnancy [24].

Pregnancy Planning and Preconception Optimization in Women on Dialysis

Pregnancy planning and preconception optimization are essential in women with ESKD on dialysis due to high maternal and fetal risks. A structured counseling framework, such as that proposed by Jesudason et al., emphasizes shared decision‐making by integrating clinical risks with patient values and desire for autonomy [25]. While the decision to pursue parenthood may bring about emotional, social, and ethical dilemmas, clinicians should avoid catastrophizing or issuing blanket prohibitions, as such approaches can cause significant distress and undermine the patient‐physician relationship [26, 27]. At the same time, clinicians should give due importance to patients' core values—especially the desire for motherhood—while also addressing limitations and fears, and remaining attentive to feelings of guilt or external pressures that may influence decision‐making.

Timing of pregnancy should be individualized, balancing the benefits of transplantation against the patient's reproductive window, anticipated time to transplant, and recommended transplant‐to‐pregnancy interval [5, 6, 28, 29]. It is important to have early and thorough discussions about potential pregnancy outcomes, maternal and fetal risks, and the implications of dialysis during pregnancy. Jesudason et al. have also developed a traffic‐light system to help women understand the potential risks associated with pregnancy with kidney failure [25]. Early referral to maternal‐fetal medicine, reproductive endocrinology, genetics, and other relevant specialties supports comprehensive planning [25]. Contraception should be discussed until pregnancy is actively planned, given the high likelihood of unplanned pregnancies—most of which occur within the first year of dialysis initiation [1, 6]. Continuation of an unplanned pregnancy depends on multiple factors including patient preferences, gestational age, risks of pregnancy, potential teratogenic exposures and availability of abortion services [30]. Despite the probable safety advantage of planned pregnancies, comparative outcome data are lacking, and contraceptive use remains low in the dialysis population [31]. Progestin‐only methods (pills, injectables, and implants), intrauterine devices (copper IUD and levonorgestrel‐IUD) are safe and effective choices; barrier methods are safe but less effective; and estrogen‐containing contraceptives (combined pills/patch/ring) are preferably avoided in dialysis patients [32].

Preconception optimization includes discontinuation of teratogenic or potentially teratogenic medications (e.g., mycophenolate, RAAS blockers, statins, calcimimetics, and non‐calcium phosphate binders) and substitution with pregnancy‐safe alternatives. Blood pressure should be controlled with pregnancy‐safe agents such as methyldopa, labetalol, nifedipine, or hydralazine, aiming for < 140/90 mmHg, although specific targets in dialysis pregnancies have not been studied [33]. Comorbidities such as diabetes and lupus should be optimized, and anemia should be corrected with iron and erythropoiesis‐stimulating agents as appropriate. Folic acid and multivitamin supplementation are recommended, and smoking/alcohol cessation and vaccination should follow general population guidelines [34].

Finally, intensive dialysis may improve fertility by normalizing reproductive hormones and reducing prolactin levels, and should be considered where feasible to enhance fertility [35]. An optimal approach to pregnancy involves careful planning during the safest possible window, preceded by thorough patient counseling, coordinated care from a specialized multidisciplinary team, access to high‐risk antenatal services, and the use of effective contraception until conception is medically and personally appropriate [25]. However, nephrology provider confidence in managing pregnancy and women's health issues in CKD remains low, underscoring the need for better training and multidisciplinary care [36, 37, 38].

Dialysis Management During Pregnancy

Dialysis management in pregnancy is complex and requires a highly coordinated, patient‐centered approach. Residual kidney function should be considered when prescribing dialysis, although dialysis intensification remains the cornerstone of management, particularly in women without residual kidney function [39]. Choice of modality—home based or in‐center—depends on local resources, patient preference, health literacy, and clinical stability. Hemodialysis is supported by a substantially large evidence base, whereas data for PD are limited to case reports and small series [39]. While PD may be continued in early pregnancy, many patients require transition to hemodialysis later in gestation and in selected cases supplemental intermittent hemodialysis may be added as a part of a hybrid approach [40, 41, 42].

Dialysis access planning should ideally occur during preconception counseling in patients anticipated to require dialysis during pregnancy. Both arteriovenous fistulas and tunneled central venous catheters have been successfully used, with acceptable complication rates [43]. Conventional and low‐molecular‐weight heparin can be safely used during pregnancy.

Evidence supporting dialysis intensification has accumulated over several decades. Early survey data suggested improved infant survival and longer gestational age with dialysis durations ≥ 20 h per week [44]. Subsequent case series demonstrated markedly improved outcomes with nocturnal and intensive hemodialysis regimens [45]. A landmark comparative study by Hladunewich et al. showed significantly higher live birth rates, longer gestational age, and higher birth weights in Canadian women receiving intensive dialysis (mean dialysis duration 43 ± 6 h/week) compared with a less intensively dialyzed American cohort (mean dialysis duration 17 ± 5 h/week), establishing a clear dose–response relationship between dialysis intensity and pregnancy outcomes [20].

Targeting maternal blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels has been proposed as an alternative or complementary strategy to increasing dialysis duration. In a 2008 study of 28 women on chronic hemodialysis in Japan, Asamiya et al. demonstrated that maternal BUN levels < 50 mg/dL were associated with birth weights ≥ 1500 g or gestational age ≥ 32 weeks [46]. In a larger single‐center case series from Brazil involving 93 pregnancies, Luders et al. found that mid‐week maternal BUN levels > 35 mg/dL were associated with increased risk of perinatal death or extreme prematurity, supporting a target mid‐week BUN < 35 mg/dL [24]. An alternative strategy of progressively increasing dialysis duration across trimesters has also been reported, with live birth rates of approximately 78% [47].

Evidence guiding PD management during pregnancy remains limited. In early pregnancy, pre‐pregnancy prescriptions may be continued with close monitoring of transport status and residual kidney function should also be considered [39]. As pregnancy progresses, reduced fill volumes (1–1.5 L instead of 2.0–2.5 L), tidal PD, and increased exchange frequency may improve tolerance. High‐glucose dialysate and icodextrin should be avoided due to insufficient safety data [39]. Adequacy targets such as Kt/V are not validated in pregnancy; therefore, clinical parameters and maternal BUN levels (target < 50 mg/dL) should guide prescription adjustments. A low threshold should be maintained for transitioning to or supplementing with hemodialysis when PD adequacy becomes difficult to maintain [39, 40, 41, 42].

Despite improved outcomes with intensive dialysis, significant challenges remain. Intensive regimens impose substantial time demands and may contribute to physical and psychological fatigue, patient burnout, and logistical strain on dialysis units. Financial barriers, insurance limitations, transportation costs, and lost wages may further limit access. Increased dialysis frequency also raises the risk of vascular access complications and metabolic disturbances, including hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and increased losses of protein and water‐soluble vitamins, necessitating close monitoring and proactive supplementation (Figure 2).

Clinical Challenges and Management in Pregnant Dialysis Patients

Pregnancy is a hyperdynamic and hyperhydrated state, making dry weight and volume management particularly challenging in women on dialysis [48]. Expected gestational weight gain of 300–500 g/week from the second trimester should be incorporated into dry‐weight targets. Blood pressure targets in pregnant dialysis patients are not well defined; recommendations are extrapolated from CKD populations and general obstetric studies. Consensus guidelines suggest targets below 135/85 mm Hg in pregnant women with CKD, but these have not been validated in dialysis patients, necessitating individualized management [49, 50, 51]. In the Chronic Hypertension and Pregnancy (CHAP) trial, targeting < 140/90 mmHg improved pregnancy outcomes, suggesting this threshold may be reasonable although the earlier CHIPS trial had found no significant differences in pregnancy loss, neonatal intensive care, or maternal complications between less‐tight (diastolic < 100 mmHg) and tight (diastolic < 85 mmHg) BP control [52, 53]. Intradialytic hypotension (< 120/70 mmHg) should be avoided to prevent placental ischemia [54]. Amniotic fluid index (AFI) may guide ultrafiltration, with dialysis intensification considered when AFI increases [33].

Diagnosing preeclampsia is difficult because chronic hypertension, fluctuating volume status, and baseline proteinuria or anuria are common in dialysis patients, making standard American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) or the International Society for the Study of Hypertension In Pregnancy (ISSHP) criteria less reliable [55, 56]. However, there are no separate definitions or diagnostic criteria for women with CKD or ESKD. Weekly maternal clinical assessments and fortnightly fetal growth surveillance after 20 weeks' gestation are recommended, with Doppler evaluation of uterine and umbilical artery flow potentially useful [17].

Preeclampsia involves abnormal placentation and maternal vascular dysfunction, leading to increased antiangiogenic factors like soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1) and reduced placental growth factor (PlGF) [57]. The sFlt‐1 to PlGF ratio has been proposed as a diagnostic biomarker for preeclampsia and severe preeclampsia [58, 59]. sFlt‐1, PlGF, and sFlt‐1/PlGF ratio can effectively predict and enhance the diagnostic accuracy of preterm preeclampsia in patients with CKD, though a lower cut‐off may be needed in this population [60]. Case reports suggest the ratio may also be useful in dialysis patients, and because both sFlt‐1 and PlGF rise post‐dialysis while the ratio remains stable, testing may be performed independent of dialysis timing [61, 62].

Dialysate composition should be adjusted during intensive dialysis treatments to prevent electrolyte disturbances. Suggested targets include dialysate potassium ≥ 3 mEq/L, calcium 1.5 mmol/L, bicarbonate 25–30 mEq/L and addition of phosphate up to 1.5 mmol/L [33]. Dietary restrictions can be relaxed and nutritionist involvement is recommended. Protein intake of 1.5–1.8 g/kg/day and caloric intake of 35 kcal/kg/day is suggested [48]. Target hemoglobin in pregnant dialysis women is 10–11 g/dL, with iron supplementation increased erythropoiesis‐stimulating agent dosing often required (up to 50%–300% of pre‐pregnancy doses).

Obstetric and Postpartum Care in Pregnant Women on Dialysis

Toward the end of first trimester, low dose aspirin (75–150 mg/day) should be started. High dose folic acid supplementation is recommended to compensate for losses due to intensive dialysis. Dialysis patients may have elevated beta‐human chorionic gonadotrophin (β‐HCG) and pregnancy associated plasma protein A (PAPPA) levels which may lead to false‐positive tests of Down syndrome screening [63, 64]. Assessment of nuchal thickness on ultrasound, amniocentesis or non‐invasive prenatal testing (cell‐free DNA in maternal blood) may be preferred for Down syndrome screening [33].

In the second trimester, a 20‐week ultrasound for fetal anomalies and cervical incompetence is recommended. Pregnant women on dialysis should be screened regularly for signs and symptoms of preeclampsia, preferably at 2‐week intervals. At 22 weeks, a placental doppler ultrasound for uterine and umbilical artery waveforms and resistance is performed. Fetal growth and wellbeing are assessed every 2–4 weeks, and more frequently if needed. Amniotic fluid index and biophysical profile should be done at least biweekly, or more as needed [17].

By the third trimester, weekly assessments of mother and fetus are required along with weekly ultrasound and biophysical profile. Planning for delivery should be made if not done already. Delivery should preferably occur in a tertiary care center with the availability of high‐risk obstetricians and neonatologists. If there are no obstetric or fetal indications for cesarean delivery, vaginal delivery should be preferred. Planned induction of labor at or just beyond 37 weeks may be preferred if there are no complications [17]. Patient should be well dialyzed, off anticoagulation in preparation of delivery. Magnesium dose should be reduced by 50% if use is necessary for management of preeclampsia/eclampsia [33]. Trimester‐wise care is summarized in Figure 3.

Postpartum care is also equally important for women with ESKD on dialysis (Figure 4). Patients may revert to pre‐pregnancy dialysis regimen following delivery. However, it may be reasonable to wait 4–6 weeks after cesarean delivery to resume peritoneal dialysis [40, 65]. Residual kidney function should be measured for those not on dialysis prior to pregnancy to determine if dialysis could be safely held. Medication management should focus on safety in lactation for breast‐feeding mothers. Excessive ultrafiltration should be avoided as it can affect breastmilk secretion. Women should be counseled on contraception and family planning, and appropriate methods should be prescribed. Social and psychological support is critical, and women should be monitored closely for signs of post‐partum depression.

Conclusions

Pregnancy in women on dialysis remains high risk and challenging; however, outcomes have been improving in recent times. Pre‐pregnancy counseling and pregnancy planning may help reduce adverse outcomes. A multi‐disciplinary team approach, including nephrologist, high risk maternal‐fetal medicine specialist, and neonatologist, is important in the management of pregnant women on dialysis. Provision of intensive dialysis remains the cornerstone of management, and home‐based therapies may be preferably utilized if available. Frequent monitoring and adjustment of dialysis prescription and ultrafiltration are required. Blood pressure should be well controlled with pregnancy safe medications. Patients should be closely monitored for signs and symptoms of pre‐eclampsia. Other aspects of care, including nutrition, anemia management, and medication management, are important. However, given the lack of equitable access to care for women with kidney failure across the world, supporting pregnancy with end stage kidney disease on dialysis may not be feasible in resource‐poor settings [25, 66, 67].

Future Directions

With the evolution of reproductive and obstetric nephrology as a dedicated subspecialty of nephrology, we hope to see more research and advancements in the management of pregnant dialysis patients. Integrated, multidisciplinary, and interprofessional clinics will improve the clinical care of these complex patients. Patient and clinician peer‐support networks can aid in complex decision‐making and provide valuable support for both clinicians and patients. Enhancing obstetric nephrology training within the core nephrology curriculum is essential to improve provider confidence in managing pregnancy in patients with end‐stage kidney disease.
