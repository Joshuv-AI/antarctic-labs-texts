The staining potential of the dental enamel with cerium oxide nanoparticle mouthwash and Glycyrrhiza glabra nanoparticle mouthwash: An in vitro study
Authors: M. A. Lavanya, H. Esther Nalini, S. R. Bharathi, S. Tamil Selvi, S. Nivethitha, S. Reshma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aims to formulate and evaluate the staining effect of three different types of mouthwash containing nanoparticles (NPs) and compare them with chlorhexidine (CHX) mouthwash on dental enamel.Materials and Methods:Twenty intact premolars were randomly assigned to four groups. The specimens were then immersed in CHX mouthwash (Group I), colloidal solutions containing cerium oxide NPs (Group II), Glycyrrhiza glabra NPs (Group III), and a cerium oxide NP and G. glabra NP composite (Group IV). The baseline color determination was made (T1) using a digital spectrophotometer. After 24 h, the shade of the samples was recorded (T2). The third color assessment was accomplished on the 15th day (T3) and 30th day (T4).Results:The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality was performed on four groups. One-way ANOVA revealed no significant differences in the ∆ E between groups at various time intervals. On the 1st day, the highest staining was found in Group IV, and the least staining was found in Group I. On the 15th day, the highest staining was found in Group III, and the least staining was found in Group II. On the 30th day, the highest staining was found in Group III, and the least staining was found in Group II. P > 0.05, indicating that the results show no significant difference.Conclusion:Cerium oxide NPs containing mouthwash produced equal enamel discoloration as compared with CHX, which can be used as an alternative to CHX.

INTRODUCTION

Gingivitis and periodontitis are inflammatory conditions commonly caused by plaque accumulation, which leads to gingival inflammation and destruction of the periodontal ligament (PDL) and alveolar bone. These pathological changes can either be controlled or prevented by professional or home-applied plaque control. Plaque control can be either mechanical or chemical. Mechanical plaque control is the most effective and efficient way to control and prevent the inflammatory condition. Chemical plaque control is considered an adjunct but not a substitute for mechanical plaque control. The dynamic properties of the chemical plaque control agent are substantivity, selectivity, and penetrability. The gold standard chemical plaque agent used in our dentistry is chlorhexidine (CHX). CHX is a diguanidohexane that has enhanced antiseptic properties and has produced the most positive antibacterial results.[1] The disadvantages of this gold standard agent are staining, allergy, and burning mouth.[23] The staining property of this agent is due to the degradation of the CHX molecule, catalysis of the Maillard reaction, protein denaturation with metal sulfide formation, and precipitation of dietary anionic chromogens. This demerit might limit their role in oral hygiene measures.[4] Therefore, attention is drawn towards manufacturing a chemical plaque agent that produces a lesser staining effect when compared to CHX. The dissolution and saturation properties of nanoparticles (NPs) are greater, which makes complete utilization of the obtained product.[5] Nanoceria and Glycyrrhiza glabra have enhanced antioxidant properties, high biocompatibility, etc.[6] Karakoti et al. did the first study inquiring about the superoxide dismutase mimetic activity of nanoceria.[7] Santos et al. proved that nanoceria had enhanced antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutans.[8] Sedighinia et al. suggested that G. glabra helps to control oral microorganisms.[9] Taking the beneficial effects of NPs and herbs into consideration, the present study focused on the preparation of mouthwashes containing cerium oxide NPs and G. glabra (liquorice) NPs. This study aims to formulate and evaluate the staining effect of cerium oxide NPs and G. glabra (liquorice) NPs-containing mouthwash, and compare them with CHX mouthwash on dental enamel.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The cerium nitrate [Figure 1] and G. glabra powder [Figure 2] were purchased. The ball milling method was used to prepare the G. glabra NPs [Figure 3]. Cerium oxide NPs were chemically generated from cerium nitrate using the sonochemical technique [Figure 4]. The size of the produced NPs was determined using X-ray diffraction. The particle size of cerium oxide NPs was 27–60 nm, and the particle size of G. glabra NPs was 10–60 nm [Figures 5 and 6]. The colloidal solutions of cerium oxide NPs, G. glabra NPs, and a composite of cerium oxide NPs and G. glabra NPs were prepared by the stirring method [Figure 7]. The concentration of NP powder was determined using the sedimentation and filtration method [Figure 8]. 100 mg of cerium oxide NPs and 25 mg of G. glabra NPs were mixed with 100 mL of distilled water, and colloidal solutions were prepared. The zeta potential of the cerium oxide NPs, G. glabra NPs, and a composite of cerium oxide NPs and G. glabra NPs solution was −16.7, −20.3, and − 9.25, respectively [Figures 9–11].

The 20 extracted permanent maxillary and mandibular premolar teeth were collected and were intact, noncarious, and free of developmental abnormalities. The teeth were mechanically cleaned and stored in artificial saliva at room temperature, with calibrations made [Figures 12 and 13].[10] These teeth were grouped into 4 individual groups, with each group containing 10 teeth, 5 maxillary permanent and 5 mandibular permanent premolars, and the groups were Group I - CHX mouthwash, Group II - Cerium oxide colloidal solution, Group III - G. glabra colloidal solution, and Group IV - Composite of cerium oxide NPs and G. glabra colloidal solution. These teeth were exposed to the control and colloidal solutions daily for 30 s.

The Easyshade spectrophotometer was used to assess the color of the mid-buccal enamel surface according to the Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage L*, a*, and b* (CIELAB) color space system in the Dental Bay Dental Clinic, Coimbatore [Figure 14]. In the CIELAB color space system, the L coordinate represents the value or degree of lightness, the a value represents positions on the red/green (+a = red, −a = green) axis, and the b value represents the yellow/blue (+b = yellow, −b = blue) axis, respectively. The color measurements were performed thrice, and the mean values of L, a, and b were determined for each specimen. The color change (∆E) between the different stages was calculated using the following formula:

Staining potential analysis using an Easy Shade spectrophotometer

∆E = [(∆a)2+ (∆b)2+ (∆L)2]0.5.[11]

A total of 4 measurements were taken: T1 – Baseline measurement, T2 – After 24 h, T3 – After 15 days, and T4 – After 30 days. Before taking measurements, the teeth were dried using cotton rolls [Tables 1-4].

Table 6 depicts the mean and standard deviation of ∆E values and intergroup comparison of Groups I, II, III, and IV at 3 different intervals. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the mean values, respectively. On the 1st day, the highest staining was found in Group IV with a mean value of 6.27, and the least staining was found in Group I with a mean value of 4.85. On the 15th day, the highest staining was found in Group III with a mean value of 13.2, and the least staining was found in Group II with a mean value of 9.15. On the 30th day, the highest staining was found in Group III with a mean value of 13.93, and the least staining was found in Group II with a mean value of 11.15. One-way analysis revealed no significant E-value difference between the four groups at all intervals [Figures 15-17].

DISCUSSION

Plaque accumulation is considered an important causative factor for gingival/periodontal disease. Complete biofilm control includes professional/self-care hygiene measures. The self-care oral hygiene measures include toothbrushing, mouthrinses, and interdental cleaning aids. Effective plaque removal can be done with professional oral hygiene measures combined with self-care hygiene measures. Oral mouthrinses can be used as an adjunct to mechanical toothbrushing in enhancing oral health.[1]

CHX is considered a gold standard mouthrinse that is available commercially.[1] Löe and Schiott in 1962 did the first antiplaque study using 0.2% CHX mouthrinse and concluded that usage of CHX twice daily for 60 s effectively reduced the plaque accumulation.[2] Grossman et al. conducted a 6-month clinical trial and found that twice-daily rinsing with 0.12% CHX for 30 s reduced gingival inflammation.[12] Hence, in our research, we further reduced the rinsing time to 30 s (immersion period) in an attempt to reduce the staining effect without altering its anti-inflammatory property.

Bagis et al. investigated the staining effect of CHX and concluded that the staining effect was expected to be higher in the first few days of use.[4] Eriksen et al. attempted to reduce the extrinsic discoloration caused by CHX by combining 0.2% CHX and 1% oxidizing peroxymonosulfate solution.[13] Jhingta et al. concluded that CHX, when combined with hydrogen peroxide, produced lesser extrinsic discoloration when compared with CHX alone.[14]

In our research, we used an alternative chemical and herbal compound as a mouthwash that produces a lesser staining effect. Eslami et al. reported that mouthwash containing zinc oxide (ZnO) showed a higher staining effect, and titanium dioxide showed the least staining effect when compared with 0.2% CHX.[10] Priya et al. evaluated the staining potential of green tea extract mouthwash on the tongue and teeth, concluding that there was no significant difference between the CHX and green tea extract mouthwash for both tongue and tooth stains.[15] Varghese et al. reported that guava leaf extract mouthwash, when compared with CHX for staining potential, displayed a lesser staining potential.[16]

Barma et al. evaluated the antibacterial activity of silica NP-incorporated mouthwash against Staphylococcus aureus, S. mutans, and Enterococcus faecalis and concluded that they showed good antibacterial potential against Gram-positive bacteria.[17] Selvaraj et al. investigated the anti-inflammatory activity of clove and ginger formulated along with ZnO NPs in a mouthwash, and the result suggested that the ZnO NPs strengthened with ginger and clove extracts may have anti-inflammatory characteristics.[18] Abadi et al. developed a silver NP mouthwash and evaluated its antimicrobial activity against S. mutans, S. aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans. The results showed that the developed mouthwash killed the microorganisms in lower concentrations.[5]

In our research, we used cerium oxide NPs as an alternative chemical compound and G. glabra as an herbal compound. Kumari et al. reported the cytotoxicity of cerium oxide NPs due to loss in cell viability was obvious at 200, 100, and 50 mg/mL of CeO2 NPs and found to be 76.40%, 77.49% (P < 0.01), and 78.68% (P < 0.05), respectively, as compared to the control (100%).[19] According to Akhavan and Ghaderi, the typical Gram-negative bacterium E. coli has a thin layer about 7–8 nm in thickness consisting of peptidoglycan with an outer membrane, whereas S. aureus, a Gram-positive bacterium, has a thicker peptidoglycan layer of about 20–80 nm but without any outer membrane.[20] Badr et al. (2011) reported that the treatment of PDL fibroblasts with liquorice extract resulted in 87% ± 1.91 cell viability, while 60% Ca (OH)2 resulted in 100% killing of the PDL fibroblasts. The viability of PDL cells was 58% ± 1.73 when treated with the mixture of Ca (OH) 2 and liquorice extract. The minimal bactericidal concentration of liquorice extract against E. faecalis was 12.3 μg mL−1. Liquorice extract had the largest mean inhibition zone, 3.97 ± 0.24 mm, and thus showed the most significant microbial inhibition against E. faecalis.[21] Lee et al. reported that hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin-inclusion complexes (27.16 ± 1.73%) exhibited a lower protective ability of liquorice extract than β-cyclodextrin-inclusion complexes (45.06 ± 2.00%), and bio accessibility significantly improved compared to free LE. The most enhanced bio accessibility was obtained in CA-IC, and more than half of the LE (56.26 ± 2.57%) encapsulated in CA was retained after the GIT process, which was 2.75-fold higher than that of free LE.[22] The mouthwash containing G. glabra, an herbal product, showed an enhanced staining effect compared to others in this study. This could be related to the bioactive material present, which will be examined in future investigations.

On considering the outcomes of our research, the mouthwash containing cerium oxide NPs produces a staining effect similar to CHX. Further research is needed to understand the process of tooth discoloration caused by NP-containing mouthwashes. Future research should investigate the staining effect of NP-containing mouthwashes in the presence of saliva and any interactions that may exist. The primary limitation of this study is that the mechanical approach was not used at any point during the trial. So, further studies must include the mechanical method and antibacterial studies, anti-inflammatory property, and evaluation of various biological properties to consider it for in vivo studies.

CONCLUSION

The current study gives scientific justification for the administration of NPs in the preparation of mouthwash. CHX’s well-known adverse effects, including tooth and prosthesis staining and an altered taste sensation, may limit its long-term use. The results show that cerium oxide NP mouthwash creates the same or higher shade difference compared to CHX. Future studies are directed to evaluate the antibacterial properties of the NPs used in this study.
