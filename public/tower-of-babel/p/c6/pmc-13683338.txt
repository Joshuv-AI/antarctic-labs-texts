Structured reasoning failures compromise LLM interpretation of clinical oncology notes
Authors: Matthew W. Kenaston, Umair Ayub, Mihir Parmar, Priya Kumar, Muhammad Umair Anjum, Syed Arsalan Ahmed Naqvi, Aadel A. Chaudhuri, Yousef Zakharia, Elisabeth I. Heath, Tanios S. Bekaii-Saab, Cui Tao, Eliezer M. Van Allen, Ben Zhou, YooJung Choi, Chitta Baral, Irbaz Bin Riaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) show strong performance on clinical benchmarks, yet their reasoning reliability in real-world oncology care remains unclear. We evaluated LLM reasoning on authentic oncology notes using a novel hierarchical error taxonomy across two retrospective cohorts spanning breast, pancreatic, and prostate cancer. GPT-4 produced reasoning errors in 23.1% of note interpretations, the majority reflecting cognitive bias patterns. Errors were more frequent in recommendation tasks and were strongly associated with guideline-discordant recommendations and lower clinician-rated clinical impact scores. Confirmation bias, anchoring bias, and omission errors were most strongly linked to potentially harmful outputs. Compared to GPT-4, GPT-5.1 demonstrated reduced error rates and improved clinical performance but retained structured reasoning failure patterns. Automated LLM-based evaluators detected error presence but failed to reliably classify subtypes, and a preliminary self-mitigation strategy yielded only modest improvement. Endpoint accuracy alone may mask clinically meaningful reasoning failures. Therefore, evaluating and monitoring reasoning fidelity should be a prerequisite for safe deployment of LLMs in oncology decision support.

Introduction

Advances in large language model (LLM) technology promise to transform clinical practice, with several emerging impactful use cases1. LLMs can extract structured data from unstructured text, streamlining administrative processes like summarizing patient records2, generating discharge paperwork3, and medical coding4,5. They can also synthesize disparate pieces of information across convoluted electronic health records (EHR). LLMs have been effective at analyzing clinical notes6, imaging reports7,8, and laboratory results9 to construct differential diagnoses10–12. Large language models are also considered for patient-facing settings: answering general medical questions13, clarifying instructions14,15, and integrating within telehealth services16.

While these effective use cases are promising, unpredictable errors preclude deployment in clinical oncology practice17–20. Apparent success on benchmark measures of endpoint accuracy, including multiple-choice or structured classification tasks, can mask internal reasoning failures21. This occurs because most optimization strategies primarily refine surface-level fluency rather than logical consistency or causal validity22,23. Indeed, LLMs may reach correct answers through faulty reasoning, and when confronted with open-ended clinical scenarios, they often display false confidence or skip critical steps24. Such behavior has been linked to inaccurate answer selection on medical board exams13,20,25, and aligns with human cognitive biases26 that cause practicing physicians to err27. Yet, few studies have systematically examined these failures in real-world, unstructured clinical data28. This gap carries disproportionate risk. An LLM that misinterprets chart context or infers causality incorrectly may sound persuasive, but its recommendations could deviate from safe, guideline-concordant care. Thus, understanding how and why these reasoning failures occur is essential before any clinical deployment.

Here, we investigated whether GPT models can reason safely through the same oncology notes used in clinical practice. Drawing on recently curated oncology datasets6 and computational work on reasoning evaluations29, we developed a framework to classify reasoning errors in EHR interpretation. Extending existing non-clinical taxonomies, our approach captures logical fallacies that mirror human cognitive biases. We use the term “cognitive bias” to describe reasoning patterns in model-generated rationale, recognizing LLMs do not inherently possess human-like cognition or internal psychological states. We found these reasoning failures correlate with potential clinical risk, underscoring the need for precise error identification to guide targeted model corrections before deployment.

Results

Taxonomy development

To create an error taxonomy for clinical reasoning breakdowns, we analyzed chain-of-thought (CoT) responses generated by GPT-4-32k from extraction-level prompts. We used extraction-only prompts in this phase to minimize confounding sources of variability in model behavior introduced by guideline knowledge or complex clinical decision-making. Briefly, a stepwise review of each reasoning sequence identified recurring patterns of clinical logic breakdowns. Through iterative refinement to saturation (Supplementary Fig. 1), these patterns were organized into a hierarchical taxonomy with two Tier 1 domains of Comprehension and Reasoning, further subdivided into four Tier 2 categories and nine Tier 3 cognitive or logical error types. Among erroneous annotations, reasoning failures dominated (86.4%, 82.2–89.7), while comprehension errors were conversely rarer (Fig. 2C). In Tier 2, the most common error subtypes were False Association (40.6%, 35.4–46.0) and Missing Step (45.5%, 40.2–50.8), as both arise from Tier 1 Reasoning errors. Tier 3 errors were more diverse, with Confirmation Bias, Factual Error, use of Chart Context, and Stark Omission representing the most frequent subtypes. Remaining subtypes accounted for <10% of errors. Error frequency did not vary by chart note or its file size (p = 0.736). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that even during extraction tasks, GPT-4 produced consistent, clinically interpretable reasoning failures, supporting the taxonomy’s validity for identifying error patterns.

GPT-4 exhibited error rates of 17.0% (14.1–20.2) in evaluation, 22.4% (19.2–25.9) in management, and 22.3% (18.8–26.2) in presentation. When considering specific clinical tasks, analysis prompts were more accurate on average (0.13, 0.11–0.16), yet recommendation prompts showed a notable increase in error rates for clinical management specifically (Fig. 3B). Error rates differed slightly by cancer stage (Fig. 3C), though all within overlapping confidence intervals. Examining the error distribution again revealed that reasoning failures (Tier 1) dominated across tasks (Fig. 3D). Tier 3 errors clustered more variably. Confirmation bias was highest for analysis tasks, while premature closure and anchoring bias were highest for recommendation tasks. These patterns mirror how real-world clinical tasks elicit different cognitive pressures, with decision-making under uncertainty particularly prone to shortcut reasoning.

Multivariable modeling identified prompt and context factors associated with error risk (Fig. 3E). Compared with diagnostic queries, laboratory and imaging prompts were significantly less error-prone (OR 0.57 [0.40–0.81]). Evaluation-phase contexts carried lower error risk than presentation (OR 0.52 [0.31–0.88]), and queries regarding CSPC had higher odds of error versus localized disease, though this was relatively minimal. Analysis tasks were also less error-prone than extraction, while recommendation tasks did not differ significantly, albeit more likely to result in reasoning errors for clinical management (Fig. 3B).

To determine whether specific reasoning errors in GPT-4 reasoning chains correlated with clinically meaningful risks, we analyzed 487 reasoning chains restricted to clinical recommendation tasks. Such tasks were specifically aligned with 2024 NCCN guidelines30. Clinical impact was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = potentially harmful, 5 = clinically optimal; Table 2), and we also evaluated for NCCN guideline concordance. Inter-reviewer agreement was strong for both metrics (Fig. 4A). Recommendations containing reasoning errors received markedly lower clinical-impact scores (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B). These responses were also more likely to deviate from NCCN guidelines (Fig. 4C). Assumed risk was greatest for management-phase prompts and metastatic castration-resistant disease. Certain reasoning errors were associated with lower clinician-rated clinical impact scores and greater guideline discordance. Among all errors detected within this dataset, Tier 1 comprehension errors were rare and carried limited clinical consequence (Fig. 4E). Among Tier 2 subtypes, false associations and missing reasoning steps accounted for a large fraction of subjectively harmful recommendations. Tier 3 biases, such as confirmation bias, anchoring bias, and stark omission, were consistently linked to the lowest impact scores (Likert = 1). Thus, certain GPT-4 failures in elicited reasoning manifest as guideline-discordant recommendations that could adversely influence patient care if acted upon.

To characterize how clinically harmful errors emerged within the reasoning chain, we performed a case-series analysis of the five Tier 3 error subtypes consistently associated with Impact = 1, NCCN-discordant outputs (Supplementary Fig. 3). We discovered anchoring bias and stark omission errors that reflected failures to appropriately contextualize retrieved evidence: diagnostic procedures previously completed during workup were incorrectly reframed as surveillance recommendations, while active-surveillance eligibility criteria for untreated low-risk disease were improperly extended to the post-prostatectomy recurrence setting. When prior non-treatment decisions constrained the enumeration of guideline-supported salvage options at recurrence, this led to a premature closure in reasoning. A factual error reflected the retrieval and application of incorrect stage-specific medical knowledge, wherein guideline-supported combination therapy for metastatic CSPC was incorrectly asserted as appropriate management for biochemical recurrence based on PSA trajectory alone.

Comparative replication in GPT-5

We replicated our prostate cancer validation experiment using GPT-5.1 under identical zero-shot prompting conditions. Direct comparison of paired responses between GPT-4 and GPT-5 revealed substantial semantic divergence between models across tasks (Fig. 5A–C). We used cutoffs for normalized Levenshtein distance ≥0.50 to represent substantial textual rewriting, and TF-IDF cosine similarity ≤0.20 to suggest minimal shared semantic content. The most pronounced divergence occurred in recommendation tasks, where 51.5% of answers exhibited semantic shift, compared with 46.9% rewrite-only and 1.5% stable (Fig. 5C). Reasoning chains showed predominantly structural rewriting (74.7%) with fewer semantic shifts (25.3%), aligning with a comparable number of reasoning steps between models (GPT5: 5.43 vs. GPT4: 5.36; Table 1). Overall, GPT-5 responses contained fewer errors (GPT-5: 15.1% ± 2.4% vs. GPT-4: 20.2% ± 2.7%, McNemar’s p = 0.02277; Fig. 5D), and error reductions were highest for recommendation tasks (Fig. 5E). Stage-stratified error rates showed a similar trend, with GPT-5 improving marginally across most disease stages and significantly in CSPC (Fig. 5F). Among recommendations, GPT-5 outputs containing reasoning errors carried significantly higher clinical impact scores than comparable GPT-4 erroneous outputs (p < 0.0001), likely due to a smaller fraction of clinical impact = 1 in GPT-5 (Fig. 5G). GPT-5 also demonstrated improved NCCN guideline concordance overall (p = 0.00019; Fig. 5H), consistent with more frequent guideline references in both its answers (4.3% vs. 0%) and reasoning chains (15.0% vs. 2.1%; Table 3).

Within erroneous responses, the distribution of error subtypes differed meaningfully between models (Fig. 5I and Supplementary Fig. 5). Reasoning failures remained the dominant Tier 1 category in both models (84.7% GPT-4 vs. 73.2% GPT-5), though comprehension errors accounted for a larger fraction in GPT-5 (26.8% vs. 15.3%). At Tier 3, confirmation bias—the most prevalent subtype in GPT-4 (23.4%)—was markedly reduced in GPT-5 (4.2%), while anchoring bias increased (20.3% vs. 12.5%). Redundancy also increased in GPT-5 (13.1% vs. 5.0%), consistent with its longer, more verbose output style in final answer outputs (Table 1). These findings indicate that while a newer architecture reduced the prevalence of several bias subtypes, it exhibited a distinct error profile rather than uniformly diminishing all error types.

To contextualize GPT-5 reasoning, a board-certified oncologist independently generated reasoning chains for 61 matched question–note pairs from the prostate cancer dataset. 67.2% of clinician reasoning chains were classified as a significant semantic shift (cosine similarity ≤0.20), whereas 32.8% were rewrite-only (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Clinician responses contained fewer errors than GPT-5 (6.6% [4/61] vs. 16.4% [10/61]), though this difference was not statistically significant (McNemar p = 0.077; Supplementary Fig. 4C). All clinician errors were reasoning failures, including three false associations and one missing reasoning step (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Notably, all clinician errors occurred in cases where GPT-5 also erred, suggesting shared case difficulty. Mean clinical impact scores were higher for clinician responses (4.87 ± 0.46 vs. 4.51 ± 1.13; Wilcoxon p = 0.080; Supplementary Fig. 4E). In recommendation tasks, all 33 clinician recommendations were NCCN guideline-concordant, whereas GPT-5 produced four discordant or alternative recommendations (Fisher p = 0.114; Supplementary Fig. 4 F). Although not statistically significant, the consistent direction of these findings suggests greater reasoning fidelity and a safer clinical baseline in oncologist responses within this small subset.

While aggregate agreement with the gold standard was reasonable across all three models and both evaluators, this dropped markedly when evaluations were restricted to responses containing true reasoning errors (Fig. 6A). Accuracy on true mistakes was consistently poor (tier-based: P = 0.25–0.35, error-based: P = 0.28–0.44; Fig. 6B), with the tier-based approach performing best for Gemini (P = 0.35) and the error-based approach achieving higher true mistake accuracy in Claude (P = 0.44 vs. P = 0.26). We also observed divergent failure profiles driving variable performance (Fig. 6C), wherein GPT-4o and Gemini exhibited increased false negatives with the error-based evaluator, under-flagging genuine errors. Claude showed the opposite, with higher false positive rates under the error-based approach, suggesting a tendency toward over-classification (Fig. 6C). Thus, gains in true mistake detection co-occur with model-dependent shifts in failure type, trading under-flagging for over-classification rather than improving accurate classification altogether.

Considering the limitation of evaluators as scalable post-hoc pipelines, we repurposed the tier-based prompting strategy as a few-shot self-correction mechanism, embedding the same reasoning chains and error examples directly within the prompt at inference time. This approach was validated on a subset of eight prostate cancer notes, using GPT-5.1 to generate 274 responses from the same prompts as previously described. Across paired responses, this self-mitigation was associated with only a marginal reduction in reasoning errors compared with baseline GPT-5 performance (Fig. 7A). The mean error rate decreased slightly from 15.5% (12.7–18.7) at baseline to 13.5% (10.9–16.6) with mitigation. Stratification by task type showed this effect was somewhat uniform (Fig. 7B). Of note, mitigation resolved more baseline errors than it introduced. Among erroneous responses, 19 (33% of errors) were resolved, whereas 6 new errors were introduced (Fig. 7C). For error subtypes, improvement was consistent but most pronounced in missing-step errors, particularly premature closure (Fig. 7D, left). However, confirmation bias—the only subtype that persisted following GPT-5 self-mitigation and appeared in the clinician subset—occurred when an existing management framework (e.g., active surveillance or prior staging assumptions) suppressed retrieval of escalation-relevant evidence in our case-series (Supplementary Fig. 3). Modest improvements could reflect how such mitigation targets context-specific error patterns observed in elicited reasoning chains, which may not alter the LLM’s internal reasoning for more persistent errors.

Discussion

GPT-4 committed errors in 23.1% of oncology note interpretations. Among recommendations containing reasoning errors, clinical impact scores were significantly lower, with confirmation bias, anchoring bias, and stark omissions disproportionately represented among unsafe outputs. Progressing to GPT-5, we indeed observed lower overall error rates, consistent across tasks and most pronounced in recommendation contexts. Although a gap remains between reasoning fidelity and clinical safety in current LLM evaluations of endpoint accuracy31,32, this may narrow with architectural refinement and the emergence of “reasoning-native” models. Contemporary efforts, such as MedR-Bench, also evaluate reasoning across structured clinical cases, scoring efficiency, factuality, and completeness33. Our work extends this approach by grounding evaluation in real oncology notes and attempting to provide further nuance in LLM reasoning.

Our reproducible hierarchical taxonomy reveals that model-elicited reasoning accounted for 85.4% of errors in GPT-4 and 73.2% in GPT-5. This three-tier framework, validated across multiple common solid tumor types with high inter-rater reliability, provides a clinically conscious approach to classifying LLM reasoning errors. By mapping computational failures onto established cognitive bias frameworks, our taxonomy bridges computer science error analysis and clinical reasoning literature34,35, offering both precision and theoretical grounding. Confirmation bias dominated extraction and analysis tasks, wherein GPT-4 selectively attended to data supporting preliminary impressions while discounting contradictory evidence. Anchoring bias was most prevalent in recommendation tasks, reflecting the overweighting of initial clinical cues. Patterns of omission and overweighting data are consistent with studies showing that completeness deficits are prevalent in clinical reasoning33. When applied to GPT-5 outputs, the taxonomy demonstrated high reproducibility while revealing a lower overall frequency of reasoning failures and a shift in error composition, with marked reductions in confirmation bias and factual errors. The modest effect size observed with self-mitigation is consistent with emerging evidence that current LLMs often struggle to reliably critique or correct their own reasoning failures, particularly when the underlying latent error is preserved despite revised verbalized explanations36.

For both models, we observed task-dependent error distribution, implying different contexts elicit distinct failure modes: extraction triggers pattern-matching errors, while recommendation tasks weighing uncertainty expose premature closure and insufficient deliberation37–39. Error rates appeared higher in advanced disease states, though these stage-stratified comparisons are exploratory and underpowered for definitive inference. Such vulnerabilities may be amplified in rarer malignancies, as LLMs appear to struggle with heterogeneous and rapidly evolving clinical pathways in rarer diseases40–42. While these distinctions may appear academic43, they become critical in clinical practice. EHR notes are nonlinear documents where contextual cues, provisional impressions, and objective data coexist in complex relationships44. Reasoning through these requires causal inference, temporal sequencing, and some skepticism—capabilities not captured by endpoint accuracy45,46. Even with GPT-5’s improvements, notable reasoning failures persist and remain clinically meaningful.

The interpretation of such rationale-level analyses warrants caution. Recent work shows models may rely on latent decision factors not faithfully represented in their chain-of-thought, especially in harder tasks47. Explainable AI literature has long emphasized how post hoc explanations may reflect alignment objectives or stylistic conventions rather than underlying mechanisms48,49. In LLMs specifically, prompting strategies, instruction tuning, and reinforcement learning from human feedback can substantially influence the structure and style of verbalized reasoning traces independent of answer accuracy38,50,51. So, observed error distributions in our taxonomy may better reflect how reasoning is verbalized under elicitation, rather than any internal LLM decision-making. Consistent with this, our preliminary mitigation strategy using explicit reasoning traces produced only modest changes in error prevalence.

Perhaps our most actionable finding is the strong association between reasoning errors and NCCN guideline discordance. GPT-4 recommendations with reasoning errors were significantly more likely to deviate from guideline-concordant care. GPT-5 recommendations marginally improved upon this, and more often referenced specific NCCN pathways. Error types most strongly associated with discordance—confirmation bias, anchoring bias, and stark omissions—represent targetable failure modes. These could be mitigated through agentic or multi-step architectures incorporating reflector or verifier components24,52, debiasing prompts requiring counter-evidence generation53–55, or risk-stratified oversight where management-phase queries in complex disease warrant mandatory human review56,57. Of course, the natural attenuation of several bias subtypes in GPT-5 suggests some safeguards may emerge from architectural scaling and training alone.

While models demonstrated reasonable sensitivity for detecting error presence, they performed poorly at classifying subtypes. Furthermore, prompt-based mitigation informed by annotations yielded only modest reductions in overall reasoning errors for GPT-5. Because we did not systematically explore evaluator configurations like in-context calibration or evaluator ensembling58–61, we only describe initial feasibility, and results should be interpreted as exploratory, as our small subset limits the precision of effect estimates and increases sensitivity to case-specific variability. Nonetheless, this remains relevant since not all reasoning failures carry equal clinical risk17,62, and automated systems alone are often insufficient for clinical deployment. Instead, a pragmatic framework resembles distributed quality assurance: automated evaluators perform initial screening while human experts conduct targeted review of flagged cases63–66. Such “reasoning audits” embedded in tumor boards or decision-support workflows would protect patients while improving future models.

Several other limitations should be considered. For study design, this was a retrospective, note-based simulation conducted upstream of actual clinical care in small cohorts. The prostate validation cohort was underpowered for stage-stratified analyses, and the CORAL development set was not intended for precise prevalence estimation. The clinician reasoning comparison was limited to a single oncologist and a small set of responses, which was underpowered for detecting statistically significant differences and may not capture the full range of inter-clinician variability. Thus, absolute error rates and subgroup differences may vary across broader clinical contexts. In terms of model evaluation, we focused on GPT-4 and GPT-5 to enable in-depth characterization of widely used architectures, but findings may not fully generalize across other model families. Regarding generation strategy, we applied zero-shot prompting to approximate clinician-style queries, which likely overestimates error prevalence relative to structured clinical deployments. Contemporary implementations increasingly incorporate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), structured outputs, and oversight mechanisms that may reduce factual errors, though such approaches do not inherently resolve flawed reasoning patterns. Finally, although inter-rater reliability was high, overlapping annotators across study tasks may introduce bias. Larger, multi-institutional evaluations will be necessary to refine prevalence estimates and contextualize model performance relative to human variability.

As LLMs move from experimental tools to clinical decision-support systems, their value will depend on reasoning soundness, not output fluency. GPT-4 elicited reasoning failures correlated with guideline-discordant and potentially harmful recommendations, albeit GPT-5 demonstrated a significant error reduction under identical conditions. Our hierarchical taxonomy offers a diagnostic schema for identifying these failures and transferring them across architectures and domains. Ensuring clinical deployment requires models to reach correct conclusions through logically valid and clinically defensible reasoning. Anything less risks encoding the very cognitive biases that cause human clinicians to err, transforming a tool meant to augment judgment into one that undermines it.

Methods

This study followed a staged design to evaluate how LLMs reason through real oncology notes (Supplementary Fig. 1). Using the published CORAL dataset of breast (BCa) and pancreatic cancer (PDAC) notes, we refined prompts and response formats to develop a stable taxonomy of reasoning errors, which then served as the gold standard for baseline characterization. We next applied this framework to an independent cohort of outpatient prostate cancer consult notes, encompassing more complex reasoning tasks and assessing correlations between reasoning errors, guideline concordance, and clinical risk. The study follows TRIPOD-LLM reporting standards67. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent given its retrospective nature.

Data sources

Curated Oncology Reports to Advance Language model inference (CORAL) Database: The CORAL database, accessed via the controlled-access repository PhysioNet68, includes deidentified progress notes from BCa and PDAC patients at the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF)6. 40 expert-labeled notes and an additional 100 deidentified notes for each cancer type were accessed through the PhysioNet repository.

Mayo Clinic Cohort: The prostate cancer dataset includes 24 prostate cancer (PCa) cases from 2021 to 2024. These cases spanned the clinical stages of localized disease, biochemical recurrence, metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC), and metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC). This was chosen as an additional dataset for its complexity, including distinct disease states (localized disease, biochemical recurrence, mCSPC, mCRPC), providing a complementary component to the CORAL cancer notes.

Prompts

We sought to assess LLM capabilities on (1) extraction of basic clinical information from the chart note, (2) analysis of the chart notes to make conclusions about said clinical information, and (3) recommending clinical decisions beyond what was explicitly documented (Table 2). Each task was further stratified by the phase of care: initial presentation, evaluation and diagnosis, and ongoing clinical management. Prompts were designed collectively with an interdisciplinary team. For questions specific to clinical recommendation tasks, prompting was aligned with NCCN guidelines (V.2.2024). All prompts followed a zero-shot format with an additional request to provide chain-of-thought reasoning in the following format: “Reason: Let’s think step by step [provide explanation here]/therefore, [answer].” All prompting experiments were performed using GPT-4-32k, configured with default parameters and a fixed temperature of 0 to minimize variability. Annotators

Taxonomy development and validation were led by a medical student in the clinical clerkship phase who participated in all annotations, under direct supervision from at least one actively practicing Mayo Clinic oncologist. All other annotators in this study were either MD, DO, or IMG graduates serving as research fellows within the lab over the study period. Prior to independent labeling, fellows reviewed exemplar annotated cases and participated in guided discussions to ensure a consistent understanding of rubric criteria. When generating gold standards, any disagreements were adjudicated through consensus review involving at least one supervising oncologist and at least one additional research fellow who was not directly involved in annotations.

Building error taxonomy

We used GPT-4-32k to generate chain-of-thought reasoning responses from 10 oncology notes in the CORAL dataset. 15 extraction-level prompts were iteratively refined across four engineering cycles to optimize output fidelity (Supplementary Fig. 1). Two supervised annotators independently reviewed 150 responses, identifying the first deviation from clinically valid reasoning, regardless of answer correctness. Initial error types were identified through open coding and iteratively refined by axial coding until theoretical saturation was reached, defined as no new codes in the last 25 reviewed responses. The taxonomy was developed de novo but informed by established literature on cognitive errors in clinical reasoning69–72, natural language processing error classifications29,73, and prior failure mode analyses. The final schema comprises a three-tier hierarchical taxonomy organized under two broad domains: Comprehension errors and Reasoning errors, with two subsequent error tiers (Fig. 1). Each error type was accompanied by a formal rubric, relative criteria, and abstracted examples to provide decision boundaries for reviewers (Supplementary Table 1). Multi-label classification was permitted when distinct and independent CoT failures were identified within a single response. However, annotators were instructed to identify the initiating error and not to assign additional labels to downstream errors if those were judged to have directly resulted from the initial failure. Responses from all annotated notes in the CORAL dataset (notes 0–39) were then categorized into the taxonomy to assess the prevalence and distribution of reasoning errors. Discrepant labels were adjudicated via consensus review to reach a gold standard.

Testing the taxonomy

We validated the taxonomy on outpatient prostate cancer consult notes. Assessment and plan sections were removed to ensure blind inference. An expanded prompt set was comprised of the original 15 prompts and 45 new NCCN-related prostate cancer queries stratified by task (extraction, analysis, recommendation) and clinical phase (presentation, evaluation, management) (Supplementary Table 3). Using identical generation parameters, GPT-4 and GPT-5 generated 822 new CoT responses. Two independent annotators reviewed all outputs following the same schema as in development. Each recommendation was rated using a 5-point Likert scale that assessed potential impact on patient outcomes, ranging from clinically harmful (1) to clinically optimal (5). Guideline concordance was determined using the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology, Version 2.2024 (Prostate Cancer). During prompt development, NCCN stage-specific management flowsheets were used as the primary reference to construct recommendation queries, with each prompt mapped to a discrete guideline decision node. Thus, annotators evaluated each GPT-generated recommendation against the corresponding NCCN decision pathway. Semantic similarity analysis

GPT-4 and GPT-5 outputs were compared using paired case–question matches. Model responses were parsed into reasoning steps and final answers using structured delimiters present in the output format. For each channel, similarity between GPT-4 and GPT-5 responses was quantified using complementary metrics. Normalized Levenshtein distance was used to quantify the magnitude of textual rewriting as shown previously74. Levenshtein distance measures the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions, or substitutions required to transform one string into another. To account for differences in response length, distances were normalized by the maximum character length of the two responses, producing values between 0 (identical text) and 1 (complete divergence). TF-IDF cosine similarity was used to quantify semantic overlap, as shown previously75. Responses were tokenized into words, converted into term-frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) vectors, and cosine similarity was computed between the resulting vectors. Cosine similarity measures the angle between the two high-dimensional vectors, yielding values between 0 (no shared weighted vocabulary) and 1 (identical semantic representation). Thresholds of Levenshtein ≥0.50 and cosine ≤0.20 were selected to represent substantial textual divergence and low semantic overlap, respectively.

Clinician-generated responses

A board-certified oncologist, specializing in prostate cancer, independently generated stepwise reasoning chains for a subset of 61 question–note pairs spanning four prostate cancer patients across all disease stages (localized, biochemical recurrence, mCSPC, mCRPC). They responded to identical prompts using only the source oncology notes, blinded from LLM responses and from the assessment and plan. Each clinician-generated reasoning chain was converted to a stepwise format and graded by an independent reviewer using the same three-tier error taxonomy, five-point clinical impact scale, and NCCN concordance classification applied to LLM-elicited reasoning chains.

Auto-evaluation and self-mitigation prompting

We developed an automated evaluation framework wherein LLMs classified response errors using the same hierarchical taxonomy applied in the previous manual annotation phases. Three models: GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2024), Claude 3.5-Sonnet (Anthropic, 2024), and Gemini 2.0-Flash (Google, 2024), were tested as automated evaluators. Each model categorized every response at Tier 1 as a correct response, Comprehension Error, or Reasoning Error, then advanced through Tier 2 and Tier 3 classifications for more specific subtypes of failure. We implemented two approaches for the evaluation framework. In the triple-prompting strategy, the evaluation advanced sequentially across taxonomy tiers. A first prompt assigned the response to a Tier 1 class, a second prompt refined that classification into the appropriate Tier 2 subtype, and a third prompt further resolved it to a Tier 3 error category. The decomposed strategy began similarly by distinguishing correct from incorrect responses at Tier 1, but it bypassed Tier 2 classification to query each Tier 3 subtype directly. The model was asked a series of binary questions (e.g., “Is this Tier 3 error present? Yes/No.”) across all possible subtypes. A self-mitigation prompt was constructed from the triple-prompting (tier-based) automated evaluator prompt by incorporating the same hierarchical reasoning error definitions and annotated examples used for evaluation. Following the generation of the initial reasoning chain and structured JSON answer, this prompt was applied as a secondary system instruction directing the model to sequentially audit each reasoning step and answer component against the source chart note, query, and reference error patterns, and to revise only those elements requiring correction while preserving the original reasoning structure and output schema.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data processing and statistical analysis were conducted in R (v4.3.1). Cohen’s kappa was calculated across tiers to assess inter-rater reliability. Error rates were defined as the proportion of reviewer annotations containing an error and were summarized with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. These were calculated across cancer type, task type, context, and reviewer, and summarized both by individual tier and aggregated categories. For multivariable logistic regression models, predictor variables included reasoning task, clinical context, clinical concept, and prostate cancer stage. Error presence was modeled as a binary outcome. Model coefficients were exponentiated to generate odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. For clinical recommendation tasks, average impact scores were calculated as the mean of two independent reviewers' Likert ratings (range: 1 = harmful to 5 = optimal). Between-group comparisons of impact scores (error vs. no-error) were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All categorical and continuous analyses were conducted using standard thresholds (p < 0.05 for significance).
