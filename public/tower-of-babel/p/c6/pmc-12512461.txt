Longitudinal associations between maternal and child screen use at 1 year of age and child behavior and development at 3 years of age
Authors: Stephanie Sim, Juliana Zabatiero, Natasha Bear, Amber Beynon, George Thomas, Ravisha Srinivasjois, Brad Jongeling, Desiree Silva, Leon Straker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYoung children are increasingly exposed to evolving screen technology. International guidelines recommend no screen use for children under the age of 2 years, due to the potential for detrimental effects on behaviour and development. However, evidence for these guidelines is limited by inadequate consideration of device-specific effects (TV and mobile phone/tablet computer), maternal screen use, confounders such as maternal mental health and importance of effect sizes. Our aim was to investigate longitudinal associations of device-specific maternal and child technology use and covariates (including maternal mental health) with behavioural and developmental outcomes, to guide adults on screen use behaviours by and with their children.MethodsParticipants were part of a large birth cohort (The ORIGINS Project). Measures of mother and child screen use (TechU-Q) at child’s 1 year of age and child behaviour and development (Connors Early Childhood) at 3 years of age were used. Demographic data and covariates were obtained antenatally and at 1 year post-birth. Data was extracted in March 2023 for the n = 887 who had completed the Conners EC by this date. Covariates included maternal age, child sex, number of children in the household, maternal education, socioeconomic status, and maternal mental health. To examine associations of screen use and confounding variables with Conners EC t scores, univariable regression and multivariable regression was used. Differences in median Conners EC t scores between the 1st and 4th quartiles of maternal and child screen use were assessed using the Mann Whitney U test.ResultsMaternal and child screen use was inconsistently and weakly associated with behavioural outcomes, whilst poorer maternal mental health was consistently and moderately associated with poorer behavioural outcomes. Maternal and child screen use was inconsistently and weakly associated with developmental outcomes, whilst maternal mental health was not associated with developmental outcomes. There were differences between TV and mobile/tablet use associations with behavioural and developmental outcomes.ConclusionsMaternal and child screen use at 1 year of age appear only weakly related with child behaviour and development at 3 years of age. Maternal mental health is likely to have a more clinically meaningful relationship with child behaviour, but not development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05701-w.Keywords: Screen use, Child development, Mental health, Socioeconomic equity, Cohort, Origins

Introduction

Digital technology is increasingly becoming part of young children’s daily lives [1, 2]. Young children today are exposed to a wider variety of screen-based technologies than in previous decades [3], with newer touch screen devices such as mobile phones and tablet computers added to older technologies such as television (TV) and computers. This change means children have an increased opportunity to access screens and therefore potential for exposure.

In Australia, 17% of infants and toddlers under the age of 3 years have their own mobile screen-based device [1] and in Britain, 75% of infants and toddlers under the age of 3 years use touch screen devices daily [4]. Total screen use in under 2-year-olds internationally has been reported to range between 42 and 120 min per day [1, 5, 6]. This amount of screen use exceeds international guidelines including the World Health Organisation, Canadian and Australian public health guidelines, that recommend no screen use for children under the age of 2 years [7–9]. These guidelines are supported by systematic reviews and meta-analyses that have shown higher levels of screen use in early childhood are associated with poorer behavioural and developmental outcomes such as aggressive behaviours, social functioning, anxiety, mood and affect, physical symptoms, motor skills, and cognitive development [10–13]. However, critique of the evidence base for current guidelines has increased in recent years, including the limited longitudinal evidence in early childhood, limited consideration of potentially differential effects of different types of screen use, parental screen use, family confounding factors such as maternal mental health [14] and household income [2], and a lack of consideration of the effect size and thus clinical importance of associations [15].

Early longitudinal studies provided information on TV use alone. For example, Christakis et al. [16] (2004) found that an increased number of hours of television watched at age 1 (n = 1278) and 3 years (n = 1345) was associated with increased attentional problems at age 7 in American children. More recent longitudinal studies have included both TV and newer mobile/tablet use but often aggregated both uses as total screen use. For example, Liu et al. [17], Kerai et al. [18] and Takashi et al. [19] found that high child screen use in early childhood was longitudinally associated with increased behavioural and developmental problems later in childhood, including conduct problems [17], hyperactivity [17], lower social competence [17, 19], emotional immaturity [17], poor communication skills [18, 19], lower cognitive development [18], poorer fine motor skills [19] and poorer problem solving [18, 19]. However, these longitudinal studies did not adequately consider the potential differential associations of device-specific screen use nor parental screen use.

Some cross-sectional studies have examined device-specific screen use, as TV and mobile/tablet can be used in different ways and therefore may have different impacts [20–22]. Veraksa et al. [23] reported poorer child language outcomes were associated with child ‘passive’ (TV) screen use but not with child ‘interactive’ (mobile/tablet) screen use. In contrast, Beynon et al. [21] found TV screen use was not related to poorer infant development but mobile/tablet use was. Cost et al. [20] argued that parent TV screen use may have different impacts on parent–child interactions than parent mobile/tablet screen use. Differences in the associations between TV and mobile/tablet and child health outcomes have also been reported [21].

The association of parental screen use with child outcomes in early childhood has been evaluated in some cross sectional studies. For example Cost et al. [20] found that parental screen use was related to child behavioural problems at 5 years of age and Wong et al. [24] reported a similar association at 3 years of age. Proposed mechanisms for such associations include the influence of parental screen use on child screen use [25], and parental screen use interference with parent–child interactions [20], due to the increasing frequency of parental distraction from mobiles/tablets whilst around their children [2, 26]. Maternal TV and mobile/tablet use was recently reported to be associated with children’s TV and mobile/tablet use at I year of age [21].

Most of the above studies did not consider maternal mental health as a covariate [10, 17, 18, 27], despite evidence that maternal mental health is associated with child behaviour and development [28]. Longitudinal studies of 4–5 year old children have found poorer maternal mental health was related to not only poorer child outcomes but also higher child TV [29] and mobile/tablet use [30]. Similarly, maternal and paternal mental health have been related to child TV and mobile/tablet use and developmental outcomes at 1 year of age [21], suggesting a potentially complex interplay of parent device-specific screen use, parent mental health, child device-specific screen use and child outcomes. Further, most of the above studies did not discuss whether the statistical associations observed were likely to be of clinical importance, but rather appear to assume that a statistically significant association is likely to have clinical impact. Whilst determining the importance of observed effects in epidemiological studies is challenging, comparing effects of different predictor variables and examining the consistency of those effects has demonstrated utility for examining epidemiological effects of screen use in older children [15].

Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate device-specific (TV and mobile/tablet) longitudinal associations between maternal and child screen use at 1 year of age and behavioural and developmental outcomes at 3 years of age. We also aimed to investigate whether other covariates such as maternal mental health may have stronger and more consistent associations with behavioural and developmental outcomes and thus be more likely to have clinical impact, compared to maternal and child screen use associations.

Methods

Design and participants

This longitudinal study evaluated the associations between maternal and child screen use at 1 year of age and child behaviour and development at 3 years of age. This study was a sub-project of The ORIGINS Project, an ongoing prospective longitudinal, community, birth cohort study recruiting 10,000 families during pregnancy with rolling recruitment over a decade from northern suburbs of Perth, Western Australia [31]. Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their parent (for children). Data collection included parent-completed questionnaires administered antenatally and around the time of the child’s 1 st and 3rd year birthday. ORIGINS recruited 3,708 families during pregnancy from 2016 to 2023. We included data for all participants who had completed the Conners Early Childhood (EC) assessment by March 2023, which included 887 children born from November 2016 to March 2020. Out of this sample, 145 children had data available for all the variables included in the multivariable analysis, collected between November 2016 and March 2023. Outcome variables

Child behaviour and development were assessed using the parent-reported Conners EC [32] questionnaire at 3 years of age. Behaviour scales include inattention/hyperactivity, defiant/aggressive, defiance subscale, aggression subscale, social functioning/atypical behaviours, anxiety, mood and affect, physical symptoms (e.g. sleep, pain, appetite), Conners EC global index: restless/impulsive, Conners EC global index: emotional lability, and Conners EC global index: total. Developmental scales include adaptive skills, communication, motor skills, play and pre-academic/cognitive. The Conners EC was designed to be used in children aged 2 to 6 years. Parents were asked to report on their child’s behaviour in the past one month. Raw scores were converted to standardised t scores for each scale according to age, with higher t scores indicating poorer outcomes. The average range falls within one standard deviation of the mean (i.e. between 40 and 59). T scores were used for analysis, as recommended [32] and to support clinical interpretation. The assessment tool has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: Behavior scales 0.86, Developmental Milestones scales 0.93) [32], 2- to 4-week test–retest reliability, inter-rater reliability, and discriminative, convergent and divergent validity [32].

Explanatory variables

Parent-reported maternal and child screen use were collected using the TechU-Q questionnaire at 1 year of age [33]. Data on technology use was collected for TV, mobile and tablet devices separately. Mothers were asked if children were allowed to use each device or if there were none in the household. Mothers were asked on a typical week (Monday to Friday) and a typical weekend (Saturday to Sunday) how many days each device was used, and the average time per day spent on each device (0, 5 min, 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h, 6 h, 7 h, 8 h, 9 h, 10 h, 11 h, 12 h or more), which were used to calculate the daily average usage (Monday to Sunday) for TV and mobile/tablet devices in minutes ((5 × daily weekday duration) + (2 × daily weekend day duration))/7), providing a continuous variable for analysis. The TechU-Q questionnaire has been found to be reliable and valid for assessing young children’s and parents’ technology use in Australian and American settings, with reasonable test–retest reliability and face and construct validity [33].

Confounding variables

Based on prior research demonstrating correlates of screen use among young children [34–36], a range of covariates were included in the multivariable analysis (see Table 1) including maternal age at birth of the child (years), child’s sex (male/female), number of children in the household (1 child/2 or more children categories), maternal education (4 categories), socio-economic status (quintile categories of index of relative socio-economic advantage and disadvantage IRSAD) [37], and maternal depression anxiety stress symptoms (DASS-21) [38] at 1 year of age (continuous score/21). A higher DASS score indicates more severe symptoms. The DASS-21 has been shown to possess adequate construct validity [39, 40].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterise maternal and child demographics, Conners EC t scores, as well as maternal and child screen use. Medians, interquartile ranges and ranges were used to describe continuous data given many (but not all) were skewed in distribution. Categorical data was described using frequencies and proportions. Associations between Conners EC t scores, maternal and child screen use, and maternal DASS-21 scores was examined using Spearman’s correlation reported in three separate correlation matrices.

To examine associations of screen use and confounding variables with Conners EC t scores, univariable regression and multivariable regression were used. Model fit was examined, and linear regression was used for subscales where residuals were normally distributed. Quantile regression, producing a median estimate, was used for Conner t scores that were skewed and had poor model fit. The multivariable models were adjusted for all covariates listed in Tables 3 and 4, that were chosen a priori as they were considered clinically meaningful predictors. Epsilon-squared measure of effect size was calculated for significantly associated variables and overall for each linear multivariable model.

As described by Orben [15], small effect sizes may be identified with large sample sizes. Therefore, to illustrate likely clinical meaning in addition to reporting epsilon-squared, spider charts were used to compare the median Conners EC t scores of the 1 st quartile (< 25 th percentile) and 4 th quartile (> 75 th percentile) of maternal and child screen use and maternal mental health. Differences in quartiles were assessed using the Mann Whitney U test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analysis was performed using Stata v 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas). Adjustment of critical alpha probability levels was not done, given the nature of the analysis and current statistical thinking [41].

Results

Descriptive statistics

As shown in Table 1, the study sample consisted of 887 children (51.5% male), aged 2.9 years to 3.8 years at time of Conners EC assessment. Most participants in the sample were from more advantaged socioeconomic backgrounds (IRSAD quintiles 4 to 5). Most children had mothers who had completed a university degree (62%). Approximately half of the sample was from a one-child family (51.3%) and the other half had two or more children in the household (48.7%). Conners EC t scores by scale ranged from a median of 43 (play scale) to 56 (mood and affect, and defiance scales) (see supporting information Table 1). Mothers spent a median daily average of 120 min watching TV and 145 min using a mobile/tablet (see Table 2). Approximately one half of the children (50.4%) were allowed to watch television and approximately one fifth (22.8%) of the children were allowed to use a mobile/tablet. Children spent a median (range) daily average of 0 (0–317) minutes watching TV and 0 (0–137) minutes using a mobile/tablet. Further analysis found no difference in maternal and child technology use or confounding variables between participants missing at least one variable from the multivariable regression and the complete case data set.

Correlation matrices for child behaviour and development, screen use and maternal mental health

All the Conners EC behavioural scale t scores were significantly and positively correlated with each other, and most of the developmental scale t scores were significantly correlated with each other (see supporting information Table 2a). Some aspects of maternal and child screen use were correlated (e.g. maternal TV and child TV use, see supporting information Table 2b). All components of the DASS-21 (stress, anxiety, depression, total) were moderately to strongly correlated with each other (see supporting information Table 2c).

Univariable associations between screen use and child behaviour and development

Univariable regression analysis of child behaviour outcomes identified inconsistent and weak associations with maternal and child screen use (see supporting information Table 3a). In addition, there were several associations with confounders (including maternal age, child sex and SES) and consistent and moderate associations with maternal mental health (see supporting information Table 3b). Univariable regression analysis of child development outcomes also identified inconsistent and weak associations with screen use (see supporting information Table 3c), several associations with confounders (including maternal age, child sex and number of children), but no association with maternal mental health (see supporting information Table 3d).

Multivariable associations between screen use and child behaviour and development

Multivariable regression analysis of child behaviour outcomes again identified inconsistent (none or only one of 9 domains) and weak associations (see epsilon-squared values) with screen use, several associations with confounders and consistent (eight of nine domains) and moderate associations (see epsilon-squared values) with maternal mental health (see Table 3). Greater maternal mobile/tablet use was associated with better child mood and affect. Greater child TV use was associated with more problems in inattention/hyperactivity and restless/implusive behaviours. However, the screen use effect sizes were small, for example an increase in child TV use of 60 min per day was associated with an increase of just 3.1/100 on the restless/implusive scale. There were no statistically significant associations between maternal and child screen use and other behavioural scales. Being female was associated with decreased inattention/hyperactivity compared to being male. Having two or more children in the household was associated with decreased inattention/hyperactivity and emotional lability, compared with having one child in the household. Maternal education being trade/apprenticeship was associated with increased inattention/hyperactivity, physical symptoms, and restless/impulsive behaviours, compared to university. Being from a poorer socioeconomic background was associated with poorer social functioning and increased atypical behaviours. Poorer maternal mental health was associated with increased child inattention/hyperactivity, defiant/aggressive behaviours, anxiety, mood and affect, physical symptoms, sleep problems, restless/impulsive behaviours and emotional lability. The strongest association with maternal mental health was seen for child anxiety and physical symptoms.

Multivariable regression analysis of child development outcomes identified inconsistent (none or only two of 9 domains) and weak associations with screen use, several associations with confounders and no association with maternal mental health (see Table 4 including epsilon-squared values). Greater maternal TV use was associated with poorer communication and pre-academic cognition, but no other developmental domains. Maternal mobile/tablet use, child TV use and child mobile/tablet use were not significantly associated with any developmental domains. Having two or more children in the household was associated with poorer pre-academic cognition compared to having one child.

Graphical representation of effect sizes

Figure 1 demonstrates no observable differences in child behavioural and developmental outcomes between groups with high and low maternal and child screen use. Further, the 4 th quartile scores did not reach ‘high average’ (60–64), ‘elevated’ (65–70) nor ‘very elevated’ > 70 which would indicate children at risk. However, in a pattern consistent with the regression analysis, children whose mother had poorer mental health had increased behavioural problems but not developmental problems, with behavioural scores above 60 for several domains. (A similar pattern of response was observed for maternal depression, anxiety and stress scores reported at 1 year of age.)

Discussion

Our study found that both maternal and child screen use at 1 year of age were inconsistently and weakly related to child behavioural and developmental outcomes at 3 years of age, and that this was somewhat similar for TV and mobile/tablet use. Poorer maternal mental health was found to have a more consistent and stronger association with worse behavioural outcomes but not developmental outcomes. Several family social factors were related to child outcomes in the multivariable analysis, including the number of children in the household, maternal education, and socioeconomic status. Univariable analysis provided similar findings.

Our findings have some similarities to the findings of Hinkley et al. [10] in 2020 who found no association between compliance with Australian screen use guidelines at ages 3 to 5 years of age and educational outcomes, social and emotional skills, behaviour and educational outcomes at follow-ups at 6 to 8 years and 9 to 11 years, although they examined an older cohort and did not examine a dose-specific relationship. Our findings differ to the findings of Liu et al. [17] Kerai et al. [18], Takashi et al. [19] and Neville et al. [42] who found that increased child screen use was consistently associated with increased developmental and behavioural problems in multiple domains. Differences may be due to different outcome measures. These prior studies used the Ages and Stages Questionnaire [17, 19], Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire [42], and Early Development Instrument [18], which categorise behavioural and developmental domains differently to Conners EC. Further, differences may be due to the young age of the children within this current study. The association between child screen use and child development and behavioural problems may change as the child grows, perhaps maternal mental health is more important during infancy, but screen use may be more important in later stages of life.

There were some differences between device-specific screen use (i.e. TV and mobile/tablet) associations with behavioural and developmental outcomes. More maternal TV screen use and less maternal mobile/tablet screen use were associated with some poorer child outcomes. Cost et al. [20] had suggested that parental mobile/tablet use may have more impact on child outcomes due to parents being more immersed and less likely to be co-viewing with their child during mobile/tablet screen use. Our findings were in the opposite direction, which may relate to differences in parent–child interactions at 1 year of age (our study) versus 5 years of age (Cost study). More child TV screen use but not mobile/tablet screen use was related to poorer behavioural outcomes in our study, mirroring Veraksa et al.’s [23] finding of device specific associations with language. Together the evidence highlights that device type may influence any relationship of screen use with behaviour and development.

Our findings that poorer maternal mental health is associated with worse behavioural outcomes is consistent with existing literature examining both antenatal and postnatal maternal mental health [28]. Large studies in Australia [43] and the Netherlands [44] have found that maternal antenatal stress and depression is associated with increased behaviour problems in early childhood. Smaller studies in France [45] and USA [46, 47] examining maternal depression, stress and anxiety have had similar findings. Similarly, several large studies in the UK [48] and Canada [49] have found maternal postnatal depression in the first year of life is associated with behavioural problems in early childhood [28]. Possible mechanisms for these findings may be both physiological and psychosocial [50]. Altered function of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis in children mediates behavioural or cognitive alterations observed to be associated with prenatal stress [51]. Maternal depression diagnosed by a structured interview has been found to be significantly correlated with the child having an insecure attachment to their mother [52], which is known to play an important role in a child’s social and emotional development [53].

However, unlike our study, prior literature has described an association between poorer maternal mental health and worse developmental outcomes [28]. Longitudinal studies in Taipei [54], France [55], the Netherlands [56] and Canada [49] found that postnatal mental health disorders were associated with child developmental delay. Our findings may differ to these studies for a number of reasons including the young age of technology use assessment (1 year) in the current study and that that exposure to screen use at later ages may be more influential for outcomes at 3 years of age. Additionally, the proportion of children in our cohort with ‘very elevated’ developmental t scores was small, which may have reduced our ability to detect an association. The differences observed in our study for behavioural versus developmental associations with maternal mental health may be due to children modelling their parents’ behaviours and emotions, whilst their cognitive development and neuromuscular coordination development may be less impacted upon by their mother’s mental state at this early age. Nevertheless, our study affirms that optimising maternal mental health may be important in improving child health outcomes, particularly behaviour.

We found that multiple family factors were associated with child behavioural and developmental outcomes. Our finding that socioeconomic status is associated with aspects of child behaviour is consistent with existing literature [57] and highlights the predisposing factors driving inequality in child health. Our finding that having two or more children in the household was associated with decreased inattention/hyperactivity problems is similar to the findings of du Prel Carroll et al. in China [58] that having a single child in a family is associated with the risk of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity (ADHD) diagnosis, although that cross-sectional study required a formal ADHD diagnosis in older children (5–18 years of age) and they suggested that their results could be explained by single children receiving more attention from their parents and therefore more likely to receive medical assessment. Our finding that maternal highest education being trade/apprenticeship is associated with increased restless-impulsive behaviours compared to children of mothers with a university degree is similar to Tovik et al. [59], who reported that lower educational attainment of mothers and fathers was associated with higher child ADHD symptoms.

The findings of our multivariable analysis suggest that there is likely a complex interplay of family factors, including maternal mental health and screen use, that contribute to child behavioural and developmental outcomes, thus implying that the focus may need to be shifted away from rigid time-based screen use recommendations in early childhood. The comparison of effect sizes of screen use and maternal mental health suggested attention could be better directed to optimising maternal mental health, maternal education, and equitable distribution of wealth. Previous research has also found an association between maternal mental health and both maternal and child screen use, further highlighting the complex interplay between these factors [21].

Strengths of our study include using device-specific data that separately examined TV and mobile/tablet use which are the two main screen exposures of young children. We also looked at maternal screen use as both TV and mobile/tablet, acknowledging that parental behaviours may also influence child development. Our multivariable analysis allowed potential confounding factors to be controlled which enabled the study to investigate the independent impact of screen use and, together with the spider plots, suggested other environmental factors that may have a stronger association.

A limitation of our study was that our evidence demonstrated an association but not causation, although it did take account of many known confounders. We had a lack of objective measurement of screen use and behavioural and developmental outcomes, and used single informant reporting for both screen use and behavioural and developmental outcomes, which risks recall bias. Maternal underreporting of screen time may reduce the association between screen time and behavioural and developmental outcomes. We did not investigate content or context of viewing (e.g. whether there was co-viewing with an adult). We did not investigate father’s screen use and mental health, which could be important in future studies considering the context of modern family households. We also did not consider maternal chronic disease, parenting style, or other aspects of family such as genetics, which may contribute to residual confounding. There was missing data which reduced our sample size. Missing data may have resulted from difficulties in engaging a large cohort with many assessments resulting in questionnaire fatigue, and technical problems with the online delivery of questions during the data collection period. This may have resulted in selection bias and the inclusion of participants who may have had less behavioural and developmental problems. Whilst the COVID-19 restrictions in Western Australia were light and of very short duration compared to many other communities, it may have impacted the variables in this study including maternal and child screen use, maternal mental health and child outcomes.

Future studies should examine technology use within a family context using longitudinal and pathway approaches and including whether content and context of TV and mobile/tablet use prospectively affects the association with child behaviour and development, and should consider whether there is an association between other family members’ screen use and child development. To provide stronger evidence, it will be necessary to use innovative forms of screen use measurement such as device- or remote-based data logging or wearable cameras [60].

Conclusions

This was the first reported longitudinal study examining both maternal and child device-specific screen use during infancy and its association with child behaviour and development in toddlerhood. Maternal and child TV or mobile/tablet use at 1 year of age was only inconsistently and weakly related to child outcomes at 3 years of age. In contrast, maternal mental health was consistently and moderately related to child behavioural outcomes at 3 years of age, but not developmental outcomes. Additionally, other family factors were associated with child behaviour and development, suggesting that developmental risk factors in childhood are multifactorial and complex. We suggest that the focus of interventions aimed at enhancing child development and behaviour should be directed to support maternal mental health, rather than focussed on maternal and child screen time.
