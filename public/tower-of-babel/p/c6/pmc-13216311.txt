MYC-induced USP10 stabilizes SOX4 to promote thymocyte proliferation and leukemia onset in mice
Authors: Mengdi Zhang, Hanrui Wu, Xiaoxi Lin, Fan Zhao, Qiaolin Deng, Linrong Lu, Xia Li, Xiang Chen, Hong Liu, Paul E Love, Jiaqi Huang, Bin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysregulation of thymic T cell development compromises immune homeostasis and can lead to leukemic transformation, but the molecular mechanisms linking developmental signals, proliferative cues, and leukemogenesis remain incompletely understood. Here, we integrate deubiquitinase library screening and publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing to analyze mouse and human thymocytes. We find the deubiquitinase USP10 to be expressed in thymocytes, and also elevated in peripheral blood from patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) compared to healthy controls; by contrast, T cell-specific USP10 deficiency blocks mouse thymocyte proliferation and differentiation. Mechanistically, USP10 interacts with SOX4, de-ubiquitinating and protecting SOX4 from degradation to promote thymocyte proliferation, with SOX4 overexpression restoring thymocyte differentiation in USP10-deficient mice. Lastly, MYC induces Usp10 expression, and pharmacologic inhibition of USP10 delays MYC-driven leukemogenesis in a mouse leukemia model. Our results thus identify USP10 as coordinator of developmental signals and oncogenic processes in thymocytes, and implicate USP10 as a potential target for T-ALL therapy.Subject terms: T cells, Systems analysis, Ubiquitylation, Leukaemia

Introduction

T cell development is a tightly regulated process essential for generating a diverse, self-tolerant repertoire, which is crucial for adaptive immunity1–4. In the thymus, thymus-seeding progenitor T cells (TSP) undergo sequential differentiation and selection to eliminate autoreactive clones and establish immune competence, ultimately maturing into functional T cells5–8. Aberrant thymocyte development serves as underling mechanism in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL), where dysregulated proliferation and survival result in malignant transformation9–14. Although numerous regulators of thymocyte differentiation have been identified, the molecular mechanisms connecting developmental signals, proliferative cues, and oncogenic transformation remain incompletely understood. Further elucidation of these pathways is essential for advancing cell-based immunotherapies and regenerative medicine approaches15–20.

The development of thymocytes is governed by a precise interplay of signaling cascades and transcriptional networks that dictate lineage commitment, proliferation, and selection. TSPs progress through CD4⁻CD8⁻ double-negative (DN) stages, where Notch, IL-7, and Kit signaling coordinate lineage commitment and initiate proliferation21,22. Subsequently, Notch and pre-TCR signaling drive a 100- to 200-fold proliferative burst, followed by rapid downregulation of TCR precursor expression in the double-positive (DP) stage, where cells undergo positive and negative selection23–26. However, in T-ALL, these carefully orchestrated processes are subverted through pathological activation of oncogenic pathways, particularly NOTCH1 or pre-TCR-driven transcriptional programs that converge on MYC networks, ultimately driving uncontrolled proliferation and developmental arrest27–31. Although therapeutic strategies targeting these core nodes are evolving, their efficacy is often limited by compensatory pathway activation and on-target toxicity in healthy lymphocytes32–34. This therapeutic challenge is exemplified by SRY-related High Mobility Group box transcription factor 4 (SOX4), a pleiotropic transcription factor essential for normal lymphopoiesis yet frequently co-opted in leukemogenesis35–43. Like many transcription factors, SOX4 is considered undruggable due to its intrinsically disordered structure outside the DNA-binding domain, precluding structure-based inhibitor design. Although siRNA delivery presents a potential strategy, SOX4’s regulation of DICER and AGO1 complicates RNA interference–based approaches42,44–46. Providentially, disrupting oncogenic signaling through proteostasis modulation offers a promising alternative. The ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) plays a critical role in regulating thymocyte development by controlling protein stability and turnover, whereas leukemic cells frequently become reliant on aberrant proteostasis, including heightened UPS-dependent degradation of tumor suppressors. This vulnerability has been clinically explored using proteasome and Nedd8-activating enzyme (NAE) inhibitors such as bortezomib and pevonedistat in acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), underscoring protein homeostasis as a tractable therapeutic axis19,47,48. While our and others’ previous work identified key E3 ubiquitin ligases (e.g., Fbxl1/12) as regulators of T cell fate, the de-ubiquitination machinery—which potentially couples developmental cues to malignant progression—remains poorly understood 15,49–53.

Ubiquitination is a reversible post-translational modification that regulates protein stability, function, and turnover through proteasome-dependent degradation54,55. This process is orchestrated by E1, E2, and E3 ligases and reversed by deubiquitinases (DUB). USP10, a member of the ubiquitin-specific protease (USP) family, regulates multiple important biological processes, including DNA repair, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis56–61. Previous studies have focused on its context-dependent roles in cancer, where USP10 may act as either a tumor suppressor or oncogene62–64. Despite these findings, the contribution of USP10 to thymocyte development and T-cell leukemogenesis has yet to be elucidated.

In this study, USP10 emerges as a central node linking thymocyte proliferative expansion to leukemogenesis. The DUB library screening in mice, publicly available single-cell transcriptomics of human thymocytes and bulk RNA-seq of mouse thymocytes identify USP10 as a highly expressed thymic deubiquitinase that peaks at proliferative stages, and further show that T cell–restricted loss of USP10 in mice impairs the DN-to-DP transition. USP10 binds SOX4 and preserves its abundance to sustain cell-cycle progression; in parallel, pre-TCR–MYC signaling induces Usp10, establishing a pre-TCR–MYC–USP10–SOX4 circuit that supports early thymocyte expansion. In addition, USP10 and SOX4 are upregulated in human T-ALL patients and MYC-driven leukemic mouse models, and USP10 inhibition delays MYC-driven leukemogenesis in vivo. Collectively, these findings position USP10 as a key integrator of developmental and oncogenic cues in thymocytes and highlight it as a potential therapeutic target in T-ALL.

Results

USP10 exhibits a dynamic expression pattern during T cell development

To identify DUBs potentially involved in T cell development, we screened 89 DUB candidates using the DUB RT2 Profiler PCR array (DUB library) (Fig. 1a). Genes with |log2FC | ≥ 1 were defined as differentially expressed. Five DUBs were upregulated and three were downregulated in thymocytes (Fig. 1b). Subsequently, we validated the top 10 upregulated DUBs via reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT–qPCR) analysis, which showed strong concordance with the DUB library and identified Usp10, along with Usp50, Usp3, Stambpl1, and Usp48, as the top 5 DUBs with the highest expression in the thymus. Among these, Usp10 exhibited a significantly elevated expression (Fig. 1b, c). In addition, RT-qPCR of mouse tissues revealed Usp10 was abundant in liver and was also expressed in spleen and thymus (Fig. 1d). This suggests that USP10 may play an important role in T cell development. Furthermore, we analyzed a public single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from human thymus (https://developmental.cellatlas.io/studies/thymus-development), tracking its variation across different stages of thymocyte development. Figure 1e shows a continuous trajectory of developing thymocytes. DN and DP cells were categorized into two phases based on cell cycle gene expression: the proliferative (P) phase, marked by strong cell cycle activity, and the quiescent (Q) phase. CDK1 and RAG1 serve as their respective markers (Fig. 1f left). Interestingly, the USP10 expression was highly dynamic in the human thymus and significantly upregulated during the peak proliferation phase of T cell development (Fig. 1f right-1g). A similar expression pattern was observed in mice, where the public scRNA-seq analysis of the mouse thymus revealed elevated Usp10 levels during thymocyte proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). This was further validated by RT-qPCR analysis of flow-sorted thymocyte subsets, which showed a notable increase in Usp10 mRNA during the DN-to-DP transition (Fig. 1h). We further validated this pattern using The Gene Expression Commons database of mouse hematopoietic cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Analysis of public T-ALL cohorts and the TARGET dataset revealed markedly elevated USP10 expression in T-ALL and ALL samples compared with healthy controls, supporting a potential role for USP10 in leukemic progression (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 1c).

To directly assess USP10 function in T cell ontogeny, we performed an in vitro DN development assay under the treatment of the retroviral knockdown of Usp1065. The loss of USP10 decreased the ratio of DP-to-DN cells and led to a blockade in T cell development in vitro (Fig. 1j, k). Together, these results demonstrate that USP10 may play pivotal roles during early thymocyte development.

The deletion of USP10 severely impairs thymocyte proliferation and deteriorates T cell development

We used the Cre–loxP system to assess the role of USP10 in T cell development. Using Lck-Cre, we generated Usp10fl/flLckcre/+ mice (cKO), in which deletion initiated at DN2 and was largely complete by DN3. Littermates expressing Lck-Cre were used as wild-type (WT) controls throughout the study. Genotyping and immunoblotting confirmed that USP10 was specifically deleted in cKO thymocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). USP10 deficiency led to thymic atrophy and decreased cellularity of the thymus (Fig. 2a), including a slight decrease in the number of ISP cells and a severe reduction in the number of DP, SP4, and SP8 cells (Figs. 2b, c and Supplementary Fig. 2d). While the cKO mice showed an increase in the percentage of DN cells, DN cell numbers were not affected (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d), indicating a profound developmental block at the DN-to-DP transition in cKO mice. The thymic architecture was also significantly disrupted in cKO mice, with a markedly reduced medullary region (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Subsequently, we next examined which step of thymocyte development was altered in cKO mice. The proportions of late-stage thymocytes, including post-positive selection (TCRβ⁺CD69⁺) and mature (TCRβ⁺CD69⁻) SP thymocytes, were comparable between cKO and WT mice. This phenotype was corroborated by the similar proportions of mature SP thymocytes (TCRβ⁺CD24⁻) observed in cKO and WT mice, indicating that USP10 deficiency did not impair late-stage thymocyte maturation. To further delineate whether TCR signal strength was altered in cKO thymocytes, we evaluated the surface expression of CD5, a surrogate marker of TCR signaling strength. We found that CD5 expression remained unchanged in cKO SP thymocytes, demonstrating the equivalent TCR signaling intensity between cKO and WT thymocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2e-2h). Furthermore, we crossed cKO and WT mice with OT-II transgenic mice to fix TCR ligand specificity, ensuring that all DP thymocytes in both groups were subjected to the same positively selecting ligands in response to identical TCR signaling. We found that the transgenic expression of pre-rearranged OT-II TCR in cKO T cells failed to reverse the DN-to-DP developmental block (Fig. 2d, e).

We next investigated whether impaired proliferation could account for the altered thymocyte subset distribution in the absence of USP10. DN4 and ISP thymocytes from cKO mice exhibited a substantial reduction in the number of thymocytes in the S/G2M phase of the cell cycle, which was followed by a further reduction in the number of DP blasts and DP thymocytes (Fig. 2f, g and Supplementary Fig. 3a–c), whereas apoptosis in DN-to-DP subsets was similar between groups (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). To address whether USP10 deletion intrinsically affects T cell development, Lin− DN cells from WT or cKO mice were isolated and cultured on plate-bound DLL4 with CXCL12, an in vitro model for thymocyte development. Consistent with ex vivo findings, the cKO group exhibited a severe blockade in progression to the DP stage and a marked reduction in proliferation compared to WT controls (Fig. 2h–i).

Consequently, peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cell populations exhibited notable reductions in both the spleen and lymph nodes (Fig. 2j–k and Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). USP10 deficiency led to an increased proportion of effector/memory T cells (CD44⁺CD62L⁻) and a decreased proportion of naïve T cells (CD44⁻CD62L⁺) in the spleen and lymph nodes. Additionally, the proportion of CD69⁺ activated T cells was elevated, consistent with lymphopenia-induced homeostatic expansion as a consequence of impaired thymic development (Supplementary Fig. 5a–f).

Collectively, these findings establish USP10 as a key regulator of thymocyte development, indispensable for proliferative expansion following β-selection. Lck-Cre–mediated ablation of USP10 led to impaired proliferation at the DN4, ISP, DP blast, and DP stages, culminating in a developmental bottleneck at the DN-to-DP transition and a substantial reduction in downstream DP and SP thymocytes.

USP10 regulates T cell development in a cell-intrinsic manner

T cell development depends on both thymocyte-intrinsic programs and thymic microenvironmental cues, including cytokines and thymic epithelial cell support. To determine whether USP10 regulates T cell development in a cell-intrinsic manner, we generated bone marrow chimeras by reconstituting irradiated Rag1-/- recipient mice with a 1:1 mixture of bone marrow cells from CD45.1+ wild-type mice and CD45.2+ WT control or cKO mice (Fig. 3a). After 10 weeks of reconstitution, CD45.2+ control cells showed a modest advantage in reconstituting both B and T lineage cells compared to the CD45.1+ competitors. By contrast, CD45.2+ cKO cells were significantly outcompeted across the DN, DP, and SP stages of thymocyte development (Fig. 3b) and among CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in the spleen and lymph nodes (Fig. 3c–d). CD45.2⁺ cKO-derived thymocytes developed in the same Rag1⁻/⁻ recipient thymus recapitulated the developmental defects observed in non-chimeric cKO mice, and similar competitive defects were evident in peripheral T cells (Fig. 3e). These results confirm that USP10 regulates T cell development in a cell-intrinsic manner.

USP10 deficiency impairs γδ T cell differentiation but not proliferation or maturation

Given that γδ T cell development also initiates in the thymus, we investigated the potential role of USP10 in regulating γδ T lineage commitment. The total number of γδTCR+ thymocytes, as well as the number and percentage of CD24- mature subset, were comparable in cKO and WT mice. Beyond lineage commitment, effector fate decisions of γδ T cells—such as IFN-γ or IL-17 production—are also shaped in the thymus. USP10 deficiency resulted in a higher proportion of IFN-γ–producing γδ T cells and a lower proportion of IL-17–producing cells compared to wild-type controls. Absolute cell counts confirmed an increase in γδT1 cells and a decrease in γδT17 cells within the total γδ T cell pool (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c), indicating that USP10 is essential for proper γδ T cell effector differentiation. While regulatory T cells (Treg) can arise from conventional CD4+ T cells in the periphery, the thymus is considered the primary site of Treg development. Thymic natural Treg (nTreg) development appeared to be unaffected by USP10 deficiency, despite a drastic reduction in DP thymocyte numbers. The proportion of FOXP3⁺ nTregs in the thymus remained comparable between cKO and WT mice. The decreased nTreg count in cKO mice was likely a consequence of the overall reduction in DP thymocytes, from which nTregs originate (Supplementary Fig. 6d).

Identification of the key substrates of USP10 regulating thymocyte proliferation

To investigate the regulatory role of USP10 in αβ T cell development, we performed RNA sequencing on WT and cKO DP thymocytes isolated from the thymus. DP thymocytes were prioritized for analysis as they exhibited the most pronounced reduction in cellularity upon USP10 deletion. In USP10-deficient mice, genes that are normally downregulated during the transition from the DN to DP stage remained abnormally elevated in DP thymocytes. Conversely, genes typically upregulated during this transition were expressed at lower levels compared to WT controls (Fig. 4a–b). These gene expression patterns provide molecular evidence that USP10-deficient DP thymocytes undergo delayed or disrupted developmental progression. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that USP10-deficient DP thymocytes were enriched for cell cycle-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 7a), consistent with changes in proliferative programs during the DN-to-DP transition in cKO mice.

As a deubiquitinating enzyme, USP10 modulates protein stability through post-translational modifications. To gain deeper mechanistic insight into how USP10 regulates thymic T cell development, we next performed comprehensive proteomic analysis to identify potential downstream targets (Supplementary Fig. 7b). The volcano plot identified 58 proteins that were downregulated and 104 that were upregulated in cKO DP thymocytes (fold change >1.5, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4c). Gene ontology (GO) analysis highlighted a considerable fraction of proteins downregulated in cKO group that belonged to the cell cycle and the key events in T cell development, such as β-selection and T cell homeostasis (Fig. 4d). Prominently downregulated proteins associated with thymocyte proliferation, differentiation, and cell cycle were summarized in the heatmap (Fig. 4e). To identify proteins directly interacting with USP10, we performed immunoprecipitation under native conditions from thymocytes isolated from wild-type C57BL/6 J (C57) mice, and then further analyzed the resultant immunoprecipitants by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), an approach hereafter referred to as immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry (IP–MS). These results were then integrated with the global proteomic profile to identify potential USP10 targets (Fig. 4f). Figure 4g displays a subset of key USP10-associated proteins with their corresponding peptide scores, along with representative MS/MS spectra for the unique peptide derived from USP10 and SOX4, respectively. Notably, SOX4 was not only identified as a USP10 binding partner, but was also significantly downregulated in the USP10-deficient proteome. To assess whether USP10 might engage SOX4, we performed molecular docking to infer potential interaction interfaces. The docking model predicted a plausible binding configuration involving residues near the ubiquitin-interacting region (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 7c), supporting a possibility in which USP10 may stabilize SOX4 through direct binding and deubiquitination. Given the critical role of SOX4 in thymocyte proliferation and differentiation35–43, this interaction may represent a key post-translational mechanism by which USP10 regulates early T cell development.

USP10 regulates thymocyte proliferation via stabilizing SOX4 through deubiquitination

To verify this interaction, immunoprecipitations were performed in HEK293T cells and in thymocytes from wild-type mice. We found that USP10 interacted with SOX4 in HEK293T cells co-transfected with Myc-USP10 and Flag-SOX4 plasmids (Fig. 5a). In parallel, Flag-SOX4 also pulled down endogenous USP10 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5b). Furthermore, endogenous USP10 and SOX4 formed a complex in thymocytes from wild-type mice (Fig. 5c).

Given the identified interaction of USP10 with SOX4, we next investigated whether USP10 affects the steady-state levels of SOX4. Increasing USP10 expression in HEK293T cells led to an elevation of SOX4 levels in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, overexpression of a catalytically inactive USP10 mutant (C424A) failed to affect SOX4 protein levels, suggesting that USP10-mediated stabilization of SOX4 is dependent on its deubiquitinase activity (Fig. 5d). To further assess whether USP10 stabilizes SOX4, we performed cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays in HEK293T cells overexpressing USP10. USP10 markedly extended the half-life of SOX4, indicating enhanced protein stability (Fig. 5e–f). Consistently, USP10 knockdown reduced SOX4 levels, an effect that was reversed by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 5g). Moreover, ectopic expression of wild-type USP10—but not the catalytically inactive C424A mutant—significantly diminished K48-linked polyubiquitination of SOX4, consistent with USP10 stabilizing SOX4 by removing its degradative ubiquitin chains (Fig. 5h–i). Also, Sox4 mRNA levels were comparable between cKO and WT thymocytes, whereas SOX4 protein levels were markedly reduced in the cKO group (Fig. 5j–k). These results support a model in which USP10 stabilizes SOX4 by removing K48-linked polyubiquitin chains and preventing its proteasomal degradation.

To delineate the transcriptional network downstream of SOX4, we profiled genes involved in cell cycle and proliferation. Several SOX4 targets exhibited concordant changes at both the mRNA and protein levels in thymocytes, consistent with the USP10-deficient pattern and supporting a link between SOX4 activity and thymocyte proliferation. Notably, p27, a key regulator of cell cycle arrest, remained unchanged, suggesting it may not be a major SOX4-responsive node in this setting (Fig. 5j–k). Taken together, these results demonstrate that USP10 deubiquitinates and stabilizes SOX4 in thymocytes and regulates T cell development through its deubiquitinase activity.

Restoration of SOX4 expression effectively rescues the T cell developmental defects caused by USP10 deficiency

To further determine whether SOX4 acts downstream of USP10 to support thymocyte development, we first restored SOX4 expression in a DLL4-driven in vitro differentiation system. DN thymocytes from WT and cKO mice were cultured on plate-bound DLL4 with CXCL12 and transduced with retroviral Sox4-GFP or vector control. After 3–4 days, SOX4 overexpression markedly enhanced DP differentiation, with cKO thymocytes showing a near-complete restoration of DP proportions and increased DP cell numbers (Fig. 6a–b). We next assessed this relationship in vivo by intrathymic delivery of AAV8–Sox4. A clear GFP⁺ thymocyte population was detected within 7–14 days. SOX4 reconstitution improved thymocyte maturation in both WT and cKO mice and alleviated the developmental defect in the cKO background. In addition, SOX4 supplementation increased thymocyte proliferation and largely normalized the proportion and distribution of DN subsets (Fig. 6c–d and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Together, these findings implicate SOX4 as a critical downstream effector of USP10 in thymocyte development.

We next sought to determine whether modulation of SOX4 protein stability could further augment its developmental activity. To this end, we systematically mapped the major ubiquitination sites of SOX4 and assessed whether stabilizing SOX4 through mutation of these critical residues could enhance thymocyte development. LC–MS/MS mapping identified five candidate ubiquitination sites on SOX4 (K76, K118, K127, K135, K148) (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Cross-referencing with PhosphoSitePlus and GPS-Uber predictions prioritized K76, K118, and K127 for functional validation (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Ubiquitination assays revealed that K118R—but not K76R or K127R—markedly reduced SOX4 ubiquitination and prolonged its protein half-life, whereas K76R and K127R produced compensatory increases (Supplementary Fig. 8e–g). These findings identified K118 as a dominant ubiquitination site governing SOX4 stability and enabled us to employ the stabilized SOX4K118R mutant for reconstitution studies. Strikingly, SOX4K118R provided a stronger rescue than SOX4WT in both retroviral and AAV-mediated settings. SOX4K118R promoted DN-to-DP transition, restored the DP/DN ratio to near-WT levels, and increased thymocyte proliferation and SOX4 protein abundance in cKO thymocytes (Fig. 6e–h and Supplementary Fig. 8h–k).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that USP10 supports thymocyte development largely through stabilization of the SOX4 protein. Importantly, supplementation with the ubiquitination-resistant SOX4K118R mutant significantly improves T-cell developmental outcomes in USP10-deficient thymocytes, highlighting SOX4 stabilization as a major contributor linking USP10 to thymocyte development.

The MYC–USP10–SOX4 axis is upregulated in T-ALL

As USP10 is required for efficient thymocyte development, the mechanisms controlling its expression are not well understood. The rapid turnover of USP10 transcripts in thymocytes (Fig. 1g) suggests that its expression is subject to tight transcriptional control. We therefore hypothesized that specific transcription factors regulate Usp10 expression during T cell development and connect developmental signaling to thymocyte maturation. We conducted a combined analysis using five bioinformatic tools—UCSC, ENCODE, KnockTF, hTFtarget, and GTEx—identifying 74, 106, 29, 180, and 1285 candidate transcriptional regulators, respectively. Notably, MYC emerged as the sole factor present in the overlap across all datasets (Fig. 7a). We further examined the correlation between MYC and USP10 in GTEx immune tissues, TCGA pan-cancer, and leukemia datasets. Interestingly, a positive association between the two was consistently observed across these sources (Fig. 7b). To determine whether MYC directly regulates USP10 transcription, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)–PCR assays. Chromatin fragments spanning the Usp10 promoter were enriched in anti-MYC immunoprecipitates from HEK293T cells (Fig. 7c–d), consistent with MYC occupancy at the Usp10 promoter. These data support a MYC–USP10–SOX4 regulatory axis relevant to thymocyte development. To assess the relevance of the MYC–USP10–SOX4 axis in leukemogenesis, we analyzed transcriptomic data from the public T-ALL cohort. Notably, MYC, USP10, and SOX4 were significantly upregulated in bone marrow samples from T-ALL patients compared with healthy controls (Figs. 1i and 7e). Analysis of the TARGET cohort confirmed the same expression pattern (Supplementary Fig. 9a). RT-qPCR and immunoblotting showed increased USP10 and SOX4 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), and their levels correlated with MYC expression, suggesting a cooperative role in T-ALL progression (Fig. 7f–g).

Notch and pre-TCR signaling coordinately regulate early thymocyte development, and both induce Myc expression25,66–68. Notch activity peaks at the DN2/3a stages but diminishes in DN4, ISP, and DP thymocytes, whereas Myc expression persists in DN4 and ISP, consistent with continued input from pre-TCR signaling beyond DN323,69–71. During β-selection, pre-TCR signaling supports thymocyte survival and drives DN-to-DP transition. To examine its effect on Myc and Usp10 expression, we induced post-β-selection signaling in Rag1-/- mice via αCD3ε antibody injection and harvested thymocytes 1–3 days after treatment (Fig. 7h). As previously reported72,73, αCD3ε antibody injection induces pre-TCR-like signaling, driving the transition from DN3 to DP thymocytes (Fig. 7i). Importantly, following anti-CD3 treatment, MYC protein levels were upregulated, accompanied by USP10 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 7j and Supplementary Fig. 9b). To directly test whether USP10 induction depends on pre-TCR signaling, we compared DN3 thymocytes from Rag1⁻/⁻ mice with those from C57 controls. As Rag1 deficiency prevents TCRβ rearrangement and pre-TCR assembly, USP10 expression was significantly decreased at both the mRNA and protein levels in Rag1⁻/⁻ DN3 cells. SOX4 protein abundance was similarly reduced (Fig. 7k–l). These findings support a model in which pre-TCR-triggered Myc expression upregulates USP10, thereby modulating SOX4 protein abundance. For further validation in vivo, we crossed Lck-Cre mice with mice carrying a Rosa26-targeted conditional Myc transgene (MycTg/Tg). Usp10 expression progressively increased, with mRNA levels rising 1.5-fold in MycTg/+Lckcre/+ and 3-fold in MycTg/TgLckcre/+ mice relative to MycTg/Tg controls (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Immunoblotting confirmed corresponding increases in USP10 protein, accompanied by elevated SOX4 levels (Fig. 7m). Thymocytes from MycTg/TgLckcre/+ mice exhibited aberrant proliferative activity and a propensity for malignant transformation30. To interrogate the role of USP10 in this context, DN cells from MycTg/TgLckcre/+ mice were subjected to retroviral-mediated Usp10 knockdown followed by in vitro DN development assays. Flow cytometry and RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that Usp10 knockdown substantially reduced the pathological proliferation of DN cells in vitro, as evidenced by reduced Ki-67 expression (Fig. 7n–o and Supplementary Fig. 9d).

Collectively, these findings delineate the MYC–USP10–SOX4 axis as a pivotal regulatory pathway in T cell development and T-ALL pathogenesis, establishing USP10 as a MYC-inducible effector that mediates, at least in part, the MYC-driven leukemogenesis.

USP10 expression and SOX4 stability in thymocytes are maintained independently of Notch signaling

Given the essential role of Notch signaling in T cell development, and prior evidence that USP10 stabilizes NOTCH1 via deubiquitination in endothelial cells56, we examined whether USP10 similarly regulates NOTCH1 in thymocytes and whether altered Notch signaling contributes to the USP10-deficient phenotype. In Usp10-cKO thymocytes, NOTCH1 and NICD protein levels were not diminished but rather elevated at baseline and following DLL4 stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 10a), likely reflecting compensatory Notch activation. Notch1 mRNA and canonical targets (Hes1, Dtx1) were also upregulated both at baseline and after DLL4 stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 10b–c), and GSEA confirmed pathway enrichment (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Co-immunoprecipitation did not detect a USP10–NICD interaction (Supplementary Fig. 10e), arguing against a direct regulatory role. Thus, USP10 is dispensable for NOTCH1 stability in thymocytes.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of human thymus showed HES1 enriched at DN but sharply reduced at DP, whereas MYC and USP10 remained elevated at the proliferative DP(Q) stage (Supplementary Fig. 10f–h). This pattern was validated in sorted mouse subsets: Hes1 declined post-DN3, while Myc persisted at DN4 and DP-blast (Supplementary Fig. 10i), supporting a model in which MYC and USP10 expression at later developmental stages is maintained by pre-TCR–dependent signaling rather than by Notch activation.

To further assess whether Notch signaling regulates USP10 and SOX4 stability, we pharmacologically inhibited Notch activity in primary thymocytes. Notch blockade with DBZ did not affect Usp10 mRNA or USP10/SOX4 protein levels, nor did it alter SOX4 ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. 10j–l), indicating that Notch activity does not measurably regulate USP10 or SOX4 stability under these experimental conditions. Functionally, enforced NICD expression in USP10-deficient thymocytes conferred only minimal rescue of developmental progression and failed to restore proliferation or DP/DN ratio (Supplementary Fig. 10m–n). While NICD co-expression in HEK293T cells partially restored USP10 and stabilized SOX4 following Usp10 knockdown, this compensatory effect was not evident in thymocytes (Supplementary Fig. 10o), likely due to temporal uncoupling—Notch activity declines sharply beyond DN3, whereas USP10 expression peaks at the DP-blast stage. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the MYC-USP10–SOX4 axis functions independently of Notch signaling during thymocyte maturation.

Inhibition of USP10 alleviates MYC-driven leukemogenesis

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting USP10 in MYC-driven leukemogenesis, we used the USP10 inhibitor Spautin-174–76. We first assessed its biochemical activity against USP10. In vitro deubiquitination assays with purified USP10 demonstrated that Spautin-1 suppressed USP10 catalytic activity in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Functionally, Spautin-1 treatment led to a time-dependent reduction in SOX4 protein abundance in both primary thymocytes and Flag–SOX4–expressing HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. 11b–c). When the treatment window was shortened to 0–4 h in the presence of MG132, Spautin-1 substantially increased SOX4 ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. 11d), consistent with reduced SOX4 stability upon USP10 inhibition. Given reports that Spautin-1 can also inhibit USP13, we examined the relevance of USP13 in wild-type and T-ALL thymus. Analyses of the Gene Expression Commons and the Human Protein Atlas revealed that USP13 is expressed at very low levels in the thymus, in contrast to higher USP10 abundance, and is relatively enriched in non-lymphoid tissues (Supplementary Fig. 12a–b). RT–qPCR of purified thymocytes showed that Usp10 transcript levels were ~80–90-fold higher than Usp13 in wild-type C57 thymocytes and ~180-fold higher in MYC-driven T-ALL (Supplementary Fig. 12c). Consistently, immunoblotting showed robust USP10 protein in both wild-type and T-ALL thymus, whereas USP13 was barely detectable (Supplementary Fig. 12d). These findings indicate that the biological activity of Spautin-1 in this system predominantly reflects USP10 inhibition, thereby supporting its use to assess the therapeutic impact of USP10 targeting in T-ALL.

Since Lck-Cre drives Myc expression in DN2-3 thymocytes, malignant transformation likely initiates in DN cells and progresses to lethal T-cell lymphoma/leukemia. MycTg/TgLckcre/+ mice exhibited rapid lethality (median survival: 70 days) (Fig. 8a), accompanied by clinical features including lethargy, ruffled fur, and thymic and splenic enlargement (Fig. 8b–c and Supplementary Fig. 13a). Flow cytometry revealed an accumulation of abnormal CD3⁺ DN thymocytes and peripheral CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells. These populations displayed blast-like morphology and aberrant CD5 and CD71 expression, consistent with human T-ALL (Fig. 8d–e and Supplementary Fig. 13b). Spautin-1 treatment improved gross appearance and significantly extended survival in MycTg/TgLckcre/+ mice, with partial restoration of T-cell subset distribution and surface marker profiles that approached those of controls (Fig. 8a–e and Supplementary Fig. 13a–b). Additionally, Spautin-1 effectively restrained the aberrant proliferation of leukemic cells (Supplementary Fig. 13c–f). To evaluate the functional requirement for USP10 in human T-ALL, we treated Jurkat cells (Notch1-mutant) with Spautin-1, which reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, at 1.45 μM and above, viability decreased by >50% (Supplementary Fig. 14). Loucy cells (Notch1–wild-type) showed greater sensitivity (IC₅₀, ~0.43 μM), indicating that USP10 supports T-ALL cell survival irrespective of NOTCH1 status (Fig. 8f). To extend these findings to non-immortalized cells, PBMCs from T-ALL patients were treated with varying concentrations of Spautin-1, resulting in a marked reduction in proliferative capacity (Fig. 8g–h). Collectively, these findings implicate USP10 as a key regulator of thymocyte development and a potential therapeutic target in T-ALL.

Discussion

While several regulators of thymocyte development have been identified, the molecular mechanisms connecting developmental signals, proliferative cues, and oncogenic transformation remain incompletely understood. Here we show that USP10 serves as a crucial regulator orchestrating thymocyte development and T-ALL progression through its dynamic interplay with SOX4 and MYC signaling. The developmental arrest at the DN-to-DP transition upon USP10 ablation highlights its indispensable role in facilitating the proliferative burst required for T cell lineage progression. USP10 functions as a deubiquitinase for SOX4 and maintains SOX4 proteostasis through K48-linked deubiquitination. Pre-TCR–MYC signaling induces Usp10 transcription, forming a feedforward circuit that synchronizes proliferative expansion during early T cell developmental signals. The clinical relevance of this pathway is underscored by the upregulation of both USP10 and SOX4 in T-ALL patients, as well as the therapeutic potential of USP10 inhibition in attenuating MYC-driven leukemogenesis. Collectively, these findings position USP10 as a molecular linchpin integrating developmental signals with oncogenic processes in thymocytes, offering a mechanistic cue for targeting USP10 in T-ALL therapeutics.

Ubiquitination is a fundamental post-translational modification that shapes immune signaling pathways77–79. In T cell biology, DUBs have emerged as critical regulators of thymocyte maturation and TCR signaling80,81. For example, USP7 promotes negative selection-induced apoptosis82, USP8 controls development via the FOXO1–IL7Rα axis83, and BAP1 supports β-selection–driven expansion84. Beyond normal T cell development, DUBs also contribute to leukemogenesis through pathways linked to proliferation, metabolism, and genomic instability85,86. USP7, identified as a T-ALL risk locus by GWAS, is upregulated in leukemic cells and stabilizes NOTCH1, thereby promoting leukemic growth87–89. By contrast, CYLD is downregulated in T-ALL and inhibits NOTCH1-driven transformation; its cleaved form has been shown to reprogram PBMCs toward a T-ALL-like phenotype90,91. The dual roles of USP7 and USP9X as oncogenes or tumor suppressors underscore the context-dependent nature of DUB function in leukemogenesis and highlight the need for selective therapeutic targeting85,92. Despite the growing understanding of these enzymes, direct mechanistic links between dysregulated deubiquitination and malignant transformation remain to be elucidated92,93. To address this, we performed a systematic screen for thymus-enriched DUBs. This targeted strategy identified USP10 as a critical regulator of thymocyte homeostasis and a key driver of leukemic transformation.

Prior to transitioning to the DP stage, thymocytes undergo robust expansion at the DN4, ISP, and DP-blast stages to generate a sufficient cell pool for subsequent selections23. Consistent with this requirement, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that USP10 expression is selectively upregulated during these proliferative windows, aligning with a role in supporting the expansion program that underpins efficient thymocyte development. Although USP10 has been implicated in apoptosis regulation in cancer studies, its deletion had no measurable impact on thymocyte survival94–96. This discrepancy likely reflects the context-dependent nature of USP10 function—a phenomenon commonly observed across diverse biological contexts. In contrast, USP10-deficient thymocytes exhibited a marked reduction in proliferative capacity during the expansion phase. As this stage involves rapid cell-cycle entry, USP10 contributes by stabilizing or enhancing the expression of key cell-cycle regulators. Together, these findings define a context-dependent role for USP10 in sustaining thymocyte proliferation.

Interestingly, although USP10 deletion profoundly compromises αβ T cell proliferation and development, total γδ T cell numbers remain largely unchanged. γδ lineage commitment is regulated by ligand-dependent γδTCR signaling at the DN3–DN4 transition, independently of pre-TCR engagement97, and initiates distinct transcriptional programs compared to the αβ lineage98. Although this checkpoint elicits thymocyte proliferation, the extent of expansion is considerably lower than that in the αβ lineage. Accordingly, γδ T cell development may be less dependent on MYC–USP10–coupled proliferative circuitry. Notably, USP10 deficiency selectively skews γδ T cell differentiation toward the γδT1 lineage, with a concomitant reduction in γδT17 cells, despite unchanged total γδ T cell numbers. Mechanistically, USP10 may calibrate γδTCR signal strength to restrain T-bet activation and IFN-γ polarization99,100, while sustaining Notch–RORγt signaling and mTOR activity to promote γδT17 lineage commitment56,101–104. In parallel, concomitant destabilization of SOX4—an essential regulator of Rorc and Blk—likely contributes to the loss of γδT17 differentiation37,105. Further studies are warranted to delineate the molecular mechanisms that define these critical lineage decisions.

SOX4, a member of the SOXC transcription factor involved in cell cycle regulation and malignant transformation, was identified as a pivotal downstream node of USP10 in thymocyte development. USP10 deficiency reduced SOX4 protein levels by approximately threefold, coinciding with impaired proliferation of DN4, ISP, and DP-blast populations. This phenotype is consistent with prior reports showing that even modest decreases in SOX4 are sufficient to constrain cell-cycle progression44. Furthermore, transgenic overexpression of SOX4 restored thymocyte expansion in USP10-deficient mice both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, enhancing SOX4 stability via mutation of critical lysine residues further potentiated its developmental activity, reinforcing the conclusion that SOX4 is a principal functional target of USP10 in thymocytes. Beyond thymopoiesis, SOX4 is overexpressed in multiple tumors and is implicated in the regulation of apoptosis and proliferation106,107. Notably, SOX4 has been positioned as a downstream effector of MYC and Notch signaling in multiple cancers45,108–111. In thymocytes, pre-TCR signaling induces MYC, which promotes SOX4 transcription, and concurrently upregulates USP10 to stabilize SOX4 at the protein level. This multi-level regulation keeps SOX4 within a physiological range necessary for thymocyte expansion following β-selection, while preventing uncontrolled accumulation associated with malignant transformation. Previous studies have shown that early T and B cell development is markedly impaired in the absence of SOX435,37,39,40. In contrast, in hematologic malignancies, including acute lymphoblastic and myeloid leukemia, SOX4 expression is frequently elevated and drives leukemogenesis in vivo41–43. Nonetheless, SOX4 remains pharmacologically intractable due to its intrinsically disordered structure44,46. Targeting USP10, a post-translational regulator of SOX4, may offer a tractable strategy to modulate SOX4-dependent transcriptional programs in T cell development and malignancy.

USP10 has been reported to regulate Notch1 stability in endothelial cells56, but whether this mechanism operates in thymocytes remains unclear. Here, we show that USP10 does not regulate NOTCH1 or NICD stability in thymocytes; rather, USP10 loss is associated with enhanced Notch pathway activation, suggesting a cell-type-specific mode of regulation. Separately, although Notch signaling has been implicated in MYC regulation during early thymocyte development, pharmacological inhibition of Notch under the conditions examined did not measurably affect USP10 or SOX4 expression, nor did it alter SOX4 ubiquitination in primary thymocytes. These observations align with prior reports showing that Notch activity peaks at the DN2–DN3a stages and declines sharply at the DN4–DP transition, when proliferation becomes predominantly driven by pre-TCR. Consistently, analysis of human thymic single-cell transcriptomes and RT–qPCR of sorted mouse thymocyte subsets revealed that canonical Notch target genes are enriched at the DN2–DN3 stages but downregulated at the DP-blast stage, whereas MYC and USP10 remain relatively highly expressed. Accordingly, the USP10–SOX4 axis functions largely independently of acute Notch signaling at the stages analyzed, defining a distinct post-translational regulatory layer governing thymocyte proliferation. While a role for Notch signaling in regulating USP10 or SOX4 at earlier stages cannot be excluded, these findings underscore a stage- and context-dependent uncoupling of Notch input from MYC–USP10–SOX4 protein homeostasis.

T-ALL represents 15% of pediatric and 25% of adult ALL cases and arises from malignant transformation of immature T cells, primarily driven by constitutive NOTCH1 activation112,113. MYC acts as a key downstream effector of NOTCH1 and a central driver of T-ALL pathogenesis114–116. Additionally, SOX4 has been identified as part of core regulatory circuits implicated in T-ALL oncogenesis117–121, and recent evidence indicates that it cooperates functionally with MYC in the leukemic setting109. Unlike B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), T-ALL has fewer targeted therapies, relies on intensive chemotherapy, and exhibits higher relapse rates, underscoring the urgent need for improved treatments19. USP10 inhibition has been shown to suppress chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cell growth, but its role in T-ALL remains undefined122,123. Here, we identified elevated MYC expression, along with sustained upregulation of USP10 and SOX4 in T-ALL through analysis of public T-ALL datasets and primary patient samples. Using a mouse model that recapitulates MYC-driven leukemogenesis, we found that pharmacological blockade of USP10 partially ameliorates MYC-driven pathological thymocyte expansion and developmental dysregulation30. Notably, USP10 expression is predominantly confined to the proliferative phase of thymocyte development, with only modest levels in early progenitors and mature T cells. While the functional relevance of this residual expression remains to be defined, its stage-specific distribution suggests a potential therapeutic window with limited risk of systemic immunosuppression. These findings suggest that USP10, by stabilizing SOX4, acts as a central node connecting upstream oncogenic cues to downstream effector programs, thereby highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target in T-ALL.

In conclusion, we identified a key pre-TCR-MYC-USP10-SOX4 signaling axis that orchestrates T cell development and T-ALL progression (Fig. 9). This MYC–USP10–SOX4 feed-forward circuit integrates pre-TCR and MYC signaling, coordinating transcriptional and post-translational regulation of cell cycle, and explaining how its dysregulation promotes T-ALL transformation. Importantly, USP10 emerges as a druggable node in T-cell malignancies, and its inhibition may provide a promising T-ALL therapeutic strategy that limits immunosuppressive toxicity while preserving normal thymic output.

Methods

Mice

All experiments used Mus musculus. Usp10fl/fl mice were generated by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Stock. #S-CKO-06526, Guangzhou, China). Lck-Cre (Stock. #B008730), CD45.1 (Stock. #T054816), Rag1−/− (Stock. #T004753), and C57BL/6 J (Stock. #N000295) mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China). OT-II transgenic mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Stock. #004194). MycTg/Tg mice were a gift from Prof. Changchun Xiao (Xiamen University, China)10,30,124. All mice used in this study were on a C57BL/6 J background and maintained under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of Central South University (Changsha, China). Animals were housed in a climate-controlled environment (22–24 °C with 50 ± 10% relative humidity) under a 12-h light/dark cycle. They had ad libitum access to a standard chow diet and water. Mice of both sexes were used, with the same sex assigned per experiment. The age of the animals varied by experimental procedure: mice aged 4–6 weeks were used for AAV transfection experiments; Spautin-1 treatment in leukemic mice commenced at 3–4 weeks of age; and all other experiments were performed using mice aged 6–10 weeks. For animal studies, no randomization or blinding was done, and no sex-specific differences were observed. Experimental and control mice were housed in separate cages within the same SPF room under identical husbandry conditions. Animals were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation, and tissues were collected immediately. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Central South University (approved no. 2021sydw0252).

Cells

The HEK293T (Cat. #ACS-450) and Loucy (Cat. #CRL-2629) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The Plat E cells were kind gifts from Prof. Changchun Xiao (Xiamen University, China). The Jurkat cells were kind gifts from Prof. Zhiguo Xie of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. HEK293T and Plat E cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. Jurkat and Loucy cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) with the same supplements mentioned above. All cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

DUB PCR array analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the thymus and other organs, including liver, heart, lung, spleen, brain, and kidney, of C57BL/6 J mice and reverse-transcribed into cDNA according to standard protocols. Equal amounts of cDNA from non-thymic tissues were pooled to serve as the control group. DUB expression was profiled using a DUB PCR array (Wcgene, #wc-mRNA0360-M). cDNA was dispensed into a PCR plate preloaded with immobilized primer sets. Real-time PCR amplification was performed, and threshold cycle (Ct) values were obtained. Differentially expressed DUBs between the thymus and other organs were identified by subsequent data analysis.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

The thymus, spleen, and lymph nodes were harvested from mice, mechanically dissociated, and sequentially filtered through a 70-μm strainer to generate single-cell suspensions. Flow cytometry was performed following standard surface and intracellular staining protocols. Antibody panels are listed in Supplementary Table 1. For surface marker staining, cells were incubated with fluorescently conjugated antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. For intracellular (IC) or intranuclear staining, cells were stained for surface markers, and subsequently fixed and permeabilized using the Fixation/Permeabilization Buffer Kit (eBioscience) before intracellular staining for cytokines and cytotoxic molecules. For intracellular cytokine detection, cells were pre-stimulated with Cell Stimulation Cocktail (eBioscience) at 37 °C for 4–5 h. Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V and PI staining (BD Biosciences). Samples were acquired on a Fortessa X20 or FACSymphony A3, sorted on a FACSymphony S6 (BD Biosciences), and analyzed with FlowJo v10.8.1. Gating strategies for flow cytometry analyses and cell sorting are shown in Supplementary Fig. 15.

Mixed bone marrow chimeras

Rag1−/− recipient mice were administered drinking water containing ciprofloxacin hydrochloride for 2 weeks prior to the experiment, and then were subjected to whole-body irradiation with a sublethal dose of 5.5 Gy delivered over 5 min using an RS 2000pro-225 irradiator (Rad Source). Lethally irradiated Rag1−/− mice were intravenously transplanted with a 1:1 mixture of 2 × 106 bone marrow (BM) cells from cKO (CD45.2+) or control (CD45.2+) mice, along with BM cells from congenic CD45.1+ wild-type mice. Recipients were analyzed 10 weeks post-reconstitution.

In vitro DN thymocyte differentiation assays

DN thymocytes were purified by negative selection using biotinylated antibodies against CD4, CD8, TCR γ/δ, and NK1.1. The purified cells were resuspended in 500 μl T cell culture medium (α-MEM with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin/ streptomycin, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10 mM HEPES) and incubated with plate-bound DLL4 (Sinobiological) and 100 ng/ml soluble CXCL12 (Novoprotein) as described. After 4 days, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Retroviral transduction

The pMSCV-Sox4WT-IRES-EGFP (Cat. #G53696), pMSCV-Sox4K118R-IRES-EGFP (Cat. #G95640), pMSCV-NICD-IRES-EGFP (Cat. #G90469), and pRV-MMLV-U6-Usp10-shRNA1-EGFP-Puro (Cat. #G73843) plasmids were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). For retrovirus production, the plasmids and the retroviral packaging plasmid (pCL-Eco, MiaoLing Plasmid Platform, Cat. #P0334) were co-transfected into Plat E cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Retrovirus-containing supernatants were collected 48 hours post-transfection and filtered through 0.45 μm filters. Usp10 shRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Purified DN thymocytes were co-cultured with plate-bound DLL4 for 18 h. The pre-stimulated DN cells were then spin-infected with 500–800 μl of viral supernatant, containing polybrene (10 μg/ml) and 10 mM HEPES, at 800 g for 3 h. Following spin-infection, cells were resuspended in T cell culture medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL soluble CXCL12. Flow cytometry analysis was performed 3 days post-transduction.

In vitro cell treatments

To inhibit Notch signaling, primary thymocytes were treated with 0.2 μM DBZ (γ-secretase inhibitor; (S, S)−2-[2-(3,5-difluorophenyl)acetylamino]-N-(5-methyl-6-oxo-6,7-dihydro-5H-dibenzo[b,d]azepin-7-yl)propionamide; Selleck, #S2711). Control cells received equivalent volumes of vehicle. For functional assays, cells were pretreated with DBZ for 1 h, followed by co-stimulation with DLL4 and continued DBZ exposure for 24 h.

To block proteasomal degradation, cells were treated with MG132 (10 μM for 4 h or 5 μM for 12 h; Selleck, #S7888) prior to lysis for immunoprecipitation or immunoblotting.

To inhibit USP10 activity, primary thymocytes and HEK293T cells were exposed to Spautin‑1 (Selleck, #S7888) at the indicated concentrations. For SOX4 degradation assays, cells were treated with 10 μM Spautin‑1 for 6–20 h. For ubiquitination analyses, cells were pretreated with MG132 for 2 h, followed by Spautin‑1 for an additional 2–4 h to preserve ubiquitinated proteins.

Plasmid construction and transfection

The Myc-USP10 (Cat. #P53606), Flag-SOX4 (Cat. #P23890), His-NICD (Cat. #P66624), HA-Ub (Cat. #P69179), HA-Ub-K48 (Cat. #P69194), and HA-Ub-K63 (Cat. #P69177) plasmids were sourced from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). The Myc-USP10-C424A mutant was generated by introducing a cysteine-to-alanine substitution at position 424 in the USP10 catalytic domain via standard site-directed mutagenesis. Flag-tagged SOX4 K76R (Cat. #G87091), K118R (Cat. #G87092), and K127R (Cat. #G87093) mutants were generated by substituting lysine residues at positions 76, 118, and 127 with arginine using standard site-directed mutagenesis (MiaoLing Plasmid Platform, Wuhan, China). Plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences of RNAi are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunoblot, coimmunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays

For immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed in 1% NP40 lysis buffer with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake). Lysates were incubated with primary antibodies and Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck) at 4 °C. Immunocomplexes were washed four times with 500 µl of 1% NP40 lysis buffer. Both lysates and immunoprecipitates were analyzed with the indicated primary antibodies. For in vivo ubiquitination assays, HEK293T cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids, including plasmids encoding HA-Ub, HA-Ub-K48, or HA-Ub-K63. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were lysed in 1% NP40 buffer and processed for immunoprecipitation. Immunoblot analysis was performed by separating cellular lysates on 10% SDS-PAGE, transferring to PVDF membranes, blocking with 5% non-fat milk, and incubating with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used for chemiluminescent detection of target proteins. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of the blots/gels are provided in the Source Data file.

Histology

Mouse thymi were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and processed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining following standard protocols.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Cells were homogenized in TRIzol (Invitrogen), followed by the addition of chloroform (1:5 v/v) and vortexed for 15 s, and incubation at room temperature for 10 minutes. After centrifugation (12,700 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), the aqueous phase was transferred to a fresh tube, mixed with isopropanol (1:1), and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. RNA was precipitated by centrifugation (12,700 × g, 15 minutes, 4 °C), washed twice with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in RNase-free water. cDNA was synthesized using Hifair III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yeasen) and analyzed with a SYBR Green Master Mix (Bimake) on the CFX96 Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression was normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2 − ΔCT method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

RNA sequencing and analysis

DP thymocytes from 8-week-old WT and USP10 cKO mice were sorted and lysed in TRIzol (Invitrogen). RNA quality and quantity were assessed using NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent), with integrity confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Poly(A) RNA was isolated from 1 μg of total RNA using Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25-61005 (Thermo Fisher) and fragmented with the Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB). cDNA synthesis was performed with SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), followed by U-labeled second-strand DNA synthesis using E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB), RNase H (NEB), and dUTP (Thermo Fisher). After adapter ligation and size selection, the cDNA library was amplified by PCR. The final library, with an average insert size of 300 ± 50 bp, was subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 (LC-Bio Technology CO.). Raw reads were processed for quality control, genome alignment, and differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (Bioconductor). Differentially expressed genes were identified with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. GSEA was performed using MsigDB gene sets (https://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/).

4D Label-free Proteomics and data analysis

Frozen cells from the DP population were homogenized in phenol extraction buffer, followed by centrifugation and ammonium acetate-methanol precipitation. The protein pellet was resuspended, incubated, and centrifuged to remove insoluble fractions. Protein concentration was measured using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Proteins were reduced with 5 mM DTT, alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide, and precipitated with acetone. The pellet was digested overnight at 37 °C in buffer (protein: enzyme = 50:1), then lyophilized. For mass spectrometry, peptides were separated on a C18 column using an EASY-nLCTM 1200 system and analyzed on a TimsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker). Ion mobility was set to 0.6–1.6 Vs/cm², and collision energy ranged from 20 to 59 eV. MS/MS spectra were searched using MaxQuant (version 1.6.17.0) against the Uniprot Mus Musculus database, with the Andromeda search engine for LFQ analysis. Search parameters were optimized with false discovery rate (FDR) control to minimize peak misalignment. Data were processed with log2 transformation and median normalization. Differential expression was assessed using Foldchange (log2(Foldchange) = experimental mean - control mean) and P-values derived from a t-test. Differentially expressed proteins were selected with Foldchange ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 1/1.5 and P-value < 0.05. Annotation of all identified proteins was performed using GO (http://www.blast2go.com/b2ghome; http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). DEPs were further used for GO and KEGG enrichment analysis.

Mass spectrometry

Thymocyte proteins were co-immunoprecipitated with anti-USP10 or anti-SOX4 antibodies, fractionated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by Coomassie staining, followed by mass spectrometry analysis. MS raw data were analyzed with Proteome Discoverer 2.5.

Molecular docking

Gene names were used to retrieve protein sequences from UniProtKB, which were input into SWISS-MODEL to obtain the optimal protein structures. The resulting PDB files were prepared by removing water molecules, adding hydrogens, and performing energy minimization. Molecular docking was conducted using the ZDOCK module within Discovery Studio to predict binding modes between USP10 and SOX4. Docking poses were ranked based on the ZDOCK scoring function, and the top 10 poses were analyzed further. Binding interface features, such as hydrogen bonding and solvent-accessible surface areas, were analyzed using PyMOL (v2.6) and UCSF Chimera (v1.19).

Adeno-Associated Virus production and intrathymic AAV gene therapy

Mouse Sox4 (NM_009238.3) was obtained from the Genechem cDNA library (Shanghai, China) using the following primers: forward, 5’-GAACCGTCAGATCCGCTAGCCGCCACCATGGTACAACAGACCAACAAC-3’, and reverse, 5’-TAGTCCATGGTGGCACCGGTGTAGGTGAAGACCAGGTTAGAGATG-3’. The AAV vector plasmid GV825 (CMV-MCS-3Flag-FT2A-GFP-WPRE-BGH polyA) was purchased from Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. The vector and Sox4 (WT or K118R) gene were digested with AgeI and NheI enzymes, followed by In-fusion recombination for cloning. Recombinant vectors were confirmed by DNA sequencing. For viral production, plasmid DNA was transfected into HEK293T cells with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) along with pHelper and pRepCap plasmids. AAV8 was harvested 72 h post-transfection and purified using iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation. The purified virus was concentrated, titered via quantitative PCR, and stored in 0.001% Pluronic F-68 solution (Poloxamer 188).

Intrathymic injections were performed in 2.5- to 3-week-old nonconditioned mice using rAAV vectors (1–3 × 1013 vector genomes/kg)125,126. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a supine position on a warming pad. The chest area was shaved and disinfected. Vectors were loaded into a Hamilton syringe (701 N, 10 μl) fitted with a fine-gauge needle, and delivered by percutaneous intrathymic injection. The needle was oriented from the cranial (head) side at an approximately 30° angle and inserted at the intersection between the sternum and the ribs, then advanced into the thoracic cavity toward the thymic lobes. A volume of 10 μl was slowly injected into each thymic lobe, and the needle was held in place briefly before withdrawal to minimize reflux. Mice were monitored until full recovery from anesthesia. Thymi were harvested 7–14 days after injection for flow cytometric analysis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP was performed using the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #26157) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated with antibodies against MYC, RNA Polymerase II, or normal rabbit IgG (negative control). Enriched DNA was purified and quantified by real-time PCR. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

USP10 enzymatic inhibition assay

USP10 deubiquitinase activity was assessed using a fluorometric Ub–AMC–based assay (BPS Bioscience, #79094). Recombinant human USP10 protein was preincubated with increasing concentrations of Spautin-1, followed by the addition of ubiquitin–AMC substrate. Enzymatic activity was quantified by monitoring AMC fluorescence (excitation: 350 nm; emission: 460 nm), which reflects substrate cleavage.

PBMC preparation and treatment

Peripheral blood samples were collected from T-ALL patients at the Clinical Laboratory of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Age- and sex-matched healthy donors were recruited via advertisements at local preschool education institutions and medical centers. All participants were of Asian ancestry and were recruited from both rural and urban regions in China. Both males and females were included (age range, 0–63 years). For healthy donors, medical history was reviewed to exclude infection, autoimmune disease, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or systemic steroid use. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (approved no. 2022-819). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, with no financial compensation provided.

Blood was drawn into EDTA tubes from fasting participants and processed within 2 h. PBMCs were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using human lymphocyte separation medium (Dakewe), followed by RBC lysis (BD Biosciences) and washes with DPBS. For analyses of human PBMC, biological sex was self-reported by participants according to the original study protocols. Samples were matched for sex where applicable; however, sex was not included as a biological variable for stratified analyses.

For drug treatment assays, PBMCs were seeded onto DLL4-coated plates and treated with graded concentrations of Spautin-1 for 24 h, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

T-lymphocytic leukemia experimental model

MycTg/Tg and MycTg/TgLckCre/+ mice (3–4 weeks old) were randomly allocated to receive either Spautin-1 (20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) or vehicle, administered three times per week for 3 weeks.

Animals were monitored at least daily, and twice daily when clinical signs developed. Because leukemia burden cannot be measured by caliper, humane endpoints were predefined and included sustained weight loss (≥15–20%), marked lethargy or impaired mobility, labored breathing, dehydration, or sustained inability to eat or drink. Animals reaching endpoints were euthanized by isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved protocols.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) cell viability assay

Jurkat or Loucy cells (4 × 104 per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with varying concentrations of Spautin-1 for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent (Selleck) and 90 μl of fresh medium were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Agilent).

Prediction of upstream transcription factors

Upstream transcription factors regulating USP10 were predicted using TF-Target Finder (https://jingle.shinyapps.io/TF_Target_Finder/) and the UCSC Genome Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/), based on previously published datasets127.

The correlation between MYC and USP10 expression was evaluated using GEPIA2 with default settings, based on TCGA and GTEx RNA-seq datasets. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to determine the strength of association.

Analysis of public expression datasets

For target gene expression analyses across thymic subsets, AnnData objects from published human thymus single-cell atlases (https://developmental.cellatlas.io/thymus-development) were reanalyzed using R version 4.1.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria), with original cell-type annotations retained. USP10 expression was visualized across thymocyte subsets and developmental stages following standard normalization workflows. In parallel, expression of target genes in mouse thymocyte subsets was examined using the Human Cell Atlas (https://developmental.cellatlas.io/) and Gene Expression Commons (https://gexc.riken.jp/models/3/genes) platforms. Human tissue-level USP13 protein distribution was analyzed using the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/).

For leukemia expression data analysis, expression profiles of USP10, MYC, and SOX4 were analyzed using the pediatric T-ALL dataset GSE26713, which includes normal bone marrow controls. Processed matrix files were analyzed in R (v4.1.0). To extend the analysis, harmonized RNA-seq data from TCGA, TARGET, and GTEx were retrieved from the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/). Samples from the TARGET-ALL-P2 cohort and matched healthy tissues were selected, and expression values for USP10, MYC, and SOX4 were extracted. All expression values were log₂-transformed prior to analysis. Differential expression between normal and leukemic samples was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Prediction of SOX4 ubiquitination sites

Ubiquitination-prone lysines on SOX4 were prioritized by integrating LC–MS/MS data with predictions from PhosphoSitePlus (https://www.phosphosite.org/homeAction) and GPS-Uber (https://biocuckoo.cn/). K76, K118, and K127 were selected based on overlap between MS-identified residues and high-confidence sites from both databases.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least two independent experiments. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v9; GraphPad Software). Comparisons between groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Survival analyses were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated as appropriate.
