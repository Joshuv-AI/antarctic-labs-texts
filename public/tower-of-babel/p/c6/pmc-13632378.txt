HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review of individual social and structural determinants
Authors: Patrick Mukokoma, Basake Julius Alochere, Lekia Nkpordee, Nicholas Ngomi, Babra Adong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15 to 24 years continue to bear a disproportionate burden of HIV infections in sub–Saharan Africa. Although substantial research has examined behavioral risk factors, evidence on how individual, interpersonal, and structural determinants interact to shape HIV vulnerability remains fragmented. A clearer synthesis of these intersecting determinants is essential for informing integrated HIV prevention strategies and guiding the implementation of emerging biomedical interventions such as long-acting pre-exposure prophylaxis.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Five electronic databases, namely PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and CINAHL, were searched for peer reviewed studies examining determinants of HIV vulnerability among AGYW in sub–Saharan Africa. Eligible studies included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods designs reporting empirical evidence on factors associated with HIV vulnerability. Findings were synthesized using a social ecological framework. Meta analysis was conducted for determinants with comparable quantitative effect estimates.ResultsA total of 49 studies conducted across 18 countries were included. Of these, 12 studies contributed to quantitative synthesis, while the remaining studies contributed to narrative synthesis. Meta analysis showed that age discordant partnerships, transactional sex, and intimate partner violence were significantly associated with increased HIV vulnerability among AGYW, with pooled estimates indicating consistent elevated risk across studies. Across studies not included in meta-analysis, poverty, food insecurity, school dropout, and migration were consistently identified as structural drivers of vulnerability. At the individual level, low perceived susceptibility to HIV and inconsistent condom use were frequently reported risk behaviors, often influenced by gender power imbalance, partner control, and economic dependence.ConclusionHIV vulnerability among AGYW in sub–Saharan Africa is driven by interacting behavioral, relational, and structural determinants. These findings highlight the need for integrated HIV prevention strategies that combine behavioral interventions with structural responses addressing poverty, gender inequality, and access to youth friendly health services. At the policy level, national HIV response frameworks should strengthen cross sector collaboration across health, education, and social protection systems to address the broader conditions that sustain vulnerability. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and implementation studies to better understand causal pathways and evaluate multilevel interventions across diverse contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28416-8.Keywords: HIV vulnerability, Adolescent girls and young women, Sub-Saharan Africa, Social determinants, Structural determinants

Introduction

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) epidemic remains a major global public health challenge, with an estimated 39 million people living with HIV worldwide as of 2024 [1]. Despite advances in antiretroviral therapy and prevention strategies, sub–Saharan Africa continues to carry the greatest burden of the epidemic, HIV related accounting for the majority of global HIV infections and HIV related mortality [1, 2]. Within this region, adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) aged 15 to 24 years remain disproportionately affected. According to recent estimates, AGYW account for approximately 63% of new HIV infections in sub–Saharan Africa, with incidence rates up to three times higher than those of their male counterparts [1, 3]. This persistent gender disparity reflects not only biological susceptibility but also deeply rooted social and structural inequalities.

In many sub-Saharan African settings, including Uganda, AGYW face a wide range of interconnected risk factors that increase their likelihood of acquiring HIV. Epidemiological evidence consistently shows higher HIV prevalence and incidence among young women compared with young men of the same age group [4, 5]. Key drivers include age discordant relationships, transactional sex, gender-based violence, and limited access to sexual and reproductive health services [6, 7]. Although biomedical interventions such as oral pre-exposure prophylaxis have demonstrated strong efficacy in preventing HIV, uptake and adherence among AGYW remain suboptimal due to social, economic, and health system barriers [8]. These patterns suggest that vulnerability is shaped by more than individual behavior alone.

HIV vulnerability is therefore better understood as a multidimensional construct that reflects the interaction of factors operating across different levels of influence. In this review, HIV vulnerability is operationalized as an increased likelihood of HIV acquisition measured through incidence, prevalence, or validated proxy indicators such as high-risk sexual behavior. Conceptually, this vulnerability arises from the combined effects of individual level factors such as early sexual debut and condom nonuse, interpersonal dynamics such as partner control and peer influence, and structural conditions including poverty, limited education, and restricted access to prevention services [9, 10]. This perspective is grounded in the social ecological framework, which emphasizes that health outcomes are shaped by layered and interacting determinants rather than isolated individual choices.

At the individual level, studies have identified behavioral and psychological factors such as multiple sexual partnerships, substance use, and low perceived risk as important drivers of HIV acquisition among AGYW [2, 7]. However, these behaviors often occur within broader relational contexts. Interpersonal factors, including age discordant partnerships and transactional relationships, are consistently associated with increased HIV risk due to unequal power dynamics that limit young women’s ability to negotiate safer sexual practices [3, 6]. Exposure to intimate partner violence further compounds this vulnerability by reducing autonomy and increasing both biological and behavioral susceptibility to infection [5, 11].

Beyond individual and relational influences, structural determinants play a central role in shaping HIV vulnerability. Poverty, food insecurity, and limited educational opportunities are widely documented as underlying drivers that constrain life choices and increase reliance on high-risk coping strategies such as transactional sex [6]. Educational attainment, in contrast, has been shown to provide protective benefits by delaying sexual initiation, improving health literacy, and enhancing access to prevention services [12]. At the same time, stigma, weak health systems, and limited availability of youth friendly services continue to restrict access to effective prevention interventions, including pre-exposure prophylaxis [8, 9, 13].

Although a substantial body of research exists on HIV risk among adolescent girls and young women in sub Saharan Africa, the evidence base remains consistently fragmented across thematic and analytical domains. This fragmentation is evident in three main patterns. First, most studies are concentrated at the individual level, focusing on behavioral factors such as condom use, number of sexual partners, and risk perception, while fewer studies integrate interpersonal determinants such as partner power dynamics and intimate partner violence, and even fewer examine structural drivers such as poverty, education, and health system access. Across the included studies in this review, approximately 78% examined individual level factors, about 65% addressed interpersonal determinants, while only around 55% explicitly considered structural determinants, indicating uneven thematic coverage. Second, this fragmentation is reflected in the tendency of earlier and even recent systematic reviews to isolate specific domains such as transactional sex, gender based violence, or economic vulnerability without synthesizing their interconnections within a unified ecological framework. Third, this pattern has remained relatively stable over time, with studies from the early 2000s through the 2020s continuing to emphasize behavioral determinants despite increasing recognition of structural and biomedical prevention approaches such as pre exposure prophylaxis and long acting prevention technologies. This persistence suggests that fragmentation is not only disciplinary but also temporal, with limited progression toward fully integrated multilevel analyses. As a result, important linkages between behavioral, relational, and structural determinants remain underexplored, limiting the development of comprehensive HIV prevention strategies that reflect the complexity of vulnerability in this population [8, 14].

Given the complex and interconnected nature of HIV vulnerability, there is a clear need for a comprehensive synthesis that integrates evidence across multiple levels of influence within a unified framework. Applying a social ecological perspective allows for a more systematic examination of how individual behaviors are shaped by social relationships and structural conditions, and how these layers interact to produce differential risk among AGYW. Such an approach is essential for identifying consistent patterns across settings and for informing the design of more effective and context responsive HIV prevention strategies.

This systematic review therefore aims to synthesize empirical evidence on the determinants of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub–Saharan Africa using a multilevel ecological framework. Specifically, the review seeks to identify individual level determinants, examine interpersonal and relational drivers, and assess structural factors that contribute to HIV vulnerability. By integrating findings across these levels, the study provides a more coherent and policy relevant understanding of HIV risk among AGYW and supports the development of comprehensive prevention strategies that extend beyond individual behavior change.

Methods and materials

Study design and reporting framework

This review is a systematic review of empirical studies examining factors associated with HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years in sub Saharan Africa. The article was designed and reported in line with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Every stage of the review process, from literature searching to study selection, followed the PRISMA 2020 checklist. A PRISMA flow diagram describing the study selection process is presented in the Results section.

Protocol registration

Before commencing the literature search and review process, the authors developed a detailed review protocol to guide the conduct of the study and to minimize selection and reporting bias while enhancing transparency and reproducibility. The protocol specified the review background, research question, eligibility criteria, search strategy, study selection procedures, data extraction plan, and synthesis approach.

Although the protocol was developed in line with the methodological expectations of systematic reviews and was guided by established systematic review principles consistent with the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews, it was not formally registered in PROSPERO. This was due to time constraints during the initial phase of the study and the parallel progression of the review process alongside protocol development. However, all procedures outlined in the protocol were strictly adhered to throughout the conduct of the review to ensure methodological rigor and consistency in reporting.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility for inclusion in this review was defined using the Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome and Study design framework to ensure consistency and transparency in study selection. Only studies conducted in countries classified within sub–Saharan Africa were included in the review.

Inclusion criteria comprised empirical studies that focused on adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years, including studies reporting disaggregated findings for those aged 15 to 17 years where available. Studies were required to examine HIV vulnerability and its determinants, particularly individual, interpersonal, social, and structural factors associated with HIV risk or infection. All quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods designs reporting primary data from sub-Saharan Africa were included.

Exclusion criteria included studies that did not focus on AGYW within the specified age range or did not report separable data for this group, studies without any focus on HIV vulnerability or related determinants, purely theoretical or opinion based papers, conference abstracts without full texts, and studies conducted outside sub Saharan Africa. Non peer reviewed publications were also excluded.

Population

Studies were eligible if they included adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years. Where studies included broader age categories, they were retained only if results specific to the 15 to 24 year age group were reported separately or could be clearly extracted from the dataset. Only studies conducted within sub Saharan Africa were included to ensure alignment with the geographical scope of the review.

Exposure

Eligible studies examined determinants of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women. These determinants were conceptualized as any individual, interpersonal, or structural factor that increases the likelihood of exposure to HIV infection or reduces the ability to adopt effective prevention strategies. These included biological susceptibility, behavioral risk practices, psychosocial influences, gender power relations, economic disadvantage, and exposure to violence, educational attainment, health system barriers, and broader structural or policy level conditions.

Comparator

Because this review focused on determinants of HIV vulnerability rather than intervention effectiveness, a formal comparator was not mandatory for study inclusion. However, where available, studies compared AGYW exposed to specific vulnerability determinants with AGYW who were unexposed or less exposed to those determinants. Examples included comparisons by exposure status for transactional sex, age discordant partnerships, poverty status, educational attainment, or experience of intimate partner violence.

Outcome

For the purpose of this review, HIV vulnerability was operationally defined as an increased likelihood of HIV acquisition or exposure, measured through either direct biological outcomes or validated proxy indicators.

Biological outcomes included HIV incidence and HIV prevalence confirmed through laboratory testing or clinical diagnosis.

Surrogate or proxy indicators of vulnerability were also included where they were explicitly linked to HIV risk in the primary studies. These included condom nonuse, inconsistent condom use, transactional sex, multiple sexual partnerships, age disparate or age mixing relationships, early sexual debut, substance use during sexual activity, and exposure to intimate partner violence. These proxies were accepted because they are widely established in epidemiological literature as intermediate behavioral and social predictors of HIV infection risk.

Studies were included only when the outcome or proxy indicator could be clearly interpreted in relation to HIV vulnerability within the study context.

Study setting

Information on study setting was extracted where reported and categorized as urban, rural, peri urban, or mixed settings. Setting characteristics were considered during interpretation because contextual differences may influence HIV vulnerability patterns and determinant profiles.

Study design

All empirical study designs were considered. Quantitative observational studies including cross sectional, case control, and cohort designs were eligible. Qualitative studies that explored lived experiences or perceived drivers of HIV vulnerability were included to enrich contextual interpretation. Mixed methods studies were included where extractable empirical findings were available. Intervention studies were included only when baseline data on determinants of HIV vulnerability were reported prior to implementation of the intervention. Randomized controlled trials were not a primary focus of this review because the objective was to synthesize determinants of HIV vulnerability across real world settings rather than evaluate intervention efficacy. However, where RCTs reported relevant baseline or observational determinant data, these were considered for inclusion. Study setting was also extracted and classified as urban, rural, peri urban, or mixed, since contextual differences may influence patterns and magnitude of HIV vulnerability.

Language and publication status

The review included only peer reviewed journal articles published in English. Conference abstracts without full text, editorials, commentaries, opinion papers, and purely theoretical publications were excluded. The restriction to English language publications may introduce language bias, particularly by excluding studies published in French and Portuguese within sub Saharan Africa. To minimize its potential impact on the findings, the search was conducted across multiple international databases with broad regional coverage, and key findings from included studies were synthesized thematically and quantitatively using consistent inclusion criteria. The direction and magnitude of reported associations were also compared across study designs and settings to ensure robustness of the overall conclusions.

Study selection process

The search results were exported into a reference management tool to eliminate any duplicate studies. The process of selecting studies occurred through two stages. For all studies, the title and abstract were skimmed to ensure they met the inclusion criteria. For studies that were deemed potentially relevant to the research question, the full text of the studies was read.

Two researchers were responsible for selecting studies based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Where necessary, these two researchers discussed any discrepancies to come to a consensus. The number of studies included was shown using a PRISMA flowchart.

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of all included studies was assessed using standardized critical appraisal tools appropriate to each study design. Quantitative studies were evaluated using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale, while qualitative studies were assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist.

For quantitative studies, the Newcastle Ottawa Scale examined three main domains. These included selection of study groups, comparability of groups, and ascertainment of exposure or outcome. Each study was awarded a score based on predefined criteria within these domains, reflecting the overall methodological rigor of the study. The total score was used to classify studies into quality categories. Studies scoring 7 to 9 were classified as high quality, those scoring 4 to 6 were classified as moderate quality, and those scoring below 4 were classified as low quality.

For qualitative studies, the CASP checklist was applied to assess methodological rigor and transparency. This included evaluation of the clarity of study aims, appropriateness of the qualitative methodology, adequacy of participant recruitment strategies, rigor of data collection processes, consideration of reflexivity, ethical considerations, robustness of data analysis, and the overall contribution and value of the study to existing knowledge. All included studies were independently appraised by two reviewers to minimize subjective bias. Any discrepancies in quality ratings between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data synthesis

Given the expected heterogeneity in study designs, measurement of vulnerability, and contextual factors, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted as the primary method of data integration. Findings were organized using a social ecological framework to allow structured comparison across levels of influence. Determinants were grouped into three broad domains:

Individual level factors: These included age at sexual debut, condom use, number of sexual partners, substance use, HIV knowledge, risk perception, and biological susceptibility.

Quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis

Meta analysis was conducted only where sufficient similarity existed among studies in terms of study design, definition of exposures, and measurement of outcomes. Statistical pooling was performed when at least three studies reported comparable effect estimates on the same determinant and outcome.

Where feasible, that is, where at least three studies had comparable data on adjusted effects such as odds ratios, meta-analysis was conducted. We used a random effects model to account for variability among studies. We tested for statistical heterogeneity using I-squared and Cochran’s Q-test. If I-squared was found to be above 50%, then substantial statistical heterogeneity was said to exist. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding studies that were deemed to be of poor quality.

Ethical considerations

Since this study relied on published studies, no ethical clearance was necessary. However, all studies included in this study were assessed to ensure that they had been ethically cleared and that consent had been sought.

Reporting transparency

The completed review included a PRISMA flow diagram, quality appraisal tables, and detailed characteristics of the included studies. Supplementary materials contained full search strategies and additional analytical outputs.

This structured and transparent approach ensures methodological rigor and strengthens the reliability of conclusions regarding the individual, interpersonal, and structural determinants of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub–Saharan Africa.

Results

Study selection

A total of 3,842 records were identified through electronic database searching. An additional 36 records were identified through manual searching of reference lists of relevant reviews and primary studies. This yielded 3,878 records in total. After removal of 1,124 duplicates, 2,754 unique records remained for title and abstract screening.

At the screening stage, 2,601 records were excluded due to irrelevance to the study population, geographical scope, or absence of focus on HIV vulnerability determinants among adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years in sub–Saharan Africa.

A total of 153 full text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 104 were excluded for the following reasons:

Characteristics and outcome levels of included studies

The 49 included studies were conducted across 18 countries in sub Saharan Africa, with South Africa, Uganda, Kenya, and Tanzania being the most represented settings. Study designs included 34 quantitative observational studies, 9 qualitative studies, and 6 mixed methods studies. Sample sizes ranged from 182 to 12,674 participants. The dominance of a few countries may have influenced the findings by weighting the evidence toward settings with more extensive HIV research infrastructure and higher publication output. However, the consistency of key determinants across both highly represented and less represented countries suggests that the observed patterns are not solely driven by geographical concentration.

Thematic synthesis of determinants using ecological framework

Findings are organized strictly into three ecological levels.

Individual level determinants

Thirty-eight studies reported individual level determinants.

The most frequently reported factors were early sexual debut, inconsistent condom use, multiple concurrent partnerships, substance use, and low HIV risk perception.

Age discordance and quantitative evidence

Five studies provided comparable adjusted estimates for age discordant relationships. Meta analysis showed that AGYW involved in relationships with partners at least five years older had significantly higher odds of HIV vulnerability.

Pooled effect estimate

Age discordant relationships were associated with increased HIV vulnerability (pooled OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.21, based on 5 studies), with moderate heterogeneity. Across all included studies, 31 out of 49 studies reported age mixing as a significant risk factor.

Condom use and behavioral risk

Inconsistent condom use was reported in 22 studies and was consistently associated with higher HIV vulnerability across all regions.

Educational attainment

Eight studies reported protective effects of secondary education completion. Lower education was associated with increased vulnerability in all studies reporting this outcome.

Overall evidence strength

Evidence at individual level was strong, supported by multiple cohort studies and consistent cross-sectional findings.

Interpersonal and social determinants

Thirty-two studies examined interpersonal and social determinants.

Transactional sex

Transactional sex was one of the most consistent determinants. Across 10 studies included in meta-analysis, transactional sex was associated with increased HIV vulnerability.

Pooled estimate

Transactional sex increased HIV vulnerability (pooled OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.60 to 2.78) Across the full dataset, 28 out of 49 studies identified transactional sex as a significant determinant.

Intimate partner violence

Twelve studies reported statistically significant associations between IPV and HIV vulnerability.

Pooled estimate

Exposure to IPV was associated with increased HIV vulnerability (pooled OR 1.92, 95% CI 1.45 to 2.54).

Gender power imbalance and peer norms

Seventeen studies reported that gender power imbalance reduced condom negotiation capacity. Peer norms reinforcing age mixing were reported in 14 studies.

Overall evidence strength

Evidence at this level was moderate to strong, supported by consistent qualitative and quantitative convergence.

Structural and policy level determinants

Twenty-seven studies addressed structural determinants.

Poverty and economic vulnerability

Poverty was reported in 21 studies and was consistently linked to increased HIV vulnerability.

Food insecurity

Food insecurity was reported in 11 studies, with strong qualitative evidence linking it to transactional relationships.

Education and structural protection

School attendance was consistently protective across 15 studies, reducing early sexual debut and risky partnerships.

Migration and urban residence

Migration or informal settlement residence was reported in 13 studies and associated with increased vulnerability.

Service access constraints

Only 4 studies explicitly assessed health system or policy environment factors, indicating a major evidence gap.

Overall evidence strength

Evidence at structural level was moderate due to limited longitudinal studies and reliance on proxy measures.

Integrated ecological interpretation

Across all studies, HIV vulnerability among AGYW emerged as a layered phenomenon shaped by interactions between individual behavior, relational power, and structural inequality.

Economic dependence frequently mediated the relationship between structural deprivation and transactional sex. Similarly, age discordant relationships amplified risk where gender inequality constrained negotiation power.

Protective factors such as school retention and supportive social environments reduced vulnerability across multiple contexts.

Only a minority of studies were longitudinal, limiting causal inference, but consistency across methods strengthens overall confidence.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 49 studies conducted across 18 countries in sub–Saharan Africa to examine determinants of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women aged 15 to 24 years. The evidence shows that vulnerability is not driven by a single pathway but emerges from interacting influences operating at multiple levels, with clear implications for interpretation, mechanism, and policy response. Variation in urban and rural contexts appeared to influence vulnerability patterns, particularly with respect to migration, transactional relationships, and access to prevention services.

At the individual level, early sexual debut, inconsistent condom use, multiple concurrent partnerships, substance use, and low perceived HIV risk were consistently associated with increased vulnerability. Age discordant partnerships emerged as one of the most robust predictors of HIV acquisition, with pooled estimates indicating significantly elevated risk. These findings suggest that individual behaviors are shaped not only by knowledge gaps but also by constrained agency and limited ability to translate awareness into protective action, particularly in contexts where gender and economic dependence are present.

At the interpersonal level, transactional sex, gender power imbalance, and exposure to intimate partner violence were strongly linked to HIV vulnerability. These relational dynamics often operate through power asymmetry within partnerships, which reduces negotiation capacity for safer sexual practices and increases sustained exposure to risk. The consistency of these associations across studies suggests that vulnerability is reinforced within relational environments where economic dependence and unequal decision making are persistent.

At the structural level, poverty, unemployment, food insecurity, limited educational attainment, and migration were recurrent determinants. These factors often act as upstream drivers that shape both the formation of high-risk relationships and the ability of young women to avoid them. Although structural determinants were less frequently examined using longitudinal designs, the convergence of evidence indicates that economic deprivation and educational disadvantage amplify both behavioral and relational risks. Overall, the synthesis demonstrates that HIV vulnerability among AGYW is cumulative and context dependent rather than the result of isolated individual choices.

The interpretation of these findings is consistent with earlier empirical studies that identify age discordant relationships and transactional sexual partnerships as major drivers of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub–Saharan Africa. Evidence from epidemiological research in Uganda shows that young women involved in relationships with older male partners face significantly higher risk of HIV acquisition due to unequal power dynamics and limited ability to negotiate safer sexual practices [3]. Similarly, research based on the Uganda Violence Against Children Survey reported that experiences of transactional sex during adolescence are strongly associated with risky sexual behaviors and adverse mental health outcomes among young women [5]. These patterns support earlier arguments that economic vulnerability and gender inequality continue to shape sexual decision making among adolescent girls and young women in the region [6].

The association between intimate partner violence and HIV vulnerability observed in this review also aligns with previous studies highlighting the influence of gender-based violence on HIV risk. Evidence indicates that exposure to violence within relationships can reduce young women’s ability to negotiate condom use and increases their biological and behavioral susceptibility to HIV infection [5, 11]. Such findings reinforce the broader literature suggesting that patriarchal social structures and gender power imbalances remain central drivers of HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub–Saharan Africa [12].

At the structural level, the role of poverty and food insecurity identified in this review supports earlier research showing that economic hardship can push young women toward transactional sexual relationships as a means of coping with financial constraints. Studies examining HIV vulnerability in Uganda have documented how limited economic opportunities, school dropout, and household poverty contribute to increased exposure to high-risk sexual partnerships [6, 7]. Similarly, broader regional analyses have identified structural barriers such as poverty, limited access to prevention services, and social inequality as key factors influencing both HIV vulnerability and access to prevention technologies among young women [9, 13]. These findings also align with global HIV reports which indicate that adolescent girls and young women continue to account for a disproportionate share of new infections in sub–Saharan Africa, largely due to intersecting social and economic inequalities [1, 4]. Taken together, the consistency of evidence across multiple studies highlights the need for comprehensive HIV prevention strategies that address behavioral risk factors while simultaneously targeting structural determinants such as poverty, gender inequality, and limited access to youth responsive health services.

Theoretical interpretation of the findings supports the usefulness of a social ecological framework in explaining HIV vulnerability among AGYW. Risk behaviors were rarely independent at the individual level but were consistently embedded within relational and structural contexts that shape exposure and response. The evidence further aligns with cognitive and behavioral theories in HIV prevention, particularly in relation to low perceived susceptibility, which was repeatedly documented across studies. This mismatch between actual and perceived risk has important implications for behavior change interventions and prevention communication strategies. More importantly, the findings highlight the limitation of approaches that focus only on individual behavior change, since such models do not fully capture the influence of gender relations, poverty, and structural inequality on decision making and risk exposure.

From a policy perspective, the findings suggest that effective HIV prevention strategies in sub–Saharan Africa must extend beyond individual level messaging on condom use or abstinence. Interventions need to simultaneously address gender power imbalance and economic vulnerability. Strengthening secondary school retention emerges as a key structural intervention, given the consistent protective role of educational attainment across studies. Programs addressing intimate partner violence should be fully integrated into HIV prevention frameworks, as violence functions as both a direct and indirect driver of infection risk. In addition, social protection strategies targeting poverty and food insecurity may reduce reliance on transactional relationships and improve long term prevention outcomes. These approaches together point to the importance of combining behavioral, social, and economic interventions rather than implementing isolated program components.

Implications for national HIV prevention strategies further reinforce the need for integrated programming. Frameworks such as DREAMS illustrate the value of combining biomedical, behavioral, and structural interventions for adolescent girls and young women. Similarly, ongoing PrEP scale up efforts highlight the importance of ensuring that biomedical prevention is supported by demand generation, risk perception correction, and improved service accessibility. However, the findings of this review indicate that without addressing underlying poverty, gender inequality, and social vulnerability, the impact of PrEP and other biomedical interventions may remain limited. Social protection initiatives, including cash transfer programs and education support schemes, therefore represent important complementary strategies within national HIV response plans. Overall, the multidimensional nature of HIV vulnerability identified in this review suggests that sustainable prevention outcomes depend on coordinated action across health, education, and social welfare systems.

The findings further suggest that effective HIV prevention among AGYW requires intervention packages that operate simultaneously across ecological levels rather than isolated program approaches. At the individual level, interventions should strengthen HIV literacy, improve risk perception, and expand access to biomedical prevention options including PrEP. At the interpersonal level, programs should address gender power relations through partner engagement, violence prevention, and social norm transformation. Structural interventions should focus on poverty reduction, school retention, economic empowerment, and expansion of youth responsive services. Frameworks that integrate these dimensions may achieve stronger and more sustainable reductions in HIV vulnerability than interventions targeting behavior alone.

A potential ecological prevention model emerging from this review would combine school retention initiatives, conditional cash transfers, youth friendly sexual health services, violence prevention programs, and biomedical prevention delivery within coordinated implementation platforms. Such approaches acknowledge that HIV vulnerability is produced through interacting systems rather than independent risk factors.

Strengths, limitations, and conclusions

This review has several methodological and analytical strengths. It synthesized evidence from 49 studies conducted across 18 countries in sub–Saharan Africa, providing a broad regional perspective on HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women. The review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and used a systematic search across multiple databases, which improves transparency and reproducibility. Study quality was assessed using established tools, including the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and CASP checklist, allowing for structured appraisal of methodological rigor. In addition, the integration of quantitative and qualitative evidence enabled a more comprehensive understanding of how individual, interpersonal, and structural determinants interact to shape HIV vulnerability.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the possibility of publication bias cannot be fully ruled out. Although funnel plot assessment for key pooled estimates such as age discordant relationships did not show strong asymmetry, the relatively small number of studies included in meta-analysis limits the strength of this assessment. Second, the review was restricted to English language publications, which may have excluded relevant evidence from French and Portuguese speaking countries, particularly in West and Central Africa. Third, substantial variability was observed in how key concepts such as transactional sex and HIV vulnerability were defined and measured across studies. This heterogeneity limited direct comparability and may have influenced the precision of pooled estimates. Fourth, most included studies were cross sectional in design, which limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between determinants and HIV outcomes. Finally, determinants at the policy and health system level were underrepresented, indicating an important gap in the current evidence base.

In conclusion, HIV vulnerability among adolescent girls and young women in sub Saharan Africa emerges through interacting behavioral, interpersonal, and structural determinants rather than isolated risk behaviors. Strong evidence supports age discordant partnerships, transactional sex, intimate partner violence, and poverty as major drivers of vulnerability. These findings support integrated prevention approaches that combine behavioral interventions with structural and social responses. However, interpretation should consider heterogeneity across studies and the predominance of observational evidence. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and policy focused studies to strengthen causal understanding and support scalable prevention strategies.

Summary of major findings

This review synthesized evidence from 49 studies across 18 countries and demonstrated that HIV vulnerability among AGYW is multidimensional and socially patterned. Individual factors including early sexual debut, condom nonuse, substance use, and low perceived HIV risk contributed to vulnerability but rarely operated independently.

Interpersonal determinants including transactional sex, age discordant relationships, and intimate partner violence consistently increased vulnerability, while structural conditions such as poverty, educational disruption, food insecurity, and limited service access amplified risk. The findings suggest that sustainable HIV prevention requires integrated responses across ecological levels.
