Cardiovascular Risk Factor Control Among Young Adults With Diabetes in the United States Between 2013 and 2023
Authors: Alexander R Zheutlin, Joshua A Jacobs, Alexander Chaitoff
Published: 2026

Young adults with diabetes face a disproportionate cardiovascular disease burden as those diagnosed before age 40 years carry more than twice the excess cardiovascular disease mortality risk compared with those diagnosed later in life. The number of young adults with diagnosed with diabetes now represents nearly 5% to 10% of US adults with diabetes and has risen annually for 2 decades, making cardiovascular risk factor control a defining priority for this population. 1 , 2 , 3 We examined secular trends in cardiovascular disease risk factor control for young adults with diabetes (type 1 or type 2) between 2013 and 2023 to characterize this cardiovascular care gap.

All data used are publicly available via the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. We pooled the 2013 to 2023 cross‐sectional cycles of the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), a publicly‐available multistage probability survey to estimate accurate reflections of the noninstitutionalized US population. All participants provided written informed consent with all cycles approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board. We included adults 20 to 40 years with diabetes (self‐report, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5, fasting glucose ≥126, taking insulin or medication to lower blood sugar). We assessed secular trends for change in systolic blood pressure (BP), diastolic BP, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, body mass index, hemoglobin A1c, and smoking status as well as optimal risk factor levels using survey‐weighted linear regression accounting for the complex sampling design. All analysis was performed using complete case analysis with separate samples created for fasting laboratory tests and nonfasting risk factors (R v2024.04.2).

Between 2013 and 2023, a total of 3.6 million adults were included (unweighted n=432; Table). The mean age was 30.7 years and 48.6% female. Self‐identified race or ethnicity was 45.0%, 16.9%, 6.4%, and 24.8% non‐Hispanic White race, non‐Hispanic Black race, non‐Hispanic Asian race, or Mexican or Hispanic ethnicity, respectively.

Over the 10‐year period from 2013 to 2023, there was a nonsignificant increase in mean systolic BP (3.0 mm Hg) and significant increase in diastolic BP (12.0 mm Hg; P<0.01). There was a nonsignificant decrease in low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (−9.6 mg/dL), triglycerides (−41.2 mg/dL), and body mass index (2.9 kg/m2). Hemoglobin A1c increased 0.8% over the 10‐year period (P=0.04; Table1).

In 2013 to 2014, 92.0% of young adults with diabetes had a BP <140/90 mm Hg; however, only 72.3% did by 2021 to 2023 (P=0.03; Table). There was a significant decrease in the proportion of young adults with controlled BP <130/80 mm Hg during the same time period (71.6% to 46.0%; P=0.05). There was no statistically significant overall trend in optimal low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (<100 mg/dL or <70 mg/dL), triglycerides, body mass index (<25 kg/m2 or <30 kg/m2), or smoking (Table).

In this nationally representative study, cardiovascular risk factor control among young adults with diabetes was consistently inadequate from 2013 to 2023. The decline in BP goal achievement overall, and the increase in diastolic BP, which may reflect vascular and metabolic dysregulation, is particularly concerning in this young population, with decades of life expectancy. Even modestly elevated BP over decades substantially increases lifetime cardiovascular risk and reflects gaps in care delivery for young adults with diabetes.

The economic burden of cardiovascular disease is anticipated to increase nearly 4‐fold by 2050, alongside a projected 311% rise in diabetes care costs and a 600% increase in new diagnoses among adolescents and young adults by 2060. 2 , 4 This increase is likely to be driven by adults like those in the current study—those diagnosed in young adulthood with decades of undertreated cardiovascular risk. Strategies to improve BP control in this population should address medication costs, fragmented transitions from pediatric to adult care, improved clinician awareness of rising prevalence, and increased leverage of mobile‐health or team‐based care protocols tailored for age‐specific competing life demands. Further risk‐modification in young women requires deliberation surrounding pregnancy, lactation, or family planning periods. Although the American Diabetes Association does not provide specific thresholds for young adults, ideal values include an A1c <6.5%, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <100 mg/dL, and BP <120/80 mm Hg. 5 Given the growing burden of diabetes among young adults who have decades of unrealized health quality, intervening on modifiable cardiovascular risk factors in this population offers the greatest opportunity to bend these cost and mortality curves.

There are notable limitations. The cross‐sectional design limits causal inference about secular trends in risk factor control and much of the data relies on self‐report. We cannot discern those with type 1 or type 2 diabetes or those with gestational diabetes. Moreover, given the age studied, the sample size of young adults with diabetes in each cycle was modest, limiting power to detect small changes. Despite modest per‐cycle unweighted sample sizes, survey weighting yielded nationally representative population estimates exceeding 600 000 annually, consistent with prior NHANES‐based trend analyses in high‐risk subgroups. Although many of the changes were not statistically significant, this may reflect uptake of newer classes of diabetic medication which impact parameters beyond blood glucose.

Cardiovascular risk factor control among young adults with diabetes remained inadequate and, in some instances, worsened from 2013 to 2023. Concrete policy interventions are urgently needed, including expanded Medicaid coverage for diabetes‐specific therapies such continuous glucose monitors and GLP‐1 (glucagon‐like peptide‐1) agonists, mandatory integration of cardiovascular risk screening into routine diabetes care, and adherence programs designed for young adults who face distinct structural barriers including insurance gaps and competing work and family demands. Given the duration‐dependent relationship between risk factor exposure and atherosclerotic progression, inadequate control during early adulthood portends disproportionate long‐term cardiovascular risk necessitating early targeted action.
