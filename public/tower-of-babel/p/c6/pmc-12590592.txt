Assessment of the relation between orthorexia nervosa and self-esteem in high school students
Authors: Rukiye Kural Atak, Huseyin Elbi, Fatih Ozcan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthorexia Nervosa is a condition characterized by an obsession with healthy eating that can negatively impact an individual’s psychosocial functioning. The risk of developing orthorexia nervosa increases during adolescence due to environmental influences like social media and cultural norms. It is indicated that low self-esteem is linked to orthorexia nervosa, causing individuals to potentially use their fixation on healthy eating to seek social acceptance. This study aims to explore the connection between orthorexia nervosa and self-esteem among high school students.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, the Ortho Scale and the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale were used to assess the relationship between Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies and high school students’ self-esteem. The sample was selected through stratified random sampling. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and multiple linear regression analysis were applied to analyze the data. The effects of variables such as gender, age, body mass index, diet history, physical activity habits, and attitudes towards organic nutrition on Orthorexia Nervosa were assessed using multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe study’s findings revealed a significant relationship between tendencies toward Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem. The overall Ortho Scale score was lower among overweight individuals, upper-class students, those dissatisfied with their weight, individuals who had previously dieted, regular exercisers, those who believed in the benefits of organic nutrition, and those with low self-esteem. A significantly small positive correlation was observed between the total score of the Ortho Scale and the total score of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale.ConclusionsThis study showed a strong link between orthorexia nervosa tendencies and self-esteem among high school students. The results suggest that low self-esteem increases the risk of orthorexia nervosa. Additionally, factors such as dieting, dissatisfaction with weight, regular exercise, and beliefs in organic nutrition are associated with tendencies toward orthorexia nervosa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-025-01433-5.Keywords: Orthorexia nervosa, Self-esteem, Adolescence, Students

Background

Eating disorders are the third most common chronic illness among adolescents after obesity and asthma [1]. These disorders are defined by significant impairments in eating behaviors and body weight, often resulting in psychiatric and physical complications [1, 2]. The typical age of onset for eating disorders ranges from 14 to 19 years, and epidemiological studies indicate that these disorders are more widespread than previously assumed, with a considerable proportion remaining undiagnosed [2]. These disorders, which start in adolescence, usually continue into adulthood and negatively affect the welfare of individuals [3].

One of the eating disorders that has attracted attention in recent years is orthorexia nervosa. Orthorexia Nervosa is characterized by an individual’s excessive focus on healthy eating and strict adherence to strict dietary rules, and this situation negatively affects daily life functions [4]. Although it is not included as an official diagnostic category in the DSM-5, Orthorexia Nervosa is receiving increasing attention, and researchers emphasize that this condition can lead to psychological, social, and nutritional problems [5, 6].

In the literature, it is noted that the prevalence of eating disorders in adolescents ranges from 5.7% to 15.2% in girls and from 2.9% to 12% in boys [7]. The prevalence of eating disorders among adolescents in Turkiye varies from 2.33% to 18.3% [8, 9]. Various studies on the prevalence of Orthorexia Nervosa yield conflicting data; for instance, Donini et al. reported it as 6.9% [10], while Abdullah et al. found this rate to be 72.0% among nutrition students and nutritionists [11]. In a study by Arusoğlu et al., this rate was 26.1% among university students in Türkiye [12]. The variability in findings regarding the prevalence of Orthorexia Nervosa results from methodological differences, such as varying measurement tools, diverse sample groups, and a lack of standardized diagnostic criteria. For example, recent studies in Türkiye indicate that Orthorexia Nervosa symptoms are more prevalent among adolescents and university students (53.2%) and that this condition significantly affects eating habits and psychological well-being. Research on adolescents in Turkiye suggests that healthy eating trends in young people’s social environments and body perceptions presented on social media can influence eating behaviors and create psychological pressure [13]. Another study indicates that social media use enhances orthorexic tendencies among young people and shapes eating behaviors [14].

Orthorexia Nervosa is a complex condition that affects an individual’s psychological and social functioning. It is primarily associated with factors such as perfectionism, a need for control, obsessive-compulsive tendencies, and self-esteem [4]. Research indicates that perfectionists often rigidly manage their eating habits, which can lead to psychological distress [15]. Studies reveal that perfectionism and obsessive-compulsive traits can compel individuals to adhere to strict dietary rules, thereby increasing orthorectic tendencies [16, 17]. Furthermore, excessive control over food choices and health-related self-perceptions may play a critical role in the development of Orthorexia Nervosa [4].

The relationship between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem should be examined within the context of individuals’ eating habits and psychological well-being. While those with Orthorexia Nervosa may experience an increase in self-worth when they strictly adhere to a healthy diet, they often feel guilt and anxiety when unable to follow dietary rules [18, 19]. Additionally, individuals with orthorexia nervosa may assess their food choices through a moral lens, negatively impacting their social relationships and overall quality of life [18]. Furthermore, social pressure and nutrition trends significantly influence the development of Orthorexia Nervosa. Research suggests that excessive idealization of healthy eating can lead individuals to establish rigid rules regarding their food choices [4, 20]. The socially imposed ideal of a thin appearance may exacerbate orthorexic tendencies by adversely affecting individuals’ body image and self-esteem [21].

In this context, understanding the psychosocial effects of Orthorexia Nervosa, identifying risk factors during adolescence, and developing early intervention strategies are essential. This study aims to explore the relationship between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem and the interaction of sociodemographic factors. We anticipate that the findings will enhance awareness of eating disorders and aid in identifying risk factors for adolescents.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in all public high schools in the central (Şehzadeler) district of Manisa Province to evaluate the possible effects of high school students’ attitudes toward healthy nutrition on their self-esteem. The data were collected over two months between January 3, 2022, and February 28, 2022. Data on students’ Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem levels were collected via surveys and scales during this process.

The study universe consists of 5574 students studying in all public high schools (high school education in Turkey is 4 years.) in the Şehzadeler district (Supplementary Table 1). The sample size was calculated as a minimum of 521 people with a 95% confidence interval, with an expected frequency of 50% and a pattern effect of 1.45 for 5574 students via the Epi-info program. For this study to be conducted on students, a 12% margin of error was added, and the total number of participants targeted to be reached was 626.

The distribution of students who participated in the study was calculated via a stratified sampling method based on school and class. At least one class from each class level was selected by lottery from each school. Participants who volunteered to participate were included in the study. At least one class from each school was included in the study, according to classes 9, 10, 11, and 12. Therefore, since the number of classes included in the study and the number of students for each class were different, 645 students were reached. 45 students who did not agree to participate or had a chronic disease or psychiatric illness were excluded from the study. Ultimately, the study was completed with 600 students.

Data collection

Students received information about the study’s scope in the classroom where data collection would occur. After obtaining parental and student consent, those students who agreed to participate completed the questionnaire and scale. Throughout this research, ethical guidelines were strictly adhered to, and all necessary precautions were taken to ensure the confidentiality and anonymity of participants. Data were collected voluntarily, and participants were informed that their information would remain completely confidential. To minimize social desirability bias, surveys were administered anonymously, and participants were encouraged to provide accurate and honest responses. The purpose of the study was explained, along with its content and objectives. Additionally, this study was not part of a larger research project, and participants only completed the measures reported in this article: self-esteem, the Orthorexia Nervosa scale, and demographic information. These precautions helped establish an ethical framework to reduce potential bias in the study.

Data were collected using an information form, the Ortho Scale, and the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. The explanatory information form includes 11 questions designed to assess sociodemographic characteristics.

Ortho scale

The Ortho Scale is a measurement tool developed to assess orthorexia nervosa. This scale aims to identify individuals’ obsessive behaviors regarding healthy eating and their excessive tendencies associated with it. It consists of questions that evaluate participants’ healthy food choices, nutritional behaviors, and attitudes toward food [10]. Arusoğlu et al. adapted the scale into Turkish, and it is regarded as a valid and reliable tool for measuring healthy eating obsessions. A decrease in scores indicates a tendency toward orthorexic behavior, while an increase in scores reflects a tendency toward normal eating behavior [12].

Rosenberg self-esteem scale

The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale is a widely recognized psychological tool developed by Morris Rosenberg to assess an individual’s overall self-esteem. This scale evaluates a person’s general attitude toward themselves and their subjective value judgments [22]. Çuhadaroğlu et al. investigated the scale’s validity and reliability among Turkish youth [23]. Interestingly, due to the procedures in the scale development process, individuals tend to increase their self-esteem levels as the scores on the scale decrease.

Data analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics 15.0 software was used to analyze the collected data. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, while numerical data are presented as means and standard deviations. Normally distributed variables were compared using Student’s t-test and ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed variables were assessed with the Mann‒Whitney U test and the Kruskal‒Wallis test. Simple and multiple linear regression analyses evaluated how demographic factors such as age, years of education, gender, and BMI predicted Ortho Scale scores. For the multiple linear regression analysis, the β-value (regression coefficient) was used to assess the explanatory power of the independent variables [24]. This study employed a stepwise regression analysis model suitable for incorporating multiple independent variables as predictors [25, 26]. In the regression model, the class was categorized as binary, with students divided into two groups: those in the early high school years (lower classes, 9th and 10th grades) and those in higher classes. Software was used to analyze the collected data. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The majority of participants were female (57.3%). According to the body mass index classification, 59.5% were classified as normal weight, 27.5% as underweight, and 2% as obese. 57% of students expressed satisfaction with their height, 45.5% with their weight, and 60% with their overall appearance. Furthermore, 68.3% indicated that they had never gone on a diet, and 68.8% reported not exercising regularly. Additionally, 58.5% of students believed that organic nutrition positively influences health, and 90.3% stated that no one in their family was overweight (Table 1).

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

In this study, no statistically significant relationships were found among participant gender, satisfaction with height and appearance, and orthorexia nervosa. Furthermore, no significant differences were observed between participants’ high school classes and Ortho Scale scores (p = 0.090).

A significant relationship was observed between body mass index values and Orthorexia Nervosa (p = 0.009). The Tukey test results showed that individuals classified as underweight or normal weight had lower tendencies toward Orthorexia Nervosa compared to those who were overweight. Additionally, a significant correlation was found between weight satisfaction and Orthorexia Nervosa (p = 0.012), indicating that participants who were satisfied with their weight exhibited lower levels of Orthorexia Nervosa. Dieting status was also significantly associated with Orthorexia Nervosa (p < 0.001); individuals on diets demonstrated more pronounced tendencies toward Orthorexia Nervosa than those not on a diet. Similarly, participants who exercised regularly tended to exercise more often than those who did not (p < 0.001). A significant relationship was identified between attitudes toward organic nutrition and Orthorexia Nervosa (p < 0.001). It was determined that individuals who believed organic nutrition benefited their health showed a greater tendency toward Orthorexia Nervosa than those who were undecided or held opposing views. Furthermore, participants with an overweight family member reported higher levels of orthorexia nervosa (p = 0.008) (Table 2).

Discussion

This article examines the connection between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem among high school students, as well as its links to various demographic and lifestyle factors. The results revealed a low-level positive correlation between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem. It has been determined that individuals with low self-esteem are more likely to have Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies. Additionally, significant relationships have been found between variables such as body mass index, weight satisfaction, diet status, regular exercise, beliefs about organic nutrition, and having an overweight family member and Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies.

In this study, no significant relationship was found between participants’ gender and Orthorexia Nervosa. This finding aligns with research conducted by Depa et al., which involved university students [27]. However, Oberle et al. reported that women showed a greater tendency toward Orthorexia Nervosa among university students as well [28]. These differences in the literature may be related to the sample age range, sociocultural contexts, and the diversity of measurement tools. In a study of university students in Turkiye, no gender differences were observed [29].

Furthermore, it was observed that orthorexic tendencies tend to increase during high school years. This pattern may be related to awareness of health and nutrition issues, academic stress, and changes in body perception that occur with age. It is noted that Orthorexia Nervosa does not show a consistent gender distribution and has a complex psychological structure influenced by age and environmental factors; however, drawing a definitive causal conclusion about the relationship between gender and Orthorexia Nervosa is limited by the available data.

The significant association between being overweight and Orthorexia Nervosa, along with the connection between weight satisfaction and Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies, suggests that both psychological and physical factors may influence healthy eating behaviors. Additionally, the fact that Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies are more common in individuals with higher body mass index indicates that these individuals might develop more structured or rigid attitudes toward healthy eating [30]. This study differs from existing research by showing that the risk of Orthorexia Nervosa is higher in overweight individuals compared to others. A series of three studies from the USA and the United Kingdom involving obese and overweight participants predicts that overweight individuals are more likely to overeat to cope with stress, which could lead to increased weight gain [31]. Kiss-Leizer and others have similarly observed that individuals concerned about their weight often try not only to lose weight but also to gain control over their bodies through what they consider “healthy” eating, which may be related to Orthorexia Nervosa [32].

The study found that participants who were satisfied with their weight showed a lower tendency toward orthorexia. However, no significant relationship was observed between height, overall appearance satisfaction, and orthorexia. Research involving university students in Spain and Poland suggest that general body image dissatisfaction and concern about being underweight may be associated with orthorexia [33, 34]. Furthermore, a study of participants from the general population over 18 in Turkey demonstrated a strong correlation between weight perception and eating attitudes [14]. The differences between the findings in this study and the relevant literature may be due to the cognitive and emotional characteristics of the sample group specific to adolescence.

Significant associations between dieting, regular exercise, and Orthorexia Nervosa suggest that these behaviors may coexist with more stringent attitudes toward healthy eating in some adolescents [34, 35]. Research findings revealed that individuals who dieted exhibited a higher tendency toward Orthorexia Nervosa compared to those who did not. Similarly, participants who engaged in regular exercise showed increased levels of Orthorexia Nervosa than those who did not. This observation aligns with literature indicating that behaviors such as dieting and excessive exercise can be linked to eating disorders and Orthorexia Nervosa [34–36]. The pressure to maintain a healthy lifestyle, particularly among adolescents, may heighten the risk of Orthorexia Nervosa by reinforcing strict eating and exercise routines [37]. Brytek-Matera et al. proposed that physical activity and dieting behaviors are closely connected to Orthorexia Nervosa and that these factors may mutually reinforce each other [34]. Segura-Garcia et al. noted that dietary restriction, even when initially for health reasons, can evolve into obsessive thoughts and uncontrolled behaviors over time, contributing to the development of Orthorexia Nervosa [36]. Therefore, intervention programs should address the adverse effects of unhealthy eating while also considering potentially problematic aspects.

The results of this study are consistent with the findings of Dunn et al., suggesting that participants with a positive attitude towards organic nutrition may be more likely to have a higher level of Orthorexia Nervosa [38]. Research involving university students has highlighted that healthy eating choices can pose a risk for some individuals, and the belief in the health benefits of organic nutrition may lead to excessive focus and obsessive thoughts, particularly among adolescents [39]. In line with the existing literature, it has been evaluated that the perception of individuals in this age group towards organic nutrition may be one of the risk factors associated with Orthorexia Nervosa.

The observed association between having overweight or obese family members and tendencies towards Orthorexia Nervosa may indicate the importance of genetic and environmental factors influencing dietary behaviors. The body structure of family members may be linked to children’s body image and nutritional attitudes [40]. In addition, the family environment plays a significant role in shaping children’s perceptions of healthy eating and is the primary socialization context in which eating habits develop [41]. These observations suggest a multidimensional structure of family dynamics that may be associated with Orthorexia Nervosa. However, instead of suggesting that these associations are causal, the possibility that social and psychological interactions within the family may play a role in the development of Orthorexia Nervosa should be considered.

The results of the multiple linear regression analysis in this study reveal a pattern consistent with existing literature. It has been observed that individuals with characteristics such as being overweight, not being satisfied with their weight, having a history of dieting, doing regular exercise, believing that organic food is beneficial, and having low self-esteem are more associated with Orthorexia Nervosa. This situation yields similar results to those reported in studies conducted by Brytek-Matera et al. [34] and Dunn et al. [38]. The presence of factors such as organic diet preferences, regular exercise, and family dynamics suggests that Orthorexia Nervosa may be related not only to nutritional motivation but also to social and environmental factors. The spread of healthy lifestyle ideologies through social media may be associated with Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies in some individuals [30]. Brytek-Matera et al. found that Orthorexia Nervosa is linked to a psychological emphasis on healthy food preferences rather than weight management [34]. The lack of a direct significant relationship between physical characteristics (such as body mass index and weight satisfaction) and Orthorexia Nervosa may point to aspects of this condition that differentiate it from other eating disorders. Orthorexia Nervosa can be defined as a tendency towards rigid control and anxiety regarding the consumption of “healthy” foods rather than focusing on weight loss or body image [42]. Psychological and social factors such as adherence to social norms, ideals of a healthy life, low self-esteem, and high stress may contribute to the development of Orthorexia Nervosa [43, 44]. These findings suggest that Orthorexia may have a multilayered structure; it may be related not only to individual eating behaviors but also to broader psychological processes such as social identity development during adolescence, body image, and health norms.

This study also examined how variables such as gender, body satisfaction, diet status, and having an overweight family member affect self-esteem in high school students. The observation that male students had higher self-esteem than female students suggested that gender roles may influence self-esteem. Recent studies indicate that social acceptance enhances males’ self-esteem during adolescence, while females are more susceptible to social pressures regarding body image [45–47]. Additionally, the significant relationship between body satisfaction and self-esteem reveals that self-esteem is influenced by body perception. Studies have shown that students who are satisfied with their bodies tend to have higher self-esteem, and that physical satisfaction contributes to adolescents’ sense of self-worth [48]. In this context, individuals with high self-esteem are generally more satisfied with their bodies and consequently are less preoccupied with the need for healthy eating. In this context, it has been observed that individuals with high self-esteem are more likely to exhibit a positive attitude towards body satisfaction.

The relationship between Orthorexia Nervosa tendencies and low self-esteem aligns with current research, showing that individuals with low self-esteem often adopt healthier eating habits more rigidly to seek social acceptance, control their bodies, and offset feelings of inadequacy [19, 49]. In this study, individuals who were not dieting had higher self-esteem levels, suggesting an indirect link between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem [50]. There is also evidence that social media and cultural norms may increase societal pressures for healthy eating and body image, potentially supporting behaviors associated with Orthorexia Nervosa [51, 52]. Research indicates that content focused on “healthy eating” and the “ideal body,” which circulates widely on social media platforms, can lead individuals to develop an excessive commitment to healthy eating, thereby increasing the risk of Orthorexia Nervosa [53–55]. In particular, among adolescents, social media use and the tendency to conform to social expectations may be linked to adopting healthy eating as a means of social acceptance [56]. In this context, it is recommended that future research should explore the role of social and environmental factors in developing Orthorexia Nervosa more comprehensively and focus on strategies to prevent overcontrol and obsessive behaviors while raising awareness of healthy eating.

Limitations and strengths

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting this study’s findings. First, the sample was limited to high school students in a particular region, which may affect the generalizability of the results to different cultural or demographic groups. The fact that most participants were underweight and very few were overweight or obese may raise questions about how representative the sample is of the general population. Given the impact of regional and cultural differences on dietary habits, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other regions of Turkiye.

The limitations of the Ortho Scale’s reliability, which is used to assess tendencies of Orthorexia Nervosa in the study, should also be considered. Literature indicates that the Ortho Scale has a relatively low composite reliability coefficient and is viewed as a weak tool for measuring Orthorexia Nervosa. While this scale is useful for assessing individuals’ attitudes toward healthy eating, its validity for diagnostic purposes is limited. Therefore, interpreting the study findings solely based on the Ortho Scale presents challenges. Future studies should utilize alternative measurement tools with improved psychometric properties to comprehensively evaluate Orthorexia Nervosa.

Ultimately, this study relied solely on a questionnaire-based data collection method. Data obtained through self-reporting may contain bias, as participants might respond based on their own perceptions. Social acceptability bias can be particularly evident in eating behaviors and body perception. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate semi-structured clinical interviews and psychiatric assessments to gain a deeper understanding of psychological processes such as Orthorexia Nervosa.

Despite these limitations, this study makes significant contributions by focusing on high school students. This age group may be vulnerable to Orthorexia Nervosa behaviors because of their identity formation and sensitivity to peer and social pressures. This study provides an integrated psychosocial perspective on Orthorexia Nervosa risk by considering the relationship between Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem together with factors such as healthy eating habits and body image perception. This approach adds depth to the literature on the eating behaviors of adolescents. This study provides a basis for further research on the psychological and social factors affecting Orthorexia Nervosa in this age group.

Conclusion

This study examined the factors influencing tendencies toward Orthorexia Nervosa in high school students and evaluated their relationship with self-esteem. The findings indicated a low-level positive correlation between tendencies for Orthorexia Nervosa and self-esteem. Additionally, factors such as dissatisfaction with weight, previous dieting behavior, regular exercise habits, and positive attitudes toward organic nutrition were significantly associated with Orthorexia Nervosa. The study also found that orthorexic tendencies were more common among overweight individuals and students in higher classes of high school.

These factors related to Orthorexia Nervosa underscore the importance of body image, motivation for healthy eating, and psychosocial variables, particularly during adolescence. Specifically, dissatisfaction with weight and low self-esteem can drive individuals to adhere to strict dietary guidelines and develop excessively controlled eating habits. The finding that these tendencies are more pronounced among those with a history of dieting suggests that such a dietary background may reinforce orthorectic attitudes. Likewise, individuals who exercise regularly often have a heightened awareness of healthy living; however, when this awareness is combined with a desire for excessive control, it can increase the risk of developing Orthorexia Nervosa.

The findings are crucial for gaining a deeper understanding of the psychosocial and behavioral factors that influence orthorexic tendencies in adolescents. A detailed analysis is essential to examine how body image, dietary habits, and attitudes toward healthy eating affect individuals’ eating behaviors, particularly among high school students. Future research should focus on developing early intervention strategies for these risk factors and promoting awareness of healthy eating in adolescents while considering psychological well-being.
