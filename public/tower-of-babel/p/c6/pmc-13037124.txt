Quality of drinking water in the United Arab Emirates: a systematic review
Authors: Ghada S. M. Al-Bluwi, Virgie Guy Pedo, Saif Al-Shamsi, Asma H. AlNababteh, Linda Östlundh, Janusz A. Jankowski, Balázs Ádám
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProviding sufficient amount and adequate quality of drinking water for the public is one of the most important and challenging environmental health tasks worldwide, especially in arid regions. Water shortage is a critical issue in most parts of the Middle East, even for wealthy countries. The United Arab Emirates (UAE) is not an exception, where bottled water is favored to tap water, which poses sustainability issues to the fight against climate change and microplastics pollution. This systematic review aims to synthesize evidence on the quality of bottled water in comparison of tap water in the UAE.MethodsThe systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. Structured search was performed in five scientific databases, screening, data extraction and risk of bias assessment were carried out by two reviewers and data were synthesized in summary tables.Results14 papers were finally included in this systematic review. All were cross-sectional studies from four of the seven Emirates of the UAE. Seven studies investigated components in bottled water, six in tap water and one in both, comparing their results to various national and international standards. The studies detected several parameters out of the normal range, especially in tap water, where physical parameters, such as turbidity and electrical conductivity, chemical parameters, such as chlorine and several metals, including carcinogens, and biological parameters, such as various bacteria and fungi, were found at unhealthy levels. In contrast, problems with bottled water were mainly limited to a few chemicals, such as fluoride, nitrite and bicarbonate.ConclusionsThe findings revealed quality issues with tap water in the UAE. To assure an environmentally and economically sustainable provision of healthy drinking water to the population, evidence-based actions need to be taken.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26111-8.

Introduction

Water is a vital resource for human health and wellbeing, and ensuring access to safe drinking water remains a global challenge. According to a World Health Organization (WHO) [1], approximately 27% of the world’s population lacks access to safely managed drinking-water, exposing millions to diseases caused by microbiological and chemical contaminants [2]. The United Arab Emirates (UAE), despite its rapid economic development and wealth from oil reserves, faces severe water scarcity due to its arid climate and limited natural freshwater resources [3, 4]. Groundwater and desalinated seawater are the primary drinking water sources in the UAE [5, 6]. Since the establishment of the first desalination plant in 1973, desalinated water has become the dominant supply source, accounting for most of the nation’s drinking water by 2019 [6, 7]. While desalination has reduced groundwater depletion, it remains costly, energy intensive, and may result in reduced intake of essential micronutrients [8-11].

In the UAE, drinking water is consumed mainly as tap water or bottled water. Public preference often favors bottled water, driven by perceived concerns over tap water quality and possible contamination from corroded household pipes and water tanks [12]. However, bottled water can also contain variable micronutrient composition or trace element contaminants due to factors such as source quality, packaging, and storage conditions [13-15].

Despite widespread bottled water consumption in the UAE, systematic evidence comparing its quality with that of tap water remains limited. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to assess existing studies comparing the quality of tap and bottled drinking water in the UAE to determine whether bottled water offers superior quality. The findings may help guide safe consumption practices and inform future water quality management strategies in the country.

This systematic review was performed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [16], and the protocol is registered online on the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with registration number CRD42021265781.

The PECO statement

All articles that reported any quality indicators of bottled and/or tap water in the United Arab Emirates were included. Studies where the analysis was done in the field as well as in a laboratory were considered. No restrictions were made on the type of contaminants being studied; including microbial, chemical, and radiological contaminants. Studies were excluded if they did not report primary data or the sort of water sample investigated. Only primary peer-reviewed scientific research publications were considered, governmental reports, commentaries, proceedings of conferences, and any other non-peer-reviewed publications were excluded. There was no publication date, language, or study design restriction applied.

Literature search

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) 2020 checklist was consulted for the literature search. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided as a supplementary file (S1). A broad search for literature was conducted in September 2021 by a medical librarian (LO) with input from the subject specialists (BA) and reviewers (AHA & GA). The search was updated in 27 May 2024 ahead of the manuscript preparation. No deviations or amendments were made to the registered protocol during the literature update. Search terms were constructed based on the pre-defined PECO statement and inclusion and exclusion criteria, and developed to a systematic search string through pre-searches in PubMed in May–June 2021. In PubMed, the search combined the MeSH terms “Drinking Water” and “United Arab Emirates” with text words representing all Emirates (Abu Dhabi, Al Ain, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Ras Al Khaimah, Fujairah, and Umm al-Quwain), as well as related terms such as UAE, emirat*, GCC, Gulf Cooperation, and Arabian Peninsula, linked by Boolean operators (OR/AND). No filters or date restrictions were applied. The full search string is provided as a supplementary file (S2).

Five biomedical and food science databases were included in the final search (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, FSTA-Food Science and Technology Abstracts) that was conducted without any filters or limitations. Following the study’s inclusion criteria, we did not include any separate sources for non-peer-reviewed or grey materials. A combination of the search fields; title, abstract, keywords, and MeSH (Medical Subject Headings)/thesaurus was used in every database to ensure that all possible evidence on the topic was identified. Finally, the reference of the included studies was manually screened by two independent reviewers (AHA & GA). The academic quality of eligible studies published in open-access journals were verified by consulting Cabell’s Predatory Reports [18].

Screening

The results from the database search were uploaded to the systematic review software Covidence [19] where the unique records were identified through automatic de-duplication. The software provided settings for blinded screening and selection. A detailed search log with results, notes and reproducible search strings for each database are appended as a supplementary file (S2). Risk of bias assessment

For assessing risk of bias (RoB) in individual studies, we used the RoB-SPEO tool, which is suitable to evaluate biases related to studies estimating prevalence of exposure to occupational and environmental risk factors, such as selection bias, performance bias, exposure misclassification, incomplete data, selective reporting, differences in numerator and denominator, and other biases. The World Health Organization and the International Labour Organization (ILO) jointly developed this tool for the estimation of work-related burden of disease and injury (WHO/ILO Joint Estimates) [20]. To ensure that the RoB tool is effective, the Excel-based tool was pilot tested. Two reviewers (AHA & GA) independently evaluated the RoB of the selected studies. Data extraction

The data extraction process was conducted by two reviewers (AHA & GA) independently in Covidence. Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by a third reviewer (BA) to ensure accuracy. The information extracted for this study included data on the publication (title, first author, year of publication, journal), the settings of the studies (study objective emirate, city, location, study design, period of data collection), sampling (sampling strategy, sampling point, amount, size, temperature, number), sample analysis (lab, standard, technique), components detected (types of physical, chemical and biological parameters), conclusion and notes.

Results

Study selection

The process of study selection is presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). After removing all duplicates, our initial search from the five databases yielded 7,472 citations for title and abstract screening. At the end of the title and abstract screening, 32 studies were included for full-text screening. 18 studies were excluded during the full-text screening, the reasons for exclusion were “wrong water sample” (n = 10), “wrong study design” (n = 6), “duplicate” (n = 1), and “conference paper” (n = 1). No additional citations were identified by hand searching reference lists of included studies and identified previous reviews. In total, 14 papers included in this systematic review, all of which were written in English Cohen’s κ values indicated moderate agreement between reviewers for both title and abstract screening (κ = 0.534) and for full-text screening (κ = 0.510), demonstrating acceptable inter-rater reliability.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias in individual studies was assessed in eight domains. The risk of bias in the selection of water samples into the study was probably high or high in 78.6% of the reviewed articles, typically due to purposeful sampling. There was no article identified with probably high or high risk for performance bias, bias due to exposure misclassification, bias due to incomplete exposure data, selective reporting of exposures, and conflict of interests. Risk of bias due to differences in numerator and denominator was not assessable in these articles. Five articles (35.7%) had probably high risk of bias due to other problems (e.g., unclear sample amounts, unclear sampling strategies, unclear standard limits used for comparison, and publication in open-access journals not listed in Cabell’s Predatory Reports). Detailed results of the risk of bias assessment are summarized in Table 2. None of the 14 selected studies were excluded from further analysis due to the level of risk of bias.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 3; additionally, a tabular comparison of study characteristics and measured parameters across Emirates are provided as a supplementary file (S3). All of the included studies were of cross-sectional design. Three of these studies were conducted in the Emirate of Sharjah [21-23], two in Abu Dhabi [24, 25], and one in Dubai alone [26]. Three included a combination of Emirates (Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, and Ajman) [27-29] and five were unclear regarding the location [30-34]. Discussion

The present systematic review summarized the quality of tap and bottled water in the UAE. The review included 14 research articles published from 1999 to 2021, yielding eight studies that investigated bottled water, six studies tap water and one study both. The studies analyzed a total of 2,657 water samples. It was not reasonable to collate the evidence from the included studies in a pooled meta-analysis, since substantial methodological variability was observed across the studies. The reviewed 14 studies identified several parameters out of the normal range, mainly in tap water samples (Fig. 3, supplementary file S6). Quality problems with tap water in the Middle East have been identified by several studies [45-47], which is likely the main reason why people prefer consuming bottled water in the region [48–51].Fig. 3Summary of unhealthy levels of contaminants measured in bottled and tap water in the United Arab Emirates

Summary of unhealthy levels of contaminants measured in bottled and tap water in the United Arab Emirates

Quality of bottled water

Bottled water had no quality issues regarding physical parameters in the reviewed studies. Most of the chemical parameters that were found out of the normal range were below the recommended range, which typically pose no particular risk to human health. The exception is fluoride, since level of fluoride below the recommended range in drinking water can cause fluoride deficiency if intake is also low from the diet, resulting in increased risk of dental caries [52, 53]. On the other hand, fluoride was also found exceeding the threshold in one study, which carries the risk of fluorosis [54, 55]. Bicarbonate and nitrite were also found acceding the considered threshold. Bicarbonate shifts the pH of drinking water to the alkaline range, although problem with alkalinity was not specifically reported by the same study [30]. Above threshold nitrite level, which is an indication of relatively new contamination with organic material, may induce methemoglobinemia in infants [56] as well as it can increase the risk of gastrointestinal cancer in long-term exposure [57].

An early study in 1999 found microbiological contamination in bottled water samples; however, the public heath significance of the isolated bacteria was questionable [34]. In addition, heterotrophic plate count and fungi were found above threshold in bottle dispensers in a recent study, although in this case the dispenser might have been responsible for the contamination [28].

Quality of tap water

Much more quality problems were detected in tap water by the reviewed studies. Turbidity and electrical conductivity were found above threshold by several studies [21-23, 26]. Increased turbidity is an aesthetic issue, as well as an indication of potential external contamination, likely occurring in the pipeline or in the water tank, that can lead to gastrointestinal illness [58, 59]. Increased electrical conductivity in samples from Sharjah is an indication of saltiness that may also be a result of contamination. Acidity was found above threshold as well as below the recommended range [22-25]. Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) and nitrate was found above threshold [24, 25], which indicates new and old contamination of tap water with organic materials, respectively [60, 61]. Chloride was above the recommended range in some samples [21, 22], while total chlorine, total residual chlorine and free chlorine were below the range in others [21, 22, 26], indicating an inadequate level of protection from organic pollution, including infectious agents [62]. Several metals were found above the considered threshold in tap water samples, such as iron, manganese, lead, copper, arsenic and cadmium [22-25]. While iron and manganese contamination typically originate from natural sources or the pipeline and is mainly an aesthetic issue [35], arsenic and cadmium are harmful metals with several potential health effects at concentrations above threshold [63–65]. The majority of studies found bacterial and fungal contamination above the threshold [21, 24, 26], which is a clear sign of microbial contamination that poses biological risk to human health [66].

Environmental health implications

In most places of Europe and North America, the quality of tap water is suitable for human consumption. Consequently, the majority of the population uses water from the tap not only for washing but also for cooking and direct consumption in everyday practice. To assure a high standard of potable water at the consumption site, the water distribution needs good quality water at the entry point and a reliable pipeline system with high pressure, without leaks and any other critical points of possible external contamination [35]. The summarized results of this systematic review highlight several quality issues of tap water in the UAE, which point out the weaknesses in the distribution system. A straightforward solution for the substandard quality of tap water is the use of bottled water, the quality of which can be assured by the producing companies. However, this system comes with economic as well as environmental costs. The production costs of potable tap water are less than that of bottled water if reliable distribution system is installed and maintained [67, 68]. In addition, the enormous number of plastic bottles that the existing custom generates constitute huge environmental burden. If most of the bottles are not reusable or the recycling system is not in place, which is the situation is most parts of the world, including the UAE [69], the plastics disposed in landfills will add to the microplastics and nanoplastics burden of the environment with largely still unforeseeable ecological and human health consequences [70-73].

Strengths and limitations

This study's strength comes from the fact that it is the first in the UAE to conduct a thorough systematic review on the quality of both tap and bottled water. The search for studies from five large databases, which yielded a substantial size of water samples (n = 2,657) screened for contaminants add strength to it. The systematic review closely followed the PRISMA guidelines, the possibility of human error was decreased by two independent reviewers who carried out screening, data extraction and risk of bias assessment. The ability of this study to point out evidentiary gaps, particularly the dearth of information regarding the quality of potable water throughout the entire UAE, is another highlight. Nevertheless, there are also some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, there was lack of uniformity in water sampling methods among the studies. Second, there was no consistent reference limit utilized throughout studies to compare the level of pollutants, which could have caused the quality of water to be over- or under-estimated. Third, no studies were done in Umm Al Qaiwain, Fujairah and Ras Al Khaimah Emirates. Furthermore, the assessment of publication bias revealed high to possibly high bias in sample selection in 78.6% of the included studies, which may have an impact on the generalizability of the findings to the overall water quality in the UAE, since purposeful selection of water sources with likely suspected problems in some studies can typically lead to the overestimation of the exposure level. There also remains a selection bias in data collation, as access to water quality government data is not openly available. Finally, because of the high level of methodological heterogeneity, the review was restricted to a narrative analysis, meta-analysis could not be performed.

Conclusion

The synthetized information points out quality issues with tap water in the UAE. Measures to reduce tap water contamination to a safe level, such as regular cleaning and maintenance of household water tanks, or even restructuring the drinking water distribution system, are advisable. The high level of water consumption from disposable plastic bottles in the UAE should be considered in terms of its economic, environmental and human health effects. Although bottled water has been proved to be quite safe, the fluoride and nitrite content should be closely followed and clearly labelled. The UAE Water Security Strategy 2036 well address the aims of reducing water demand and increasing water supply security in normal and emergency situations [74]. In addition, environmental health policy initiatives should include considerations aimed at further strengthening monitoring, reducing dependence on bottled water, increasing the proportion of recycling and the share of water bottles made of biodegradable materials.

Due to the still limited information on water quality in the UAE, especially in some Emirates, and the heterogenous methodology of existing studies, a nationwide study involving all the seven Emirates to evaluate the quality of tap water should be initiated and regularly repeated. In addition to monitoring water quality, future studies should also assess health risks and epidemiological burden of water contamination in the UAE, as well as should address the required actions of water suppliers, including efficient techniques for removal of contaminants.

This systematic review points out the human health, environmental and economic issues of drinking water supply in the UAE, highlighting the importance of ensuring safe, sustainable, and equitable drinking water provision as a public health and sustainability priority for the country.
