Emotional, technical, and ethical experiences of nursing students performing oral hygiene for dependent older adults: a qualitative study
Authors: N. Oulachguer, A. Ahaggach, C. Ed-Daoui, R. Bakassi, I. ElHafidi, M. S. Afif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores the perceptions of nursing students specializing in geriatrics when performing toothbrushing for dependent older adults. It aims to highlight the emotional and technical responses associated with this care activity, which is often underestimated in clinical practice, and to emphasize its importance for the overall health and quality of life of older people.MethodsA descriptive qualitative design was adopted. Thirty-four nursing students participated in a pedagogical exercise integrated into the geriatrics module. After performing toothbrushing on an older adult, each student produced a free reflective written account. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis approach [1], following a systematic process of independent double coding, triangulation, and consensus-based validation supported by qualitative research standards [2–5].ResultsFive main themes emerged: initial discomfort and disgust, progressive adaptation, neutral or professional perception, emotional engagement and empathy, and technical difficulties. The findings indicate that toothbrushing is perceived as a multidimensional care activity involving sensory, emotional, technical, and relational dimensions.ConclusionsOral hygiene in dependent older adults is an essential but often underestimated component of care. Insufficient oral care may contribute to malnutrition, muscle weakness, chronic pain, halitosis, and local or systemic infections, thereby compromising health, dignity, and quality of life in older populations [6–10]. These findings align with World Health Organisation recommendations emphasizing strengthened oral health systems and universal coverage by 2030 [6].

Background

Demographic trends, both in Morocco and globally, indicate a significant increase in the proportion of older adults, reflecting an ongoing demographic transition [11, 12]. According to the World Health Organization, older adults are defined as individuals aged 60 years and above, considering functional capacity and environment [11]. In Morocco, projections estimate that by 2050, this group will represent approximately 25% of the population [12].

This demographic shift is associated with increased dependency due to physiological ageing and chronic diseases [11, 13], reinforcing the need for specialized geriatric training.

Preventive care is essential to maintain quality of life in older adults. Oral hygiene is a fundamental determinant of general health [6, 7] but remains frequently neglected in care settings [8, 14].

For dependent older adults, toothbrushing is mainly performed by caregivers or healthcare professionals [9, 15], making their attitudes and emotional preparedness crucial for care quality.

Literature shows that nursing staff often experience insufficient training and low prioritization of oral care [10, 15, 16]. Oral diseases significantly affect nutrition and quality of life [7, 8, 10], while institutional barriers such as lack of time and discomfort remain common [17–21].

However, most studies focus on qualified professionals. The emotional and sensory experiences of nursing students during initial exposure to oral care remain underexplored, particularly in Moroccan contexts.

This study therefore aims to explore the experiences of geriatric nursing students during oral hygiene care to identify emotional barriers, adaptation processes, and pedagogical implications.

Methods

Study design

A descriptive and exploratory qualitative design was used to explore emotional, cognitive, and technical experiences of nursing students performing oral hygiene [1–5].

Participants

Thirty-four nursing students from ISPITS (Institut Supérieur des Professions Infirmières et Techniques de Santé, Morocco) participated. All participants belonged to the same academic cohort and were enrolled in the same geriatrics module during the study period. Convenience sampling was used, drawing on an entire accessible pedagogical cohort enrolled in the geriatrics module, consistent with the exploratory qualitative design, Participation was voluntary and anonymous.

No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. the study included the entire accessible cohort of students enrolled in the geriatrics module during the study period.

Procedure

Students performed a single toothbrushing procedure for an older adult in institutional or family settings under supervision. Standard instructions regarding technique, positioning, and infection control were provided to ensure safety and ethical practice.

Data collection

Students produced free reflective written narratives within 24 h. No guiding questions were used to ensure spontaneity.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke [1]. Coding was inductive and iterative, performed manually by two independent coders. Themes were refined through consensus. The analysis was guided by an experiential learning framework. Researchers maintained reflexivity throughout, acknowledging their dual role as educators and analysts as a potential influence on interpretation.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Credibility and dependability were ensured through independent double coding, reflexivity, triangulation, and audit trail documentation [2–5]. Trustworthiness criteria as defined by Lincoln and Guba were applied [5].

Given the exploratory nature of the study and the inclusion of a complete cohort, data sufficiency was considered adequate for the purposes of thematic description. Although formal data saturation was not the primary aim, thematic sufficiency was achieved, as no substantially new themes emerged from the dataset.

Member checking was not conducted due to the anonymous nature of data collection; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Results

Analysis of participants’ written reflections revealed several major themes describing their emotional, cognitive, and practical experiences of performing toothbrushing in older adults. These findings illustrate a complex interplay between emotional reactions, emerging professional awareness, and reflective learning processes (Fig. 1).

Theme 1 — disgust or initial discomfort (44.1%, n = 15)

The most prevalent reaction was an immediate sensory and emotional discomfort, characterized by nausea, olfactory aversion, and reflexive feelings of repulsion at first contact with the patient’s oral cavity. This was the most frequently reported and most vividly described experience across the reflective accounts.

Representative accounts include:

“When the elderly patient opened their mouth, an unpleasant smell made me hesitate… I felt nauseous but continued out of respect…” (Student X)

“At the sight of inflamed gums and food debris, I felt like vomiting and left the room before returning…” (Student Y)

These narratives suggest that disgust is not a superficial reaction but a strongly embodied response to intimate care. It reflects the sensory and emotional challenges inherent in oral hygiene, which are often insufficiently addressed in preclinical training.

Theme 2 — gradual habituation (20.6%, n = 7)

A second group of students described an initial discomfort that progressively diminished, replaced by increasing professional composure. This transition was often associated with cognitive reframing or relational engagement with the patient.

“At first, I felt hesitant… but after talking with the patient about his memories, I became more at ease…” (Student Z)

“At first, I felt hesitant… but after talking with the patient about his memories, I became more at ease…” (Student Z)

This pattern suggests that emotional adaptation during care can occur rapidly within a single supervised encounter, particularly when the patient is perceived as a person rather than a clinical object. It highlights the role of interaction in facilitating emotional regulation.

Theme 3 — neutral or professional perception (14.7%, n = 5)

Some students reported minimal emotional disturbance, framing the task as a routine professional duty. Their narratives emphasized procedural correctness and professional responsibility.

“This is part of my profession, so I focused on doing it correctly.” (Student W)

“This is part of my profession, so I focused on doing it correctly.” (Student W)

This response reflects early professional normalization and suggests that some students are able to cognitively distance themselves from emotional reactions from the outset.

Theme 4 — emotional connection and empathy (11.8%, n = 4)

Several participants emphasized relational engagement, empathy, and a sense of ethical responsibility toward the older adult. These elements emerged spontaneously without prompting.

“I felt I had a duty to fulfill, not just an exercise…” (Student V)

“I felt I had a duty to fulfill, not just an exercise…” (Student V)

This reflects the emergence of professional values such as dignity, respect, and responsibility through lived experience rather than theoretical instruction.

Theme 5 — technical difficulties (8.8%, n = 3)

A minority of students reported difficulties related to technique, confidence, and fear of causing discomfort.

This highlights that technical competence and emotional adaptation are distinct learning processes requiring separate pedagogical attention.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to document empirically, within a Moroccan geriatric nursing training context, the distribution and qualitative nature of emotional, sensory, and ethical reactions experienced by nursing students during oral hygiene care. This work provides novel evidence on an underexplored dimension of nursing education, namely the lived experience of students during intimate care procedures. This perspective remains largely absent from North African health education literature and is still underrepresented internationally in geriatric nursing education research.

The findings reveal that toothbrushing — an essential component of oral hygiene [9] — elicits diverse emotional, sensory, and technical reactions among nursing students. The predominance of initial disgust (44.1%) reflects the highly intimate and sensory nature of oral care, and aligns with previous literature showing that oral hygiene is often perceived by caregivers as unpleasant, low priority, or emotionally challenging [10, 15, 16]. The vividness of this reaction is illustrated in students’ narratives, in which nausea, olfactory aversion, and even temporary withdrawal from the care situation were reported. These testimonies indicate that disgust in this context is not a superficial reaction but a deeply embodied response emerging at the intersection of sensory exposure and professional obligation. Although understandable at this stage of training, this instinctive reaction may negatively affect care quality if not explicitly addressed through structured pedagogical preparation. Indeed, inadequate oral care in dependent older adults is associated with malnutrition, halitosis, chronic pain, and systemic infections [7, 8, 10], reinforcing the clinical relevance of this emotional barrier.

Importantly, the data demonstrate that emotional responses are not static but evolve through experience. The transition observed from discomfort toward acceptance or professional neutrality (20.6% and 14.7%, respectively) suggests that emotional adaptation can occur rapidly within a single supervised clinical encounter. This dynamic reflects the principles of experiential learning, where meaning is constructed through direct engagement with practice [1]. In this study, adaptation was often facilitated by relational engagement with the patient, where dialogue and personalization of care transformed the patient from an object of procedure into a person with a life history. This process is illustrated by Student Z, whose interaction with the patient’s memories and personal narrative contributed to a marked reduction in emotional discomfort. This finding highlights that emotional regulation in clinical practice is not solely a cognitive effort, but is strongly influenced by relational and contextual factors.

Furthermore, the emergence of empathy and ethical awareness (11.8%) reinforces the importance of experiential learning in the development of professional identity. Students spontaneously expressed concepts such as dignity, respect, and responsibility, suggesting that ethical sensitivity is not exclusively transmitted through theoretical teaching but can emerge directly from clinical exposure. This is consistent with literature emphasizing the role of practice-based learning in shaping professional values in nursing education [13, 22, 23]. In this sense, reflective engagement with lived clinical experience appears to be a key mechanism in the internalization of person-centered care principles.

However, the presence of technical difficulties (8.8%) highlights an important pedagogical issue: emotional adaptation does not automatically translate into procedural competence. This finding reinforces the need for dual-focused training approaches that simultaneously address emotional preparedness and technical skill acquisition. Previous studies have similarly emphasized that oral hygiene care in nursing education requires both psychomotor training and attitudinal development [14, 20]. These two dimensions must therefore be integrated rather than taught in isolation.

Finally, institutional and epidemiological literature consistently demonstrates that inadequate oral care contributes to systemic complications in older adults, including nutritional decline, infections, and reduced quality of life [7, 8, 10]. In this study, the predominance of initial disgust (44.1%) highlights a potential risk in clinical practice: without structured preparation and supervision, students may unconsciously avoid, shorten, or inadequately perform oral hygiene procedures. Such behaviors, even when unintended, may compromise patient outcomes and reinforce the marginalization of oral care in clinical settings. This underscores the necessity of structured pedagogical interventions that address both sensory exposure and emotional regulation from the earliest stages of nursing training.

Taken together, these findings suggest that oral hygiene care is not merely a technical act, but a complex learning experience involving sensory perception, emotional regulation, relational engagement, and ethical development. Strengthening this dimension within nursing curricula is essential to ensure both competent practice and high-quality geriatric care.

These findings should also be interpreted in the context of nursing education in Morocco. Although oral health is generally addressed within broader nursing and geriatric care curricula, dedicated training specifically focused on oral hygiene assessment and care remains relatively limited. Consequently, students may have few opportunities to develop technical competence and emotional preparedness for intimate oral care procedures before encountering dependent older adults in practice. The discomfort and uncertainty reported by some participants may therefore partly reflect limited prior exposure to oral health care. Strengthening oral health education through simulation-based learning, supervised practice, and reflective activities may help address these challenges.

These findings therefore highlight the need for structured educational interventions that explicitly prepare students for the sensory and emotional dimensions of intimate care. This is consistent with WHO priorities for strengthening oral health systems and promoting universal oral health coverage by 2030 [16]. It also aligns with practical recommendations for improving oral care delivery in geriatric populations through structured protocols and training strategies [24]. In this context, oral hygiene should be understood not as a minor technical procedure, but as a complex act of care situated at the intersection of physical comfort, dignity, and systemic health.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the subjective and spontaneous nature of the students’ responses, which were not guided by a structured questionnaire, may have introduced variability and reduced the consistency of the data. Second, the limited number of participants restricts the generalizability of the findings beyond the studied group. Moreover, the anonymous design of data collection did not permit linking individual narratives to demographic characteristics such as gender, thereby preventing subgroup analyses. In addition, the primary objective of this exploratory qualitative study was to identify common experiential themes related to oral hygiene care rather than to compare responses across demographic groups. Given the relatively small sample size and the qualitative nature of the dataset, the study was not designed to examine potential differences according to gender or other participant characteristics. Consequently, possible variations in emotional responses and perceptions of oral hygiene care across demographic subgroups could not be explored. Future research using larger samples and purposive data collection strategies should investigate whether gender, prior caregiving experience, clinical exposure, or other demographic factors influence students’ experiences of oral hygiene care. Furthermore, all participants were recruited from a single institution and belonged to the same academic cohort. While this approach ensured a relatively homogeneous educational context, it may limit the transferability of findings to other nursing programs or healthcare settings. Multicenter studies involving diverse student populations would help confirm the broader applicability of these findings.

Finally, the pedagogical context in which the exercise was conducted may have influenced the sincerity or spontaneity of the responses, thereby shaping the outcomes in ways that reflect the educational setting rather than broader clinical practice.

Despite these limitations, the study retains its originality and relevance, as it represents the first initiative of its kind in Morocco and across the MENA region. The findings highlight both the preventive role of oral hygiene and the pedagogical value of engaging geriatric nursing students in such exercises, thereby paving the way for future research and educational innovation.

Conclusions

This study highlights that toothbrushing in dependent older adults cannot be reduced to a simple hygiene procedure but represents a complex act of care involving technical, relational, and ethical dimensions. The findings indicate that this practice, often considered secondary or overlooked by families, caregivers, and nursing students, is in fact central to the health, dignity, and quality of life of older adults [8, 9, 15].

Beyond the initial discomfort reported by the majority of students (44.1%), the observed trajectory from disgust to habituation (20.6%) and, in some cases, professional neutrality (14.7%) suggests a structured emotional learning process that may be shaped by educational exposure and clinical experience. These results are consistent with previous research highlighting that attitudes and emotional responses toward oral care are influenced by training context and professional socialization [16, 25].

Based on these findings, oral hygiene training in geriatric nursing education should integrate a structured pedagogical approach combining three complementary components: (i) preparatory sessions before first patient contact addressing the sensory and emotional dimensions of intimate care and normalizing initial discomfort; (ii) repeated supervised practice to support the development of technical competence alongside emotional adaptation; and (iii) structured reflective debriefing, including written reflection, to facilitate integration of lived experiences into professional learning. This is in line with established models of experiential learning and reflective practice in nursing education [1, 25].

The emergence of empathy- and dignity-related expressions in students’ narratives further suggests that reflective practice may contribute not only to data collection but also to the development of ethical awareness in clinical training, consistent with previous literature on caring science and person-centered nursing education [22, 23].

The clinical implications are significant. Inadequate oral hygiene, often associated with insufficient caregiver preparation, may contribute to malnutrition, muscle weakness, chronic pain, halitosis, and local or systemic infections in dependent older adults [8–10]. These outcomes reinforce the fact that oral health is closely linked to overall well-being, including nutrition, comfort, and dignity, and highlight the importance of adequate training as a determinant of care quality [6, 7].

It is therefore important to strengthen the place of oral care within nursing curricula and geriatric training programs by integrating technical skills, emotional preparation, and ethical reflection. Such an integrated approach may help transform toothbrushing from an underestimated routine task into a fully recognized component of person-centered care.

Further multicenter research is warranted to confirm the transferability of these findings and to evaluate the effectiveness of structured educational interventions aimed at improving both technical competence and emotional readiness in nursing students [13, 14, 26, 27].
