Pre-admission statins and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction
Authors: Shuji Morikawa, Haruki Kato, Nao Takahashi, Ryo Ohinata, Yuki Sato, Takashi Mishina, Keisuke Iwata, Naoya Inoue, Yohei Takayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCirculatory collapse at hospital arrival represents one of the most severe clinical presentations of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and is associated with poor prognosis. Statins exert pleiotropic effects beyond lipid-lowering, including anti-inflammatory, endothelial, microvascular and plaque-stabilising actions which may influence early clinical severity in myocardial infarction. We investigated the association between pre-admission statin therapy and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival in patients with STEMI undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).MethodsThis retrospective single-centre study included 551 consecutive patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI. Pre-admission statin therapy was defined as continuous statin use for ≥30 days before admission. The primary outcome was circulatory collapse at hospital arrival, defined using an operational definition of advanced haemodynamic compromise before primary PCI. Multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analyses including propensity score adjustment and inverse probability of treatment weighting were performed.ResultsCirculatory collapse occurred in 77 patients (14.0%) and was less frequent among patients receiving statins than among those not receiving statins (7.5% vs 16.6%, p=0.005). In the primary multivariable model, pre-admission statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse (adjusted OR 0.23; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.50; p<0.001). This association remained consistent across sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of lactate-based classification without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest or pharmacological/mechanical circulatory support, propensity score adjustment, inverse probability of treatment weighting and additional adjustment for background preventive medications. 30-day mortality did not differ significantly according to pre-admission statin therapy status.ConclusionsPre-admission statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse at hospital arrival in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI. These findings suggest that chronic statin therapy may be associated with a less severe early haemodynamic phenotype at STEMI presentation, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Introduction

ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality despite advances in reperfusion therapy. Although primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has improved survival, some patients present with severe haemodynamic compromise at hospital arrival. Cardiogenic shock occurs in approximately 5%–10% of patients with STEMI1–3 and remains associated with mortality rates approaching 40%–50%.4–6 Therefore, identifying factors associated with haemodynamic severity at STEMI presentation is clinically relevant. Previous studies have described clinical and procedural predictors of cardiogenic shock, including advanced age, delayed reperfusion, impaired renal function and large infarct size,1 7 but have mainly focused on in-hospital outcomes or mortality. Whether chronic cardiovascular medications, such as statins, influence initial haemodynamic severity at hospital arrival remains unclear.

Statins are the cornerstone of primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Beyond lipid lowering, statins exert pleiotropic effects, including improved endothelial function, anti-inflammatory effects, reduced oxidative stress and plaque stabilisation.8–10 Experimental and clinical studies suggest that statins may attenuate ischaemia-reperfusion injury and improve coronary microvascular function.11–13 Statin pretreatment before PCI has also been associated with improved myocardial perfusion, reduced periprocedural myocardial injury and improved in-hospital outcomes, including in patients with STEMI.14–17 Randomised trials have suggested improved outcomes with early intensive statin therapy in acute coronary syndromes.18–20 Nevertheless, whether chronic statin therapy before admission influences initial haemodynamic severity, rather than in-hospital outcomes, has not been well investigated. Therefore, we investigated the association between pre-admission statin therapy and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective observational cohort study included consecutive patients diagnosed with STEMI who underwent primary PCI at a single tertiary cardiovascular centre between 1 April 2019 and 11 March 2026 (last patient enrolment date). The database was locked on 15 March 2026, and all analyses were performed using data available up to that date. An opt-out approach was used to give patients the option to decline participation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients were consecutive adults (≥18 years) with a final diagnosis of STEMI (type 1 myocardial infarction) who underwent primary PCI during the study period. Patients were excluded if any key study variables were missing, including pre-admission statin use, circulatory collapse at hospital arrival or 30-day mortality. Patients without 30-day follow-up data were also excluded. Additionally, patients with symptom-to-arrival times exceeding 24 hours were excluded. Patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest who achieved return of spontaneous circulation and subsequently underwent primary PCI were included, whereas those who did not achieve return of spontaneous circulation and therefore did not undergo coronary angiography or PCI were not included.

STEMI definition

STEMI was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction.21 STEMI requires ischaemic symptoms consistent with myocardial infarction and persistent ST-segment elevation on electrocardiography.

Data collection

Clinical data were obtained from electronic medical records and the institutional cardiovascular registry. Collected variables included age, sex, smoking status, prior coronary artery disease, renal function, laboratory findings including serum lactate and peak creatine kinase levels, pre-admission medications, echocardiographic left ventricular ejection fraction and angiographic findings including final thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) flow grade. Prior coronary artery disease was defined as a history of myocardial infarction, prior PCI or coronary artery bypass graft surgery. Angiographic findings were reviewed by two experienced interventional cardiologists. Coronary angiography was performed to identify the culprit lesion. The culprit lesion location included involvement of the left anterior descending artery (LAD), a major epicardial coronary artery. Diagnosis was independently adjudicated by two board-certified cardiologists based on electrocardiographic findings, clinical presentation and angiographic data. Symptom-to-arrival time was defined as the interval between symptom onset and hospital arrival, as reported by the patient or a witness in the medical records. Sex was recorded as the biological sex documented in the medical records.

Assessment of pre-admission medication

Information regarding pre-admission medication use was obtained from the electronic medical and prescription records. When necessary, medication histories were confirmed through interviews with patients, family members or the patient’s primary care physician.

Pre-admission statin therapy definition

Pre-admission statin therapy was defined as continuous statin use for at least 30 days before hospital admission to ensure that potential pleiotropic vascular effects of statins were present at the time of STEMI onset.8–10 This exposure window was selected to distinguish chronic statin therapy from short-term or newly initiated statin treatments immediately before hospital presentation.

Circulatory collapse definition

Circulatory collapse at hospital arrival was defined using an operational definition of advanced haemodynamic compromise at hospital arrival before primary PCI. This definition was broadly informed by the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) shock classification,22 but was not intended to be identical to formal SCAI shock staging. Because formal prospective adjudication according to the SCAI classification was not performed in this retrospective study, circulatory collapse was defined as clinically significant systemic hypoperfusion requiring therapeutic intervention at the time of hospital arrival and before primary PCI, fulfilling at least one of the following criteria: (1) persistent hypotension, defined as systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg lasting ≥30 min or requiring vasopressors or inotropes; (2) biochemical and clinical evidence of systemic hypoperfusion, defined as serum lactate ≥4.0 mmol/L along with at least one of the following: altered mental status, oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h) or cold extremities; or (3) initiation of mechanical circulatory support, including intra-aortic balloon pump or venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO), for the treatment of circulatory failure. Prophylactic use of mechanical circulatory support was excluded. The lactate threshold of ≥4.0 mmol/L was selected to identify clinically significant systemic hypoperfusion in this operational endpoint, rather than to reproduce the original SCAI lactate threshold. These criteria were intended to capture clinically overt hypoperfusion requiring immediate therapeutic support and broadly correspond to advanced haemodynamic compromise in the context of acute myocardial infarction, including clinical presentations comparable to Killip class IV.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was circulatory collapse at hospital arrival. The secondary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality. Mortality status was confirmed using hospital medical records or follow-up contact with patients, family members, or referring physicians, when necessary.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median (IQR), as appropriate, according to data distribution. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between pre-admission statin therapy and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival. Three models were constructed. Model 1 was minimally adjusted and included age and sex. Model 2, designated as the primary model, included clinically relevant covariates selected as confounders based on prior knowledge, including age, sex, prior coronary artery disease, symptom-to-arrival time, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitor use. Model 3 was exploratory and included pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, prior literature and the need to avoid model overfitting given the number of outcome events.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to age (<75 vs ≥75 years), sex, renal function (eGFR≥60 vs <60 mL/min/1.73 m²), prior coronary artery disease and symptom-to-arrival time (≤3 vs >3 hours). Interaction terms between statin therapy and each subgroup variable were included to assess potential effect modification.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings. First, a complete-case analysis was performed, excluding patients with missing lactate values. Second, patients with pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest were excluded, and the primary multivariable model was repeated. Third, the primary model was additionally adjusted for culprit lesion location, defined as LAD involvement. Fourth, additional sensitivity analyses were performed with further adjustment for pre-admission beta-blocker and antiplatelet therapy.

To further evaluate the clinical composition of the primary endpoint, the individual components of circulatory collapse were summarised among patients who met the primary endpoint. Because lactate-based classification may be influenced by missing or variably measured lactate values, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed after reclassifying patients who were classified as having circulatory collapse primarily on the basis of lactate ≥4.0 mmol/L with hypoperfusion, but without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest, vasopressor or inotrope use before PCI, intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) for circulatory failure before PCI, or VA-ECMO for circulatory failure before PCI, as not having the strict endpoint.

Because dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and smoking history were markedly imbalanced between groups and may influence the likelihood of receiving statin therapy, an expanded multivariable model was additionally constructed by adding these cardiovascular risk factors to the primary model.

Propensity score analyses were performed to further address potential treatment selection bias inherent to observational studies. The propensity score was estimated using a logistic regression model for the probability of receiving pre-admission statin therapy. The expanded propensity score model included age, sex, prior coronary artery disease, symptom-to-arrival time, eGFR, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitor use, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and smoking history. The estimated propensity score was first included as a covariate in a logistic regression model.

In addition, stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting was performed using the same expanded propensity score model. Stabilised weights were calculated according to pre-admission statin therapy status. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardised mean differences, with values close to or below 0.10 considered to indicate adequate balance. Weighted logistic regression models with robust SEs were used to estimate the association between pre-admission statin therapy and circulatory collapse.

Exploratory analyses were also performed to evaluate available markers of infarct severity, cardiac function and postprocedural reperfusion according to pre-admission statin therapy status. Peak creatine kinase was examined as an exploratory marker of infarct severity, left ventricular ejection fraction as a marker of cardiac function, and achievement of final TIMI flow grade 3 as a postprocedural reperfusion marker. These variables were not included as adjustment covariates in the primary model because they may be influenced by post-presentation clinical course, infarct evolution, reperfusion, sampling frequency or postprocedural factors.

For the secondary outcome of 30-day all-cause mortality, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate HRs, adjusting for age, sex and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival. Covariates included in the Cox regression model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and the number of outcome events to avoid overfitting. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals and was not violated.

Patients with missing key exposure or outcome variables were excluded. There were no missing data for the variables included in the primary multivariable model, except for serum lactate values used in the operational definition of circulatory collapse, which were missing in a small proportion of patients. Therefore, a complete-case sensitivity analysis was performed.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.19 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). SM had full access to all the data in the study and takes responsibility for the integrity and accuracy of the data analysis. All tests were two-sided; significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Study population

The study population comprised 589 consecutive patients with STEMI who underwent emergent coronary angiography at our institution. Of these, 38 patients with symptom-to-arrival times >24 hours were excluded. The remaining 551 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. No patients were excluded because of missing key exposure or outcome variables, although serum lactate values were missing for 48 patients (8.7%). The patient selection flow diagram is shown in figure 1. In the overall cohort, 160 patients (29.0%) received pre-admission statin therapy.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics stratified by pre-admission statin therapy are summarised in table 1. Patients receiving statin therapy were older and had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia and smoking history. They also had a markedly higher prevalence of prior coronary artery disease, with more frequent use of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors, beta-blockers and antiplatelet agents. Renal function was significantly lower in the statin group (mean eGFR 51.6±23.7 vs 61.5±20.5 mL/min/1.73 m², p<0.001). Culprit lesions involving the LAD were less frequent in the statin group than in the non-statin group. Other characteristics, including symptom-to-arrival time, systolic blood pressure, heart rate and prevalence of pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest, were comparable between the two groups.

Primary outcome: circulatory collapse at hospital arrival

Circulatory collapse occurred in 77 patients (14.0%). The incidence of circulatory collapse was significantly lower in patients who received pre-admission statin therapy than in those who did not (7.5% vs 16.6%, p=0.005). In multivariable logistic regression (table 2), pre-admission statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse across all models. In model 1, adjusted for age and sex, statin therapy was associated with significantly lower odds of circulatory collapse (OR 0.40; 95% CI 0.21 to 0.77; p=0.006). In model 2, the primary model, statin therapy remained significantly associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse (OR 0.23; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.50; p<0.001). This association persisted in model 3 after additional adjustment for pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest (OR 0.17; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.41; p<0.001). Among the covariates, lower eGFR was independently associated with circulatory collapse in the primary model, whereas pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest was independently associated with circulatory collapse in the exploratory model. An additional adjustment for culprit lesion location (LAD involvement) in the primary model did not materially change the results (adjusted OR 0.22; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.49; p<0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

The association between pre-admission statin therapy and a lower risk of circulatory collapse remained consistent across sensitivity and propensity score analyses (table 3 and online supplemental tables S1–S9). Pre-admission statin therapy remained significantly associated with lower odds of circulatory collapse after excluding patients with pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.46; p<0.001), in the complete-case analysis excluding patients with missing lactate values (OR 0.22; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.50; p<0.001), and after additional adjustment for pre-admission beta-blocker and antiplatelet therapy (OR 0.25; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.58; p=0.001).

Additional analyses incorporating cardiovascular risk factors

The expanded multivariable model included age, sex, prior coronary artery disease, symptom-to-arrival time, estimated glomerular filtration rate, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitor use, dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and smoking history. The expanded propensity score model included the same variables. Stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting was performed using the expanded propensity score model, and weighted logistic regression with robust SEs was used.

Because dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and smoking history were clinically important determinants of statin prescription and were imbalanced between groups, we performed additional analyses incorporating these cardiovascular risk factors into the adjustment strategy. In the expanded multivariable model, pre-admission statin therapy remained significantly associated with lower odds of circulatory collapse (OR 0.16; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.38; p<0.001). Similarly, the association remained significant in the expanded propensity score-adjusted analysis (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.43; p<0.001) and in the expanded inverse probability of treatment weighting analysis (OR 0.18; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.39; p<0.001).

Among patients with circulatory collapse, most fulfilled clinically overt shock components, including vasopressor or inotrope use before PCI, IABP or VA-ECMO for circulatory failure before PCI or pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest (online supplemental table S5). Only 10 patients (13.0%) were classified primarily on the basis of lactate elevation with hypoperfusion without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest or pharmacologic/mechanical circulatory support. After reclassifying these patients as not having the strict endpoint, pre-admission statin therapy remained significantly associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse in the expanded multivariable model (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.45; p<0.001). Similar findings were observed in the expanded propensity score-adjusted analysis (OR 0.19; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.46; p<0.001) and in the expanded inverse probability of treatment weighting analysis (OR 0.17; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.39; p<0.001) (online supplemental tables S6–S8).

In the inverse probability of treatment weighting analysis, covariate balance was substantially improved after weighting, with standardised differences close to or below 0.10 for all included variables (online supplemental table S7). In exploratory analyses, peak creatine kinase tended to be lower with pre-admission statin therapy, whereas left ventricular ejection fraction and final TIMI flow grade 3 did not differ significantly between groups (online supplemental table S9).

Secondary outcome: 30-day all-cause mortality

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed no significant difference in 30-day all-cause mortality between patients with and without pre-admission statin therapy (online supplemental figure S1; log-rank p=0.52). During the 30-day follow-up, 26 patients died.

In multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis adjusted for age, sex and circulatory collapse at hospital arrival, pre-admission statin therapy was not significantly associated with 30-day all-cause mortality (HR 1.84; 95% CI 0.80 to 4.23; p=0.149). Conversely, circulatory collapse at hospital arrival was strongly associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality (HR 9.93; 95% CI 4.47 to 22.08; p<0.001), whereas age and sex were not independently associated with 30-day mortality. In the multivariable model, circulatory collapse at hospital arrival was the strongest predictor of 30-day mortality.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI, pre-admission statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse at hospital arrival. This association remained consistent after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates and across multiple sensitivity analyses, including complete-case analysis, exclusion of patients with pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest, additional adjustment for background preventive medications, exclusion of lactate-based classification without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest or pharmacological/mechanical circulatory support and inverse probability of treatment weighting. By contrast, pre-admission statin therapy was not significantly associated with 30-day all-cause mortality. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and suggest that chronic statin therapy may be associated with a less severe early haemodynamic phenotype at STEMI presentation.

To the best of our knowledge, the association between pre-admission statin therapy and haemodynamic severity at hospital arrival has not been well established in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI. Cardiogenic shock remains one of the most severe manifestations of acute myocardial infarction and is associated with poor outcomes.4 7 23–25 In the present study, circulatory collapse was operationally defined to capture advanced-stage haemodynamic compromise requiring immediate therapeutic support. Although the incidence of circulatory collapse in this cohort was higher than that reported in prior STEMI studies,1–3 7 26 this likely reflects differences in endpoint definition, inclusion of patients with pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest and the tertiary referral nature of our institution. Importantly, most patients meeting the primary endpoint had clinically overt shock components requiring pharmacologic or mechanical circulatory support, whereas only a small proportion were classified primarily on the basis of lactate elevation with hypoperfusion without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest or pharmacological/mechanical support. The association between pre-admission statin therapy and lower risk of circulatory collapse remained significant even after excluding these patients, supporting the clinical interpretability of the endpoint.

Several biological mechanisms may plausibly underlie the observed association, although causality cannot be inferred from this study. Statins may stabilise atherosclerotic plaques, improve endothelial and microvascular function, and attenuate inflammation, oxidative stress and ischaemia-reperfusion injury.8–13 These effects could theoretically contribute to less severe myocardial injury or haemodynamic compromise at the time of STEMI presentation. In exploratory analyses, peak creatine kinase tended to be lower in patients receiving pre-admission statin therapy, whereas left ventricular ejection fraction and final TIMI flow grade 3 did not differ significantly between groups. Because peak creatine kinase is influenced by infarct evolution, reperfusion, sampling frequency and post-presentation clinical course, these findings should be regarded as descriptive and do not establish a mechanistic pathway. Nevertheless, they provide additional context for interpreting the observed association and highlight the need for future studies incorporating more direct measures of infarct size, myocardial salvage and microvascular obstruction.

A major consideration is residual confounding and healthy user bias. Patients receiving statins had a higher prevalence of prior coronary artery disease and background preventive medication use and also differed in dyslipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and smoking history. Dyslipidaemia and statin use were related but not interchangeable; only half of patients with diagnosed dyslipidaemia were receiving statins before admission, potentially reflecting under-treatment, nonadherence, discontinuation, intolerance or contraindications, differences in healthcare access or practice variation. Renal dysfunction also remained associated with circulatory collapse in the primary model, consistent with the recognised vulnerability of patients with chronic kidney disease to adverse cardiovascular outcomes.27 We therefore performed expanded multivariable, propensity score-adjusted and stabilised inverse probability of treatment weighting analyses incorporating these cardiovascular risk factors. Covariate balance was substantially improved after weighting, with standardised mean differences close to or below 0.10, and the association remained consistent. However, residual confounding from frailty, socioeconomic status, healthcare engagement, medication adherence, lipid levels, plaque morphology or prehospital decision-making cannot be excluded.

The absence of a significant association between pre-admission statin therapy and 30-day mortality should be interpreted cautiously. Mortality after STEMI is multifactorial and may be influenced by infarct size or location, arrhythmic complications, reperfusion success, comorbidities, postprocedural management and events occurring after presentation.4–6 In addition, the limited number of deaths may have reduced statistical power. Although circulatory collapse at hospital arrival was strongly associated with 30-day mortality, pre-admission statin therapy was not significantly associated with mortality. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as an association with early haemodynamic severity at presentation, rather than evidence of a short-term survival benefit. Prospective multicentre studies are needed to validate these findings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-centre observational study conducted at a tertiary cardiovascular centre; therefore, the findings may not be generalisable to broader STEMI populations or institutions with different referral patterns, emergency systems or thresholds for mechanical circulatory support. Because the cohort included only patients who survived to hospital presentation and underwent coronary angiography or primary PCI, selection bias related to prehospital death or cardiac arrest without return of spontaneous circulation cannot be excluded.

Second, residual confounding and healthy user bias remain important limitations. Patients receiving pre-admission statin therapy differed substantially from those not receiving statins, including higher rates of prior coronary artery disease and background preventive medication use. Although multivariable adjustment, additional adjustment for preventive medications, propensity score adjustment and inverse probability of treatment weighting were performed, unmeasured confounding related to frailty, socioeconomic status, healthcare engagement, medication adherence, lipid control, symptom recognition or prehospital decision-making cannot be excluded.

Third, circulatory collapse was retrospectively defined using an operational definition broadly informed by, but not identical to, the SCAI shock classification, rather than prospectively adjudicated formal SCAI shock staging. Thresholds for vasopressor, inotrope or mechanical circulatory support use may vary by clinician and institutional practice. Although endpoint components were summarised and sensitivity analyses excluding lactate-based classification without pre-PCI cardiopulmonary arrest or pharmacologic/mechanical circulatory support yielded consistent results, endpoint misclassification cannot be fully excluded.

Fourth, detailed information on statin type, intensity, treatment duration, adherence and lipid control before STEMI was not consistently available. Medication history was obtained retrospectively, and misclassification of pre-admission medication use cannot be completely excluded.

Fifth, direct measures of infarct size, myocardial salvage and microvascular obstruction, such as cardiac MRI, were not available. Peak creatine kinase, left ventricular ejection fraction and final TIMI flow grade 3 were examined only as exploratory markers and were not included as adjustment covariates because they may be influenced by post-presentation clinical course or postprocedural factors.

Finally, serum lactate values were missing in a small proportion of patients, although complete-case analysis yielded consistent results. The number of 30-day mortality events was limited, and no formal sample size calculation was performed; therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

Pre-admission statin therapy was associated with a lower risk of circulatory collapse at hospital arrival in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI, with consistent findings across sensitivity analyses, including propensity score adjustment, inverse probability of treatment weighting and additional adjustment for background preventive medications. These findings suggest that chronic statin therapy may be associated with a less severe early haemodynamic phenotype at STEMI presentation, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.
