Perioperative Veno-Venous Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation in Non-Elective Thoracic Surgery: A Propensity-Matched Bicentric Comparison With Medical Indication
Authors: Christophe Beyls, Quintana Soulier-Zaninka, Olivier Georges, Maxime Nguyen, Thomas Dheilly, Mollet Nicolas, Pierre Huette, Florence De Dominicis, Osama Abou-Arab, Pierre Grégoire Guinot, Yazine Mahjoub
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPerioperative veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) can be used during non-elective thoracic surgery to manage hypoxaemia and facilitate complex surgical procedures. However, outcome data in this setting remain limited, particularly regarding bleeding and thrombotic risks. We aimed to evaluate whether perioperative VV-ECMO during non-elective thoracic surgery is associated with increased 90-day all-cause mortality compared with VV-ECMO used for medical indications, using a propensity score-matched analysis. Secondary outcomes included major bleeding and thrombotic events.MethodsThis retrospective bi-centre cohort study included adults treated with VV-ECMO for acute respiratory failure between January 2011 and January 2025 in 2 university intensive care units. Patients were categorized according to ECMO indication: perioperative thoracic surgery or medical. A propensity score was built using multivariable logistic regression, followed by 1:4 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement. The primary endpoint was 90-day mortality; secondary endpoints included major bleeding and thrombotic events.ResultsAmong 372 patients, 44 (12%) received perioperative VV-ECMO. Ventilator-associated pneumonia (78% vs 57%, P = .01) and haemothorax (36% vs 2%, P < .001) were more frequent in the perioperative group. Ninety-day mortality did not differ between groups, both before (54% vs 51%, P = .71) and after matching (log-rank P = .95). Multivariable Cox analysis confirmed no association between surgical ECMO and mortality (HR = 1.003, 95% CI [0.64-1.57]). No significant differences were found in major bleeding (48% vs 47%, P = 1.00) or thrombotic events (41% vs 32%, P = .31) between groups.ConclusionsPerioperative VV-ECMO during non-elective thoracic surgery was not associated with increased 90-day mortality, bleeding, or thrombotic complications compared to medical VV-ECMO, both before and after matching. These findings support its use in carefully selected patients.Clinical trial registration numberNCT07016685

INTRODUCTION

Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) has become a cornerstone in the management of severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), with its use having expanded markedly during the COVID-19 pandemic,1,2 thereby accelerating global adoption in intensive care units (ICUs). Survival rates now approach 60%, primarily due to improved patient selection, enhanced team expertise, and refined anticoagulation strategies.3

Beyond ARDS, VV-ECMO is increasingly employed as a perioperative support modality during high-risk thoracic procedures—such as major lung resections, airway reconstructions, and lung transplantation—particularly when conventional ventilation techniques, including single-lung ventilation, prove inadequate.4 In this context, VV-ECMO ensures adequate gas exchange and facilitates safe operative conditions in patients with limited pulmonary reserve or complex airway anatomy. It also represents a valuable solution when conventional airway management interferes with surgical access. For instance, during technically demanding procedures such as tracheal or carinal resections,5,6 VV-ECMO allows reconstruction without the need for an intraluminal endotracheal tube, thereby improving surgical exposure, precision, and overall safety.

However, VV-ECMO carries inherent risks, including bleeding, thrombotic events, and systemic inflammation, which may be exacerbated in perioperative contexts with high haemorrhagic potential.7 In thoracic surgery, it remains particularly challenging to distinguish ECMO-related complications from those associated with the surgical site. Despite its expanding use in this setting, current evidence is largely limited to case reports and small case series.4,8

Furthermore, large databases such as the Euro Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) registry do not report specific data on the use of VV-ECMO in non-elective thoracic surgery, underscoring the need for dedicated studies in this population.9

The main objective of this study was to compare 90-day mortality between patients receiving VV-ECMO for perioperative support during non-elective thoracic surgery and those receiving VV-ECMO for medical indications, with the hypothesis that perioperative patients may face higher mortality due to an increased risk of postoperative bleeding.

This bicentric retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between VV-ECMO used for non-elective thoracic surgery and 90-day all-cause mortality using a propensity score-matched analysis. Secondary endpoints included the incidence of bleeding and thrombotic complications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This is a retrospective, bicentric cohort study that includes patients who required VV-ECMO at Amiens University Hospital and Dijon University Hospital, 2 tertiary care centres in France. All consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) admitted to the cardiac ICUs of these institutions between January 2011 and January 2025 were screened for eligibility.

Patients were included if they had a VV-ECMO run and had complete data available for the primary endpoint (90-day mortality) between January 2011 and January 2025.

The exclusion criteria were:

Previous treatment with veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) before the VV-ECMO run.

Previous treatment with veno-arterial ECMO (VA-ECMO) before the VV-ECMO run.

Elective VV-ECMO was initiated for intraoperative lung support during scheduled thoracic surgery.

Elective VV-ECMO was initiated for intraoperative lung support during scheduled thoracic surgery.

VV-ECMO is implanted in non-thoracic surgery, including cardiac or major abdominal procedures.

VV-ECMO was implanted postoperatively for medical indications.

Patients under guardship.

Patients were included on the day of VV-ECMO implantation and categorized based on the indication for VV-ECMO: perioperative VV-ECMO or VV-ECMO for a medical indication.

Perioperative VV-ECMO was defined as ECMO initiated within 24 hours before or during thoracic surgery performed in a non-elective setting.

VV-ECMO indications

VV-ECMO was initiated for medical indications primarily in cases of refractory hypoxaemia or hypercapnia according to the EOLIA criteria.10 In the perioperative thoracic surgery setting, ECMO was considered in situations of refractory hypoxaemia, for airway management during complex surgical procedures, or to facilitate ventilation during one-lung ventilation. Importantly, these indications were not mutually exclusive, and several patients presented with overlapping criteria, reflecting the complexity and severity of the perioperative context.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Amiens University Hospital (reference: PI2020_843_0026) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07016685). By French regulations governing non-interventional clinical research, written informed consent was waived. However, oral and written information about the study was provided to patients whenever feasible and systematically to their families. This manuscript was prepared in compliance with the STROBE guidelines for retrospective observational studies and with current reporting standards for propensity score matching.11

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality, assessed through a time-to-event analysis from the date of VV-ECMO initiation following non-elective thoracic surgery. Mortality data were collected from electronic medical records and structured follow-up letters.

Secondary outcomes included

Bleeding events were defined by the occurrence of at least one of the following: intracranial haemorrhage, upper or lower gastrointestinal bleeding, peripheral cannulation site bleeding, retroperitoneal bleeding, or pulmonary haemorrhage.

Bleeding events were defined by the occurrence of at least one of the following: intracranial haemorrhage, upper or lower gastrointestinal bleeding, peripheral cannulation site bleeding, retroperitoneal bleeding, or pulmonary haemorrhage.

Thrombotic events were defined by the presence of at least one of the following: ECMO circuit thrombosis (including circuit clotting or oxygenator/pump failure), haemolysis, or ischaemic stroke.

Thrombotic events were defined by the presence of at least one of the following: ECMO circuit thrombosis (including circuit clotting or oxygenator/pump failure), haemolysis, or ischaemic stroke.

Definitions of bleeding and thrombotic events were based on the international ELSO guidelines.7

Data collection

Clinical, biological, VV-ECMO-related, therapeutic, and ICU data were retrospectively collected using a computerized database. The surgical indication and procedure were documented based on the operative report provided by the attending thoracic surgeon.

Data were extracted from local electronic health record systems, including DxCare (Medasys), Diane Réa (Bow Medical, France), Clinisoft (Centricity Critical Care Clinisoft, GE Healthcare), and ICCA (Intelispace Critical Care and Anesthesia, Philips).

Collected variables included demographic data (age, sex, body mass index), medical history, and indications for VV-ECMO. The severity of illness at ICU admission was assessed using the sepsis-related organ failure assessment (SOFA) score.12 The respiratory ECMO survival prediction (RESP) score was calculated at ECMO initiation to estimate survival probability.13

Pre-ECMO data included ventilator settings, arterial blood gas parameters, and laboratory results. The use of rescue therapies, such as prone positioning and inhaled nitric oxide, was also recorded.

During the ICU stay, we documented complications, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, prone positioning under ECMO, and the need for renal replacement therapy. Bleeding and thrombotic events were systematically recorded, based on predefined criteria, and 90-day vital status following ECMO initiation was assessed for all patients.

VV-ECMO management

The VV-ECMO implantation and management protocols used in both centres have been previously described.2,14,15 Peripheral VV-ECMO was initiated in all patients based on established criteria.10 The ECMO circuit consisted of a polymethylpentene hollow-fibre oxygenator, a magnetically levitated centrifugal pump, and heparin-coated tubing. Cannulation was performed percutaneously under ultrasound guidance using the Seldinger technique.

Systemic anticoagulation was achieved using unfractionated heparin and monitored via anti-Xa activity, in line with local centre protocols.

During surgery, respiratory support was primarily provided by VV-ECMO, with adjustments of oxygen fraction and sweep gas flow guided by real-time arterial and venous blood gas analyses. During ongoing VV-ECMO support, a standardized ultra-protective ventilation strategy was applied, targeting tidal volumes below 4 ml/kg of predicted body weight and plateau pressures under 25 cmH2O. ECMO pump flow and sweep gas settings were further titrated according to serial arterial blood gas results.

Literature review methodology

To provide context for our findings and assess existing evidence on the use of VV-ECMO in thoracic surgery, we performed a structured literature review. We conducted our search in PubMed, covering the last 10 years (from January 1, 2014 to April 1, 2025). We used combinations of the following keywords and MeSH terms: “ECMO” or “extracorporeal membrane oxygenation,” “thoracic surgery,” “non-elective surgery,” “trauma,” “chest trauma,” and “bleeding.”

We selected articles that specifically reported clinical outcomes of patients undergoing thoracic surgical procedures with ECMO support, with a focus on non-elective contexts and trauma-related indications. We considered case reports, case series, retrospective cohorts, and prospective studies. Articles that exclusively addressed cardiac surgery, lung transplantation, or elective surgical settings were excluded. The reference list resulting from this review serves as the bibliographic basis for comparative interpretation (see Table S1).4,5,16–23

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as mean ± SD or median [interquartile range] for continuous variables, depending on distribution. Group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was not determined a priori, given the retrospective nature of the study. However, based on the available cohort of 380 patients (40 patients undergoing ECMO for thoracic surgery vs 340 patients undergoing ECMO for medical indications), we estimated that this sample would allow for the detection of a 20% absolute difference in 90-day mortality between groups with 80% power and a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Considering the wide range of mortality reported in the literature (20%-70% for perioperative ECMO and 35%-60% for medical ECMO, depending on the aetiology), the current sample size was considered sufficient to explore potential outcome differences.

Propensity score

Missing values were handled using predictive mean matching imputation. In accordance with Rubin’s rules, missingness was assumed not to be at random. Importantly, there were no missing data for the primary or secondary outcomes. We also assessed the distribution of missingness across the study period and did not identify any relevant temporal variation. For the primary outcome of 90-day all-cause mortality, variables associated with mortality at P < .10 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model. Stepwise selection based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used to select the final model. Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (see Table S2).

A propensity score was derived from the selected variables to estimate the likelihood of receiving VV-ECMO for thoracic surgery. Patients were matched using a 1:4 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD <0.1 considered acceptable (see Table S3).

To account for potential temporal effects, particularly related to the COVID-19 pandemic, we created a binary variable “COVID era,” coded as 0 for cases before January 1, 2020, and 1 for cases thereafter. This variable was included in Cox regression models as a covariate to test whether the study period influenced 90-day mortality. In addition, descriptive analyses of annual ECMO case volume and outcomes were performed to visualize potential temporal trends (Figure 1S).

Flowchart of the Study.

Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. The adjusted association between VV-ECMO for thoracic surgery and 90-day mortality was evaluated using a Cox proportional hazards model in the matched cohort.

As a secondary analysis, we evaluated whether surgical VV-ECMO was associated with an increased risk of bleeding or thrombotic complications. Each outcome was analysed separately using both univariate and multivariable logistic regression models, which included clinically relevant covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

All tests were two-sided, and the threshold for statistical significance was set to P < .05. Statistical analysis was performed using RStudio software for macOS (version 2021.09.1 + 372), and the following packages: “stats,” “MASS,” “car,” “ResourceSelection,” “MatchIt,” “cobalt,” “tableone,” “survival,” “broom,” and “ggplot2.”

RESULTS

During the study period, 414 patients hospitalized at the ICUs of Amiens and Dijon University Hospitals for acute respiratory failure requiring VV-ECMO were screened for inclusion (Figure 1). After exclusion of 6 patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria (2 died during cannulation, 4 were under 18 years of age) and 36 patients excluded due to missing outcomes (n = 3), prior VA-ECMO (n = 8), or scheduled perioperative VV-ECMO for thoracic surgery (n = 10) and VV-ECMO for ARDS following thoracic surgery (n = 15), a total of 372 patients were included in the final analysis. Follow-up was complete for all included patients.

Among them, 328 patients received VV-ECMO for a medical indication, and 44 patients received perioperative VV-ECMO.

Baseline characteristics, surgery indication, and outcomes

Patients receiving perioperative VV-ECMO were more often male (n = 33/40, 82% vs n = 219/340, 64%; P = .03) and had a lower body mass index (26.9 [23.9-30.0] vs 30.0 [20.0-35.5] kg/m2, P = .006) (Tables 1-3). Smoking history (n = 23/44, 53% vs n = 88/328; 27%; P = .001) and solid cancer (n = 12/44; 27% vs n = 40/328; 12%; P = .01) were more frequent in this group.

Pre-ECMO-prone positioning (n = 15/44; 34% vs n = 194/328; 60%; P = .003) and neuromuscular blockade (n = 39/44; 88% vs n = 323/328; 98%; P = .003) were less common in the perioperative group (Table 1).

Indications for non-elective thoracic surgery under VV-ECMO support included thoracic trauma (69%), lung abscess (16%), haemoptysis (7%), and complex tracheobronchial injuries (9%) (Table 2). Reoperations were required in 18 patients (41%), primarily for surgical clot evacuation and repair of bronchial fistulas.

During ECMO, ventilator-associated pneumonia (n = 33/44; 78% vs n = 174/328; 57%; P = .01) and haemothorax (n = 16/44; 36% vs n = 5/328; 2%; P < .001) were more frequent in the perioperative group. Bleeding and thrombotic events

In multivariable analysis, prone positioning during ECMO support was independently associated with an increased risk of bleeding events (adjusted OR 2.10, 95% CI [1.32-3.38], P = .002), while ventilator-associated pneumonia was strongly associated with thrombotic complications (adjusted OR 3.75, 95% CI [2.24-6.47], P < .001) (see Supplementary Files Table). Perioperative VV-ECMO for thoracic surgery was not significantly associated with either bleeding or thrombotic events (P > .05 for both).

Propensity score

To limit baseline confounding, a 1:4 propensity score matching was performed based on clinically relevant covariates selected through multivariate logistic regression. The balance between groups improved significantly after matching, as indicated by the reduction in SMDs (all SMDs < 0.12), which was confirmed graphically by the Love plot (Figure 3A). In the matched cohort (n = 220), 90-day survival was similar between patients with perioperative VV-ECMO and those with medical indications (n = 24/44; 54% vs n = 93/176; 52%; log-rank P = .95), as illustrated by the Kaplan-Meier survival curve (Figure 3B). This result was confirmed in a Cox proportional hazards model, in which perioperative VV-ECMO was not independently associated with 90-day mortality (HR = 1.003, 95% CI [0.64-1.57], P = .99).

Sensitivity analysis across the COVID era

When adjusting for the pre- vs post-COVID era, the association between perioperative VV-ECMO and 90-day mortality remained unchanged (HR 1.03, 95% CI 0.65-1.61, P = .91), with no significant temporal interaction effect. Similarly, including the COVID era as a covariate in the Cox model did not materially alter the estimates (≤10% change). The annual distribution of VV-ECMO cases and corresponding 90-day outcomes is displayed in Figure 1S. No consistent temporal trend in mortality was observed across the study period, including during the COVID-19 outbreak years.

DISCUSSION

Our retrospective study of 372 patients supported with VV-ECMO specifically aimed to compare outcomes between perioperative support for non-elective thoracic surgery and medical indications. The primary endpoint, 90-day mortality, was not significantly different between groups, both before and after propensity score matching. Secondary endpoints, including bleeding and thrombotic complications, also did not differ significantly between perioperative and medical patients.

VV-ECMO is inherently prothrombotic and prohaemorrhagic,24 which raises safety concerns when used during non-cardiovascular surgery.8 However, our data suggest that even in the context of non-elective thoracic procedures—including major trauma—VV-ECMO does not increase the risk of death or bleeding. This challenges the common assumption that ECMO inherently worsens surgical outcomes, supporting its feasibility in appropriately selected candidates.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort to date assessing outcomes of perioperative VV-ECMO during non-elective thoracic surgery, addressing a significant gap not covered in large registries such as EuroELSO.

Trauma-induced thoracic surgery and ECMO

In this high-risk setting—characterized by hypoxaemia and haemorrhage—VV-ECMO enables respiratory stabilization and facilitates complex surgical procedures,20 including anatomical lung resections.4 Once considered contraindicated in haemorrhagic shock, ECMO is now more viable due to improvements in circuit biocompatibility, heparin-sparing strategies,25 and increased clinical expertise. VV-ECMO may contribute to the reversal of trauma-induced coagulopathy by improving physiological parameters such as hypoxia, acidosis, and hypothermia.

In our cohort, major thoracic trauma was the leading indication for perioperative VV-ECMO, especially for managing active haemothorax. Thoracic trauma accounts for 25%-50% of trauma-related deaths, with outcomes influenced by injury severity and bleeding control.26,27 Despite this burden, the literature predominantly focuses on post-traumatic ARDS and provides limited data on intraoperative ECMO. A recent multicentre French study (Traumabase) reported that 47% of VV-ECMO patients had severe chest trauma (n = 68/143) without specifying intraoperative use.28

In our cohort, 50% of patients (n = 15/30) underwent surgical control of haemothorax due to major chest trauma, primarily through intercostal artery ligation. Pulmonary resection for penetrating thoracic injuries was performed in 40% (n = 12/30), while 16% of patients underwent surgery for lung abscess. Redo surgery was necessary in 44% (n = 18/44), mainly for surgical clot evacuation. These indications align with those reported by Sommerauer et al, who described a series of 29 patients undergoing non-elective thoracic surgery under VV-ECMO, with 45% operated on for haemothorax and 17% for lung abscess, and a 52% rate of redo surgery (n = 15/29).

These findings highlight the diversity and complexity of surgical indications in trauma patients requiring VV-ECMO, particularly in cases involving Haemorrhage and parenchymal injury.

Mortality outcomes

VV-ECMO mortality varies by indication. In the EuroELSO registry, mortality for medical VV-ECMO indications reached 59.7%.3 In the Traumabase registry, mortality among trauma patients requiring VV-ECMO was 38.3% (n = 22/68).28 However, neither study reported outcomes for patients undergoing major thoracic surgery.

In our study, the 90-day mortality rates were similar between perioperative and medical VV-ECMO patients (54% vs 51%; P = .71). These findings are consistent with the largest published cohort to date evaluating VV-ECMO in thoracic surgery (n = 102), in which the in-hospital mortality was 44.8% (n = 29/102).19 Besides, perioperative VV-ECMO was not associated with increased mortality in either unadjusted analysis (OR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.18-1.64; P = .28) or after propensity score matching (HR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.64-1.57; P = .99).

Overall, the existing literature remains sparse and heterogeneous. Most studies involved <30 patients and reported mortality rates ranging from 14% to 57%,19,28 with limited information on intraoperative ECMO use. Notably, the most significant prior cohort, reported by Sommerauer et al (n = 29), indicated 45% haemothorax rates and a 44.8% mortality rate. To date, no studies have applied propensity score matching or directly compared surgical vs medical indications for VV-ECMO. These limitations underscore the need for more robust, multicentre evaluations—such as the present study. Despite the overall high mortality rates, our findings indicate that non-elective thoracic surgery performed under VV-ECMO does not elevate mortality risk in appropriately selected patients.

Even in situations with significant haemorrhagic potential, VV-ECMO should remain an available option when predicted outcomes justify the intervention (eg, a RESP score of ∼1, predicting a mortality rate of ∼43%).

Bleeding, thrombosis, and perioperative VV-ECMO

Bleeding and thrombotic complications are common during VV-ECMO due to anticoagulation, inflammation, and circuit-related factors.29 In our study, bleeding occurred in 48% of surgical patients compared to 47% in non-surgical patients (P = 1.00), and thrombosis occurred in 41% vs 32% (P = .31), despite 41% of surgical patients undergoing reoperation. Additionally, in multivariable analyses, perioperative VV-ECMO was not found to be associated with an increased risk of bleeding or thrombosis contrary to prone positioning (OR: 2.10; 95% CI: 1.32-3.38; P = .002) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (OR: 3.75; 95% CI: 2.24-6.47; P = .001), which were significantly linked to bleeding and thrombosis events.

These findings are consistent with EuroELSO data, which indicate that 40.2% of patients experienced such events, with bleeding accounting for 46%.7 Cannulation site bleeding remains the most frequent complication29 during prone positioning,30 occurring in up to 10% of cases. In our cohort, prone positioning and peripheral cannulation were more frequent in non-surgical patients (34% vs 11%, P = .003 and 28% vs 11%, P = .03), which may have contributed to the observed bleeding profile.

The high mortality observed in both surgical and medical VV-ECMO patients is consistent with previous reports and reflects the severity of this population. Perioperative VV-ECMO, although rare, should be confined to high-volume expert centres with multidisciplinary teams able to anticipate perioperative challenges. Our findings indicate that non-elective thoracic surgery under VV-ECMO does not intrinsically increase haemostatic complications when appropriately managed, and a high bleeding risk should not be considered an absolute contraindication but rather a matter for careful ECMO team discussion.

Limitations

We acknowledge several limitations inherent in the retrospective design of our study. Although a 1:4 propensity score matching was applied to reduce baseline confounding, the risk of residual confounding remains. Some key prognostic factors may not have been fully identified, particularly those influencing the decision to initiate surgery and VV-ECMO, which often depends on clinical judgement. Therefore, selection bias is likely, especially in patients deemed eligible for both surgery and ECMO—such as those with major trauma but without severe concomitant injuries like traumatic brain injury.

Second, anticoagulation management was not standardized across the study population. Although anticoagulation was monitored using anti-Xa activity, detailed data on anticoagulation intensity, temporal variations, or deviations from local protocols were not systematically recorded. Consequently, we were unable to explore the relationship between the anticoagulation strategy and bleeding or thrombotic events, even though anti-Xa levels do not appear to be a reliable predictor of haemorrhagic risk in this context.31

Moreover, while we reported the occurrence of major bleeding and thrombotic complications based on ELSO definitions, the grading of bleeding severity and its precise timing (eg, early vs late onset) were not available. This limits our ability to characterize the clinical impact and temporal dynamics of these complications.

Third, some relevant clinical outcomes—such as long-term respiratory function, ECMO-related complications beyond 90 days, and quality of life32—were not assessed. Additionally, cause-specific mortality was not systematically available.

Fourth, because of the limited number of patients in the surgical ECMO group (n = 44), we were unable to conduct sensitivity analyses across the various medical indications and surgical subtypes. While this limits our ability to detect potential differential effects, it is essential to highlight that our study represents the largest existing cohort to date evaluating VV-ECMO in the context of non-elective thoracic surgery. Furthermore, we acknowledge that our study lacks a comparator group of patients undergoing non-elective thoracic surgery without ECMO support. In real-life practice, such patients often deteriorate rapidly and die before data can be collected, making the constitution of a meaningful “surgery without ECMO” group unrealistic and highly biased.

Finally, the bicentric nature of the study may limit external validity, as surgical practices, perioperative management, and ECMO protocols may differ across institutions.

CONCLUSION

In non-elective thoracic surgery, VV-ECMO provides sufficient respiratory support, facilitating complex procedures in patients with severe hypoxaemia or airway compromise. In this cohort, the 90-day mortality rate was 54%, which is comparable to that of VV-ECMO for medical indications, with no excess bleeding or thrombotic events.

These results demonstrate the feasibility and safety of perioperative VV-ECMO in selected high-risk patients. Larger prospective studies and international registry data are necessary to better define its role in this context.
