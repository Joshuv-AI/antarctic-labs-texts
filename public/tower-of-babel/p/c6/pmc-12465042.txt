Primary Ewing’s sarcoma of the vagina: A rare case report and literature review
Authors: Die Fan, Xiaoxia Jiang, Lin Wu, Yujie Hao, Chengbin Lu, Zheng Li
Published: 2025

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma family tumors (ESFT) refer to a group of small round cell neoplasms, which includes Ewing sarcoma, primitive neuroectodermal tumor (PNET), bone PNET, and extraosseous soft tissue Ewing sarcoma.Primary extraosseous ES/pPNET (EES) is uncommon, and the most common sites of extraosseous occurrence are the chest wall, lower extremities, and paravertebral regions. However, it is rarely reported in the female genital tract, with some tumors occurring in the vulva, vagina, cervix, uterine corpus, broad ligament, and ovaries (Applebaum, Worch, Matthayet al, 2011).

The histopathological features of Ewing's sarcoma (ES) typically present as a singular cluster of small round cells, while the molecular hallmark of this tumor is the fusion gene EWS/FLI, In the context of diagnosis, PET-CT is valuable for assessing the initial staging of the disease and for detecting metastatic lesions. For the treatment of extraosseous Ewing sarcoma (Juurikka et al., 2019), the therapeutic approach employed is consistent with that of classical Ewing sarcoma of bone, incorporating a multimodal strategy that includes induction chemotherapy, local therapy (surgery and radiotherapy), and consolidation chemotherapy. The most commonly utilized chemotherapy regimens for Ewing's sarcoma involve the alternating administration of VDC (vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide) and IE (ifosfamide and etoposide).This study not only presents a comprehensive analysis of a rare clinical case, but also provides significant clinical and pathological evidence for the diagnosis of Ewing sarcoma/peripheral neuroectodermal tumor (ES/PNET). Furthermore, it investigates the tumor's treatment strategies and molecular biological features, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of its pathogenesis, therapeutic approaches, and prognosis.

Case presentation

The patient, a 35-year-old woman, presented to a local community hospital with a painless vaginal mass. She reported regular menstrual cycles and had no prior history of surgery or trauma. Laboratory tests revealed no abnormalities. Gynecological examination identified a tumor located on the posterior vaginal wall; the lesion was firm, non-tender, fixed in position, and measured 2.5 × 2 × 0.9 cm. The uterus was aligned with the mass, and no abnormalities were detected in the cervix or vaginal fornices. The patient subsequently underwent surgical excision of the mass at a local hospital. Macroscopic examination of the resected specimen revealed a grayish-yellow, soft-tissue mass with an irregular contour. Histologically, the neoplastic cells were uniform small round cells with fine chromatin and a high frequency of mitotic activity. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was negative for CK, CgA, CD56, SYN, CGA, S-100, SOX-10, HMB45, desmin, MyoD1, myogenin, LCA, and WT1, while positive for CD99, NKX2.2, and vimentin (Fig. 1). Further immunohistochemical analysis at Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Fudan University confirmed positive Fli-1 staining, a Ki-67 proliferation index of 60%, and negative AE1/AE3. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified an EWSR1-FLI1 gene fusion. Consequently, the patient was diagnosed with vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma. Due to economic and geographical limitations, combined with the fact that our hospital is the sole tertiary referral center specializing in oncology in Yunnan Province and possesses the necessary diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, the patient presented to our institution for clinical evaluation on April 10, 2024. Serum tumor markers, including CA125, HE4, CA153, NSE, SCC, CEA, and AFP, were all within normal limits. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed no evidence of systemic or local disease spread (see Fig. 2). Following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion, and taking into account the patient’s young age and absence of metastatic findings on PET-CT, further surgical intervention was deemed unnecessary. A comprehensive treatment strategy incorporating both radiotherapy and chemotherapy was therefore recommended. The patient subsequently underwent volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) with a total radiation dose of 50Gy, delivered in 25 fractions of 2Gy each. In addition, she completed five cycles of alternating VDC and IE chemotherapy regimens. The treatment protocol was as follows: vincristine 2mg administered via intravenous injection on day 1; doxorubicin 50mg given as a continuous intravenous infusion over 6 h on days 1 and 2; cyclophosphamide 1.8g intravenous injection on day 1, accompanied by mesna 0.6g intravenous injection at 0, 4, and 8 h post-cyclophosphamide administration; ifosfamide 3g administered via intravenous infusion on days 1 through 5; and etoposide 150mg delivered via intravenous infusion on days 1 through 5. The regimen was repeated every 3 weeks (q3w). During the second cycle, the patient developed grade II myelosuppression. She declined further treatment following the completion of the fifth cycle. Literature review

Vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma is a notably infrequent affliction, with only 22 cases delineated across 21 articles in medical literature, in addition to the present case (Table 1). The age distribution of these patients spans from 13 months to 54 years, encompassing one individual under 10 years of age, three within the 10–19 year range, two between 20–29 years, nine between 30–39 years, four between 40–49 years, and four older than 50 years; the median age at diagnosis is 35 years, with the 30–39 year age bracket comprising approximately 40% of the cohort. The smallest tumor diameter noted is 3 cm, whilst the largest extends to 13 cm. Patients with vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma typically present with vague clinical signs, most commonly abdominal distension and palpable pelvic masses upon physical examination. A minority of patients may also exhibit fever, nocturnal perspiration, weight loss, and urinary retention, which can obscure the differential diagnosis from other pelvic neoplasms. From the literature, 18 cases (78.3%) were presented with a vaginal mass or a prolapsed mass at the vaginal introitus, 8 cases (34.8%) had abnormal vaginal bleeding, another 5 cases (21.7%) experienced vaginal discharge, and 2 cases (8.70%) had symptoms of menorrhagia and urinary retention. Moreover, atypical presentations included painless vaginal masses and vaginal discharge. Our patient was presented with a painless vaginal mass. The tumor characteristics showed that 12 cases (52.2%) were solid masses, 7 cases (30.4%) were cystic solid tumors, and the tumor nature was unspecified in 4 cases (17.4%).Out of the 20 patients discussed, 13 remain disease-free (56.5%), the prognosis for 3 patients (13.0%) is unknown, and 2 patients (8.7%) have deceased. In this cohort, 6 patients (26.1%) developed metastases, with 3 exhibiting metastatic disease at initial treatment. One patient had pelvic and inguinal lymph node metastases at diagnosis and succumbed to pelvic tumor recurrence; another had pelvic bone metastases at diagnosis and passed away 22 months post-treatment; and the third had liver, breast, and lung metastases at initial treatment with progression post-treatment, but the exact prognosis remains undetermined. Additionally, three patients developed new metastases post-treatment: a 28-year-old developed a right frontal-parietal skull metastasis post-surgery and radiotherapy, subsequently underwent resection and radiotherapy to the cranium, and completed 12 cycles of vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide chemotherapy. This patient was alive after 18 months of follow-up. (Yip et al., 2009). A 12-year-old patient who, following chemotherapy treatment with vincristine, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, doxorubicin, and etoposide, developed metastatic lung disease and a left frontal-parietal brain metastasis (Cross et al, 2017). The precise prognosis for this patient remains undisclosed.A further case involved a 53-year-old woman who received only 1 cycle of chemotherapy with vincristine, adriamycin, cyclophosphamide, isocyclophosphamide, and etoposide because of a high toxicity response to chemotherapy was reviewed 4 months later for right cavernous sinus metastasis and bone marrow metastasis (Ganguly, Abbagoni and Narmala, 2024), and specific prognostic information has not been reported.

Discussion

In this case report, we delineate the most diminutive case of vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma recorded thus far. Imaging is fundamental in the diagnosis and staging of Ewing’s sarcoma. Regrettably, in this instance, pertinent imaging evaluations were not performed preoperatively, which may be attributed to frequent misdiagnoses such as a vestibular gland cyst or lipoma prior to surgery, leading to a lack of specific focus. Post-surgical imaging involved a positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan, which detected no evidence of widespread metastatic disease.

The diagnosis of Ewing’s sarcoma is principally contingent upon histopathological examination. Under the microscope, the patient’s cells are observed to be small, round, and uniform in shape, with closely arranged nuclei. No characteristic rosette sign was observed. Literature reports indicate that six patients were presented with characteristic Homer-Wright rosette-like structures or pseudo-rosettes. IHC demonstrated that nearly all cancer cells exhibited characteristic membranous CD99 positivity (22/23; 96%), nuclear FLI-1 positivity in 35% of cases (7/23;30.4%), and NKX2.2 nuclear positivity in 10% of cases (2/23;8.7%). Additionally, most tumor cells expressed Vimentin (VIM) with a positivity rate of 55% (11/23), and a few showed ERG expression. Neuroendocrine markers, such as S-100, were present in varying degrees, and abnormal expressions of cytokeratin (CK) and desmin were noted. However, WT1, myogenin, and MyoD1 were absent. In our patient, IHC revealed tumor cell expression of the characteristic markers CD99, NKX2.2, and FLI1, while the following markers were negative: CK (−), CD10 (−), LCA (−), MyoD1 (−), desmin (−), and myogenin (−).

Ewing sarcoma is characterized by relatively specific molecular genetic features, including chromosomal translocations that lead to the formation of chimeric fusion proteins. These fusion proteins play a central role in driving tumor initiation and progression. The most notable involve members of the ETS (Erythroblast transformation-specific) transcription factor family, such as ERG, FLI1, ETV1, ETV4, and FEV, which fuse with members of the FET gene family, including EWSR1, FUS, and TAF15.The ETS family contributes DNA-binding domains, whereas the FET family supplies domains responsible for transcriptional activation. The activation of these components occurs through complex transcriptional mechanisms that involve both gene activation and repression (de Alava and Gerald, 2000), ultimately resulting in the dysregulation of genes associated with tumorigenesis. The most prevalent genetic alteration in Ewing sarcoma is the EWSR1-FLI1 gene fusion (Fig. 3.), which arises from the t(11;22)(q24;q12) translocation. This translocation fuses the 5′ end of exon 7 of EWSR1 with either the 3′ end of exon 6 (type I) or exon 5 (type II) of FLI1, leading to the formation of the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion gene and its corresponding fusion protein. This genetic event occurs in approximately 85% of cases and is associated with a relatively favorable clinical prognosis. (Patócs et al., 2013).In the remaining approximately 15% of cases, EWS plays a critical role in cellular transformation, with its fusion partners influencing cell type specificity and tumor phenotypic characteristics. In clinical settings, when confirmation of the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion is the sole objective, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) are typically the methods of choice. However, these two techniques are limited by their inability to identify the fusion protein directly. For comprehensive genomic analysis, NGS is employed. Among the 23 patients studied, the EWSR1-FLI1 gene fusion was identified in 6 (26%) using FISH, in another 4 (17%) through RT-PCR, and in 3 (13%) who NGS. The detection of the EWSR1-FLI1 gene fusion was consistent between the RT-PCR and NGS methods, further confirming its status as the most prevalent fusion in these patients.

Given the rarity of extraosseous systemic Ewing’s tumors, there are limited guidelines for their adjuvant treatment. Both the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) recommend that extraosseous Ewing’s sarcoma (EES) should receive the same treatment regimens as other members of the Ewing sarcoma family (Gerrand, Amary, Anwaret al, 2025). Characterized by high aggressiveness and a propensity for distant metastasis, Ewing’s sarcoma is typically managed as a systemic disease with a treatment strategy that includes neoadjuvant chemotherapy, local therapy (surgery or radiotherapy), and adjuvant chemotherapy.

This case report examines vaginal Ewing's sarcoma, highlighting surgical approaches combined with adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy to guide optimal treatment strategies. Preoperative multidrug chemotherapy (≥9 weeks) is advised to downstage tumors and improve complete resection rates with negative margins. Chemotherapy elevates localized Ewing’s sarcoma cure rates from ∼10% to 70%.. (Balamuth and Womer, 2010).In a phase III clinical trial (INT-0091) conducted by the Pediatric Oncology Group–Children's Cancer Group (POG-CCG), the efficacy of the VACD/IE regimen was compared with that of the VACD regimen. The results demonstrated that the 5-year event-free survival rate in the VACD/IE group was significantly higher than that in the VACD group alone (69% vs. 54%). Moreover, the 5-year overall survival rate was also significantly improved in the VACD/IE group (72% vs. 61%), further supporting the superior therapeutic efficacy of the VDC/IE regimen. Based on these findings, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) now recommends the alternating use of the VDC + I/E treatment protocol as the standard of care for Ewing's sarcoma.

Among 23 vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma cases, 20 (87.0%) received chemotherapy, supporting adjuvant chemotherapy for all extraosseous Ewing sarcoma (EES) cases. Radiotherapy is effective for pelvic/coccygeal tumors where surgery risks incomplete margins (Houdek et al., 2023). The EICESS-92 trial showed whole-lung irradiation improves 5-year survival by 12% in metastatic lung cases. For recurrent/refracto tumors, topotecan/irinotecan with cyclophosphamide/temozolomide achieves favorable responses. A phase 1 trial (NCT02657005) demonstrated YK-4–279 blocks EWS/FLI-RHA binding to suppress tumors and enhance treatment sensitivity, though limited by bioavailability and resistance (Conn et al., 2020).

The CABONE phase 2 trial evaluated cabozantinib (VEGFR2/MET inhibitor) in recurrent Ewing’s sarcoma, yielding a 28% response rate and 24% 6-month PFS (Italiano et al., 2020). A Phase 1/2 trial (NCT04239092) demonstrated antitumor activity of Elraglusib combined with cyclophosphamide/topotecan in pediatric relapsed/refractory cases, including complete tumor resolution (2 patients) and a 933-day survival (1 patient) (Raiser et al., 2024). NCCN guidelines advise prioritizing clinical trial enrollment for recurrent/refractory Ewing’s sarcoma (Biermann et al, 2025).

We present a case involving a 35-year-old patient with primary vaginal Ewing’s sarcoma, Admittedly, this study has its limitations. First, the sample size was limited to a single patient, which restricts the generalizability of our findings. Second, the complex etiology and pathogenesis of the disease remain unclear, leaving uncertainties regarding the prognosis and recurrence patterns within this population. Consequently, future research with larger and more diverse sample sizes is essential to overcome these limitations and to deepen our understanding of the disease.

Conclusion

Vaginal Ewing's sarcoma is an exceedingly rare malignancy that predominantly affects young and middle-aged women, with the highest incidence observed in the 30–39 age group. Clinical presentations are often nonspecific, typically including painless vaginal masses, abdominal distension, or vaginal bleeding. Pathologically, most tumors are solid and exhibit varying degrees of necrosis and hemorrhage, while cystic variants occur in a minority of cases. The small size of the lesion in this case highlights the risk of being overlooked or misdiagnosed, underscoring the importance of early imaging combined with histopathological evaluation for unexplained vaginal masses. Molecular diagnosis, particularly the detection of characteristic gene fusions such as EWSR1-FLI1, plays a pivotal role in confirming the disease. Given its rarity, early pathological diagnosis and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential for timely detection and optimal treatment planning. A review of previously reported cases indicates that the primary treatment approach involves surgical resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Patients diagnosed at an early stage generally have a favorable prognosis. For recurrent or refractory cases, therapeutic strategies should focus on refining chemotherapy regimens and exploring novel targeted therapies.
