A comprehensive survey of artificial intelligence methods for cardiovascular disease detection: Recent advances and future challenges
Authors: Anita Gunjal, T Judgi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal health is increasingly concerned with and interested in Cardiovascular Diseases (CVD), which necessitates new and innovative ways of identifying them earlier and treating them more effectively. AI techniques, such as ML and DL provide a promising pathway to address these challenges. This study investigated the interplay between advancing technology and medical science, focusing on AI’s application in improving CVD diagnosis. Traditionally, CVD diagnosis has relied on clinical assessments, laboratory tests, and imaging modalities such as echocardiography and angiography. Several researchers are using online datasets as well as utilizing inexpensive sensors to collect data in the healthcare field to carry out their research to develop different ML and DL algorithms that can detect diseases automatically. Feature-based ML algorithms, CNNs, RNNs, and hybrid models are commonly used techniques in this area. This study highlights the importance of ML and DL in cardiac health and emphasizes precise and enhanced prediction of cardiovascular disease. The developments in state-of-art technologies and the increasing influence of cardiovascular disease on public health, this study attempts to present an in-depth analysis of the topic based on current AI-based methods used for CVD management based on reports from Electronic Health Records (EHR) and Electrocardiogram (ECG). It shows areas which requires improvement, and proposes avenues for future investigation. This study aims to direct future advancements in diagnostic tools by highlighting the critical role of AI in rethinking methods to CVD diagnosis and treatment approaches to enhance the patient outcomes.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cardiovascular disease, Deep learning, Healthcare, Machine learning, Disease diagnosis

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD), commonly referred to as heart disease, encompasses a wide range of conditions impacting the heart and blood vessels. These conditions are worsened by risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, and poor lifestyle choices [1]. It persists as a primary cause of morbidity and mortality globally, posing a significant public health challenge. It imposes a substantial worldwide health burden, as stated by the World Health Organization (WHO) anticipating 17.9 million deaths related to CVDs in 2019 [2]. CVD is regarded as one of the major public health concern that affects millions of people worldwide. Nevertheless, in the face of these difficulties, technology has emerged as a vital partner for transforming healthcare delivery and monitoring [3]. Healthcare professionals can enhance clinical workflows, boost diagnostic confidence, and provide more accurate, patient centered treatment by utilising AI-driven decision support tools. Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) techniques exhibit unparalleled capabilities in handling large-scale, multi-modal datasets, including medical imaging, Electronic Health Records (EHRs), and genomic information. Making use of the power of these advanced computational methods holds the promise of enhancing diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification, thus it will be helpful to medical practitioners for taking informed decisions and deciding strategic treatments to individual patient profiles. Moreover, the integration of ML and DL algorithms into clinical practice has the capacity to transform traditional approaches to CVD detection [4]. By leveraging predictive modelling and pattern recognition, these techniques can help in the early detection of cardiovascular abnormalities, facilitating timely intervention and preventive measures. Furthermore, the ability to extract meaningful insights from intricate data sources can drive the development of personalized medicine approaches, optimizing patient care and outcomes. In addition to highlighting the transformative impact of ML and DL in CVD detection, this review attempts to identify key challenges and opportunities for future research. By critically evaluating existing methodologies and gaps in knowledge, we endeavour to present the way for the creation of more reliable and comprehensible models, fostering innovation and collaboration within the scientific community.

It is important to understand the various forms of cardiac disease for effective prevention, diagnosis, and manage health conditions. Findings from a study indicate that heart disease is associated with a variety of symptoms, including limb shivers, tightness in the chest, discomfort, pressure, shortness of breath, neck pain, abdominal pain, tachycardia, and dizziness. Coronary Artery Disease (CAD), Heart Failure, Cardiomyopathy, and Arrhythmias are the most common disorders associated with heart disease [5]. The most prevalent kind of heart illness, CAD, is brought on by a build-up of plaque that narrows or blocks the coronary arteries, which provide the heart muscle with oxygen-rich blood. Congestive heart failure, another name for heart failure, is the result of the heart's inability to pump enough blood to meet the body's demands. Cardiomyopathy refers to diseases of the heart muscle, characterized by structural and functional abnormalities that impair its pumping ability. Arrhythmias are abnormal heart rhythms that occur when the electrical impulses that regulate heartbeats do not function properly. Congenital heart disease involves structural defects existing from birth, impacting the heart's major blood vessels, valves, or chambers.

Moreover, the exponential growth of healthcare data offers both benefits and obstacles for healthcare organizations. The proliferation of EHRs, medical imaging studies, wearable sensors, and genomic sequencing data has created vast repositories of information that, if effectively analyzed and interpreted, can yield valuable insights for improving patient outcomes and population health. AI-powered analytics platforms can unlock the latent potential of these data assets, enabling predictive modeling, real-time monitoring, and evidence-based making decision across the care continuum [6]. Imaging techniques play important role in visualizing cardiac structure and function, aiding in the diagnosis of various CVDs. Techniques like echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and nuclear imaging provide valuable insights into cardiac morphology, myocardial perfusion, and vascular anatomy. These non-invasive imaging modalities are indispensable for assessing cardiac function, identifying structural abnormalities, and guiding therapeutic interventions [7].

Now a days users can actively manage their health and remotely access medical support as various gadgets such as smartphones, computers, and tablets are connected to sensors [[8], [9], [10]]. The principles of 5P-Medicine, which encourages the development of diverse technologies to enhance healthcare systems, are consistent with the trend toward patient autonomy and empowerment [11,12]. Within the field of CVDs, Electrocardiography (ECG) sensors are promising because of their cost and accessibility, and they serve as valuable instruments for enhancing existing diagnostic approaches [[13], [14], [15]]. Moreover, the incorporation of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into healthcare systems helps in disease diagnosis by evaluating sensor data to discover anomalous patterns suggestive of various disorders [16]. Although AI applications in healthcare are still growing, they have immense potential for advancing customized medicine and patient centered care. However, fulfilling this potential requires standardized and safe data-sharing protocols to overcome regulatory frameworks like the General Data Protection Regulation [17,18]. By providing remote monitoring and individualized interventions, ECG data play a vital role in early disease identification and tailored treatment techniques, particularly among continuing healthcare difficulties, including the COVID-19 pandemic [19,20].

Classification of various cardiovascular illnesses involves discrete traits. For example, heart disease diagnosis can be illustrated using different approaches such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) [21] this will lead to quick diagnosis. Reference [22] presents ECG wave detection and feature extraction strategy using Discrete Wavelet transform is represented. Also, the probabilistic neural network (PNN) is done here to determine which category it falls under. On top of that, adaptive filters and rational transfer functions are employed for extracting heartbeats from a one-lead ECG [23]. DL methods find widespread applications in activities such as image verification, object classification, and voice recognition because of their capacity to automate and boost precision. Current research focuses on unsupervised learning and recognition systems, and DL techniques to develop multistage architectures. One example is the application of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for ECG-based cardiac disease diagnosis, where automated feature learning and insight extraction from ECG datasets have shown enhanced accuracy [24].

Various studies have suggested DL models for ECG-based illness classification. For instance, a 2D CNN model using Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) spectrograms was recommended in [25], while a Grasshopper Optimization Algorithm-enhanced CNN model was proposed in [26]. Additionally, novel techniques such as the 5 L-CNN architecture [27] and 18C—CNN model [28] have been developed to use ECG data for automatic disease detection. Despite the popularity of DL methodologies, issues persist in utilizing ECG for reliable heart disease identification owing to the loss of unique cardiac wave properties when directly applying CNNs to raw ECG data [29].

Motivation and paper contributions

CVD is among the main causes of mortality worldwide, thus creating an overwhelming pressure on health services and individual lives. Although medical science has advanced, early detection and precise diagnosis of cardiovascular conditions nevertheless present difficulties and remain paramount for efficient intervention and improved patient outcomes. However, the increasing importance of ML and DL techniques has offered promising avenues to revolutionize the landscape of CVD detection. This review aims to explore and consolidate the recent advancements in ML and DL methodologies applied to CVD detection. By synthesizing existing research, we seek to present a thorough understanding of the innovative methods, their strengths, limitations, and potential directions for future. Understanding the potential of ML and DL in CVD detection is crucial for several reasons. Firstly, these techniques offer the ability to analyse enormous volumes of diverse data, containing medical images, genomic data, EHRs, and physiological signals, facilitating the creation of highly precise prediction models. Secondly, the early detection of cardiovascular abnormalities can enable timely interventions, which will enhance patient outcomes and lower medical expenses. Moreover, personalized risk assessment and treatment strategies tailored to individual patient profiles can be realized through the integration of advanced ML and DL algorithms.

Furthermore, by critically examining the existing literature, this review attempts to find gaps in present research and emphasize areas for future investigation. Addressing these gaps can take part to the refinement and development of more robust and broadly applicable models, ultimately enhancing their clinical utility. In summary, this review paper endeavors to serve as a comprehensive resource for scholars, clinicians, and policymakers eager to use ML and DL techniques for CVD detection. By elucidating the current landscape and future prospects, we aim to foster interdisciplinary collaboration and accelerate the translation of these advanced technologies into clinical practice, eventually improving cardiovascular health outcomes globally.

Papers selection strategy

The primary goal of the present survey paper is to highlight the role of AI methods for CVD prediction. Therefore, we conducted a thorough search across several scholarly databases such as ScienceDirect, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink and Google Scholar using keywords aligned with the survey’s theme, like ‘Cardiovascular Disease Detection’, ‘Heart Disease Detection/Prediction’, ‘Applications of DL/ML in Healthcare’, ‘Challenges in multimodal approach in healthcare’ and their combination from multiple disciplines, such as computer science, engineering, and medicine to guarantee inclusivity and diversity in the papers we chose. During this search, reference papers are selected based on their relevance, methodology, contribution, recent publications and earlier influential works as well while non-peer-reviewed and out-of-scope works are excluded. Past studies which are focusing on the use of ML/DL techniques for CVD detection, diagnosis, risk prediction or prognosis are included in this review paper. Data extraction from these reference papers were conducted to extract key information, including study objectives, methodology, datasets used, ML/DL algorithms employed, performance metrics, and main findings.

Datasets used for CVD detection

This section covers a discussion of data used for CVD detection in healthcare systems.

Major risk factors for heart problems are age, diabetes, hypertension, higher cholesterol levels, stressful life, lack of exercise, smoking, obesity, unhealthy diet, family history and poor hygiene practices. Due to the severity of CVD, a screening protocol must be used for its diagnosis [30]. As a part of the screening process, medical practitioners perform a variety of diagnostic tests such as clinical records like blood sugar, cholesterol level, blood pressure, calcium score, and stress testing, also imaging tests like ECG, ultrasound imaging, cardiac CT. The screening process needs to be performed manually and major involvement of humans is required which takes significant time for diagnosis.

For prediction of CVD using ML and DL involves gathering diverse datasets. These datasets vary in size, complexity, and the types of data they contain, ranging from clinical data and medical imaging studies to genomic information and biomarker profiles relevant to CVD detection. Clinical data contains EHRs, encompassing patient demographics, medical history, vital signs, symptoms, medications, and laboratory test results. Biomarker profiles include biomarker data obtained from blood tests, such as lipid profiles, cardiac enzymes (e.g. troponin), and inflammatory markers [31]. Imaging studies gathers medical imaging data, like ECGs, echocardiograms, cardiac MRI, CT scans, and angiograms. These imaging techniques offer insightful information on cardiac structure, function, and blood flow dynamics, which are essential for CVD diagnosis and risk stratification [32]. Lifestyle elements including food, physical exercise, smoking status and environmental factors such as air pollution, occupational hazards are also important, contributing to CVD development and progression [30]. Currently, research in CVD detection is carried out by using clinical data containing EHR as well as medical imaging data. Integrating these data can facilitate personalized risk assessment and preventive interventions. Here are some notable datasets frequently utilized in CVD detection studies. An overview of datasets that are commonly used in CVD detection is given in the below section.

ML in CVD prediction

ML has emerged as a transformative approach for predicting CVD risk. ML can incorporate high-dimensional, nonlinear, and multimodal data to uncover prognostic signals not readily captured by human-crafted features. Multiple fundamental limitations, gaps, and risks must be addressed before widespread clinical translation. ML techniques, especially Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and Support Vector Machines, are widely used to detect CVD by analyzing diverse health data, including EHR, ECG, heart rate, and cholesterol levels. Ensemble methods often improve accuracy by combining multiple algorithms.

ML models for CVD prediction are significantly influenced by the choice of input data modality. Various data sources provide complementary insights on cardiovascular risk, ranging from traditional demographics to high-dimensional imaging and genomics. The following summary outlines the primary modalities represented in the research studies.

1)Structured Clinical and EHR Data: It includes tabular predictors like age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, etc. which remain the most common input to models. Many ML based studies use EHR data to predict CVD events.

2)Electrocardiogram (ECG): ECG/time-series signal datasets with millions of samples have enabled training of deep CNN and transformer models. They can extract prognostic features not visible to clinicians; the availability of large-scale ECG cohorts has further enabled the development of models that can anticipate future occurrences of AF, heart failure, and mortality with increasing precision.

3)Imaging Modalities: It includes Echocardiography, Cardiac MRI, CT Angiograph. CNNs and hybrid pipelines are used for segmentation, phenotyping, and outcome prediction. Echocardiography: Deep learning automates segmentation and quantification of chamber volumes, ejection fraction, and strain. These features predict adverse events. Cardiac MRI: CNN-based texture and shape features predict fibrosis burden and outcomes. 4)Multimodal Fusion: Emerging literature emphasizes combining modalities: ECG + labs, imaging + EHR, or multimodal (EHR + imaging + genetics). Fusion strategies include late fusion (ensemble of modality-specific models), intermediate fusion (attention-based), and cross-modal contrastive learning. Combining ECG, imaging, labs, genetics and lifestyle data improves robustness and performance, particularly in low-label regimes. Studies consistently show fusion outperforms single-modality models, but handling missing modalities remains a challenge. Data modalities used for CVD prediction span structured EHR, physiological signals, imaging, biomarkers, genomics, and lifestyle. Each offers unique strengths but also limitations. The emerging trend is multimodal fusion, which combines complementary data streams to deliver more accurate and clinically relevant predictions. However, ensuring interoperability, handling missing modalities, and validating across populations remain major challenges.

Different aspect of ML in CVD detection.

ML redefines the field of CVD forecasting by harnessing the power of algorithms to evaluate and learn from data. With an estimated 17.9 million deaths each year worldwide owing to CVDs, comprising nearly 31 % of global fatalities, the need for precise prediction and early management is paramount [2]. ML techniques, which are divided into supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning, offer a dynamic approach to understanding and predicting CVDs. These methods comprise diverse techniques, including classification, regression, decision trees (DT), clustering, and DL, each geared toward solving distinct issues in CVD prediction [4]. By leveraging labelled data for training, supervised learning techniques such as SVM examine historical patient data and real-time ECG measurements to predict the likelihood of heart disorders. Unsupervised learning algorithms identify hidden patterns within massive datasets, providing crucial insights into the etiology and evolution of CVDs. Reinforcement learning, on the other hand, offers a framework for agents to learn from interactions with the environment and potentially aid in individualized treatment plans for CVD patients.

The selection of features is pivotal role in the successful application of ML algorithms for CVD detection. It is a crucial step in the detection of CVD as it enhances the performance of ML models by reducing dimensionality, improving accuracy, and decreasing computational costs. Some key approaches and techniques used in feature selection for CVD detection are statistical methods, wrapper methods, embedded methods and dimensionality reduction techniques. Statistical methods use ANOVA and correlation. Wrapper methods are based on selection and elimination of features. Embedded method uses Lasso Regression, Decision tree and Random forest techniques to identify importance of features. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and PCA reduce the dimensionality by transforming features.

DL techniques for ECG analysis

DL has emerged as a transformative approach for CVD prediction, due to its ability to automatically learn hierarchical feature representations from high-dimensional and heterogeneous data, accelerates analysis and decision-making processes, minimizes reliance on heuristic experiences, and drastically lowers human involvement. Unlike classic ML approaches, where scaling more data may cause difficulties in scalability and increase learning time, DL techniques offer a solution by effectively managing unstructured data and demonstrating faster learning rates using hierarchical neural networks. Recent research in CVD is increasingly making use of sophisticated deep learning architectures, often in multimodal settings, to improve diagnostic accuracy. Unlike traditional machine learning methods that rely on handcrafted features, DL models exploit raw signals, images, and multimodal inputs to capture subtle patterns that are often missed by conventional approaches.

This section outlines the types of DL algorithms, specifically those which allow for arrhythmia to be detected, that are used as ECG diagnostic tools. DL technique with recurrent neural networks as one of its major parts is a neural network that has several layers having the ability to develop a hierarchical feature in the dataset which makes it very vital in the ECG analysis because it can discover the complicated features hidden from the human data. DL algorithms trending upwards in terms of the accuracy of the arrhythmia detection has been one the areas of amalgamation disassembly of augmentation, sensitivity and classification. The main DL architectures, i.e. feed forward NN, CNN, RNN for sequence recognition, LSTM, GRU, and Transformer focusing on attention mechanism are majorly used techniques for ECG examination. Different DL approaches are on the rise, and their potential in ECG interpretation is being explored. In medical imaging, DL approaches, notably CNNs and RNNs, display extraordinary efficiency in interpreting MRI and X-ray images, thereby allowing precise illness identification and categorization. Additionally, DL models improve in analysing time-series data, like patient diagnostic histories and EHRs, ensuring accurate predictions with minimal human interaction. Through its integration into healthcare systems, DL not only boosts disease diagnosis, but also plays a vital role in individualized treatment options for diverse ailments, thus paving the way for improved patient outcomes and increased medical decision-making. Multilayer perceptron (MLP)

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) which is a part of the ANN that as input has a number of layers formed by interconnected nodes – artificial neurons. In a MLP, every neuron will receive the inputs, compute a weighted sum, and pass the result after an activation function for the output. In this sense, the multi-layered structure takes good account of the ECG data process since it allows the model to capture nonlinear interactions at higher levels which are challenging to express with simpler models. Superiority of MLP neural networks to any other neural network is they possess the potential to solve even the most complex nonlinear problems by modelling complicated non-linear interactions. This quality made them to be a feature of the DL methods [5].

ECG data, which represents electrical activity of the heart during a particular point in time, are being pre-processed and divided into fixed-length segments, which are the input values to the MLP. The MLP decompression values for ECG analysis can vary depending on the length and complexity of ECG segments and can cope with different qualities of ECG signals. Each of these inputs represents a particular time interval or on the other hand specific diagnostic taken from the ECG signal. The layered network structure of MLPs allows them to learn to extract more abstract patterns and representations at later levels by learning from input data, which helps them to capture arrhythmia patterns of variable complexity.

For arrhythmia detection and classification, the output layer of MLP performs the function of assigning attributes to the input segments in order to classify them into a specified arrhythmia class [49]. That's what MLP models accomplish by training on labelled ECG segments with the arrhythmia types the models learn to understand and correctly classify segments they are not familiar with and that are why they are useful in the clinical situation as a diagnostic tool.

CNN

CNN is a specific instance of a neural network generally utilized for tasks such as processing of signals, recognizing images, processing of pixel data, and natural language processing. Moreover, CNN allows data to be examined for spatial hierarchies or patterns by built-in stacked trainable tiny filters called kernels [61]. These cones are knowledgeable in operating from the environment of ECG data in order to provide specific information such as the form and duration of heart beats which is essential in the analysis of arrhythmias. When applied to raw ECG data, CNNs can take two major forms: 1D CNNs and 2D CNNs. 1D CNNs are trying to process the given information over the time of the ECG signal of the patient. While 2D CNNs have shown utility in ECG analysis, they often operate on transformed representations rather than raw ECG signals. These transformations can introduce distortions or loss of fidelity, potentially compromising the clinical relevance of the data. CNNs by themselves learn and automatically extract vital features of the raw ECG signals which in turn help with arrhythmia identification precision. Through the detection of typical patterns of heart wave, CNNs can distinguish the healthy from the pathological beats. Although CNNs are good at pattern recognition, they have a limitation in terms of receptive field size, which can be a barrier to fulfilling the extensive sequence processing. Moreover, they might underestimate the importance of long-term dependencies in the data which represent that decision about beat intervals to be understood correctly.

CNNs have been widely adopted in cardiovascular imaging, particularly for echocardiography and cardiac MRI. Some studies developed a CNN-based framework to automate ventricular segmentation in cardiac MRI, reporting accuracy comparable to human experts. Similarly, in few studies, researcers trained CNNs on echocardiographic videos to predict left ventricular ejection fraction, a central metric for heart failure diagnosis. Beyond imaging, 1D-CNNs have been applied to ECG waveforms to detect arrhythmias and myocardial infarction. These studies demonstrate that CNNs excel at learning spatial and temporal filters relevant to cardiac structure and electrical activity.

RNN

RNN belong to one more line of artificial neural networks whose one of the most functions is to gather sequent correlated (in a time frame) data from time series data. At the root of RNNs' diversity and complexity compared with conventional feed forward neural networks lie cyclical connections that make it possible for the former kind of networks to store temporal information. This property of RNN allows them to show excellent performance while processing the ECG analysis data that are sequential in nature and thus can identify or detect irregularities. ECG Analysis undergoes pre-processing of raw signal, whereby reduction of noise and artifacts is done before arrhythmias finding and categorizing. Then, the ECG data is thoroughly cleaned up, and subsequently divided into equal parts matching to one heartbeat or a time segment. The segments on the other hand are the continuous stream of data into an RNN through which the network can grasp the inherent temporal changes within an ECG. In the process of sequential scanning, the RNN is capable of finding and extracting the feature set that is imperative for the recognition and classification of arrhythmia. Nonetheless, RNNs confront the disappearing gradient problem, which makes the neural networks unable to distinguish long-time patterns present in the input sequences. The inability to do this task may affect their ability to judge complex sequential patterns or assess long-term ECG segments. On the one hand, RNNs pose a fundamental issue for CVDs, but at the same time, they are the critical basis for more precise and reliable methods of arrhythmia recognition and classification.

Given the sequential nature of physiological data, RNNs and their gated variants (LSTM, GRU) have been applied to ECG and clinical time-series. Recurrent models have been used for continuous blood pressure and heart rate variability monitoring, enabling early detection of ischemic events. These architectures capture long-range dependencies and temporal evolution of cardiovascular signals, providing advantages over static models.

Significant results are achieved using DL models, including CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, GANs, and autoencoders in CVD detection based on ECG data. These models have better capabilities in terms of feature extraction, handling complex data, and providing accurate and real-time diagnostic solutions. The integration of Internet of Things (IoT) with DL further enhances the capability of these models, making them viable for widespread use in remote and continuous health monitoring systems.

While DL has enabled state-of-the-art performance across multiple CVD modalities, several limitations remain. First, most studies are retrospective, single-center, and rely on curated datasets, raising concerns of generalisability. Second, interpretability is a major clinical barrier although techniques like saliency maps, SHAP, and attention visualization provide partial transparency; further work is needed to build clinician trust. Finally, the computational and data requirements of DL models remain significant, which can hinder adoption in low-resource healthcare environments. Nonetheless, the growing body of evidence demonstrates that DL techniques when rigorously validated can enhance diagnostic accuracy, risk stratification, and potentially guide personalized interventions in cardiology. Discussion

ML and DL methods have been increasingly adopted for CVD detection, leveraging diverse data modalities such as structured clinical features, electrocardiograms (ECG), echocardiography, and cardiac MRI.

Classical ML models including LR, SVM, RF, GB have shown strong performance on small, structured datasets such as the UCI Cleveland dataset [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. These models are computationally efficient, interpretable, and particularly suitable when sample sizes are limited, but they rely heavily on feature engineering and often fail to capture the complex patterns in imaging and waveform data. Despite these advances, both ML and DL studies in CVD prediction face recurring methodological and translational challenges. Many reported results come from retrospective, single-center datasets with small sample sizes, raising concerns of overfitting and limited generalisability. Internal validation methods such as k-fold cross-validation are common [48,49], but external validation across institutions or populations is rarely performed [52,53,55]. Moreover, while discrimination metrics such as accuracy and AUC are often emphasized, calibration, uncertainty quantification, and decision-curve analyses critical for clinical adoption are rarely reported.

The discussion highlights that ML and DL models are the main tools to identify CVD using ECG signals, especially when we talk about ECG analysis. In the case of these DL models, in particular, it becomes possible to get an advantage over the other ML models because they are able to preserve nonlinear relationships between the input electrocardiographic signals and depending on the task type. DL tools give the chance for a speedy and accurate identification of CVDs, which in turn allows earlier treatment and lead to better outcomes. While there has mainly been an interest in the use of ML and DL techniques for CVD prediction in the recent literature reviews, this review paper successfully presents a different perspective by applying several approaches on various elements of ECG data-driven models. It stages an in-depth exploration of ML/DL architectures and the essential acceptance factors related to clinical features including explainability and value-based consideration. It additionally highlights DL models, particularly those yields by integrating CNN with other neural networks as being potent in arriving at suggested results in ECG analysis. Such models depict the ability to learn from complicated inputs in the manner assimilating the necessary features to make deductions automatically and without the need for implied pre-treatments.

DL approaches, especially CNNs and recurrent architectures (LSTMs, GRUs), have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on raw, high-dimensional inputs. CNNs have been highly effective in echocardiography and MRI for segmentation and ejection fraction estimation, while 1D-CNNs [68] and RNNs have achieved cardiologist-level accuracy in arrhythmia and myocardial infarction detection from ECG signals. More recent advances, such as attention mechanisms, transformers, and graph neural networks, further extend DL’s capacity to model long-sequence data and anatomical relationships, enabling richer and more flexible representations. DL models, though powerful, also suffer from limited interpretability; clinicians often struggle to trust predictions when the underlying drivers remain opaque, even with tools like saliency maps or SHAP explanations [63]. In multimodal applications, combining imaging, waveform, and EHR data improves predictive performance, but it introduces challenges of data alignment, missing modalities, and increased model complexity. Few studies address these issues systematically, and most lack prospective validation or demonstration of clinical utility compared to existing guideline-based risk scores.

Although ECG-based AI holds significant potential through multimodal integration, most current research remains limited due to various factors as mentioned below.

1)Single-modality dominance and missed context: Despite multimodal potential (imaging + EHR + omics), most papers remain ECG-centric due to dataset availability and label complexity [61]. Most studies rely solely on ECG (raw or image-derived) or ECG + a few demographic features. Very few integrate imaging, labs, genomic or long-time wearable streams. This narrows the phenotypic view and can limit sensitivity for complex disease states. ECG based models were validated on large clinical cohorts and targeted rare diseases (HCM, amyloidosis) by Guo et al. [65] and Grogan et al. [77].

Taken together, ML and DL models hold strong promise for improving CVD detection, with DL particularly excelling in high-dimensional imaging and waveform tasks and ML remaining competitive for structured clinical features. However, translation into clinical practice requires more than high accuracy on benchmark datasets. Rigorous external validation, transparent reporting, interpretability, and demonstration of clinical benefit are essential. Future research must move beyond technical novelty to focus on prospective, multi-center evaluations, fairness across patient subgroups and integration into real-world clinical workflows. Only through such rigorous validation and contextualization can predictive models evolve from experimental tools to reliable decision support systems in cardiovascular care.

Challenges in multimodal model approach in healthcare for CVD detection

Recent studies highlight that integrating multimodal data such as imaging, electrocardiography, laboratory values, and EHR can improve CVD prediction. While single-modality models (e.g., CNNs on ECGs) often achieve high performance, multimodal fusion captures complementary information that enhances robustness. However, leveraging these diverse data streams presents substantial methodological and clinical challenges.

1.Data Complexity and Quality Assurance: Accumulating vast amounts of high-quality data, particularly for uncommon or complicated illnesses, remains difficult in the healthcare industry. Scarcity or poor quality data might result in biased models, which affects the precision of diagnosis and treatment choices. It is imperative to address both the quantity and quality of data to create resilient multimodal ML models. Most multimodal CVD studies are restricted to single-center, retrospective cohorts. Grogan et al. [77], who fused ECG signals with patient demographics to predict asymptomatic left ventricular dysfunction, demonstrated improved diagnostic accuracy, but validation was limited to a single institution. The scarcity of large, publicly available multimodal CVD datasets limits reproducibility and external benchmarking, raising concerns about overfitting and generalisability. Ground truth labels (e.g. severity of disease, presence/absence) rely on imaging, physician interpretation, or clinical outcomes; these may be noisy or inconsistent.

2.Heterogeneous Data Representation: When data from several sources are integrated for multimodal ML, different data formats and standards are produced. Challenges in merging heterogeneous data with different properties include managing missing data, reducing noise, and guaranteeing natural grouping, coherence, and smoothness in the data representation. Multimodal CVD datasets are inherently heterogeneous, encompassing continuous time-series (ECG, blood pressure), high-dimensional imaging (MRI, echocardiography), categorical EHR features, and occasionally genomic or proteomic profiles. Modalities differ in resolution, scale, and sampling frequency, making alignment and fusion non-trivial. Aligning these modalities is nontrivial due to differences in temporal resolution, feature scales, and missing values. For example, Zhang et al. [28] integrated echocardiographic imaging with EHR-derived demographic and laboratory features for heart failure prediction, but emphasized the difficulty of synchronizing image-derived functional parameters with irregular clinical records. Imaging protocols, equipment, and populations differ, which can degrade performance when moving from one site to another. Missing or inconsistent modalities may occur as not all patients have the same set of data, some may have imaging, some only EHR, some lab data, etc. Handling missing modalities or integrating partial data is non-trivial.

Collectively, the evidence suggests that while multimodal DL models hold promise for capturing complementary signals and improving diagnostic robustness, future research must prioritize large-scale, multi-institutional datasets, robust handling of missing modalities, interpretable fusion strategies, and prospective validation to ensure that performance gains translate into clinically actionable improvements. Addressing issues of dataset availability, interpretability, and external validation will be essential for translating these methods into clinical practice.

Future directions in multimodal models in healthcare for CVD detection

Using convenient data-gathering tools: Real-time data gathering is made easier by the introduction of smart wearables and IoT devices, which provide extensive insights into a range of healthcare disciplines. Institutional collaborations can improve the diversity and completeness of datasets using methods such as Federated Machine Learning to share parameters instead of exchanging raw data. Create and maintain community benchmarks that pair multimodal inputs with clinically relevant labels (e.g., stroke risk, obstructive CAD confirmed by angiography, heart failure hospitalization). Benchmarks should include external test centers, well annotated labels, and metrics beyond discrimination (calibration, fairness, clinical utility), and tests of model robustness under missing modalities.

Automation and Improvement of Data Pre-processing: For insightful information, sophisticated pre-processing methods are essential beyond data acquisition. Balanced and high-quality datasets that are prepared for model training are guaranteed by automation for processes like noise reduction, artefact removal, and data augmentation and normalisation.

Combining Advanced Data Integration Instruments: Various data formats make harmonisation and standardisation difficult. These procedures must be automated using sophisticated AI and ML algorithms in order to enable thorough analysis using multimodal ML.

Improving Explainability of Modern Techniques for Models: More interpretable and explicable models are needed to overcome the multimodal ML models' opaque structure. Transparency and confidence in treatment decisions are increased by methods such as feature relevance ranking, model visualisation, decision rules, probabilistic approaches, and neuro-fuzzy techniques, which offer insights into model judgements. Developing cardiac-specific Foundational Models (FMs) trained on massive unlabeled corpora and fine-tuned for downstream detection, segmentation, and phenotyping may reduce label needs and improve robustness to distribution shifts. Research should move beyond naive concatenation toward flexible fusion: cross-attention, gating that handles missing modalities, and architectures that propagate calibrated uncertainty from each modality. Interpretable multimodal models will be required for clinician trust and regulatory acceptance. Benchmarks should measure not only AU-ROC but calibration, attribution consistency, and sensitivity to missing inputs. Better fusion architectures that are modality-aware will be essential. Architectures that can dynamically weigh or gate modalities, incorporate cross-attention, and propagate uncertainty from each input stream will likely yield more reliable and interpretable predictions.

Putting Knowledge Transfer Methods into Practice: Strategies for efficient knowledge transfer are necessary due to the variety of models and datasets. In order to minimise redundancy or over-fitting and capture complementary information, novel approaches for multimodal feature selection, fusion, and modelling are crucial. This will result in more accurate diagnosis and treatment outcomes.

Cutting down on Computational Expenses: Multimodal ML relies heavily on computational efficiency. Performance can be maintained while reducing computational complexity by using methods like networked computing frameworks, specialised hardware accelerators, and model reduction and optimisation. This guarantees scalability and application across a range of healthcare issues, providing quicker and more precise answers. Many CVD detection use-cases live on edge devices (smartwatches, home patch monitors). Research should prioritize model compression, efficient architectures, and power-aware pipelines that keep multimodal inference feasible on device or with minimal cloud interaction enabling earlier detection and broader access.

Conclusion

This paper explains the great advances and advantages of ML and DL methods in CVD detection processes based on various data modalities. In the literature, there has been an immense range of studies of the application of ML and DL models as the primary ECG data, which have contributed to the diagnosis and prognosis of CVD. DL models are increasingly regarded as superior to traditional ML models due to their significant potential to augment human capabilities in healthcare. They offer powerful tools to support clinical decision-making and enhance diagnostic accuracy. Despite this promise, the integration of DL technologies into routine clinical practice remains an evolving process, requiring extensive validation and clear demonstration of translational maturity. A combination of CNN with other neural network architectures like LSTM has found to be a very useful approach. This joint computational framework automatically identifies the defining features from the complex signals of ECG while performing ML tasks. Besides that, a proper focus should be placed on ML and DL algorithm implementation in ECG analysis programs with a particular attention to explainable AI and a careful consideration of ethical issues, legal constraints and societal impacts that can affect their clinical acceptance. However, DL models still hold the advantages in certain cases, such as those with limited data or posting the demand for interpretability. Nevertheless, what is more substantial in CNNs, i.e., feature engineering is evident in that they can upgrade the shallow ML models. In summary, this article furnishes essential information regarding this area, pointing out a way for innovative research and clinical utilisation to achieve supreme patient outcomes and effective health delivery.

Heart disease encompasses a diverse range of conditions that can influence individuals of all ages and backgrounds. While some risk factors, like age, family history, and genetic predisposition, cannot be modified, many lifestyle factors, including diet, exercise, and smoking cessation, can influence heart health and decrease the risk of heart disease. Early detection, prompt intervention, and on-going management are essential for mitigating the impact of heart disease and improving outcomes for affected individuals.
