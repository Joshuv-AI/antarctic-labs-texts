Impact of slab thickness in digital breast tomosynthesis on radiologists’ diagnostic accuracy and interpretation time in women with breast cancer: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Saengsiri Chumsaengsri, Malai Muttarak, Apichat Tantraworasin, Jayanton Patumanond, Pailin Kongmebhol, Chumsaeng Chumsaengsri, Miguel Aristizabal, Hiroyuki Abe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDigital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) improves breast cancer detection compared to digital mammography but requires longer reading times. DBT slices are reconstructed and combined into thicker slabs. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance and interpretation time between a 6-mm thick slab and a 1-mm thin slice procedure in DBT, both using synthetic mammograms. Additionally, this study aimed to evaluate intra- and inter-observer agreement on image visibility.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated DBT performed on symptomatic women between September 2020 and June 2021. Two breast-certified radiologists independently interpreted DBT images from both procedures, with a 4-week washout period between interpretations. Of the 184 eligible women included, 97 had biopsy-proven malignancies, which consisted of 53 masses, 23 calcifications, 20 distortions, and 1 asymmetry. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare reading times between each procedure. Sensitivity and specificity were evaluated using McNemar’s test, while area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was compared using the DeLong test. The non-inferiority of thick slab compared to thin slice of DBT was assessed using a one-sided confidence interval (CI). Observer agreement was measured using Cohen’s kappa.ResultsSensitivity for the thick slab procedure of DBT was 91.8% (95% CI: 87.4–96.4%), while for thin slice procedure it was 95.9% (95% CI: 89.8–98.9%). The difference between two procedures was not statistically significant (P=0.39). Specificity for thick slab was 65.5% (95% CI: 58.6–75.4%), while for thin slice procedure it was 67.8% (95% CI: 56.9–77.4%). The difference between two procedures was not statistically significant (P=0.73). The AUC for thick slab was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.84), while for thin slice procedure it was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87). No statistically significant difference between the two procedures was found (P=0.18). The mean interpretation time for thick slab was 11.2% (0.42 minutes) shorter compared to the thin slice procedure. The intra-rater agreement on image visibility between two procedures was 0.75 (95% CI: 0.65–0.81). Inter-rater agreement was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.87) for thick slab and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.59–0.61) for thin slice procedure.ConclusionsIn this symptomatic patient cohort, the use of 6-mm thick slabs of DBT effectively reduced interpretation time without compromising diagnostic performance, demonstrating non-inferiority in sensitivity, specificity, and AUC for breast cancer detection compared to 1-mm slice standard DBT. Given the limited cohort size, further validation in a broader screening or asymptomatic population would be beneficial.Keywords: Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), slab thickness, diagnostic performance, interpretation time, breast cancer

Introduction

Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) is a quasi-three-dimensional advanced imaging technique that enhances traditional mammography. DBT improves the visualization of breast lesions by reducing the masking effect caused by overlapping fibro-glandular breast tissue. DBT has seen a significant increase in global utilization across many breast centers, driven by strong evidence of its superior cancer detection rates and reduced recall rates compared to conventional two-dimensional (2D) mammography (1-4). Many studies have shown that combining DBT with full-field digital mammography (FFDM) performs better than FFDM alone in both screening and diagnostic settings. This combination enhances early breast cancer detection and improves diagnostic accuracy (5-10). Since its initial approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2011, DBT has been increasingly used and widely adopted by many institutions around the world (11). The standard DBT images set includes 1-mm reconstructed slices of the breast along with synthetic 2D mammography. A major drawback of DBT is the increased interpretation time required for radiologists, who often need to review DBT image sets almost twice per case compared to traditional methods (6,12-15). Furthermore, as the number of image files increases, more storage is required on the picture archiving and communication system (PACS) (16). Surprisingly, the reconstruction algorithms for DBT have not changed in terms of image thickness since DBT was initially approved. As a result, several recent studies have explored various approaches to reduce interpretation time for DBT (17-20).

Currently, the standard DBT image set includes 1-mm reconstructed slices through the entire breast, regardless of breast thickness, along with synthetic 2D mammograms. DBT slices were combined into so-called slabs by reconstructing thin slices and merging them into thicker slabs. An alternative approach to creating thicker slices is to combine multiple thin reconstructed slices into new, thicker ones. One prior study found that small architectural distortions were the ones most frequently missed when using 10-mm slabs without 1-mm slices (17). A recent study revealed that a 6-mm slab with a 3-mm overlap provided better visualization of architectural distortions, identifying them on at least one additional slab image compared to the standard protocol (21). For a given breast, the first 6-mm slab includes slices from 1 to 6 mm. The second 6-mm slab covers 4 to 9 mm, the third slab includes 7 to 12 mm, and so on.

The purpose of this study was to compare the diagnostic performance, with non-inferiority, and interpretation time of a 6-mm thick slab versus a 1-mm thin slice procedure of DBT, both used with synthetic 2D mammograms for breast cancer detection. Additionally, the study aimed to evaluate the secondary outcomes of intra-observer agreement in visibility scores between the thick slab and thin slice procedures and inter-observer agreement for each DBT procedure. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2828/rc).

Methods

This is a retrospective observational cross-sectional study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University (No. RAD-2564-08018) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

This study was performed at Maharaj Nakorn Chiang Mai Hospital (a university-affiliated, tertiary care center) and the Women’s Health Center, Center for Medical Excellence, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University. It was conducted between September 1, 2020, and June 30, 2021. The subjects of this study were women who had undergone DBT with reconstructed synthetic 2D mammography.

The patient cohort was drawn from individuals who underwent DBT at our institute during the study period for a range of diagnostic reasons, including breast pain, breast lumpiness, palpable breast masses, or nipple discharge. During the study period, 253 women with breast symptoms underwent DBT. All DBT examinations were conducted using equipment from a single manufacturer, Hologic (Marlborough, MA, USA). The reference standard was based on tissue pathology. The reference standard for a true-positive case was a biopsy-proven breast malignancy, which included ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive mammary carcinoma, encompassing both ductal and lobular types. The true-negative cases consisted of patients with negative or benign findings [Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) category 1 or 2], those with BI-RADS 3 findings that remained stable for at least 365 days with mammographic and clinical follow-up, and patients with biopsy-confirmed negative for malignancy. Data on follow-up evaluations of the contralateral breast were not included in our study.

The exclusion criteria included patients with known breast cancer, breast implants or breast reconstruction, those who had undergone lumpectomy within the past 5 years, and those who had incomplete data.

A total of 184 eligible patients were included in this study. Among this cohort, 97 were diagnosed with malignancies, while 87 were classified as true-negative cases.

Imaging procedure

Mammographic images, mammographic reports, and pathology reports were accessed through PACS, mammography reporting system, and electronic medical records at our institution. All patients in the study underwent imaging with the Selenia Dimensions 2D/3D system (Hologic), and synthetic 2D mammograms were generated using a commercially available software module (C-view, version 1.83, Hologic). All images were anonymized and uploaded to workstations (Hologic Securview, Hologic) for viewing. When available, a mammogram from up to one year prior was used for comparison. Twenty-four cases (13%) had a prior mammogram for comparative analysis.

For our study, the thick slab procedure used a 6-mm DBT slab combined with a synthetic 2D mammogram, without including 1-mm slices. Whereas the thin slice procedure utilized standard 1-mm DBT in conjunction with a synthetic 2D mammogram.

Readers involved in the conducting of the study

The mammographic images were interpreted by two observers. Both were board-certified with fellowship training in breast imaging, having four and eighteen years of experience respectively, and three and five years of experience specifically with DBT. For both readers, this study represents their first experience with the 6-mm thick slab DBT. However, they have comprehensive familiarity and have routinely used 1-mm slices of DBT and synthetic 2D mammograms. Both procedures of the DBT are based on the same fundamental concepts of the imaging technique. All datasets were independently reviewed by readers in a randomized order. Within each mode, the 184 DBT cases were presented as a mixed batch and randomly assigned to workstations prior to interpretation. A crossover randomization was implemented, in which each case was assessed using both the thin slice and thick slab procedures with a 4-week washout period between assessments to minimize recall bias. The two observers reviewed the images at the workstation (Hologic Securview, Hologic) following a step-by-step procedure; this included: (I) opening the image dataset, (II) interpreting the images without access to the clinical history or chief complaint, (III) documenting any relevant findings in a provided case-record form, and (IV) assigning a final BI-RADS assessment. All readers were blinded to the clinical history, DBT mode, pathological outcomes, and the interpretations of the other observers. If available, comparisons with the 1-year prior studies were provided to simulate routine clinical practice. The DBT plus synthetic 2D mammogram datasets were independently randomized. Reader 1 first reviewed the DBT images using the thin slice procedure, while Reader 2 began with the thick slab procedure. A 4-week washout period was observed between the two readings. Following this, Reader 1 interpreted the DBT images using the thick slab procedure, while Reader 2 used the thin slice procedure. Computer-aided detection software was not used in this study due to a lack of availability at our institution.

Each reader evaluated and categorized the mammographic images according to the fifth edition of the American College of Radiology BI-RADS, 2013 guidelines (22), assigning each case to the appropriate BI-RADS category.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was to compare the diagnostic performance with non-inferiority of thick slab and thin slice procedures in DBT, both integrated with synthetic 2D mammograms, by assessing sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for breast cancer detection. Additionally, the study aimed to compare the reading times between the two DBT procedures.

The secondary outcome was to evaluate intra-observer agreement on the visibility scores of mammographic findings and inter-observer agreement for each DBT procedure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using STATA statistical software version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The threshold for statistical significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05. Continuous variables were tested for normality through visual inspection of a histogram and a normal density plot. Normally distributed data are reported as mean and standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data are summarized using median and interquartile range.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each DBT procedure with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These curves were based on the likelihood of malignancy percentages for the most suspicious breast lesion. The comparison of the AUC between the thick slab and thin slice DBT procedures was analyzed using the DeLong test.

Sensitivity and specificity were calculated on a case-level basis and stratified by procedure, with BI-RADS 4 and 5 considered positive. The reference standard was the histopathologic result. This study compared the sensitivity and specificity of these two procedures using McNemar’s test.

The non-inferiority of AUC was calculated using a one-sided CI. It was approximated that the AUC for both the thick slab and thin slice procedures would be roughly 80%. The non-inferior margin (delta) was set at 10%, establishing the non-inferiority margin at AUC – delta, i.e., 80%−10% =70%. Non-inferiority would be confirmed if the lower bound of the one-sided CI did not fall below this 70% margin. The analysis was also conducted using a one-sample test of proportion equivalence through two one-sided t-tests (TOST).

Using a one-sided CI, non-inferiority of sensitivity and specificity was also assessed. The non-inferiority margin (delta) was set at 10%. Non-inferiority would be confirmed if the lower bound of the one-sided CI of the thick slab did not fall below the predefined margin.

Each reader independently reviewed and interpreted the mammographic images in separate sessions, with each session lasting approximately one hour. The reading time for each case was recorded individually in minutes on a per-case basis by the operating system. Subsequently, the reading times were categorized according to the pathological reports as either malignant or benign using STATA software. The comparison of reading times between the different DBT procedures for each reader was performed using a paired t-test if the data followed a normal distribution, or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test if the data did not follow a normal distribution. Reading times that exceeded 1.5 times the interquartile range above the third quartile or fell below 1.5 times the interquartile range below the first quartile were considered suspected outliers and were excluded from the analysis.

Cohen’s kappa value, along with the 95% CI, was calculated to assess intra- and inter-observer agreement on the visibility score of mammographic findings for each DBT procedure. The visibility scores were rated using a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (where 1 = minimally confident and 5 = strongly confident). The agreement level was divided into five categories based on Cohen’s kappa values, as follows: 0.00–0.20 (poor agreement); 0.21–0.40 (fair agreement); 0.41–0.60 (moderate agreement); 0.61–0.80 (good agreement); and 0.81–1.00 (excellent agreement).

In this study, the sample size is calculated based on a paired proportion test by using marginal proportions, which are referred to from the previous study (21).

Power of test =0.80, α (significance level) =0.05, ρ (correlation between paired observations) =0.80.

Based on this assumption, a paired-proportion test using STATA indicated that a sample size of 145 would be required to achieve 80% power at a 95% confidence level. Our sample size was 184 eligible patients, exceeding the required minimum.

Results

During the study period, there were 253 women who had breast symptoms and underwent DBT. Of this number, 69 women were excluded (Figure 1). Among the 184 eligible patients included in this study, imaging was available for 350 breasts, due to 18 patients having undergone unilateral mastectomy. Nevertheless, all analyses were conducted on a per-patient basis (n=184). The mean age was 53.8 years (SD =11.1) for all cases and 57.8 years (SD =11.0) for malignant cases. The patients were classified based on menstrual status, with 54.9% (101 patients) being premenopausal and 45.1% (83 patients) postmenopausal. Breast composition among the cases was categorized as follows: 0.5%: almost entirely fat; 13.6%: scattered fibro-glandular tissue; 76.1%: heterogeneously dense and 9.8%: extremely dense breast tissue.

None of the individuals had an additional suspicious or worrisome lesion that needed further biopsy.

Among the 152 biopsied cases, 97 were histopathologically confirmed as malignancies. The distribution of these 97 malignant lesions according to their mammographic characteristics was as follows: 53 masses [comprising 1 DCIS, 47 invasive ductal carcinomas (IDC), 3 invasive lobular carcinomas (ILC), 1 mucinous carcinoma, and 1 papillary carcinoma], 23 suspicious calcifications (including 5 DCIS and 18 IDC), 20 architectural distortions (consisting of 1 DCIS, 15 IDC, and 4 ILC), and 1 asymmetry, which was diagnosed as IDC. Diagnostic performance and non-inferiority outcome

Among the 184 women included in the study, there were 97 malignant breast lesions. The AUC for the thin slice procedure was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87), compared to 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.84) for the thick slab procedure (P=0.18). The sensitivity for breast cancer detection was 95.9% (95% CI: 89.8–98.9%) for the thin slice procedure, compared to 91.8% (95% CI: 87.4–96.4%) for the thick slab procedure (P=0.39). The specificity was 67.8% (95% CI: 56.9–77.4%) for the thin slice procedure and 65.5% (95% CI: 58.6–75.4%) for the thick slab procedure (P=0.73) (Table 3). They showed that there was no statistically significant difference in AUC, sensitivity, and specificity between the two DBT techniques for breast cancer detection.

Reading time

Overall, the mean reading time for the thick slab procedure was shorter compared to the thin slice procedure, with a reduction of approximately 11.2% (0.42 minutes) for Reader 1 and 6.0% (0.25 minutes) for Reader 2. Specifically, the mean reading time for the thin slice procedure was 3.75±0.74 minutes for Reader 1 and 4.15±0.70 minutes for Reader 2. Whereas, the thick slab procedure took 3.33±0.67 minutes for Reader 1 and 3.90±0.67 minutes for Reader 2, respectively.

The mean reading time for the 97 malignant breast lesions was reduced for both readers when using the thick slab procedure compared to the thin slice procedure, with reductions of 11.2% for Reader 1 and 6.1% for Reader 2. In the thin slice and thick slab procedures, the mean reading times for the malignant group were 4.03±0.46 versus 3.58±0.44 minutes for Reader 1 and 4.42±0.35 versus 4.15±0.33 minutes for Reader 2, respectively.

For both procedures, the mean reading time was stratified according to the BI-RADS category for each reader. Noticeably, the mean reading time for all BI-RADS categories was shorter with the thick slab procedure for both readers. Specifically, BI-RADS 1 (negative findings) had the shortest reading time, with average times for thin slice versus thick slab recorded at 2.36±0.16 versus 2.11±0.10 minutes for Reader 1 and 2.82±0.18 versus 2.63±0.15 minutes for Readers 2, respectively. Conversely, BI-RADS 4 (suspicion of malignancy) had the highest reading time among all BI-RADS categories for both readers. The average time of Reader 1 was 4.06±0.41 versus 3.62±0.43 minutes, and Reader 2 was 4.41±0.37 versus 4.15±0.35 minutes for the thin slice and thick slab procedures, respectively.

The following reading times were excluded as outliers: 18 cases from the thin slice procedure for Reader 1 (less than 2.45 minutes), 3 cases from the thin slice procedure for Reader 2 (less than 2.6 minutes) and 3 cases from the thick slab procedure for Reader 2 (less than 2.4 minutes). All of these excluded cases involved benign findings. The majority of outliers were cases involving extremely thin breasts with no concerning mammographic findings. The comparison of each reader’s reading time among the different DBT procedures was calculated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. There was a significantly decreased reading time when using the thick slab procedure for Reader 1 (P<0.001). Similarly, the reading time for the thick slab procedure was shorter for Reader 2 compared to the thin slice procedure, although this difference was not statistically significant (P=0.25) (Figure 6).

Intra-rater and inter-rater agreement

Intra- and inter-observer agreements were calculated using kappa statistics to assess the visibility score of mammographic findings in each DBT procedure. The visibility score was based on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5 (where 1 = minimally confident and 5 = strongly confident).

Cohen’s kappa value for intra-rater agreement between the thick slab and thin slice procedures was 0.77 (95% CI: 0.70–0.81) for Reader 1 and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65–0.78) for Reader 2.

Cohen’s kappa value for inter-rater agreement was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.84–0.87) with the thick slab procedure, indicating excellent agreement, and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.59–0.61) with the thin slice procedure, indicating moderate agreement. Additionally, for the thick slab procedure, correlation coefficients were examined in subgroups of masses, calcifications, and architectural distortions. The results demonstrated substantial inter-rater agreements in all three groupings, with correlation coefficients of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.40–1.00) for masses, 0.64 (95% CI: 0.36–0.93) for calcifications, and 0.64 (95% CI: 0.46–0.73) for architectural distortions.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we demonstrated the non-inferiority and comparable diagnostic accuracy of 6-mm thick slab DBT relative to 1-mm slices of DBT, while also efficiently reducing image interpretation time. Moreover, substantial intra-rater agreement was observed in the assessment of visibility scores for mammographic findings between the thick slab and thin slice techniques, accompanied by excellent inter-rater agreement for the thick slab procedure.

Diagnostic performance and comparison with previous studies

Our study found no statistically significant differences in sensitivity between the thick slab [91.8% (95% CI: 87.4–96.4%)] and thin slice [95.9% (95% CI: 89.8–98.9%)] DBT procedures (P=0.39). Similarly, specificity did not differ significantly between the thick slab [65.5% (95% CI: 58.6–75.4%)] and thin slice [67.8% (95% CI: 56.9–77.4%)] procedures (P=0.73). In contrast to the 2019 study by Iotti et al. (17), which found a 1.6% improvement in specificity with 10-mm slabs and synthetic 2D mammograms but a 7.8% decrease in sensitivity, our study demonstrated no significant difference in sensitivity when using 6-mm slabs for breast cancer diagnosis. This indicates that the 6-mm slab approach maintains comparable sensitivity to the standard 1-mm slices, while offering potential benefits in interpretation time.

The 2020 study by Pujara et al. (21) similarly found no decrease in sensitivity and specificity when using 6-mm slabs with a 3-mm overlap, compared to the standard 1-mm slices. The data therefore suggest that this approach might mitigate the loss of sensitivity while improving efficiency, as shown by the lack of significant difference in diagnostic performance between the 6-mm slab and the standard 1-mm slice protocols.

Consistently, the AUC for the thick slab procedure was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.73–0.84), while the AUC for the thin slice procedure was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.77–0.87; P=0.18), indicating no statistically significant difference between the two procedures. This outcome indicates comparably good performance in discriminating between malignant and benign lesions for breast cancer detection. This result is consistent with the prior study (21).

In further non-inferiority analysis, our study discovered that the thick slab procedure of DBT, using 6-mm slabs, is non-inferior to the thin slice procedure in terms of AUC, sensitivity, and specificity for breast cancer detection.

The majority of the lesions missed when using the thick slab procedure were small masses and architectural distortions, each less than 8 mm in size. This finding aligns with the results of the prior study by Pujara et al. (21), which reported a mean size of 10±9 mm for false-negative lesions in 6-mm slabs. On the other hand, clusters of faint microcalcifications were often more clearly visualized with the thick slab procedure. Additionally, for large architectural distortions (size >8 mm), the 6-mm thick slab provided enhanced delineation of lesion extension. These results are consistent with those of a prior study by Sauer et al. (23).

Interpretation time and comparison with prior studies

There are primarily two approaches to reduce reading time for DBT interpretation: using computer-aided detection (CAD) systems and decreasing the number of DBT image slices.

The first approach, concurrent CAD, has recently been developed mainly for mass detection. A recent study showed a 14% decrease in reading time with CAD, though there was no statistically significant difference in AUC for the BI-RADS scale between using CAD and not using CAD (18). Two additional studies also demonstrated that radiologist interpretation performance was not inferior when using concurrent CAD, with reading times reduced by 23.5% and 29.2% (19,20). The second approach involves reducing the number of DBT images to simplify the visualization procedure. A 2013 study found that increasing slab thickness to 2-mm slabs instead of 1-mm planes reduced the reading time for normal cases by approximately 20% (24).

Our investigation, which used a thicker 6-mm slab, aligns with the second approach of reducing image thickness. Comparing our results to the thin slice procedure, which used 1-mm slices, we observed a reduction in reading time by 11.2%. Furthermore, the average reading time for malignant cases was longer compared to benign cases. This indicates that more complex cases, regardless of the DBT technique used, require more time for interpretation.

The recent study by Pujara et al. (21) demonstrated a 12–18% reduction for mean interpretation time by using a 6-mm slab with a 3-mm overlap and no 1-mm slices. A 2019 study by Iotti et al. (17) reported that removing 1-mm slices and using 10-mm slabs with synthetic 2D mammograms resulted in a 20–30% reduction in median interpretation time.

A few studies that discuss reading time have been reviewed (17,21,23). In our study cohort, there were many more cases with positive findings in mammograms than negative ones, and over fifty percent of these symptomatic patients were biopsy-proven to have malignancy. The reading time observed in our study is comparable to that reported by Pujara et al., which was conducted in both screening and diagnostic settings (21).

However, our study’s reading times are relatively greater than in the earlier research by Iotti et al. and Sauer et al. In contrast to our analysis, Iotti et al.’s work was performed in a screening context (17). Apart from differences in the proportion of lesions observed in mammograms, our study identified 55% masses, 24% suspicious microcalcifications, 16% architectural distortions, and 5% asymmetries. In comparison, Sauer et al.’s study reported 56% masses, 14% microcalcifications, 7% architectural distortions, and 23% asymmetries (23). A main distinction between the imaging procedures utilized in Sauer et al.’s study and our study is likely the vendor-specific algorithm, which incorporates artificial intelligence to generate thick slabs. This approach mitigates blurring and improves the delineation of regions of interest. Additionally, the machine-learning model analyzed 1-mm slices for various features, such as circumscribed densities, to enhance lesion conspicuity (23). These methodological differences likely contribute to the longer reading times observed in our study compared to previous research.

Our study had some limitations. First, being conducted at a single institution with a single DBT manufacturer may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study used a clinical workstation in a dedicated setting, and some interruptions in interpretation could not be entirely avoided. Third, the study involved only two readers, which may limit the diversity of interpretation and generalizability of the results. Fourth, only patients presenting with breast symptoms were included in the study cohort. Broadening the inclusion criteria to incorporate cases from both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients would improve the study’s generalizability and validity. Finally, the absence of a CAD system at our institution meant that we could not assess its potential impact on reading time and diagnostic performance. Future research incorporating CAD systems could provide valuable insights and potentially reflect more routine clinical practice.

Conclusions

In this symptomatic patient cohort, the use of a 6-mm thick slabs of DBT effectively reduced interpretation time without compromising diagnostic performance, demonstrating non-inferiority in sensitivity, specificity, and AUC for breast cancer detection compared to 1-mm slices DBT. Given the limited cohort size, further validation in a broader screening or asymptomatic population would be beneficial.
