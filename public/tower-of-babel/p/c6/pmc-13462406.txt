Cerebral Hemodynamic Effects of Ketamine-propofol vs Ketamine-Dexmedetomidine Sedation in Mechanically Ventilated Patients with Severe Traumatic Brain Injury: A Prospective Randomized, Double-Blinded Study
Authors: Pritam Panigrahi, Kajal Jain, Suman Arora, Nidhi Bhatia, Sunil Gupta, Chandrasekhar Gendle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough studies have demonstrated favorable outcomes with ketamine for sedation in traumatic brain injury(TBI), evidence in adults remains limited.1,2 Propofol remains a widely used sedative in TBI3, while dexmedetomidine4 has been increasingly studied as an adjunct sedative. We compared continuous Ketamine–Propofol (KP) and Ketamine–Dexmedetomidine (KD) sedation on cerebral hemodynamics in adults with severe TBI. By assessing efficacy, safety, and effects on cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), intracranial pressure (ICP) and Hemodynamics, it aimed to guide optimal sedation strategies in neurocritical care.ObjectiveThe primary objective was to evaluate the effect of continuous infusion of either sedative combination on maintaining CPP above 60 mmHg. Secondary objectives included assessing excursions in ICP and CPP, mean arterial pressure (MAP) trends, vasopressor use, emergency neurosurgical interventions, ICU stay, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) at discharge, and Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOS-E) at 3 months.Materials and methodsForty-two adults with severe TBI (GCS ≤8) requiring mechanical ventilation were enrolled and randomized using the Sequentially Numbered, Opaque Sealed Envelopes (SNOSE) method into two groups—Ketamine–Propofol (KP) and Ketamine–Dexmedetomidine (KD)—for 48-hour sedation. Intraparenchymal ICP catheters were used for continuous monitoring of ICP, CPP, MAP, and heart rate (HR). ICP >22 mmHg and CPP <60 mmHg were considered crisis events. CPP was maintained between 60–70 mmHg through titration of sedatives and vasopressor support.ResultsBaseline CPP was comparable (p = 0.238). CPP >60 mmHg was maintained in both groups, but KP showed fewer brief period of CPP excursions (median 1 vs 3, p = 0.001); (23 vs 144 minutes, p = 0.001), and longer time within target range (47.6 vs 45.6 hours, p = 0.001). On intragroup analysis, the KD group showed significant CPP fluctuations (p = 0.008), while KP remained stable (p = 0.133). ICP control was superior with KP—fewer excursions (0 vs 2, p = 0.001), lower peak ICP (21 vs 30 mmHg, p = 0.001), and shorter crisis duration (0 vs 28 minutes, p = 0.001). MAP within accepted range remained stable in KP(p = 0.090) but fluctuated in KD(p = 0.036) with increased median vasopressor requirement(1.8 vs 0 µg/kg/min, p = 0.001). HR was steady in KP but declined significantly in KD(p < 0.001) with more bradycardia episodes(median 0 vs 1, p = 0.312). Ventilation duration (6 vs 5 days, p = 0.692), ICU stay (9 vs 8 days, p = 0.500), GCS (7 vs 7, p = 0.563), and GOS-E (6 vs 6, p = 0.106) were comparable. Tiered interventions—decompressive craniectomy (9.5% vs 9.5%), osmotherapy (14.3% vs 23.8%), repeat CT (38.1% vs 42.9%), and hyperventilation (28.6% vs 42.9%)—and mortality (9.5% vs 14.3%, p = 0.634) were also similar.ConclusionKetamine–Propofol provided superior ICP control, greater CPP stability, and sustained MAP with minimal vasopressor use, while Ketamine–Dexmedetomidine was associated with higher hemodynamic variability, bradycardia, and greater vasopressor dependence. Despite these differences, neurological outcomes and mortality were similar. Thus, Ketamine–Propofol offers a more stable and opioid-sparing sedation strategy for severe TBI patients requiring neurocritical care.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, Sedation, Ketamine, Propofol, Dexmedetomidine, Cerebral perfusion pressure, Intracranial pressure
