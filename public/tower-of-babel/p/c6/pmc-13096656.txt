Comparison of explosively driven shock tube and open-air blast wave propagation
Authors: Rachel L. Bauer, Catherine E. Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Explosive research hinges on the comprehension of shock waves, characterized by rapid pressure spikes and an exponential decay back to ambient conditions. Despite the foundational role of shock waves in understanding high-energy events, consistently replicating them accurately remains a challenge. Real-world scenarios rarely conform to the idealized Friedlander waveform, and obstacles such as terrain and structures often introduce complex shock wave interactions. This study examines the dynamics of shock waves, exploring how their propagation and pressure profiles are shaped by distinct test configurations: unconfined, partially confined, and confined charges. Unconfined configurations have charges situated directly on the ground, and produced shock waves marked by a consistent velocity of 400 ± 1.7 m/s and statistically similar Friedlander waveforms. Partially confined tests, featuring an elevated explosive source, had comparable velocities to unconfined charges, but introduced ground reflections, resulting in positive phase impulses up to 16.5% higher than unconfined tests and were not significantly similar. Peak pressures from the partially confined tests varied with charge height, with some 43.9% lower and others 9.5% higher than the unconfined tests. Confined configurations, created within shock tubes, demonstrated a wide range of variability due to vortex rings and internal reflections, ultimately resulting in positive phase impulse 67.5% higher and peak pressure 2% lower than the unconfined configuration and were not statistically similar when the full waveform was considered. These findings underscore the fundamental role of test configuration in shaping shock wave characteristics and the need to understand the full waveform rather than just the initial peak.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42282-9.

Introduction

The study of shock waves is the basis of understanding for the complex dynamics of high-energy events1. A shock wave is commonly characterized by a rapid increase in pressure, followed by an exponential decay back to ambient conditions2–6, often referred to as a Friedlander waveform2,5,7,8. Such waves play a pivotal role in a multitude of scenarios, ranging from studies of traumatic brain injury (TBI)9–11, to assessments of body armor12, architectural structures13, and vehicular components12,14,15.

One of the major challenges in shock wave research lies in replicating real-world situations that seldom match the textbook Friedlander waveform. In practical scenarios shock waves interact with obstacles and structures, leading to reflections that alter the waveform shape4. When a shock wave encounters a solid surface, be it a wall or the ground itself, a reflected wave is generated16–18. This secondary shock wave trails behind the incident shock wave, imparting extra force on objects situated downstream19 and eventually merging with the incident wave to form a Mach stem20. Reflection effects are observed in many weapon systems such as the Carl Gustaf recoilless rifle, the M72 LAW or the AT4, where multiple peaks can occur from reflections off the surrounding environment and the operator8,21. Similarly, in breaching scenarios, reflected waves interacting with personnel can produce additional pressure spikes21,22.

In confined environments, like shock tubes23–26 or subterranean passages27, interactions of multiple reflections leads to waveforms that deviate from the Friedlander curve5,8,28. Many pre-clinical studies report only the initial peak pressure, sometimes truncating the profile5,11,29–35, potentially omitting crucial information regarding subsequent shock wave reflections. This limitation becomes particularly evident outside a shock tube exit, where a vortex ring forms in the wake of the shock wave36,37. The influence of this vortex ring on the shock wave persists until their eventual separation. Following this, the pressure profile begins to align with the Friedlander wave. Extending the scope of the pressure profile past the initial shock wave impact reveals distinctive pressure spikes attributable to the vortex ring collision, as well as reflections originating from within the shock tube itself28.

Explosive test sites and university laboratories use different geometries, shock tunnel designs33,38,39, and open-air setups40–42. Given this diversity in test setup, there is a need to understand how nominally similar tests compare across laboratories. A recent multi-laboratory study demonstrated that even when peak pressure is matched, the resulting waveforms can differ in duration and impulse39. Such findings show the need to quantify how test configuration alone alters the pressure-time history, particularly when results are compared across facilities.

Unconfined, partially confined, and confined test configurations are capable of producing shock waves with comparable velocities and peak pressures, yet the resulting pressure profiles differ due to their varying degrees of confinement. Figure 1 provides a visual representation of the unconfined, partially confined, and fully confined configurations, complemented by projected pressure profiles drawn from extensive literature and prior research2,3,5,8,11,28,43–45. While it is known that setup configuration can change the pressure waveform profile, to the authors knowledge, no study has directly compared all three under the same input energy to understand how much the waveform shape truly changes to quantify the difference. The current work isolates the influence of geometry by using a repeatable small scale energy source and matching incident shock velocity for all three configurations. This allows differences in waveform shape, duration, and impulse to be attributed to the confinement effects rather than charge variability, environmental conditions, or scaling. While classic cube-root scaling can match peak pressure between different charge sizes, duration will change. Since unconfined, partially confined, and confined setups inherently alter both the pressure and duration, direct comparisons must be made at the same scale with the same input energy.

Shock wave research is complex and requires a thorough understanding of the environment the waves travel in. While peak overpressure is a commonly reported metric, it does not fully describe the loading experienced by a target. Duration, impulse, and negative phase of the shock wave can affect structural response and injury mechanisms3,5,11. By evaluating the full positive and negative phases, this study clarifies how confinement modifies the shock environment even when the incident shock appears similar. This comparison shows how nominally similar laboratory tests may misrepresent open-field or operational blast conditions. It also helps researchers design experiments that better reflect real world exposures and interpret results without overestimating the protective performance of materials or structures based solely on peak pressure.

Methods

Three different test configurations, unconfined, partially confined and confined, were used to study shock formation, travel, and impact. Gold wire, 0.038 mm in diameter, was used to form the shock wave. The gold wire was initiated using 4,000 volts from a Teledyne Risi FS-43 firing system. Gold wire was wrapped between metal bolts two cm apart and held in place using locking nuts. A high voltage cable was attached to the back of the bolts and connected to the firing system. This test set-up of the nuts, bolts, and gold wire was kept consistent for all the tests. The use of gold wire is based on the principle of an exploding bridgewire (EBW) detonator, in which a thin wire is rapidly vaporized by a high-voltage discharge to produce a shock front. Although no formal testing standard currently exists, this approach has been applied in prior blast and shock research to provide consistent and controlled initiation across unconfined, partially confined, and confined environments46–51. In this study, the exploding wire is not the subject of investigation, but rather a repeatable laboratory scale tool used to isolate the influence of test configuration. The fixed 4,000 V input ensures that each test begins with the same energy release at the source. Unconfined tests had the gold wire at the base of the test setup, or floor, partially confined tests placed the gold wire higher on the bolts, introducing a ground reflection, and confined tests utilized the same bolts placed inside a 3D printed shock tube. For the unconfined and partially confined tests, the gold wire was placed 18 cm away from the target which was located at a height of 10 cm. Partially confined tests had the gold wire raised to different heights for each test, ranging from 0.5 to 4 cm from the surface. The shock tube had a length of 5 cm with an inside diameter of 0.5 cm giving a length to diameter ratio of 10. The end of the shock tube was 4.5 cm from the target. This study aimed to deliver the same energy to the target across all test configurations using incident shock waves that traveled at similar velocities. Previous research has shown relationships between pressure and velocity52, supporting the use of shock velocity as a constant. In the confined case, the shorter 4.5 cm standoff distance was selected based on preliminary testing to match the incident shock velocity observed in the unconfined and partially confined cases. The shock front exiting the shock tube had a smaller surface area initially, meaning the decay occurred over a reduced timeframe. This resulted in a closer distance between the charge location and sensor compared to the unconfined or partially confined tests setups for the same shock velocity. Schlieren imaging with a two-mirror, Z-pattern setup utilizing 135-mm diameter parabolic mirrors was used to observe the shock waves from each test configuration. The mirrors had a focal length of 1 m, and a point light source of 50 lumens was used in experimental testing. This schlieren configuration has been used previously in studies of small-scale explosive and shock tube tests26,36,38, providing high spatial and temporal resolution of shock front evolution. A Phantom v201253 high-speed camera with a frame rate of 100 000 fps and resolution of 384 × 384 captured the propagation of the shock waves. A PCB flush mount pressure sensor (Model 102B18)54 was used to measure the pressure from each test configuration at the target location, mounted in a solid wood structure, 10 cm from the floor. The wood structure was 2000 times the size of the sensor in order to mimic and an infinite wall condition and to capture all the energy from the shock waves. Five repeats of each test were performed with the pressure sensor to study the effect of the different test setups and height of the partially confined test. Point tracking of the shock wave indicated that while there was initial decay observed after the first frame, subsequent analysis showed a consistent velocity over the remaining travel distance. The velocity measurements were taken from the tracked velocity from the second frame until impact with the sensor which showed no decay over this travel distance.

The vaporization of the wire was recorded using a Shimadzu HPV-X2 ultra high-speed camera55 at 10 million fps to verify that the wire vaporized uniformly along its entire length. Figure 3A-C shows the gold wire vaporization at 0, 200, and 400 ns respectively, with the entire wire illuminated at once. After this validation, shock wave propagation was recorded at 500,000 fps to observe its expansion. To verify radial symmetry, tests were imaged from two orthogonal orientations, a front view aligned with the wire axis (0° orientation, Fig. 3D–F), and then with the setup rotated 90 degrees about the vertical axis (Fig. 3D–F). This two-angle imaging confirms the shock wave propagated radially and uniformly in all directions.

Results and analysis

Three different charge configurations were evaluated: unconfined, partially confined, and confined. All shock waves were generated by running 4000 Volts through a 0.038-mm gold wire. Each setup was evaluated using incident and reflected shock wave velocity, and statistical analysis of the pressure waveforms. To compare the pressure profiles from each test, the values were normalized with respect to the unconfined charges. Timeline figures for each test configuration can be found in the Supplemental Information. These evaluation methods helped to gain an understanding of the reliability and characteristics of each test setup.

Unconfined configuration

The unconfined test setup consisted of a gold wire placed flat on the ground, producing a single incident shock wave. Figure 4 shows this shock wave moving right to left towards the target, with relative positions of both the sensor and gold wire. In all schlieren figures, the black dot in the center is the alignment dot for the two mirrors. Figure 4A shows the shock wave at 180 µs and Fig. 4B is taken 60 µs later to show the propagation. The shock wave is still expanding radially and does not become planar before impacting the target. Incident shock waves from the unconfined setup travelled at a consistent average velocity of 400 ± 2.7 m/s. Phantom Camera Control software56 was used to track shock front position frame-by-frame over the entire visible propagation. Distance and time data in the software was used to calculate the resulting shock velocity and there was no decay present over the recorded duration of travel.

Figure 5 shows the pressure profiles for each of the five tests which exhibit a sharp rise to peak pressure followed by decay and substantial negative phase. Measurements from the unconfined configuration have little to no coefficient of variation, and the pressure profiles are aligned, showing good repeatability in the test setup. To enable a comparison between charge setups, all measurements were normalized to the positive unconfined results. The average unconfined peak overpressure was 4.76 ± 0.2 kPa, which was set at a normalized value of 1.00. The same normalization was applied to the incident impulse, and total positive phase impulse. Negative phase metrics including peak underpressure, negative impulse, and total negative impulse are expressed relative to the positive phase baseline allowing comparison of all waveform features.

Impulse was calculated as the time integral of the measured pressure history and was evaluated separately for the positive and negative phases. Incident positive phase impulse was calculated using only the first positive pressure spike and all data before the pressure drops below zero. Total positive phase impulse was also calculated for each test using all the positive values within a fixed 0.2 ms integration window. This window was selected based on the unconfined configuration, which returned to ambient conditions after the full positive and negative phase within this time, and was applied uniformly across all configurations to ensure consistency. Incident negative phase impulse was calculated using the first negative pressure spike while total negative impulse was calculated over a 0.2 ms window starting from the onset of underpressure. The coefficient of variation for each normalized value was calculated as the ratio between standard deviation and mean and is presented as a percentage (shown in Table 2 for the unconfined tests). Additionally, the true engineering units and confidence interval are provided in Supplementary Information Tables S1 through S5 as a reference for amplitude of the shock waves. Statistical analysis was performed using two-sample t-tests to evaluate repeatability of each test configuration. T-tests were applied both to the initial peak region and to the full pressure-time waveform.

The unconfined tests show the strongest and most classical negative phase with a similar positive and negative phase impulse. After normalization using the positive phase baseline, the peak underpressure is -0.62 as seen in both Fig. 5; Table 2. Since all negative phase values were normalized using the positive unconfined baseline, the negative phase impulse of -0.99 indicates that the negative impulse is almost identical in magnitude to the positive impulse. The unconfined configuration produces only a single incident shock with no reflected wave returning to the sensor, as such, the incident positive phase impulse and total positive phase impulse are identical. Additionally, the high coefficient of variation for the rise time reflects the fact that the rise occurs almost instantaneously, even with the 2 MHz sampling rate, the data acquisition system only captures about 7 data points on the rising edge, so small differences appear as large percentage variations.

Partially confined configuration

The partially confined configuration utilized the same test setup as the unconfined, but raised the gold wire by 0.5 cm in each of the eight tests. The horizontal distance was held constant for all partially confined tests. Increasing the charge height alters the angle of incidence of shock wave arrival at the pressure sensor, causing the incident wave to strike at a less oblique (more normal) angle. This effect may slightly influence the measured peak pressures at different charge heights. The partially confined setup produces a ground reflection and second pressure spike. The 0.5 and 1-cm charges do not have a secondary spike because the incident shock wave and the ground reflection have merged to form one shock wave before hitting the target. This convergence of the incident shock wave and the ground reflection creates a Mach stem. The point of convergence of the incident shock, reflected shock, and Mach stem is called the triple point. Areas above the triple point will receive impacts from both the incident shock and the reflected shock, while areas below the triple point only experience the effects of the Mach stem. The incident shock wave, reflected shock wave, Mach stem, and triple point can all be seen in Fig. 6 for the 2.5-cm charge. The velocity of the incident shock wave was point tracked using the Phantom Camera Control (PCC) software during the entire time it was visible, resulting in a velocity of 400 ± 3.4 m/s. As the incident shock wave travels towards the target, it merges with the reflected wave, creating the Mach stem and planar shock wave that will impact the target.

Figure 7 shows the pressure profiles for the eight partially confined configurations at charge heights of 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, and 4 cm off the ground. Each pressure profile represents one of the five repeats from each test. All heights show a fast rise time and incident peak pressure, but reflections from the ground arrive at different times. Arrival time of the ground reflection, the second peak in the figure, increases with increasing charge height. The 0.5 and 1-cm charges do not have a visible ground reflection as the triple point is above the sensor. These two charges also have visibly higher peak pressures despite the similarities in incident shock velocity among the charges. With the visibly higher peak pressure, the area under the initial peak is also higher, resulting in a higher incident impulse. Although peak pressure generally decays with propagation distance57, the present experiments were conducted over short distances specifically chosen to minimize decay, with average shock velocity of 400 ± 2.7 m/s. Under these conditions, differences in peak pressure observed at matched shock velocity are attributed to confinement effects rather than distance.

Figure 7 clearly shows the difference in the timing of the shock reflection, which is a result of different triple point heights (TPH). There are models to determine the TPH if certain charge metrics are known such as charge size, height from the ground, and distance from the measurement point58,59. Some of these formulas, such as the Unified Facilities Criteria (UFC) and Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC) model require the charge weight in order to calculate scaled measurements60,61. The Kinney and Graham model, and subsequent Needham formula do not require a charge mass, instead using the incident shock velocity58,62. Since charge weight was not known in this study, but incident shock velocity was, the Kinney and Graham formula for two different charge heights is shown in Fig. 8 with approximated incident and reflected wave locations based on spherical wave expansion. The models show a clear difference between the arrival of the reflected shock on the sensor.

Table 3 shows the normalized and actual measurements of the partially confined configuration, where the normalized measurements are normalized from the average of the unconfined configuration. For charges above 1.5 cm, the incident positive phase impulse is lower than the unconfined setup, but when including the shock wave reflection, the total positive phase impulse is equal to or higher than the unconfined setup. The additional pressure peak from the ground reflection provides more positive pressure and thus an increase in total impulse. For the 0.5 and 1-cm charges, the incident impulse values are 14.8% higher than the unconfined setup. The unconfined configuration just has the incident wave hitting the target while the partially confined has a Mach stem impacting the target. This Mach stem travels at a slightly higher velocity than the incident shock from the unconfined, averaging 411 ± 4 m/s compared to 399 ± 2 m/s. Despite the modest increase in velocity, the Mach stem produces both increased pressure and impulse. This is due to the merging of the incident wave and reflection, creating a stronger shock at the Mach stem. The 0.5 and 1-cm charges are the only ones in which a planar wave impacts the sensor, which could also contribute to the higher peak pressure and impulse values. This difference in geometry can be seen in Fig. 4 (radially expanding shock wave) and Fig. 6 (planar shock wave) where the planar shock wave will have a greater surface area to impact the target and thus increased pressure and duration. This has been shown in literature in reflected shock measurements where a larger target results in increased pressure and impulse2. The measurements for the partially confined configuration exhibit greater variation compared to unconfined configuration due to variations in height from the ground, resulting in unique reflected waves for each test. At charge heights of 0.5 and 1 cm where no ground reflection occurs, the coefficient of variation across each measurement is low. However, the introduction of ground reflections increases inconsistencies, leading to increased coefficient of variation, with the highest being in incident negative phase impulse at 2 cm of 89%. While this study produced relatively low pressure (less than 5 kPa), and at these magnitudes, even small deviations are exaggerated in the coefficient of variation, other configurations in this work have much lower coefficients of variation. This indicates that the higher variation in partially confined heights above 1 cm comes from the test configuration itself rather than the magnitude of measurement. Peak pressures were consistent across repeats, but variability increased when ground or internal reflections were present, showing the need to evaluate the entire pressure profile, rather than just the peak pressure. Elevated charges are often used to prevent the cratering caused by a surface hemispherical (unconfined) charge or to mimic battlefield conditions that have multiple peaks. Larger scale tests with elevated charges have reported consistent results42,57,63,64.

Table 3 shows variation in peak pressure, impulse, and rise time observed across different charge heights for partially confined configuration. Notably, up to the 2 cm charge height, the total positive phase impulse surpasses that of the unconfined configuration, increasing from 1.00 up to 1.18. For the 0.5 and 1 cm charges, where just the Mach stem impacted the sensor, the normalized peak pressures were 1.09 and 1.10, corresponding to 9–10% higher values than the unconfined configuration baseline. However, for charges exceeding 2 cm, both peak pressure and impulse values demonstrate a decline compared to the unconfined configuration, with peak pressure as low as 0.64 and total positive phase impulse 0.86. As the charge height increases, the reflected shock occurs later, due to the increased time and distance from the charge before the incident wave impacts the ground. The reflected shock can push behind the incident shock, helping to increase peak pressure and impulse. With the increase in charge height, and later occurrence of the reflected shock, its force pushes less on the incident shock, ultimately leading to the lower peak pressure and impulse. This trend may appear counterintuitive since raising the charge height also reduces the radial distance between the explosion and the sensor. However, the reduced peak pressure can be attributed to two main effects: diminished constructive interference and geometric spreading. In the unconfined configuration (0 cm), the incident and ground-reflected shock waves arrive at the sensor simultaneously and constructively interfere, boosting the peak pressure. As the charge height increases, the reflected wave arrives with a time delay and at a different angle, reducing this additive effect. Additionally, increasing the standoff causes the shock to spread over a larger spherical surface before reaching the sensor, leading to more rapid attenuation. These combined effects explain why peak pressure decreases, even though the source moves closer in terms of horizontal distance. The 1.5-cm and 2-cm charges exhibit lower peak pressures (0.84 and 0.78) and initial positive phase impulses (0.77 and 0.66) compared to the unconfined configuration. However, they have a higher total positive phase impulse (1.18 and 1.16) due to the additional influence from the ground reflection. Total positive phase impulse is up to 16.5% higher than the unconfined configuration for charges 2 cm and lower, but above 2.5 cm, the total positive phase impulse is up to 15.1% lower than the unconfined configuration. Rise times for both the unconfined and partially confined configuration are similar to each other, with the partially confined configuration having a higher variation as the charge height increases. This variation could be due to small changes in the setup if the wire wasn’t level or tight.

Unlike the confined configuration, the partially confined tests produced weaker negative phases and the magnitude depends on the charge height. Table 3 shows that as the charge heigh increases, peak underpressure and negative phase impulse tend to decrease. When a test condition results in reflected waves and the formation of a Mach stem, the resulting waves have decreased negative characteristics. At heights where just the Mach stem impacts the sensor (0.5 and 1 cm) the negative phase is strongest of all the partially confined conditions. At heights of 1.5 cm and above, the reflected wave can begin to overlap with early portions of the incident negative phase, essentially interrupting the negative phase. This can explain the high variation in negative phase impulse and high coefficients of variation as very small differences in reflected wave timing can drastically affect the negative phase.

Confined configuration

A shock tube was used to create a confined environment where the shock wave reflects off all surfaces and all the reflected shocks from the back of the tube contribute to the positive phase impulse. This tube measured 5 cm in length and 0.5 cm inside diameter, for a length to diameter ratio of 10. Figure 9A shows the shock tube, incident shock wave and vortex ring created at the tube exit with the shock wave and vortex ring traveling from right to left. Figure 9B shows the vortex ring with a reflected shock exiting the tube 250 µs after the vortex ring. Additional shock reflections exit the tube after the one depicted. This shock, travelling at 395 ± 6.4 m/s, is caused by the closed back end of the tube, a common design, especially in gas driven confined test setups. The incident shock waves from the confined configuration travelled at an average velocity of 404 ± 4.6 m/s measured between 0 and 100 µs, which is the total time from when shock wave leaves the shock tube to impacting the target. The shock wave impacting the target is radially expanding and does not impact the target as a planar wave. The vortex ring travelled at an average velocity of 89 ± 0.1 m/s, measured over the entire travel duration.

Figure 10 shows the pressure profile from the confined configuration and clearly shows the effect of the vortex ring and reflections inside the shock tube. Pressure from the vortex ring occurs at around 0.12 ms, with additional peaks occurring after due to the shock reflections depicted in Fig. 9B. An incident peak similar to the unconfined and partially confined configuration is present, but the positive phase duration is shorter and there is a notable absence of a large negative phase following the incident shock. This lack of negative pressure is because the vortex ring is continually pushing forward and gases from the shock tube keep providing pressure on the target. The reflections and oscillating pressure spikes continue for multiple spikes, unlike the partially confined configuration that just have a single reflection. Although the present study did not include velocity-field measurements capable of isolating the vortex contribution, the combination of repeated reflected shocks and sustained outflow from the tube provide an explanation for the observed prolonged positive pressure and suppressed negative phase.

The confined configuration produces the smallest negative phase of the three setups. As seen in Table 4, the peak underpressure reached only − 0.23, and the total negative phase impulse was − 0.39, less than half the magnitude seen in unconfined tests. This reduction can be attributed to the confinement from the shock tube. Multiple shock reflections and a vortex ring maintain sustained forward momentum of air, preventing the pressure from dropping below ambient conditions. Even behind the vortex ring, additional reflected shocks further interrupt the negative phase development.

Figure 12 shows a distinct pressure profile from each of the three test configurations to show the characteristic waveform features. For the unconfined and confined configurations, shock wave behavior was repeatable across repeats, so a single curve is representative of all tests in those setups. For the partially confined tests, waveform shapes varied with standoff height; the curve shown corresponds to the 2 cm standoff, which is the midpoint of the tested range and represents an average waveform for this configuration. The unconfined tests return to ambient conditions with no additional reflections at 0.2 ms while partially confined tests only return to ambient conditions around 0.26 ms. Confined tests never fully return to ambient conditions during the entire 0.4 ms recording period.

All configurations also have visibly different negative phases. The unconfined configuration produces a waveform with a rapid pressure rise, followed by a gradual decay and a pronounced negative phase. This negative phase occurs because, in an open-field explosion, air is rapidly pushed outward by the expanding shock wave and fireball, creating a low-pressure region behind the shock front. Ambient air then rushes back in to fill this void, generating a significant underpressure or vacuum effect. This phenomenon is typical in free-field blast environments and is responsible for the large negative phase seen in the unconfined tests. The partially confined tests produce a slightly smaller negative phase compared to the unconfined case, due to reflections from the nearby ground surface partially filling the low-pressure zone sooner. However, the general mechanism is similar, and a clear negative phase is still present. In contrast, the confined tests exhibit little to no negative phase. This is because the continuous flow of gas and multiple shock reflections exiting the shock tube maintain positive pressure on the target surface for an extended time. The sustained influx of gases prevents the formation of the low-pressure zone typical in open-field blasts, thereby suppressing the negative phase entirely.

Partially confined tests have a smaller initial negative phase. Confined tests have little to no negative phase due to the vortex ring and shock reflections exiting the shock tube providing more positive pressure and not allowing time for a negative phase. The negative phase is important in explosives research as it can cause loads on structures in the opposite direction of the positive phase resulting in additional damage and some test setups may not accurately represent this.

Table 5 shows the total positive phase duration for all configurations up to 0.2 ms with their associated coefficient of variation. Unconfined tests show a consistent positive phase duration from the five conducted, with a coefficient of variation of ± 6.6%. The partially confined tests have varying positive phase durations. For the tests in which there is no reflected wave that impacts the sensor, the positive phase duration is 16.2% lower than the unconfined tests. But when a shock reflection is present, the positive phase duration increases compared to the unconfined test. Above 2 cm, the duration increases with increasing charges height. The confined configuration has the highest total positive phase duration, primarily attributed to the vortex ring generated at the shock tube exit. Additionally, reflections within the tube contribute to sustained pressure, further elongating the positive phase duration. The positive phase duration is significantly influenced by shock reflections, both from the ground in partially confined tests, and from the tube walls and back of the confined tests.

The trend observed in Table 5, where the positive phase duration decreases initially and then increases with greater height for partially confined tests, can be linked to the interaction of the shock wave with the ground. For the unconfined configuration (directly on the ground), the shock wave is quickly reflected, resulting in a relatively short duration. In urban or battlefield environments, rigid structures act similarly to ground reflections, causing variations in duration extensions to the pressure wave. These reflections off walls and buildings introduce second, third, or more peaks in the pressure profile, contributing to longer positive phase durations. This extended duration increases the risk of damage to nearby structures or injury to personnel. Similarly, confined blasts (such as those in enclosed spaces or tubes) would exhibit even longer durations due to multiple reflections, emphasizing the need for protective measures that account for both initial shock impacts and secondary effects from extended pressure durations.

The Bass model68 considers not only peak pressure but also the duration of the positive phase in determining injury outcomes from blast exposure. This model highlights that higher peak pressures alone do not fully account for the severity of injuries; rather, the combination of sustained pressure over time (i.e., positive phase duration) plays a critical role in the damage caused to tissues and organs. While this study does not propose a new model, the experimental measurements presented here provide data that reflect trends consistent with the principles of the Bass model. By analyzing positive phase duration alongside peak pressure, this work acknowledges the importance of prolonged exposure to elevated pressure. This is particularly relevant in situations where partially confined or confined blasts produce longer durations due to reflections or multiple shock waves, which could result in more severe injuries even if the peak pressure is not high.

Conclusion

This study directly compared unconfined, partially confined, and confined shock generation configurations under identical input energy to isolate how test geometry alone alters shock waveform characteristics. By matching incident shock velocity and using a repeatable small scale energy source, the effects of confinement and reflections could be evaluated independently of charge variability or scaling assumptions. The results show that when a reflected wave is present, the total positive phase impulse is up to 20.1% higher and positive phase duration is 16.5% higher than the unconfined charges. Confined configurations result in a 2% lower peak pressure, 67.5% higher total positive phase impulse and 49.6% higher positive phase duration when compared to unconfined charges. The vortex ring and shock wave reflections from inside are the driving forces behind this increase and show that extended time durations should be analyzed. The purpose of this study was to highlight the differences between confined, unconfined, and partially confined blast scenarios and care must be taken when selecting a blast model so that it represents what is intended in the study.

The three test setups produced distinct waveform features, unconfined configurations generated repeatable Friedlander-type waveforms with strong negative phases, and similar positive and negative impulse. The partially confined configuration introduced ground reflections and Mach stems that increased pressure and impulse at lower charge heights, and confined configurations produced extended positive phase impulse and duration with little to no negative phase due to vortex rings and internal reflections. This study represents the first direct comparison of all three common laboratory test setups using the same input energy. Previous work has generally compared only two configurations, but by examining all three side by side, these results demonstrate how different waveform characteristics can emerge even when a simple shock wave is expected.

Peak pressure is not the only factor that should be considered when evaluating different test setups. The Friedlander waveform is a good benchmark for comparison and is widely recognized among researchers in the field. While the unconfined configurations produce a waveform with a rapid rise, decay and negative phase, many real-life scenarios include shock reflections and complex pressure profiles such as those from partially confined and confined configurations. Therefore, understanding the test setup and its implications on other parameters such as secondary peaks, duration, impulse and negative phase will allow for the design of relevant tests designed to mimic scenarios from the field. This study highlights several opportunities for future work. For example, the observed relationship between charge height, distance, and the time interval between incident and reflected shocks suggests that a method could be developed to calculate triple point height from timing between peaks. Extending this work through multi-scale simulations and multi-scale studies would help quantify how these configuration dependent waveform differences translate to large scale blasts.
