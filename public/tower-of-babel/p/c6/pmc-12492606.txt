Amelioration of emotional processing deficits in individuals with high trait anxiety following high-intensity interval training
Authors: Chengcheng Li, Xiaoyi Che, Jingjing Yu, Fei Yan, Yu Fang, Pengjun Zhao, Jian Zhang, Lin Zhao, Xue Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHow does exercise alleviate anxiety related emotional processing deficits? This study measured the effect of both high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) on motor cortical excitability corresponding to emotional perception in individuals with high trait anxiety.MethodsParticipants included 16 adults (10 women) with high trait anxiety and were asked to execute an emotion recognition task before and after HIIT, MICT and a control session. During the task, Transcranial magnetic stimulation was applied to the right primary motor cortex 150 ms (early stage) or 300 ms (late stage) after the onset of positive, negative, or neutral images, with the motor-evoked potential (MEP) amplitudes measured. State anxiety was measured using the state portion of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory.ResultsIt showed that at 150 ms, HIIT increased MEP amplitudes corresponding to negative stimuli (p < 0.001), and MICT increased MEP amplitudes corresponding to positive stimuli (p = 0.022). State anxiety score was decreased only after HIIT (p = 0.027).ConclusionsBoth HIIT and MICT could ameliorate emotional processing deficits by enhancing motor cortical excitability corresponding to emotional perception, and the improvement especially to negative emotions perception by HIIT may be more important for alleviating anxiety. The trial was retrospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2500108605; date: 2025/09/02).Keywords: Anxiety, Emotion, Exercise, Cortical Excitability, Motor-Evoked Potential, Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation

Introduction

Anxiety is defined as feelings of excessive worry, restlessness, tiring easily, trouble concentrating, and difficulty sleeping (American Psychiatric Association & American Psychiatric Association, 2013). Individuals with high anxiety are characterized by emotional processing deficits [1] such as abnormal emotional perception, especially with a tendency to suppress negative emotions, which in turn increases anxiety severity [2, 3]. Emotional processing deficits greatly influence how individual’s experience and regulate emotion [4]; hence, figuring out how to alleviate emotional processing deficits is essential for anxiety relief.

Both high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) have anxiolytic effects in healthy and clinical populations [5, 6]. HIIT, characterized by alternating short bursts of high-intensity exercise with recovery periods of rest or low intensity exercise, has proven highly effective for improving various mental health parameters [7]. In the case of generalized anxiety disorder, it has been found that both HIIT and lower-intensity training (LIT) showed moderate or large effects on clinical outcomes, with HIIT generally producing effects approximately twice as large as those observed with LIT [8]. MICT has also been found to reduce state anxiety to a greater extent than low-intensity and high-intensity continuous training [9]. When comparing MICT and HIIT, the findings are mixed. Some studies suggest that MICT may offer greater anxiolytic benefits, showing significant reductions in anxiety levels among middle-aged overweight men and patients with Parkinson's disease, while HIIT did not produce a notable change in anxiety levels [10, 11]. Conversely, other research reports superior anxiolytic effects with HIIT, particularly noting significantly greater reductions in anxiety scores among women with polycystic ovary syndrome compared to the MICT group [12, 13]. A meta-analysis further indicated that the difference in anxiety levels between HIIT and MICT groups was not statistically significant [14]. However, how these two exercise modes improve emotional processing deficits in anxious individuals is less well-explored.

Psychophysiology, a field of study examining the physiological bases of psychological processes, enables the measure of both exercise and emotional perception. Substantive evidence has demonstrated that both MICT and HIIT increase cortical excitability at the physical level [15–18]. Motor cortex excitability, as an electrophysiology index, is correlated with anxiety, with abnormalities in motor cortex excitability reported in individuals with anxiety [19–21]. Moreover, motor cortex excitability is also involved in emotional perception, reflecting the degree of the mobilization of the motor system to launch adaptive motor responses to specific emotion stimuli [22, 23]. The temporal dynamics of emotional perception–related cortical excitability can be investigated through single-pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) during performance of an emotion recognition paradigm that asks participants to categorize an emotional stimulus [24, 25]. With the TMS coil applied over the primary motor cortex (M1), motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) are collected from a contralateral target muscle to index motor excitability [26]. Two functionally distinct stages of motor cortex involvement in emotional perception have been detected: an early attention phase at 150 ms after the onset of an image that corresponds to action preparation, and a late evaluation phase at 300 ms that corresponds to action regulation [27]. Lateralization of motor excitability during emotional perception has also been demonstrated using single-pulse TMS and has highlighted the role of the right hemisphere in implementing a rapid and transient facilitatory response to emotionally arousing stimuli [28, 29].

Here, we investigated the effect of an acute bout of HIIT or MICT on emotional perception–related cortical excitability. There is some evidence that, compared with individuals scoring within the normal range of anxiety, those scoring with elevated anxiety will experience greater psychological improvements from exercise [30]. In the present study of individuals with high trait anxiety, we applied TMS to the right M1 150 ms or 300 ms after the onset of a positive, negative, or neutral image displayed on a computer screen during an emotion recognition task that was performed before and after HIIT, MICT, and a control session. Based on the greater beneficial effect of HIIT compared with MICT observed in previous studies [14, 31], we hypothesized that while both types of exercise would modulate emotional perception–related cortical excitability, HIIT would be significantly more effective.

Materials and methods

Participants

The aim of this participant recruitment was to select a group of individuals with high-trait anxiety. In total 362 students from Shanghai University of Sport prescreened on the basis of scores on the trait portion of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [32]. The participant inclusion criteria were a trait score of ≥ 45, consistent with previous studies [33, 34] and meeting the requirements of the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q) [35]. The other exclusion criteria were diagnosis of a psychiatric or neurological disorder and use of medications or other nonmedical substances that may affect the central nervous system. In total, 16 students participated this study (10 women; mean age: 20.4 ± 2.5 years; trait anxiety score: 54.9 ± 3.5). The included participants were right-hand dominant and reported vision that was normal or corrected to normal. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport and was conducted consistent with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). All selected participants provided written informed consent prior to engaging in the study.

Questionnaire

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) is a widely used tool designed to evaluate anxiety through two components: state anxiety, which captures temporary emotional responses to specific situations, and trait anxiety, which reflects a more enduring predisposition to experience anxiety [32, 36]. The state section consists of 20 items that measure how individuals feel right now, and the trait section, also comprising 20 items, evaluates general anxiety tendencies by assessing how individuals typically feel. Each item was rated on a 4-point scale from “almost never” to “almost always”.

The Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q) included seven standard screening questions assessing participants' readiness for physical activity by identifying potential health risks such as cardiovascular issues, musculoskeletal problems, and other medical conditions that might require consultation with a healthcare professional before engaging in exercise [35]. Participants who answered "yes" to any one of the questions should be excluded from this experiment.

Procedure and experimental design

A randomized controlled crossover design was employed in this experiment, comprising three visits scheduled one week apart, each conducted at the same time of day. In each visit, the participant was asked to complete the emotion recognition task with TMS applied over right M1 before and after an exercise intervention with either HIIT, MICT or a control session (randomly assigned). State anxiety was also measured before and after the intervention using the state anxiety portion from STAI. Additionally, participants were instructed not to engage in exercise or consume beverages containing caffeine, alcohol, or tea for 24 h before each visit. Exercise/control sessions

All participants exercised on a treadmill according to the provided instructions for each session, which included a warm-up (5 min), main exercise (25 min), and cool down (5 min). The procedure of HIIT and MICT is shown in the Fig. 1B. The maximum heart rate (HRmax) was calculated as [(208 - Age) × 0.7]. The intensity of each exercise intervention for each participant was defined based on the heart rate reserve (HRR), calculated as HRmax minus resting heart rate (HRrest). Hence, the target HR was calculated as [(% exercise intensity × HRR) + HRrest]. The HIIT session consisted of five 2-min periods at approximately 90% HRR, each period followed by a 3-min recovery period at approximately 40% HRR [37]. The MICT session was for 25 min at 60% HRR [38]. Heart rate was monitored by a Polar H10 heart rate sensor (Finland) during the entire exercise session to ensure the correct exercise intensity. For the control session, participants were asked to read for 30 min. The rating of perceived exertion (RPE) was assessed during the final 1 min of exercise, for the HIIT session, RPE was collected during the final 1 min of 90% HRR period and 60%HRR period.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

We used a 70-mm coil in a figure-eight shape attached to a Magstim 200 stimulator (Magstim Company, Dyfed, UK) to apply single-pulse TMS. Stimulation was applied over the right M1. We determined the motor hotspot for each participant by finding the position of the coil with the handle aimed rearward at an angle of 30°–45° from the mid-sagittal line that would generate the largest MEP in the target muscle when we applied slight suprathreshold stimulation. At 150 or 300 ms after an image was displayed on the computer screen, we applied single TMS pulses to the right M1. The TMS intensity was set for each participant at 120% of their resting motor threshold (RMT), which was the lowest intensity that produced MEPs of > 50 μV in the resting target muscle in at least 5 of 10 attempts.

Electromyographic recording

We used electromyography (EMG) to record signals from the left first dorsal interosseous muscle. Participants sat in a chair with their eyes open (except to blink) during recording with Ag–AgCl surface electrodes (9 mm in diameter) attached in a muscle belly–tendon montage. We amplified the EMG signals by 1 K before band pass filtering them at 20 Hz–2.5 kHz. We digitally sampled the signals at 5 kHz using a Micro 1401 (Cambridge Electronics Design, Cambridge, UK). The data were stored on a computer and were analyzed offline using SIGNAL 6.0 software (Cambridge Electronics Design, Cambridge, UK).

Emotional images

The emotional stimuli comprised 20 positive images (mean valence: 6.3 ± 0.3, mean arousal: 5.3 ± 0.3), 20 neutral images (mean valence: 5.0 ± 0.2, mean arousal: 4.0 ± 0.9) and 20 negative images (mean valence: 4.1 ± 0.4, mean arousal: 5.2 ± 0.2) from the International Affective Image System [39]. There was a significant main effect of image type on valence (F(2, 38) = 285.317, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.938) and arousal (F(2, 38) = 48.961, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.720) as indicated by the results of one-way repeated-measures (RM) analyses of variance (ANOVA). Post-hoc analyses indicated that the differences in valence between each of the two image types (ps < 0.001) were statistically significant, and that arousal values for positive and for negative images were significantly larger than those for neutral images (ps < 0.001).

Emotion recognition task

We used MATLAB to program and to run the emotion recognition task and to control TMS. Participants viewed each image and then indicated whether they considered the image to be positive, negative, or neutral [28]. Each participant viewed and categorized 60 images that were presented once each in a random order. Those 60 trials were separated into two blocks of 30 trials each. Before each trial, a fixation point was presented for 1000 ms in the center of the screen. When the fixation point disappeared, an image appeared on the left or right side of the screen with equal probability. The image persisted for 160 ms or 310 ms, again with equal probability for each. When the image was presented for 160 ms, TMS was applied 150 ms after the presentation onset, and when the image was presented for 310 ms, TMS was applied 300 ms after stimulus onset. A random-dot mask appeared for 1 s after each image, followed by the question ‘‘What did you see?’’. Since initiating hand movements may require motor preparation, which could affect MEP amplitude [40], participants were asked to respond verbally with a forced choice, indicating whether the image was positive, negative, or neutral, to minimize any behavioral influence. Participant responses were collected by an investigator who pressed the corresponding computer key. After each verbal response, the screen remained dark for 7 s. This extended blank screen was to ensure that the TMS inter-pulse interval was greater than 10 s to avoid changes in motor excitability caused by TMS itself. For the collection of baseline control values before the emotion recognition task, participants rested during 10 applications of TMS with an intensity of 120% RMT. The procedure of emotion recognition task is shown in the Fig. 1C.

Data analysis

One-way RM ANOVA was conducted for HR and RPE separately, with exercise session (HIIT-90% HRR period, HIIT-40% HRR period, MICT, and control) as the within-subject factor. Two-way RM ANOVA was conducted for state anxiety score, with exercise session and test timing (pre- vs. post-exercise) as within-subject factors. The behavioral component assessed in this experiment was accuracy on the emotion recognition task. The neurophysiological component was the mean peak-to-peak MEP amplitude in each condition for all correct trials and was expressed as a ratio of the mean peak-to-peak MEP amplitude for the condition to that at baseline (condition/baseline). The peak-to-peak MEP amplitudes were extracted through a custom script in SIGNAL 6.0. Trials were included for further analysis if they met the following criteria: (1) the verbal response was correct; (2) voluntary root mean square EMG in the 100 ms before the TMS pulse was within 2 SD of the mean. Four-way RM ANOVA was conducted for both accuracy and MEP ratio, with exercise session, TMS timing (150 vs. 300 ms), image type (positive, negative, and neutral), and test timing as within-subject factors. Greenhouse–Geisser was used to correct for violations of sphericity. Paired t-test with Bonferroni correction was used for post-hoc analysis if ANOVA showed significant interaction effects. SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 and data were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation. With an α level of 0.05, we estimated the statistical power of the outcome post hoc by using G∗Power 3.1 with the generic F-test model [41].

Results

Exercise response

One-way RM ANOVA results showed a significant main effect of exercise session on HR (F(3, 45) = 812.232, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.982]. Post-hoc analyses revealed significant differences in HR across the four conditions, following the pattern: HIIT-90% HRR period > MICT > HIIT-40% HRR period > control (ps < 0.001; see Fig. 2A). These was also a significant main effect of exercise session on RPE data (F(3, 45) = 86.636, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.852), with post-hoc analyses showed RPE values during both HIIT and MICT were higher than that during control (ps < 0.001), and RPE values during the HIIT-90% HRR period was higher than that during the HIIT-40% HRR period and MICT (ps < 0.001, see Fig. 2B).

STAI state score

One-way RM ANOVA of STAI state score at the baseline of three exercise session showed the main effect of exercise session was not significant (F(2, 30) = 0.327, p = 0.723, ηp2 = 0.021). Two-way RM ANOVA of STAI state score showed a significant main effect of test timing (F(1, 15) = 7.250, p = 0.017, ηp2 = 0.326), as well as a significant interaction between them (F(2, 30) = 3.502, p = 0.043, ηp2 = 0.189), with the non-centrality parameter of 7.003, post hoc analysis indicated that the power (1 − β) was 60.9%. Post-hoc analyses showed a significant decrease in state anxiety after HIIT (p = 0.027, 95% CI = [−5.252, −0.373]) but not after MICT (p = 0.089, 95% CI = [−2.713, 0.213]) or control (p = 0.633, 95% CI = [−0.671, 0.421]) sessions, see Fig. 3.

State anxiety score change before and after exercise sessions. *p < 0.05 indicate significant differences between pre- and post-exercise tests within one exercise session

Behavioral data

The results of a four-way RM ANOVA for accuracy indicated a significant main effect of image type (F(2, 30) = 7.049, p = 0.003, ηp2 = 0.320). Post-hoc analyses showed that the accuracy for negative images (82.3 ± 7.1%) was lower than that for both positive (89.0 ± 9.5%, p = 0.020, 95% CI = [−12.258, −0.971]) and neutral (90.3 ± 5.8%, p = 0.002, 95% CI = [−12.852, −3.083]) images. The main effect of test timing was also significant (F(1, 15) = 15.562, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.509). Post-hoc analyses showed that accuracy was higher after exercise than before exercise (pre: 86.0 ± 5.4% vs. post: 88.4 ± 6.0%, p = 0.001, 95% CI = [1.069, 3.583]). The interaction between image type and TMS timing was significant (F(2, 30) = 6.067, p = 0.006, ηp2 = 0.288, Fig. 4); with the non-centrality parameter of 12.133, post hoc analysis indicated that the power (1 − β) was 85.1%. Post-hoc analysis showed that when TMS was delivered at 150 ms, the accuracy for negative images was lower than that for both positive (p = 0.008, 95% CI = [−16.365, −2.385]) and neutral images (p < 0.001, 95% CI = [−19.605, −6.020]); as well as lower than the accuracy for negative images when TMS was delivered at 300 ms (p = 0.027, 95% CI = [−11.511, −0.781]). No other significant main effects or interactions were found.

Neurophysiological data

Figure 5 shows the results of the MEP ratio analyses. The four-way RM ANOVA for MEP ratio results demonstrated a significant two-way interaction between image type and TMS timing (F(2, 30) = 7.750, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.341) and a three-way interaction between exercise session, image type, and test timing (F(4, 60) = 4.928, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.247). The four-way interaction was also significant (F(4, 60) = 3.634, p = 0.010, ηp2 = 0.195); with the non-centrality parameter of 14.535, post hoc analysis indicated that the power (1 − β) was 85.0%. No significant main effects or other interactions were observed.

Motor-evoked potential (MEP) ratios for the three image types before (Pre) and after (Post) each type of exercise with TMS delivery at (A) 150 ms and (B) 300 ms. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001 indicate significant differences between pre- and post-exercise tests within one exercise session for one image type; # p < 0.05, ## p < 0.01 represent significant differences between the indicated image types within one exercise session during the post-exercise test

We further performed two separate post-hoc three-way RM ANOVAs (exercise session, image type, and test timing as factors), with TMS timing at 150 ms and 300 ms to examine whether the modulatory effects with exercise were expressed differently at the early vs. late stage of emotion processing. The results of a three-way RM ANOVA for TMS timing at 150 ms indicated a significant main effect of image type (F(2, 30) = 3.502, p = 0.043, ηp2 = 0.189) as well as a significant interaction between exercise session, image type, and test timing (F(4, 60) = 8.377, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.358), with the non-centrality parameter of 33.508, post hoc analysis indicated that the power (1 − β) was 99.8%. Post-hoc analysis showed that the MEP ratio for responding to negative images was significantly larger after HIIT than before HIIT (p < 0.001) and larger than that for positive (p = 0.002) and neutral images (p = 0.022) after HIIT. After MICT, the MEP ratio responding to positive images was significantly larger than before MICT (p = 0.022). The three-way RM ANOVA results for TMS timing at 300 ms indicated a significant main effect of image type (F(2, 30) = 8.794, p = 0.001, ηp2 = 0.370), with the MEP ratio for responding to positive images larger than for both negative (p = 0.003) and neutral (p = 0.042) images. No other significant main effects or interactions were observed.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of HIIT and MICT on emotional perception–related cortical excitability in individuals with high trait anxiety. The key findings were that (1) HIIT decreased state anxiety and enhanced MEP amplitude at early stage during the perception of a negative stimulus, and (2) MICT enhanced MEP amplitude at early stage during the perception of a positive stimulus.

Physical exercise appears to be protective against the symptoms of anxiety, with the underlying mechanisms likely involving a combination of biological and psychological factors [5, 7]. More specifically, it has been suggested that the anxiolytic effects of acute exercise may be resistant to the potentially detrimental effects on mood after exposure to arousing emotional stimuli [42]. Numerous studies have highlighted the effect of MICT on the processing of positive emotional stimuli, showing that MICT reduces demands on emotion regulation during the processing of a positive stimulus [43] and induces a preferential processing of a positive emotional stimulus [44, 45]. In addition, compared with MICT, changes following HIIT were associated with more negative (or less positive) feelings, making participants more sensitive to a negative emotional stimulus [46–48]. Here, our findings further elucidate these effects from a psychophysiology perspective, showing that both HIIT and MICT ameliorated the emotional processing deficits in part by enhancing the cortical excitability corresponding to the emotional perception. In particular, HIIT worked on the negative emotion, and MICT on the positive emotion. It has been reported that anxiety-related disorders are associated with a more blunted pattern of physiological reactivity, characterized by hypo-reactivity in response to emotional stimuli [49]. Enhanced emotional perception–related cortical excitability may reflect a larger motor system recruitment for motor preparation in response to emotional information, which could be conductive to rapid motor initiation, such as approaching behavior to positive stimuli and avoiding behavior to negative stimuli [22, 28].

We found that modulation for both types of stimuli occurred in the early stage of emotional perception, that is, at 150 ms after stimulus onset, but not the late stage (300 ms after stimulus onset). Early cortical excitability may correspond to the early (100–200 ms) occipitotemporal components of event-related potentials that are modulated by the visual processing of emotional stimuli [50] and reflect the motor preparation of an orienting response to an emotional stimulus that would manifest as adaptive approaching or fight-flight-freeze action reactions [24, 51]. Late cortical excitability corresponding to later neural activity (approximately 250–350 ms) is related to motor resonance and motor evaluation [52] and is thought to be comparable to motor facilitation for both positive and negative stimuli [25]. Our results suggested that acute HIIT and MICT modulated only the preparation of the motor reaction to an emotional stimulus, not the evaluation. Early cortical excitability is primarily related to visual processing to distinguish between positive and negative stimuli, which involves a rapid bottom-up processing; by contrast, late cortical excitability is related to top-down processing of motor evaluation, involving the individual's long-term cognitive experience [53]. Hence, a further plausible mechanism associated with our findings may be that acute exercise easily influenced visual processing during emotional perception, but was insufficient to influence the cognitive experience, which may require more cumulative exercise effects.

Although both HIIT and MICT affected emotional perception, state anxiety was significantly reduced only after HIIT, while MICT showed a nonsignificant reduction in state anxiety (p = 0.089). The difference in state anxiety reduction between HIIT and MICT may be attributed to the distinct workload characteristics of each protocol. HIIT consists of repeated high-intensity bouts interspersed with short recovery periods, which require rapid engagement and disengagement of both physiological and psychological resources [54]. This dynamic modulation of effort likely increases prefrontal cortex engagement, which could enhance top-down emotion regulation of limbic structures such as the amygdala [55], which is particularly involved in the processing of negative emotions [56]. According to the emotion dysregulation model of mood and anxiety disorders, anxiety is conceptualized as a dysregulation of negative emotions and a deficiency in experiencing positive emotions [57]. More importantly, research supports the prioritization of regulating negative emotions over savoring positive ones in alleviating anxiety symptoms [2]. Therefore, the higher negative emotional perception–related MEPs after HIIT likely reflects the enhancement in the motor system's readiness to respond adaptively, particularly to negative stimuli, thus contributing to anxiety reduction.

Although we observed no significant modulation of either HIIT or MICT on the accuracy of emotion recognition, we detected lower accuracy for the recognition of negative images when TMS was delivered at 150 ms. This finding suggested that early TMS disturbed emotion processing specifically for negative information. Several behavioral effects have been reported with the delivery of TMS over the motor cortex [58], including a delay in the early release of planned movement [59]. Here, we showed that TMS may affect the accuracy of negative information processing, which could support a negative stimulus bias in the early processing stage [24].

There are several limitations that need to be addressed. First, while it can be difficult to recruit individuals with high trait-anxiety, there are some concerns with the low sample size. Future study should be conducted in a larger sample size and the gender difference could also be investigated. Secondly, state anxiety could be assessed again after the emotion recognition task to make it clear if these changes were maintained or immediately disappeared. Thirdly, a more active but non-exercise control task might have been better suited to isolate the effects of exercise. Finally, the present study only focuses on individuals with high-trait anxiety, and further research is needed to expand the beneficial effect of HIIT in other groups with different levels of anxiety or clinical individuals.

There are several limitations that need to be addressed. First, future studies should aim to include a larger sample and also consider investigating gender differences. Second, state anxiety could be re-assessed after the emotion recognition task to determine whether any observed changes are sustained or quickly dissipated. Third, a more active, non-exercise control task might have been more suitable for isolating the specific effects of exercise. Finally, the present study focused solely on individuals with high trait anxiety, and further research is needed to explore the potential benefits of HIIT in other populations with varying levels of anxiety or in clinical groups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of the present study offered insight from the perspective of psychophysiology on the mechanisms whereby exercise may reduce state anxiety. HIIT was more beneficial than MICT for decreasing state anxiety in individuals with high trait anxiety. This effect appeared to occur through enhancing the cortical excitability associated with the emotional perception of negative emotions, which would be conductive to initiating rapid action and could benefit survival.
