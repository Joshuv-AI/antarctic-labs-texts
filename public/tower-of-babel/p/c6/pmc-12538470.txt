Caffeine improves diethylnitrosamine-induced hepatocellular carcinoma by inhibiting cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling in rats
Authors: Liang Shan, Chuanying Wang, Xing Fang, Zhengsheng Zhang, Shujuan Dai, Jianjun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Studies have shown that long-term coffee consumption is significantly inversely associated with the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). It has been shown that caffeine (a naturally occurring compound) can inhibit HCC in animal models. However, the mechanism of caffeine on the prevention and treatment of HCC has not been fully clarified. Diethylnitrosamine (DEN) is considered a potent carcinogen. Based on literature in China and overseas, we adopted a DEN-induced HCC model in rats to observe the preventive and therapeutic effects of caffeine on HCC induced by DEN in rats and elucidate the mechanisms. We found that caffeine had protective effects on HCC induced by DEN in rats through inhibition of ‌cyclic adenosine monophosphate-protein kinase A-cAMP-response element binding protein‌ (cAMP-PKA-CREB) signaling. This study provides: (i) an in vivo and in vitro experimental basis for the application of caffeine in the clinical prevention and treatment of HCC; (ii) new perspectives for the evaluation and screening of efficacious drugs for the prevention and treatment of HCC.

Introduction

Presently, no effective treatment exists for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). However, with the introduction of nucleotide/nucleoside analogs, therapies for chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection that can reduce the risk of HCC have been improved significantly [1]. The only drugs currently approved by the US Food and Drug Administration to treat HCC are Nexavar™ (sorafenib) and Regorafenib™ (regafenib). These agents target the vascular system of the tumor, but they are mostly ineffective and used only when other methods fail [2]. Due to the rapid development of drug resistance and toxicity, these treatment options are not entirely satisfactory. Therefore, there is an urgent need for systematic combination therapy targeting different mechanisms of signal transduction, thereby reducing the prospect of drug resistance in cancer cells.

Studies have shown that the molecular structure of caffeine (Fig. 1A) is similar to that of adenosine. Hence, caffeine can bind to adenosine receptors (ARs), block adenosine, and inhibit its action to have a different physiological effect [3]. Long-term consumption of coffee has been found to have a strong negative association with HCC risk [4]. Caffeine metabolites reduce collagen deposition in liver cells, and caffeine has been shown to inhibit HCC in animal models. Diethylnitrosamine (DEN), an organic solvent used in organic synthesis and in industry, is a potent carcinogen [5]. The mechanism of DEN-induced HCC may be related to its interference with the repair of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) double-strand breaks by interfering with the activity of key enzymes in DNA repair/replication [6,7]. HCC is a highly lethal malignancy, accounting for 85–90 % of liver-cancer cases, with a poor prognosis and high recurrence rate [8,9]. According to the World Health Organization, chronic infection with HBV or the hepatitis C virus account for ∼80 % of HCC cases worldwide, and constitutes a serious global health burden [10,11]. A previous study by our research team showed that caffeine has a preventive effect on rats with alcoholic liver fibrosis, and that the ‌cyclic adenosine monophosphate-protein kinase A-cAMP-response element binding protein‌ (cAMP-PKA-CREB) signaling pathway may have a role in this process [13]. In addition, the hepatic stellate cell-T6 is stimulated by acetaldehyde to create an in vitro model of alcoholic liver fibrosis, and the PLC-DAG-PKC signaling pathway may play a part in the cellular model. However, the effect and mechanism of caffeine on the prevention and treatment of HCC have not been fully clarified. In this study, we adopted a DEN-induced model of HCC in rats [14] and explored the preventive and therapeutic effects of caffeine on HCC induced by DEN in rats, in addition to elucidating some of its mechanisms. Accordingly, our study provides: (i) an in vitro experimental basis for caffeine to be applied in the clinical prevention and treatment of HCC; (ii) new perspectives for evaluating and screening efficacious drugs for HCC.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval of the study protocol

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of Hefei Hospital affiliated to Anhui Medical University (The Second People's Hospital of Hefei) in Hefei, China (Ethics approval number: LLSC 20251937).

Animal grouping and drug administration

One-hundred and twenty male Sprague–Dawley rats were purchased (license number: SCXK (Anhui) 2022-001) from the Laboratory Animal Center of Anhui Medical University. They were divided randomly into five groups: normal control; model (DEN); caffeine low dose; caffeine high dose; and positive drug group (sorafenib). In addition to the normal control group, a DEN-induced HCC model was created in the other four groups as described in a previous study [15]. The model group was given DEN (10 mg/kg, i.g.) six times a week for 16 weeks. Simultaneously, caffeine (20 mg/kg) was injected into the stomach six times a week in the high-dose group; caffeine (5 mg/kg) was injected into the stomach six times a week in the low-dose group; sorafenib (5 mg/kg) was injected into the stomach six times a week in the positive control group. The grouping of animals and a flowchart of administration are shown in Fig. 1B.

Measurements of biochemical markers in serum

At the end of the 16-week experiment, rats were anesthetized with 10 % chloral hydrate (0.6 mL/200 g) and about 6–10 mL of blood was taken from the abdominal aorta. The blood was centrifuged (1500–2000×g, 10 min, 4 °C), after which about 2 mL of the supernatant serum was collected and stored at −20 °C. Further, 1 mL of serum was taken, and 13 indices of liver function were detected by an automatic biochemical analyzer (BS-2000 M; Mindray, Shenzhen, China). The serum level of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was measured by an automatic chemiluminescence immunoassay system (CL-8000i; Mindray). The AFP level in the culture supernatant of HCC cell lines was detected after centrifugation (6000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). Test work was completed in Hefei DaAn Medical Laboratory (Hefei, China). Serum levels of cytokines and metabolic indicators (reactive oxygen species (ROS), glutathione (GSH), protein carbonylation, glucose, and lactate) were detected by enzyme-linked immunoassays (ELISAs) using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA).

Staining of liver tissues

Rats were anesthetized and euthanized. About 50 mg of liver tissue was harvested, stored in formalin, embedded in paraffin wax, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and gradient dehydration was performed with 70 %, 80 %, and 90 % solutions of alcohol. Liver tissue was sliced to thickness 4 μm with a cryostat (CM1950, EG1150H, and IP1020; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, or Sirius Red.

Immunohistochemistry of liver tissues

Liver sections of thickness 4 μm were dried at 66 °C. Gradient elution with different concentrations of ethanol (100 %/95 %/80 %) for 3-min each time was done. Sections were washed, treated with citrate (pH 6.0) for antigen repair, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked. We added a primary antibody (Cluster of differentiation (CD)73/A2AR/hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-α), incubated the sections at 37 °C for 60 min, and rinsed them with PBST thrice. We then added the secondary antibody (Elabscience Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) and incubated it for 30 min at 37 °C. Next, a developing agent (DAB) was added. Sections were dehydrated and cleared with xylene. Neutral gum seal and a digital microtome scanner (Pannoramic MIDI; 3DHISTECH, VirtualExpo Group, France) were used for fluorescent microscopic observation of the slices.

Immunofluorescence of liver tissues

Liver sections of thickness 4 μm were dried. Antigen repair was done with citrate (pH 6.0) followed by washing. TritonX-100 (0.5 %) was added at 37 °C, followed by incubation for 60 min. Goat serum sealer was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C. Excess goat serum was removed, and then we added the primary antibody (CD73/A2AR/HIF-1α), followed by incubation for 60 min at 37 °C. Excess liquid was removed and an immunofluorescent secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit at 1:600 dilution) was added, followed by incubation in the dark for 30 min at 37 °C. Slices were sealed with Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). A digital microtome scanner (Pannoramic MIDI; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to scan fluorescent slices.

Ribonucleic acid (RNA) extraction and real-time reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Liver tissue and cells underwent different methods of preparation. Liver tissue (20 mg) with 600 μL of TRIzol® Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was placed in a tissue crusher. With respect to cells, after the media had been discarded, cells were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 600 μL of TRIzol Reagent was added to each well. Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue or cells using a total RNA extraction kit (Foregene, Chengdu, China). Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The loading sequence was a mixture of upstream and downstream primers, complementary-DNA, and SYBR green. After sample loading, plates were sealed with sealing plate film, centrifuged for 1 min, and then placed into the RT-qPCR instrument (Supplementary Table 2) for specific procedures.

Western blotting of proteins

We extracted liver-tissue proteins from 30 mg of liver tissue and cell proteins according to the instructions of a total protein (TP) extraction kit, and stored at −20 °C. Samples were subjected to constant-pressure electrophoresis (80 V, 30 min; 120 V, 1 h), constant transmembrane current flow (400 mA, 20–30 min), and then sealed with 5 % skimmed milk for 3 h. The TBST-cleaned polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane was incubated overnight with primary antibody. Next, the PVDF membrane was cleaned and closed with secondary antibody for 1 h. Each PVDF membrane underwent addition with 30 μL of developer solution (developer solution A and developer solution B were prepared at a 1:1 ratio). Target protein bands were developed by Chemi Capture software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Images were analyzed by ImageJ (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

HCC cell lines

The culture of all cell lines was carried out under dry and sterile environmental conditions. Three HCC cell lines derived from rats (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) were cultured in 89 % Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (high-glucose), 10 % fetal bovine serum, and 1 % antibiotics (penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 μg/mL)). All 3 HCC cell lines were cultured in a 37 °C incubator with a mixed gas (5 % CO2 and 95 % air). All three rat HCC cells had adherent growth. When cell confluence reached 70–80 %, logarithmic growth phase was achieved, so HCC cells could be passaged and used for assays.

Viability of HCC cell lines

Cell viability refers to the viability and health of cells under certain conditions. The viability of cells is usually assessed by measuring their metabolic activity, their ability to proliferate, or the integrity of the cell membrane. A smart cell analyzer (Mira BF; Countstar, Shanghai, China) was used. The cell sample and counting-board pipette were prepared in advance. Then, 20 μL of the cell sample was absorbed with the pipette and dropped evenly into the counting board. We clicked the “AOPI Cell Viability” button to insert the counter plate into the injection port. The cell counter displayed the result, and we calculated the viability of HCC (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) cells.

Proliferation of HCC cells

First, 1 × 104 cells/well of HCC cell lines (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) were cultured in 96-well plates for 24 h. Second, when HCC cell lines grew to the logarithmic growth phase (70–80 % cell confluence), drug treatment was carried out, and five repeat wells were set up simultaneously. Cell culture kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (10 μL/well) was added 24 h after drug administration, followed by incubation at 37 °C for an additional 2 h. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (SM-iD-3; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown in HCC cells

First, 1 × 104 cells/well of HCC cell lines (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) were cultured in six-well plates for 24 h. When HCC cells grew to 70–80 % confluence, siRNA could be transfected instantaneously. Following the operation instructions of the cellular gene silencing kit, the cell status was observed, the upper medium was discarded, and fresh complete medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum was added to extract cell RNA after 24 h, and cell proteins after 48 h.

Plasmid transfection in HCC cells

When the HCC cell lines (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) grew to 70–80 % confluence, plasmid transfection was done. Following the instructions of the plasmid transfection kit, cell status was observed every 6 h using a microscope. After cell transfection, the upper medium was discarded and fresh complete medium was replaced. Then, cellular RNA could be extracted after 24 h and cellular protein could be extracted after 48 h.

Intracellular cAMP concentration in HCC cells

ELISAs were used to measure the cAMP level in liver homogenates and cell culture supernatants, as well as intracellular cAMP. The rat anti-cAMP antibody in the kit was coated on an ELISA plate. The rat anti-cAMP antibody became bound with rat cAMP, and biotin was bound specifically to avidin to form an immune complex. The operation was carried out in accordance with kit instructions. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader. The cAMP level was proportional to the absorbance at 450 nm. A standard curve was drawn to calculate the cAMP level in samples.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

First, several pieces of fresh tissue (1 mm3) were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 24 h. Tissue samples were rinsed with PBS, fixed with 1 % osmic acid for 2 h, and dehydrated with alcohol and epoxypropane. Samples were soaked in a mixture of propylene oxide and epoxy resin (1:1 ratio) and pure epoxy resin, and then embedded. Second, samples were dried in a 45 °C oven for 12 h and solidified in a 60 °C oven for 48 h. Finally, we created sections (thickness = 70 nm) of embedded tissue. Images were taken using a TEM (JEM1400; JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The Student's t-test was used to compare mean values between two groups. ImageJ was employed to measure protein expression. Molecular formulas were drawn using ChemWindow 6.0 (John Wiley, Hoboken, NY, USA). Prism 8.0.1.244 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for statistical analyses and graph plotting.

Results

Protective effects of caffeine on HCC induced by DEN in rats

Caffeine, with the chemical formula C8H10N4O2, is a xanthine alkaloid (CAS number: 58-08-2) (Fig. 1A). Several studies have revealed the protective effect of oral intake of coffee and caffeine on HCC, and caffeine-derived compounds can be used as adjunctive therapies for different types of cancer [16,17]. The DEN-induced HCC model was replicated according to the literature [18]. A dose of 10 mg/kg was given by intragastric administration six times a week for 16 weeks The HCC model could be replicated in rats (Fig. 1B), but 70 % of rats died (Fig. 1C), possibly due to the toxicity of DEN. In the literature, rats weighing 180–220 g are commonly used, but the mortality rate is high [19]. We found that using rats of weight 300–350 g for modeling could improve the success of the model (Fig. 1D). In our study, the model compound and drug were administered simultaneously. After 16 weeks of modeling, rats were anesthetized with chloral hydrate, killed, and the liver was harvested. The liver index of rats in the model group was significantly lower than that of the normal group, and a high dose of caffeine significantly improved the liver index of rats with DEN-induced HCC (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1E). Rats with DEN-induced HCC showed significant weight loss, ∼50 % of rats had abdominal swelling, and clear intestinal flatulence was observed after opening the abdominal cavity after anesthesia. In addition, blood was taken from the abdominal aorta, and 6–10 mL of blood was available for each rat (Fig. 1F). The liver of rats in the model group was significantly smaller than that in other groups (Fig. 1G). These results suggested that: (i) DEN was highly toxic to the liver and could induce HCC in rats; (ii) caffeine had a protective effect on DEN-induced HCC in rats.

Caffeine improved the serum biomarkers of glycolytic metabolism

To explore the toxicity mechanisms of DEN inducing HCC, the levels of ROS, GSH, protein carbonylation, glucose consumption, and lactate production in serum were determined. DEN (10 mg/kg) gavage for 16 weeks could significantly increase the serum levels of ROS (Fig. 2A) and protein carbonylation (Fig. 2C) (P < 0.01), and significantly reduce the serum level of GSH (Fig. 2B) (P < 0.01). However, caffeine at a high dose (20 mg/kg) significantly improved the above symptoms (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A–C). These results suggested that DEN-induced HCC in rats may be related to oxidative stress. We found that the serum lactate level in rats with DEN-induced HCC was increased significantly (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2D), and the symptoms stated above were reversed significantly by a high dose of caffeine (20 mg/kg) (P < 0.05). The serum lactate level is significantly correlated with glycolysis, and the above results suggested that DEN-induced HCC may be associated with glycolysis. In addition, cancer cells need energy to grow and proliferate, so most patients with HCC have a low glucose level and emaciation. The serum glucose level of rats with DEN-induced HCC was reduced significantly (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2E). Caffeine improved the serological and pathological markers of rats with HCC induced by DEN

After 16 weeks of administration, blood was taken from the abdominal aorta, 2 mL of supernatant serum was absorbed after centrifugation, and 13 serological indices of rats were detected by an automatic biochemical analyzer. Total bilirubin (TBIL) is the sum of direct bilirubin (DBIL) and indirect bilirubin (IBIL), which does not bind to glucuronic acid. IBIL is insoluble in water and cannot be excreted through the kidney or urine, but DBIL can. IBIL is transformed in hepatocytes and binds to glucuronic acid to form DBIL. Excretion of DBIL from the biliary tract is obstructed after hepatocyte damage. DEN increased serum TBIL and DBIL levels significantly in rats, but caffeine reversed these effects (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A and B). Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) exists primarily in hepatocyte mitochondria and is the most sensitive indicator of liver-function damage. If hepatocytes are damaged, AST is released into the blood, resulting in an increased serum level of AST. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is stored mainly in the plasma of hepatocytes. HCC damage hepatocytes through the release of various cytokines, resulting in increased permeability of the hepatocyte membrane, and the release of intracellular ALT into the blood, resulting in a rapid increase in the ALT level. Caffeine significantly improved the increase in serum levels of AST and ALT in rats with HCC induced by DEN (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C and D). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) usually exists in the free form in the liver and must be excreted by the liver with bile. Abnormal excretion in the liver will lead to a high level of ALP. A high dose of caffeine significantly improved the increase in the serum ALP level induced by DEN in rats (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) is distributed mainly in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes and epithelium of the intrahepatic bile duct. HCC tissues release various cytokines to increase cell-membrane permeability or rupture damaged normal hepatocytes, and GGT is released into blood to increase its level. Caffeine had a significant improvement effect on the DEN-induced increase in serum GGT level in rats (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3F). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an important enzyme in anaerobic glycolysis and gluconeogenesis. LDH activity is increased significantly in liver (especially metastatic liver) cancer. High dose (20 mg/kg) caffeine significantly improved the increase in serum LDH level in rats induced by DEN (Fig. 3G). Albumin (ALB) is synthesized by liver parenchymal cells and is the most abundant protein in serum, accounting for 40–60 % of the TP in serum. Liver-cancer tissues release various cytokines to damage hepatocytes, and ALB and TP levels decrease due to impaired synthesis. Caffeine significantly improved reductions in serum levels of ALB and TP in rats with HCC induced by DEN (Fig. 3H and I). Caffeine significantly improved the increase in the serum IBIL level in HCC induced by DEN in rats (Fig. 3J). Caffeine significantly improved the continuous inversion of serum albumin-globulin (A/G) ratio in HCC induced by DEN in rats (Fig. 3K). If hepatocytes become cancerous, the related genes are reactivated, and the hepatocytes that had lost the ability to synthesize AFP begin to synthesize again, resulting in a significant increase in serum AFP content (Fig. 3L). Caffeine significantly improved the increase in serum AFP level in HCC induced by DEN in rats (Fig. 3L). H&E staining showed that caffeine could alleviate the chronic injury in rats with HCC induced by DEN (Fig. 3M and P). Masson-trichrome staining showed that caffeine reduced collagen production in DEN-induced HCC in rats (collagen is blue in Fig. 3N and Q). Sirius Red staining showed that caffeine decreased collagen production in DEN-induced HCC in rats (collagen is red in Fig. 3O and R). Caffeine ameliorated DEN-induced HCC in rats by inhibiting cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling

Immunohistochemical staining of liver tissue showed that high-dose caffeine significantly improved the DEN-induced increase expression of the adenosine receptor A2AR (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A and D), CD73 (Fig. 4B and E) and HIF-1 in rat liver (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C and F). In health, the proteins A2AR, CD73, and HIF-1 in the liver are closely related to energy production and the maintenance of body demands. Our results suggested that DEN is toxic to the liver, which may be related to its interference with normal hepatic energy metabolism. cAMP hydrolysis can release energy, and cAMP is also a second messenger that can regulate downstream PKA and CREB to have physiological roles in various cell types. Studies have found that the cAMP-PKA-CREB pathway has a critical role in promoting the growth of liver-cancer tumors [20]. To further explore the effects and mechanism of caffeine on DEN-induced HCC in rats, we investigated the relationship between the protective effect of caffeine on DEN-induced HCC in rats and inhibition of cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling. First, we measured the protein expression of PKA, CREB, A2AR, CD73, and HIF-1 in the liver of rats in each group (Fig. 4G). DEN-induced protein expression of CREB (Fig. 4H), PKA (Fig. 4I), A2AR (Fig. 4J), CD73 (Fig. 4K), and HIF-1α (Fig. 4L) increased significantly in the liver of rats (P < 0.05). Second, the cAMP content in liver homogenates was measured: DEN was a poison that could induce an increase in cAMP level in the liver of rats with HCC. A high dose (20 mg/kg) of caffeine could alleviate the DEN-induced increase in cAMP level in the liver of rats with HCC (Fig. 4M). Gene detection showed significant differences in the expression of cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling-related genes in the liver tissues of all groups (Fig. 4N). Caffeine protected against DEN-induced HCC in rats

To further investigate the protective effect of caffeine on HCC in rats induced by DEN, the liver tissues of each group were analyzed by immunohistochemistry. DEN significantly induced increased protein expression of PKA (Fig. 5A) and CREB (Fig. 5B) in the liver tissue of HCC rats, and caffeine (20 mg/kg) could significantly improve this effect (P < 0.05). Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key molecule in the regulation of energy metabolism [21]. Mitochondria are the key organelles of AMPK that maintain cellular homeostasis [22]. An increased AMPK level in the liver tissue of rats with DEN-induced HCC (which is closely related to cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling) was documented to be reversed by caffeine (Fig. 5C). We used ImageJ to calculate the expression of these three proteins and found significant differences (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D, E, 5F). ELISAs were employed to detect increased levels of the cytokines interleukin (IL)-1 (Fig. 5G), IL-6 (Fig. 5H), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (Fig. 5I), and PKA (Fig. 5J) in the serum of rats with DEN-induced HCC, suggesting that DEN-induced HCC may be related to inflammation. TEM (magnification × 10,000) showed that the number of mitochondria in the model group was reduced, some mitochondria were swollen or necrotized, mitochondrial ridges were broken or had disappeared, and autophagic lysosomes were visible (Fig. 5K). DEN-induced HCC could be improved in the high-dose caffeine group, suggesting that DEN-induced HCC may be related to mitochondrial damage resulting in abnormal energy metabolism, but the specific mechanism needs further investigation (photographs at × 25,000 magnification are not shown). Caffeine protected HCC induced by DEN by inhibiting cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling in rats

DEN is commonly found in some industrial wastewater and pollution sources in the environment [23]. DEN can cause damage to human cells and gene structure, which may cause various types of cancer [24]. Immunofluorescence staining was undertaken in the liver tissues of rats with DEN-induced HCC (Fig. 6A–C). Expression of A2AR (Fig. 6A), CD73 (Fig. 6B), and HIF-1α (Fig. 6C) was increased, but a high dose of caffeine significantly reduced expression of A2AR (Fig. 6D), CD73 (Fig. 6E), and HIF-1α (Fig. 6F) in the liver of rats with DEN-induced HCC (P < 0.05). A2AR, CD73, and HIF-1α are closely related to energy metabolism, suggesting that the toxic effect of DEN on the liver may be related to its interference with normal metabolism of hepatocyte energy. We further investigated the relationship between inhibition of cAMP-PAK-CREB signaling and activation and proliferation of three HCC cell lines (McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35) in rats by cellular experiments. A high concentration of caffeine (>10 mM) significantly inhibited the viability of McA-RH7777 (Fig. 6G), CBRH7919 (Fig. 6H), and RH-35 (Fig. 6H) cells (P < 0.05). Therefore, caffeine at doses of 5 mM and 10 mM were used in subsequent cell assays. We investigated the inhibitory effects of different concentrations of caffeine on the activation and proliferation of these three HCC cell lines (Fig. 6I). Caffeine (10 mM) inhibited the protein expression of CREB and PKA in McA-RH7777 (Fig. 6J and K), CBRH7919 (Fig. 6M and N), and RH-35 (Fig. 6P and Q) cells. We simultaneously detected the effects of different concentrations of caffeine on the intracellular cAMP level in these cell lines. Caffeine (10 mM) inhibited the intracellular cAMP level in McA-RH7777 (Fig. 6L), CBRH7919 (Fig. 6O), and RH-35 (Fig. 6R) cells. Activation of cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling enhanced the activation and proliferation of HCC cells

To further explore the relationship between cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling and the activation and proliferation of HCC cells, we used a potent and selective cAMP-dependent PKA inhibitor: H89. Studies have shown that H89 (20 μM) inhibits the activity and expression of PKA in different HCC cell lines [25]. We found that incubation with H89 (20 μM) for 2 h inhibited protein expression of PKA in McA-RH7777, CBRH7919, and RH-35 cells (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A). Caffeine (10 mM) reversed the reduction in PKA expression induced by H89 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7B). The main function of cyclin D1 is to promote cell proliferation. Cyclin-D1 overexpression leads to uncontrolled cell proliferation and malignancies. Studies have shown that cyclin-D1 expression is significantly increased in the liver tissue of rodent HCC models induced by DEN, but the specific mechanism needs further study [26,27]. Caffeine (10 mM) markedly inhibited the H89-induced increase in cyclin-D1 protein expression (Fig. 7C), suggesting that caffeine may suppress the activation and proliferation of HCC by inhibiting the expression of cyclin-D1 protein. Similar results were documented in CBRH7919 and RH-35 cells (Fig. 7D and E). CCK-8 assays revealed that caffeine (10 mM) had a remarkable inhibitory effect on McA-RH7777 cells, which proliferated rapidly during the logarithmic growth phase (Fig. 7F). We investigated the effects of CREB overexpression and silencing on these three HCC cell lines (Fig. 7G). Incubation with caffeine (10 mM) for 24 h inhibited CREB overexpression in McA-RH7777 cells (Fig. 7H) and protein expression of cyclin D1 was also inhibited (Fig. 7I). In addition, incubation with caffeine (10 mM) for 24 h downregulated CREB overexpression in CBRH7919 and RH-35 cells (Fig. 7J and K). CCK-8 assays manifested that caffeine (10 mM) suppressed the rapid proliferation of McA-RH7777 cells induced by CREB overexpression (Fig. 7L). To further explore the effects of CREB silencing on these three rat HCC cell lines, we conducted more cellular assays (Fig. 7M). Silencing of CRER expression could reduce CREB protein expression in McA-RH7777 (Fig. 7N), CBRH7919 (Fig. 7P), and RH-35 (Fig. 7Q) cells. Silencing of CRER expression in McA-RH7777 cells significantly inhibited cyclin-D1 expression (Fig. 7O) and the proliferation of McA-RH7777 cells (Fig. 7R). Inhibition of cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling reduced the activation and proliferation of HCC cells

To explore the effects of caffeine at different concentrations on cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling, CREB genes of three HCC cell lines were silenced (Fig. 8A). Caffeine (10 mM) enhanced the inhibition of siRNA-CREB on CREB protein (Fig. 8B) and cyclin D1 (Fig. 8C) in McA-RH7777 cells. Caffeine (10 mM) enhanced siRNA-CREB inhibition of CREB protein in CBRH7919 (Fig. 8D) and RH-35 (Fig. 8E) (P < 0.05) cells. Caffeine (10 mM) dramatically enhanced inhibition of siRNA-CREB in the rapid proliferation of McA-RH7777 cells during the logarithmic phase (Fig. 8F). The AFP level is highly correlated with the malignancy of HCC [28]. Caffeine (10 mM) notably strengthened the inhibition of siRNA-CREB on the extracellular AFP level in McA-RH7777 cells (Fig. 8G) (P < 0.05). To further explore the effect of caffeine on the activation and proliferation of HCC cell lines through cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling, we used the cAMP analog Bt2-cAMP (equivalent to an agonist) (Fig. 8H). Studies have shown that Bt2-cAMP (0.2 mM) significantly activates cAMP-PKA signaling [29]. Caffeine (10 mM) prominently inhibited an increase in PKA protein expression and proliferation of McA-RH7777 cells induced by incubation with Bt2-cAMP (0.2 mM) for 1 h (Fig. 8I and J). In addition, caffeine (10 mM) markedly restrained increased expression of PKA protein in CBRH7919 and RH-35 cells induced by Bt2-cAMP (0.2 mM) incubation for 1 h (Fig. 8K and L). Furthermore, caffeine (10 mM) significantly repressed the activation and proliferation of McA-RH7777 cells induced Bt2-cAMP (by 0.2 mM) incubation for 1 h (Fig. 8M) and increased the cAMP level in HCC cells (Fig. 8N). Discussion

The novelty of this study is that the mechanism of DEN-induced HCC model may be related to inhibition of mitochondrial energy metabolism, induction of oxidative stress and glycolysis. And caffeine exerts a protective effect on DEN-induced HCC in rats by inhibiting A2AR and its downstream cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling. DEN is an organic compound of chemical formula C4H10N2O. Recent studies have shown that the toxicity of DEN on the liver may be related to mutagenic degeneration and DNA damage to liver cells [30]. We investigated the protective effect and mechanism of caffeine on DEN-induced HCC in rats. We found that 20 mg/kg of caffeine (a naturally available and non-selective adenosine receptor antagonist) had a significant protective effect on DEN (10 mg/kg)-induced HCC in rats. There have been reports of the use of caffeine (and its derivatives) in cancer treatment [31]. In our study, ∼70 % of rats died during replication of the DEN-induced HCC model using classical modeling methods. Serological tests showed that the serum indices of rats were significantly changed. Levels of ALT and AST (important indices of liver function) were significantly changed, and the serum level of AFP (specific index of HCC) was significantly higher than that of the normal group. H&E staining showed that DEN elicited obvious damage to the liver of rats. Staining with Masson's trichrome and Sirius Red revealed that collagen deposition in the liver tissues of rats with HCC induced by DEN increased noticeably. TEM showed that DEN-induced HCC may be related to mitochondria damage and interference with the energy metabolism of cells, but the specific mechanism merits further study.

Animal experiments, cellular assays, and large-scale epidemiological studies have revealed the protective effect of caffeine on the liver, and the mechanism may be related to inhibition of adenosine receptors [32,33]. Based on previous studies from our research team, we further confirmed that caffeine inhibits cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling and that the downstream A2A receptor exerts protective effects on DEN-induced HCC in rats. CD73 catalyzes the hydrolysis of ATP to AMP, and further hydrolysis to adenosine releases energy. ATP is closely related to the energy metabolism of the body. Studies have found that adenosine is required for sustained inflammasome activation via the A2A receptor and HIF-1α pathway [34]. In our study, HIF-1α protein expression was significantly increased in the liver tissues of rats with DEN-induced HCC, and DEN caused significant damage to the liver. TEM showed that mitochondria in the liver tissue of the model group rats were significantly damaged, suggesting that DEN-induced HCC in rats may be related to inhibiting and destroying the energy metabolism of normal hepatocytes. In cellular assays, we found that caffeine (10 mM) dramatically suppressed the activation and proliferation of three rat HCC cell lines. In addition, inhibition of cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling inhibited the activation and proliferation of these three cell lines, and silencing or overexpression of CREB could have corresponding effects.

CREB is a transcription factor that mediates Ca2+, cytokine, and cell stressor signaling by regulating gene expression [35]. CREB is involved in cell differentiation, cell proliferation, as well as migration of various neurotrophic factors, cell growth factors, and cytokines [26]. CREB-induced inflammation promotes the growth of malignant tumors. We found that expression of IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α proteins in the serum of rats in the model group was increased. We demonstrated through animal experiments and cellular assays that caffeine has a protective effect on DEN-induced HCC in rats by modulating the downstream cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway via the A2A receptor. Notably, only one molecule would be insufficient and ineffective as an adjuvant in therapeutic protocols for HCC. Future research should pay more attention to the concentration of caffeine and its derivatives in local tumor tissues [36]. It should also be noted that imbalance in energy metabolism is gradually becoming an important risk factor for HCC. The malignant transformation of normal hepatocytes is usually accompanied by readjustment of the overall metabolic network [37]. The transformed hepatocytes exhibit the Warburg phenotype. In the liver, this phenotype is manifested by the expression of key glycolytic enzymes and the rearrangement of their phosphorylation patterns. HCC cells rely mainly on the Warburg effect to generate energy, and lactate has a crucial role. In the present study, the serum level of ROS, protein carbonylation, and lactate production in HCC rats was increased, whereas serum levels of GSH and glucose consumption were decreased. TEM revealed impaired mitochondrial morphology and function in the model group. Whether the protective effect of caffeine on DEN-induced HCC in rats is related to antioxidation and glycolysis needs further investigation.

Mitochondria are the main sites for energy metabolism within cells [38,39]. We found that rats with DEN-induced HCC had fewer mitochondria and some of them were swollen or necrotic, suggesting that their energy metabolism was impaired. On the contrary, the high-dose caffeine group could significantly improve such damaged mitochondrial morphology. The specific mechanism needs further research. HCC is associated with chronic inflammation and fibrosis caused by different factors. Chronic inflammation has a central role in HCC, and inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 and their downstream target nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) drive inflammation-related HCC. We found that the serum levels of the cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α were increased in rats with HCC induced by DEN. The transcription factor NF-κB is a major regulator of the inflammatory response and shows an antagonistic relationship in controlling inflammation. Its role in HCC progression needs further study. Ouyang et al. [34] reported that adenosine did not replace signals provided by stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide or ATP, but sustained inflammasome activity via the cAMP/PKA/CREB/HIF-1α pathway. Sun et al. [40] demonstrated that adenosine binding to the A2AR receptor activates the typical G protein and triggers the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway. Studies have indicated that the cAMP/PKA/CREB pathway may promote HCC progression [41]. In addition, studies have shown that the A2A receptor is coupled to a stimulatory adenylate cyclase, which results in upregulation of cAMP expression and activation of PKA [34]. We demonstrated that adenosine, acting through the A2A receptor, is a key regulator of the activation and proliferation of HCC cells. In addition, to test the requirement for the adenylate cyclase/cAMP/PKA pathway on A2A receptor signal-induced IL-1β production, we measured the serum level of IL-1β in HCC rats and supernatants of HCC cell cultures: the IL-1β level was increased. These data suggest the central role of inflammation in HCC progression. The protective effect of caffeine on HCC was achieved through its competitive inhibition of the adenosine receptor A2A. We investigated the effects and mechanism of caffeine on the activation and proliferation of HCC from the cAMP-PKA pathway downstream of NF-κB signaling. We found that caffeine inhibited A2A to downregulate the proliferation, migration, invasion, and induce the apoptosis of HCC cells.

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to clarify through cellular assays and animal experiments that caffeine exerted a protective effect on HCC in rats induced by DEN by inhibiting the cAMP-PKA-CREB signaling pathway. Furthermore, a new perspective was provided on the adjuvant-treatment strategy of caffeine and its derivatives for preventing HCC. As such, the results of the present study have demonstrated practical importance and can contribute to a deeper understanding of the various protective effects of caffeine in assisting HCC alleviation.
