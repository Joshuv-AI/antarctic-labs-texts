Targeting hepatocellular carcinoma growth and metabolism: A synergistic approach with para-toluenesulfonamide and radiofrequency ablation
Authors: Na Liu, Jianzeng Zhang, Jiaojiao Wu, Fan Feng, Yantao Chai, Yongwu Li, Bo Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal forms of cancer, posing challenges to standard treatment methods. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has emerged as a valuable technique for the local control of advanced HCC, especially in cases unsuitable for surgical resection. Para-toluenesulfonamide (PTS) has demonstrated antitumor properties in various cancers, yet its efficacy against HCC remains underexplored. The present study aims to investigate the therapeutic potential of PTS in HCC and examine potential synergistic effects with RFA.MethodsPTS was injected directly into subcutaneous tumor tissues and its effects on cell survival, gene expression, and metabolic activity in HCC cells were evaluated. The effect of PTS and RFA in combination on HCC growth was examined using a nude mouse model of subcutaneous HCC. Data were visualized using heat maps.ResultsPTS inhibited the survival of HCC cells in a dose-dependent manner, modulating the expression of key genes related to cell survival, apoptosis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and proliferation. The combination of PTS and RFA markedly enhanced the inhibition of HCC tumor growth and induced alterations in cellular metabolism.ConclusionCombining PTS and RFA may be a promising approach to improve the efficacy of HCC treatment.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Para-toluenesulfonamide, Radiofrequency ablation therapy, PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a major global health challenge, particularly in regions with a high prevalence of viral hepatitis, such as China, where over 80 million people are infected with hepatitis viruses B and C [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. HCC is often diagnosed at advanced stages, rendering patients ineligible for curative treatments like liver transplantation or surgical resection [5,6]. In this setting, radiofrequency ablation (RFA) has emerged as a promising local ablative therapy for advanced HCC [7,8]. However, recurrence of advanced HCC post-RFA treatment remains a significant clinical challenge, highlighting the need for more effective therapeutic strategies [9,10].

Para-toluenesulfonamide (PTS) is a small molecular compound that has exhibited antitumor activities in various human cancers, including lung cancer, prostate cancer, and squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue [11,12]. Notably, PTS is known to inhibit critical pathways like PI3K/AKT and mTOR/p70S6 kinase (p70S6K), which are essential not only for cancer cell proliferation but also for mediating resistance to conventional anti-tumor treatments [13,14]. However, unlike in other cancers, there has been little or no exploration of PTS as a treatment for HCC. This study aimed to address this gap by investigating, for the first time, the potential of intra-tumor injection of PTS to enhance the efficacy of RFA for HCC treatment.

This work reports a dual role for PTS in HCC treatment: (1) as a direct inhibitor of tumor cell survival; and (2) as a synergistic enhancer of RFA therapy. We present evidence that when combined with RFA, PTS significantly inhibits tumor growth and alters key cellular pathways in HCC. These findings not only extend the understanding of the antitumor capabilities of PTS but also suggest a novel and promising approach to overcoming the limitations of current HCC treatments, potentially improving outcomes for patients with advanced HCC.

Material and methods

Cell lines and agents

Cell lines derived from both normal hepatic cells (L-02) and HCC (HepG2, MHCC97-H, MHCC97-L, BEL-7402, SMMC-7721, and Huh-7) were purchased from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resources and the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were maintained under recommended culture conditions in our laboratory, as described in previous publications [[15], [16], [17]]. Hepatic cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The medium and FBS were purchased from GIBCO/BRL Life Technologies (Thermo-Fisher, Grand Island, NY, USA). PTS was purchased from Selleck Corporation (Houston, Texas, USA). PTS was dissolved in DMEM without FBS for cell-based experiments. For treatments, the PTS solution was prepared in DMEM with 0.5% FBS, under the same cell culture conditions. For animal experiments, PTS was dissolved and diluted in sterilized saline.

MTT assay for cell survival

Cell lines were cultured to create single-cell suspensions, which were then seeded into 96-well plates (8000 cells per well). Cells were treated with varying concentrations of PTS (1, 0.3, 0.1, 0.03, and 0.01 mmol/L) for 48 h. MTT assays were carried out after PTS treatment. Cells were treated with 5 mmol/L MTT for 5 h and absorbance values were read at 490 nm (OD490) using a full-wavelength multifunctional enzyme labeling instrument (Cat. No.: 1,410,101, Thermo Fishier Corporation, Waltham, MA, USA). Inhibition rates of hepatic cell growth by PTS were calculated using the formula (control group OD490 - PTS group OD490) /(control group OD490) × 100%. Heat maps were generated based on these inhibition rates [18,19].

RFA and subcutaneous tumor models

HepG2 cells and the HCC cell line MHCC97-H were cultured and injected subcutaneously into nude mice to establish a subcutaneous tumor model [20]. Cells were prepared as single-cell suspensions and injected subcutaneously in the medial groin area surrounding the femoral vein (5 × 106 cells per animal). Female nude mice, 5–6 weeks old, were raised under specific pathogen-free conditions. Subcutaneous tumor volumes were documented weekly, and after reaching 800 mm3, they were treated with RFA or an intratumor injection of PTS. For RFA experiments, tumors were treated using a thyroid ablation needle (UniBlate 700–103587, AngioDynamics Corporation, Queensbury, NY, USA) for 2 min at four temperature settings: 65 °C, 60 °C, 55 °C, and 50 °C [[21], [22], [23]].

For PTS experiments, three different doses (32 mmol/L, 16 mmol/L, 8 mmol/L, and 4 mmol/L) were injected in a volume of approximately 20 μL per tumor [24]. For single injection experiments, the animals were continuously monitored after PTS injection until the end of the experiment, when tumor tissues were collected. Then, tumor volume and weight were determined, and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and biochemical analyses were performed on the tissues. For multiple injection experiments, PTS was injected into HCC tissue and then two further injections were given for a total of three injections over 2 weeks.

For combination treatment with RFA and PTS, an 8 mmol/L dose of PTS was injected twice, followed by RFA (50 °C for 2 min). Tumor volume was calculated every 4 days using the formula [½ × (Length × Width2)] [25], and growth curves were plotted. At the end of the experiment—16 days after RFA treatment—tumors were harvested, volume and weight were measured, and qPCR and biochemical analyses were performed.

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

The qPCR assays were performed on both cultured cells and subcutaneous tumor tissues. For cell-based experiments, MHCC97-H cells were cultured, treated with PTS, and harvested for qPCR assays. For subcutaneous tumors, the tissues were first ground using liquid nitrogen, then collected using diethyl pyrocarbonate-treated double-distilled water to prepare homogenates. Finally, total RNA was extracted for one-step qPCR experiments. These were performed following previously described methods and the manufacturers' instructions [26,27]. Total RNA samples were extracted from cultured cells or subcutaneous tumors using PARIS™ Kits (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and mRNA samples were reverse transcribed into cDNA using Multiscribe™ Reverse Transcriptase kits (Applied Biosystems). The expression levels of target genes in the cDNA were quantified using one-step qPCR. Primers were synthesized and used based on universal primer sequences for each gene provided by OriGene-China Corporation (Wuxi City, Jiangsu Province, China).

Biochemical examination

Three types of biochemical assays were used: metabolic signature detection in cells or tissues, ferroptosis assays, and drug metabolism-clearance assays. Glycolytic activity was assessed by measuring glucose uptake levels (Glucose Uptake Assay Kit [Colorimetric], ab136955, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), lactate levels (Lactate-Glo Assay Kit, Promega, Promega Corporation, WI, USA), ATP production (ATP Assay Kit [colorimetric/fluorometric], ab83355, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity (Lactate Dehydrogenase Activity Assay Kit, MAK066, Sigma, Sigma Aldrich, MO USA) According to the manufacturers' instructions [27]. For ferroptosis, the relative concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) in HCC cells or subcutaneous tumor tissues was assessed using an MDA Assay Kit (ab118970; Abcam) [28]. For drug metabolism/clearance assays, tumor tissue and blood samples were collected at serial time points post-injection of PTS into HCC tissue. Blood was collected from the tail vein (approximately 20 μL per timepoint). The amount of PTS in samples was assayed by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and retention curves of PTS in tumor tissue versus blood concentration curves were obtained [29,30].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad 8.0 (GraphPad Software Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The main comparison in this study was between multiple experimental groups. Ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multiple comparison methods were used for multi-grouped data. ANOVA was used to detect differences between groups, and multiple comparisons (t-tests) were used to identify differences between pairs of data. IC50 values of PTS in each cell line were calculated using Origin 6.1 software (MicroCal Software Inc., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

PTS treatment inhibits the survival of hepatic cells

The anti-tumor efficacy of PTS was initially evaluated in hepatic cell lines. As shown in Fig. 1A, PTS inhibited hepatic cell survival in a dose-dependent manner. This effect was observed in HCC cell lines and the non-tumor hepatic cell line L-02. The IC50 values of PTS for MHCC97-L, MHCC97-H, SMMC-7721, BEL-7402, HepG2, Huh-7, and L-02 cells were 0.28 ± 0.05 mmol/L, 0.93 ± 0.27 mmol/L, 0.16 ± 0.04 mmol/L, 0.20 ± 0.11 mmol/L, 0.23 ± 0.08 mmol/L, 0.26 ± 0.12 mmol/L, and 0.65 ± 0.33 mmol/L, respectively. Additionally, qPCR analysis showed that PTS treatment led to suppression of various genes, including pro-survival/anti-apoptosis genes (cIAP-1 [Cellular inhibitor of apoptosis 1], cIAP-2 [Cellular inhibitor of apoptosis 2], survivin), EMT-related genes (N-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail, and Twist), and factors related to cell proliferation (cyclin D1, cdk4 [cyclin dependent kinase 4], cyclin E, cyclin B, cdk1 [cyclin dependent kinase 1], and cdk2 [cyclin dependent kinase 2]) (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, PTS had a minimal effect on the expression of fatty acid synthesis-related genes (ACC [Acetyl-CoA carboxylase], ACLY [ATP citrate lyase], FASN [Fatty Acid Synthase], and ACS [Acyl-CoA synthetase]) but significantly reduced the expression of cholesterol synthesis-related genes (SREBP-2 [sterol regulatory element binding protein 2], ACAT [Acyl coenzyme A Cholesterol acyltransferase], HMGCR [3-Hydroxy-3-methylglutarate monoacyl coenzyme A reductase], MVK [Mevalonate kinase], and MVD [Mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase]). The expression of GLUT1 and HIF-1α, two critical hypoxia/glycolysis-related factors, remained largely unaffected by PTS (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, treatment of MHCC97-H cells with PTS resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in aberrant metabolic activities (Warburg effect/glycolysis) and notably induced ferroptosis in these cells (Fig. 1C). These findings demonstrate the potent antitumor activity of PTS in HCC cells.

In vivo antitumor effect of PTS on MHCC97-H cells in nude mice

Following the assessment of PTS antitumor efficacy in MHCC97-H cells in vitro, efficacy was assessed in vivo using a subcutaneous tumor model. As shown in Fig. 2, MHCC97-H cells formed subcutaneous tumors in nude mice. The antitumor activity of a single injection of PTS varied with dose: 32 mmol/L showed clear anti-tumor activity, very weak activity was seen with 16 mmol/L, while 8 mmol/L had minimal activity; and at this same concentration (4 mmol/L), PTS somewhat stimulated subcutaneous growth of MHCC97-H cells (Fig. 2A–C). PTS injection also influenced gene expression levels, with 32 mmol/L and 16 mmol/L doses suppressing pro-survival/anti-apoptosis genes (cIAP-1, cIAP-2, and survivin), EMT-related genes (N-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail, and Twist), and proliferation-related factors (cyclin D1, cdk4, cyclin E, cyclin B, cdk1, and cdk2) (Fig. 2D). PTS had limited impact on fatty acid synthesis-related genes (ACC, ACLY, FASN, and ACS), but inhibited cholesterol synthesis-related genes (SREBP-2, ACAT, HMGCR, MVK, and MUD) (Fig. 2D). Additionally, PTS minimally affected the expression of GLUT1 and HIF-1α (Fig. 2D). Higher doses of PTS (32 mmol/L) were significantly more effective than lower doses (16 mmol/L and 8 mmol/L); the 4 mmol/L dose had minimal effect on the expression of these genes in HCC tissues (Fig. 2D). Intratumoral injection of PTS at the highest dose (32 mmol/L) inhibited aberrant metabolic activity (Warburg effect/glycolysis) in MHCC97-H cells and significantly induced ferroptosis (Fig. 2E), indicating that effective antitumor activity requires a dose of at least 32 mmol/L.

Antitumor activity associated with repeated PTS administration

The antitumor activity of PTS was further evaluated with repeated intratumoral administration to HCC tissues. Using an 8 mmol/L PTS dose, we administered one, two, or three injections (Fig. 3). Three injections effectively inhibited the subcutaneous growth of MHCC97-H cells, while a single injection showed the weakest antitumor activity. Two injections had moderate antitumor activity but significantly affected both gene expression (Fig. 3D) and metabolic activity (Fig. 3E) in MHCC97-H cells. Consequently, two injections of 8 mmol/L PTS were selected for subsequent experiments.

Metabolism and clearance of PTS in nude mice

To understand the anti-tumor activity of PTS against HCC, we initially examined PTS metabolism and clearance in nude mice (Fig. 3F and G). After injecting an 8 mmol/L dose of PTS into tumor tissues, we measured PTS content in tumor tissues and its concentration in blood. PTS was gradually metabolized and cleared from tumor tissues, with complete clearance by 48 h (Fig. 3F). Blood concentration peaked at 12 h and was also cleared by 48 h (Fig. 3G). Importantly, PTS injection did not significantly affect the body weight, the weight of major organs (Table 1), or routine blood indices (Table 2) in nude mice, suggesting that PTS has a favorable safety profile as well as antitumor activity.

Effect of RFA on MHCC97-H cells in a nude mouse model

To mimic RFA treatment in HCC, MHCC97-H cells were cultured and injected into nude mice to form subcutaneous tumor tissues. These tissues were treated with RFA at varying temperatures (65 °C, 60 °C, 55 °C, and 50 °C) for 2 min (Fig. 4). The anti-tumor activity of RFA varied with temperature, with significant activity at 65 °C, weak activity at 60 °C, minimal activity at 55 °C, and some stimulation of MHCC97-H cell growth at 50 °C (Fig. 4A–C). RFA treatment induced the expression of various genes in tumor tissues and affected metabolic activity (Fig. 4D and E). RFA treatment induced expression of anti-survival, anti-apoptosis, EMT-related, and proliferation-related factors; it significantly increased expression of hypoxia/glycolysis-related factors, induced aberrant glucose metabolism (Warburg effect), and inhibited ferroptosis in HCC (Fig. 4E). Given these effects, RFA at 50 °C for 2 min was chosen to simulate incomplete ablation in further experiments.

Enhanced antitumor effect of combination treatment with PTS and RFA

Building on the previous findings, we investigated the effects of PTS and RFA in combination. The dosages selected (RFA at 50 °C for 2 min and two intratumor injections with 8 mol/L PTS) demonstrated limited anti-tumor activity individually compared with the control group. However, the combination significantly inhibited subcutaneous growth of MHCC97-H cells (Fig. 5A–D). Additionally, PTS inhibited changes in HCC cell properties induced by incomplete ablation (RFA at 50 °C for 2 min), affecting both gene expression (Fig. 5E) and metabolic activity (Fig. 5F). Thus, PTS upregulated the antitumor effect of RFA on HCC cells, demonstrating the efficacy of the combination.

PTS enhanced the antitumor activation of RFA in HCC

Based on the results presented above, the combined effect of PTS and RFA was examined. Consistent with expectations, the selected dosages of RFA (50 °C for 2 min) and PTS (two intra-tumor injections at 8 mol/L) used alone had extremely limited anti-tumor activity compared with the control (untreated) group. However, the combination of RFA and PTS significantly inhibited the subcutaneous growth of MHCC97-H cells (Fig. 5A–D). PTS also inhibited changes in HCC cell activities induced by incomplete ablation (RFA at 50 °C for 2 min), including the expression of some genes (Fig. 5E) and metabolic activity (Fig. 5F). Similar results were obtained using HepG2 cells, another typical HCC cell line (Appendix Fig. A1). Therefore, PTS upregulated the anti-tumor effect of RFA in HCC cells, suggesting that combining the two has considerable potential.

Discussion

Increasing evidence suggests that PTS could be a promising therapeutic agent for the treatment of various human cancers [31]. Specifically, the PI3K/Akt/mTOR/p70S6K pathway, a key regulator of cell proliferation and resistance to antitumor strategies, is a target for PTS [32]. This pathway is often activated by aberrant receptor tyrosine kinase signaling; it promotes the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells through multiple mechanisms and contributes to resistance against treatments like chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and RFA [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. In this study, we found that PTS downregulates the expression of several key factors in HCC cells: (1) pro-survival and anti-apoptotic factors, including survivin; (2) EMT-related factors like N-cadherin; (3) factors related to fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis; (4) hypoxia-glycolysis-related factors; and (5) cell proliferation-related factors. Interestingly, inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by PTS aligns with its downregulation of resistance-associated factors like EMT [37,38]. Notably, the effect of PTS on cholesterol metabolism, which is crucial for membrane integrity and lipid raft formation, suggests that its potential mechanism involves lipid raft disruption, leading to PI3K/AKT pathway inhibition [39,40].

To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to report the antitumor activity of PTS in hepatic cells and HCC. While the antitumor properties of PTS have been reported in malignancies like non-small cell lung cancer and prostate cancer, its application in HCC has been largely unexplored [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. We demonstrated that PTS, particularly when injected intratumorally, can directly inhibit HCC tissue growth and enhance the antitumor efficacy of RFA. Moreover, PTS exhibited rapid metabolism and clearance in HCC tissues, suggesting a favorable safety profile and potential for precision drug delivery in HCC treatment.

Despite RFA being a suitable treatment for advanced-stage HCC, post-RFA recurrence remains a clinical challenge. In our study, we used four RFA dosages (65 °C, 60 °C, 55 °C, and 50 °C for 2 min) to simulate varying degrees of ablation. RFA at 50 °C for 2 min, chosen to mimic incomplete ablation, acted as a stressor, leading to resistance and metabolic alterations in HCC cells. These induced the expression of pro-survival, anti-apoptotic, and EMT-related factors crucial for cellular resistance and the stress response, as well as aberrant glucose metabolism [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. The modulation of these RFA-induced changes by PTS, coupled with its safety and antitumor activity, suggest that PTS is an ideal candidate for combination treatments (e.g., percutaneous or transhepatic artery interventions with RFA).

This study also examined the metabolic profile of HCC cells, highlighting the Warburg effect (the propensity of HCC cells to take up glucose even under oxygenated conditions) and leading to glycolytic metabolism and lactic acid ACCumulation, which is linked to EMT, metastasis, and invasion [47,48]. While the effect of PTS on key hypoxia-glycolysis factors (HIF-1α and GLUT1) was minimal, it may impact HCC cell metabolism through other pathways. Indeed, its influence on cholesterol metabolism and cell membrane lipids suggests a strong link to glycolipid metabolism [49,50]. Additionally, it is noteworthy that PTS induced ferroptosis in HCC cells, a process closely related to lipid metabolism [51,52].

In this study, we considered several oncologic characteristics of HCC, including EMT, ferroptosis, hypoxia/glycolysis, and lipid-cholesterol metabolism. Prior studies have established that incomplete ablation can induce EMT in HCC tissues, which is closely associated with the potential recurrence of HCC [53,54]. Various molecularly targeted therapies have been shown to inhibit EMT triggered by incomplete RFA, and have been effective when used in combination with RFA [53,54]. HCC is marked by distinct metabolic abnormalities, such as the hypoxia/glycolysis-related Warburg effect, which plays a critical role not only in EMT and the tumor microenvironment but also in the resistance of HCC to antitumor treatment strategies [55]. Given that the liver is a central organ for material and energy metabolism, and that glucose and lipid metabolism are intricately linked, our study also performed tests on both glucose and lipid metabolism [55]. Furthermore, we investigated markers associated with ferroptosis, a process intimately connected to lipid metabolism [18,56,57].

While our study demonstrated the promising combined effects of PTS and RFA in animal models, several challenges must be addressed before this therapy can be implemented in clinical settings. Firstly, the rapid metabolism and clearance of PTS necessitate the development of more stable formulations, such as drug-carrying microspheres or nanoformulations, to ensure sustained therapeutic levels. Additionally, comprehensive clinical trials are needed to evaluate the safety, optimal dosing, and efficacy of this combination therapy in patients. Factors such as variability in tumor biology, patient comorbidities, and potential side effects must also be carefully considered. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for translating our preclinical findings into a viable clinical treatment option.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reveals that PTS not only effectively kills HCC cells but also enhances the sensitivity of HCC tissues to RFA. These findings broaden our understanding of PTS and offer new avenues for HCC treatment.
