Soft tissue effects of aesthetic and conventional twin block appliances in the treatment of skeletal class II malocclusion: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mohamed Najati Alsilq, Mohamed Youssef
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancement of facial profile represents one of the primary objectives of orthodontic treatment, particularly in the light of the growing aesthetic concerns among patients. This randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of aesthetic (ATB) and conventional (CTB) Twin Block appliances on the facial soft tissues of patients with skeletal Class II Division 1 malocclusion. 52 pubertal patients were randomly assigned to two groups: 26 received ATB, and 26 received CTB. Pre- and post-treatment lateral cephalometric radiographs were used to assess 16 soft tissue variables. The data were analyzed via paired and independent t tests, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. Both ATB and CTB significantly improved facial convexity and mentolabial angles, with no significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05). The CTB group presented significant upper and lower lip retraction relative to the Ricketts’ E-line, whereas the ATB group did not, with no significant difference between the groups (p < 0.05). Holdaway analysis indicated significant soft tissue improvement in both groups. Notably, the ATB group demonstrated greater advancement in the lower lip sulcus (p = 0.02) and less nasal prominence (p = 0.03) than did the CTB group. No serious harm was reported. Functional treatment with both appliances caused favorable soft tissue changes. However, The ATB appliance demonstrated more advantageous outcomes in enhancing the concavity of the lower labial sulcus.Trial Registration: NCT05418413 (14/06/2022). Introduction

Facial aesthetic enhancement is a fundamental goal for contemporary orthodontic treatment, and existing therapy concepts note the importance of soft tissue improvement as one of the primary outcomes1. Class II malocclusions represent a high percentage of orthodontic cases2. These subjects have a retrognathic mandible, a large overjet, and a convex facial profile, features that may have an adverse influence on self-esteem and self-perception3. Therefore, patients with Class II malocclusion, in most cases, visit the orthodontist only for facial aesthetic improvement. Functional appliances are most frequently applied in the orthodontic treatment of developing skeletal Class II malocclusion, particularly to stimulate mandibular growth in the retrognathic cases4. Among these appliance types, the Twin Block appliance is one of the most widely utilized due to its clinical efficacy. The esthetic Twin Block (ATB) appliance is a recently modified version of the conventional Twin Block (CTB), designed to promote greater patient acceptance as it becomes more aesthetically pleasing5. Primary reports have indicated that the ATB appliance has the potential to result in greater patient compliance, more favorable skeletal changes, and fewer unwanted dentoalveolar effects6,7. These advantages may contribute to improved facial profile outcomes. A review of the literature reveals a lack of clinical studies assessing the soft tissue changes associated with the ATB appliance. To date, only one clinical trial has addressed this issue, and the evaluation has been limited to a small set of soft tissue parameters (including the nasolabial angle and the linear distance from the E-line to the lips)8. This randomized clinical trial (RCT) was therefore aimed at comprehensively assessing the changes in soft tissues following ATB treatment and comparing these changes with those following CTB treatment. The null hypothesis that was tested was that no difference in soft tissue changes occurred between patients treated with the ATB appliance and those treated with the CTB appliance.

Materials and methods

Study design

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of ATB in the treatment of skeletal Class II malocclusion and compare it with that of a control group treated with CTB. The details of this RCT are given elsewhere6, in which a cephalometric and dental casts analysis was employed to assess post-treatment soft-tissue, dental and skeletal changes after the active phase of functional therapy. For completeness, a brief description of the randomized controlled trial (RCT) is provided here.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7 (Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), based on changes in the ANB angle reported in a previous study. The calculation was performed assuming a paired t-test with 90% statistical power, a significance level of 0.05, and an effect size of 0.83. This analysis indicated that 24 subjects were required per group. To account for a potential 10% dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 26 patients per group.

Study sitting

This study was conducted at the Department of Orthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, University of Damascus, between June 6, 2022, and April 4, 2023. This research project was approved by the University of Damascus Local Research Ethics Committee (no. 1205-06-12–2021). All methods were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study has been registered with ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT05418413 (June 14, 2022).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: skeletal class II division 1 relationship (ANB > 4°) with the retrognathic mandible (SNB < 78°), an overjet of 5–10 mm, a normal growth pattern (Bjork sum < 402°), and patients at the pubertal growth spurt peak (S or MP3cap) epiphyseal stages on hand wrist radiographs9.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: previous orthodontic treatment; systemic diseases that may affect the treatment results; severe facial asymmetry; posterior crossbite or severe maxillary transverse deficiency; flared lower incisors (L1: MP > 97°); poor oral hygiene; and inability to close lips due to respiratory disorders.

Patient assignment and randomized allocation

Patients received information sheets, and written informed consent forms were collected after permission was obtained. Briefly, the patients who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to.

participate were randomized to groups receiving treatment with either ATB or CTB appliance. Randomization was performed at this stage according to a previously prepared random number list.

Twenty-six patients were included in the ATB group (15 females and 11 males, with an average age of 12.41 ± 0.75), and 26 patients were included in the CTB group (18 females and 8 males, with an average age of 12.05 ± 0.76). Patient and practitioner blinding was not feasible. Therefore, blinding was implemented only for the outcomes assessor while plaster-distributed cephalometric radiographs were recorded with serial number to ensure blinding and avoid bias. Parent-reported compliance charts were utilized to monitor compliance and appliance wear (Patient compliance with appliance wear was considered satisfactory, with a documented minimum wear time of 16 h per day). A flow chart of participant recruitment, follow-up and entry into the data analysis process is given elsewhere6.

Treatment groups

One specialist orthodontist (M.N.A.) treated all patients in the treatment groups. The CTB appliance was constructed according to the design described by Clark10. The ATB consisted of two 1.5 mm vacuum-formed hard plates (VFPs) (3 A MEDES®, Easy-Vac Gasket, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) with inclined acrylic bite blocks similar to the CTB (Fig. 1). The reconstruction bite has a single-step mandibular advancement and an edge-to-edge incisal relationship with a 2–3 mm bite opening between the central incisors. In the ATB group, bite registration was performed while the VFPs were in the patient’s mouth, accounting for the thickness of the plates. All participants and their parents received both oral and written information about the treatment, oral hygiene, and appliance maintenance. They were instructed to wear the appliance full-time except for eating and brushing (nighttime included) and to breathe from the nose with closed lips while the appliance was put.

The process ends when the active phase of functional therapy is completed, the overjet is reduced to 1–2.5 mm, and the occlusion is stabilized in a Class I relationship.

Outcome measures

Lateral cephalometric radiographs were taken at the start (T0; before treatment) and end (T1; at the end of the active phase) using the same machine, i.e., a PAX 400 (VATEH Co., Ltd., Hwaseong, Korea), with the same settings. Cephalometric tracings were manually performed by the same author (M.N.A.), who was blinded to the patients’ names during the measurement. This process was carried out under the supervision of the (M.Y), who reviewed and validated the tracing to ensure accuracy and reliability. Sixteen measurements, 5 angular and 11 linear, were measured on each radiograph11,12 (Table 1) (Fig. 2).

Error of the method

Six weeks after the initial measurement, fifteen randomly selected cephalograms were remeasured and analyzed again to determine method error. Reliability was evaluated via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which indicated strong intraexaminer consistency (ICC = 0.995). The overall errors were quantified via Dahlberg’s formula and did not exceed 0.47 for linear variables or 0.29 for angular variables.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS for Windows (version 26.0; SPSS, Chicago, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk normality test was used to ensure the normal distribution of the data. A paired-sample t test was used to assess the significance of differences between pre- and post-treatment variables within each group, detecting intragroup changes after treatment. Independent t tests were performed to compare treatment outcomes and identify differences between the two groups. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline data

The basic sample characteristics are presented elsewhere6. Independent-sample t tests were conducted to assess significant differences between the study groups before treatment. The P values for all the examined variables were considerably greater than 0.05, indicating that there were no statistically significant differences between the groups at the 95% confidence level. This confirms that both groups were equivalent before treatment in terms of angular and linear values (Table 2).

The results revealed significant changes in facial convexity and mentolabial angles in both groups. The facial convexity angle decreased by −3.76° in the CTB group and − 4.16° in the ATB group. Similarly, the mentolabial angle increased by 16.32° in the CTB group and 17.40° in the ATB group (p = 0.000). No significant difference was observed between the two groups (p > 0.05).

For the position of the lips with Ricketts’ esthetic line, in the CTB group, the results showed a significant change: the upper lip moved forward by 0.92 mm (p = 0.009), and the lower lip moved forward by 1.39 mm (p = 0.000). Conversely, no significant change was detected in the ATB group (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Holdaway analysis revealed a significant reduction in facial convexity depth in both groups, by −1.00 mm (p = 0.004) in the CTB group and − 1.26 mm in the ATB group. Additionally, a significant decrease in the H angle, by −2.04° (p = 0.001) in the CTB group and − 2.34° (p = 0.000) in the ATB group, was detected. Neither of these variables showed significant differences between the two groups (p > 0.05).

For the lower lip, there was a significant decrease in the distance of the lower lip sulcus from Holdaway’s esthetic line in both groups. This change was significantly greater (p = 0.02) in the ATB group than in the CTB group (−1.70 mm (p = 0.000) and − 0.73 mm (p = 0.02), respectively). Additionally, a significant increase in the distance of the lower lip from Holdaway’s esthetic line was observed in the CTB group by 0.46 mm (p = 0.03), with no significant difference compared with the ATB group.

With respect to the upper lip, both upper lip thickness and basic upper lip thickness increased significantly in both groups. In the CTB group, the values were 0.93 mm and 0.85 mm, respectively (p < 0.05), whereas in the ATB group, they were 1.42 mm and 1.21 mm, respectively (p < 0.01). No significant differences were observed between the two groups. Finally, the nasal prominence increased more significantly in the CTB group than in the ATB group, with a mean difference of 0.96 mm (P = 0.03).

Harms

No serious harm and cases of appliance breakage were observed during the study period. The two appliances caused a little amount of pain, and oral constraint, The CTB caused a greater level of speech impairment6.

Discussion

Enhancing facial aesthetics is one of the key objectives of orthodontic treatment, as facial appearance significantly influences an individual’s social well-being and self-esteem1,13,14. Skeletal Class II malocclusion is one of the most prevalent types of malocclusion15, and the pubertal growth spurt represents the optimal period for functional treatment aimed at mandibular advancement16. Among the available functional appliances, the Twin-Block is one of the most widely used for this purpose17,18. The ATB appliance, a modified version of the Twin-Block, has certain advantages over the CTB appliance, which may promote a positive influence on the facial soft tissue profile6,7. This RCT suggested evaluating the effects of ATB on soft tissue and comparing them with those of CTB.

Facial soft tissue profile changes

The facial convexity angle significantly decreased in both treatment groups (P = 0.000), contributing to improvements in the facial soft tissue profile. This result agrees with most previous studies with TBs or other removable functional appliances (i.e., activators, herbivores, and trainers)1,14,19,20. However, the comparison between the two groups did not reveal a statistically significant difference (p > 0.05). Despite the greater forward repositioning of the mandible in the ATB6, this might be because the retraction of the maxillary incisors was less significant. This influences the degree of upper lip retraction and the position of point SN.

The results revealed a nonsignificant increase in the nasolabial angle in the CTB group (p > 0.05), likely attributed to the retraction of the maxillary incisors, which led to repositioning of the upper lip. This finding aligns with8,21,22. However, it contrasts with studies1,23 which reported a significant increase, potentially due to differences in inclusion criteria; those studies included patients in later stages of growth spurts. Conversely, the ATB group presented a nonsignificant decrease in the nasolabial angle (p > 0.05), with no significant difference observed between the two groups (p > 0.05). This is because ATB is associated with less upper incisor retraction6. Golfeshan et al. reported a nonsignificant increase in angle changes, possibly due to variations in sample age, but similarly reported no significant difference between the two groups8. Previous studies have reported no significant differences in nasolabial angle changes between untreated control groups and those treated with the Twin Block appliance14,24. This finding may be attributed to increased upper lip thickness resulting from reduced lip strain, which potentially minimizes the impact of maxillary incisor retraction on upper lip.

However, the Mentolabial Angle increased significantly in both groups (P = 0.000), with no significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). Compared to untreated control groups, a significant reduction in the labiomental angle was observed, highlighting a notable difference relative to the Twin Block appliance14,25. This increase can be attributed to improvements in the sagittal incisal relationship, allowing the embedded lower lip to rest and reposition. These findings agree with those of most studies on TB and/or removable functional appliances1,14,20–22.

A significant decrease in facial convexity depth was observed in both ATB and CTB groups (P = 0.000 and P = 0.004, respectively), leading to H angle improvement, which decreased significantly in both groups (P = 0.000 and P = 0.001, respectively). These findings are in agreement with those of previous studies23,26,27, and those who compared twin block with an untreated control group14. On the other hand, despite greater mandibular repositioning in the ATB, there were no significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05), possibly because the monitoring period was limited to the active treatment phase.

Upper lip and nose changes

Significant posterior movement of the upper lip relative to the E-line was observed in the CTB group (P = 0.009), whereas the ATB group showed nonsignificant posterior movement (P > 0.05). This difference may be due to greater maxillary incisor retraction in the CTB6. These findings agree with those of previous studies concerning CTB21,24,28, which had confirmed a statistically significant upper lip retraction in the Twin Block compared to untreated controls, and do not agree with those of Golfeshan et al. (2018)8, who reported greater and statistically significant retraction in the ATB group, possibly due to differences in sample age. No significant difference was found between the two groups (P > 0.05), and Golfeshan et al. reported similar results8.

Both ATB and CTB presented significant increases in upper lip thickness (P = 0.000 and P = 0.014, respectively) and basic upper lip thickness (P = 0.000 and P = 0.015, respectively), with no significant difference between them (P > 0.05). This increase is likely due to improved incisor relationships and reduced muscular tension associated with lip incompetence before treatment. Previous studies reported a statistically significant increase; however, this change was not clinically relevant when compared to untreated control groups14,29. On the other hand, no significant changes were found in the SN distance from Holdaway’s esthetic line or upper lip sulcus depth in either group.

These changes in the upper lip contributed to a significant increase in nasal prominence (The vertical distance between the nasal tip and the vermilion border of the upper lip) in the CTB group (P = 0.002), whereas the ATB group showed a nonsignificant increase, with a significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.036). This may be due to greater upper incisors retraction in CTB6, leading to a greater upper lip retraction. When compared with clinical studies evaluating the Twin Block appliance against untreated control groups, the increase in nasal prominence appears to be clinically insignificant, as similar changes were also observed in non-treated patients without a statistically significant difference1,14,24.

Changes in the lower lip and chin

The lower lip showed statistically significant retraction relative to the Ricketts line in the CTB group (P = 0.001), whereas the retraction in the ATB group was not significant (P > 0.05). This outcome agrees with the findings of25,28 for CTB but disagrees with those of Golfeshan et al. regarding ATB8, possibly due to differences in the sample’s age. No significant difference was found between the groups (P > 0.05), which aligns with the findings of Golfeshan et al.8. Similarly, the CTB group revealed significant retraction of the lower lip relative to Holdaway’s line (P = 0.037), with no significant intergroup difference (P > 0.05). These improvements likely result from mandibular advancement and the release of the lower lip from its previous position behind the maxillary incisors. The lower lip sulcus advanced significantly in ATB and CTB groups (P = 0.000 and P = 0.026, respectively), but the ATB group showed more significant advancement (p < 0.05). This difference may be related to the lower degree of incisor protrusion, which was greater in the CTB group, leading to deeper sulcus concavity and less forward movement. Several studies reported similar findings and noted significant forward movement of the lower labial sulcus following CTB therapy1,8,14,22,30. This difference is considered clinically significant, as it contributes to a more aesthetically pleasing soft tissue profile of the lower lip (Fig. 3) demonstrate this difference. Finally, no significant changes were observed in soft tissue chin thickness in either group, which also agreed with the findings of14,23,29.

Soft tissue changes in both groups.

Limitation

A limitation of this study is the lack of an untreated control group due to ethical constraints. However, the differences observed between the two treatment groups reflect appliance-specific effects, fulfilling the study’s main objective, also blinding was limited to the outcome assessor during the evaluation of cephalometric radiographs, which may be considered a methodological limitation that may have introduced performance bias. The present study is single-center and needs to be conducted as a multicenter trial to enhance the external validity of the results. Limited duration of the observation period. The active treatment phase may not fully capture the long-term stability of soft tissue changes. We recommend follow-up assessments after the retention phase in future studies. This would allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment outcomes and their long-term stability.

Conclusions

The functional treatment of Class II Division 1 malocclusion has positive effects on facial soft tissues with both twin block appliances. The facial convexity and mentolabial angle decreased, and the nasolabial angle increased. Compared with CTB, ATB offered more favorable effects at the lower oral soft tissue profile level with more enhanced lower labial sulcus concavity.
