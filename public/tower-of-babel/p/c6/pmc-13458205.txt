High level of patient satisfaction with incremental haemodialysis: insights from a single‐centre patient‐reported experience study
Authors: Kavita L Dilip, Louis L Huang, Peter F Mount
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA single‐centre, anonymous survey study was conducted to investigate patient experience and quality of life (Kidney Disease Quality of Life‐36) in individuals receiving incremental haemodialysis, with all 20 eligible patients participating. Patients expressed high satisfaction with incremental haemodialysis and a strong preference over conventional haemodialysis regimens. Quality‐of‐life scores were consistent with broader dialysis populations, but overall satisfaction with care was high, supporting the potential for incremental haemodialysis expansion.Keywords: incremental haemodialysis, patient experience, quality of life, Kidney Disease Quality of Life‐36

Incremental haemodialysis (HD) refers to a strategy in which the time, dose or frequency of a HD prescription is adjusted for residual kidney function (RKF). 1 In Australia and most other developed countries, HD is usually prescribed as treatment of 4–4.5 h of HD three times per week and this regimen is rarely changed, with RKF not considered in the prescription. 2 Patients undergoing incremental HD are required to do regular timed urine collections and the HD prescription is adjusted based on RKF. 3 For example, patients with sufficient RKF may do twice a week HD, rather than a standard regimen of three times a week. People with kidney failure on maintenance HD report high treatment‐related and symptom‐related burden. 4 A significant benefit of incremental HD is that it may allow patients with sufficient RKF to do less HD, while still safely achieving an adequate HD dose and net solute clearance. 3

There is growing interest in incremental HD for managing patients with kidney failure. 2 , 5 Reported advantages of incremental HD include preservation of RKF, reduced hospitalisations and lower healthcare costs. 6 , 7 , 8 Additionally, mortality with incremental HD appears non‐inferior, with some studies suggesting it may even be superior, as compared to standard HD schedules. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Although there is growing evidence that incremental HD is non‐inferior to conventional dialysis for clinical outcomes, there remains limited evidence regarding patient‐reported experience associated with this approach.

Patient‐reported experience measures are emerging as an important component in evaluating the quality of care provided to people with kidney failure doing HD. 13 Since HD is a time‐intensive and life‐altering treatment, understanding the patient experience is important when evaluating models of care to achieve the optimal impact on patient well‐being and adherence to treatment. To understand better the patient experience of incremental HD, we performed a single‐centre cross‐sectional survey study at Austin Health, Melbourne, Australia. All adult patients currently receiving incremental HD at the centre were invited to participate in an anonymous survey, which was distributed during dialysis sessions. The survey included two components: a customised patient‐reported questionnaire evaluating the experience of incremental HD and the Kidney Disease Quality of Life‐36 (KDQOL‐36) instrument, a validated tool used to assess health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) in patients with kidney disease. The project was approved by the Austin Health Office for Research as a quality improvement project in March 2023.

All 20 eligible patients participated, yielding a 100% response rate. Although the survey was anonymous, demographic questions included in the questionnaire revealed that the cohort was predominantly male (70%) and 85% were older than 50 years (Table 1). Duration of dialysis ranged from less than 3 months to over 2 years, with 35% of respondents receiving HD for 6–12 months. A history of diabetes was reported by 40% of participants. End‐stage kidney disease aetiology was uniformly distributed, with diabetes mellitus, hypertensive/renovascular disease, polycystic kidney disease and other causes each accounting for 25% of cases. RKF, measured as urea clearance (KRU), was measured at the time of dialysis initiation by timed interdialytic urine collections. KRU values were 3–4 mL/min/1.73 m2 in 55% of participants and >4 in 45%, consistent with adequate RKF to support incremental HD in this cohort.

Results of the patient experience survey are summarised in Table 2. Nearly all participants (95%) reported agreement they understood what incremental HD was and why they were having this treatment. This reflects the policy for incremental HD in our service, which requires significant patient education regarding incremental HD before the therapy is commenced. A high level of patient understanding was demonstrated by the finding that 85% of participants agreed that a decline in kidney function, as indicated by urine collection results, would necessitate an increase in dialysis. Similarly, 80% of participants reported that they would be willing to increase the duration or frequency of their HD treatments if recommended by their nephrologist, which is a reassuring indication of adherence readiness. Most participants (70%) agreed that they liked that incremental HD means they spend less time doing dialysis than a standard HD schedule. Regarding the need to do regular timed urine collections, while 45% reported these to be burdensome, the majority of participants (85%) considered it was worth the effort of doing these in order to enable incremental HD. Notably, all 20 participants (100%) indicated a preference for incremental HD regimen, as compared to a standard three times per week, 4–4.5 h per session regimen. Regarding overall satisfaction with their dialysis care, 90% agreed or strongly agreed that they were satisfied.

HRQOL, assessed via the KDQOL‐36, demonstrated results in keeping with known impairments in dialysis populations. Median scores for the kidney‐specific domains were 68.8 (Symptoms and Problems), 57.8 (Effects of Kidney Disease) and 25.0 (Burden of Kidney Disease), indicating moderate to high symptom burden and disease impact (Fig. 1A). General physical and mental well‐being, assessed via the Physical Component Summary and Mental Component Summary composite scores, were 31.0 and 37.0, respectively, both below population norms (Fig. 1B). These findings are consistent with known limitations in physical and mental health domains in the dialysis population.

Discussion

These findings provide novel empirical data about the patient experience of incremental HD and offer support for its wider clinical consideration. All surveyed patients expressed a preference for incremental HD over standard HD, underscoring the acceptability of this approach. While concerns regarding the burden of timed urine collections were reported, most participants viewed this monitoring as an acceptable trade‐off to preserve a less intensive dialysis schedule. High satisfaction rates and a strong understanding of treatment principles reflect positively on the feasibility of implementing incremental HD more broadly.

Data on patient perspectives specific to incremental HD are limited. An Australian qualitative study by Hegerty et al. surveyed individuals receiving standard HD regarding hypothetical incremental regimens and found unanimous conceptual support. 14 Participants identified quality of life, RKF and survival as key priorities. The present study, however, is the first, to our knowledge, to capture directly the patient‐reported perspectives of individuals actively receiving incremental HD. These results corroborate prior preferences for individualised care and demonstrate practical endorsement of incremental HD when offered as part of clinical care.

Quality‐of‐life outcomes in this cohort reflected those previously reported in other dialysis populations. Previous studies examining HRQOL in incremental HD versus conventional HD have demonstrated either comparable or improved scores, particularly in mental health and symptom burden domains. 7 , 15 Some data suggest that incremental HD may reduce depressive symptoms and improve psychological well‐being, although findings are inconsistent and limited by small sample sizes and methodological heterogeneity. 7 , 15 , 16 The KDQOL‐36 scores observed in our study cohort align with outcomes reported in comparable HD populations, including those observed in a local study conducted in Melbourne and an international cohort study involving patients in Poland. 17 , 18 Importantly, the present study highlights a persistent gap between treatment satisfaction and broader quality of life, which may reflect the multifactorial challenges of living with kidney failure, including comorbid conditions, treatment fatigue and social determinants. Further research is needed on how best to improve quality of life for people with kidney failure on HD, including those managed with incremental regimens.

Limitations of this study include its single‐centre design, small sample size, lack of a control group and the potential for selection bias. While response rates were high, the findings may not be generalisable to other populations or healthcare systems. Furthermore, this cross‐sectional design does not permit assessment of temporal changes or long‐term outcomes associated with incremental HD. The KDQOL‐36, although widely validated, lacks specific normative values for Australian dialysis populations, limiting interpretability. Randomised controlled trials and larger studies are needed to assess more definitively the clinical and patient‐reported outcomes of incremental HD.

This study adds valuable insight into the patient acceptability of incremental HD and supports its incorporation into patient‐centred dialysis care models. Implementation of incremental HD requires additional resources and systems for regular monitoring of RKF to enable adjustment of HD prescription, as well as the need for flexible scheduling within dialysis units. The positive reception by patients in this cohort suggests that such models are feasible and may align more closely with patient‐centred care. This raises the need to review funding models for maintenance HD, which currently favour a one‐size‐fits‐all approach, at odds with the goal of patients and their clinicians for more personalised care. Further insights about the outcomes of incremental HD are awaited from the INCH‐HD trial, which is a randomised controlled trial currently being conducted in Australia evaluating HRQOL, measured using the KDQOL‐SF, in patients initiating incremental HD compared to those commencing standard HD. The outcomes of this trial will be of significant interest, as they may provide further insights and help contextualise the findings observed within our own study cohort.

Measures of patient experience and quality of life are increasingly recognised as critical components for assessing the quality of dialysis care. While conventional HD has long been structured around fixed schedules, this uniform approach may not reflect individual patient needs or preferences, particularly for those who have recently started kidney replacement therapy and still have enough RKF to make a meaningful contribution to solute clearance. RKF has long been routinely incorporated into the prescription of peritoneal dialysis, but this approach is far less commonly applied for HD, despite KDOQI and European Best Practice Guidelines supporting this approach. 19 , 20 Incremental HD offers a personalised approach that may enhance treatment tolerability, reduce healthcare exposure and costs and improve patient autonomy. Although quality of life in dialysis patients remains impaired, likely reflective of co‐morbidity burden, a more positive treatment experience may reduce the negative impact of dialysis per se on quality of life.

In conclusion, in this single‐centre cross‐sectional survey, patients receiving incremental HD report a high level of satisfaction, treatment understanding and willingness to engage in the required monitoring to sustain the approach. Despite continued impairments in HRQOL, incremental HD was perceived favourably by patients and offers a model for more personalised dialysis care. These findings provide a foundation for further prospective and comparative studies to evaluate the clinical, psychosocial and economic implications of broader incremental HD implementation in models of care.
