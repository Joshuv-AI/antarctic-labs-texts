Association of serum zinc concentration with multiple surrogates of insulin resistance in a population-based study
Authors: Fateme Ghafouri-Taleghani, Zahra Bahadoran, Sajad Jeddi, Maryam Tohidi, Fereidoun Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global prevalence of insulin resistance is increasing and it plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of chronic non-communicable diseases. Lipid metabolism disorder is a key mediator of insulin resistance. This cross-sectional study investigated the possible association between serum zinc (SZn) concentration and distinct surrogates of insulin resistance (IR). SZn were measured using flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS) in 3078 adults (mean age of 48.8 ± 14.5, and 39.8% were male) participated in the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (2009–2011). The associations between SZn concentrations and surrogates of IR including homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, the TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI index), the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR), and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG-to-HDL-C ratio), were assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis. The mean serum zinc concentration was 112 ± 37.1 µg/dL. Serum zinc concentrations exceeding 119 µg/dL were significantly associated with a 32% increased probability of IR, identified by elevated TyG index (OR = 1.32; 95% CI = 1.08–1.59). Similarly, SZn concentrations greater than 91.3 µg/dL and 119 µg/dL were associated with a 30% and 34% increased probability of IR, as identified by the TG-HDL-C ratio, respectively. Our study revealed that elevated SZn concentrations, probably due to exposure to environmental pollutants, may be associated with increased probability of IR, identified by the high TyG index and TG-to-HDL-C ratio.Keywords: Zinc, TyG index, TG-to-HDL-C ratio, HOMA-IR, METS-IR, TyG-BMI indexSubject terms: Biochemistry, Environmental sciences, Endocrinology, Health care, Medical research, Pathogenesis

Introduction

Insulin resistance (IR) is a pathological state marked by reduced cellular responsiveness to insulin, necessitating elevated insulin concentrations to achieve normoglycemia, and is characterized by hyperinsulinemia, impaired glucose tolerance, attenuated adipose tissue lipolysis, diminished skeletal muscle glucose uptake, and inadequate hepatic gluconeogenesis suppression1. IR is implicated in the pathophysiology of obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, polycystic ovary syndrome, and certain malignancies2–4. Impaired metabolism of lipids, free fatty acids, ceramides, and triglycerides along with disrupted insulin signaling, elevated inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress play a key role in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance5. With the global prevalence of IR rising6, early diagnosis is critical to prevent associated clinical conditions7. The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) is the gold standard for assessing peripheral insulin sensitivity8, but its complexity and resource intensity restrict its routine clinical and epidemiological use. Various surrogate markers, including homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG index), the TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI index), the metabolic score for IR (METS-IR), and the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG-to-HDL-C ratio), have been developed as efficient tools to integrate multiple metabolic parameters for detecting IR9–11.

Zinc, the second most prevalent essential trace element, is crucial for various aspects of insulin function, including its production, storage, release, and removal. This makes zinc indispensable for regulating and maintaining healthy glucose balance12–15. Zinc plays a dual role in human health. As an essential micronutrient, it is crucial for maintaining proper insulin signaling; however, the effects of dysregulated zinc levels, particularly elevated concentrations, on insulin resistance are not yet fully understood16–18. Zinc can also act as a toxic environmental pollutant when present in excess. Pollution from heavy metals such as zinc is an increasing public health concern due to associated adverse health effects19. In our population, serum zinc levels exceed global averages20 and have been linked to higher risks of diabetes21 and metabolic syndrome22. Since dietary intake alone is unlikely to account for these elevated levels, environmental exposure, through contaminated soil and water, air pollution, industrial activities, cosmetics, and consumer products made from zinc or zinc alloys, may contribute significantly19. This study employed large-scale population-based data through a cross-sectional design to investigate the possible association between SZn concentration with IR using five direct and indirect surrogates, i.e., HOMA-IR, TyG index, TyG-BMI index, TG-to-HDL-C ratio, and METS-IR.

Methods

This study was a prospective cohort conducted within the framework of the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS), a population-based initiative launched in 1999 to address the prevention and management of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in District 13 of Tehran23. For the current cross-sectional study, we included 4767 adults, over 21 years old participated in the fourth phase of TLGS (2009–2011) with SZn measurement, of whom insulin data were available for 3113 individuals. After excluding 35 individuals who were received exogenous insulin, the final analysis comprised 3078 eligible participants.

Detailed methodologies regarding data collection and variable measurement in the TLGS have been previously published and are available elsewhere23,24. Comprehensive information on study participants, including smoking habits, medication use, physical activity levels (PAL), anthropometric measurements [body weight, height, waist circumference (WC), and body mass index (BMI)], as well as systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressures, has been previously documented24. Additionally, detailed descriptions of biochemical measurements conducted within the TLGS are available in prior publications24. Following a 12–14 h overnight fasting period, blood samples were collected from participants to measure fasting serum glucose (FSG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and creatinine (Cr) levels24,25. A standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was conducted for participants not on glucose-lowering medications. The test involved the administration of 82.5 g of glucose monohydrate solution (equivalent to 75 g of anhydrous glucose; Cerestar EP, Spain), followed by the measurement of serum glucose levels 2 h post-administration24. Fasting serum insulin was measured by the electrochemiluminescence immunoasaay (ECLIA), using Roche Diagnostics kits and the Roche/Hitachi Cobas e-411 analyzer (GmbH, Mannheim, Germany)24. The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for insulin were 1.2 and 3.5%, respectively. SZn concentrations were measured using flame atomic absorption spectrometry (FAAS) with the CTA-2000 instrument (Chem Tech Analytical Co., Kempston, UK). Detailed methodologies for SZn measurement have been described previously20. Both inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variation for all biochemical measurements were maintained below 5%, ensuring high precision and reliability.

The study protocol was approved by the ethics research council of the Research Institute for Endocrine Sciences, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (Ethics code: IR.SBMU.ENDOCRINE.REC.1403.151). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The IR surrogate indices considered as study outcomes were TyG index, TyG-BMI index, METS-IR, TG-to-HDL-C ratio, and HOMA-IR. The methodology for calculating IR surrogate indices is outlined in Table 110. Having IR was defined based on the cut-off values of these surrogates in relation to the risk of metabolic syndrome and T2D (see Table 1): TyG-index ≥ 8.6126, TyG-BMI index ≥ 22427, HOMA-IR was defined as values > 2.38 and 2.68, in male and female, respectively24, METS-IR ≥ 51.1310, TG-to-HDL-C ratio ≥ 2.75 and 1.65, in male and female, respectively28. The quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) was calculated as 1 ÷ [log FSI (µU/mL) + log FSG (mg/dL)]. HOMA-β was calculated as FSI (µU/ml) × 20/FSG (mmol/L)−3.529. Type 2 diabetes (T2DM) was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL or using glucose-lowering drugs30.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows version 20 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The normality of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, along with a post-hoc Bonferroni test. In cases where the continuous variables did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA was applied. For categorical variables, the Chi-square test was used. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for variables following a normal distribution, as median (interquartile range) for those that do not, and as percentages for non-continuous and categorical variables.

To assess linear association between SZn and surogates of IR, multivariable linear regression model adjusted for potential confounding variables: Model 1 included adjustments for age (years), sex (male/female), and waist circumference (cm); and Model 2 further incorporated smoking status (never-past/current), physical activity level [metabolic-equivalent (MET) < 600 min/week and MET ≥ 600 min/week], menopause status (yes/no), and use of anti-diabetic medications (yes/no). The relationship between SZn concentrations [both as continuous per SD increment and as tertile categories (< 91.3, 91.3–119, and ≥ 119 µg/dL)] and surrogate indices of IR assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis, with the outcomes presented as OR and 95% CIs. Two distinct regression models were constructed to account for potential confounding variables. Potential covariates were selected based on scientific31 and statistical32 evidence. A univariate analysis was conducted to identify potential confounding variables, with a PE (P value for entry) < 0.2 considered as an inclusion criterion in the final multivariable models32. Potential multicollinearity was assessed by calculating the variance inflation factor (VIF) for the fully adjusted model. The VIF was less than 2, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern and the variables could be reliably included in the model.

A sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding participants with T2DM (n = 405), to verify that the observed associations between SZn and insulin resistance surrogates were not influenced by the presence of overt T2D, and to confirm that they remained robust in the remaining population (n = 2673).

Results

We included 3078 eligible participants. The mean SZn concentration was 112 ± 37.1 µg/dL. Participants had a mean age of 48.8 ± 14.5 years, and 39.8% were male. The prevalence of IR based on HOMA-IR, METS-IR, TyG index, TyG-BMI-index, and TG-HDL-C index was 28%, 17.3%, 58.8%, 65.4%, 63.3%, respectively.

The mean and SD of SZn concentrations in the first, second, and third tertiles of SZn were 77.2 + 10.9, 104 + 7.86, and 155 + 28.72 µg/dL, respectively. The TyG index, TC, and TG in the third tertile of SZn concentrations were significantly higher than those in the first tertile (P < 0.05). Additionally, serum creatinine concentrations in the second tertile of SZn were significantly higher than those in both the first and third tertiles (P < 0.05).

Table 5 outlines the associations between SZn concentrations analyzed as tertile categories (< 91.3 µg/dL, 91.3–119 µg/dL, and ≥ 119 µg/dL) and surrogate indices of IR. After excluding patients with T2DM, the results remained consistent. The analysis of SZn concentrations, both as continuous variables (per SD increment) and as tertile categories (< 91.1 µg/dL, 91.1–118 µg/dL, and ≥ 118 µg/dL), demonstrated that SZn concentrations exceeding 118 µg/dL were significantly associated with a 29% increased probability of IR, as determined by the TyG index (OR = 1.29, 95% CI = 1.04–1.58). Additionally, SZn concentrations above 91.1 µg/dL and 118 µg/dL were associated with a 32% higher probability of IR, based on the TG-HDL-C ratio (OR = 1.32; 95% CI = 1.06–1.63).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study showed that elevated SZn concentrations were positively associated with increased probability of IR, as identified by the TyG index and TG-to-HDL-C ratio. Specifically, SZn levels exceeding 91.3 µg/dL and 119 µg/dL were associated with a 30% and 34% increase in the TG-to-HDL-C ratio, respectively. Additionally, SZn levels above 119 µg/dL were Linked to a 32% increase in the TyG index. While no significant Associations were observed with other surrogates of IR. The mean SZn concentration in our study population was 112± 37.1 µg/dL, which exceeds levels reported in other populations (75–90 µg/dL)20,33–36. As dietary intake alone is unlikely to explain this elevation, environmental pollution may play a significant role in contributing to the increased SZn19.

The growing knowledge of the insulin-like actions of trace elements such as zinc has sparked researchers’ interest in further investigating the role of zinc in diabetes and IR37. Study findings emphasize the complex role of zinc status in pathophysiology of IR, also glucose and lipid metabolism, and highlight the importance of further studies7. To date, no large-scale, population-based cross-sectional study has investigated the association between SZn concentration and surrogate markers of IR. An observational study investigating the association between SZn concentrations and IR reported findings consistent with the results of our study38. In this cohort study of 751 Finnish male followed over 20 years, individuals in the highest quartile of SZn concentrations exhibited a 60% increased risk of developing diabetes compared to those in the lowest quartile (95% CI: 1.2–2.13, P < 0.001). Additionally, elevated SZn levels were associated with higher serum insulin concentrations and increased IR38. Other observational studies have reported a positive association between SZn concentrations and the incidence of diabetes, though not specifically with IR. A case–control study conducted in China involving 551 participants demonstrated that higher SZn concentrations were associated with an increased likelihood of diabetes [OR = 2.26, 95% CI = 1.29–3.98, P < 0.01]39. Similarly, a cross-sectional analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2011 to 2016, including 5,153 adults, revealed a linear relationship between SZn concentrations and diabetes, with higher SZn concentration associated with elevated diabetes risk [OR = 1.64, 95% CI = 1.27–2.11, P < 0.01)]40. Furthermore, another cross-sectional study in China involving 1,837 participants found that higher SZn concentrations were significantly associated with an increased risk of diabetes [OR = 1.79, 95% CI = 1.13–2.84, P < 0.001)]41. Some observational studies have reported findings contrary to our results in clinical conditions associated with IR. A cross-sectional study analyzing NHANES data from 804 subjects found that higher SZn concentrations were associated with lower IR in prediabetic individuals17. Additionally, several studies have explored the relationship between zinc and IR in gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). A meta-analysis by Fan et al. demonstrated that zinc measurements during the second trimester of pregnancy could help identify high-risk cases for GDM42. Furthermore, multiple clinical trials have investigated the effects of zinc supplementation on IR, reporting improvements in IR following supplementation43–45. However, it is important to note that human intervention studies typically employ appropriate doses over short durations. In contrast, an animal study revealed that chronic or high-dose zinc administration in rats led to increased cortisol production, reduced peripheral glucose uptake, and subsequent hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and IR46. To contextualize the findings of our study, which demonstrated that elevated SZn concentration were associated with a significant increase in the TyG index and TG-to-HDL-C ratio, we will now explore the potential mechanisms linking high zinc to IR. Additionally, we will discuss the interplay between zinc, lipid metabolism, and IR.

While some studies suggest that the solute carrier family 30 member 8 (SLC30A8) genotype does not significantly influence peripheral insulin sensitivity15,47,48, others have highlighted zinc’s critical role in regulating hepatic insulin clearance15,46. Zinc through its interaction with the SLC30A8 gene polymorphism rs13266634, inhibits clathrin-dependent insulin endocytosis, thereby reducing insulin degradation15,46. Specifically, zinc co-secreted with insulin suppresses insulin degradation at the liver plasma membrane, potentially contributing to hyperinsulinemia in the context of elevated zinc levels15,46. Although zinc enhances the affinity and intensity of insulin binding to its receptor, its inhibition of hepatic insulin degradation may lead to hyperinsulinemia and an increased risk of diabetes15,46. Furthermore, reduced hepatic insulin clearance primarily induces IR in muscle tissue rather than in the liver15.Notably, individuals with IR due to impaired insulin clearance, often observed in normal-weight or less obese Asian populations, differ from those with IR associated with obesity, who tend to exhibit more pronounced adiposity49.

Impaired lipid metabolism is a significant risk factor for IR and related metabolic disorders50. The interaction between SLC30A8 and altered lipid metabolism in humans remains poorly understood51. However, studies have demonstrated that fat accumulation in hepatocytes, by protein kinase C epsilon (PKCε) activation via hepatic diacylglycerol (DAG), contributes to hepatic IR52. Given the liver’s central role in regulating glucose and lipid metabolism, hepatic IR can subsequently lead to systemic IR52. The minimal effective dose of zinc required to alter lipid metabolism remains undetermined and further investigation is warranted to elucidate this. However, approximately twice the recommended daily allowance (RDA) for zinc has been suggested53. While the majority of short-term intervention studies have reported that zinc administration either has no significant effect on HDL cholesterol or results in a modest increase54, contrasting findings have emerged from a long-term investigation55. Notably, a 7.5-year intervention study demonstrated a significant reduction in HDL cholesterol levels and a significant increase in serum TG concentrations among male participants consuming 20 mg of zinc daily (in combination with low doses of beta-carotene, vitamins C, E, and selenium)55.

On the other hand, elevated SZn concentrations result from increased zinc bioavailability, which can induce deficiencies in copper and magnesium due to competitive absorption46,56. Deficiencies in magnesium and copper are associated with hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and IR7,46,57,58.

Several studies have proposed a potential association between Zn-iron regulated transporter-like protein (ZIP) and IR, as briefly discussed in the following sections. These findings underscore the possible involvement of zinc in the pathophysiology of IR and emphasize the necessity for further research to elucidate the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms. Studies have demonstrated that ZIP13 plays a critical role in inhibiting the differentiation of beige adipose tissue, promoting obesity, and subsequently increasing IR59. Similarly, ZIP14 is implicated in hepatic iron accumulation and the development of hemochromatosis60, a condition associated with elevated ferritin levels, diabetes, and retinopathy61. In an animal model, ZIP14 knockout mice exhibited mild inflammation, hyperinsulinemia, and increased adipose tissue deposition62. Additionally, research has explored the role of ZIP7 in zinc release from the Golgi apparatus and its involvement in skeletal muscle cell signaling63. Given the high zinc content in skeletal muscle and its significance in IR, further investigation into ZIP7 may provide valuable insights into the mechanisms linking zinc metabolism to IR.

HOMA-IR is a widely utilized method for evaluating IR, however, its reliance on the measurement of fasting insulin levels limits its practicality in community and resource-limited settings64. In such contexts, the TyG index has emerged as a simpler and more feasible alternative64. The TyG index, which is strongly correlated with HOMA-IR, provides a reliable surrogate for assessing IR without the need for insulin assays, making it particularly advantageous in low-resource environments64. Additionally, the TG-to-HDL-C ratio has been identified as a valuable biomarker for IR, reflecting underlying alterations in lipid metabolism that are closely associated with IR65. Studies also suggest that the TG-to-HDL-C ratio should be used more widely to identify patients at high risk66.

This study represents the first investigation to explore the association between SZn concentration and IR utilizing five direct and indirect surrogate markers, i.e., HOMA-IR, TyG index, TyG-BMI index, TG-to-HDL-C ratio, and METS-IR. The analysis was conducted using large-scale, population-based data, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between SZn and IR. One of the inherent limitations of cross-sectional study designs is the inability to establish causality, and this study is no exception. While the findings provide valuable insights into the association between SZn concentration and IR as identified by the TG-to-HDL-C ratio and the TyG index, and have generalizability, the cross-sectional nature of the analysis precludes definitive conclusions regarding causal relationships. A significant difference was observed between male and female participants in this study. However, due to limited statistical power, it was not feasible to conduct separate subgroup analyses stratified by sex. Future studies are warranted to explore potential sex-specific associations in greater detail.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that elevated SZn concentrations are associated with a higher likelihood of IR, as evidenced by the TG-to-HDL-C ratio and the TyG index. These results suggest a potential role of zinc metabolism in the development of IR and highlight the need for further mechanistic studies to clarify the underlying biological pathways. Given the complex interplay between zinc and key metabolic processes, including glucose and lipid metabolism, insulin concentration, and insulin signaling, further research is warranted to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying this relationship. A deeper understanding of these pathways may provide critical insights into the role of zinc in metabolic regulation and its potential implications for IR. Given the high mean SZn concentrations observed in our population, we hypothesize that environmental pollutants contribute substantially to this elevation. Such increased zinc exposure may, in turn, influence lipid metabolism and insulin resistance, highlighting the need for further investigation in future studies.
