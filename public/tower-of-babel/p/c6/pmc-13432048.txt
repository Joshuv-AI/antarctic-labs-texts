Comparison of laser-assisted and intrapulpal local anesthesia for intra- and postoperative pain management in mandibular molars: A randomized clinical trial
Authors: M. Muthukumaran, R. S. Rathna Piriyanga, I. Anand Sherwood, Manishaa Jeyachandran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Intraoperative pain control in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis remains a challenge, particularly when nerve blocks fail. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of intrapulpal and laser-assisted local anesthesia in controlling intraoperative and postoperative pain during single-visit root canal treatment.Materials and Methods:One hundred and forty-three patients with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis in mandibular molars expressing pain on access opening were included. Patients were randomly allocated to receive either intrapulpal or laser-assisted anesthesia. Pain was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at predefined time points. Data were analyzed using statistical tests.Results:Both groups produced a marked reduction in intraoperative pain. Mean VAS scores decreased from 6.69 ± 1.22 to 1.23 ± 1.40 in the laser group and from 6.31 ± 1.35 to 1.32 ± 1.40 in the intrapulpal group. Pain levels were low during biomechanical preparation in both groups, with no significant difference. At 24 h, pain scores were comparable between both groups, but at 48 h, the laser group demonstrated significantly lower scores.Conclusion:Both intrapulpal and laser-assisted local anesthesia are effective techniques for managing intraoperative pain in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis. Laser-assisted anesthesia may serve as a better-tolerated alternative, with significantly less postoperative pain at 48 h.

INTRODUCTION

Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis remains one of the most common indications for endodontic intervention and is frequently associated with significant procedural pain. While the biological success of root canal treatment (RCT) is defined by effective microbial control and periapical healing, patients often judge treatment success primarily by their pain experience. Inadequate intraoperative pain control can increase anxiety, reduce patient confidence, and negatively affect acceptance of dental care, regardless of technical treatment quality. Therefore, achieving effective pulpal anesthesia with minimal postoperative discomfort is a critical determinant of patient satisfaction and clinical efficiency in endodontic practice.[12]

The management of mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, commonly referred to as hot teeth, poses a significant anesthetic challenge. Inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) failure rates in such cases have been reported to range from 44% to 81%, even when appropriate technique and anesthetic volume are employed.[3] The reduced efficacy of local anesthesia in such cases has been attributed to several pathophysiological mechanisms, including inflammatory acidosis that limits the availability of the lipid-soluble base form of local anesthetics, upregulation of tetrodotoxin-resistant sodium channels, increased pulpal blood flow, increased nociceptor excitability, and central sensitization within the trigeminal system. In mandibular molars, dense cortical bone, anatomical variations, and accessory innervation further compound the difficulty in achieving reliable pulpal anesthesia.[4] Various strategies have been proposed to improve anesthetic success, including repeat nerve blocks, modification of anesthetic solutions, preoperative anti-inflammatory medication, and supplemental techniques.[356] Although nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and corticosteroid premedication may enhance IANB success by reducing pulpal inflammation, their effects remain inconsistent-making supplemental approaches such as intraligamentary, intraosseous, infiltration, and intrapulpal injections necessary.[789]

Among these, intrapulpal anesthesia is considered one of the most reliable methods once pulp exposure is achieved, with its effectiveness primarily attributed to back pressure rather than the pharmacological action of the anesthetic agent.[10] However, it is often associated with intense pain during administration, which may adversely affect the patient’s overall treatment experience, particularly in anxious individuals.[11] Despite this, it continues to serve as an irreplaceable tool in managing hot teeth as it provides sufficient desensitization until the pulp can be fully extirpated. This is also the rationale behind why it serves as a benchmark for newer techniques of achieving pulpal anesthesia.

Recently, diode lasers have gained interest in endodontics for their potential analgesic effects, attributed to modulation of nerve conduction, inflammatory mediators, and photobiomodulation,[12] in addition to their established roles in disinfection and irrigant activation.[13] Preliminary clinical studies suggest that laser application to the pulp chamber may reduce intraoperative and postoperative pain in irreversible pulpitis, although evidence remains limited and heterogeneous.[141516]

Direct comparisons between intrapulpal anesthesia and laser-assisted analgesia in mandibular molars are scarce, particularly with concurrent evaluation of intraoperative and postoperative outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of intrapulpal anesthesia and laser-assisted local anesthesia applied directly to the pulp chamber in controlling intraoperative pain in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, and to evaluate postoperative pain at 24 and 48 h following single-visit RCT. The null hypothesis was that there would be no significant difference in intraoperative or postoperative pain between the two supplemental anesthetic techniques.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective, randomized, parallel-group, single-center clinical trial was conducted in the Postgraduate Clinic of the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, CSI College of Dental Sciences and Research, between February 2025 and November 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (CSICDSR/IEC/0300/2024) and was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2025/01/079286). The study followed the ethical principles of the latest version of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024) and was designed and reported in compliance with the updated CONSORT 2025 guidelines for randomized clinical trials.[17]

Participants and eligibility criteria

Patients were recruited after obtaining written informed consent and screened according to a predefined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were systemically healthy adults aged 18–60 years (American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] I and II) presenting with mandibular first or second molars diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with normal apical tissues, a periapical index (PAI) score = 1, and willingness to comply with study procedures and follow-up.

Exclusion criteria included systemically compromised or immunocompromised patients (ASA >II), teeth that did not elicit pain upon pulp exposure, retreatment or intentionally planned multi-visit cases, patients who had consumed analgesics or anti-inflammatory drugs within 24 h before treatment, teeth without opposing dentition or with significant occlusal or parafunctional discrepancies, severely curved roots (>30°) or aberrant root morphology, pregnant or lactating women, and patients with known allergies to materials used in the study.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software for Windows (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich - Heine - Universitat Dusseldorf, Germany). Based on a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50), an α-error probability of 0.05, and a power of 0.80 with an allocation ratio of 1:1, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 64 participants per group, in accordance with conventional effect size interpretation.[18] To compensate for potential dropouts and exclusions during the study period, the sample size was increased to 75 participants per group, resulting in a total sample size of 150 participants. The final analysis included 143 participants.

Preoperative assessment and diagnosis

All patients underwent a standardized preoperative clinical and radiographic evaluation. Intraoral periapical radiographs were obtained to aid clinical diagnosis, assess periapical status, and confirm eligibility based on the PAI score. All participants reported preoperative pain that was continuous, spontaneous, radiating, nocturnal, or throbbing in nature. Pulp sensibility testing was performed using a cold test (Endo-Frost, Coltene Whaledent, Switzerland). Teeth included in the study exhibited an exaggerated response to cold testing, with lingering pain, consistent with a diagnosis of symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with normal apical tissues. In addition, bleeding was observed on gaining access to the pulp chamber, further confirming pulpal vitality.

Anesthetic protocol and interventions

All clinical procedures were performed by a single experienced operator to ensure standardization. A 0.2 mL intradermal injection of the local anesthetic agent to be used was given before the IANB in order to rule out any potential allergic reactions. All patients received an initial IANB using 1.8 mL of 2% lignocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline (Lignox®, Warren Pharmaceuticals Pvt. Ltd., Mumbai, India).

Following rubber dam isolation, access cavity preparation was initiated using sterile burs from the Mani Endo Access Diamond Bur kit (MANI Inc., Tochigi, Japan). If pain was present, it was determined as a failure of primary anesthesia. 0.2–0.5 mL of the same anesthetic solution was then given intraligamentarily at the four corners of the tooth within the gingival sulcus as an adjunct. The procedure was then resumed. If the patient continued to report pain, they were confirmed as hot teeth and were included for the study and randomly allocated into one of the two anesthetic interventions.

Randomization and blinding

A total of 150 patients who had pain upon access opening following IANB and intraligamentary failure were randomly allocated into two groups of 75 participants each:

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random sequence table (www.random.org), with allocation concealment ensured through sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent endodontist not involved in treatment delivery or outcome assessment. Due to the nature of the interventions, blinding of the operator and participants was not feasible.

Laser-assisted anesthesia group

In the laser group, an InGaAsP semiconductor diode laser (iLase®, Biolase, CA, USA) was initially applied directly to the pulp tissue following its exposure. Laser activation was performed using a wavelength of 940 nm at a power output of 1.5 W in continuous mode, while adhering to international laser safety standards. The laser was delivered using an E2–20 endodontic tip (200 μm diameter, 20 mm length), and application duration ranged from 60 to 180 s depending on clinical feasibility. Where complete analgesia was not achieved, laser application was repeated as access cavity preparation progressed and additional areas of the pulp chamber and canal orifices were exposed.

Intrapulpal anesthesia group

In the intrapulpal group, initial anesthesia was administered at the site of pulp exposure. A 27G needle was placed directly into the coronal pulp tissue, and incremental deposition of local anesthetic solution was performed until adequate back-pressure was obtained. The volume injected was minimal (approximately 0.2–0.5 mL), and the injection was continued while maintaining firm pressure until the patient reported anesthesia at the site. In cases where complete analgesia was not achieved, both techniques were repeated as access cavity preparation progressed and additional pulp tissue or canal orifices were exposed.

Pre- and intraoperative pain assessment and time points

Pain intensity was assessed using a 10-point Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst imaginable pain. Baseline pain was recorded preoperatively before administration of IANB on the day of treatment. During access cavity preparation following primary anesthesia, if the patient reported pain, the VAS score was recorded and the patient was included in the study. The allocated supplemental anesthetic intervention was then administered. Immediately after the intervention, access cavity preparation was resumed, and pain intensity was recorded again using the VAS. Pain was subsequently assessed during biomechanical preparation at the time of rotary instrumentation to evaluate the effectiveness of the supplemental anesthetic technique beyond the coronal pulp tissue.

Root canal procedure

Working length determination was carried out using an electronic apex locator (E-Pex Apex Locator, Eighteeth, Changzhou, China) with size #10 K-files (MANI Inc., Tochigi, Japan) and was confirmed radiographically. Initial preparation was performed manually up to sizes #20 or #25 K-files. Biomechanical preparation was performed using a brushless endodontic motor (Woodpecker EndoPace, India) with the NeoEndo S rotary file system (Orikam Healthcare Pvt. Ltd., Delhi, India) up to a standardized apical preparation size of 25/0.06, terminating 0.5 mm short of the working length.

Intermittent irrigation was performed using copious amounts of sterile saline mixed with 2% w/v povidone-iodine (Puradine®, Leeford Healthcare Ltd., Mumbai, India).[1920] After the canals were dried using sterile paper points, obturation was performed in the same visit using the single-cone technique with size 25/0.06 gutta-percha cones (NeoEndo, Orikam Healthcare Pvt. Ltd., India) and a zinc oxide eugenol-based sealer. Excess gutta-percha was removed, and the access cavity was restored using the Te-Econom Plus composite system (Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein) to re-establish occlusal anatomy. No occlusal reduction was performed in any case to simulate functional clinical conditions.

Postoperative pain assessment and time points

Postoperative pain levels were recorded at 24 h and 48 h posttreatment using the same 10-point VAS via structured telephonic interviews. Each patient was reminded of the scale before rating their pain. The nature of pain and a trigger factor (if any) was also recorded and categorized as spontaneous continuous, spontaneous occasional, or stimulus-based pain (during mastication or thermal stimuli).

In addition to absolute VAS scores, the magnitude of intraoperative pain reduction was calculated. Change in intraoperative pain score (ΔIP) was calculated as the difference between preintervention and postintervention VAS scores. Percentage reduction in intraoperative pain was calculated using the formula from a previous study.[14]

Change in intraoperative pain score (ΔIP) = Preintervention VAS Score − Postintervention VAS score

% reduction in intraoperative pain score = (ΔIP/Preintervention VAS Score) ×100

All patients were prescribed ketorolac 10 mg (Ketorol DT, Dr. Reddy’s Laboratories Ltd., Hyderabad, Telangana, India) as rescue medication and were instructed to take it only in case of considerable pain. Alternate phone numbers were noted so that the participants could be efficiently contacted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and baseline variables. The distribution of pain intensity scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As VAS scores were ordinal in nature and did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric tests were employed for inferential analysis. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Within-group comparisons of pain scores across different time points were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while between-group comparisons at corresponding time points were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 143 patients were included in the final analysis, with 73 patients in the laser-assisted anesthesia group and 70 patients in the intrapulpal anesthesia group, as illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram [Figure 1]. The study population demonstrated comparable baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between groups, with similar mean age (laser: 32.28 ± 13.09 years; intrapulpal: 31.46 ± 12.21 years), gender distribution (female: 53.42% vs. 55.71%), tooth type (mandibular first molars: 68.49% vs. 71.42%), and preoperative pain characteristics, predominantly spontaneous continuous pain (53.42% vs. 42.85%). No statistically significant differences were observed between groups across these variables, indicating adequate baseline homogeneity and absence of selection bias.

DISCUSSION

Management of intraoperative pain in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis continues to represent a significant clinical challenge, particularly when IANB fails to achieve profound pulpal anesthesia. Supplemental techniques are frequently required in such situations, yet comparative evidence evaluating their effectiveness across different intraoperative stages remains limited. The present study was designed to compare two supplemental pain control modalities – intrapulpal anesthesia and laser-assisted local anesthesia – within a standardized single-visit endodontic protocol.

Building on the findings of Subashri et al. evaluating laser-assisted local anesthesia for intraoperative and postoperative pain control in multirooted teeth with irreversible pulpitis,[14] the present study represents a continuation of this line of investigation within the same academic setting, while being conducted independently with a comparable core methodology. The present investigation extends prior work by incorporating pain assessment during biomechanical preparation, thereby allowing evaluation of the depth and anatomical extent of analgesic action beyond coronal pulp anesthesia. In addition, postoperative pain assessment up to 48 h enabled appraisal of short-term outcomes following single-visit treatment.

In the present study, both intrapulpal anesthesia and laser-assisted anesthesia resulted in a significant reduction in intraoperative pain following intervention. Within-group comparisons demonstrated effective pain attenuation during access continuation and biomechanical preparation. Between-group comparisons did not reveal statistically significant differences at corresponding intraoperative time points, indicating that both modalities were capable of achieving clinically meaningful analgesia when used as supplemental techniques. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was rejected only with respect to selected postoperative outcomes.

An incidental observation in the present study was that preintervention intraoperative pain scores were numerically higher in the laser group compared to the intrapulpal group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Despite this modest baseline variation, laser-assisted anesthesia produced a comparable magnitude of pain reduction throughout the procedure.

The effectiveness of intrapulpal anesthesia is well established. The mechanical compression of pulpal nerve fibers results in rapid conduction block, producing profound anesthesia even in inflamed pulp tissues.[2122] Laser-assisted anesthesia operates through a fundamentally different mechanism. When applied directly to exposed pulp tissue in contact mode, diode lasers deliver localized thermal energy that may induce superficial tissue vaporization and controlled thermal coagulation at the point of contact.[23] This localized thermal effect can contribute to immediate pain attenuation by disrupting nociceptive nerve endings within the exposed pulp.[14]

In addition to thermal effects, diode lasers are known to exert photobiomodulatory actions that influence nerve conduction and inflammatory signaling. Experimental studies have demonstrated that laser irradiation can reversibly inhibit depolarization of C-fibers, reduce nerve conduction velocity, and modulate the release of inflammatory mediators involved in pain transmission.[12] These effects may contribute to both intraoperative analgesia and early postoperative pain modulation. Unlike conventional low-level laser therapy applied in noncontact modes, the present study employed direct application to exposed pulp tissue, thereby combining localized thermal effects with photobiomodulatory mechanisms.

Previous clinical investigations evaluating laser-induced pulpal analgesia have largely focused on single-rooted teeth or employed noncontact irradiation protocols. Asthana et al. utilized noncontact low-level laser therapy and reported reduced intraoperative pain during access preparation in single-rooted teeth, suggesting neuromodulatory rather than thermal mechanisms.[24] Similarly, a more recent study by Abarna et al. also employed noncontact diode laser irradiation in mandibular molars undergoing single-visit RCT and reported improved anesthetic outcomes, further supporting a photobiomodulatory mechanism of action.[25] In contrast, studies employing direct contact application have demonstrated immediate analgesic effects consistent with both photobiomodulation and localized tissue interaction.[14] The present findings align with these observations while extending their relevance to deeper stages of canal preparation.

Postoperative pain outcomes revealed comparable but insignificant pain levels between groups at 24 h, and a statistically significant difference was observed at 48 h. While the clinical magnitude of this difference should be interpreted cautiously, it suggests that laser-assisted anesthesia may confer a similar level of desensitization as intrapulpal anesthesia at 24 h, and a decrease in postoperative pain at 48 h. This observation may be related to the combined neuromodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects attributed to laser application, although further investigation is required to establish definitive mechanisms.

Irrigation in the present study was performed using sterile saline combined with povidone-iodine to minimize chemical confounding.[1920] Although sodium hypochlorite and chlorhexidine are considered gold-standard irrigants, both have been shown to influence postoperative pain, particularly in cases of apical extrusion or direct contact with inflamed tissues. In addition, sodium hypochlorite was avoided during the intraoperative phase to prevent potential interaction with residual intrapulpal lidocaine-based solution, which has been reported to result in the formation of 2,6-xylidine, a carcinogenic precipitate.[112627] This neutral irrigation protocol allowed a more isolated evaluation of the anesthetic interventions, strengthening internal validity. However, it may limit direct extrapolation to clinical settings where sodium hypochlorite is routinely used.

From a patient-centered perspective, fear of needles and apprehension toward multiple injections remain significant barriers to effective pain management during endodontic treatment. Intrapulpal anesthesia, despite its reliability, is often perceived as distressing due to the intense pain during its administration. Laser-assisted anesthesia offers a less invasive alternative that may be better tolerated by anxious patients while providing comparable analgesic efficacy.

The strengths of the present study include randomized allocation, standardized diagnostic criteria, uniform treatment protocols, assessment of multiple intraoperative time points, and complete follow-up. Limitations include the single-center design, inability to blind the operator and participants, omission of gold standard irrigants, recall bias due to the telephonic nature of the postoperative pain interviews, and reliance on subjective self-reported pain scores. Future studies should explore longer follow-up periods, different lasers and clinical parameters, and objective biomarkers of pulpal and periapical inflammation to further elucidate the role of laser-assisted anesthesia in endodontic pain management.

CONCLUSION

Both intrapulpal anesthesia and laser-assisted local anesthesia were effective supplemental techniques for controlling intraoperative pain in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis following failed IANB. While intrapulpal anesthesia remains a reliable method for achieving profound pulpal anesthesia, laser-assisted anesthesia offers a less invasive alternative that may be better tolerated by certain patients, particularly those apprehensive about additional injections. Within the limitations of this study, laser-assisted anesthesia demonstrated comparable intraoperative efficacy and was associated with lower postoperative pain at 48 h.
