Background

Regional anaesthesia of the eye, including retrobulbar, peribulbar, and sub-Tenon's block techniques, remains a cornerstone of analgesic practices in ophthalmologic surgery worldwide.1,2 In the hands of experienced practitioners, these techniques support a broad range of procedures, from routine cataract surgeries to complex retinal and buckle operations. Their versatility is further enhanced when combined with varying levels of sedation, from no sedation at all to general anaesthesia3 with secured airways, making these blocks invaluable tools for both ophthalmic surgeons and anaesthetists alike.

Despite their widespread use, the potential complications of these regional techniques, though rare, can be severe. They include retrobulbar haemorrhage, bulbar perforation, and intra-orbital compartment syndrome, potentially leading to vision or eye loss.4 Orbital compartment syndrome (OCS) is a rare but serious complication of regional blocks, with an estimated incidence ranging between 0.02% and 1%, depending on the specific block technique and patient population.1 In addition, injection along the optic nerve's dura sheath can escalate to brainstem anaesthesia,5 respiratory and cardiac arrest, or generalised seizures.

Vision loss in cases of OCS is primarily attributed to ischaemia of the optic nerve and retina caused by elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP), which compromises perfusion. Timing is critical, as irreversible optic nerve damage can occur within 60 to 90 min of sustained IOP above 40 mmHg.2,3 Immediate intervention by performing a lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis (LCIC)6 is crucial to rapidly decompress the orbit and restore perfusion, emphasising the need for clinician proficiency in performing this procedure. Therefore, every clinician administering regional eye blocks should – as an increasing number of emergency medicine clinicians are doing – be proficient in this swift and safe procedure.7 Sadly, retrobulbar bleeding in trauma and ocular surgery cases is frequently overlooked or addressed too late, underscoring the importance of training clinicians in canthotomy skills.8,9,10

This work aims to validate the pig-head teaching model by investigating IOP variations during a simulated intra-orbital haemorrhage and following the LCIC procedure.11 This study seeks to demonstrate the clinical utility of this procedure in managing acute orbital compartment syndrome and preventing optic nerve damage. For several decades, animal models have facilitated the transfer of knowledge and therapeutic strategies to human medicine. Porcine models are especially valuable due to their anatomical similarities to humans (Fig. 1).12,13 Like other authors, we have extensively used porcine eyes to teach sub-Tenon's blocks.14 The null hypothesis (H0) posited that there would be no significant difference in IOP reduction between the LCIC and control groups. Conversely, the alternative hypothesis (H1) proposed that the LCIC group would exhibit a more significant reduction in IOP than the control group.

Methods

This study followed the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects and complied with the Swiss Human Research Act. The Cantonal Ethics Committee of Bern (KEK Bern) Switzerland waived ethical approval for this study (BASEC-number: Req-2024-00798) on 18 June 2024. The animal material was obtained and treated in accordance with guidelines given by the Swiss academy of Medical Sciences.15 Human material was obtained from the body donation program of the Institute of Anatomy, University of Bern, in accordance with the Guidelines of the Swiss Academy of Medical Sciences adapted 2014 and to the Federal Act on Research involving Human Beings.16

Study design

The study employed a randomised comparative design to evaluate the effectiveness of the LCIC procedure in reducing IOP in a simulated model of retrobulbar haemorrhage using porcine heads.

Outcomes

Primary outcome: Analysis of the difference in IOP trends between the LCIC and control groups at the defined time points (T0, T1, T2, T3).

Secondary outcome: The rate and extent of reduction in IOP following the LCIC procedure. We decided on a target rate of IOP decrease of ≥3 mmHg per minute. Although no formal guidelines exist for the ideal rate of pressure release, the choice of ≥3 mmHg per minute as the target rate of IOP reduction is supported by clinical observations and case reports. A case study documented a patient with orbital compartment syndrome (OCS) who underwent LCIC, resulting in an immediate IOP decrease from 50 mmHg to 20 mmHg.4 This rapid reduction – approximately 30 mmHg within a short timeframe – demonstrates the feasibility and clinical relevance of this rate of decrease. Pressures above 40 mmHg are recognised as a critical threshold for LCIC due to the risk of optic nerve and retinal ischaemia.5 Given the estimated orbital volume of 30 ml, reducing IOP by 30 mmHg over 10 min was considered a reasonable goal to normalise pressures and restore perfusion.6

Randomisation

We employed a randomisation procedure to ensure unbiased allocation of porcine eyes into the experimental and control groups. Each eye was assigned a unique identification number from 1 to 26. A computer-generated random number list (www.random.org) was used to randomly assign the eyes into the LCIC and control groups. Eyes allocated to the LCIC group would undergo the lateral canthotomy with an inferior cantholysis procedure after model preparation. The remaining eyes were assigned to the control group, which received no decompressing intervention.

Setting

Retrobulbar bleeding Bernese model

Our porcine anatomic models were first visually inspected concerning similarity compared to the human orbit (Fig. 1). We verified suspension of the lids and whether the lateral canthal ligament was present, similar to human anatomy.

Additionally, the globe and retrobulbar structures were inspected by ultrasound and referenced to Dutton's Atlas.17 The arrangement of optic nerve, vascular networks and orbital fat were comparable. In both human and pig orbits, the eye and its cone-shaped fascial envelope were fixed by bony walls except anteriorly. Here, the orbital septum and eyelids form a tight suspension (Fig. 1), impeding anterior transposition of the globe in a situation of increasing retrobulbar pressure. Every significant retrobulbar mass effect, therefore, results in a compartment syndrome, potentially reducing perfusion of the optic nerve.18

Retrobulbar bleeding Bernese model preparation

Thirteen porcine heads, sourced from a local abattoir, were used to develop a model of retrobulbar haemorrhage. After visual inspection, all twenty-six eyes were screened using a Philips ultrasound machine (CX 50) and an L 12–5 MHz transducer, ensuring no intra- or retrobulbar tumours, fluid collections, or other anomalies were present. Intra-ocular pressure (IOP) was measured with an iCare PRO applanation tonometer (Icare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland). We performed five readings per eye and considered the average value to establish a baseline IOP (T0).

Following the baseline measurements, a 23 G 38.1 mm length, sharp tip, disposable Atkinson retrobulbar needle (Hurricane Medical, Bradenton, FL, USA) was used to inject 25 ml of Hydroxyethyl Starch 130/0.4 in a balanced electrolyte solution (Voluven 6%, Fresenius Kabi, Kriens, Switzerland) in the retrobulbar space. This injection volume was chosen to induce hardening of the eye, proptosis, conjunctival chemosis, and lid swelling, effectively simulating the conditions of a retrobulbar haemorrhage likely to entrain functional damage in clinical life.6 Finally, ultrasound images were captured, illustrating intra- and extra-conal fluid deposits, conjunctival chemosis and lid swelling, thus validating the model's ability to replicate the clinical signs of retrobulbar bleeding.19 After verifying the fluid deposits, we performed five pressure readings per eye again and considered the average value (T1).

Lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis procedure

After model preparation, we performed LCICs on 13 porcine eyes, following the technique described by Rowh et al. 20 The method consisted of a first horizontal cut from the lateral canthus towards the lateral margin of the orbit (canthotomy), using a pair of blunt-tipped scissors, aiming to separate the upper and lower eyelids. Afterwards, the conjunctiva of the lower eyelid was cut open to expose the superior and inferior crus of the lateral canthal tendon. The cantholysis was made by cutting the inferior crus of the tendon until it is completely released from the lower lid, and the latter moves back from the lid margin. We observed for the falling away of the lid from the globe and any oozing of the injected liquid from the canthotomy wound as an indicator of successful pressure release.

In the LCIC group, IOP was measured at two further points: immediately after the LCIC (T2), and 10 min post-LC (T3). Specifically, in the LCIC group, T2 refers to the IOP measurement immediately post-LCIC, while T3 is recorded at 10 min post-LCIC. In the control group, T2 is equivalent to the 10-min postinjection measurement, and T3 is at 20 min postinjection.

The rate of IOP decrease was evaluated with a benchmark of ≥3 mmHg per minute and starting at a departure point of well above 40 mmHg, the objective measure of the clinical sign ‘a rock-hard eye’.6 This rate of decrease was crucial for aligning with the clinical justification for rapid decompression, preventing optic nerve damage, and preserving vision.21 The remaining 16 eyes from intact porcine heads underwent the same injection regimen without any surgical intervention, allowing us to compare the trends in intra-ocular pressure with and without the LCIC procedure.

Sample size calculation

For an experiment comparing two independent groups with a continuous endpoint, expecting at least a decrease in 20% of IOP, an alpha of 0.05, and a power of 80%, the required sample size was 12 subjects per group (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx). We accounted for 10% more eyes (n = 2) due to the possibility of unsuitable eyes for model preparation.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated to determine the median and interquartile range of IOP measurements at each time point for both the LCIC and control groups. To assess the overall effectiveness of the LCIC procedure over time, a repeated measures ANOVA was conducted within the LCIC and No LCIC groups. This test compared IOP measurements across the different time points to evaluate whether the procedure significantly reduced IOP from baseline to postprocedural stages. The repeated measures ANOVA also allowed for analysing within-subject variations and determining time-dependent changes in IOP. An independent sample Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare the IOP changes between the LCIC and control groups at each corresponding time point. This test aimed to identify statistically significant differences in IOP trends between the treated and untreated eyes. A post hoc analysis with the Bonferroni correction was applied in cases where the ANOVA revealed significant differences. This analysis helped identify specific time points where significant changes in IOP occurred, providing more detailed insights into the effectiveness of the LCIC procedure at various stages of the intervention. A significance level (P-value) of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Analyses were performed with SPSS v.27 (IBM, Armonk, USA).

Results

After model preparation, three eyes allocated to the control group were excluded due to excessive fluid leakage from the orbital tissue (Fig. 2).

The initial screening measurements at T0 resulted in a median intra-ocular pressure of 15.34 mmHg [13.78 to 6.98], with no statistical differences between groups (P = 0.693). After injection (T1), the median intra-ocular pressure rose to 64.7 mmHg [49.94 to 67.62], remaining similar in the two groups (P = 0.257). Pressure differences were observed at 10 min (P = 0.006) and 20 min (P < 0.001) after Voluven injection, with LCIC significantly affecting the pressure changes over time. After LCIC, a median decrease of −45.8 mmHg was observed, compared with a mean decrease of −20.9 mmHg in the control group (P = 0.002, large effect size r = 0.63, Table 2). After that, the pressure decay in both groups was not statistically different (−3.38 mmHg vs. −6.95 mmHg, P = 0.161, Fig. 3). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the decrease in IOP with the LCIC manoeuvre in a porcine model. The results highlight that LCIC significantly reduced IOP immediately and 10 min after the procedure compared to the control group, indicating effective management of elevated IOP caused by retrobulbar haemorrhage. The primary outcome revealed that LCIC significantly reduced IOP, indicating that LCIC can effectively alleviate the pressure associated with retrobulbar haemorrhage. The agreed target rate of IOP decrease (≥3 mmHg per minute) was achieved only in the LCIC group. This underscores the procedure's efficacy in rapid decompression, which is crucial for preserving optic nerve and retinal perfusion and preventing vision loss.

Our findings align with previous studies emphasising the importance of timely intervention in cases of retrobulbar haemorrhage to prevent optic nerve damage and potential vision loss.22,23 Some publications describe a management of intra-orbital haemorrhage that awaits spontaneous cessation of bleeding and delivers reduction of intra-orbital pressures through cooling, sitting the patient up, administration of mannitol and repeated testing of intra-ocular pressures for around 90 min.24 Due to the scarcity of data, reliable guidelines to adequately deal with retrobulbar haemorrhage are unavailable. But with IOP significantly above 40 mmHg, proptosis, lid chemosis and a cadaver setting that modelled afferent pupillary defect, our model rather indicates swift LCIC.21 Hence, LCIC needs to find its place in different management approaches.

LCIC performed by a trained clinician seems to provide a swift improvement of intra-ocular pressures with an improvement in retinal and optic nerve perfusion, provided systemic pressures are adequate.25,26 The significant reduction in IOP observed in the LCIC group highlights the necessity for clinicians administering regional eye blocks to be proficient in performing LCIC. Given the potential complications of regional anaesthesia, including retrobulbar haemorrhage and intra-orbital compartment syndrome, immediate and effective intervention is critical. In this view, we propose that this training model, which seems anatomically and clinically appropriate, could be used to train clinicians to aid in quick recovery of the potentially catastrophic condition of intra-orbital bleeding.

Given the emergency nature of OCS and the potential for rapid vision loss, training in LCIC should be prioritised for clinicians most likely to encounter this condition. This includes anaesthetists performing regional eye blocks and surgeons specialising in ophthalmology, trauma, and otorhinolaryngology. Each speciality has a unique role in identifying and managing OCS, and proficiency in LCIC ensures that intervention can be delivered promptly, regardless of the clinical setting. As for the training timing, we recommend that LCIC instruction be introduced during advanced residency or fellowship training in these specialities. This timing allows trainees to develop the foundational anatomical and procedural knowledge required for effective LCIC performance. Practical training should involve high-fidelity models like the porcine head model validated in our study to ensure skill acquisition in a safe and controlled environment. Including LCIC in standardised emergency management courses, such as Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS), could further ensure widespread competency across specialities.

The use of porcine models for teaching and training in ophthalmologic procedures is well documented, owing to their anatomical similarities to human eyes. The porcine cadaveric model is cost-effective, accessible, and offers a reproducible learning curve, making it suitable for training. It is less expensive than human cadaveric models or live animals.27,28 Furthermore, the IOP measured at T0 was within the normal range described in the literature; the same applied for the measured pressures simulating retrobulbar haemorrhage.21 Despite the absence of comparative pressure measurements in humans, we can assume that the pressure behaviour in the human eye after decompression is similar to the phenomenon described in the study. Our findings align with previous studies emphasising the importance of timely intervention in cases of retrobulbar haemorrhage to prevent optic nerve damage and potential vision loss. This study further validates the pig-head model as an effective training tool, particularly for emergency procedures like LCIC, which can be organ-saving in acute settings.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly using porcine eyes, while anatomically similar to human eyes, may not perfectly replicate human responses. Additionally, the study's sample size, though calculated to provide sufficient power, was relatively small. Pig eyes from abattoirs can be damaged during slaughter or postmortem, which may have led to the loss of three eyes for the study. The coarser pig tissue might lead trainees to use more force than needed in human situations. The timing framework between T2 and T3 differs between the intervention and control groups, which may have contributed to the lack of significant differences in the observed IOP reduction trends during these intervals. Future studies with larger sample sizes and possibly using human cadaver models could further validate these findings. Finally, the control group in our study was subjected to no intervention following injection, which allowed us to establish a baseline for the natural regression of IOP in the absence of decompression. While this approach facilitated a clear comparison with the LCIC group, it may not fully reflect real-life scenarios where nonsurgical interventions, such as diuretics or positional changes, are commonly employed. Future studies could include additional control groups receiving such treatments to better simulate clinical practice and enhance the generalisability of the findings.

Despite these limitations, the model may be valuable for trauma emergency training and for clinicians performing eye blocks. It simulates three of the four signs of imminent visual damage (eye hard as rock, lid tightness and proptosis), though it cannot simulate afferent pupillary defects.

Further research is warranted to explore the long-term outcomes of LCIC in various clinical settings and its potential role in other ocular emergencies. Additionally, studies focusing on anatomical and histological similarities to validate the pig-head model to more closely mimic human anatomy and responses could enhance the training and educational value of this model. Investigating the efficacy of LCIC in combination with other interventions or under different sedation levels could also provide deeper insights into optimising patient outcomes during ophthalmic surgeries (Fig. 4).

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the effectiveness of LCIC in significantly reducing IOP. The findings underscore the importance of training clinicians in LCIC to manage acute orbital compartment syndrome. Incorporating such training into standard practice could significantly improve the potential for preventing optic nerve damage and preserving vision in emergency settings. This may be especially important in the population of clinicians performing sharp needle regional anaesthesia of the eye.
