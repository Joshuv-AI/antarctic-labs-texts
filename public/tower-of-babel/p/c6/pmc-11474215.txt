Reduced brain connectivity underlying value-based choices and outcomes in stimulant use disorder

Published: 2024

Introduction

Stimulant drug addiction (encompassing cocaine use disorder and amphetamine use disorder) is difficult to treat (Bentzley et al., 2021, Degenhardt et al., 2014), and has a particularly high rate of relapse (Brecht and Herbeck, 2014, Kampman, 2019). Neural systems that underpin the ability to make optimal choices are an essential component for successful life functioning, and may be particularly important for stimulant use disorder patients in recovery (Gowin et al., 2015, Gowin et al., 2014, Paulus et al., 2005, Stewart et al., 2014). Functional neuroimaging studies examining the neural correlates of value-based choices have identified localised disturbances across numerous brain regions in stimulant use disorder patients (Ersche et al., 2011a, Gowin et al., 2014, Kohno et al., 2014, Paulus et al., 2003, Paulus et al., 2002, Stewart et al., 2014). The widespread distribution of these disturbances, however, likely represents disruptions to wider functional connectivity across larger complex brain networks (Bullmore & Sporns, 2009), required for the prediction and processing of action-related outcomes (Ersche et al., 2020, Tanaka et al., 2008, Valentin et al., 2007). Whole brain connectivity disturbances during other cognitive processes have previously been reported in patients with stimulant use disorder (Nestor and Ersche, 2023, Nestor et al., 2023a, Nestor et al., 2023b), that highlight the prevalence of latent disruptions in neural processing at a network level. Similarly, disruptions in widespread functional connectivity between multiple distributed brain regions may also underpin impaired value-based choices in stimulant use disorder patients.

Patients with stimulant use disorder show impairments on tasks that involve making value-based choices (Gonzalez et al., 2007, Rogers et al., 1999, Stout et al., 2004, Verdejo-Garcia et al., 2007), but these tasks are frequently confounded by other factors that may account for performance deficits and associated disturbances in neural processing. These involve factors such as impulsivity (Ersche et al., 2010a, Ersche et al., 2010b, Leeman and Potenza, 2012), risk-taking (Chen et al., 2020, Guttman et al., 2023), and choice inconsistency (Stout et al., 2004), that do not provide a balanced account of how stimulant use disorder patients learn and act on feedback due to their choices. Reinforcement learning tasks may provide an integrative and quantitative account of how the past consequences of actions are able to guide future value-based choices (Sutton & Barto, 1998). Making value-based choices involves a number of processes, such as prediction and action selection (Niv, 2009), and the processing of outcome feedback, all of which are thought to involve dopamine signalling (Bayer and Glimcher, 2005, Montague et al., 2004, Pagnoni et al., 2002, Sands et al., 2023). There is now evidence that patients with stimulant use disorder also show impaired value-based choices under conditions of reinforcement learning (Ersche et al., 2016, Lim et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2019). These findings in stimulant use disorder patients, however, remain largely isolated, with no knowledge of which brain networks underpin impairments in decision-making. Brain regions are organised into networks that mediate different behaviours, with dysfunctional connections between regions likely to underlie addictive disorders (Ersche et al., 2020). Therefore, examining functional connectivity may reveal distinct and shared disturbances across brain networks during loss and reward value-based choices and their associated outcomes in patients with stimulant use disorder. Importantly, this approach extends beyond the constraints imposed by simple voxel-wise analyses, which can only identify localised disturbances at single brain regions, as opposed to capturing disruptions in functional connectivity at a network level that more closely represent circuit dysfunction.

Using a reinforcement learning task (O'Doherty et al., 2004), which has been effectively used to probe financial choices in stimulant use disorder (Lim et al., 2021), we investigated functional connectivity underlying financial choices in stimulant use disorder patients. As optimal value-based choices are associated with optimal dopamine signalling (Ersche et al., 2011a, Ghahremani et al., 2011), we examined brain networks by modulating dopamine signalling with two drugs that selectively target dopamine D2 receptors: the dopamine receptor antagonist amisulpride and the dopamine receptor agonist pramipexole. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging we hypothesise that (1) compared with healthy control participants, patients with stimulant use disorder show disturbances in brain connectivity when making loss and reward values-based choices, that are associated with the duration of stimulant drug use, and (2) that these disturbances are differentially modulated by amisulpride and pramipexole.

Methods and Materials

Participants

Thirty-six participants were recruited for this study through either local community advertisements or a GlaxoSmithKline volunteer panel. Half the volunteers were non-treatment-seeking patients with stimulant use disorder (subsequently referred to as SUD, mean age 34.5 ± 1.7 years; 83 % male), with the other half control participants (mean age ± SEM, 32.7 ± 1.6 years; 83 % male). Unrelated data from this sample have been published elsewhere (Ersche et al., 2010a, Ersche et al., 2010b, Ersche et al., 2011b, Lim et al., 2021). All participants underwent a clinical interview to determine the clinical diagnosis of stimulant drug dependence (amphetamine or cocaine) using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-TR Axis I Disorders (First et al., 2002). Data were also collected on participants' personal history of substance use, physical health (including signs of acute intoxication and withdrawal), and mental health. Exclusionary criteria that applied to all participants included a lifetime history of a psychotic, neurological, or neurodevelopment disorder, and traumatic head injury. Control participants were also excluded if they had a personal history of drug and alcohol abuse (except nicotine) and a positive drug urine screen prior to each study visit. Patients with SUD were active users of stimulant drugs with moderate to severe levels of compulsive using patterns, as reflected by the Obsessive-Compulsive Drug Use Scale (mean ± SEM: 26.5 ± 1.9) and a positive urine screen for amphetamines or cocaine. All patients with SUD met DSM-IV criteria dependence for amphetamines (44 %) or cocaine (56 %), 17 % of whom reported intravenous use. Some of the SUD patients also met the diagnostic criteria for cannabis (11 %) or alcohol dependence (28 %). The study was approved by the National Research Ethics Committee (REC06/Q0108/130). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrolment and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Drug administration procedure

The study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo controlled, crossover design. (see supplementary section for further details).

Experimental paradigm

All participants performed a probabilistic reinforcement learning task whilst undergoing MRI scanning, which has previously been reported (Lim et al., 2021, Murray et al., 2019). The task required participants to make choices to maximize winning and minimize losing money (Fig. 1). On each trial, one of three pairs of abstract stimuli was randomly presented: reward trials (win 50 pence), loss trials (lose 50 pence), or neutral trials. There were a total of 40 trials for each trial type. For each trial type, participants were required to choose between the pairs of abstract stimuli with a simple button response. For reward trials, the selection of one of the stimulus pair provided a 70 % probability of a financial reward, and a 30 % probability of a no-change, whereas the selection of the other stimulus provided a 30 % probability of a financial reward, and 70 % of no-change. For loss trials, the selection of one of the stimulus pair provided a 70 % probability of no financial change, and a 30 % probability of financial loss, whereas the selection of the other stimulus provided a 30 % probability of no financial change, and 70 % of financial loss. The selection of either stimulus on the neutral trials led to no change in outcome. Participants received outcome feedback after each trial. We report on how participants learned to make optimal choices to gain rewards and avoid loss – i.e. where they learned to choose the 70 % probability stimulus between each pairing that led to gaining or avoiding losing money. Therefore, the main dependent variable was the number of optimal choices on financial gain and loss trials. To assess how much participants valued 50 pence, we asked them to rate on a visual analogue scale how likely they were to pick up 50 pence off the floor (always—never). Participants were informed that they would receive all the money they won during the task.

Upon stimulus cue presentation, participants have a maximum of 2 s to make a choice. The chosen stimulus is circled in red and presented for 3 s. Outcome feedback or no feedback is presented for 1 s, which is followed by a fixation cross (0.5––2 s).

Functional magnetic resonance imaging data acquisition

Whole-brain MRI data were acquired at the Wolfson Brain Imaging Centre, University of Cambridge, Cambridge, UK, using a Siemens Magnetom Tim Trio whole-body scanner operating at 3 T (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). During the performance of the learning task, 32 transaxial sections of gradient echo, echoplanar imaging (EPI) data depicting blood oxygen level–dependent contrast were acquired parallel to the intercommissural line with the following parameters: repetition time = 2000 ms, echo time = 30 ms, flip angle = 78°, slice thickness = 3 mm plus 0.75-mm interslice gap, image matrix size = 64 X 64, and within-plane voxel dimensions = 3.0 mm X 3.0 mm. Prior to data analysis, the first 5 echoplanar images were discarded to account for T1 equilibration effects. Therefore, 404 volumes were collected per visit for analysis. A 3D MPRAGE structural T1 scan was also acquired on each participant to facilitate standard space mapping.

Functional MRI analyses

Data pre-processing was conducted using FEAT from the FMRIB Software Library (https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). Pre-statistical processing was as follows: motion correction utilizing FMRIB’s Linear Image Registration Tool (MCFLIRT), non–brain matter removal using BET (Brain Extraction Tool), spatial smoothing with a 5-mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel, mean-based intensity normalization, and nonlinear high-pass temporal filtering (Gaussian-weighted least-squares straight line fit, with sigma = 25.0 s). The six rigid body movement parameters were also included as regressors in the model.

For each participant, each individual reward value-based choice, reward outcome (collapsing ‘Win 50p’ and ‘No feedback given’), loss value-based choice, and loss outcome (collapsing ‘Lose 50p’ and ‘No feedback given’) trial was separately modelled within the context of the general linear model (stick functions for trial period regressors were convolved with the hemodynamic response function). The neutral trials of the task served as the implicit baseline. This analysis approach yielded unique voxel-wise beta value images, with each image reflecting the magnitude of the hemodynamic response evoked by each trial type of the task. Each image for each trial type was then subsequently registered into standard (MNI avg152 template) space before being concatenated to generate a beta value “trial-wise” (e.g., reward value-based choice) time series. This trial-wise time series method has previously been used to examine connectivity during cognitive tasks in various clinical populations (Fornito et al., 2011, Nestor et al., 2020, Nestor and Ersche, 2022, Nestor et al., 2023a, Nestor et al., 2023b, Nestor et al., 2020, Ray et al., 2017, Verdejo-Roman et al., 2017).

Time series extraction and correlation matrices

Using the Brainnetome Atlas (210 cortical and 36 subcortical nodes) as our connectome of interest (Fan et al., 2016), we extracted the mean beta value from each of the 246 anatomical regions of interest (ROI) for the reward value-based choice, reward outcome, loss value-based choice, and loss outcome timeseries for each participant. Spearman's Rho correlation coefficient analyses were then conducted on these ROI time series outputs to construct whole-brain ROI-to-ROI pairwise matrices in MATLAB version 2021b (The MathWorks, Inc.).

Functional connectivity

Analyses of ROI-to-ROI connectivity across matrices for the reward value-based choice, reward outcome, loss value-based choice, and loss outcome conditions were assessed using the Network-Based Statistics (NBS) Toolbox (Zalesky et al., 2010) in MATLAB. These analyses employed models to examine group effects, treatment effects, and group-by-treatment interactions to identify graph subcomponents of regions across the connectome of interest. Graph subcomponents were identified among the connections using a statistic threshold of t > 3.1. From here, a family-wise error-corrected p value (<0.05) was calculated for the size of each resulting component using permutation testing (5000 permutations). All analyses were conducted while controlling for subjective alertness (see Table 1 – demographic results below).

Network visualization

Brain connectivity maps and circular connectograms were generated using the NeuroMArVL software (https://immersive.erc.monash.edu/neuromarvl/).

Graph theory measures

We additionally extracted the graph theory measure of strength from the resulting group, treatment and group-by-treatment networks that emerged from the functional connectivity analyses. Connectivity strength of a network is the sum of weights (correlation coefficient values) between each pair of nodes across the network. This was done on each participant’s matrix using the GraphVar (https://www.rfmri.org/GraphVar) toolbox for functional brain connectivity (Kruschwitz et al., 2015). This was done to conduct follow-up post-hoc pairwise comparisons to verify the direction of treatment effects, and group-by-treatment interactions that emerged from the functional connectivity analyses, and to perform correlation analyses in the SUD group (see Other Statistics below).

Other statistics

Demographic data were compared using simple unpaired t-tests. For optimal choice on the reinforcement learning task, two (group: control versus SUD) by three (treatment: placebo versus amisulpride versus pramipexole) generalized linear mixed effects models were used to analyse performance on a trial-by-trial basis. Models were constructed separately for the loss and reward trials, with trial number as a fixed effect, participant as a random effect, and optimal choice (optimal = 1, not optimal = 0) as the dependent variable. Wald chi-square statistics were used to determine the main effects of drug and drug-by-group interactions. All these analyses were conducted using permutation testing. Group comparisons for cigarette smoking status were compared using Fisher’s exact tests. Non-parametric Spearman's Rho correlation coefficient analyses were conducted to examine associations between stimulant drug use duration and network strength in SUD patients, but only on those measures where significant group differences emerged. All analyses were conducted using the R statistical software package version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Demographics

Table 1 shows demographic and drug use information for both groups. Participants were well-matched on gender, verbal IQ, and their subjective value of financial reward. Information about drug treatment order and plasma drug levels can be found in the supplementary section. Consistent with other studies, our SUD patients reported higher levels of dysphoric mood and smoked more cigarettes per day than control participants. As SUD patients reported lower levels of alertness on a visual-analogue-scale on all three visits, we included subjective alertness as a co-variate in all analyses.

Experimental paradigm

All participants improved their performance as they completed more trials; this was observed in both reward (Fig. 2A) learning (main effect of trials: Wald χ2(1) = 6.36, p = 0.012) and loss (Fig. 2B) learning (main effect of trials: Wald χ2(1) = 7.76, p = 0.005). Analysis of the reward trials revealed an overall effect of drug (Wald χ2(2) = 48, p < 0.001), such that pramipexole reduced the optimal choices made compared to placebo (Z = − 5.29, p < 0.001). However, there were no group (Wald χ2(1) = 0.25, p = 0.617) or group-by-drug interaction effects (Wald χ2(2) = 5.75, p = 0.056). By contrast, analysis of the loss trials revealed an overall effect of group, such that SUD patients had lower rates of optimal choices (Wald χ2(1) = 16.4, p < 0.001) compared to control participants. There was also a group-by-drug interaction (Wald χ2(2) = 13.2, p = 0.001), such that SUD patients improved on pramipexole compared to placebo (Z = 2.16, p = 0.031), but not compared to amisulpride (Z = − 1.57, p = 0.116). These results are in line with prior computational analyses of reward and loss reinforcement learning in SUD patients and control participants, and the effects of dopaminergic modulation (Lim et al., 2021).

Functional connectivity

Information about movement during scanning, drug treatment effects, and interactions, can be found in the supplementary section. SUD patients had less connectivity strength compared to the control group across four separate networks (p < 0.01). These connectivity differences were for the reward value-based choice, reward value-based outcome, loss value-based choice, and loss value-based outcome conditions. While there was some minimal overlap in connectivity between the value-based choice and value-based outcome phases of each condition (see supplementary section), the vast majority of connectivity was exclusive to those networks where SUD patients showed reduced connectivity compared to control participants. The reward value-based choice network comprised 97 connections involving 58 regions (Fig. 3A). Connections were mostly right intra-hemispheric (45 %) or interhemispheric (45 %), with the remaining connectivity confined to the left hemisphere (10 %). The highest number of connections were between the temporal and subcortical modules (35 %). The reward outcome network consisted of 108 connections over 74 regions (Fig. 3B) that showed an even distribution of connectivity, involving left intra-hemispheric (31 %), right intra-hemispheric (30 %), and interhemispheric (39 %) connections. Over a quarter (28 %) of the connections were between the temporal and limbic modules.

Group effect results for (A) reward value-based choice, and (B) reward outcome, where the SUD patient group showed less connectivity across treatments compared to control participants. Connectivity is represented by a brain connectivity map and a circular connectogram.

The loss-related choice network comprised 139 connections involving 71 regions (Fig. 4A). Most connections were interhemispheric (45 %), with the remaining connections contained within the left (21 %) and right hemispheres (34 %). Regions of the subcortical module had the highest number of connections (81 %). A similar picture emerged with respect to network connectivity associated with loss outcomes. SUD patients showed significantly reduced connectivity strength compared with control participants (p < 0.01). This network was made up of 156 connections involving 88 regions (Fig. 4B). Most connections were interhemispheric (45 %), with the remaining connectivity within the left (34 %) and right (21 %) hemispheres. The subcortical module had the highest number of connections (53 %).

Correlations

We found that the duration of stimulant drug use was significantly associated with connectivity strength across the reward value-based choice network (r17 = -0.43, p < 0.05), as well as the reward outcome (r17 = -0.63, p < 0.01 − Fig. 5A) and loss outcome (r17 = -0.6, p < 0.01 − Fig. 5B) networks.

Discussion

This study reveals that brain network connectivity underlying the processing of value-based choices and outcomes is disrupted in patients with SUD. Importantly, reductions in connectivity strength across these networks were, in part, associated with the duration of stimulant use, suggesting a putative influence of drug use on neural processing. These novel findings may provide important insights into the underlying neural basis of impaired value-based choices and outcomes in patients with SUD.

Disruptions to connectivity during reward and loss value-based choices in SUD patients

SUD patients showed reduced functional connectivity compared to the control participants across the different drug treatments when making value-based choices for rewards. This involved less connectivity across regions of the frontal-striatal network (e.g., ventral putamen, dorsolateral prefrontal, lateral orbitofrontal, and anterior cingulate cortices), which are known to be involved in the motivational processes (Kouneiher et al., 2009, Pauli et al., 2016), that support goal-directed behaviour (Tanaka et al., 2008, Valentin et al., 2007). Patients with addictive disorders have shown blunted localised brain responses to stimuli that predict rewards (Bustamante et al., 2014, Luijten et al., 2017, Nestor et al., 2017, Patel et al., 2013), with the current findings supporting previous reports of functional connectivity disturbances (Nestor and Ersche, 2023, Nestor et al., 2020). The current findings additionally identify brain network uncoupling during value-based choices for rewards. This result, however, does not suggest that reductions in connectivity across this network are sufficient to impact the value of monetary reward as a salient reinforcer, given that there were no group differences in making value-based reward choices. This suggests that these network disturbances can be overcome when providing suitable reward choices to patients with SUD.

SUD patients were less likely to avoid financial loss compared with control participants, consistent with previous studies (Ersche et al., 2016, Lim et al., 2021). This was accompanied by a significant reduction in connectivity that involved regions of the subcortical module (amygdala, ventral caudate, ventral striatum) and their connectivity with regions of the frontal and temporal systems. There is empirical evidence for impairments in decision-making under conditions of financial loss (Bechara and Damasio, 2002, Ersche et al., 2016, Lim et al., 2021), as well as blunted responsivity across frontal-striatal regions during loss anticipation (Bischoff-Grethe et al., 2017, Mortazavi et al., 2024, Patel et al., 2013, Wilhelm et al., 2023) in addictive disorders. One possible explanation for both the behavioural and connectivity differences is that SUD patients may be less motivated to avoid losses (McNally et al., 2023). While connectivity underlying reward value-based choices was also reduced in SUD patients, they may have been able to compensate for this disruption due to their motivation to obtain reward. Indeed, reward-based learning is used in clinical practice (Stitzer & Petry, 2006) to promote abstinence (e.g., reward forms the basis of contingency management). Furthermore, there appears to be less connectivity involving a more diffuse network of regions during loss value-based choices, possibly suggesting that behavioural impairments were underpinned by these disruptions in neural processing. These findings may further highlight the ineffectiveness of punitive approaches to shaping behaviour in treating SUD, that are in contrast to using more salient rewards, such as financial gain (Festinger et al., 2014, Petry et al., 2007, Stoops et al., 2010, Vandrey et al., 2007).

Disruptions to connectivity in response to loss and reward outcomes in SUD patients

The current task involved the trial-by-trial monitoring of outcomes using feedback that enabled participants to adjust ongoing appetitive and avoidance choices. SUD patients also had reduced connectivity in response to reward and loss outcomes across the drug treatments compared with control participants. These reductions in connectivity during the processing of outcomes may be consistent with impairments in outcome prediction error signalling, as previously reported in addictive disorders (Chiu et al., 2008, Kanen et al., 2019, Lim et al., 2019, Park et al., 2010). Reductions in connectivity reward and loss processing in SUD patients involved regions known to encode positive and negative outcome prediction errors, such as regions of the prefrontal cortex and striatum (Chase et al., 2015, Corlett et al., 2022, Nobre et al., 1999, Tobler et al., 2006). This reduction in connectivity may represent non-specific impairments to the monitoring and updating of outcomes, commonly reported in addictive disorders (Carey et al., 2015, Forman et al., 2004, Franken et al., 2007, Hester et al., 2009, Hester et al., 2007, London et al., 2005), and which concurs with previous findings in SUD patients (Nestor & Ersche, 2023). Importantly, these reductions in connectivity may be due to chronic stimulant drug use, as we observed correlations between the reported duration of use and network efficiency, similar to that previously reported (Nestor & Ersche, 2023). The current results are, however, an important extension − they reveal that network disturbances during the processing of outcomes could be due to increasing levels of stimulant drug exposure. The reduced functional connectivity during outcome processing may explain why learning performance was suboptimal in SUD patients. Overall, these results represent a blunted sensitivity during the processing of appetitive and avoidance outcomes. These findings may inform us about some of the more selective disruptions during loss avoidance choices in SUD, that may be underpinned by disturbances to functional brain connectivity.

Possible mechanism

The current study reports that SUD patients show a distinct uncoupling of neural systems during reward and loss learning. These results could have implications for how patients respond to treatments that modulate dopamine transmission across neural systems when learning about reward and loss in real world settings. Despite previous evidence for disruptions to dopamine transmission in stimulant addiction (Ashok et al., 2017, Volkow et al., 1997, Volkow et al., 1996, Wang et al., 2012), our findings may suggest that SUD patients do have an adequately responsive dopamine system for the processing of rewards, given their learning performance and patterns of dysconnectivity during this condition. While there was evidence for dysconnectivity between striatal and parietal/temporal regions, SUD patients showed most dysconnectivity between thalamic and inferior temporal regions during reward value-based choices. These disruptions in connectivity during reward choice may suggest that deficits in SUD patients are not involved in reward learning because performance is relatively intact. While there was evidence for striatal-frontal dysconnectivity during reward outcome processing, the most widespread breakdown in connectivity was between limbic and parietal regions. The implication here, therefore, is that these deficits in connectivity during reward choice and outcome processing are unlikely to respond to the modulation of dopamine transmission with drugs because they do not sufficiently encompass dopamine circuitry.

There is evidence, in addition to the current findings, that SUD patients do not adjust their behaviour during loss avoidance learning (Ersche et al., 2016, Lim et al., 2021). This may suggest that, in contrast to reward processing, dopamine systems in SUD patients do not adequately respond to the processing of losses. The implication here is that a failure to process the negative consequences of actions is more likely to incur serious health and economic costs to the individual. Possibly in support of this, the current study showed that SUD patients had a considerable breakdown in striatal-thalamic and striatal-frontal connectivity when making loss choices. Information about losses is also encoded by the striatal dopaminergic system (Montague et al., 2016, Sands et al., 2023), with loss learning impairments in SUD patients possibly reflecting a disruption in connectivity involving these neural systems. There was also a prominent loss of connectivity between striatal and parietal regions during loss outcomes. Furthermore, we observed a shared uncoupling of ventral striatal regions during the loss choice and outcome phases of the task, that involved a reduction in connectivity with thalamic, frontal, and parietal regions. This may suggest that there is a disruption to neural resources that enable the ventral striatum to integrate information specifically about losses, as opposed to a non-specific deficit in striatal dopamine responsivity that also involves rewards. The current findings, however, would appear to undermine the scientific rationale for using medicines to acutely modulate dopamine transmission to improve loss avoidance learning in SUD patients, despite patterns of dysconnectivity clearly containing dopamine circuitry. The current findings around loss avoidance learning and associated disturbances in connectivity may point to the need for multiple dosing with dopaminergic drugs, or alternatively, novel interventions that use other forms of neuromodulation (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation) to treat addictive disorder patient populations (Mehta et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

There were limitations in the current study that included not being able to match groups on cigarette smoking and mood, although such differences are likely to reflect typical clinical and behavioural characteristics of SUD patients (Ersche et al., 2012, Weinberger and Sofuoglu, 2009). We cannot, however, dismiss their potential influence on the reported findings. We also recruited non-abstinent SUD patients, all of whom tested positive for either amphetamines or cocaine at all study visits. SUD patients, conceivably, could have been in various states of craving and/or withdrawal at the time of testing, which were not assessed. Some of the SUD patients additionally demonstrated alcohol and cannabis dependence. While this study was exploratory in nature, the small sample size in each group makes it difficult to generalise these findings to the wider SUD population. The small sample size, therefore, may have reduced the likelihood that the statistically significant results reported reflect a true effect (Button et al., 2013).This study was, however, a well-balanced randomized, double-blind, placebo controlled, crossover design, that used standard therapeutic doses of amisulpride and pramipexole. We recruited non-treatment seeking SUD patients with less psychiatric comorbidity than that typically seen in SUD patients receiving treatment. The findings further corroborate evidence for impairments in loss, as opposed to reward, value-based choices in SUD patients. We also took a novel analytical approach to assessing neural processing underlying value-based choices, that revealed widespread latent disruptions in connectivity strength across a network of brain regions, that were associated with the duration of stimulant drug use.

Conclusion

This study reports that non-treatment-seeking SUD patients showed widespread reductions in brain network connectivity during loss and reward value-based choice behaviour and their associated outcomes. Furthermore, these reductions in connectivity were not modulated by drug treatments that target dopamine D2 receptor functioning, suggesting that remediating brain functions cannot be achieved through pharmacological agents alone. Reduced connectivity strength across these networks in the SUD patients were negatively associated with the duration of reported stimulant drug use. In the absence of approved pharmacological treatments for stimulant use disorder, interventions that attempt to shape changes in behaviour may also need to rely on psychosocial and cognitive approaches. These may involve helping stimulant drug users to make more informed choices that guide their behaviour and to learn about the outcomes of their actions.
