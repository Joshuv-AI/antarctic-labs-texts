Global and Chinese trends in disease burden of interstitial lung disease and pulmonary sarcoidosis from 1990 to 2021 with projections to 2040: a COVID-19 impact analysis based on the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Si-Si Liu, Zhang-Yi Dai, Fu-Qiang Wang, Jia-Jun Cheng, Yun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the growing recognition of interstitial lung disease (ILD), updated estimates of its global burden that account for the disruptions of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic are currently unavailable. This study updates the global and Chinese ILD burden using Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 data, analyzing trend deviations during the COVID-19 period and examining disparities between high- and low-resource settings.MethodsWe used data from the GBD 2021 study to analyze the global and Chinese burden of ILD. Joinpoint regression analyzed temporal trends in age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), prevalence rate (ASPR), mortality rate (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life years rate (ASDR). Age-period-cohort (APC) analysis and autoregressive integrated moving average projection model was also used.ResultsGlobally, ILD and sarcoidosis caused 390,267 incident cases and 188,222 deaths in 2021, with males bearing over half the disease burden. From 1990 to 2021, ASIR increased from 3.77 to 4.54, ASPR from 45.99 to 50.01, ASMR from 1.52 to 2.28, and ASDR from 37.15 to 47.62 (all per 100,000 individuals). These age-standardized rates (ASRs) demonstrated distinct temporal patterns, with an initial increase from 1990 to 2019, followed by a decline from 2019 to 2021. In China, specifically, the ASRs exhibited a more complex pattern: an initial decrease, followed by an increase and subsequent decrease, ultimately showing an upward trend over the 1990–2021 period. APC analysis indicated a positive correlation between disease burden and age. Future projections indicate a declining trend across all metrics by 2040, with consistently higher rates observed in males compared to females.ConclusionsDue to the impact of COVID-19, the global and Chinese burden of ILD and sarcoidosis showed a decline during 2019–2021, interrupting its previous upward trend. The disease burden was particularly severe among economically disadvantaged regions, elderly populations and males. While projections indicate a potential stabilization or decline in ASRs through 2040, these trends must be interpreted with caution due to potential confounding effects of pandemic-related diagnostic disruptions. Substantial regional disparities and increasing burdens in specific age groups demand targeted interventions.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Global age-standardized rates for interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary sarcoidosis increased from 1990 to 2019 but declined from 2019 to 2021, attributed to coronavirus disease 2019 disruptions.

• The burden is higher in males, older populations, and high-Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions, with the latter likely reflecting greater diagnostic capacity.

• Trend patterns in China were more complex and distinct from the global average.

• Future projections to 2040 suggest a potential stabilization or decline in rates, yet significant demographic and geographical inequities are expected to persist.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior studies established a rising global incidence and identified disparities in ILD burden.

• This analysis documents the pandemic-related reversal of upward trends (2019–2021), provides novel 2040 projections, and critically frames regional disparities as indicators of diagnostic inequity.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The impact of healthcare disruptions on chronic disease surveillance necessitates building resilient health systems.

• Public health strategies must be targeted: enhancing early management in high-SDI regions and building diagnostic capacity in low-SDI regions to address underdiagnosis.

• Resource allocation should prioritize high-risk groups (e.g., elderly males) and plan for equitable, long-term management in an aging population.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary sarcoidosis represent a heterogeneous group of over 200 respiratory disorders characterized by inflammation and progressive fibrosis of the lung parenchyma (1-3). Despite advancements in classification standards by the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society (4,5), ILD remains a formidable clinical challenge (6). Unlike chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), which has seen a global decrease in age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) over recent decades due to effective tobacco control and management strategies (7), the burden of ILD has shown a concerning upward trajectory or stagnation in improvement. This divergence highlights a critical “Public Health Significance”: as the global population ages, ILD is becoming a leading cause of respiratory failure, imposing a growing strain on healthcare systems that were previously designed to manage obstructive rather than restrictive lung diseases.

Several comprehensive analyses based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2017 and 2019 datasets have laid the groundwork for understanding these trends (8-11). Studies by Ma et al. (9) and Zeng et al. (8) identified increasing incidence globally, while Chen et al. (12) highlighted complex mortality shifts in China, with rising absolute numbers despite fluctuating standardized rates. However, these analyses concluded in 2019, leaving a significant knowledge gap regarding the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2020–2021)—a period defined by unprecedented respiratory health crises, diagnostic delays, and potential viral triggers for fibrotic lung changes. Furthermore, recent GBD 2021-based work by Xie et al. (10) focused exclusively on China, leaving the global context of the pandemic’s impact on ILD unexamined.

Crucially, interpreting global ILD trends requires viewing data through the lens of Global Health Inequity. In GBD epidemiology, the reported “incidence” of ILD is often a proxy for “diagnostic capacity” rather than a purely biological phenomenon. The disparity in burden between high-Socio-demographic Index (SDI) and low-SDI regions is likely driven by the availability of high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and multidisciplinary team (MDT) infrastructure (6,13). In High-SDI regions, widespread screening detects early-stage disease, artificially inflating incidence while potentially lowering mortality through early intervention (13,14). Conversely, in Low-SDI regions, ILD often remains underdiagnosed until advanced stages, masking the true disease burden. Ignoring this distinction risks misinterpreting lower reported incidence in developing regions as “lower risk”, when it essentially represents “unmet medical need”.

The newly released GBD 2021 dataset offers a unique opportunity to address these gaps (15,16). It incorporates data spanning the COVID-19 pandemic, accounts for the transition in coding practices (ICD-11 adaptations), and updates demographic shifts (15). This study utilizes GBD 2021 to: (I) update the global and Chinese trends in ILD burden, explicitly isolating the impact of the COVID-19 period; (II) critically assess regional disparities as reflections of diagnostic inequity rather than just biological susceptibility; and (III) project future trends to 2040 using age-period-cohort (APC) modeling to guide targeted resource allocation in an aging world. We present this article in accordance with the GATHER reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1816/rc).

Methods

Data source and GBD estimation strategy

This study evaluates the global, regional, and national burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis using data from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2021 (15,16). While the GBD 2021 framework integrates over 100,000 data sources generally, the input data specifically for estimating the burden of ILD were primarily derived from vital registration systems (death certificates) for mortality estimates and hospital discharge records or insurance claims data for non-fatal outcomes (prevalence and incidence) (17). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

It is crucial to acknowledge the heterogeneity in data availability across regions and time periods. For high-income countries, high-quality registry and administrative data were readily available. However, for many developing nations (low-SDI regions) and during the early years of the study period (the 1990s), actual registry data for ILD were often sparse or non-existent. To address these gaps, the GBD methodology utilized predictive covariates with strong epidemiological associations with respiratory disease (e.g., smoking prevalence, air pollution levels, SDI) to infer estimates. Mortality was modeled using the Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm), while incidence and prevalence were estimated using DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool that borrows strength from regions with high-quality data through spatiotemporal smoothing. Consequently, estimates for developing regions represent modeled approximations rather than raw observations and should be interpreted with awareness of the inherent uncertainty, as reflected in the 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).

Case definition

ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis were classified as chronic respiratory diseases marked by reduced lung function and oxygen absorption due to inflammation or scarring. The 2021 GBD study incorporated the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-11, which introduced new diagnostic codes (e.g., CB03 for idiopathic interstitial pneumonia, CB05 for systemic disease-related ILD) (9,15,18). This improved classification allows better differentiation of conditions like acute interstitial pneumonia and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, but excludes ILDs caused by external agents or drugs (Content 1 in Appendix 1).

Statistical analysis

We employed rigorous statistical models to characterize secular trends and identify underlying drivers of disease burden. All analyses were performed using Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0) and R software (version 4.4.2).

Trend analysis (joinpoint regression)

To move beyond simple linear descriptions of change, we utilized joinpoint regression analysis to examine trends in age-standardized rates (ASRs) [(age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized disability-adjusted life years rate (ASDR)]. The principle of this method is to identify statistically significant inflection points (joinpoints) in temporal trends, which serve as markers for potential changes in underlying risk factors, diagnostic criteria, or public health interventions. The annual percent change was calculated for each segment, and the average annual percent change (AAPC) summarized the overall trend.

APC analysis

An APC model was constructed to disentangle the confounding effects of three distinct temporal dimensions on ILD incidence and mortality: (I) age effects: reflecting biological aging and physiological changes associated with lung fibrosis risk; (II) period effects: Reflecting immediate historical events, advancements in diagnostic technology (e.g., widespread use of HRCT), or treatment interventions affecting all age groups simultaneously; (III) cohort effects: reflecting generational differences in lifestyle, environmental exposures (e.g., smoking habits in different birth generations), or early-life conditions. Net drift (overall trend) and local drift (age-specific trend) were calculated. Parameters were estimated using the Wald χ2 test, and the estimable functions approach was applied to address the identification problem (collinearity) inherent in APC models.

Autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) forecasting

An ARIMA model (19) projected global and China-specific trends in ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR by sex through 2040, incorporating data from both the 2021 and 2019 GBD studies to account for COVID-19’s impact. Details on the ARIMA model are provided in the supplement material (Content 2 in Appendix 1).

Methodological limitations

It is important to note that since our analyses are applied to GBD modeled estimates rather than raw observational registry data, the detected trends—particularly in data-sparse regions—may partly reflect smoothing assumptions in the GBD modeling process. Caution is exercised to avoid over-interpreting minor fluctuations as biological phenomena.

Results

Global overview

In 2021, there were an estimated 390,267 (95% UI: 346,393–433,403) incident cases, 4,306,628 (95% UI: 3,802,951–4,898,714) prevalent cases, 188,222 (95% UI: 161,406–212,252) deaths, and 4,042,150 (95% UI: 3,489,795–4,516,883) disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis globally. Men accounted for over half of this burden (details in Table S1). ILDs ranked as the third most common chronic respiratory disease worldwide, following asthma and COPD, with an ASIR of 4.54 and an ASPR of 50.01 per 100,000. Similarly, ILDs were the third leading cause of respiratory mortality and DALYs, after COPD and asthma, with an ASMR of 2.28 and an ASDR of 47.62 per 100,000 (Table 1).

Chinese context

China accounted for 12.4% of global incident cases (48,514; 95% UI: 41,541–55,949), 14.6% of prevalent cases (628,383; 95% UI: 534,993–737,822), 4.1% of deaths (7,674; 95% UI: 4,637–10,371), and 5.5% of DALYs (222,288; 95% UI: 158,551–288,301) related to these diseases (Table S1). While incidence and prevalence patterns in China mirrored global trends, ILDs ranked fourth for ASMR and fifth for ASDR (Table 1).

Temporal trends (1990–2021)

Globally, both incidence and prevalence of ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis steadily increased from 1990 to 2021 (Figure 1). ASIR and ASPR exhibited a phased pattern: gradual increase (1990–2006), sharp increase (2006–2009), plateau (2009–2012), and modest decline (2012–2021) (Figure S1A,S1B). Despite the decline, ASIR and ASPR in 2021 were higher than in 1990 (4.54 and 50.01 vs. 3.77 and 45.99 per 100,000, respectively; P<0.05 for both AAPCs) (Tables S2,S3). Mortality and DALYs also trended upwards from 1990 to 2021 (Figure 1), with ASMR and ASDR increasing until 2019 followed by a decline through 2021 (P<0.05 for all annual percent changes) (Figure S1C,S1D). By 2021, ASMR and ASDR had risen to 2.28 and 47.62 per 100,000 from 1.52 and 37.15 per 100,000 in 1990 (P<0.05 for both AAPCs) (Tables S4,S5). Age and sex-specific variations in global and Chinese burdens are detailed in Content 3-4 in Appendix 1 and Figures S1-S7.

Regional disparities (2021)

Among the 21 GBD regions, South Asia had the highest absolute burden of ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis in 2021, while Oceania had the lowest (Table S6). Andean Latin America had the highest ASIR (20.47 per 100,000) and ASMR (11.37 per 100,000), as well as the highest ASDR (209.34 per 100,000). Eastern Europe had the lowest ASIR (1.04 per 100,000), Western Sub-Saharan Africa the lowest ASPR (12.79 per 100,000), and Southeast Asia the lowest ASMR (0.33 per 100,000) and ASDR (8.93 per 100,000). The High-income Asia Pacific region had the highest ASPR at 151.6 per 100,000 (Table S6).

Regional trends (1990–2021)

Significant regional variations in disease patterns were observed between 1990 and 2021. Australasia experienced the largest increases in ASIR (88% change; AAPC: 2.05, 95% CI: 2.02–2.09), while Andean Latin America had the largest increase in ASPR (85% change; AAPC: 1.88, 95% CI: 1.82–1.93). Conversely, Eastern Europe saw the most substantial declines in both ASIR (−52%; AAPC: −2.21, 95% CI: −2.27, −2.15) and ASPR (−43%; AAPC: −2.01, 95% CI: −2.06, −1.96) (Figure 2 and Tables S2,S3,S6). Most regions showed positive correlations between these rates and SDI, with exceptions in Eastern Europe and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (Figure 3A,3B). In 2021, significant positive correlations were observed between SDI and both ASIR (R=0.38, P=0.06) and ASPR (R=0.53, P=0.01). Australasia also had the largest increases in ASMR (180%; AAPC: 3.32, 95% CI: 2.64–4.00) and ASDR (149%; AAPC: 2.89, 95% CI: 2.11–3.67), while Eastern Europe continued to exhibit declining trends (Figure 2 and Tables S4-S6). Most regions showed positive correlations between these rates and SDI, except for Eastern Europe, Central Asia, and Southern Sub-Saharan Africa (Figure 3C,3D). However, in 2021, no significant correlations were found between SDI and either ASMR (R=0.16, P=0.50) or ASDR (R=0.11, P=0.62). APC effect analysis

Age effect

The incidence of ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis is low in individuals aged 0–14 but increases with age (Figure S15). Content 9 in Appendix 1 and Tables S10,S11 details net and local drifts in incidence and mortality. Age-adjusted incidence rate ratios (RRs) increase with age, with females exhibiting higher RRs than males and the overall population after age 35 (Figure S16). Mortality follows a √-shaped pattern across age groups, decreasing initially in those aged 0–10 and subsequently increasing after age 10 (Figure S15). Age-adjusted mortality RRs increase with age, exceeding 1.0 after ages 30–34. Females demonstrate higher mortality RRs than males and the overall population after age 45 (Figure S16).

Period effect

Between 1990 and 2021, incidence rates increased steadily in individuals aged 65 and older, with minimal changes in other age groups (Figure S15). Globally, period-adjusted incidence RRs fluctuated, peaking in 2012–2016 (RR =1.046, 95% CI: 0.803–1.363) before decreasing through 2021 (Figure S16). This initial increase followed by decline was observed in both sexes. Female incidence RRs, initially lower than males and the overall population, increased at a greater rate after 2002–2006, surpassing the reference period (RR =1.0). Mortality decreased over time in the 0–9 age groups, increased in those aged 70 and older, and remained stable in other age groups (Figure S15). Period-adjusted mortality RRs gradually increased for the overall population and males (Figure S16). Female mortality RRs peaked in 2012–2016 (RR =1.071, 95% CI: 1.05–1.092) before declining slightly. Similar to incidence, female mortality RRs were initially lower, then increased significantly after 2002–2006, exceeding the reference period (RR =1.0).

Cohort effect

Incidence is lowest in the most recent birth cohorts (Figure S15). Cohort-adjusted incidence RRs fluctuated. For the overall population and males, RRs increased from the 1897–1901 birth cohort, peaking in the 1947–1951 cohort (overall: RR =1.02; male: RR =1.039) before decreasing through the 1987–1991 cohort. From the 1992–1996 cohort onward, RRs fluctuated below 1.0 (using the 1957–1961 cohort as reference). Female incidence RRs increased from the 1897–1901 cohort, fluctuated between the 1947–1951 and 1957–1961 cohorts, and peaked in the 1962–1966 cohort (RR =1.002) (Figure S16). Mortality decreased in more recent birth cohorts, except for those born in 2011–2021 (Figure S15). Cohort-adjusted mortality RRs increased from the 1897–1901 cohort to the 1942–1946 cohort (overall population and males) and to the 1947–1951 cohort (females), then fluctuated, peaking in the 1992–1996 cohort (overall: RR =1.155; male: RR =1.103; female: RR =1.228) before declining (Figure S16).

Analyses of global APC models for prevalence and DALYs, as well as Chinese APC models for the four metrics are provided in Content 10-11 in Appendix 1 and Figures S17-S20.

Projection ILD trends until 2040

To account for the potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, projections of ASIRs, ASPRs, ASMRs, and ASDRs for ILDs and pulmonary sarcoidosis were generated using both 2021 and 2019 GBD study data. Projections based on the 2021 GBD data indicate a consistent decline in all metrics from 2021 to 2040 (Figure S21A-S21D). Specifically, ASIR is projected to decrease from 4.54 (95% CI: 4.05, 5.04) to 3.91 (95% CI: 2.14, 5.68) per 100,000. ASPR is projected to decline from 50.01 (95% CI: 44.24, 56.77) to 47.98 (95% CI: 35.32, 60.63) per 100,000 (Table S12). ASMR is predicted to fall from 2.28 (95% CI: 1.96, 2.56) to 1.91 (95% CI: 0.85, 2.97) per 100,000. ASDR is projected to decrease from 47.62 (95% CI: 41.26, 53.16) to 41.10 (95% CI: 29.19, 53.01) per 100,000 (Table S13). Projected trends for males and females mirror these overall declines, with males consistently exhibiting higher rates (Figure S21A-S21D). In contrast, projections based on the 2019 GBD data show increasing ASMRs and ASDRs from 2019 to 2040. However, the 2019 projections for ASIRs and ASPRs also show a declining trend, consistent with the 2021 GBD predictions (Figure S21E-S21H). Projections for the Chinese disease burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis until 2040 are presented in Content 12 in Appendix 1 and Figure S22.

Discussion

This study examines the global burden of ILD and pulmonary sarcoidosis from 1990 to 2021 using GBD 2021 data, including COVID-19 impact. We analyze trends across regions, demographics, and time periods, with a focus on China and future projections.

Unlike other chronic respiratory diseases (7,20), ILD and sarcoidosis showed increasing global trends in incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs from 1990 to 2019. In China, ILD patterns evolved through distinct phases: from 1990 to 2006, incidence and prevalence rates remained stable or slightly declined (annual percent changes in ASIR and ASPR: −1.35 to 2.06). A significant increase occurred during 2006–2009, with annual percent changes of 7.76, 4.89, 3.18, and 3.16 for ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and ASMR, respectively. Post-2009, while incidence and prevalence stabilized (0< annual percent change <0.4), mortality declined (annual percent change <0). These changes likely reflect improved healthcare access through China’s 2007 urban resident basic medical insurance and the 2013 updated idiopathic interstitial pneumonia diagnostic guidelines (21).

The global increase in ILD and sarcoidosis can be attributed to three primary factors. First, ILD, especially idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, is strongly associated with aging (9,22). According to the 2023 World Social Report (23), the global population aged ≥65 years will be more than double from 761 million (10%) in 2021 to 1.6 billion (16%) by 2050. Our findings show that ILD incidence and prevalence peak in the 65–74 age group and positively correlate with age, suggesting a continued increase in ILD burden with population aging. Second, environmental factors, including air pollution, occupational dust exposure, and smoking behaviors, contribute to the increasing ILD burden (8,9). Third, improved disease detection through advanced medical imaging and artificial intelligence-based machine learning techniques has led to increased identification of ILD cases (4,24).

Impact of COVID-19

From 2019 to 2021, global ILD metrics showed a decline in ASRs (Tables S2-S5 and Figure S1). However, we strongly caution against interpreting this decline as a genuine reduction in disease burden or a success of pandemic mitigation measures. From an epidemiological perspective, a sudden drop in the incidence of a chronic, progressive fibrotic disease is biologically implausible. Instead, this phenomenon likely reflects severe “Surveillance Artifacts” and “Diagnostic Debt”. During lockdowns, non-emergency imaging (HRCT) and lung function testing—essential for ILD diagnosis—were widely postponed or cancelled, leading to significant under-detection of new cases (13). Furthermore, “Competing Mortality” played a critical role; patients with pre-existing ILD were at high risk for severe COVID-19 outcomes. Many deaths among these patients were likely coded as COVID-19 (U07.1) rather than ILD (J84) in the ICD-10 system, artificially lowering ILD-specific mortality rates. Therefore, this statistical “dip” should be viewed as a warning sign of uncounted cases and a potential future “rebound” in advanced-stage diagnoses, rather than a public health victory.

The SDI paradox

Global ILD trends reveal a striking paradox across SDI levels: High-SDI regions reported the highest incidence and prevalence, yet low- and middle-SDI regions bore a disproportionately high burden of mortality and DALYs. We interpret this discrepancy as a marker of profound global health inequity rather than biological differences alone. The high incidence in developed nations likely reflects “Ascertainment Bias” driven by superior healthcare infrastructure, where widespread access to HRCT and screening programs ensures early detection of mild cases (25-27). Conversely, the elevated mortality in lower-SDI regions highlights a critical “Treatment Gap ” (28). In many developing nations, access to guideline-recommended antifibrotic therapies (e.g., nintedanib and pirfenidone) and lung transplantation is severely limited due to cost and availability (27). Consequently, patients in these regions are often diagnosed at later stages and lack access to life-prolonging interventions, resulting in higher case-fatality rates. These findings underscore the urgent need to bridge the gap in respiratory care infrastructure and medication accessibility between the Global North and South.

Among the 21 GBD regions and 204 countries/territories, Andean Latin America, particularly Peru, shows the highest global rates across all metrics. Despite its middle-SDI status, this region’s elevated ILD burden stems from multiple factors: (I) environmental and occupational exposures; (II) limited healthcare resources; (III) socioeconomic challenges and widespread poverty; (IV) inadequate public health infrastructure (25,29). The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated these challenges, particularly affecting Latin America and Peru through severe socioeconomic disruption (17). Addressing these elevated ILD rates requires improving environmental quality, occupational safety, healthcare capacity, and public health systems.

Analysis of ILD shows consistently higher burden in males across all metrics globally, in all SDI regions, and in China. While both sexes show parallel temporal trends from 1990 to 2021, as confirmed by previous studies (8,9), the male predominance can be attributed to: (I) higher male incidence of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, a major ILD subtype with poor prognosis (21,22); (II) greater male exposure to risk factors, including smoking and occupational hazards (30,31).

Age-related analysis demonstrates an elevated RR for ILD across all age-standardized metrics. This confirms ILD as an age-associated condition (9,21). Aging naturally affects lung tissue through mechanisms such as alveolar wall and interstitial thickening, alveolar enlargement, and epithelial cell deterioration (32). These physiological changes compromise lung function, increasing susceptibility to ILD development. The age-related burden of ILD reflects both cumulative and concurrent factors. Lifetime accumulation of environmental exposures increases the risk of lung inflammation and fibrosis (33). Concurrently, the higher prevalence of comorbidities in older adults—including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and COPD—exacerbates ILD-associated mortality and disability (34). Decreased treatment tolerance in older adults further affects disease outcomes (35). Furthermore, genetic predispositions (36), including telomere shortening, surfactant protein gene mutations, and MUC5B gene variants, may interact with environmental factors to contribute to ILD onset with advancing age.

While global ILD incidence and prevalence continued rising after 2017, the RR of ASIR and ASPR has decreased. This decline may reflect several factors: slowing global population growth (32), enhanced tobacco control initiatives (37), improved environmental protection measures (33), and advances in medical technology and public health interventions (33). The stabilization of RRs in ASMR and ASDR further supports these positive impacts.

Birth cohort analysis shows the highest ILD risk among those born between 1947 and 1951 (ages 70–74 during the study period), consistent with previous findings (8,9). Improved diagnostics and updated clinical guidelines (21) have enabled earlier detection in older cohorts (pre-1951), while recent cohorts (post-1951) show the lowest risk. For mortality and DALYs, lowest risk appears in those born after 1996. The higher mortality and DALYs risks in earlier cohorts (pre-1996) reflect limited treatment options and poor prognosis (38), while the introduction of antifibrotic medications has improved survival rates (22,39). Lower risk in post-1996 cohorts also reflects naturally lower disease incidence in younger populations. China’s ILD birth cohort patterns mirror global trends, likely influenced by improved diagnostics, therapeutics, and universal healthcare implementation.

Twenty-year ILD burden forecasts using GBD data show divergent trajectories between pre-pandemic (1990–2019) and post-pandemic (1990–2021) projections. While post-pandemic projections suggest declining trends based on recent data, these models should be interpreted with caution. The projected decline may partly result from the model extrapolating the artificial dip observed during 2020–2021. If the “diagnostic debt” incurred during the pandemic is repaid in the coming years, we may observe a compensatory surge in incidence and mortality that current ARIMA models cannot fully predict. Therefore, continuous surveillance is essential.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the complex nature of ILD, comprising over 200 distinct conditions with varying international definitions, challenges comprehensive burden assessment and may underestimate true disease impact. Second, data acquisition remains limited in certain regions, and while the GBD 2021 study used MR-BRT methodology (15) to address epidemiological data biases, incomplete coverage may affect result accuracy. Third, the current disease classification framework excludes several significant ILD subtypes, including pneumoconiosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and drug-related ILDs. Future GBD studies should incorporate these categories to provide more comprehensive burden estimates.

Conclusions

This analysis highlights both challenges and opportunities in addressing ILD’s global burden. While projections indicate a potential stabilization or decline in ASRs, these trends must be interpreted with caution due to potential confounding effects of pandemic-related diagnostic disruptions. Regional disparities and increasing age-specific burdens necessitate targeted interventions. Policymakers should focus on early detection, addressing healthcare disparities, and developing prevention strategies based on demographic risk patterns. These findings provide crucial evidence for global health planning and underscore the importance of ongoing research into ILD’s environmental and genetic factors.
