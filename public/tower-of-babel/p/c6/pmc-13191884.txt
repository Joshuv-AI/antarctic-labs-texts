Socio-demographic, lifestyle, and anthropometric predictors of ambulatory blood pressure among adults in semi-urban Nigeria
Authors: Ebenezer Adekunle Ajayi, Taofeek Adedayo Sanni, Okechukwu S Ogah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmbulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) offers greater predictive capability for cardiovascular outcomes than clinic blood pressure measurement. However, the factors influencing ambulatory hypertension in African populations continue to be insufficiently researched.ObjectiveTo identify the socio-demographic, lifestyle, and anthropometric factors that predict 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime ambulatory blood pressure levels among adults in a semi-urban Nigerian community.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 348 adults who underwent 24-hour ABPM, along with a structured assessment of socio-demographic, behavioural, and anthropometric factors. Associations were evaluated using bivariate, multivariate linear regression and logistic regression analyses.ResultsAge, higher body mass index (BMI), lower income, and lower educational level were consistently associated with elevated blood pressure (BP) over 24 h, during daytime, and nocturnally. Frequent salt consumption was significantly correlated with abnormal BP across all time periods. Linear regression analysis identified education (β = − 0.24; p = .007), BMI (β = 0.35; p = .012), and age (β = 0.47; p = .029) as independent predictors of 24-hour systolic blood pressure. Logistic regression models for abnormal 24-hour, daytime, and nocturnal hypertension (Nagelkerke R² = 0.51–0.62; accuracy 75–86%) demonstrated strong effects of age, obesity, income, physical activity, and salt intake.ConclusionAmbulatory blood pressure patterns in this population are significantly influenced by factors such as age, adiposity, educational attainment, income, and dietary salt intake. These findings highlight critical targets for population-level hypertension prevention strategies, including reducing obesity, regulating salt intake, and enhancing socioeconomic health literacy.Keywords: Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, Hypertension, Socioeconomic factors, Anthropometry, Nigeria

Introduction

Hypertension constitutes a significant contributor to global cardiovascular morbidity, encompassing stroke, myocardial infarction, renal impairment, and premature mortality [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa, with Nigeria as a notable example, is experiencing an increasing prevalence of hypertension [2]. While Nigeria predominantly relies on office-based blood pressure measurements for data collection, low uptake of out-of-office blood pressure monitoring, including home blood pressure measurement-based studies (HBPM) and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) persist, even though ABPM provides a more precise 24-hour blood pressure profile.

While the biological and genetic influences on blood pressure (BP) are well documented, lifestyle and socio-demographic variables increasingly influence blood pressure levels and circadian rhythms [1, 3–5]. Dietary habits, sodium intake, physical activity, alcohol intake, tobacco exposure, body mass index, and socioeconomic status interact with age, sex, and other demographic factors to produce diverse blood pressure patterns [3, 4, 6]. Understanding these factors at the population level is essential, particularly in nations such as Nigeria, where shifts in diet, activity levels, and stress are driven by epidemiological transitions [2, 6].

This study aimed to identify lifestyle and socio-demographic factors predicting ambulatory blood pressure among Nigerian adults using 24-hour ABPM. It assessed relationships between socio-demographics and BP indices, evaluated lifestyle impacts on 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime BP, and predictive models for abnormal ambulatory BP patterns. The findings intend to inform strategies for hypertension prevention and to tailor community interventions within Nigeria.

Methods

This study was part of the Ambulatory Blood Pressure Study in Ido-Ekiti, a community-based cross-sectional survey conducted in southwestern Nigeria. Its objective was to investigate socio- demographic, lifestyle, and anthropometric factors influencing ambulatory blood pressure among adults. A minimum sample size of 316 was calculated utilising Cochran’s formula [7], based on a 28.9% prevalence of hypertension [2], subsequently increased to 355 to account for potential non-response; ultimately, data from 348 participants were deemed usable. The inclusion criteria included adults aged 18 years and above who had resided in the community for a minimum of six months, excluding individuals with severe illness, pregnancy, arrhythmias, psychiatric disorders, or conditions incompatible with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM). A multistage sampling approach was employed, which involved randomly selecting four residential quarters, systematically identifying households, and selecting one adult per household using the Kish method [8].

Data collection encompassed a structured questionnaire, anthropometric assessment, and blood pressure measurement. The questionnaire adhered to the WHO Stepwise approach [9], capturing self-reported information on socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, physical activity level using questions from the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) included in the WHO STEPS Instrument, and clinical history. Anthropometric measurements followed these WHO protocols, with body mass index (BMI) calculated and classified according to WHO criteria [9]. Office blood pressure was measured using validated automated devices after a minimum of five minutes of rest, averaging two of three readings. ABPM was performed using the CONTEC ABPM 60® on the non-dominant arm, with readings every 30 min during the day (07:00 and 22:00 h), and every 60 min (22:00 and 07:00 h) at night; at least 20 daytime and 7 nighttime measurements were considered valid. Participants maintained their usual activities but avoided vigorous exercise; sleep and wake times were recorded. ABPM data comprised mean 24- hour, daytime, and nighttime systolic and diastolic pressures. Hypertension was defined according to European thresholds: ≥130/80 mmHg for 24-hour, ≥ 135/85 mmHg for daytime, and ≥ 120/70 mmHg for nighttime measurements [10].

Statistical analyses were conducted using chi-square tests to examine bivariate associations between participant characteristics and abnormal ABP across the three time periods. Multivariate linear regression was employed to identify independent predictors of systolic ABP (24-hour, daytime, nighttime). Logistic regression models were subsequently constructed to determine predictors of abnormal ambulatory hypertension for each period. Variables were retained in the multivariable models based on clinical relevance and statistical significance observed in bivariate analyses. Model fit was evaluated utilising R², adjusted R², likelihood ratios, Hosmer–Lemeshow statistics, and classification accuracy. A significance threshold of p < .05 was applied throughout.

Results

A total of 348 participants had valid 24-hour ABPM recordings and were included in the analysis. The mean age was 48.6 ± 17.9 years (range: 18–90), with 55.7% being female. Fifty-one (14.5%) of the participants were known hypertensives on antihypertensive medication. The prevalence of office hypertension and ambulatory hypertension was 43.4% and 32.4%, respectively.

Bivariate associations

Age showed the strongest link to abnormal ambulatory blood pressure (ABP), with significant associations across 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime periods, increasing with age. Marital status and educational attainment significantly correlated with all ABP categories. Lower income was inversely related to normal ABP. Frequent salt use was linked to higher hypertension prevalence. Higher BMI was significantly related to abnormal ABP. Tobacco and alcohol did not show consistent associations (See Table 1).

Multivariate linear regression: systolic ambulatory blood pressure

The multivariable linear model for 24-hour systolic blood pressure (BP) was statistically significant (F(19,211) = 5.88, p < .001) and accounted for 29% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.29). The predictors included higher body mass index (BMI) (β = 0.35, p = .012), increased age (β = 0.47, p = .029), and lower educational attainment (β = − 0.24, p = .007), with higher education levels associated with lower BP. Increases in BMI and age were predictive of higher BP (refer to Table 2). The model for daytime systolic BP was also significant (F(19,211) = 7.33, p < .001; adjusted R² = 0.34). Age (β = 0.39, p = .022) and BMI (β = 0.31, p = .017) emerged as positive predictors, whereas monthly income demonstrated an inverse relationship (β = − 0.20, p = .018) (see Table 3). The nighttime systolic BP model explained approximately 37% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.37; F(19,211) = 8.18, p < .001). Monthly income was the most significant predictor (β = − 0.22, p = .002), while the educational level was marginally significant (β = − 0.16, p = .051). Age exhibited a positive but non-significant association (refer to Table 4).

Multivariate logistic regression: ambulatory hypertension

Three logistic models, which were intended to identify key correlates of abnormal ambulatory blood pressure rather than to develop a definitive predictive risk score, pertaining to 24-hour, daytime, and nocturnal hypertension demonstrated statistical significance, exhibiting acceptable discrimination with Nagelkerke R² values ranging from 0.51 to 0.62, and an overall accuracy between 75% and 86%. The 24-hour model (Table 5) indicated elevated odds of abnormal blood pressure among age groups 40–59 and over 60 years relative to 18–39 years, with a modest increase for females (Adjusted OR = 2.98, p = .046), and reduced odds associated with higher income levels (₦70,000–₦300,000). Notably, obesity and frequent salt consumption emerged as robust predictors. The model’s statistical parameters are as follows: χ² (32) = 146.86, p < .001; Cox & Snell R² = 0.46; Nagelkerke R² = 0.61; Hosmer- Lemeshow p < .001; and an accuracy rate of 75.4%. The daytime model (Table 6) revealed similar age-related effects, with a borderline protective effect of higher income (p = .056) and a tendency for reduced risk with decreased salt intake (p = .074). Its statistical parameters are: χ²(32) = 112.37, p < .001; Cox & Snell R² = 0.37; Nagelkerke R² = 0.51; Hosmer–Lemeshow p = .064; and an accuracy of 78.3%.

Summary of principal findings

Across analytical approaches, advancing age and increased adiposity emerged as the most consistent predictors of elevated 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime systolic BP. Lower levels of education and income were correlated with higher ambulatory blood pressure, particularly during nighttime periods. The frequent addition of salt during cooking and at the table was associated with increased odds of abnormal ambulatory hypertension, whereas adherence to the World Health Organisation (WHO) physical activity guidelines provided protective effects, especially against nocturnal hypertension. Overall, the models accounted for a significant proportion of variance in systolic ambulatory blood pressure (adjusted R² ranging from 0.29 to 0.37) and demonstrated strong discrimination for binary outcomes (Nagelkerke R² ranging from 0.51 to 0.62).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that ambulatory blood pressure patterns in this semi-urban Nigerian population are shaped by a combination of demographic, socio-economic, behavioural, and anthropometric factors. Age and adiposity consistently emerged as the strongest predictors of elevated 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime systolic blood pressure. These findings align with global evidence showing that vascular ageing and obesity are central drivers of ambulatory hypertension and circadian BP disruptions [11, 12].

The contribution of socio-economic status, particularly income and education, to ambulatory blood pressure patterns underscores the significance of structural determinants of health [13–15]. Lower income was correlated with elevated ambulatory blood pressure across all time periods, suggesting that economic hardship may reinforce behavioural risk factors, restrict access to preventive healthcare, or exacerbate psychosocial stress. Similarly, higher educational attainment was associated with reduced BP, consistent with studies that associate education with enhanced health literacy and earlier engagement with healthcare systems [16, 17].

Salt intake (i.e., “always adding salt during cooking”) emerged as a strong behavioural determinant of elevated ambulatory blood pressure, particularly during both daytime and nighttime periods. This finding reinforces growing evidence that sodium intake plays a pivotal role in the development and persistence of hypertension within African populations [18, 19]. Public health initiatives aimed at reducing dietary salt intake could therefore confer considerable cardiovascular benefits. Additionally, physical activity appears to protect against nocturnal hypertension, suggesting that regular exercise may enhance autonomic regulation and promote nocturnal blood pressure dipping [20, 21]. Nevertheless, the cohort’s predominantly active nature suggests that other factors, such as sleep quality or psychosocial stress, may interact with physical activity to influence blood pressure patterns.

The usage of alcohol and tobacco did not exhibit consistent associations, which is likely attributable to the low prevalence within the study population. Caution must be exercised in interpreting these findings, and future research employing more detailed measures of consumption may be necessary.

Overall, the findings underscore the multifactorial nature of hypertension in semi-urban African settings, where lifestyle behaviours coexist with socio-economic disparities and demographic transitions. They highlight the need for integrated intervention strategies that combine lifestyle modification, socio-economic empowerment, and improved access to diagnostic tools such as ABPM.

The primary strengths of this study include the community-based sampling methodology, the use of 24-hour ABPM (an advanced instrument for blood pressure measurement), and the comprehensive assessment of socio-demographic and behavioural factors. Conversely, the limitations include the study’s cross-sectional design, reliance on qualitative self-reported lifestyle data, and the absence of objective biomarkers, such as urinary sodium. Other limitations include a lack of assessment of psychosocial stress, which could contribute to high ABPM values. The generalizability of the findings may be limited to analogous semi-urban populations.

In conclusion, ambulatory blood pressure in this Nigerian population is influenced by a complex interplay of demographic, behavioural, and socio-economic factors. Advancing age, increased adiposity, low income, lower educational levels, and high salt intake are associated with higher 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime blood pressure, while physical activity appears to act as a protective factor, particularly at night. These findings provide a basis for targeted public health strategies and emphasise the importance of ambulatory blood pressure monitoring in assessing cardiovascular risk.
