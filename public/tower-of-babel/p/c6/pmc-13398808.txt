Comparative Risks of Pregnancy Complications in Women Prescribed Psychostimulants or Atomoxetine During Pregnancy Following Pre‐Pregnancy Stimulant Use
Authors: Wen‐Jan Tuan, Nandini Sarkar, Paul Griffin, Dara E. Babinski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePrescription stimulants are commonly used to manage attention deficit hyperactivity disorder among women of reproductive age, yet their safety during pregnancy remains unclear. This study leveraged large‐scale electronic health record data to assess whether use of these medications during pregnancy was associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and to support clinicians in making informed treatment decisions.MethodsThe study included adult women with deliveries from 2010 to 2022 who had two or more stimulant prescriptions before pregnancy. Participants were categorized into three mutually exclusive groups: stimulant, atomoxetine, and control (neither medication during pregnancy). The outcomes of interest included prenatal maternal, placental, fetal growth, labor‐delivery, and birth‐related complications occurring during pregnancy. Propensity score matching analysis was applied to reduce confounding effects in patient baseline characteristics, followed by logistic regression to assess the likelihood of each adverse pregnancy outcome type.ResultsThere were 32,152 pregnant women in the analysis, including 8394 (26.1%) prescribed stimulants, 405 (1.3%) prescribed atomoxetine, and 23,353 (72.7%) controls during pregnancy. The stimulant group was 1.2–3.1 times more likely to develop prenatal maternal, placental, and birth‐related complications than the atomoxetine and control groups, while the atomoxetine group was 1.1–2.3 times more likely to experience fetal growth abnormality and labor‐delivery issues than the control groups. Interestingly, the stimulant group showed 54% lower odds of fetal growth restriction than the atomoxetine group.ConclusionsThis study highlighted differential risk profiles between stimulant and non‐stimulant therapies, contributing evidence that informs future research on perinatal safety.Relevance to Clinical PracticeThe distinct risk profiles identified for stimulant and atomoxetine therapies underscore the need for personalized treatment decisions during pregnancy.

There has been a significant increase in stimulant prescriptions among adults in the United States over the past two decades (1), particularly among women of reproductive ages (2). Stimulant medications have been used as the first‐line treatment for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (3), and in some formulations, is also approved as a treatment for sleep and binge eating disorder (4). While persons taking sustained stimulant medications have demonstrated reduced ADHD symptoms and long‐term improvements in overall functioning (5, 6), stimulants can increase heart rate and constrict blood vessels, potentially raising the risk of cardiovascular complications (7, 8). Atomoxetine is a non‐stimulant pharmacotherapy serving as an alternative for managing ADHD. It has also been used for treating various conditions, including fatigue, depression, obesity, and nicotine cravings, despite mixed evidence supporting its effectiveness (7). Yet, evidence regarding the safety of both prescription stimulants and atomoxetine remains inconclusive, with current pharmacovigilance data highlighting notable variations in their adverse event profiles across age groups and between genders (9).

Recent studies have indicated a rising trend in the number of women prescribed stimulants and atomoxetine during pregnancy, especially among those with ADHD (10, 11). While previous literature has linked the use of stimulants during pregnancy to pre‐eclampsia, preterm birth, low birth weight, and decreased head circumference (12, 13), more recent research on the relationship between stimulant therapy and adverse pregnancy outcomes is less conclusive (14, 15). Additional research on the safety profiles of prescription stimulants and atomoxetine during pregnancy is warranted to inform women and their health care providers about potential risks associated with exposure to these medications before and during pregnancy.

Despite these concerns, discontinuing stimulant or atomoxetine therapy during pregnancy may exacerbate symptoms such as inattention, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation (16, 17). Poorly controlled symptoms may also indirectly affect fetal and infant outcomes through mechanisms such as disrupted sleep, elevated stress responses, and reduced capacity to engage in recommended prenatal and postpartum care. Currently, there are no definitive guidelines or clear clinical recommendations for the use of these medications in pregnancy (14). The inconsistent findings across studies may reflect challenges in disentangling the effects of medication from confounding factors such as maternal comorbidities, psychiatric conditions, and lifestyle behaviors. Previous studies have examined overall stimulant exposure during pregnancy but did not specifically distinguish between women who were already receiving stimulant therapy before conception and those who continued treatment during pregnancy (18, 19). Recognizing these differences allows for more accurate characterization of exposure patterns and may help disentangle underlying maternal characteristics from medication effects when evaluating perinatal outcomes. The objective of this study was to leverage large national electronic health record (EHR) data to assess whether stimulant and atomoxetine treatment are associated with an increased risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes among women on stimulant therapy before pregnancy, offering empirical data to support continued evaluation of their perinatal safety profiles during pregnancy. By focusing on women with pre‐pregnancy stimulant use, this study aims to address that limitation and provide more clinically relevant evidence to inform treatment decisions in this population.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective observational study utilized EHR data from the TriNetX Research Network, involving more than 80 million patients from 103 participating healthcare organizations predominantly from the United States. Data in the TriNetX database has undergone extensive curation and mapping to common clinical entities and terminologies to ensure referential integrity and reliability (20), consistent with the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected Data guidelines (21). The study used deidentified TriNetX research datasets, and was determined to be exempt from the Institutional Review Board oversight by the Pennsylvania State University's Human Research Protection Program.

Study Population

The study population included adult women aged ≥18 years at the time of delivery with documented birth delivery records between January 2010 and December 2022. Pregnancy episodes were ascertained by leveraging pregnancy‐related diagnosis or laboratory codes, which helped estimate the onset and trimester of pregnancy, alongside delivery‐related diagnoses and procedure codes to establish the end of the pregnancy episode (22, 23). Eligible participants were also required to have at least two stimulant prescriptions prior to onset of pregnancy, ensuring that each participant had a confirmed history of prescription stimulant use. In addition, eligible participants were required to have two or more outpatient visits during the pregnancy period to confirm that they were actively engaged in care and had sufficient clinical information for longitudinal assessment. For individuals with multiple pregnancies, only the first recorded pregnancy was included in the analysis.

The study categorized participants into three mutually exclusive groups. The Stimulant group included women receiving two or more refills for stimulant medications and no atomoxetine during pregnancy, while the Atomoxetine group comprised persons receiving two or more prescriptions for atomoxetine but no stimulants during pregnancy. The Control group consisted of individuals receiving neither stimulant nor atomoxetine medications during pregnancy. Individuals with only one stimulant or one atomoxetine prescription were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome Measures and Baseline Characteristics

The study evaluated six outcome domains, each representing a major category of pregnancy complications: prenatal maternal complications (gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia), placental complications (placental abruption, placental previa), fetal growth restriction, large for gestational age (LGA), labor‐delivery complications (Cesarean section, instrumental delivery, shoulder dystocia, induction of labor), and birth‐related adverse outcomes (preterm delivery, spontaneous abortion/miscarriage). The outcome measures were identified using ICD‐10 diagnosis codes indicating that at least one of the prespecified conditions was present during the pregnancy period.

Data on available baseline characteristics of the study participants were extracted at the estimated pregnancy start date. Demographic data included advanced maternal age (≥35 years), race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Other), smoking/drinking status, diagnoses of medical conditions known to increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (diabetes, obesity/overweight, hypertension, arrhythmias, chronic pain, immunodeficiency, substance use disorders), and mental health‐related conditions (ADHD, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder). Stimulant prescriptions within 6 months prior to pregnancy onset were also included as a separate covariate to capture recent medication use. References for the rationale for the baseline characteristics and outcomes chosen are provided in Supporting Information S1, and detailed information on the extracted variables describing baseline characteristics is provided in Supporting Information S1: Table S1.

Data on the number of stimulant and atomoxetine prescriptions during pregnancy were also extracted to construct the proportion of days covered (PDC) measure to account for differences in treatment duration and consistency during pregnancy. PDC was calculated as the proportion of days during pregnancy for which stimulant or atomoxetine therapy was available to each participant, allowing us to account for variation in refill timing, supply duration, and overall exposure intensity across individuals (24).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations [SD] or frequencies) were computed to describe the study population at baseline. Differences in baseline characteristics between the groups were assessed with t‐test for continuous variables and Chi‐square test for categorical variables.

The study utilized propensity score analysis followed by logistic regression to compare the likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes (at the domain and the condition levels) between the stimulant and no medication groups, the atomoxetine and no medication groups, and the stimulant and the atomoxetine group. Essentially, this analysis began with a propensity score analysis using the inverse probability of treatment weighting method (25) to balance the comparison groups and reduce potential confounding effects in their baseline characteristics. A weighting value was assigned to each person representing the probability of the individual receiving a treatment they would not have been expected to receive (25). Covariate balance before and after propensity score weighting was evaluated using standardized mean differences to measure the effectiveness of the propensity score approach. Logistic regression was then performed on the weighted sample to assess the effect of the comparison groups on the risk of developing adverse pregnancy outcomes. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals were computed for each treatment group in the model. Statistical significance level was established based on two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05.

RESULTS

Population Summary

The study population consisted of 32,152 women receiving stimulant therapy prior to pregnancy (see Figure 1 for the sample selection flow diagram). Among them, 8394 (26.1%) continued prescribed stimulant use (stimulant group), 405 (1.3%) switched to atomoxetine (atomoxetine group), and 23,353 (72.6%) received neither medication (serving as controls) during pregnancy (Table 1). The stimulant group had the highest mean maternal age of 32.3 years (SD = 8.0), followed by the atomoxetine group of 31.1 years (SD = 10.1) and the control group of 29.9 years (SD = 7.8). The stimulant group also had a higher PDC during pregnancy (mean = 38.7%, SD = 35.8%), while the atomoxetine group had a lower PDC (mean = 22.5%, SD = 21.5%).

Overall, maternal age at delivery differed significantly across groups (p < 0.01). Women prescribed stimulants (mean age 32.3 years) and atomoxetine (31.1 years) were, on average, older than those in the control group (29.9 years), with a higher proportion meeting criteria for advanced maternal age. Overall, women receiving stimulants or atomoxetine during pregnancy had a higher prevalence of prenatal maternal and birth‐related complications (p < 0.01), while rates of placental complications, fetal growth restriction, and labor‐delivery complications did not differ significantly across groups.

Risk of Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes

Compared with atomoxetine, stimulant use during pregnancy was associated with higher odds of prenatal maternal complications (aOR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.19–1.53) and placental complications (aOR = 3.11, 95% CI: 2.39–3.89), as well as increased odds of birth‐related adverse outcomes (aOR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.90–2.44) (Table 3 and Figure 2). Stimulant use was also associated with elevated odds of these domains compared with controls, whereas the atomoxetine cohort did not differ significantly from controls for prenatal maternal or placental complications. In contrast, stimulant exposure was associated with lower odds of fetal growth restriction compared with atomoxetine (aOR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.39–0.54) and controls (aOR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.64–0.85), while atomoxetine exposure was associated with substantially higher odds compared with controls (aOR = 2.29, 95% CI: 2.00–2.62). Findings from condition‐level analyses were generally consistent with these domain‐level associations, with no single condition disproportionately driving the observed results.

For LGA, stimulant use was associated with higher odds relative to atomoxetine (aOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.29–2.40) but lower odds relative to controls (aOR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.78–0.97), whereas atomoxetine exposure was associated with increased odds compared with controls (aOR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.71–2.21). Differently, stimulant exposure was associated with lower odds of labor‐delivery complications compared with atomoxetine (aOR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.61–0.79) and controls (aOR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.81–0.90), but no difference was found between atomoxetine exposure and controls. Condition‐level analyses showed that atomoxetine exposure was strongly associated with increased odds of instrumental delivery and induction of labor, whereas stimulant exposure was associated with lower odds of Cesarean section and induction of labor. For birth‐related outcomes, stimulant use was associated with increased odds of spontaneous abortion/miscarriage compared with both atomoxetine and controls, while preterm delivery was more frequent among stimulant users compared with controls.

DISCUSSION

This study, leveraging large‐scale EHR data on women receiving stimulant therapy before pregnancy, found that both continuation of stimulants or switching to atomoxetine during pregnancy were associated with modestly increased risks of selected adverse pregnancy outcomes. The findings address a critical evidence gap, as prior research has largely focused on exclusively stimulants, leaving limited data to guide decision‐making around prenatal atomoxetine use. The results of this study will help inform clinical decision‐making by weighing the potential maternal benefits of continued stimulant or atomoxetine therapy against possible risks to maternal and fetal health.

Women prescribed stimulants exhibited higher risk of prenatal maternal and placental complications compared to those receiving atomoxetine. They also demonstrated elevated odds of maternal, placental, and birth‐related complications relative to those receiving neither stimulants nor atomoxetine during pregnancy. Atomoxetine exposure, on the other hand, was generally comparable to the control cohort for maternal and placental complications but was associated with increased risk of fetal growth abnormalities. The higher risk of fetal growth restriction observed among women prescribed atomoxetine may partly reflect indication bias, as non‐stimulant therapies are more often selected for patients with greater psychiatric complexity or substance use histories, which are themselves associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Notably, women treated with stimulants during pregnancy appeared to confer lower odds of fetal growth abnormalities and labor delivery complications relative to those prescribed atomoxetine, suggesting potential differences in the safety profiles of these pharmacologic agents during pregnancy. The study findings underscore the importance of differentiating between stimulant and non‐stimulant therapies when evaluating maternal‐fetal risks, highlighting the need for careful, individualized treatment planning to optimize both maternal and neonatal outcomes. For instance, atomoxetine is linked to higher risk of fetal growth restriction and labor delivery complications, so clinicians may consider serial fetal growth ultrasounds for patients receiving atomoxetine, especially in the second and third trimesters.

Although we did not evaluate specific medication dosages or product formulations, these factors may be clinically important, as differences in cumulative exposure, release mechanisms, and pharmacokinetic profiles could differentially affect maternal cardiovascular physiology, placental function, and fetal development. In this study, we partially addressed exposure heterogeneity by incorporating the PDC measure to capture variation in treatment duration and consistency. However, we recognize that future research with more granular dosing and formulation data is needed to further elucidate these relationships.

These results add to the limited literature on the safety of psychostimulant and related therapies in pregnancy. Our findings align with prior literature suggesting that prescription of stimulants during pregnancy may be linked to maternal cardiovascular complications, pre‐eclampsia, and preterm birth (12, 13). Although the pregnancy safety profile of atomoxetine has been less well‐characterized, our study results indicate a possible association with fetal growth and birth‐related complications. Our analysis also highlights the importance of interpreting observational data cautiously, as differences may reflect underlying maternal characteristics, comorbidities, or treatment indications rather than direct causal effects. The observed variations in risk between stimulants and atomoxetine also align with established pharmacovigilance data indicating heterogeneity in adverse event profiles across age and sex groups (9). As such individual patient factors may substantially influence pregnancy outcomes.

Regarding clinical implications, our results reinforce the critical role of individualized risk‐benefit discussions for women with ADHD or other indications for stimulant therapy who are planning to be pregnant. Clinicians and patients should recognize that while continued exposure may carry modest but measurable risks, particularly for maternal and birth‐related complications, discontinuing these medications may exacerbate ADHD symptoms such as inattention and emotional dysregulation, potentially impairing maternal functioning and adherence to prenatal care (16). As a result, treatment decisions should carefully balance maternal functioning and safety with modest but measurable risks to pregnancy outcomes. For individuals with milder symptoms, interventions such as behavioral therapy, structured routines, cognitive‐behavioral therapy, or coaching may serve as useful adjuncts or medication alternatives. Further research is needed to guide evidence‐based recommendations for the management of ADHD and related conditions during pregnancy.

Study Limitation

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, individuals in the research network may have received stimulants or atomoxetine from facilities outside of the participating network and therefore be uncaptured in our analysis, potentially leading to the exposure misclassification. Similarly, miscarriage and spontaneous abortions were also difficult to be captured in EHR data, as some individuals may not seek care for these events or might receive care outside participating healthcare organizations, leading to potential under‐ascertainment of these outcomes. In addition, the requirement of at least two pre‐pregnancy stimulant prescriptions reflected prior engagement with the participating healthcare systems; thus, medication use among individuals with limited pre‐pregnancy care or care received elsewhere could be under‐captured. Second, EHR data does not reliably indicate whether a recorded delivery involved a singleton or multiple gestation, limiting our ability to account for plurality, which may independently influence pregnancy and birth outcomes (26). Third, the study relied on diagnosis codes commonly used in clinical and administrative data to document adverse pregnancy outcomes; however, these codes may not capture the underlying clinical reasons for these events. As a result, some outcomes may be subject to misclassification and should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, because our study relied on retrospective observational data, the absence of a prospective, randomized design may have affected the validity of the outcomes. Fifth, geographic (e.g., state, county), socioeconomic (e.g., health insurance, education), and organization‐level variables are not available in the research database. These factors are known to influence both access to healthcare services and treatment adherence, as well as the risk of developing adverse pregnancy outcomes. Their absence limited our ability to adjust for health policy‐ or socioeconomic‐related effects that could confound the observed associations. Lastly, the relatively small number of women exposed to atomoxetine may limit statistical power for some outcomes involving that group. Future prospective studies that incorporate advanced data mining techniques and consider social determinants of health could provide deeper insights and better account for previously unmeasured but important confounders.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides empirical evidence that exposure to either stimulant or atomoxetine exposure during pregnancy is associated with modestly increased risks of selected adverse maternal, placental, and birth‐related outcomes. Our findings highlight differential risk profiles between stimulant and non‐stimulant therapies and provide robust evidence to guide individualized clinical decision‐making, balancing maternal symptom management with potential perinatal risks. These results underscore the need for careful, patient‐centered counseling and ongoing monitoring when prescribing stimulant or atomoxetine during pregnancy.
