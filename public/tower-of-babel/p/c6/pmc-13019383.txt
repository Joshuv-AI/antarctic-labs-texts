Molecular Constraints and Electronic Structure Direct Multi-Step PCET Mechanisms in the Electrochemical Oxidation of Ruthenium Complexes
Authors: Laura C. Castaño Castañeda, Bruno Angelillo, Brandon Q. Mercado, Florencia Di Salvo, Mauricio Cattaneo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extensive studies on the dehydrogenative oxidation of alkylamines on transition metals represent a generalized reactivity, with some reports on accessible intermediates and reactive species offering different interpretations. Here, the electrochemical oxidation of ruthenium amine complexes is examined to evaluate the reactivity of high-valent species. We report a combined spectroelectrochemical and DFT study of the complexes [RuII(bpy)2(LL)]2+ with LL = (NH3)2, en (ethylenediamine), and en* (2,3-dimethyl-2,3-diamino-butane), and bpy = 2,2′-bipyridine. Comparison among these three complexes revealed different electrochemical oxidation mechanistic pathways, yet all of them follow the same overall acid–base–dependent reactivity. In both protic and aprotic solvents, the first oxidation proceeds as a pure one-electron process under strongly acidic conditions. In the presence of a base, a multiproton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) process completes the net 2e–/2H+ reaction. The oxidation mechanism of [Ru­(bpy)2(en*)]2+ is revisited to achieve improved consistency with long-standing experimental proposals. Electrochemical and spectral analysis, along with DFT calculations, support the assignment of the intermediates formed. Molecular orbital analysis reveals interactions between dπRu and pNsp2 orbitals, which stabilize specific reaction intermediates through geometric constraints. This report highlights the relevance of Ru-amine/amido complexes as multi-PCET redox models for bond-activation catalysis.

Introduction

The study of high-valent reactive species is crucial for the development of new multielectron catalytic mechanisms that enable bond activation in small molecules, such as water, CO2, N2, and O2. In biological systems, metalloenzymes such as cytochrome P450 activate O2 to cleave C–H bonds and functionalize otherwise unactivated hydrocarbons. In photosynthesis, the oxygen evolution complex in PSII cleaves O–H bonds to produce molecular oxygen, while Rubisco and Nitrogenase activate CO and NN bonds, respectively, for CO2 reduction and N2 fixation in plants. , To perform these multiple-electron processes, several parameters, including geometry, substrate association, electronic structure, and the energetics of accessible intermediates, interplay together. −

In catalysts, the activation of O–H bonds is crucial to produce green hydrogen. Meanwhile, the reduction of CO2 and the activation of C–H and C–C bonds in organic molecules facilitate their conversion into products of higher energy content and economic value. Within this context, ruthenium complexes have attracted considerable interest because of their broad applications in catalysis, synthetic versatility, structural stability, and well-established role as model systems. −

High-valent chemistry of oxo-ruthenium complexes has been extensively studied. − These compounds can reach high oxidation states through proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) steps, generating highly reactive species capable of oxidizing water and organic compounds such as alcohols and alkanes via O–H and C–H bond activation, respectively.

In particular, RuIVO species with polypyridine and triazacyclic ligands have shown remarkable efficiency in the oxidation of alcohols and alkanes through mechanisms such as hydrogen atom abstraction (HAT) or concerted proton and electron transfer (CPET). − Similar mechanistic pathways have been proposed for RuVIO2, which present a greater oxidizing power and act like active intermediates in the process of selective oxidation of C–H bonds, functionalization of alkenes, and epoxidation. − Furthermore, RuVO species are widely recognized as key intermediates in the O–O bond formation via electrophilic attack during water oxidation and several of these species have been isolated. − Factors such as the acidity of the medium, the presence of Lewis acid functionalities, and the electronic structure of the ligand significantly influence the selectivity and catalytic efficiency of these systems.

The relatively limited number of reports and experimental characterizations of ruthenium amine/amido species does not necessarily reflect the importance and reactivity of the Ru–N bond. However, there has been an increasing interest in recent years. Oxidation of [RuII(NH3)­(N4Py)]2+ has been shown to allow access to RuVNH species that are capable of activating C–H bonds in water-soluble organic compounds via a hydride transfer mechanism. Additionally, a RuVI-nitride species was isolated from the [RuIII(salchda)­(NH3)­(CH3CN)]+ complex, which plays a crucial role in the catalytic oxidation of NH3 to N2 via an electrophilic attack leading to N–N bond formation. Another stable complex, [RuIV(bpy)­(en*-H)2]2+ (en*-HH2N–C­(Me)2–C­(Me)2–NH̅), has been shown to undergo reversible 2e–/2H+ reactions. Therefore, ruthenium complexes with amine ligands can achieve high oxidation states that allow bond activation through mechanisms like those of Ru-oxo complexes.

Ligands play a fundamental role in the efficiency, stability, and reactivity of catalysts in the activation mechanism, modulating geometry and electronic structure. In particular, alkylamine-type ligands containing αC–H groups undergo an oxidative dehydrogenation process in the presence of high-valent transition metals such as ruthenium, − nickel, − copper, osmium, − iron, platinum, − etc., resulting in the irreversible formation of stable α-imine/diimine compounds, due to the stabilization of low-valent metal centers by π-retro-donation effect.

Initial studies on this process in [Ru­(en)3]2+ reported that one of the ethylenediamine ligands underwent oxidative dehydrogenation of 4e– with the formation of two imine groups coordinated to the metal. , Subsequently, Keene et al. proposed that RuIII and RuIV species participated as intermediates, providing some mechanistic insights into the process. ,

To prevent the formation of imine groups, the ligand 2,3-diamine-2,3-dimethylbutane (en*) blocks the αC–H positions with methyl groups, avoiding the αC–H elimination. The first Ruthenium complex reported with the tetra-methylated ethylenediamine ligand (en*) was [RuII(bpy)2(en*)]2+, allowing access to interesting chemistry from high-valent intermediates. This ligand allows the reversibility of the first redox reaction to access the RuV oxidation state as a stable complex. Investigations about this complex also reported a redox process of 4e–/4H+ to [RuVI(bpy)2(en*-H4)]+2, which finally decomposes by C–C bond cleavage. − These studies reported access to RuV and RuVI intermediates, with an initial 3e–/3H+ transfer reported to occur in a single step at 0.75 V vs SCE. Chemical oxidation with CeIV resulted in the N–O bond formation to the complex [RuII(bpy)2(ONCMe2CMe2NO)]+2. Although these experimental results were further supported by DFT calculations, there are some inconsistencies because a simultaneous three-electron transfer process with high electrochemical reversibility and a low oxidation potential should be mechanistically unlikely. This interpretation could be contrasted with reports on the oxidative dehydrogenation mechanism based on other findings and Keene’s proposals, which involves an initial 1e– process. ,− ,,

In this work, we explore the electrochemical, structural, and chemical behavior of a family of ruthenium complexes with diamine and diammine ligands, [Ru­(bpy)2(LL)]2+ with LL = ethylenediamine (en) (1), and 2,3-dimethyl-2,3-butanodiamine (en*) (2), (NH3)2 (3) (Scheme ). We evaluated the already proposed oxidative mechanism of the [RuII(bpy)2(en*)]2+ complex, in which oxidative dehydrogenation is prevented by the blocked αC–H positions of the en* ligand. This behavior was then compared with two well-studied model molecules: [RuII(bpy)2(en)]2+, having available α-hydrogens that allow ligand dehydrogenative oxidation reaction, and [RuII(bpy)2(NH3)2]2+, in which this transformation is not structurally feasible. This analysis contributes to a deeper understanding of the behavior and distinct reaction pathways of these systems in the context of high-valent chemistry, electronic structure, and multiredox PCET reactivity.

Results

We selected the complexes [Ru­(bpy)2(en)]2+ (1), [Ru­(bpy)2(en*)]2+ (2) and [Ru­(bpy)2(NH3)2]2+ (3) (Scheme ) because their slightly different structures allowed us to explore the variations in term of reactivity, structural changes and intermediate formation. All complexes were synthesized as PF6 – salts and purified according to the reported methods, ,, yielding good-quality crystals for each one. Despite the low water solubility of these complexes as PF6 – salts, no solubility issues were observed at the concentrations used in the experiments, likely due to the solubilizing effect of the coordinated amine ligands. Further spectroscopic and electrochemical measurements were conducted under the same conditions to compile comparable data. Spectroelectrochemical measurements for each complex in aqueous and acetonitrile solutions were carried out under different conditions to access the spectral information on accessible species. With the experimental XRD structures, DFT calculations were performed, and subsequent abstractions of protons and electrons were used to prepare each different species that could be accessible for each complex. From the optimized structures, additional calculations were conducted to obtain electronic structure information, molecular orbital diagrams, spectroscopic profiles, and thermochemical energies for each species. Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)

One-dimensional (1H and 13C) and two-dimensional (COSY 1H–1H, HSQC 1H–13C, HMBC 1H–13C, HMBC 15N–1H spectra) NMR experiments in acetone-d 6 were used to complement the study of the overall spatial structure of the complexes. Table summarizes the signals observed, which were assigned based on a combined analysis of the spectra, considering the electronic effects induced by coordination to the metal center. The cis-amines spatial configuration of complexes generates split signals in two distinct rings on bipyridine, one of them trans to each other and the other trans to the LL ligand. The axial rings are spatially closer to the LL ligand, so they experience a slight increase in electron density, which generates a high-field shift with respect to the signal of equatorial rings, observed by NOESY effect. H6′ is pointing toward the LL ligands, therefore showing nuclear correlation in the NOESY spectrum with N–H and C–H protons, as observed in Figures S6 and S14. H4 and H5 are in the para and meta positions of the rings, which resonate at a lower chemical shift, due to the π-backbonding effect from the metal center to the π* orbitals of the ligand, which increases the electronic density on the para carbons and generates a high-field shift. The H6 protons show the most pronounced downfield shift. Their proximity to the coordinated nitrogen makes them particularly susceptible to the electron density of the metal center. This interaction decreases the local electron density, reduces shielding, and causes a low-field shift.

Electrochemistry

To expand the spectroscopic information available for isolable species under electrochemical conditions, cyclic voltammetry measurements were performed for each compound in buffered aqueous media (using H2SO4 and H3PO4 as electrolytes) and in acetonitrile (with 0.1 M TBA-PF6 as the supporting electrolyte). These were combined with spectroelectrochemical experiments, which allowed the identification of spectral features of the generated species. In aqueous media, pH < 1 was adjusted using 0.1 or 1 M H2SO4 solutions, whereas phosphate buffers adjusted with NaOH were used for pH ≥ 1.6.

Aqueous Media

In an aqueous media at pH 1.6, cyclic voltammetry revealed a well-defined and reversible wave for complexes (2) and (3) at potentials of 0.71 and 0.69 V vs Ag/AgCl, respectively. By contrast, for complex (1), a more strongly acidic solution (pH ∼ −0.5) was required to suppress deprotonation and subsequent oxidative dehydrogenation, allowing observation of a reversible wave at 0.70 V vs Ag/AgCl. These potentials are close to each other, indicating similar electron density at the metal center induced by the different diamine ligands (LL). They are consistent with the values reported in the literature for the highly reversible RuIII/II couple associated with the RuII d6-RuIII d5 transition in analogous ruthenium polypyridine complexes. ,− These redox processes were evaluated under more basic conditions, where the redox potential and reversibility begin to decrease at pH 5 for complex (2) and pH 3 for complex (3) (see Figure S21). This behavior is consistent with a Nernstian effect arising from proton-coupled redox processes, as supported by the calculated Pourbaix diagrams (see below) and by pH-dependent behavior previously reported in the literature. , Such proton coupling was first reported by Taube for ruthenium amine complexes and later explored by Meyer in the context of ammonia oxidation, where species such as RuIII–H1, RuIV–H1, or RuIV–H2 could be generated.

The presence of the RuIII–H0 species, generated by a one-electron oxidation, was confirmed by spectroelectrochemistry in strong acidic solutions, at pH ∼ −0.5 for complexes (1) and (3), and at pH 1.6 for complex (2), as shown in Figure . In this case, more acidic conditions were necessary to preserve the reversibility in complex (3) because of the longer experimental times required for spectroelectrochemical measurements (5 mV/s) compared to conventional electrochemistry (100 mV/s). Oxidations present the vanishing of the metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) transitions at ∼490 nm and ∼360 nm, and the ligand-centered (LC) at ∼290 nm, with the appearance of a new band centered at ∼310 nm for complexes (1), (2) and (3), associated with the formation of the oxidized species RuIII–H0 and agrees with the spectra calculated for each. These spectra are characterized by the absence of MLCT, without spectral features above 350 nm. These are chemically reversible one-electron processes evidenced by the reappearance of the initial bands, all of which have well-defined isosbestic points at 298 and 328 nm, supporting a clean interconversion between two species, RuII–H0 and RuIII–H0. Potential-controlled electrolysis reveals that the RuIII–H0 species remain stable over experimental time when strong acidic conditions are used. Nevertheless, when the potential is applied to complex (2) for an additional 20 min under the same conditions, a UV–vis spectrum that closely matches the simulated spectrum of RuII-diimine (which has the C–C bond broken) is obtained (Figure S22). Moreover, a sequential RuII–H0 → RuIII–H0 → RuII-diimine transformation was observed for complex (2) by cyclic voltammetry at pH 1.6 and a scan rate of 1 mV/s (Figure ).

Under less acidic conditions, all complexes unlocked different reaction pathways. Complex (1), at pH 1.6 and 6, underwent a fully irreversible oxidation process, accompanied by the appearance of new bands at 454, 281, and 242 nm, consistent with the formation of the [RuII(bpy)2(α,α-diimine)]2+complex, , confirming the oxidative dehydrogenation of the ligand (Figure S23). These results are in line with the low chemical reversibility observed in cyclic voltammetry experiments (Figure S21). Therefore, at pH < 0.5, the oxidative dehydrogenation of the ligand could be effectively suppressed, whereas at pH 1.6 this reaction remains. In complex (2), the redox process remains reversible up to pH 6. At higher pH values, reversibility is lost and significant changes in the isosbestic points are observed, highlighting the formation of new species as seen in Figure S24. Complex (3) at pH 1.6, shows that as the RuIII–H0 species is generated, a follow-up chemical process is involved, with the appearance of a new band at 330 nm (see Figure S25a). At pH 6, deprotonation is more favorable, preventing the capture of the RuIII–H0 species and showing the same final product as pH 1.6. Meanwhile, at pH ≥ 7, another product with a band in the visible region was formed, probably associated with a further decomposition/oxidation process (Figures S25c and S26b).

Integrating these results for all complexes, it is clear that deprotonation of the RuIII–H0 species is an essential step for the subsequent reactivity. In other words, in strongly acidic media these processes are inhibited due to the suppression of deprotonation.

Complexes (2) and (3) display a highly reversible anodic wave with less than 80 mV peak separation. These anodic waves were assigned to a pure one-electron transfer between the species RuII–H0 and RuIII–H0, in line with the results obtained in aqueous media. This assignment was made taking into account the limiting potential reached by acid aggregates (Table S15) and by spectroelectrochemical identification of the RuIII–H0 species for complexes (2) and (3). In particular, oxidation is accompanied by the appearance of new bands around 308 nm for (2) and 312 nm for (3), together with bleaching of the MLCT band in the visible region. Well-defined isosbestic points were observed at 299 and 335 nm for (2), and at 298 and 324 nm for (3) (see Figure b,c). Complex (2) showed high chemical reversibility, whereas for complex (3) such reversibility was maintained only under strongly acidic conditions, suggesting the involvement of secondary chemical processes that are suppressed in the presence of strong acids (Figure S28).

In contrast, complex (1) exhibited a less reversible anodic wave, even in the presence of HClO4 aggregates or TfOH excess (Figures S27 and S29). With TfOH, a slight band formation at 310 nm was observed, suggesting partial formation of the RuIII–H0 species. This chemical irreversibility of the RuIII/II couple was associated with the subsequent formation of the [RuII(bpy)2(α,α-diimine)]2+ complex, as evidenced by its characteristic spectrum (Figures a and S28a).

Electrochemical experiments employing 2,6-lutidine as a base resulted in complete irreversibility for the oxidation of complexes (2) and (3) (Figure S30). However, with the addition of acid to neutralize the lutidine, the reversibility was restored. This evidence reveals two distinct redox processes: one is fully reversible in the presence of excess acid, corresponding to a pure one-electron transfer, and another irreversible under basic conditions, consistent with a PCET reaction. Spectroelectrochemistry with 2,6-lutidine in complexes (2) and (3) reveals that the oxidation is completely irreversible. The ligand deprotonation causes a significant change in the isosbestic points, which indicates the formation of different species compared to the acidic media (Figure S31).

In the presence of 2,6-lutidine, the irreversibility of the redox process in (1) is complete, favoring the oxidative dehydrogenation of the ligand, as depicted in Figure S32. During a potential scan between 150 and 1350 mV at 10 mV/s, the formation of [RuII(bpy)2(α,α-diimine)]2+ was followed by a decrease in the absorbance of MLCT band, likely due to its further oxidation to [RuIII(bpy)2(α,α-diimine)]3+ at higher potential (Figure S31a). At a constant potential of 600 mV, it was possible to avoid further oxidation and to capture the MLCT band of [RuII(bpy)2(α,α-diimine)]2+ with λmax = 454 nm as presented in Figure S32. These results highlight the increase in the RuIII/RuII oxidation potential upon stabilization of the HOMO orbital through enhanced π-back-donation to the newly formed α,α-diimine ligand.

Computational Analysis

Density Functional Theory (DFT) geometry optimizations were performed for each compound at the BP86/6-311G­(d,p)-SDD and B3LYP/6-311G­(d,p)-SDD levels of theory, following a previous reported methodology. Optimizations were initiated from the crystallographic structures of the RuII–H0 species for each complex, followed by optimizations of the relevant oxidation and protonation states for thermochemical and spectral calculations, including RuII–H0, RuIII–H0, RuIII–H1 and RuIV–H2 (Figures S33–S35 and Tables S16–S18). These calculations allowed access to the electronic structure, thermochemistry, and simulated spectra of the different species, enabling direct comparison with experimental results. The solvation model SMD was employed for acetonitrile, as it represents experimental results with better accuracy by lacking hydrogen bond interactions with the solvent. All compounds exhibited pseudo-octahedral symmetry with the chelating angle N–Ru–N deviating from 90° due to steric effects and the structural chelating nature of the ligands, as discussed above. For complex (3), the calculated Nam–Ru–Nam angle for RuII–H0 is 87.5°, whereas in complexes (1) and (2), it is distorted to 80.6° and 78.1°, respectively. The smaller angles arise from the chelate restriction imposed by the bidentate ligands, and this contraction is further increased by the steric effect of the methyl groups, leading to a smaller angle in (2) than in (1). Calculated structures accurately matched the experimental crystallographic data, supporting our procedure for further analysis of intermediates (see Table ).

Upon oxidation of the ruthenium center and deprotonation of the amine ligand, systematic changes in bond distances and angles were observed. In all cases, the Ru–Nam bond distance decreased as the metal center became more positively charged, increasing electron demand from the ligand and, consequently, the acidity of the amine protons. As protons were removed, nitrogen-centered p-orbitals became available for π-interaction with the ruthenium center, producing a change in the nitrogen hybridization from sp3 to sp2, as reflected in the corresponding bond angles and distances. In complex (3), the Nam–Ru–Nam angle increased to 111.5° in the RuIV–H2 species, whereas in complexes (1) and (2), this angle is restricted to ∼80° by the rigidity of the bidentate ligand, thereby increasing the energy of these intermediates. Although the Nam–Ru–Nam angle did not change significantly, the N–C–C–N dihedral angle flattened in complexes (1) and (2), enhancing the tension over the C–C bond distance in the RuIII–H1 species. The chemistry observed for the oxidative dehydrogenation of complex (1) indicates that the release of the αC–H proton is favored prior to C–C bond cleavage because the acidity of the C–H is higher than the next N–H bond (see Table ).

Thermochemical Analysis

Thermochemical calculations for each proton or electron exchange, represented in the square diagrams, enabled the construction of simulated Pourbaix diagrams incorporating pK a, E 0, and BDFE for each process.

Figure shows the RuIII–H1/RuII–H0 and RuIV–H2/RuIII–H1 couples for each compound. These plots illustrate the stability ranges of the different species in solution and identify regions where high-energy, less accessible intermediates are expected. The calculated values are listed in Table . Additionally, Pourbaix diagrams are provided in Supporting Information (Figures S57–S59). It was not possible to optimize the RuVI species for complex (2), in agreement with previous studies, and therefore thermochemical values for the RuVI/RuV couple were not obtained.

Pourbaix diagrams calculated for complexes (a) (1), (b) (2), (c) (3) in MeCN.

The pK a values for the different RuII–H0/RuII–H1 equilibria exceed 30, in agreement with experimentally measured and computed values for a similar complex. Consequently, access to the RuII–H1 species was excluded from all proposed mechanisms due to the prohibitively high energy required for its formation. In contrast, the calculated E 0 value for the RuII–H0/RuIII–H0 couple corresponds to an experimentally accessible and reversible oxidation. At the RuIII–H0 oxidation level, the RuIII–H0/RuIII–H1 equilibria exhibits pK a values of 6.5, 4.5, and 8.8 for complexes (1), (2), and (3), respectively, reflecting the substantial increase in acidity upon oxidation. This is a crucial step in the mechanistic analysis proposed. The BDFE values for the PCET process involving the RuIII–H1/RuII–H0 couple are ∼72 kcal/mol for alkylamine-containing complexes (1) and (2), increasing to ∼77 kcal/mol for complex (3). Further oxidation requires additional energy due to the structural reorganization imposed by the restriction of bidentate LL-ligands in complexes (1) and (2). This effect is reflected in the increment of the BDFE for the RuIV–H2/RuIII–H1 PCET process to 82 and 87 kcal/mol for complexes (1) and (2), respectively. In contrast, in complex (3), this BDFE is lower due to more favorable orbital overlap within the N–Ru–N framework, enabled by expansion of the chelating angle to 111.5°. This structural reorganization seems fundamental to favor the formation of the RuIV–H2 species through enhanced π-bonding, which affects pK a’s and E 0’s. In complex (1), after reaching the RuIII–H1 species, it could be deprotonated to form RuIII–H2 in two different ways: (i) deprotonation at the other NH2 group (pK a = 29.6) or (ii) deprotonation at the αC–H position (pK a = 16.7). Thermochemical analysis of these options indicates that option 2 is overwhelmingly favored by ∼13 orders of magnitude, with the corresponding BDFE decreasing from 82 to 38 kcal/mol for the PCET process leading to the formation of the imine bond. A concerted proton–electron transfer from this stage is consistent with KIE of the base assisted mechanism reported by Sargeson. ,− ,, Complex (2) lacks αC–H bonds, restricting access to this pathway; however, a decrease in the E 0 value for RuIII–H1/RuIV–H1 couple opens the possibility of C–C bond cleavage from either the RuIII–H1 or RuIV–H1 species.

Molecular Orbitals (MO)

Molecular orbital ordering provides insight into metal–ligand interactions, their stabilization, and the resulting reactivity. A comparison of molecular orbital ordering and orbital contributions from different fragments of the molecule is presented in Figure , with additional details provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S39–S42). The molecular orbital diagrams for RuII–H0 species present the typical fully occupied d6 t2g- like ruthenium orbitals, which are closely spaced in energy. In the RuIII–H1 species, one of the Ru d-based orbitals undergoes splitting, leading to the formation of a new MO with mixed p-N and d-Ru contribution. Thus, π-interactions stabilize not just RuIII–H1 but also other high-valent intermediates with further deprotonation processes. In complex (1), the subsequent deprotonation depends on which π-system provides more stability to the molecule. Stabilization through the Namido–Ru–Namido π-system is limited by poor orbital overlap caused by bidentate ligand constraints. Consequently, Ru–N−αC π-interaction is favored in complex (1), as deprotonation at the α-carbon leading to a RuII-monoimine intermediate is preferred over formation of the RuIV–H2 species. As shown in Figure a, the nearly degenerate d-t2g orbitals exhibit minimal LL-ligand contribution, indicating that the π-interaction is mainly localized within the NC bond. Similarly, complex (2) structurally restricts Namido–Ru–Namido interaction because of the chelating nature of the en* ligand. In this case, NC bond formation requires the C–C bond cleavage due to the lack of αC–H bonds.

Discussion

Here, we analyze the different mechanisms followed by complexes (1), (2) and (3). The results obtained in this paper and from the literature were evaluated together in order to identify the structural factors underlying the varied behavior of these complexes.

In the present work, the UV–vis spectra corresponding to the RuIII–H0 species within the [RuII(bpy)2LL]2+ family were unambiguously identified. The comparison between experimental and calculated UV–vis spectra supports that electron transfer in acidic media, both in MeCN and in water, corresponds to the stable and highly reversible one-electron oxidation, giving rise to the RuIII–H0 species. These results emphasize that the initial oxidation is followed by deprotonation, a step that is essential for the subsequent reactivity and for accessing higher oxidation states, confirming an initial EC mechanism to form RuIII–H1.

According to Keene, oxidative dehydrogenation of the ligand proceeds as a sequential process that begins with a one-electron oxidation, resulting in RuIII–H0. This initial step increases the acidity of the N–H bond in the ligand, favoring its deprotonation and producing the RuIII–H1 species in agreement with our results. It is necessary to distinguish between two different experimental conditions. On one hand, in free solution with chemical oxidants, RuIII–H1 has been reported to undergo a disproportionation reaction with RuIII–H0, generating the RuIV–H2 intermediate. ,, On the other hand, in the proximity of the electrode interface, as in our experimental setup, disproportionation competes with subsequent electron transfer to the electrode. Because disproportionation is a bimolecular process, the low concentration of RuIII–H1 at the electrode surface disfavors this pathway, thereby favoring electron transfer to the electrode. For complex (1) at RuIII–H1 stage, the formation of RuII-monoimine species by a deprotonation of αC–H and 1e– transfer was suggested by DFT calculations (Figures a and S37). Further transformation to the final RuII-diimine product is expected to follow a similar reaction pathway. Both of these reactions occur fast, probably due to the stability of α,α′-diimine ligands formed, therefore, no other intermediates were experimentally captured.

The reactivity of complex (2) has also been discussed in the literature. The first report describes C–C bond cleavage occurring at high valence states of Ru (V–VI). In that study, cyclic voltammetry in an acidic aqueous media reveals a highly reversible couple at 0.75 V vs SCE, assigned to a RuV/II oxidation involving three electrons and protons; and a second couple at 1 V vs SCE, attributed to a RuVI/V couple, which is irreversible. Electrolysis at 0.85 V leads to the cleavage of C–C bond to form two separate imine groups. A subsequent study showed that oxidation in the same acidic conditions, but using CeIV as strong chemical oxidant, results in formation of the dinitroso compound without C–C bond cleavage. The oxidation with CeIV as a synthetic method to generate high-valent ruthenium species is quite common. However, some details differ between the two reports. Whereas one study informs C–C bond cleavage by electrolysis at the first oxidation couple (RuV/RuII), the other describes the oxidation at the nitrogen by accessing to the RuVI species and the further water nucleophilic attack to form two nitroso groups, but without C–C bond cleavage. Nevertheless, if the RuVI species is formed electrochemically at the second oxidation couple in water solution, the water nucleophilic attack should also be feasible and lead to the dinitroso final product but it is not the case. This inconsistency was explained in the literature by proposing that, when cerium­(IV) ammonium nitrate is used as oxidant, other cerium species present in solution may attack the imide group, forming the dinitroso compound at a rate comparable to or faster than the C–C bond cleavage process.

The preceding discussions suggested the 3e–/3H+ redox process in complex (2), which has some important considerations of the cyclic voltammetry results. A fully reversible, one-step three-electron transfer exhibiting a ΔE of 59 mV is highly unlikely. For this to occur, the intermediate species must undergo minimal structural changeslow reorganization energy and fast kineticsdespite the variation in charge. Nevertheless, Table shows that as the degree of oxidation and deprotonation increases, there are significant changes in the distances and angles of the bonds, requiring a high reorganization energy for the RuV/II couple to be accessible so easily, which should be reflected in a redox potential significantly higher than 0.75 V vs SCE and low electrochemical reversibility. An additional requirement would be exceptionally favorable solvation effects in aqueous media that stabilize the RuV species over RuIII or RuIV, implying a strong potential inversion, in disagreement with our calculations.

The experimental oxidation potential value for RuV–H3/RuII–H0 is similar to those reported by Guan et al. Still, they did not provide any discussion regarding the higher intermediates such as RuIII and RuIV that must be overcome first to reach this state. Figure S57 shows that the RuV–H3/RuIV–H2 couple in complex (2) has a lower potential than RuIII–H1/RuII–H0, which could be interpreted as a potential inversion and a direct step from RuII to RuV. However, this idea loses support when it is considered that the RuIV–H2/RuIII–H1 couple is at a higher potential and should overpass this step first. The lack of potential inversion rules out the possibility of a direct step from RuII to RuV.

The complex (2) undergoes cleavage of the C–C bond, but this process must originate from the deprotonated RuIII–H1 species rather than from RuV–H3. A cyclic voltammogram similar to that reported in the literature was obtained for this complex (Figure ). The spectroelectrochemical results, which has not been reported previously, identify the RuIII–H0 species as the product of the first oxidation step and, therefore, as an intermediate in the C–C bond cleavage process. In addition, the species with the cleaved C–C bond is formed upon the second irreversible oxidation step and is also the final product obtained by electrolysis at 900 mV (Figure S22d). Since RuIII–H1 is formed via deprotonation of RuIII–H0, further oxidation to RuIV–H1 is expected to be thermodynamically favorable, given that the RuIII–H0/RuII–H0 redox potential is higher than that of the RuIV–H1/RuIII–H1 couple (Scheme and Figure b). According to Figure b, the experimental UV–vis spectrum appears to correspond to a mixture of the RuIII–H1 and RuIV–H1 species or to the RuII-diimine final product. In the RuIV–H1, the increased demand for electron density from Ru shortens the Ru–Nsp2 distance, changes the dihedral angle (Table ) and enhances the steric tension imposed by the methyl groups, which drives the C–C bond cleavage and the formation of two imine groups after a second deprotonation. However, in complex (1), this tension is alleviated by rapid αC–H deprotonation.

Complex (3) accommodates an increase in the Nam–Ru–Nam angle to 111.5° (Table ) with better overlap and potential inversion (Table and Figure c). For comparison, complex (3) presents the higher RuIII–H1/RuII–H0 potential and the lowest RuIV–H2/RuIII–H1 potential among the three complexes (Figures S58 and S59, respectively). This compound can evolve directly to the more reactive RuIV–H2 species, supporting its high reactivity toward water nucleophilic attack and formation of N–O bonds, whose UV–vis spectrum is likely that observed in Figure S25a,b and is consistent with literature reports.

Thus, the DFT calculations support the identification of the experimentally observed RuIII–H0, further RuIII–H1, and RuIV–H2 intermediates. Therefore, the molecular orbital diagrams show that the interaction between the dπ-Ru/p-N orbitals increases with the degree of oxidation and deprotonation of the ligand, which is the main mechanism of stabilization of the higher valence intermediate species, as shown in the Supporting Information (Figures S39–S42).

The general interest in these compounds lies in the generation of high-valent species relevant to several catalytic processes such as water oxidation, nitrogen fixation, or C–H bond activation. The accessibility to different intermediates, reversibility, and efficient recovery of the catalyst are key aspects to evaluate. In oxidative catalysis, the reactive intermediate species should act as an electron-deficient reactant, capable of accepting e–, H• (e–/H+), or H– (2e–/H+) from the species to be activated. If some of the ligands taking part in the complexes are prone to react, the reaction takes place within the same ligand, becoming intramolecular rather than the desired intermolecular reactivity. A detailed understanding of the electronic structure and mechanistic pathways involved allows us to pursue modifications of the active catalysts, thereby protecting the final structure. In the oxidative dehydrogenation of ethylenediamine in coordination compounds, the reaction is intramolecular, producing an irreversible transformation of the molecular structure of the coordination compound.

It is essential to note that complexes with ethylenediamine ligands can reach high oxidation states if oxidative dehydrogenation of the ligand is prevented (Scheme ). According to the results of this study, once the metal reaches a high oxidation state, its increased electronic demand promotes the orbital overlap with the nitrogen of the ligand, facilitating the transfer of electron density from N to Ru, and as a result, nitrogen acquires electrophilic character. In Ru-oxo complexes, by contrast, the electrophilic character resides on oxygen, leading to differences and similarities in the reactivity between the two systems.

Proposed Mechanisms for Each Complex, Including All Relevant Intermediates and in Agreement with Our Experimental and Computational Findings

Conclusions

In this work, we explored access to high-valent species of ruthenium diamine and diammine complexes, their electronic structure, and the mechanism for the multiredox processes involved. The proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) reactivity of this family of complexes enabled a reevaluation of the ligand oxidation mechanism. We demonstrated that the overall 2e–/2H+ oxidation of the ligand in [Ru­(bpy)2en*]2+, complex (2), is initiated by formation of the RuIII–H1 intermediate through amine deprotonation, which stabilizes the accumulated charge and destabilizes the C–C bond. In acidic media, the first oxidation is attributed to the formation of RuIII–H0 species. In the presence of a base, one of the amines loses a proton, leading to strong Ru–N orbital interaction. Structural constraints restrict opening of the Nam–Ru–Nam angle, precluding effective orbital overlap within the Nam–Ru–Nam framework required for the subsequent deprotonation of the second amine. This is reflected in the lack of potential inversion and the inaccessibility to the RuIV–H2 intermediate. This analysis, extrapolated to complex (1), indicates that deprotonation of the RuIII–H1 species occurs at the αC–H position, enabling imine formation via a base-assisted mechanism. In addition, the lack of geometric constraints in complex (3) could allow the potential inversion and access to the RuIV–H2 intermediate, which subsequently undergoes nucleophilic attack by water. Thus, the intrinsic characteristics of the ligand, together with metal-centered distortions induced upon oxidation, dictate different reaction pathways. Complex (1) undergoes oxidative dehydrogenation via αC–H deprotonation to form a monoimine intermediate; complex (2) proceeds through C–C bond cleavage, and complex (3) forms the RuIV–H2 intermediate that undergoes nucleophilic attack by water to give rise to oxygenated N–O ligands.

This peculiar proton-coupled stabilization of electron transfer in amine-containing complexes shares features with Mn–OH2/Mn+2O complexes, − modulating multi-PCET reactivity and pointing to a rich landscape for the development of new multiredox catalysts for bond activation.
