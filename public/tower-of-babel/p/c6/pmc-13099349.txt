Prospective Cohort Study of Felzartamab in Rituximab-Resistant Primary Membranous Nephropathy
Authors: Matias Trillini, Federica Casiraghi, Alessia Gennarini, Valentina Portalupi, Maddalena Marasà, Manuel Alfredo Podestà, Silvia Prandini, Silvia Nozza, Marta Todeschini, Francesco Pecoraro, Marilena Mister, Fabiola Carrara, Nadia Stucchi, Tobia Peracchi, Diego Fidone, Alessandro Villa, Nadia Rubis, Olimpia Diadei, Davide Martinetti, Giuseppe Remuzzi, Piero Ruggenenti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionApproximately 30% of patients with primary membranous nephropathy (MN) and nephrotic syndrome (NS) fail rituximab treatment through mechanisms that could be overcome by the human IgG1 monoclonal anti-CD38 antibody, felzartamab.MethodsIn this prospective, single-arm, single-center, open-label trial, 10 consenting Caucasian adult patients with MN, rituximab-resistant NS, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (GFR) > 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2 received a 5-month, 9-dose course of 16 mg/kg felzartamab infusions at the Nephrology Unit of Bergamo Hospital, Italy between November 9, 2021 and February 1, 2023 and were followed up with for 24 months. Clinical and laboratory parameters were evaluated at baseline, 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 18, and 24 months, whereas GFR as well as albumin and IgG fractional clearances were measured at baseline and at 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months posttreatment. The primary outcome was 24-hour proteinuria (median of 3 consecutive measurements) at 12 months.ResultsTwelve-month 24-hour proteinuria was similar to baseline. Linear-mixed model analyses showed no significant time-dependent changes in 24-hour proteinuria and albuminuria; serum total-protein, albumin, creatinine and lipid levels, GFR and albumin fractional clearances. Circulating anti–phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antibodies transiently decreased but were never depleted. All considered Igs transiently decreased up to month 12, and recovered to baseline thereafter. Felzartamab deeply and persistently decreased natural killer (NK), B cells, and antigen-inexperienced transitional B cells; however, it did not affect CD20-expressing memory B cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cells. Treatment was safe and well-tolerated.ConclusionOne course of felzartamab was safe and well-tolerated, but ineffective in patients with MN and rituximab-resistant NS, possibly because of the inability to persistently deplete nephritogenic autoantibody-producing CD38-expressing B cells.

Primary MN is the most frequent primary glomerular disease associated with NS in adults.1 Persistent NS is associated with disabling and life-threatening complications, and is a major risk factor for end-stage kidney disease.1,2 For decades, treatment has been based on steroids and nonspecific immunosuppressive medications, such as alkylating agents and calcineurin inhibitors, which are associated with major systemic and renal toxicity.1

Following the pioneering report of the antiproteinuric effect that the first-in-class type 1 anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, rituximab, had in 8 patients with MN and persistent NS, despite optimized supportive therapy,3 evidence that autoimmune response against PLA2R and other glomerular antigens is the key pathogenic mechanism of MN4,5 drove several observational studies and randomized trials that confirmed the nephroprotective effect of rituximab and its favorable safety profile in this context.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Therefore, rituximab is currently recommended for the treatment of patients with MN with moderate or high risk of progressive loss of kidney function.11

The effect of this chimeric monoclonal IgG1 is predominantly mediated by its binding to CD20 on B lymphocytes, with consequent cell lysis through the activation of complement-dependent cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.12 However, in approximately 30% of treated patients, rituximab is not effective, in most cases because of too severe, irreversible structural kidney lesions.13 However, primary, biological resistance to treatment can be mediated by cell internalization and degradation of the CD20-rituximab complex12 with impaired cytotoxicity.1 In addition, the fully human type 1 anti-CD20 antibody ofatumumab is poorly effective in rituximab-resistant patients.14

Conceivably, in patients with MN refractory to CD20-targeted therapy, the production of nephritogenic autoantibodies might be sustained by plasma cells and plasmablasts that express high levels of CD38 but lack or have down-regulated CD20 expression.15, 16, 17, 18 Because CD38+CD20- long-lived plasma cells produce autoantibodies to PLA2R and other antigens,19 therapies targeting plasma cells and plasmablasts may reduce pathogenic autoantibodies and halt MN progression.

Felzartamab is an investigational, fully human IgG1 monoclonal anti-CD38 antibody that depletes plasma cells and plasmablasts through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis.20,21 It was initially developed to target malignant plasma cells,20 but a recent proof-of-concept phase 1b/2a open label, multicenter study tested its safety and efficacy in patients with newly diagnosed MN or in patients with MN who were refractory to nonspecific immunosuppressants such as steroids, cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus, and/or to the anti-CD20 antibody rituximab.22 Treatment was safe and well-tolerated; however, evidence about its efficacy was inconsistent, in particular in patients with a history of previous resistance to other immunosuppressive medications. Therefore, we formally tested the efficacy (and tolerability) of felzartamab treatment in the context of a prospective cohort trial in 10 consecutive patients with primary MN who were resistant to rituximab and other immunosuppressive medications.

Methods

In this pilot, phase 2, prospective, single-arm, open-label study (clinicaltrials.gov: NCT04893096; MONET EudraCT number: 2021-000835-30), we evaluated the response to felzartamab rescue therapy in rituximab-resistant adults with primary, biopsy-proven (within 6 months) MN, estimated GFR (using the Chronic Kidney Disease-Epidemiology Collaboration equation) ≥ 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2, and persistent nephrotic range proteinuria (median proteinuria > 3.5 g/24 h in 3 consecutive measurements) despite optimized conservative therapy with renin-angiotensin-system inhibitors without anti–B-cell antibody therapy for ≥12 months. Resistance to rituximab was defined as the failure to achieve complete remission (urinary protein excretion < 0.3 g/d [protein-to-creatinine ratio < 300 mg/g]) or partial remission (urinary protein excretion < 3.5 g/d or [protein-to-creatinine ratio < 3500 mg/g] with ≥50% reduction from baseline values) of the NS, with persistent hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin concentration < 3.5 g/dl). Patients with secondary forms of MN, exposure for ≥2 weeks to steroids and/or other immunosuppressants over the previous 6 months, concomitant diseases jeopardizing study completion or data interpretation, who were pregnant, breastfeeding, or noncompliant with effective contraception,23 were not included (study protocol in the Supplementary Material). Potentially eligible patients who were identified among the subjects referred to the outpatient clinics of the Nephrology Unit of the Azienda-Socio-Sanitaria-Territoriale Papa Giovanni XXIII in Bergamo, Italy were referred to the “Aldo and Cele Daccò” Clinical Research Center for Rare Disease of the Istituto di Ricerche Farmacologiche Mario Negri IRCCS in Ranica, Bergamo, Italy for eligibility evaluation. The study protocol was revised and approved by the Territorial Ethical Committee and by the Italian Agency of Medicines. All participants provided written informed consent in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were reported according to the STROBE guidelines.

Aims

We aimed to evaluate the reduction in 24-hour proteinuria (primary outcome) and the proportion of patients achieving the combined end point of complete or partial remission of NS (co–primary outcome) at 12 months after the first felzartamab infusion. Secondarily, the study evaluated time to progression to the combined end point or to complete remission considered as a single end point as well as time to relapse of NS after initial complete or partial remission along with time to complete anti-PLA2R antibody (antibody titer < 2 RU/ml24 measured on a clinical-grade assay using anti-PLA2R IgG enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, Euroimmun AG, Lubeck, Germany25) or anti-THSD7A (negative IIF test) depletion or time to antibody reemergence into the circulation after initial depletion over a 24-month follow-up period. In addition the study evaluated changes at 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 months of follow-up compared with baseline in blood pressure and body weight; 24-hour urinary protein and albumin excretion; serum creatinine and directly measured GFR (using the iohexol plasma clearance technique26); total, low-density lipoprotein and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglycerides levels; anti-PLA2R or anti-THSD7A antibody titer; the frequency of circulating T- and NK-cell subpopulations and of B-cell subsets, including plasmablasts and plasma cells; changes in patient-perceived quality of life assessed using the SF-36 questionnaire to calculate patient mental and physical component summaries; felzartamab serum levels and antidrug antibodies (ADAs). All parameters were centrally evaluated at the Clinical Laboratory of the “Aldo and Cele Daccò” Clinical Research Center.

We evaluated frequency, incidence, seriousness, relatedness, and severity of treatment-emergent adverse events, including infusion hypersensitivity reactions, or late reactions, including delayed serum-sickness-like syndrome that, independent of response to treatment, could preclude further exposure to the drug. Any serious and nonserious adverse event was monitored up to resolution, recorded, and reported.

Treatment and Follow-Up

After baseline evaluations, patients entered a 24-week treatment period and were maintained on active follow-up for 24 months after inclusion. A single course 9-dose regimen of felzartamab was administered at the Unit of Nephrology. Felzartamab was administered at a dose of 16 mg/kg through 5 initial weekly infusions (days 1, 8, 15, 22, and 29 after baseline) followed by 4 subsequent doses administered every 4 weeks (days 57, 85, 113, and 141 after baseline). All participants received a standard premedication, including the i.v. infusion of 10 mg of chlorphenamine and 16 mg of dexamethasone, along with the oral administration of 1000 mg of paracetamol. One hour after completion of the premedication protocol, 16 mg/kg of felzartamab—supplied by Human Immunology Biosciences, Inc (HI-Bio) as a lyophilized powder stored at 2 to 8 °C—was reconstituted in labelled glass vials and infused over approximately 2 hours for the first 2 infusions. If no reactions occurred, the infusion rate could be gradually increased during subsequent felzartamab administrations, provided the infusion time was never reduced to < 1 hour. Vital signs were monitored before, during, and for ≥2 hours after the infusion. An intensivist was always on call and equipment for resuscitation was available at the patient's bedside. The follow-up visits and laboratory evaluations (study protocol in the Supplementary Material) were performed at the Nephrology Unit at the time of each felzartamab infusion and at the “Aldo and Cele Daccò” Clinical Research Center at months 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 after inclusion. No systematic changes in ongoing supportive therapy were introduced throughout the whole study period.

Phenotyping of Circulating Lymphocyte Subpopulations

Phenotype of circulating lymphocytes was evaluated at baseline and at months 1, 5, 6, 9, 12, 18, and 24 after the first felzartamab administration. Counts of total CD19+ B cells, Helper CD4+ T cells, cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, and CD3-NK cells were evaluated according to standard protocols at Azienda Socio Sanitaria Territoriale - Papa Giovanni XXIII, whereas deep immunophenotype analyses were centralized at the “Aldo and Cele Daccò” Clinical Research Center.

Blood samples were collected in K2–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen until analysis. After thawing, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were stained with predefined fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibody panels designed for comprehensive identification of B-cell, NK-cell, and T-cell subsets.

Acquisition was performed on a BD FACS Fortessa X20 cytometer, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software, version 10.10.0 (BD Life Sciences, Ashland, OR). For B-cell phenotyping, CD19+CD20+ B cells were characterized based on CD38, CD24, IgD, and CD27 to delineate transitional, naïve, mature, and memory populations. CD19+CD20-negative plasmablasts and plasma cells were identified as CD20-CD27+/− cells expressing high levels of CD38 and/or CD138. NK cells were defined as CD3-CD16+/CD56+ lymphocytes and subdivided into cytotoxic CD56ˆdim and cytokine-producing CD56ˆbright subsets.

All immune cell subsets were expressed as percentages of live CD45+ lymphocytes, and CD38+ fractions were quantified relative to their parent populations. Detailed antibody panels, clone specifications, and full gating strategies are reported in the Supplementary Methods.

Felzartamab Pharmacokinetics and Immunogenicity

Serum samples for the measurement of pre- and post-infusion felzartamab levels were collected on days 1, 8, 15, 22, and 29 days after inclusion and, for the measurement of preinfusion levels only, also on days 57, 85, 113, and 141. Serum levels were measured after treatment withdrawal, at months 6, 9, and 12 postinclusion. Serum levels were measured using an electro-chemiluminescence immune-assay previously developed by MorphoSys AG and transferred to and validated at SGS France (SGS France Study Rep. no. CP185382 and Analyt Met. SOP no. 2421/MorphoSys AG Ref. no. MOR202L076 - 2020).

Serum samples for the measurement of ADA levels were collected on days 1, 57, 113, 141, and at months 6, 9, and 12. ADA levels were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay system previously validated at SGS France (SGS France Analytic Study Plan Ref. no. CP185383 and Analytic Met. SOP No. 2483/MorphoSys AG Ref. no. MOR202L077 - 2020).

Statistical Analyses and Sample Size Estimation

All the analyses were performed at the Unit of Biostatistics of the “Aldo and Cele Daccò” Clinical Research Center. Continuous variables were described through descriptive statistics reported as mean ± SD for normally distributed data, or as median (Q1–Q3) for skewed data. The assumption of normality for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots. Categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages (n, %). The primary end point, defined as the reduction in proteinuria at 12 months, was evaluated using the paired t test. We described the proportion of patients achieving partial or complete remission at 12 and 24 months, reported as n (%). Survival was presented using a Kaplan-Meier curve. Comparisons of follow-up versus baseline values for continuous variables were performed using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, respectively.

To evaluate overall longitudinal changes in biomarkers over time, an exploratory analysis was performed. Linear mixed-effects models were then used, including patient as a random effect to account for intrasubject correlation. An unstructured covariance matrix was applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). All P-values were 2-sided, with a significance threshold set at 0.05.

The primary efficacy variable of the study was the absolute change in 24-hour proteinuria at 12 months compared with baseline. Based on data in patients referred to our center who previously failed anti-CD20 antibody therapy, we predicted a mean (SD) proteinuria of 10.93 ± 2.72 g/24 h at inclusion and hypothesized a 40% reduction to 6.56 g/24 h at 12 months. Thus, 7 participants were required to provide intrapatient comparisons with 90% power to detect as statistically significant (alpha = 0.05, paired t test, 2-tailed test), the reduction in proteinuria from 10.93 to 6.56 g/24 h. In total, 8 participants had to be included to account for a 10% drop-out rate.

Results

Twelve patients were screened, but 2 did not fulfill the selection criteria (Supplementary Figure S1). The remaining planned 10 participants were included between November 9, 2021, and February 1, 2023. They were all adults (age range: 23–75 years), Caucasian (7 males), with biopsy-proven MN and had nephrotic-range proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, dyslipidemia, and a measured GFR between 21.17 and 92.76 ml/min per 1.73 m2. Time to diagnosis averaged 14.1 years. Seven patients had PLA2R-related disease. At inclusion, 9 patients had hypogammaglobulinemia (serum total IgG concentration < 751 mg/dl). All the participants were on background treatment with an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor and/or an angiotensin 2 receptor blocker, and statins in most cases (Supplementary Table S1). Before felzartamab administration, 6, 4, 3, 2, and 1 participants had received treatment with steroids, alkylating agents, apheresis, calcineurin inhibitors, and mofetil mycophenolate, respectively, in monotherapy or in different combinations. Three participants had received 1 ofatumumab infusion, and 1 additional participant had received 3 infusions of daratumumab, all without benefit (Table 1). Overall, participants had received 23 rituximab infusions (1–6/patient). The last monoclonal antibody treatment preceded the first felzartamab administration by a median (interquartile range) of 33.75 (17.26–52.03) months (Table 1). One patient was withdrawn from the study immediately after the first felzartamab administration because of a SARS-CoV-2 infection that fully recovered without sequelae. Therefore, 9 patients completed 24 months of follow-up (Supplementary Figure S1). Primary and Coprimary Outcomes

Twenty-four-hour proteinuria did not change appreciably at 12 months compared with baseline (Table 2). No significant change in proteinuria was observed at any considered posttreatment time point up to month 24 compared with baseline (Figure 1, Panel a). Single patient trajectories of 24-hour proteinuria over 24-month follow-up in patients with or without PLA2R-related disease were superimposable (Supplementary Figure S2, Panel a). Felzartamab Pharmacokinetics and Immunogenicity

Pre- and post-infusion serum felzartamab concentration progressively increased during the 4 initial weekly administrations from day 1 to day 29 postinclusion. Then, preinfusion levels progressively decreased during the subsequent 5 monthly administrations from day 29 to day 141. Drug levels were virtually undetectable on subsequent evaluations (Supplementary Figure S6). Circulating ADAs were found in 1 asymptomatic patient at months 9 and 12 of follow-up.

Discussion

In this prospective, single-arm cohort study, felzartamab was safe and well-tolerated in 10 patients with MN with refractory NS, but failed to achieve significant 24-hour proteinuria reduction at 12 months of follow-up compared with baseline, the primary study outcome. Three patients achieved the coprimary combined end point of complete or partial remission: 2 of these patients, however, relapsed on subsequent follow-up, whereas the third patient achieved the combined end point just at study end (24 months of follow-up), and his subsequent outcome was not known. No patient achieved complete remission. No appreciable effects of felzartamab on proteinuria; albuminuria; as well as serum albumin, total-protein, and lipid levels considered as continuous variables was observed. Individual trajectories of 24-hour proteinuria and serum albumin levels during the whole 24-month observation period were superimposable in patients with or without PLA2R-related disease and confirmed treatment failure also at the individual level. Failure to detect any change in albumin and IgG fractional clearances provided additional evidence that felzartamab treatment was unable to improve glomerular sieving function.

In the 7 patients with PLA2R-related disease, felzartamab treatment induced a significant reduction in anti-PLA2R titer up to 5 months of follow-up—with transient antibody depletion in 1 patient—then treatment effect progressively waned until study end. Conceivably, these antibodies continued to fuel the formation of immunocomplexes on the glomerular barrier, with consequent, continued glomerular injury resulting in persistent proteinuria. Plausibly, similar mechanisms were involved in PLA2R-negative cases sustained by other nonidentified nephritogenic autoantibodies.

Transient reduction in the titer of PLA2R and all considered Igs, including different IgG subtypes, could be explained by the modest depleting effect of felzartamab on B cells. Felzartamab failed to affect CD20-expressing memory B cells whereas it significantly depleted antigen-inexperienced transitional B cells.27 CD20-negative B cells expressing CD38, which include plasmablasts and plasma cells, were only transiently depleted by felzartamab. The effect, however, was transient and CD38+ B cells as well as autoantibody levels recovered to baseline levels at 9 months of follow-up.

Consistent with previous evidence in antibody-mediated rejection,28 felzartamab mostly affected circulating NK cells. The observation that it preferentially bound to, and induced profound depletion of CD56dim NK cells may help explain its limited efficacy on B cells. This preferential binding could have reduced the amount of the anti-CD38 antibody available for targeting B cells; alternatively, CD56dim NK cells depletion could have contributed to impair the primary mechanism of action of the drug that is largely mediated by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.29

Our present findings are in harmony with those of the phase 1b/2a M-PLACE proof-of-concept study22 that assessed the safety and efficacy of felzartamab in patients with high risk PLA2R-related MN; because of the high anti-PLA2R antibody titer at inclusion. Patients were categorized into 2 groups with newly diagnosed or relapsing MN (cohort 1) or refractory MN (cohort 2). In cohort 2, as in our present study, patients had been reported to be refractory to different immunosuppressive medications in different combinations, including rituximab. Consistent with our present findings, treatment was safe and well-tolerated, never achieved complete remission of the NS, and achieved partial NS remission in only 2 of the 11 patients with refractory MN.

Notably, changes in preinfusion felzartamab levels appeared to be specular compared with changes in all considered serum Ig levels and in NK cell counts. The effects in the aforementioned parameters appeared to peak during the early intensified treatment period and progressively waned from month 6 to study end following treatment withdrawal and progressive disappearance of felzartamab from the circulation.

Therefore, in patients with MN and refractory NS, felzartamab is poorly effective or not effective at all. In our 7 patients with PLA2R-related disease and in cohort 2 patients of the M-PLACE study,22 felzartamab failed to achieve complete and persistent depletion of circulating anti-PLA2R antibodies, conceivably because felzartamab failed to deplete autoreactive antibody-producing plasma cells. Whether this limitation could be overcome by more intensive or repeated felzartamab dosing may merit further investigation. Alternatively, patients with MN with refractory NS might benefit from combined or sequential anti-CD38 and anti-CD20 antibody treatment,22 as suggested by evidence that rituximab treatment following the administration of the anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody daratumumab achieved partial remission of the NS in a patient with refractory MN.30

A further mechanistic explanation for the limited therapeutic response may lie in the compartmentalization of autoreactive plasma cells within tissue niches that are poorly accessible to circulating anti-CD38 therapies. Long-lived plasma cells preferentially reside in survival-permissive microenvironments such as the bone marrow31 and inflamed renal interstitium,32 where physical barriers, reduced drug penetration, and local prosurvival cytokines may reduce the vulnerability of these cells to CD38-targeted depletion.33 In addition, antibody-mediated cytotoxicity induced by felzartamab may be suboptimal in such compartments because of low effector-to-target cell ratios or reduced NK cell recruitment. The marked and preferential binding of felzartamab to circulating CD56dim NK cells observed in our cohort, together with their profound depletion, may further compromise antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity activity in tissue sites, thus limiting efficacy against CD38+ plasmablasts and plasma cells.

Future studies integrating posttreatment kidney biopsies, bone marrow aspirates, or high-parameter spatial immunophenotyping could provide crucial insights into the localization, phenotype, and drug susceptibility of autoreactive B-cell populations. Such investigations may help refine therapeutic strategies to more effectively address therapy-resistant MN; for instance, through more intensive CD38 inhibition, combination regimens, or agents targeting plasma-cell survival pathways.

Safety

Treatment was safe and well-tolerated in all patients. Infusion reactions were mild, transient, and confined to the first felzartamab administration. In the unique patient found to develop ADAs, no treatment-related immune-mediated adverse event was reported. Hypogammaglobulinemia was not an issue, despite total IgG and IgG1 to IgG4 subclasses reduction. This might imply that felzartamab does not impair humoral memory immune response in this population. This could also explain why no opportunistic infections were observed during the study, despite deep and persistent NK cell depletion. The maintenance of the earlier B-cell compartment could explain the reassuring capability of patients with MN to have a positive humoral response to SARS-CoV-2 despite felzartamab treatment,30 as suggested by the full and spontaneous recovery from SARS-CoV-2 infection in 1 patient of our study cohort.

Limitations and Strengths

This was a relatively small, single-center, cohort study and the effect of felzartamab was not tested in the context of a randomized controlled trial. Moreover, the wide variability of individual outcomes further affected the study's statistical power to detect a significant treatment effect, if any. Thus, our results are hypothesis-generating, and future studies should aim for larger, possibly multicenter cohorts to enhance generalizability and statistical robustness of the study findings. However, because of the major difficulties in performing an adequately powered prospective randomized controlled trial in this rare treatment-resistant form of primary MN, future studies could include carefully selected historical or matched real-world comparators possibly followed-up with for an even longer observational period.

Patients were exposed to a single course 9-dose regimen. Whether a second similar course could result in a more sustained clinical response was not evaluated. Indeed, independent of different individual outcomes, our present study provided important insight into disease mechanisms that could support future trials testing combination or sequential anti-CD38 and anti-CD20 regimens (including obinutuzumab), particularly in patients unresponsive to rituximab.34 Finding that median time from the last monoclonal antibody infusion and the first felzartamab administration approximated 3 years definitely excluded the possibility of a carry-over effect confounding the response to felzartamab therapy to any appreciable extent. The number of study patients was too small for any potentially informative analysis of the relationships (if any) between baseline levels and time-dependent changes in key clinical outcomes (24-hour proteinuria and serum albumin concentration) and between baseline levels and concomitant time-dependent changes in anti-PLA2R antibody titers in the cohort with PLA2R-related disease. Outcomes of those with or without PLA2R-related disease appeared to be virtually superimposable. The prospective cohort design, with the inclusion of rigorously selected participants who were treated and monitored with a predefined, standardized protocol based on gold standard procedures, such as serial (triplicate) measurements of 24-hour proteinuria and direct measurement of true GFR,26 was a major strength. GFR measurement allowed to evaluate the sieving function of the glomerular barrier with the calculation of albumin and IgG fractional clearances. All measurements were centralized, minimizing variability due to measurement fluctuations. Despite the small sample size, results at 12 and 24 months were consistent. Linear mixed models showed no statistically significant change over time in any of the clinical parameters assessed. Finally, the number of patients completing the study (n = 9) exceeded the a priori estimated sample size (n = 7) required to achieve 90% power to detect a treatment effect on the primary outcome of the study.

Conclusion

The anti-CD38 antibody, felzartamab was safe and well-tolerated but failed to affect proteinuria in patients with MN with refractory NS. Treatment failure could be explained by treatment inability to fully and persistently deplete nephritogenic autoantibody producing CD38-expressing B cells. Whether an intensified or second felzartamab treatment course and/or combination therapy with an anti-CD20 antibody might achieve a more sustained clinical response is worth investigating.
