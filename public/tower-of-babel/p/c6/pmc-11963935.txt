Relative Cerebral Blood Flow as an Indirect Imaging Surrogate in Patients With Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusion and Association of Baseline Characteristics With Poor Collateral Status
Authors: Hamza Adel Salim, Omar Hamam, Goksu Parilday, Rawan A. Moustafa, Samir Ghandour, Moustafa Rutgers, Muhanned Sharara, Andrew Cho, Ishan Mazumdar, Mahla Radmard, Christopher Shin, Daniel Montes, Ajay Malhotra, Javier M. Romero, Vivek Yedavalli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn acute ischemic stroke (AIS), collateral status (CS) is an important predictor of favorable outcomes in patients with AIS. Among quantitative cerebral perfusion parameters, relative cerebral blood flow (rCBF) is considered an accurate perfusion‐based indicator of CS. This study investigated the relationship between admission laboratory values, baseline characteristics, and CS as assessed by rCBF in patients with AIS‐large vessel occlusion.Methods and ResultsIn this retrospective multicenter study, consecutive patients presenting with AIS secondary to anterior circulation large vessel occlusion who underwent pretreatment computed tomography perfusion were included. The computed tomography perfusion data processed by RAPID (IschemaView, Menlo Park, CA) generated the rCBF. Binary logistic regression models assessed the relationship between patients' baseline characteristics, admission laboratory values, and poor CS. The primary outcome measure was the presence of poor CS, which was defined as rCBF <38% at a lesion size ≥27 mL. Between January 2017 and September 2022, there were 221 consecutive patients with AIS‐large vessel occlusion included in our study (mean age 67.0±15.8 years, 119 men [53.8%]). Logistic regression showed that male sex (odds ratio [OR], 2.98 [1.59–5.59]; P=0.001), chronic kidney disease (OR, 5.18 [2.44–11.0]; P<0.001), admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥12 (OR, 5.17 [2.36–11.36]; P<0.001), and systolic blood pressure <140 (OR, 2.00 [1.07–3.76]; P=0.030) were associated with poor CS.ConclusionsHigher stroke severity on admission with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥12, systolic blood pressure <140, chronic kidney disease, and male sex are statistically significantly associated with poor CS in patients with AIS due to anterior circulation large vessel occlusion as defined by rCBF <38%.

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) has a high recurrence rate with an elevated global burden. 1 Collateral status (CS) is a significant predictor of radiologic and clinical outcomes in patients with AIS due to anterior circulation occlusions. Collateral circulation refers to the supplementary network of blood vessels that compensate for blood flow and mitigate the detrimental effect of ischemia. 2 Collateral flow recruitment is crucial for maintaining regional cerebral blood flow in the event of a decrease in cerebral perfusion pressure or failure of cerebral autoregulation. 3 Good collateral circulation reduces recurrent strokes and has a protective effect toward a favorable functional outcome. 3 , 4 CS maintains perfusion in the ischemic region, thereby increasing regional cerebral blood flow, saving more extensive penumbral tissue, resulting in a smaller final infarction volume and improving functional outcomes. 5 , 6

Multiple qualitative scales have been used to evaluate the CS on computed tomography angiography (CTA) or digital subtraction angiography. Digital subtraction angiography is considered the gold standard for collateral evaluation, but clinical limitations exist. 7 , 8 Computed tomography perfusion (CTP) is crucial for guiding intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy. Evaluation of cerebral flow compensation and collateral circulation distal to chronic cerebral artery stenosis is essential for screening patients with symptomatic cerebral artery stenosis for revascularization therapy. 9 , 10

Due to significantly improved outcomes, mechanical thrombectomy has replaced intravenous tissue plasminogen activator as the therapy of choice for large vessel occlusions (LVOs). 11 CS plays a crucial part in the pathophysiology of cerebral ischemia. It is assumed that inadequate CS increases the recruitment of ischemic penumbra into infarction, resulting in a bigger infarct size; nevertheless, the actual mechanism underlying this link remains unknown. 12 , 13 M1 and M2, the first and second segments of the middle cerebral artery, respectively, are grounded in the anatomical and clinical significance of AIS. 14 , 15 The M1 segment, being proximal, typically involves larger ischemic volumes and potentially more severe clinical presentations due to its larger territory supply. In contrast, occlusions in the M2 segment might present with more variable and sometimes less‐severe outcomes, given the smaller and more variable vascular territory involved.

Among the cerebral perfusion parameters, cerebral blood flow measures blood flow velocity and represents the degree of brain tissue blood flow compensation. 16 , 17 Multiple studies have reported that the relative cerebral blood flow (rCBF) combined with the hypoperfusion intensity ratio can evaluate the condition of the collaterals (16–18). 18 , 19 , 20 In other studies, the rCBF volume was considered an accurate perfusion‐based indicator of the CS. 18 , 20 , 21 It has been established that rCBF <38% serves as a superior indicator of robust CS, outperforming other pretreatment CTP parameters. 18 , 20 , 21 Moreover, Potreck et al identified that an rCBF <38% at a lesion size of ≥27 mL is the optimal threshold for predicting poor CS. 20

The potential relationship of collateral flow with a history of cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension, congestive heart failure, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes, might exhibit complex interactions. In addition, poor collateral flow has been found to be associated with metabolic syndrome and age in patients with AIS. 22 Other risk factors associated with AIS include age, sex, smoking, and kidney disease. Multiple studies have shown an association between baseline characteristics and CS. 7 , 23 , 24

To our knowledge, no studies have described the association of baseline clinical characteristics with poor CS based on rCBF and their impact on the pathophysiology of AIS. In this study, we aimed to investigate the association of clinical characteristics with poor CS as defined by rCBF <38% in patients with AIS.

METHODS

Population and Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at 2 comprehensive stroke centers within the Johns Hopkins Medical Enterprise: Johns Hopkins Hospital–East Baltimore and Bayview Medical Campus. The study was approved by the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (JHU‐IRB00269637) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations. From January 1, 2017 to September 1, 2022, consecutive patients with anterior circulation LVO were included. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All of the patients who underwent baseline CTA and CTP for their stroke management were included. Anterior circulation LVO was defined as an occlusion of the (intracranial internal carotid artery), M1, or proximal M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery. 25

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were 18 to 85 years of age, of either sex, had acute ischemic stroke of anterior circulation LVO, and presented within 24 hours of symptom onset. Other eligibility criteria included no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage on neuroimaging, as previously described. 26 Patients were ineligible if they had evidence of intracerebral hemorrhage on initial imaging, midline shift, clinical signs of herniation, mass effect, acute bilateral strokes, multiple intracranial occlusions, or unavailable or nondiagnostic CTP. Patients with terminal internal carotid artery occlusions were included in the overall analysis but excluded from the subgroup analysis, which only stratifies for M1 and M2 occlusions due to the low number of internal carotid artery cases (n=5).

Technical Parameters

Baseline comprehensive computed tomography imaging was performed at the Johns Hopkins East Baltimore and Bayview Medical Centers using helical scanners on the Siemens Flash and/or Drive (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). For noncontrast computed tomography we used helical mode at 5 mm slice thickness, 120 kVp, 365 mAs, rotation time 1 second, acquisition time 6 to 8 seconds, collimation 128 × 0.6 mm, pitch value 0.55, scan direction craniocaudal. For CTP we used injection of 50 mL nonionic iodinated contrast with 30 mL saline flush at 5 to 6 mL/s with coverage of 70 to 100 mm at 5 mm slice thickness. CTP parameters were 70 kVp, 200 effective mAs, rotation time 0.25 seconds, average acquisition time 60 seconds, collimation 48×1.2 mm, pitch value 0.7, 4‐dimensional range 114 mm×1.5 seconds. CTP images were then postprocessed using RAPID commercial software (IschemaView, Menlo Park, CA) for generating Tmax maps. For CTA head and neck we used nonionic iodinated contrast with 50 to 70 mL injected at 5 to 6 mL/s from the aortic arch through the vertex using a bolus‐triggered method at 3 mm slice thickness. CTA parameters were 90/150 kVp with an Sn filter, quality reference mAs 180, rotation time 0.25 seconds, average acquisition time 3 to 5 seconds, collimation 128×0.6 mm, pitch value 0.7, scan direction craniocaudal. All imaging was reviewed for central adjudication of ASPECTS (Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score) values, occlusion location, and perfusion measures.

Data Collection

The baseline and clinical data for each patient were collected with the help of a manual chart review. The variables collected for each patient included patient demographics, body mass index, and admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score. The admission laboratory values included the following: hemoglobin level, hematocrit, white blood cell count, platelet count, random blood glucose level, blood urea nitrogen level, and creatinine level. Admission vitals included the following: blood pressure (systolic blood pressure [SBP] and diastolic blood pressure), heart rate, respiratory rate, and ASPECTS scores on noncontrast computed tomography calculated by a board‐certified neuroradiologist (V.Y., 6 years of experience). The CS was quantified using the rCBF and measured using the RAPID commercial software platform (IschemaView, Menlo Park, CA) after postprocessing the CTP images. The rCBF values were dichotomized into poor CS and good CS. Good CS was better identified by rCBF <38% at a lesion size <27 mL (sensitivity of 75%, specificity of 80%), and poor CS was defined as rCBF <38% at a lesion size ≥27 mL. 27 Stroke causes were classified according to TOAST (Trial of Org 10 172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria, 28 , 29 with the following designations: (1) large‐artery atherosclerosis, (2) cardioembolism, (3) small‐vessel occlusion, (4) stroke of other determined cause, and (5) stroke of undetermined cause. 28 Cryptogenic stroke was defined as a combination of categories 4 and 5. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was the presence of poor CS, which was defined as rCBF <38% at a lesion size ≥27 mL. 30

Statistical Analysis

The data of the current evidence were collected on a secure desktop using Microsoft Office Excel 2007 (Redmond, WA) and analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics (version 22.0; Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median and interquartile range based on the distribution of variables. Normality for all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Quantitative data were compared using a 2‐factor ANOVA test or the independent t test. Categorical and nominal data were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Univariate analysis was initially applied to examine each of the baseline variables independently. Bonferroni corrections were applied for post hoc subgroup analysis to reduce the risk of type 1 errors. When appropriate, binary logistic regression was used to examine the association between different variables and poor CS. All P values were 2‐sided, and the P value of <0.05 was statistically significant unless multiple comparisons were made, for which Bonferroni correction was applied.

For multivariable logistic regression models, clinical relevance variables and variables with a P value <0.1 in univariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models. The final model was determined using a backward stepwise removal method, where variables were sequentially removed based on the highest P value until only significant variables remained (P<0.05).

RESULTS

Patients' Demographics of Anterior Circulation According to CS

A total of 470 consecutive patients were reviewed, of whom 249 (53%) were excluded because (1) CTP was nondiagnostic for patients (n=13), (2) no LVO was detected on CTA (n=179), or (3) no CTP was performed because of either an NIHSS score <6 (n=13) or ≥6 (n=44) at the time of admission. For NIHSS <6, computed tomography is not recommended; however, for NIHSS ≥6, CTP is recommended, but in our study the primary reasons for not performing CTP in such patients were the presence of other critical pathologies, such as intracranial hemorrhage, which were prioritized over AIS. Of the 221 included patients, 119 (53.8%) were men and 102 (46.2%) were women. The mean age of the patients was 67.0±15.8 years. Based on rCBF, 94 patients (94/221, 42.6%) had poor CS and 127 patients (127/221, 57.4%) had good CS. The flow diagram of the study design is shown in the Figure.

Univariate Analysis of Patient Demographics

Univariate analysis showed that patients with poor CS were likely men (poor CS, 53 [56.4%]; good CS, 49 [38.6%]; P=0.009). In addition, patients with poor CS had a higher likelihood of having a history of chronic kidney disease (CKD) (poor CS, 31 [32.9%]; good CS, 11 [8.6%]; P=0.017), experienced more severe stroke NIHSS score on admission (poor CS, 17.4±6.1; good CS, 13.6±7.3; P<0.001), lower SBP (poor CS, 145.4±24.6; good CS, 154.3±28.7; P=0.017), higher hemoglobin (poor CS, 13.3±2.1; good CS, 12.6±2.0; P<0.020), and higher hematocrit (poor CS, 40.6±5.5; good CS, 39.0±5.6; P<0.034), as shown in Table 1. In the M1 segment subgroup analysis, a significantly higher proportion of male patients had poor CS (poor CS, 41 [56.2%]; good CS, 36 [37.5%]; P=0.016). Patients with poor CS had a high likelihood of a history of CKD (poor CS, 23 [24.0%]; good CS, 7 [9.6%]; P=0.015). In addition, there were significant differences in SBP (poor CS, 144.4±25.1; good CS, 154.4±28.4; P=0.016), hemoglobin (poor CS, 13.4±2.2; good CS, 12.5±2.0; P=0.011), hematocrit (poor CS, 41.0±5.7; good CS, 38.8±5.7; P=0.013). In addition, patients who presented with more severe stroke on admission NIHSS score (poor CS, 18.2±5.9, good CS, 13.8±7.5; P<0.001) and reduced last known well to door (minutes) were associated with poor CS (poor CS, 383±561.8; good CS, 495±540.1; P=0.018), as shown in Table 2.

DISCUSSION

CS is a key factor in determining both radiologic and clinical outcomes for patients with AIS due to its role in maintaining cerebral blood flow, reducing stroke recurrence, and improving functional outcomes. 1 , 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 Our multicenter retrospective study aimed to investigate the association of clinical characteristics with poor CS, as defined by rCBF <38% at a lesion size of 27 mL, in anterior circulation LVO. We stratified our analysis based on the occlusion site, M1 versus M2 segments, to evaluate how these anatomical differences affect CS and subsequent clinical outcomes.

We observed that poor CS is associated with admission NIHSS score ≥12, SBP <140, CKD, and male sex in patients with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO. In addition, in the M1 subgroup analysis, alcohol and anticoagulation were associated with poor CS.

Our data indicate that a shorter time from last known well to door was significantly associated with poor CS, with the mean time for the poor CS group being 383±561.8 minutes compared with 495±540.1 minutes for the good CS group (P=0.018). This could be explained because shorter last known well to door times may indicate more severe initial strokes, leading to quicker detection and more rapid activation of emergency medical services, which correlates with higher initial stroke severity and, consequently, poorer CS.

Consistent with previous research, we discovered that the group with a favorable prognosis had a lower admission NIHSS score and better collateral circulation. 31 The admission NIHSS score is an essential scale for assessing the neurological impairment and prognosis of stroke patients, and it has been widely implemented in clinical settings. Admission NIHSS score is correlated with CS and perfusion deficit severity. 31 , 32 In patients with AIS, a low NIHSS score at admission was reported to be an independent predictor of favorable outcomes. 33 A high NIHSS score is associated with large ischemia, which is often caused by the large thrombus‐organized occlusion of a large artery. 34 A significant association between admission NIHSS score and poor CS has been well documented by multiple studies. 31 , 32 , 35 , 36

Our results indicated that SBP <140 was an independent predictor of poor CS, with a significant correlation with poor CS in all anterior circulation and M1 subgroups. Contrary to the findings of previous studies, in which high SBP was reported to be associated with worse clinical outcomes in patients with AIS, our findings showed no such association. 37 Kim et al have found that patients with noncardioembolic stroke have higher baseline SBP than patients with embolic stroke. 38 In another study, low SBP(<130 mm Hg) was associated with hemodynamic deterioration. Uncertainty surrounds the precise cause of the diminished hemodynamic improvement in patients with low SBP. Nevertheless, low SBP may inhibit the development of collateral flow. 39 Multiple studies have shown a U‐shaped relationship between blood pressure and stroke outcome. In the International Stroke Trial, patients with SBPs <120 mm Hg and >200 mm Hg had a poorer prognosis. There appeared to be a sweet spot of optimal SBP at ≈150 mm Hg, where the risk of death within 14 days or death or dependency at 6 months was minimal. 40 The inconclusive effect of SBP is likely attributable to heterogeneous patient populations and other confounding variables, such as baseline blood pressure, timing of measurement and intervention, cerebral autoregulation, and recanalization status. 41

In line with previous studies, male sex was associated with poor CS in our study. Globally, more strokes are reported among men. 42 It has been hypothesized that biological mechanisms of cell death in the ischemic brain are partially influenced by biological sex and partially influenced by the availability of female and male sex steroids before or after injury. These hormones contribute to sex‐specific responses to cerebral ischemia but do not fully account for them. 43 It has been hypothesized that several agents or mechanisms, including steroid and sex hormones, sex chromosomes, cell death differences, immune pathways, epigenetic regulation, and sex‐specific microRNAs contribute to sex differences in stroke. 44 Smoking, alcohol consumption, atherosclerosis of large vessels, and peripheral vascular disease are known to be more prevalent in men with stroke than in women with stroke. These factors cause premature vessel aging and impaired autoregulation, which may account for men's lower collateral scores. 45

In addition, CKD was observed to be associated with poor CS. CKD is associated with a high risk of strokes. Impairments in kidney function can lead to disturbances in the regulation of blood flow in organs such as the brain that depend on constant and adequate blood flow. 46 The brain and kidney share common traditional cardiovascular risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, which can lead to vascular injuries in both organs. 47 Impairments in kidney function can lead to disturbances in the regulation of blood flow in organs such as the brain that depend on constant and adequate blood flow. 48

Moreover, in the M1 subgroup, anticoagulation was associated with poor CS. This finding may not directly indicate the effect of anticoagulants on CS but may rather reflect the characteristics of patients using them. Typically, individuals prescribed anticoagulants are older and have a higher rate of comorbidities, which may indicate poorer overall vascular health and subsequently poorer CS. 49 , 50 , 51

Interestingly, the univariate analysis indicated a higher, yet nonsignificant, percentage of alcohol consumption in the poor CS group (44.6% versus 18.1%; P=0.17). However, the multivariable analysis identified alcohol consumption as a significant protective factor against poor CS after adjusting for potential confounders (OR, 0.38 [95% CI, 0.15–0.95]; P=0.038). The protective effect of alcohol might be attributed to the anti‐inflammatory properties of alcohol, which could facilitate a healthier angiogenesis process, and the vasodilatory effect of alcohol, which might further enhance collateral vessel development. This observation is particularly important because it can partially explain the J‐shaped association between alcohol consumption and stroke risk, where moderate alcohol consumption is associated with a decreased risk of stroke. 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 Our finding of the association between alcohol intake and stroke is consistent with large epidemiologic studies. 60

In our study, although we observed a significant association between multiple clinical parameters and CS in the overall cohort and within the M1 occlusion subgroup, similar associations were not found in the proximal M2 occlusion subgroup. This differential effect may be attributed to the smaller sample size in the M2 subgroup, which likely resulted in reduced statistical power to detect significant associations. The variability in the vascular territories of the M2 segment compared with the more consistent and larger territory of the M1 segment may also contribute to these differences.

Our study has several limitations. The retrospective design may introduce bias. The relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power of our findings. Moreover, the accessibility of CTP scanning, predominantly available at comprehensive stroke centers, may not be uniformly available in smaller, rural medical facilities or community hospitals, which may also limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, our analysis was conducted using a single software platform, which might further restrict the generalizability of our results across different technological settings. Furthermore, we did not assess the patency of the circle of Willis and the status of Anterior cerebral artery occlusions, which could influence CS during middle cerebral artery occlusions. Future research should address these variables to enhance the understanding of CS determinants.

Despite these limitations, our study is strengthened by its incorporation of data from 2 comprehensive stroke centers, encompassing 3 centers in total. This aspect of the study design allowed for the inclusion of diverse patient demographics, thereby enhancing the robustness of our results. Future research endeavors, ideally involving larger patient cohorts and a broader spectrum of health care settings, are essential to validate and potentially amplify the significance of our findings in the realm of stroke treatment and prognosis.

CONCLUSIONS

Higher stroke severity on admission with NIHSS score ≥12, SBP <140, CKD, and male sex are statistically significantly associated with poor CS in patients with AIS due to anterior circulation LVO as defined by rCBF <38% at a lesion size ≥27 mL. These findings are more pronounced in M1 occlusions compared with internal carotid artery and M2 occlusions.
