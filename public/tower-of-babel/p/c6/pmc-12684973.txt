SpaTM: topic models for inferring spatially informed transcriptional programs
Authors: Adrien Osakwe, Wenqi Dong, Qihuang Zhang, Robert Sladek, Yue Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial transcriptomics enables the contextualization of gene expression with spatial organization, advancing our understanding of development, disease, and tissue architecture. However, existing analysis pipelines require multiple tools to explore spatial domains, and few methods can jointly analyse spatial data from annotation-free and annotation-guided perspectives with high interpretability. We therefore propose the Spatial Topic Model (SpaTM), a topic-modelling framework capable of annotation-guided and annotation-free analysis of spatial transcriptomes. SpaTM can learn gene programs that represent histology-based annotations while also inferring spatial domains with an annotation-free approach if manual annotations are limited or noisy. In benchmarking experiments, SpaTM achieves competitive performance at spatial label prediction and clustering when compared with existing state-of-the-art methods. We demonstrate SpaTM’s interpretability by using topic mixtures to capture transcriptional programs in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and ductal carcinoma samples and show how its intuitive framework facilitates the integration of spatial transcriptomics tasks. Finally, we showcase how SpaTM can extend the analysis of large-scale snRNA-seq atlases in human brains with Major Depressive Disorder. Overall, SpaTM provides a unified and interpretable analysis framework for spatial transcriptomics, enabling competitive performance in multiple tasks while inferring biologically informed gene programs.Keywords: spatial transcriptomics, topic modelling, Bayesian inference, spatial segmentation, neighbour prediction

Introduction

Advances in spatial transcriptomics (ST) have made it possible to reliably profile spatially oriented gene expression throughout the transcriptome. The resulting datasets have enabled novel discoveries by helping researchers identify transcriptionally distinct spatial regions in different tissues and diseases [1, 2]. Many ST analyses have been done in brain and cancer datasets, where researchers have made extensive use of spatial information to compare changes in spatial regions, cell-type composition, and cell–cell interactions [3–6].

One of the main analyses done for ST is region segmentation [1], which is an initial step in addressing the challenge of exploring domain relationships and identity changes at bordering regions. Segmentation can be performed by training a machine learning model with manual annotations based on histology and cytoarchitecture (e.g. Cell Location recovEry or CeLEry) or using spatial clustering approaches (e.g. BayesSpace and SpaGCN) [3, 7, 8]. Although these methods have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in their respective tasks, there is still a need for methods which can complement these tasks with intuitive, easy-to-interpret results. For example, BayesSpace and SpaGCN identify spatially resolved markers by first generating spatial clusters and running a differential expression analysis on the clusters. As previously demonstrated by Neufeld et al. [9], such approaches are prone to generating false positives during marker gene detection. Thus, it is preferable to identify methods that jointly learn spatial domains and their markers. Similar observations can be made with supervised approaches that do not guarantee to provide users with an interpretable way to identify the gene programs that drive histology annotations. Finally, most methods are designed to perform only one of these tasks, requiring researchers to integrate results from different methods to perform more comprehensive analyses. This raises new complications, as methods vary in their inductive biases and may not provide results with similar levels of interpretability.

In addition, the limited availability of spatial datasets for specific tissues and diseases requires researchers to find ways to impute spatial information into single-cell atlases to perform large-scale spatial analyses [10]. These imputation tasks facilitate our ability to link the findings made in single-cell studies with spatial studies, leading to more nuanced hypothesis generation for experimental efforts. For example, the brains of patients suffering from major depressive disorder (MDD) have been shown to experience distinct morphological changes [11]. Although large-scale single-cell analyses of MDD have revealed transcriptional changes in MDD onset, it is still necessary to integrate these findings with spatial information to characterize pathogenetic mechanisms [12]. Thus, finding methods that can reliably model spatial transcriptomes data and project them onto single-cell datasets is crucial to integrate the findings from both modalities.

Currently, there is a lack of an interpretable computational framework that can easily be implemented for different types of spatial analyses and impute spatial information while ensuring seamless and intuitive integration of downstream findings. Such a framework will greatly aid the analysis of single-cell disease atlases. To this end, we present the Spatial Topic Model (SpaTM), an interpretable topic modelling framework capable of spatial deconvolution, spatial label prediction, spot proximity prediction, and de novo inference of spatial gene signatures (Fig. 1). By using the interpretable and flexible framework of the topic model as its foundation, SpaTM is able to easily undertake different forms of spatial analyses with the same inductive bias. This ensures a practical analysis framework while preserving competitive performance in different tasks. We demonstrate that the SpaTM framework can be used to impute spatial information into a publicly available MDD snRNA-seq dataset to investigate spatially oriented changes in MDD and uncover novel pathogenetic insights. Finally, we use SpaTM to characterize the subdomains of invasive ductal carcinoma and investigate tumour heterogeneity in micro-environment interactions.

To train SpaTM-R for spot proximity and neighbour prediction, we define spots as neighbours if they are within a user-defined Euclidean distance from spot . This results in a large class imbalance (i.e. most spots are not a neighbour of any given spot). To address this, we subsample the non-neighbour spots, assigning sampling probabilities that are proportional to their distance from the current spot.

Spatial clustering

We evaluated whether SpaTM-R can recapitulate spatial information from gene expression. We first ran PCA on the spot proximity matrix predicted by SpaTM-R followed by the Leiden community detection algorithm to generate clusters. If the number of regions is known, we fine-tune the Leiden resolution parameter to produce the desired number of clusters. For ease of visualization, we smooth the cluster labels by updating the label of any spot based on the label of its neighbouring spots using majority voting.

A more comprehensive spatial clustering benchmark analysis was performed following the protocol proposed by the SDMBench pipeline, where 13 methods were evaluated on 34 unique ST samples [19]. SpaTM-R was run on all 34 samples using the top 10 nearest spots/cells as positive examples for each spot/cell. If samples had >3000 genes in their assay, the top 3000 highly variable genes were selected. Otherwise, all genes were used. The number of topics was set to match the number of ground truth annotations present in each sample. We evaluated the NMI, HOM, and COM accuracy metrics with respect to the reported results from SDMBench and also compared the average of the three metrics.

Breast cancer ST analysis

Our case study of tumour heterogeneity in breast cancer was performed by running SpaTM-R on a Visium sample from the 10 Genomics database [20]. We set the number of topics to the number of pathologist-derived fine annotations (20) [21] and used the top 5000 highly variable genes. We then assessed which inferred topics correlated with the ground truth annotations and visualized some of the top 10 genes for four of these topics (two tumour-specific topics and two TME topics).

MDD snRNA-seq imputation

For MDD imputation tasks, we trained SpaTM-S on two DLPFC slices using the top 1000 HVGs and eight topics based on its performance on the DLPFC Slide-Tags dataset (Supplementary Fig. S2). SpaTM-R was trained on one slice using the top 5000 HVGs. We inferred the topic mixtures for the MDD dataset using both models to then impute spatial labels and cell–cell adjacency for each patient. Spatial labels were used to calculate the layer distribution of each cell type prior to assessing sex-specific changes between MDD and control samples. Statistical testing was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

SpaTM overview

SpaTM is built around the topic model, an interpretable and principled machine learning framework designed to discover latent topics within complex, high-dimensional data (Fig. 1) [25]. In genomics, these topics correspond to modules of co-expressed genes, revealing transcriptional programs that underlie cell states, tissue regions, and biological processes. This probabilistic and interpretable representation has made topic modelling particularly effective in genomics, where data are inherently sparse and noisy. As a result, they have seen great success in many domains for the integration and analysis of scRNA-seq and ST data [5, 26, 13]. Building on this foundation, our previous Guided Topic Model for deconvolution (GTM-Decon) demonstrated that incorporating biological priors, such as known cell-type annotations, can anchor the inferred topics to cell types of interest, facilitating biologically meaningful interpretation of results [13]. Extending this concept, we propose SpaTM, which extends GTM-Decon (hereby referred to as SpaTM-G) with two additional components: SpaTM-S for spatial label prediction via supervised topic modelling [16] and SpaTM-R for cell proximity (probability of a pair of cells being neighbours) inference via relational topic modelling (Fig. 1c) [18]. By incorporating multiple modelling components, SpaTM provides users with a unified framework capable of a suite of ST analysis tasks previously achieved by using a combination of individual task-specific methods (Supplementary Table S1). Thus, this work lays the foundation for integrative spatial omics analysis, enabling an intuitive and coherent interpretation of spatial informed gene expression in task-specific contexts.

Benchmarking SpaTM-S

We evaluated SpaTM-S for the prediction of spatial cortical layers (L1–L6 and white matter) in slices of human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) using the SpatialLIBD 10 Visium dataset [2]. Benchmarking against state-of-the-art methods involved four scenarios: training on one slice and predicting on another from the same patient (scenario 1) or a different patient (scenario 2), and training on three slices to predict on the same test slices (scenarios 3 and 4, respectively). We compared SpaTM-S’s performance to the results reported by Zhang et al. [3] for CeLEry and competing methods [27–29] for the same scenarios as well as sequentially training a baseline topic model (i.e. cell type guided—SpaTM-G—with single-nuclei reference data from Russell et al. [30] and unguided as the standard topic model—SpaTM) and a post hoc classifier (Fig. 2a). A Poisson Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) model provided by the fastTopics package was also combined with a post hoc classifier as an additional interpretable baseline method [31]. Across, the four scenarios, the advantage of predicting spatial labels via latent representations was made apparent through the superior performance of SpaTM and NMF-based approaches compared with the previous state-of-the-art which were less robust to cross-sample predictions. SpaTM and SpaTM-S showed similar performance and improvements over the use of NMF, with SpaTM-S being able to better leverage the additional training data in scenario 4 and achieving an increase in accuracy of 3.56 percentage points over the SpaTM baseline. Visualizing the predictions made by SpaTM-S revealed clear recovery of the ground truth spatial labels, with easily identifiable domain boundaries (Fig. 2b). Updating SpaTM-S’s spatial label predictions based on neighbouring spots via KNN-based majority voting (Smooth) further improved its predictive performance. A subsequent evaluation of interpretable models using a range of gene, topic, and classifier parametrizations found that topic modelling (SpaTM and SpaTM-S) consistently outperformed NMF in label prediction (Supplementary Fig. S1a–c). An additional analysis of seed stability showed that SpaTM-S is more robust to randomness than the base SpaTM and NMF pipeline approaches, retaining considerably smaller variance in accuracy in multiple runs with overall greater performance (Supplementary Fig. S1d). Together, these results demonstrate the superior performance of SpaTM-S in imputing spatial labels over existing methods. A runtime analysis comparing SpaTM-S to the baseline SpaTM and NMF approaches revealed SpaTM and NMF to provide faster training times on one DLPFC slice than SpaTM-S at the cost of lower and less robust performance (Supplementary Fig. S3).

SpaTM-S interpretability

A key advantage of the SpaTM-S framework is the ability to identify spatial gene programs using the inferred topic matrices and . Visualizing the spot-topic mixtures for each spot from unseen histology slices revealed spatial topic patterns that strongly align with the ground-truth layer regions (Fig. 2c). These patterns were present in both the within- and cross-sample scenarios, demonstrating the generalizability of the inferred spatially informed topics. Next, we selected the top genes for each inferred topic in and compared them with the layer-specific markers identified in the SpatialLIBD study. We found considerable overlap, which further supports the accuracy of the spatial gene programs learned by SpaTM-S (Fig. 2d) [2].

Applicability to single-cell ST data

As our model was trained on spot-resolution data, we assessed its transferability to single-cell resolution by imputing labels on a single-cell DLPFC human sample (Slide-Tags) (Supplementary Fig. S2). We observed that cortical layer predictions overlapped with the majority of neuronal cell-types and white matter predictions aligned with most non-neuronal cell types (Supplementary Fig. S2a and b). This suggests that the predicted layers retained spatial coherence in single-cell data. Assessing the cell-type composition of the predicted layers also revealed distributions in line with what is expected for the prefrontal cortex (Supplementary Fig. S2c) [30, 32]. For example, we note the enrichment of oligodendrocytes within the white matter, and neuronal cell types with layers 2–6. In addition, we also observe that non-neuronal cells such as astrocytes, endothelial cells, and microglia, mainly occur in layer 1, with relatively lower proportions in deeper layers compared to neuronal cells. We then investigated if the topic mixtures inferred by SpaTM-S retained sufficient information to jointly discriminate spatial labels and cell types (Supplementary Fig. S2d and e). Visualization of the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings derived from the topic mixtures showed that the Slide-Tags data exhibited coherent clustering by both cell-type and imputed layers, where cell types generally formed separate clusters that could in turn be split by spatial label. This indicated that the representations inferred by the spot-level training data were successfully transferred to the single-cell resolution, capturing both cellular identity and laminar organization. Notably, we can see that both the excitatory and inhibitory neurons form separate clusters within which a clear separation between predicted layer labels can be observed. As a final line of evidence, we visualized the expression of known layer-specific markers on the generated UMAP embeddings to assess their coherence with our results (Supplementary Fig. S2f–i) [30]. We first investigated the expression of the Myelin Basic Protein (MBP) gene, a strong marker for white matter and oligodendrocytes and found that its expression was localized to oligodendrocytes and a subset of astrocytes and endothelial cells that had been predicted as white matter (Supplementary Fig. S2f). PCP4 was used as a marker for deep layer excitatory neurons and was localized to cells predicted as L3–L6 in the excitatory neuron cluster (Supplementary Fig. S2g). Finally, we assessed the expression of PLCH1 and CXCL14 as markers for deep and shallow layer inhibitory neurons (Supplementary Fig. S2h and i). We found PLCH1 was localized within the inhibitory neurons predicted as layer 3, whereas CXCL14 was found within the layer 2 inhibitory neurons. Overall, the literature-supported cell-type composition of the imputed layers, the joint discrimination of cell-type and imputed layer by the inferred topic mixtures, and the alignment of known layer markers with the imputed layers together suggest that the imputations generated by SpaTM-S are sufficient for transfer to single-cell data.

We also performed an additional ablation study on an annotated MERFISH dataset containing five mouse brain slices. Here, we trained and evaluated both the base SpaTM + MLP approach and SpaTM-S using leave-one-out cross validation (Fig. 2e). We observed higher accuracy in prediction for SpaTM-S compared with SpaTM across all folds with SpaTM-S achieving a mean accuracy of and the baseline SpaTM achieving . Furthermore, we visualized the predictions for both models to assess the qualitative differences in predictions. SpaTM-S showed greater success in recovering the paraventricular hypothalamic nucleus (PVH) and medial preoptic area (MPA) regions, whereas the baseline SpaTM struggled to distinguish both regions from medial preoptic nucleus (MPN).

In summary, SpaTM-S extends the base topic model to provide researchers with a way to improve the robustness and stability of spatial label prediction while also inferring biologically relevant gene programs.

Spatially informed gene expression correction

Sparsity and noise in ST data can affect the visibility of spatial gene expression patterns when defining region markers [2]. A solution is to apply pseudo-bulking to spots in different spatial regions to reduce noise in marker expression. However, the loss of spot-level resolution limits our ability to explore how marker expression changes across region borders. To address this, researchers have investigated spatially informed gene expression reconstruction methods and have demonstrated how it can facilitate the characterization of spatial heterogeneity [33]. We therefore propose to use the spatially informed topic mixtures learned by SpaTM-S to correct spatial expression profiles (Fig. 3a). As the topic model factorizes the data into spot-topic and gene-topic mixtures, we can use both metrics to reconstruct the gene expression of a given sample (Fig. 3a). We trained SpaTM-S on three slices and inferred for test slices within and between samples. After reconstructing the spot-by-gene matrix, we scaled the expression profile of each spot by its library size and applied log-normalization. The corrected expression profiles improved the spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I) of layer markers (identified by pseudo-bulking in [2]) compared with the uncorrected counts (Fig. 3b). The reconstructed profiles also exhibit much clearer spatial patterns (Fig. 3c). To ensure that the reconstruction process was not creating new, unrelated signals, we assessed the correlation between the uncorrected and reconstructed counts with different numbers of genes (Supplementary Fig. S4a and b). We found that both our baseline SpaTM and SpaTM-S yielded high correlations, suggesting that the de-noised expression is biologically relevant. We also observed similar performance for SpaTM-S and SpaTM at various gene counts (Supplementary Fig. S4c and d), suggesting the robustness of the topic models.

To assess if SpaTM could also impute expression for missing genes, we compared SpaTM to a recently published gene imputation method, SpaIM [22]. SpaIM is a novel gene imputation approach which leverages scRNA-seq data to predict missing genes in ST via style transfer learning. We ran a benchmarking experiment on 21 10 Visium datasets provided in the SpaIM study to assess missing gene imputation. For each dataset, we aligned genes from a corresponding scRNA-seq dataset from the same tissue. We then performed 10-fold cross validation to evaluate imputation performance. In each fold, 10 percent of the genes in the ST data were set to 0 to simulate missing expression. The models were then trained on the scRNA-seq data with the full feature set and applied to the ST data. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was then calculated for each of the masked genes. The PCCs for all folds were then combined and averaged to generate the score for the corresponding dataset. Overall, we found that the performance of SpaTM was in line with that of SpaIM 5. Although the performance is similar, we emphasize the key advantage brought by SpaTM, in the form of its interpretability which can allow users to identify which genes are contributing to missing gene imputation based on the topics inferred during training. This can provide the possibility to assess gene co-expression and discern how the model is using the underlying transcriptional programs to assign imputed expression (Supplementary Fig. S5).

These experiments demonstrate SpaTM’s strong performance in gene expression correction and imputation. Requiring minimal user effort, SpaTM offers a reliable solution for addressing technical noise and dropout in ST data, which can facilitate the visualization of spatially driven gene programs.

Benchmarking SpaTM-R

SpaTM-R complements SpaTM-S by constructing a spot or cell proximity matrix to predict neighbours (Fig. 4a). To evaluate neighbour prediction, we trained SpaTM-R in four scenarios: training on one slice and predicting on another from the same patient (scenario 1) or a different patient (scenario 2), and training on three slices to predict on the same test slices (scenarios 3 and 4, respectively). Neighbours were defined as any spot within the radius of a given Euclidean distance. For benchmarking purposes, we used a distance threshold, which provided 10–12 neighbours for each spot. To ensure a balanced training and testing dataset, we then sampled an equal number of spots beyond the threshold distance to use as negative examples. We set our sampling procedure to prioritize the spots that were further from the current spot . We then compared SpaTM-R’s performance to running a logistic regression and 1-layer MLP on the topic mixtures generated from the baseline SpaTM and a Poisson NMF model [31]. The evaluation of neighbour prediction for the generated positive and negative examples was performed using AUROC, which revealed the superior performance of SpaTM-R (Fig. 4b). To determine how SpaTM-R handles predicting neighbours within and across spatial regions, we compared the neighbour prediction for within-region and cross-region pairs of spots over different Euclidean distances (Fig. 4c). This revealed that SpaTM-R was more inclined to predict same-region pairs of spots as neighbours than cross-region pairs as their distance increased, suggesting that the neighbour predictions by SpaTM-R capture functional similarity rather than mere spatial proximity.

Thus, we evaluated the ability of SpaTM-R to generate spatial clusters when manual annotations were unavailable (Fig. 4d). Specifically, we trained SpaTM-R to predict neighbours in each slice from the SpatialLIBD dataset and used the predicted spot proximity matrices as inputs to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) followed by Leiden clustering. A visual inspection of the generated clusters reveals their close resemblance to the ground-truth layer labels (Fig. 4e). We then compared these results to previously reported benchmarks for state-of-the-art methods on the same 12 DLPFC slices [8]. We also ran IRIS, a recently published, reference-based clustering method as an additional comparison [34] (Fig. 4f). Although SpaTM-R demonstrated competitive performance against the SpaGCN, BayesSpace, and stLearn, we found that IRIS provided higher ARI accuracy in the DLPFC dataset. However, whereas the performance of IRIS is dependent on access to a high-quality and well-annotated reference dataset, SpaTM-R can be applied to tissues and diseases where there is insufficient data available. In particular, the use of reference scRNA-seq data prevents the identification of data-driven gene programs that may not be represented in the provided annotations and may be unique to the spatial transcriptomics data being studied.

We also compared SpaTM-R’s clustering performance with a previously published benchmarking study of 14 other methods on 34 spatial transcriptome samples from a variety of spatial technologies (10 Visium, Stereo-seq, Barista-seq, MERFISH, osmFISH,STARmap, and STARmap*) and tissues [19] (Supplementary Fig. S6). Clustering accuracy was evaluated with three metrics: (i) normalized mutual information (NMI), which quantifies the similarity between two sets of cluster labels, (ii) homogeneity score (HOM), which assesses the if clusters are exclusively composed of a single ground truth label, and (iii) completeness score (COM) which evaluates if all observations of the same label are found in the same cluster. As all metrics are bounded between 0 and 1, their average was used as a global accuracy metric and results were grouped by technology. We found that SpaTM-R achieved top 3 performance for the average accuracy metric for the 10 Visium, StereoSeq, and osmFISH, demonstrating its capability in clustering for spot-level, single-cell, and imaging-based spatial technologies (Supplementary Fig. S6a,c, and f). For the remaining technologies, SpaTM-R achieved the fourth best performance for BaristaSeq and sixth best for MERFISH and both STARmap technologies (Supplementary Fig. S6b,d, and e). Overall, we found that SpaTM-R achieved competitive performance in the spatial clustering task, with Visium, StereoSeq, and osmFISH being the technologies we recommend users use to ensure high accuracy in domain identification. In particular, we note SpaTM-R’s ability to easily be applied to different ST with little difficulty and render reliable performance, whereas certain methods were reported as incompatible with specific technologies. An additional advantage presented by SpaTM-R is the ability to train on multiple slices as shown in Fig. 4a. Many clustering methods included in the benchmarking such as SpaGCN, SCAN-IT, and stLearn can only be trained on a single slice, limiting their ability to leverage information from different samples. Thus, SpaTM-R provides the opportunity to incorporate spatial information from multiple slices to discern domains and spatially driven gene programs. Finally, SpaTM-R’s core utility is the trade-off provided by jointly inferring clusters and corresponding markers. Whereas many existing methods depend on downstream analyses to identify differentially expressed genes, the top genes identified by each topic directly provide the user with quantifiable markers for defining spatial regions. Furthermore, other methods such as BASS and BayesSpace have high performance but do not provide users with a latent representation for their spatial data. In contrast, the topics provided by SpaTM-R provide users with a way to not only identify gene programs without downstream analysis tools, but also assess their contribution to the identified domains.

A runtime analysis comparing SpaTM-R to one of the top performing non-deep learning clustering methods for Visium data (BayesSpace) found that SpaTM-R yielded results with a slightly lower runtime over 10 runs (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Overall, extending SpaTM with the RTM enables users to explore spatial domains and infer gene programs in scenarios where histology annotations are unavailable or difficult to generate. This feature, together with its demonstrated reliability, complements SpaTM-S by broadening the range of applicable scenarios.

Benchmarking SpaTM-G

To ensure that the state-of-the-art performance in bulk deconvolution reported in GTM-decon translates to spatial data with SpaTM-G, we compared our model to a previously reported comprehensive benchmarking analysis against 18 published deconvolution methods [23]. The competing methods included a variety of solutions including the use of spatial information (RCTD, CARD, and SpatialDWLS), optimal transport (tangram and novoSpaRc), and interpretable frameworks (NMFreg, SpiceMix, and STdeconvolve) [5, 28, 29, 35–39]. SpaTM-G and the reported methods were evaluated on simulated spot-resolution datasets from seqFISH+ and MERFISH data with different numbers of genes (3000, 6000, and 10 000 genes for seqFISH+) and spot sizes (20, 50, and 100 m for MERFISH). All reference-based methods (including SpaTM-G) were trained on the same single-cell reference dataset from the murine somatosensory cortex which contained six different cell types provided in referenced study. Performance was quantified using Jensen–Shannon Divergence and total root mean square error. We trained SpaTM-G on the full gene set of the training data (Full) and on the subset found in the simulated data. SpaTM-G demonstrated competitive performance with respect to existing methods and was very robust to gene number and spot size (8). In contrast, although spatially informed methods such as RCTD, CARD, and SpatialDWLS performed well in the seqFISH+ experiments, they struggled to retain the same level of performance with the varying spot sizes found in the MERFISH experiments. Overall, SpaTM-G consistently achieved accurate and stable deconvolution across a wide range of experimental settings, underscoring its generalizability, and robustness for spatial transcriptomics analysis.

Our benchmarking results reveal that extending GTM-decon for previously studied spatial transcriptomics tasks provides clear benefits in the form of additional analytical tasks without impacting the model’s previously demonstrated reliability at cell-type deconvolution as seen with SpaTM-G.

Integrative analysis of DLPFC with the SpaTM suite reveals spatial subregions

A crucial advantage of SpaTM is its ability to perform multiple tasks and easily integrate their results. The highly interpretable SpaTM suite makes the integration of results from its different tasks more reliable and intuitive than using a pipeline of independent methods. We demonstrate this using one DLPFC slice from the SpatialLIBD database to integrate results found using the SpaTM-S, SpaTM-R, and SpaTM-G components. This revealed that white matter spots (as determined by SpaTM-S) were represented by two clusters found by SpaTM-R (clusters 2 and 4) (Fig. 5a). We found that cluster 4 represented white matter spots bordering layer 6, whereas cluster 2 represented the core white matter region. Comparing the SpaTM-S-derived topic mixtures between white matter spots in both clusters shows a clear decrease in the white matter-associated topic weight (Fig. 5b). Finally, running a cell-type deconvolution analysis by training SpaTM-G on healthy DLPFC samples from a reference snRNA-seq dataset revealed that the core white matter cluster had a higher proportion of oligodendrocytes, while the border cluster revealed the appearance of excitatory neurons (Fig. 5c) [12]. As these observations were derived from a single slice, we repeated the analysis on three additional DLPFC slices from the same patient to ensure the replicability of our findings and exclude potential optimistic bias. This analysis revealed similar observations with the white matter regions across all four samples exhibiting a drop in oligodendrocyte proportion and an increase in excitatory neurons at the border with cortical layer 6 (Supplementary Fig. S9). Together, our proposed SpaTM suite has greatly facilitated integrating results from all three tasks and has identified that the predicted white matter region (as predicted by SpaTM-S) is composed of two domains (as predicted by SpaTM-R) that harbour distinct cell-type proportions (as predicted by SpaTM-G).

(b) Comparison of SpaTM-S-derived topic proportions across white matter subregions defined by SpaTM-R. Imputing spatial labels and cell proximity for MDD snRNA-seq

In addition to providing a flexible analysis toolkit for ST datasets, SpaTM can be used to impute spatial information into single-cell atlases, where for many diseases, large-scale ST datasets are not available. We acquired a public snRNA-seq dataset from the DLPFC of post-mortem human brains diagnosed with MDD or otherwise healthy [12]. The dataset comprised 160k nuclei from 71 patients, where male and female samples were collected in separate batches. Here, we applied both the SpaTM-S and SpaTM-R models, trained with the above SpatialLIBD data, to impute spatial labels and cell proximity information within the atlas (Fig. 6a). Analysing the imputed cell proximity matrix revealed strong clustering by both cell types and imputed layers, demonstrating the reliability and coherence between the SpaTM-S and SpaTM-R imputations (Fig. 6b). Jointly ordering the cell proximity matrix by layers and cell type reveals that imputed layers are largely composed of biologically relevant cell types and retain inter-layer cell-type-specific patterns in both control and MDD samples. As male and female samples were collected in different batches, we ran a sex-specific analysis to compare differential layer proportions at a cell-type-specific level (Fig. 6c). In women, we found that MDD cases had a higher proportion of oligodendrocytes (Oli) and oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) in layers 1 (L1) and 3 (L3), respectively. We also observed a shift of inhibitory neurons (InN) from layer 3 (L3) to layer 2 (L2). Finally, both males and females with MDD exhibited a higher proportion of microglia (Mic) in layer 2 than control samples. A Mann–Whitney test on all combinations of layers and cell types revealed the above results to be significant. In general, MDD samples exhibited a higher level of variability in layer proportions compared with healthy controls. These findings demonstrate the added value of SpaTM through its ability to enable spatially informed analyses of existing disease atlases.

Exploring breast cancer tumour micro-environment with SpaTM-R

We further explored the use of SpaTM on a Visium dataset of breast cancer (ductal carcinoma) [20]. While traditional methods require a downstream differential gene expression analysis to identify cluster-specific markers, SpaTM-R automatically learns cluster-associated markers through its inferred topics. We trained SpaTM-R on the ductal carcinoma ST sample and ran Leiden clustering on the predicted adjacency matrix. The model was trained using 20 topics to match the number of manually annotated subregions provided with the dataset [21]. The clusters generated by SpaTM-R showed considerable resemblance to the fine pathologist annotations, identifying distant tumour and tumour microenvironment (TME) regions with similar expression patterns (Fig. 7a). In particular, we identified a series of topics that represented tumour and micro-environment compartments with distinct gene expression patterns (Fig. 7b and c). SpaTM-R revealed a non-invasive and invasive tumour region captured by topic 20 (T20) and 11 (T11), respectively. T11 is defined by TTLL12, a known oncogene and immune suppressor linked with poor prognosis in colorectal cancer [40]. HMGB3, which is found in T11, is linked to cancer cell proliferation [41]. The TME topics (T19 and T17) are characterized by genes related to anti-tumour and anti-angiogenesis processes. For instance, IGHG2 (T19) encodes for the constant region of IgG2 antibodies and IGLC3 (T17) has been observed to correlate with patient overall survival and immune infiltration [42].

We extended our analysis by independently assessing the spatial heterogeneity of the co-expression of the identified top genes for each topic (Fig. 7d and e). We used the Local Lee’s L metric provided by the Voyager package to assess how each spot contributes to the spatial co-expression of a given pair of genes [43, 44]. Indeed, genes under the same topic were positively correlated and their local correlation was enriched within the corresponding tumour/TME regions. For example, we see that HMGB3 and CEBPD were highly co-expressed and correlated within tumor T20; SREBF1 and TTLL12 from T11 exhibited strong local co-expression in invasive tumour regions (Fig. 7d). Similarly, genes linked to the T19 (IGHG2 and SERPINF1) and T17 (IGLC3, C1R and CCDC80) micro-environment domains were only correlated within their boundaries. We then explored the spatial correlation of pairs of genes from distinct topics to elucidate inter-regional marker co-expression (Fig. 7e). TTLL12 (known to suppress immune response) was negatively correlated with immune marker genes (IGHG2 and IGLC3) from the two micro-environment topics within the invasive tumour [40]. In contrast, we see that HMGB3, which is linked to cancer cell proliferation [41], was spatially correlated with IGLC3 and IGHG2 in the boundary between the T20 tumour and its micro-environment. We therefore hypothesize that T20 denotes a tumourregion actively interacting with the immune micro-environment whereas these interactions are suppressed in T11.region actively interacting with the immune micro-environment whereas these interactions are suppressed in T11.

Finally, we performed GSEA analysis using FGSEA to compute the enrichment for cancer hallmark pathways in the inferred gene-topic mixtures [45, 46]. Topic 20 (active in non-invasive region) was enriched for multiple metabolic and cell cycle-associated pathways such as glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, the P53 and MTORC1 pathways, and androgen/oestrogen responses. We also observed evidence of immune interaction from the enrichment of interferon gamma response and IL2/STAT5 signalling. Topic 11 (active in invasive region) was enriched for epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), which supports the tumour region’s progression from non-invasive ductal carcinoma. Both Topics 17 and 19 were enriched for allograft rejection, supporting the TME’s immune response to the proliferation of the tumour region.

Both topics are also enriched for EMT, a possible consequence of the interaction with the tumour represented by Topic 20. This case study demonstrates SpaTM’s ability to help characterize the spatial regions in invasive ductal carcinoma in the lens of inferred gene expression programs.

Discussion

In this study, we presented a multi-purpose Bayesian topic modelling framework called SpaTM to undertake a variety of tasks for ST analyses. Combining guided, supervised, and RTM components, SpaTM enables researchers to identify gene programs through interpretable, task-informed topics. Through our analyses and benchmarking studies, we have demonstrated that SpaTM provides a competitive solution for a suite of common ST analysis tasks.

Our benchmarking of supervised histology annotation prediction models demonstrated the superior performance of SpaTM-S (Fig. 2). Most available methods for this task were designed for one-to-one annotation mapping (novosparc,Tangram, and spaOTsc), and are outperformed by MLP-based classifiers such as CeLEry which can leverage multiple training samples for training their classifier [3, 27, 28]. By extending the classifier to jointly use a topic-based inference mechanism, we demonstrate how SpaTM-S can take this observation a step further and yield higher prediction performance (Fig. 2a). This observation is complemented by the added interpretability of the topics inferred by SpaTM-S, which provides supervised topics that are representative of the histology annotations (Fig. 2c and d). This combination of features allows SpaTM-S not only to predict histology annotations in unseen samples with greater accuracy, but also to leverage the interpretability of SpaTM-S to better understand gene programs that define the spatial domains defined by histology or pathology experts. Changes in these gene programs can in turn be used to elucidate the cause behind variations in predicted spatial regions.

In addition, SpaTM can correct gene expression to improve detection of spatially variable genes. As seen in previous work, comparing gene expression in different spatial regions often requires pseudo-bulking to mitigate the effect of noise and dropout [2]. However, the gene expression reconstruction capability of SpaTM, acquired through its underlying topic model framework, gives researchers the ability to de-noise spatial data, and thus use spot-level resolution to explore spatial patterns and markers in greater detail (Fig. 3).

In the absence of histology annotations, SpaTM-R can instead model spatial proximity. Although many spatial clustering methods are available, most depend on downstream differential gene expression analyses to identify cluster-specific gene markers. As demonstrated by Neufeld et al. [9], this commonly used approach leads to ’double-dipping’ which has been shown to produce false positives in marker identification. Our analysis demonstrates how the interpretability of SpaTM resolves this issue by directly providing cluster-associated gene programs during training. Thus, SpaTM’s ability to jointly infer gene programs while learning spatial domains provides a conceptually sound solution to avoid double-dipping.

Using the three modelling components provided by SpaTM, we are able to identify and characterize white matter subregions by integrating the inferred predictions and topic mixtures (Fig. 5; Supplementary Fig. S9). The ability to rapidly undertake and integrate multiple tasks presents SpaTM as a practical solution to researchers aiming to perform multi-faceted analyses in ST data. Although individual methods may achieve higher accuracy in specific tasks, the guaranteed interpretability of the topics inferred by SpaTM, and the ease with which their composition and contribution to transcriptional states can be compared greatly reduces the interpretative burden on researchers. Moreover, this intuitive framework allows researchers to quickly evaluate the impact of optimistic bias by re-running the pipeline on additional samples with minimal overhead. Together, these advantages position SpaTM as a reliable and transparent framework for integrative analyses where interpretability and intuitiveness are crucial.

Our analysis of the MDD snRNA-seq atlas reveals how spatial imputation can extend single-cell analyses (Fig. 6). The existing literature suggests that the observed shifts in the proportions of the cell-type-specific layer may be related to neuronal damage, with the depletion of OPCs suggested to contribute to depressive-like behaviour [47]. These findings demonstrate how spatial imputation can provide a clearer explanation for MDD by integrating spatially driven changes with gene expression.

Furthermore, our case study of an invasive ductal carcinoma sample identified distinct tumour and TME subcompartments with corresponding gene programs (Fig. 7). These inferred gene programs may play a pivotal role in helping researchers define new, expression-based spatial regions in tissues where histology and pathology expertise are limited.

Although SpaTM has demonstrated reliable generalizability and cross-slice performance in the presented tasks, it is possible that additional correction is required for data with larger batch effects. In such cases, we recommend users use methods such as SCTransform or MNN to apply batch correction to the raw gene expression prior to training the model [48, 49]. Finally, to facilitate the inference of topics as unique transcriptional programs or cell states, we recommend users initially set the number of topics close to the number of spatial domains or cell types they expect to find in their dataset. After validating that the model reliably captures these domains, the number of topics can be increased to investigate transcriptionally distinct subdomains.

Conclusion

Advancements in ST analysis workflows depend on the availability of tools that can streamline and integrate multiple complementary tasks with reliable performance and interpretability. SpaTM addresses this necessity by providing researchers with a flexible and interpretable analysis framework for ST. It enables cell-type deconvolution, spatial label prediction, expression correction, cell proximity inference, and spatial clustering with a consistent inductive bias. In addition to its quantitative performance, SpaTM surpasses previous methods by enabling rapid inference and interpretation of spatially driven gene programs.

In future work, SpaTM can be extended to infer cell–cell communication from the predicted cell–cell proximity matrix by using bivariate spatial correlation metrics such as Lee’s L from the Voyager package [44]. These approaches could leverage the inferred topics to identify distant and proximal spatial regions within which distinct gene programs are interacting. In addition, methods such as CellAgentChat [4], could provide additional evidence for ligand–receptor pairs driving the spatial domains identified by SpaTM-R. Finally, exploring ways to integrate the different SpaTM components during training could enhance the accuracy of the overall results and more effectively leverage the information obtained from each task.
