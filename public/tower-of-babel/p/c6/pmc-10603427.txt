Estimating the prevalence of diagnosed Alzheimer disease in England across deprivation groups using electronic health records: a clinical practice research datalink study
Authors: Thomas Patrick Leahy, Alex Simpson, Cormac Sammon, Clive Ballard, Sandro Gsteiger
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEstimate the prevalence of diagnosed Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and early Alzheimer’s disease (eAD) overall and stratified by age, sex and deprivation and combinations thereof in England on 1 January 2020.DesignCross-sectional.SettingPrimary care electronic health record data, the Clinical Practice Research database linked with secondary care data, Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) and patient-level deprivation data, Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD).Outcome measuresThe prevalence per 100 000 of the population and corresponding 95% CIs for both diagnosed AD and eAD overall and stratified by covariates. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the sensitivity of the population definition and look-back period.ResultsThere were 448 797 patients identified in the Clinical Practice Research Datalink that satisfied the study inclusion criteria and were eligible for HES and IMD linkage. For the main analysis of AD and eAD, 379 763 patients are eligible for inclusion in the denominator. This resulted in an estimated prevalence of diagnosed AD of 378.39 (95% CI, 359.36 to 398.44) per 100 000 and eAD of 292.81 (95% CI, 276.12 to 310.52) per 100 000. Prevalence estimates across main and sensitivity analyses for the entire AD study population were found to vary widely with estimates ranging from 137.48 (95% CI, 127.05 to 148.76) to 796.55 (95% CI, 768.77 to 825.33). There was significant variation in prevalence of diagnosed eAD when assessing the sensitivity with the look-back periods, as low as 120.54 (95% CI, 110.80 to 131.14) per 100 000, and as high as 519.01 (95% CI, 496.64 to 542.37) per 100 000.ConclusionsThe study found relatively consistent patterns of prevalence across both AD and eAD populations. Generally, the prevalence of diagnosed AD increased with age and increased with deprivation for each age category. Women had a higher prevalence than men. More granular levels of stratification reduced patient numbers and increased the uncertainty of point prevalence estimates. Despite this, the study found a relationship between deprivation and prevalence of AD.Keywords: dementia, retrospective studies, epidemiology

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurological disorder and causes atrophy of the brain.1 It is associated with an abnormal build-up of a protein fragment called beta-amyloid. This protein builds up in the spaces between nerve cells, and as a result, causes damage to these cells.1 It imposes a considerable burden on patients, carers and national health systems. AD is a slowly evolving disorder; as it progresses, a range of symptoms can emerge, including confusion, more serious memory loss and behavioural changes. Based on the degree of cognitive impairment, it is often divided into three stages: the preclinical stage, characterised by a typically long period of normal cognitive ability; the prodromal stage, characterised by mild cognitive impairment; and the dementia stage, with functional impairment.2 The dementia stage is associated with a progressive accumulation of disability in cognition, behavioural skills and social skills that affects a person’s ability to function independently and is often categorised as mild, moderate or severe dementia. Although dementia may present in the absence of AD, AD is the most common cause of dementia.1

Treatments for AD are typically indicated for subgroups of patients at different stages of disease progression. In England, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommend monotherapy with acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors for the treatment of mild to moderate AD and memantine monotherapy for the treatment of severe AD; memantine is also recommended for the treatment of moderate AD for those patients who cannot tolerate or have a contraindication to AChE inhibitors.3 Novel (potentially) disease modifying therapies (DMTs) are currently being developed for the treatment of patients with early Alzheimer disease (eAD), that is, those patients with mild cognitive impairment due to AD and mild dementia.4 DMTs aim to slow the clinical progression of AD by reducing amyloid plaques.5

Health inequality is the term used to describe differences in the health of individuals or groups. Health inequality covers a wide spectrum of differences arising from variance in socioeconomic status, gender and ethnicity among other factors.6 Given the increasing prevalence of AD,7 an understanding of the relationship between health inequality and AD is crucial to not only improving overall population health, but also considering the impact on the distribution of health potential therapies may have. Inequalities can be incorporated into the assessment of new technologies possibly exacerbating heath inequities. For example, the NICE in the UK has equality objectives but does not impose specific processes or methodologies to account for health inequality in their assessments.8–10 As a result, the majority of cost-effectiveness models used to inform health technology assessments typically only consider overall effects and little or no information on possible inequities. Socioeconomic status or deprivation is one variable that can have a significant impact on overall health and therefore often serves as the main factor considered when analysing the distribution of health within a population.11

Multiple studies have been conducted to assess the occurrence and extent of AD and dementia more generally.12–17 One such study performed a comprehensive analysis of published data from various regions and stages of AD.13 According to their findings, the overall prevalence of AD in Europe among individuals aged 50 and above was estimated to be 74.5 per million.13 In the USA, it is estimated that 6.7 million individuals aged 65 and over are living with AD. This represents approximately 10.8% of the US population aged 65 years and over.18

One of the primary risk factors of AD is age.18 There have been several studies that have examined the influence of age on the incidence and prevalence of AD.13 14 17 The prevalence of AD consistently increases with age. Differences in the prevalence of AD have also been observed due to sex. Some studies have found there to be a higher prevalence of AD and dementia more generally in women than in men,13 while others have not found an appreciable difference in incidence or prevalence related to sex.14 17 Other risk factors commonly associated with AD and dementia include genetics, such as the APOE-e4 gene and family history, such as those having first-degree relatives with AD.18 19 Additionally, it has been suggested that regions with significant socioeconomic deprivation and lifestyle factors such as physical activity, diet and smoking may be associated with an elevated risk of dementia.15 20 21

This study estimates the prevalence of diagnosed AD and diagnosed eAD (ie, a subgroup of patients with diagnosed AD) in the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) with linked secondary care data (Hospital Episode Statistics). The prevalence estimates are stratified by age, sex and deprivation and combinations thereof. Deprivation is estimated by the Index of Multiple Deprivation.

Data and methods

Data sources

The CPRD GOLD is a large data set in the UK which consists of longitudinally collected primary care medical records from participating practices across the UK.22 As of May 2022, there were approximately 21 million patients in the data set, approximately 3 million of whom were actively contributing data.23 Data is collected on demographics, symptoms, diagnoses, prescriptions, referrals and tests among other information. The January 2022 CPRD data cut was used for this study.

For a subset of CPRD contributing English practices, linkages to other data sources are possible. In this study, both the Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) Admitted Patient Care and Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 2019 data sources were used. HES collects data on all admissions to National Health Service (NHS) hospitals in England, including NHS funded admissions to private hospitals.24 The data includes demographics, admission and discharge dates, diagnoses and procedures among other data fields. The IMD is a multidimensional measure of relative deprivation of small areas in the UK and weights seven domains including income, employment and education to calculate the IMD score.25

This study was conducted under Independent Scientific Advisory Committee (ISAC) approved protocol 21_000650 (online supplemental appendix 1). Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Code lists

Read and International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code lists were identified through keyword and relevant code stem searches of the relevant code dictionaries. Alzheimer’s diagnostic Read and ICD code lists were created, these code lists included codes that were both specific and non-specific to AD. Dementia codes that were considered to be AD related were also included. Codes indicative of Lewy body dementia were identified to exclude patients. Additionally, code lists were created to identify AD onset, and included diagnostic, product and symptom code lists. The diagnosis code lists to identify early onset included specific early onset AD codes and related non-specific codes such as mild cognitive disorder and unspecified dementia. The product code list included pharmacological interventions used in the treatment of AD in the UK such as acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitors (eg, rivastigmine, donepezil, galantamine) and memantine. Symptom code lists consisted of Alzheimer’s-related symptoms that, when combined with an Alzheimer’s diagnostic code, could be used for detection of early onset and were generally related to memory deficiency such as mild cognitive impairment. Further diagnostic, product and symptom code lists were created to classify patients transitioning into moderate or severe stages of AD. All code lists are included in online supplemental appendix 2.

Population

The study population consisted of all acceptable patients who, on 1 January 2020, the point of interest (POI), were alive and contributing data at an up-to-standard (UTS) practice and had at least 1 year of prior follow-up in the CPRD. The population was limited to only those individuals eligible for linkage to HES and patient-level IMD data. Patient follow-up began at the latest of the patient index date and a practice UTS date. Patient follow-up ended at the earliest of death date, the date when the patient transferred out of the practice and the last collection date for the practice. To ensure continuous capture of general practitioner contacts, patients with multiple CPRD identifiers for a single HES identifier had their CPRD records appended if the gap between records was less than 30 days.26 Where larger gaps in follow-up were identified between CPRD identifiers linked to the same HES identifier, data that did not overlap the POI were excluded.

The index date for each individual in the study population was defined as the date of the earliest indicator of disease onset. Diagnostic codes, symptom codes, drug prescriptions (AChE inhibitor and memantine) and cognitive or functional tests with results that indicated mild cognitive impairment or eAD, and severe cognitive impairment or moderate-to-severe AD were used to determine the index date for each patient.

All patients with the primary outcome were considered to have diagnosed eAD from the point of their index date to the point at which indicators of progression to moderate or severe AD dementia were identified in their record. These indicators included Read codes suggesting moderate, severe or very severe cognitive decline, the use of memantine and cognitive test results indicating moderate-to-severe AD. If indicators of moderate or severe dementia were recorded at the index date or in the 30 days thereafter, patients were coded as having moderate or severe AD dementia cases from their index date. Therefore, patients were categorised as diagnosed eAD, that is, mild cognitive impairment or mild AD unless there were indicators of progression to moderate or severe AD.

Sex was defined using the variables available in the data set. Individuals with a sex other than male or female were dropped from all analyses. The patient’s year of birth was used to define a covariate categorising patient time into 10-year age intervals.

A covariate defining patient-level IMD quintiles was created using the linked 2019 IMD data. The first and fifth quintile of deprivation represents the most deprived and the least deprived, respectively. IMD serves as a valuable tool for evaluating the relationship between deprivation and disease incidence and prevalence. By considering various factors such as income, education, housing and healthcare accessibility, IMD offers a comprehensive picture of socioeconomic disparities within a region, supporting researchers and policymakers to identify areas where disease prevalence might be disproportionately high due to underlying social and economic inequalities. IMD quintiles have been previously used in several studies to assess relationships between AD and dementia with deprivation.21 27 28

Analysis

Two primary analyses were conducted. The first primary analysis estimated the prevalence of patients with a diagnosis of AD at the POI. The second outcome sought to identify the subgroup of patients with AD that has not yet progressed to moderate or severe dementia (ie, eAD) at the POI. Estimates of prevalence can be very sensitive to the way in which the period of time prior to the POI over which prevalent cases of disease are identified is defined (ie, look-back).29 As a result, several analyses were conducted to test the sensitivity of our results to these definitions (table 1). In the primary analysis of AD and eAD, the look-back observability requirement, that is, the period of time prior to the POI that must be observed in a patient’s record to define eligibility and the look-back (LB) were set at 3 years.

In addition, several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the sensitivity of the prevalence estimates to the population definition. The first population sensitivity analysis was conducted both on the AD and eAD populations and excluded those patients with non-specific dementia diagnostic codes and no codes indicative of an underlying cause of dementia. Two further population sensitivity analyses were conducted on the eAD population. The first was to assess the impact of not using memantine prescriptions to determine how many patients progressed into the moderate/severe AD-dementia stage and therefore exclude them from the eAD prevalence estimate. The second population sensitivity analysis on eAD assessed the impact of additionally using symptom codes in determining how many patients progressed to moderate/severe AD dementia and were therefore excluded from the eAD prevalence estimation.

The prevalence per 100 000 patients was estimated as the prevalence numerator divided by the prevalence denominator multiplied by 100 000. Prevalence was estimated overall and within the categories defined by each of the covariates. The 95% CIs were calculated for all prevalence estimates using the methods described by Agresti and Coull.30 31 Equations for the 95% lower (CIL) and upper (CIU) limits were given by Equations 1 and 2, respectively.

The prevalence estimates for the primary analyses were stratified by age, sex and deprivation. All analyses were conducted in R V.4.1.2.32

Results

There were 448 797 patients identified in the CPRD that satisfied the study inclusion criteria and were eligible for HES and IMD linkage. For the main analysis of AD and eAD, the LB observability requirement was 3 years, which resulted in 379 763 patients eligible for inclusion in the denominator.

Alzheimer’s disease

The estimated prevalence of diagnosed AD at the POI was 378.39 (95% CI, 359.36 to 398.44) per 100 000.

Stratification by age demonstrated an increasing prevalence trend with age (figure 1A), with a minimal number of patients diagnosed below 50 years old. The prevalence was similar across deprivation quintiles, with no obvious trend. The prevalence of diagnosed AD was found to be much higher in women than in men, with women having an incidence approximately 28.9% higher than men.

Prevalence of diagnosed Alzheimer’s disease per 100 000 of the population (A) stratified by age, deprivation and sex individually, (B) stratified by a combination of age and deprivation, and sex and deprivation, and (C) stratified by a combination of age, sex, deprivation. The first quintile (1) of deprivation indicated the most deprived and the fifth quintile (5) represents the least deprived.

Further stratification between deprivation quintile and age indicated a slight trend in the prevalence between deprivation groups by age categories (figure 1B). Broadly, as the deprivation decreased from the most deprived to the least deprived quintile of the population, the prevalence of diagnosed AD decreased among age categories. For example, in the 70–79 years old age group the prevalence was approximately 69% higher in the most deprived versus the least deprived quintile. For the younger age groups, estimates were very uncertain because of the limited number of patients with AD in these age categories, in some cases no patients with AD were identified. Conversely, no clear prevalence trends were observed between deprivation quintiles and sex.

The most granular level of stratification estimated the diagnosed AD prevalence by sex, age and deprivation quintile (figure 1C). This resulted in smaller numbers of patients across categories, and as a result, increased the uncertainty of prevalence estimates as indicated by the wide CIs. Among women, as the deprivation of the population increased, a generally increasing trend in AD was observed for age groups. For example, women aged of 60–69 years old had an estimated prevalence of 176.16 (95% CI, 82.60 to 353.97) per 100 000 in the fourth quintile, which increased for each deprivation quintile to women in the most deprived quintile of the population having an estimated prevalence of 265.02 (95% CI, 131.01 to 511.42) per 100 000. This represents an increase in prevalence of approximately 50% for women aged 60–69 years between the fourth and the most deprived quintiles of the population. For men, consistent trends are less apparent, however, there were significant differences between the least and most deprived quintiles. For example, among men aged 80–89 years, the prevalence was approximately 15.8% higher in the most deprived quintile compared with the least deprived. The strength of these conclusions is limited by the large uncertainties associated with the point estimates.

There was significant variation in the prevalence of diagnosed AD when assessing the sensitivity to the chosen LB periods, with an estimated prevalence as low as 137.48 (95% CI, 127.05 to 148.76) per 100 000. However, the pattern of variation among the prevalence estimates was consistent with the expected prevalence pattern, given the LB definitions. In the population sensitivity analysis, where only AD-specific codes were used, there was a decrease in the overall prevalence from 378.39 (95% CI, 359.36 to 398.44) to 197.33 (95% CI, 183.68 to 211.99) per 100 000. There are large uncertainties associated with the point estimates in this analysis as the number of patients was comparatively fewer.

Early Alzheimer’s disease

The estimated prevalence of diagnosed eAD at the point of interest was 292.81 (95% CI, 276.12 to 310.52) per 100 000.

Stratification of eAD by age indicated an increasing prevalence trend as age increased (figure 2A). Those aged 80–89 years had almost 100 times greater prevalence than those aged 50–59 years. There was no evidence of a trend in the prevalence of diagnosed eAD when stratifying only by deprivation quintiles. Women had a higher estimated diagnosed eAD prevalence of approximately 33.6% compared with men.

Prevalence of diagnosed early Alzheimer’s disease per 100 000 of the population (A) stratified by age, deprivation and sex individually, (B) stratified by a combination of age and deprivation, and sex and deprivation, and (C) stratified by a combination of age, sex, deprivation. The first quintile (1) of deprivation indicated the most deprived and the fifth quintile (5) represents the least deprived.

Stratification by age categories and deprivation quintiles indicated that generally there was a higher prevalence for age categories as the deprivation increased, however the trend was somewhat variable (figure 2B). The absence of a clear trend was likely driven by the small patient numbers resulting in large uncertainty in the prevalence estimates. Similarly, no clear trend is observed as the deprivation increases when stratified by sex. Women consistently have a higher estimated diagnosed eAD prevalence.

The eAD population size when stratifying by age, sex and deprivation becomes small. Although the uncertainties were relatively large, relatively consistent trends were observed, except in younger age categories, where few if any patients were included in the numerators. Among women, an increasing trend by deprivation for age groups was apparent, although not always consistent (figure 2C). Women aged 70–79 years had a higher estimated prevalence of almost 100% being in the most deprived quintile compared with the least deprived quintile. For men, the difference was not as large. In general, those most deprived had a higher estimated prevalence of diagnosed eAD compared with those who were least deprived; however, the pattern of change across deprivation quintiles was mixed.

There was significant variation in prevalence when assessing the sensitivity with the LB periods, as low as 120.54 (95% CI, 110.80 to 131.14) per 100 000, and as high as 519.01 (95% CI, 496.64 to 542.37) per 100 000. However, the pattern of variation among the prevalence estimates was consistent with the LB definitions. The population sensitivity analysis that used only specific AD diagnostic codes to determine diagnoses and hence prevalence, estimated the diagnosed prevalence to be 124.42 (95% CI, 113.67 to 136.18) per 100 000. This represented a decrease of approximately 57.5% compared with the primary analysis of eAD. In the second population sensitivity analysis of eAD, memantine product codes were not used to determine progression to moderate/severe disease. Thus, more patients were classed as eAD compared with the main analysis of eAD, which resulted in an estimated prevalence of 332.58 (95% CI, 314.75 to 351.40). The third population sensitivity analysis used symptom codes indicative of moderate-to-severe disease to determine progression, in addition to diagnostic codes indicative of moderate or severe dementia, cognitive or functional tests with results indicative of moderate-to-severe dementia or prescriptions for memantine, a drug recommended by NICE for patients with moderate or severe AD. As expected, this meant more patients transitioned to moderate or severe disease; as a result, the overall prevalence of diagnosed eAD was smaller than in the main analysis (224.88 (95% CI, 210.30 to 240.46)).

Discussion

Understanding the prevalence spectrum across various population characteristics of a disease is important to understand potential sources of inequality. This study used the CPRD linked with HES and IMD to estimate the prevalence of diagnosed AD and eAD at the POI. The prevalence was estimated per 100 000 patients for AD and eAD, both overall and within categories defined by age, sex and deprivation and combinations of those covariates. In addition, several sensitivity analyses were explored. These included only using specific diagnostic codes, omitting the use of memantine product codes for transition to moderate or severe disease and using symptom codes to determine progression to moderate or severe disease. Additionally, several LB periods were also assessed to test the sensitivity of results.

The prevalence estimates for the entire population under study exhibited considerable variation among the analyses conducted, with estimates ranging from 137.48 (95% CI, 127.05 to 148.76) to 796.55 (95% CI, 768.77 to 825.33) per 100 000. In the main analysis, the diagnosed prevalence was estimated to be 378.39 (95% CI, 359.36 to 398.44) per 100 000. However, when a sensitivity analysis was performed, considering only specific diagnostic codes for determining AD diagnoses, the prevalence decreased to 197.33 (95% CI, 183.68 to 211.99) per 100 000. Comparing our findings to a study that synthesised published literature on AD prevalence, it was observed that the overall prevalence across all AD categories in Europe, among individuals aged 50 and above, was reported as 74.5 per million.13 Notably, this estimate falls within the range of estimates obtained in our study. Had our study limited participation to individuals aged 50 and over, it is plausible that the prevalence estimates would have increased, with the main analysis estimate potentially converging toward the published value.

The overall diagnosed eAD prevalence in the main analysis was estimated to be 292.81 (95% CI, 276.12 to 310.52) per 100 000 of the population. Therefore, at the POI, we estimated that a little more than a quarter of patients with AD were considered to have moderate-to-severe disease (overall AD prevalence less eAD prevalence). However, the estimated prevalence was dependent on the LB definition, for example, under LB sensitivity analysis 2, the prevalence of eAD was as high as 519.01 (95% CI, 496.64 to 542.37) per 100 000. Although there is little published literature estimating the prevalence of eAD, the prevalence of preclinical and prodromal AD in Europe for those aged over 50 has been estimated to be 52.3 (95% CI, 43.6 to 61.0) and 15.2 (95% CI, 9.5 to 23.6) million persons, respectively.13 Together, these estimates approximate a value close to that estimated in LB sensitivity analysis 2 of eAD.13 However, if this study had limited its population to those over 50 only, the main analysis may have more closely approximated these estimates from the literature.

Both in the main analysis and sensitivity analyses of the AD and eAD populations, similar patterns were observed with respect to the covariates. For eAD, increasing trends in prevalence were observed as age increased and women had a higher prevalence than men. Women were estimated to have a 33.6% higher prevalence of diagnosed eAD than men. This difference was generally consistent across all analyses. The LB sensitivity analyses for eAD varied analogously to the AD LB analyses, although the relative differences in prevalence between the main analysis and LB sensitivity analyses varied between the analysis of AD and eAD. For AD, as expected, the prevalence increased as age increased. Also common across analyses was that the estimated prevalence for women was higher than for men. Women were estimated to have a 28.9% overall higher prevalence than men in the AD population. This difference in prevalence has been observed in other studies.13 33 When stratifying by deprivation (IMD) only, there was some variation between the quintiles but no clear trend or pattern. However, a common pattern across the AD analyses for deprivation became apparent when stratifying by age and deprivation, as well as by age, deprivation and sex. A somewhat consistent trend of increasing prevalence was observed for age categories as deprivation increased, for both sexes together and individually. For example, for patients aged 70–79 years, the prevalence was approximately 69% higher in the most deprived versus the least deprived quintile. Similarly, large differences were observed for other age groups between the least and most deprived quintiles. The main analysis of AD was supported by similar results in the sensitivity analysis that used only specific diagnostic codes.

The observed pattern of prevalence with deprivation among the analyses stratified by age and age-sex indicates a positive correlation between deprivation and AD diagnosis, that is, higher levels of deprivation are associated with higher levels of AD diagnosis. This pattern has also been observed in dementia.21 28 34 35 Further, this link has also been explored in genetic biobank studies and have found the same conclusion.15 36 However, it is possible that this correlation between deprivation and AD diagnosis may be even stronger in magnitude compared with the results of this analysis due to underdiagnosis. There are several reports which have discussed the issue of underdiagnosis of AD37 and dementia diagnoses among ethnic minority groups.38 39 This combined with the known links between deprivation and ethnic minorities in England40 could indicate that the positive correlation between deprivation and AD diagnoses may be larger in magnitude in the general population compared with the results of this analysis.

The results of this study and of other related studies suggest that age, sex and deprivation are influential factors on the prevalence of AD.13 21 28 33 34 Age is a significant risk factor, with the incidence and prevalence of AD increasing with advancing age. As diagnosis of AD improves, it is plausible that incidence and prevalence of AD in younger populations may increase. While it has been observed in this study and many other studies consistently reporting a higher prevalence of AD among women compared with men,13 33 the influence of sex on the prevalence of AD requires further research and differences could be as a result of biological, genetic, hormonal or social factors, or combinations thereof. The relationship between deprivation and AD is complex and while individuals living in areas of high socioeconomic deprivation appear to be more susceptible to developing AD, the underlying cause is multifactorial and likely includes limited access to healthcare, educational resources and unhealthy lifestyle factors. Therefore, addressing the underlying socioeconomic factors is essential in reducing health inequalities associated with AD.

Deprivation can have a significant impact on the overall health of an individual, further, the accumulation of deprived individuals can have substantial impact on a country’s healthcare system.41 A study conducted by the Northern Health Science Alliance found that people in ‘left behind’ neighbourhoods had a life expectancy of 7.5 fewer years in good health and had higher prevalence of common health conditions.42 This increases the strain on the national health system and has a large economic impact from the loss of productivity. No specific study was available in the literature that estimated the cost of Alzheimer’s in England, however, one study estimated that the total annualised cost for people with dementia in England was £24.2 billion (2015 prices).43 The economic and societal burden of AD for the healthcare system can be significant.44 Further, combined with the results from this study which highlights the growing prevalence of AD with age, this could present substantial pressures and challenges for the national healthcare system. There are many health inequalities present in England that impact various aspects of peoples lives.45 For example, the inequality in deprivation is closely related to life expectancy, healthy life expectancy, avoidable mortality and long-term health conditions.45 Related to socioeconomic inequalities, there is also health inequity. There has been a growing interest in considering health inequity in the assessment and reimbursement of new technologies.46 47 The estimated prevalence findings in this study could provide insights to assess potential impacts on equity of novel interventions in AD. For example, these estimates could be used to inform a distributional cost-effectiveness to assess the impact of a new intervention in AD on various subgroups of the target population.48

This study has contributed to the growing body of evidence suggesting a positive association between deprivation and AD diagnoses. Our findings align with the broader understanding that socioeconomic factors can significantly impact disease prevalence, outcomes and treatment across various health conditions. In addition to AD, numerous studies have explored the intricate relationship between deprivation and other diseases. For instance, investigations into cardiovascular disease49 50 have revealed how socioeconomic disparities can affect disease risk. Similarly, studies on rheumatoid arthritis51 have shed light on the interplay between socioeconomic status and deprivation and disease outcomes. Furthermore, a study of patients with chronic kidney disease52 has shown how deprivation can contribute to the burden of renal diseases. Collectively, these investigations underscore the need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary approaches to address health disparities and promote equitable access to healthcare services.

The study has several limitations that must be considered when interpretating the results. The accurate estimation of prevalence ideally requires complete patient follow-up for a population for their entire lifetime. As the CPRD does not provide this; to accurately capture the number of people diagnosed with AD, we required that an AD diagnostic code was recorded somewhere in the patient’s record. This may have occurred at the point of registration with a practice contributing to the CPRD, as demonstrated by others,53 however if it does not it must then be recorded at a later point at which a medical history is taken or, given the use of HES data, at the point of a hospitalisation related to AD. One might therefore argue in favour of including the maximum amount of follow-up per patient to maximise sensitivity. The risk of such an approach is that the length of LB time available may vary considerably across patients; therefore, it can be difficult to define exactly what the prevalence estimate relates to and to compare it with other estimates in the literature. As a result, we chose to use one option for our main analysis and to test sensitivity to a series of alternatives in subsequent analyses in an attempt to assess the impact of differing patient follow-up all of which demonstrated relatively consistent trends.

Results were also highly sensitive to the exclusion of patients with non-specific diagnostic codes. That is, in this study, there were many patients with non-specific dementia codes but no codes indicative of the underlying aetiology. In the base-case analysis, these patients were included as having AD, given that the majority of patients with dementia also have AD. However, we are aware that this may reduce our specificity; to address this issue, we explored the effect on prevalence estimates in a sensitivity analysis by excluding these patients from our numerator and denominator. It is likely the true prevalence of AD sits somewhere between the results of these analyses. However, given the literature suggests that 60–80% of dementia cases have AD,13 54 the true results might be expected to lie closer to those of our main analysis. Within these sensitivity analyses, trends were relatively consistent across covariates.

This study describes the prevalence of diagnosed AD and diagnosed eAD since only patients that have a recorded diagnosis in the data can be identified. The overall prevalence of AD (diagnosed and undiagnosed) is likely higher, and it may be the case that there are important differences between diagnosed and undiagnosed patients. While our study did not investigate this it is an important question for future work to address. The external validity of the estimated prevalence of diagnosed AD are also dependent on the extent to which the sample population identified in the CPRD is representative of the target population, that is, the population of England. The CPRD has been well established in its representativeness and generalisability to the general population.22 Further, there is some evidence demonstrating the representativeness of linked CPRD GOLD data with socioeconomic status measures including the IMD.55

Beyond these limitations, another important consideration is the data source itself. In this study, the CPRD GOLD electronic healthcare records database was used, and in recent years, there has been a decline in the number of English practices contributing to CPRD GOLD due to the transition to CPRD AURUM. Nevertheless, during the assessment of this study’s feasibility, it was determined that the sample size of CPRD GOLD was adequate to achieve the required statistical power and effectively address our research question.

A further limitation that may impact the accuracy of the overall prevalence estimates observed in our analysis is that tests conducted in primary care may have been recorded as free-text notes. As we do not have access to free-text notes, all of those with test results indicating AD, eAD or moderate-to-severe AD, may not have been captured as part of the numerator and could result in underestimating the prevalence. Similarly, the prevalence of eAD could be overestimated relative to the overall prevalence of AD if the transition to moderate-to-severe AD was recorded only in the free-text notes.

Conclusion

The CPRD, linked with HES and IMD, was used to generate estimates of the prevalence of AD in England, overall, divided between eAD and moderate/severe AD, and stratified by age, sex and deprivation. The study found relatively consistent patterns of prevalence across both AD and eAD populations. Generally, the prevalence of AD increased with age and increased with deprivation for each age category. Women had a higher prevalence than men.

Given the potential association of deprivation and AD prevalence, targeting the modifiable socioeconomic risk factors that contribute to deprivation could support in reducing health inequalities associated with AD. Public health officials along with government agencies should develop initiatives and effective strategies for addressing socioeconomically deprived populations.
