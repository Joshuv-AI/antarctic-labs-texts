Association between gout duration and subclinical carotid arterial stiffness in male patients with gout: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Geun-Tae Kim, Joonho Jeong, Yun-Kyung Kim, Seung-Geun Lee, Aran Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gout has been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, but direct evidence on carotid vascular changes in patients with gout remains limited. This study evaluated carotid arterial stiffness and factors associated with arterial stiffness in male patients with gout. This retrospective observational study included 68 male patients with gout and 64 male patients with osteoarthritis as a comparator group. Carotid intima-media thickness and arterial stiffness index β (β-index) were assessed using carotid ultrasonography, and factors associated with the β-index were evaluated using correlation and regression analyses. Propensity score matching was performed for adjusted between-group comparison. In the overall cohort, the β-index was higher in patients with gout than in patients with osteoarthritis (11.1 ± 5.2 vs 8.7 ± 3.7, P = .003). This difference remained significant after propensity score matching (11.2 ± 5.3 vs 8.5 ± 3.0, P = .007), and the proportion of patients with a β-index >10.1 was also higher in the matched gout group (53.8% vs 25.6%, P = .021). In patients with gout, longer gout duration was associated with a higher β-index in crude and age-adjusted linear regression analyses (P = .019 and P = .036, respectively) and with a β-index >10.1 in logistic regression analysis (odds ratio, 1.205; 95% confidence interval, 1.033–1.406; P = .018). Male patients with gout showed higher carotid arterial stiffness than those with osteoarthritis, and longer gout duration was associated with increased arterial stiffness. These findings suggest that gout duration may be associated with subclinical carotid vascular changes.

1. Introduction

Gout is known to be the most frequent inflammatory arthritis, with an estimated prevalence of 3.9% in the United States,[1] and 2.5% in Britain.[2] The disease mechanism involves inflammation induced by the deposition of monosodium urate crystals in the joint space due to chronically increased serum uric acid levels. The disease is characterized by acute onset of pain, recurrent episodes of acute arthritis, and chronic joint disruption.[3,4] Gout was found to be an independent risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.[5–7] In addition, there is an association between serum uric acid levels and cardiovascular disease.[8–11] Both acute and chronic inflammation are not only risk factors for cardiovascular disease but are also associated with an early onset of cardiovascular disease.[12,13] However, data are limited to epidemiological studies with a paucity of information on vascular changes among patients with gout. Given the findings, early detection and control of atherosclerotic changes can help improve long-term prognosis.[14] Subclinical atherosclerosis is an early finding and can also be a risk factor for clinical vascular disease in patients with gout. Noninvasive tests conducted for the evaluation of atherosclerosis in clinical settings include pulse-wave velocity (PWV), incremental elastic modulus, arterial distensibility, compliance, arterial stiffness index β (β-index), and carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT). Measurement of the cIMT using ultrasonography has been found useful in estimating cardiovascular risk. However, it has limitations, particularly after structural changes in the vessel occur. Recent advances in the use of the β-index to assess temporal changes in vessel diameter over time, especially in B-mode or M-mode ultrasonography, have improved accuracy and reproducibility. These improvements are largely due to the development of e-tracking technology, which automatically tracks vessel wall movements and diameters.[15] In patients with gout, data are limited to epidemiological studies[16] with a paucity of information on vascular changes. Moreover, several studies suggested that urate-lowering agents can modulate vascular parameters or the incidence of arterial hypertension.[17–19] However, there are few studies directly presenting the association between urate-lowering agents and vascular parameters in patients with gout, except for a recent clinical trial.[20] This study aimed to evaluate subclinical atherosclerosis and arterial stiffness through cIMT and β-index using ultrasound with the e-tracking method in gout patients and a control group (patients with osteoarthritis). We also sought to identify the clinical factors that affect the arterial stiffness of the carotid artery in patients with gout.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

We reviewed the electronic medical records of gout and osteoarthritis (OA) patients who visited the rheumatology outpatient clinic at a university hospital from January 22, 2013, to January 21, 2015. The data based on the medical records were accessed on February 1, 2022. Authors did not access information that could identify individual participants after data collection. The study included 132 patients: 68 patients had gouty arthritis, and 64 patients had OA with no history of gout or autoimmune disease. All patients with gout fulfilled 1977 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for gout.[21] All patients with OA fulfilled several criteria for OA.[22–24] All subjects provided consent to undergo carotid ultrasound. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥18 years, no history of diagnosis of coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease at baseline, and no evidence of viral or bacterial infection at baseline. Patients who qualified for any of the following criteria were excluded: patients with preexisting cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease at baseline, symptoms of coronary artery disease or cerebrovascular disease, diagnosis of other inflammatory disease or autoimmune disease, acute phase of gouty arthritis at baseline or during the study, and patients who received lipid-lowering agents. Patients with OA were selected as a non-gout rheumatology control group because they were recruited from the same outpatient clinical setting during the same study period, had available carotid ultrasonography data, and had no history of gout or autoimmune inflammatory disease. Conventional cardiovascular risk factors were assessed and addressed through regression analyses and propensity score (PS) matching.

Data on baseline patient characteristics and clinical outcomes were obtained from outpatient medical records. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Kosin University Gospel Hospital (#IRB No. 2020-06-001). The requirement for informed consent of patients was waived as all data were anonymized prior to analysis.

2.2. Laboratory measurements

Results associated with the following investigations were retrieved: complete blood count with differential counts, serum chemistry, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglycerides. Serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate were included to determine renal function. Serum uric acid levels and inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rates were also measured.

2.3. Carotid ultrasonography

An experienced physician performed B-mode ultrasound (ProSound α7, Aloka) of the carotid artery, and an e-tracking study was performed on the right common carotid artery (CCA) using a real-time 13 MHz linear scanner to assess arterial stiffness (β-index). The internal diameter of the CCA at end-diastole (Dd) and peak-systole (Ds) was measured by continually tracing the intimal-luminal interface wall of the near and far wall of the CCA. The results were then averaged.[25] The measurements of the carotid artery IMT were performed at a distance of approximately 1 cm from the carotid artery bifurcation, without atherosclerotic plaque; in addition, far-wall IMT measurements were also performed.[26] For this retrospective analysis, carotid ultrasonography measurements were obtained from archived ultrasound reports and electronic medical records. The ultrasound examinations had been performed as part of a clinical evaluation, and the recorded cIMT and β-index values were extracted for analysis.

2.4. Clinical outcomes and definitions

To assess the risk of preclinical atherosclerosis, β-index and cIMT were measured by carotid ultrasound. Diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, alcohol consumption, and tobacco smoking were also considered. DM was defined as a blood glucose level of >126 mg/dL after overnight fasting for more than 8 hours, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1C) ≥6.5%, or intake of an oral hypoglycemic agent/insulin injection. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg or use of antihypertensive medications. Dyslipidemia was defined in accordance with the Korean clinical guidelines.[27] Smoking status was classified into current smokers and nonsmokers; in addition, pack-years of smoking were also investigated. Alcohol drinkers were patients who consumed >60 g alcohol/wk. The following formula was used to calculate the β-index: β-index = ln (systolic blood pressure/diastolic blood pressure) × [Dd/ (Ds − Dd)].

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student t test or the chi-square test in the overall cohort and paired tests, including paired t test and McNemar test, in the propensity score-matched cohort, as appropriate. Covariate balance after propensity score matching was assessed using standardized differences. Because this retrospective study analyzed all eligible patients with available carotid ultrasonography data during the study period, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. The study sample was therefore determined by the number of patients who met the eligibility criteria and had available carotid ultrasonography data. β-index and carotid intima-media thickness were used as markers of subclinical carotid vascular changes. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine the correlations of these vascular parameters with clinical variables in the gout and osteoarthritis groups. Regression analyses were performed to examine clinical variables associated with β-index in the overall study population and in patients with gout. PS matching was performed to reduce between-group differences in selected baseline characteristics. The PS model included age, serum uric acid, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, current smoking, and estimated glomerular filtration rate. Variables representing study outcomes, including β-index and cIMT, were not included in the PS model. The PS-matched cohort was used for the adjusted between-group comparison of β-index and carotid intima-media thickness. In contrast, analyses of clinical factors associated with β-index among patients with gout were performed in the full gout cohort, because gout duration was defined only in patients with gout. Logistic regression analysis was performed in the gout cohort to examine factors associated with significant arterial stiffness, defined as β-index >10.1 according to a previous study.[25] The results of logistic regression analyses are presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Crude and age-adjusted multiple linear regression analyses were conducted for the gout group. Variables with P values >.2 in univariate analysis were not included in multivariable analysis. P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Data management and statistical analyses were performed with the use of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 24; IBM Corp.) and the R statistical software (version 3.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing)

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

A total of 132 male patients who underwent carotid ultrasonography, including 68 gout patients and 64 osteoarthritis patients (control group), were included in the analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients in the gout and control groups are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of patients in the gout and control groups was 60.9 ± 10.8 years and 57.6 ± 11.1 years (P = .084), respectively. The mean serum uric acid levels in the gout and control groups were 6.4 ± 2.1 and 5.3 ± 1.5 (P < .001), respectively. The proportion of patients with serum uric acid levels <6 mg/dL in the gout and control groups was 45.9% (31/68) and 71.9% (46/64), respectively. The proportion of patients with DM in the gout and control groups was 17 (25.0%) and 6 (9.4%) (P = .018), respectively. The proportion of patients with hypertension in the gout and control groups was 37 (54.4%) and 12 (18.8%) (P < .001), respectively. There was no significant between-group difference with respect to prevalence of current smoking and alcohol consumption (P = .546,.304). The mean β-index in the gout group was significantly higher than that in the control group (11.1 ± 5.2 vs 8.7 ± 3.7, P = .003). However, there was no significant between-group difference with respect to cIMT, carotid plaque, neutrophil count, neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (P = .571, .101, .618, .565, and .056, respectively).

3.2. Correlation between β-index and cIMT with clinical variables in the study population

In the current study, the surrogate markers for preclinical atherosclerosis were β-index, a parameter of arterial stiffness, and cIMT. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the correlation of β-index and cIMT with clinical variables. In the control group, age showed a high positive correlation with β-index and cIMT. In the gout group, both the duration of gout and age showed a positive correlation with β-index and cIMT (Table 2, Fig. S1A and B, Supplemental Digital Content 1).

3.3. Comparison of clinical parameters after propensity score (PS) matching

PS matching was performed to balance selected cardiovascular risk factors and renal function. After matching, age, serum uric acid, estimated glomerular filtration rate, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and current smoking were well balanced between the 2 groups (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2 and Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 3). We then compared carotid vascular parameters (β-index and cIMT), the proportion of patients with β-index >10.1,[25] inflammatory markers, and lipid profiles in the PS-matched cohort. Both the mean β-index and the proportion of patients with β-index >10.1 were significantly higher in the gout group than in the OA group (11.2 ± 5.3 vs 8.5 ± 3.0, P = .007; 53.8% vs 25.6%, P = .021). In contrast, cIMT, inflammatory marker levels, and lipid profiles did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 4).

3.4. Comparison of clinical and vascular parameters according to the target serum urate level

In the gout cohort, the β-index was higher in patients with serum urate levels ≥6.0 mg/dL than in those with serum urate levels <6.0 mg/dL, whereas cIMT did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

3.5. Identification of clinical factors associated with arterial stiffness

We evaluated clinical factors associated with arterial stiffness in the overall study population and in patients with gout. In multiple regression analysis of the overall study population, age was significantly associated with the β-index (P = .013) (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 6). In crude multiple regression analysis of patients with gout, gout duration was significantly associated with the β-index (P = .019). After adjustment for age, gout duration and current smoking were significantly associated with the β-index (P = .036 and P = .030, respectively) (Table 3). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, age and gout duration were independently associated with a β-index >10.1, with odds ratios of 1.139 (95% CI, 1.048–1.238; P = .002) and 1.205 (95% CI, 1.033–1.406; P = .018), respectively (Table 4).

4. Discussion

In the current study, the β-index was higher in patients with gout than in patients with osteoarthritis in both the overall and propensity score-matched cohorts, whereas cIMT did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. In patients with gout, longer disease duration was associated with an increased β-index in both crude and age-adjusted analyses.

Previous studies primarily focused on the relationship between serum uric acid levels and the risk of cardiovascular disease.[10,28] In addition, several recent studies have shown that gout patients who received urate-lowering therapy may have a lower risk of cardiovascular disease.[29] These results suggest that changes in vascular structure and function induced by serum uric acid levels may play a pivotal role in the development of cardiovascular disease.[30] However, previous studies regarding the effects of urate-lowering agents on carotid vascular parameters showed conflicting results. Two clinical trials showed that non-purine xanthine oxidase inhibitor (febuxostat) could be more effective than purine xanthine oxidase inhibitor (allopurinol) in improving PWV[20,31]; however, another clinical trial showed that febuxostat did not delay progression of carotid atherosclerosis compared to the control group.[32] In addition, another epidemiological study and the Framingham Heart Study group suggested that the serum uric acid level is not an an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease.[33,34] The lack of clear consensus on the association between serum uric acid levels and atherosclerosis is likely attributable to the following reasons. First, elevated serum uric acid levels are associated with a number of traditional cardiovascular risk factors.[10] Hypertension, prehypertension, metabolic syndrome, vascular endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress can increase serum uric acid levels. Additionally, hyperuricemia may be observed in patients with preeclampsia or vascular disease, and serum urate levels may vary depending on sex and race.[35] Second, there is no robust evidence to suggest that elevated serum uric acid levels directly affect the occurrence of cardiovascular disease or atherosclerosis. Most of the published studies were epidemiological studies, with conflicting results.[20] Third, patients with relatively severe disease are more likely to be involved in each study, as are patients who have already started urate-lowering therapy. Fourth, it is possible that differences in the degree of disease control and adherence to medication may have been overlooked within the patient population. The association between gout and atherosclerosis is assumed to play an important role. However, hyperuricemia alone seems insufficient to elucidate relevance. Therefore, the pathogenesis of gout, disease duration, disease severity, endothelial dysfunction induced by reactive oxygen species, and chronic inflammation should be considered in addition to hyperuricemia.[10,13,36–38]

The available evidence suggests that subclinical inflammation may contribute to cardiovascular disease and persistent inflammation may be associated with arterial stiffness.[12,39] Notably, previous ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging studies for patients with gout have demonstrated subclinical inflammation in the early stages.[40,41] In addition, patients with gout treated using antitumor necrosis factor agents or interleukin-6 inhibitors showed significantly lower PWV compared with patients in the control group.[42,43] In other words, persistent inflammation and pro-inflammatory cytokines are likely to contribute to the risk of cardiovascular disease regardless of the symptoms in patients with gout. Thus, it may be important to evaluate subclinical atherosclerosis using noninvasive tests in clinical practice. Measurement of cIMT was extensively used because of its reproducibility and easy accessibility.[44] However, an increase in cIMT is not synonymous with subclinical atherosclerosis.[45] Additionally, functional assessment of vessels such as vascular rigidity, elasticity, and expandability cannot be fully evaluated. Therefore, the β-index measurements using ultrasound with the e-tracking method may be helpful for the evaluation of arterial stiffness and estimating subclinical carotid atherosclerosis. Recently, an ultrasound study of 698 asymptomatic patients demonstrated significant increases in the cIMT and β-index values in 136 patients with hyperuricemia. Moreover, age and uric acid levels were identified as risk factors for cardiovascular disease.[46] However, arterial stiffness was not adjusted for age in the analysis; moreover, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, blood glucose, and blood pressure levels were different between the hyperuricemia and control groups in that study. Unlike a prior study,[46] the current study evaluated surrogate markers for subclinical carotid arterial stiffness after adjusting for age.

A strength of this study is the use of carotid ultrasonography with e-tracking and the inclusion of a non-gout rheumatology control group. We also identified factors that could influence subclinical arterial stiffness and β-index >10.1. In this regard, the study and its findings may have clinical significance. However, some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and no a priori sample size calculation was performed because the study included all eligible patients with available carotid ultrasonography data during the study period. These associations should be considered preliminary, and prospective confirmation in larger-scale studies is imperative to establish clinical significance. Second, although OA patients were selected as a non-gout rheumatology control group from the same clinical setting, residual differences in baseline vascular risk may remain. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with consideration of possible residual confounding despite adjustment and PS matching. Third, carotid ultrasound for evaluation of preclinical atherosclerosis was performed only once. Fourth, not all patients with gout received urate-lowering agents, which may be another confounding factor. Fifth, we presented PS matching results to balance the different baseline characteristics in the supplementary materials. However, PS matching with a small sample size does not eliminate confounding factors. Lastly, this was a single-center study which involved Korean patients; thus, our results may not be generalizable to other populations.

5. Conclusion

In this retrospective observational study, male patients with gout showed higher carotid arterial stiffness than patients with osteoarthritis, whereas carotid intima-media thickness did not differ significantly between the groups. Among patients with gout, longer disease duration was associated with an increased β-index. These findings suggest that gout duration may be associated with subclinical carotid vascular changes, but confirmation in larger prospective studies is required.
