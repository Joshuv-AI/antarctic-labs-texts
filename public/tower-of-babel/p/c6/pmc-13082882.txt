The generation and consequences of N-terminal proteoform diversity
Authors: Evan J. Morrison, Olivia S. Rissland
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYN-terminal proteoforms are protein variants with altered N termini that arise from RNA-driven processes, such as alternative promoter usage, splicing, and translation initiation site usage, as well as protein alterations, such as N-terminal processing and modifications. While our understanding of these mechanisms has grown substantially over the past decade, the interplay between RNA-driven processes and N-terminal proteoforms has received less attention. Here, we summarize recent advancements in our understanding of N-terminal proteoform creation, function, and usage. We highlight advances in alternative translation initiation site usage as well as newly discovered N-degron pathways, and we explore how differences in N termini via processing and modifications can give rise to different N-degrons: elements at the extreme N terminus of proteins that trigger turnover. Many regulatory pathways converge on the N terminus and corresponding nucleotides, and an integrated, multidisciplinary approach holds the potential to reveal new insights into gene regulation and function.

INTRODUCTION

For decades, the central dogma has been the organizing principle for understanding how genetic information flows in the cell, but long gone is the idea that one gene produces a single transcript isoform, which then produces a single protein isoform. We now know that processes like transcription, splicing, and 3′ end processing generate multiple RNA isoforms and can, in turn, change the encoded protein or its expression. For instance, alternative exon inclusion can lead to inclusion of a key regulatory domain that can impact its activity or create a truncated protein with altered localization.1 Similarly, changes in poly(A) site usage can shorten the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), which contains much of the post-transcriptional regulatory information, and so modulate overall protein expression.2,3 Finally, use of alternative translation start sites can enable the production of multiple protein isoforms (also known as proteoforms) from the same transcript, which can again affect protein activity, localization, and dynamics. Our ability to detect, predict, and measure these isoforms, however, has outpaced our ability to interpret the functional roles and differences of different proteoforms, leaving open the question of what role such isoforms play in biology.

An area of crosstalk that has escaped substantial attention is how many regulatory mechanisms converge on the start of the open reading frame (ORF).4 For instance, on the one hand, the start codon and the surrounding nucleotides play a critical job regulating in translation initiation,5 and, indeed, this function of these nucleotides has led to the predominant perspective that changes in this region most often affect how much protein is produced. On the other hand, this region also encodes the N terminus of the protein, a region that is also a hotbed of post-translation regulation involving protein modifications, localization, and recognition by the ubiquitin-proteasome system. In other words, alternative transcription start sites, splicing events, and translation initiation sites do not just impact the life cycle of the RNA, but they also have the potential to change the N terminus of the protein, with potentially profound effects on gene expression and function.

The fields of transcription, splicing, translation, and protein degradation have a long history of important discoveries and concepts, and our discussion here is not intended as a comprehensive review. Instead, our goal is to highlight the rich potential interface between RNA-facing processes, such as transcription and translation initiation, and protein-facing events, especially protein degradation, and to motivate future research in this area. We suspect that the examples we highlight below are just the tip of the iceberg and that much will be learned by cross-pollination between these fields.

RNA-BASED MECHANISMS FOR GENERATING N TERMINUS DIVERSITY

The role of transcription in RNA isoforms

Transcription and nuclear processing events generate RNA isoform diversity, which can lead to different protein isoforms6 (Figure 1). Briefly, transcription of mRNAs is performed by RNA polymerase II, which is recruited to promoters and regulated by a variety of factors, including transcription factor binding and chromatin environment; transcription start sites determine the very 5′ end of the transcript.7 During elongation, co-transcriptional events occur, including capping and splicing. Carried out by the spliceosome, splicing leads to the removal of introns and the joining of exons; many of these splicing are constitutive, while others are highly regulated, generating a large amount of transcript diversity.8 RNA binding proteins, RNA structure, and even transcription speed can all impact splicing efficiency and differences between cell types.7,9,10 Finally, triggered by recognition of the poly(A) site and additional signals, the 3′ end of the mature transcript is formed by cleavage and polyadenylation, an event that is also highly regulated.11

Alternative promoter usage, alternative splicing, and alternative poly(A) site recognition all alter the overall structure and sequence of the mature transcript, and each process has the potential to change the encoded protein.1 In the case of promoter usage, many genes initiate from different promoters, leading to so-called ‘‘alternative first exons.’’ Rather than being a rare event for generating transcript diversity, alternative first exons are very common; 50% of human genes use more than one promoter, with many genes using more than two. In fact, this mechanism generates as many distinctly annotated transcript isoforms per gene as alternative splicing does.12–17

Changes in promoter usage can lead to tissue- or development-specific transcripts.18–20 These changes are also some of the most common in response to environmental stress, inflammation, and cancer, often due to changes in transcription factor binding or the underlying chromatin landscape.12,20–26 For instance, in Drosophila, the sex determination pathway is a well-cited example of the importance of alternative splicing to regulate developmental outcomes. In males, alternative splicing leads to the inclusion of a poison exon in the sex-lethal (sxl) mRNA, triggering nonsense-mediated decay of the transcript and inhibiting expression of the sxl protein. In females, the poison exon is not included, enabling production of the sxl protein and a feedback loop that maintains female identity.21–23 Interestingly, this autoregulatory loop is initiated by use of an alternative promoter in female embryos, which provides the burst of full-length sxl protein needed to initialize the system in females.24,25

Given the prevalence of alternative first exons, a key question is how these impact gene function. Alternative first exons necessarily change 5′ UTRs; given that 5′ UTRs are a major source of regulation for translation initiation (see below), they have often been examined in terms of their impact on protein abundance.27,28 Some estimates for yeast suggest that differences in translation initiation due to changes in the 5′ UTR can result in 10-fold differences in protein expression.27 One example in humans is the Aim2 gene, where changes in promoter usage due to inflammation trigger the incorporation of an iron response element in its 5′ UTR of the transcript. The iron response element is a structural element recognized by iron response proteins to negatively regulate translation of Aim2.23 Increased levels of intracellular iron release the iron response proteins from transcript enabling translational upregulation in response.

In addition, though, the first exon often (although not always) contains the start codon, and so changing the promoter can change the N terminus without necessarily impacting the rest of the protein. This is the case with sxl. In the female embryo, the initializing protein differs by just 22 amino acids at its N terminus from that made in the adult female.29 The N-terminal region of the adult Sxl protein is key for its function,30 but it is an open question what impact the embryonic N terminus has on its function, activity, or regulation. Another example from Drosophila is cyclin E during early embryogenesis, where the maternal transcript, which is deposited into the embryo, differs from the newly zygotic one; the zygotic transcript uses a distinct promoter, which changes the N terminus by removing 107 residues seen in the maternal proteoform and adding a distinct 12-residue extension.19 Although the functional impacts of the different N termini are again unknown, it is notable that the zygotic form contains a second-position lysine, which is a known N-degron (see below). Another example is CAV1.2, which encodes a calcium channel important for heart contractions and has multiple, alternatively spliced RNA isoforms. Some of the heterogeneity is due to alternative first exons, and the resultant N-terminal proteoforms display different electrophysical properties.31 Indeed, the literature is full of such examples.20,32–35 Yet, because the resultant changes to the protein are subtle, often involving only a few dozen amino residues and rarely impacting the main body of the protein, they often have not been subject to substantial investigation.

Alternative splicing and RNA diversity

Alternative splicing is another potent way to generate different N termini. Such mechanisms can involve alternative 5′ or 3′ splice site recognition, introducing extensions or deletions. Similarly, retained introns and skipped introns can also introduce transcript diversity. If these events occur within the 5′ UTR or near the start codon, they have the potential to introduce (or remove) in-frame start codons, which will then change the N terminus of the protein.18 One challenge in understanding how individual regulatory choices influence protein activity and levels is that many of these events are coupled. For instance, although not the focus of this review, changes in the last exon (so-called ‘‘alternative last exons’’) often change the C terminus of the protein and the transcript’s 3′ UTR, and regulatory events for first and last exon choices are often coupled.13,36 Similarly, promoter choice can also impact splicing decisions, and often alternative splicing events are themselves coupled or at least coincident,19 which provides further motivation for long-read sequencing as well as developments in the corresponding bioinformatics methods.37 As with promoter usage, many of these splicing events are tissue specific, contributing to proteoform diversity in multicellular organisms.

Alternative splicing is often considered with respect to large changes in the encoded protein, such as domain inclusion and exclusion.38 One classic example is the Dscam gene in Drosophila, where the combinatorics of several mutually exclusive splicing events lead to the generation of more than 38,000 different proteoforms from a single locus.39 In addition, as with alternative first exons, alternative splicing can also impact overall translation due to changes in the 5′ UTR. For instance, an early study noted that alternative splicing in MECP2, the causative gene for Rett syndrome, affected translation, where the isoform lacking the exon 2 produced substantially more protein, in part due to differences in the resultant 5′ UTR.40 In contrast, the impact of alternative splicing events that lead to small changes, especially at the N terminus, has historically attracted less attention.

Despite a lack of substantial research, there still are many examples where alternative splicing leads to different protein activity and expression due to different N termini. For instance, RSG8 encodes a member of the family of regulators of G protein signaling. In rats, exon 2 is alternatively included, leading to shift in start codons and a small switch in the N terminus from a 9-residue to a 7-residue one. Despite a seemingly modest change in the encoded protein, this alternative splicing event diminishes the activity of the resultant protein as well as reduces overall protein expression.41 The underlying molecular mechanism is unknown, but it is interesting to note that the poorly expressed proteoform begins with a well-known N-degron, Met-Arg, that is described more below.

Relative to internal regions, the N terminus has fewer structural constraints, perhaps making it an ideal place for regulation at the RNA level to have biologically relevant impacts on protein function and activity. Consistent with such an idea, acquisition of new sequences often occurs at the junctions between the coding region and UTRs, providing opportunities for the evolution of new proteoforms with N-terminal extensions (or truncations) and potentially different functions or regulation.17 Given how even small changes at the N terminus can impact many aspects of a protein’s life cycle, including localization and degradation, such regulation is perhaps even more important to investigate.

The contribution of translation to proteoform diversity

In eukaryotes, translation initiation is predominantly cap dependent. Briefly, the small 40S ribosomal subunit, as part of the pre-initiation complex, is recruited to the 5′ end of a transcript, scans along the RNA, and then recognizes a start codon, an event that triggers joining of the large 60S subunit, dissociation of a variety of initiation factors, and the formation of an elongation-competent 80S ribosome.42 A full discussion of this complex and highly regulated process is beyond the scope of this review, but there are two aspects of translation initiation most critical for generating N-terminal diversity: the 5’→3′ vectorial nature of scanning and the kinetic competition between start codon recognition and continued scanning. The first aspect means that upstream start codons have first dibs on engaging with the ribosome and that downstream ones can only be used if the ribosome fails to recognize the first start codon; this process is termed ‘‘leaky scanning.’’ The second aspect means that RNA elements and proteins that enhance or decrease start codon recognition will shift the balance between recognition and continued scanning.

RNA elements determine the strength of a start codon. The canonical start codon is AUG, but other near-cognate codons, such as CUG, can also be used.5 However, near-cognate codons are poorer at translation initiation due to weaker base-pairing with the initiator tRNA. It should be noted, though, that these codons will primarily be decoded as methionine when used for initiation rather than, say, leucine (in the case of CUG). A start codon is required for initiation but not sufficient. A strong Kozak sequence, which surrounds the start codon, also contributes to enhanced recognition.43,44 In mammals, this sequence is GCC (A/G)CC AUG G (here, the start codon is in bold), and the −3 purine and +4 guanine are particularly important.5 Highlighting the dual nature that many of these nucleotides have, the Kozak sequence will influence the encoded proteins; +4 guanine restricts the second encoded amino acid to Val, Ala, Asp, Glu, and Gly. In addition, downstream RNA structure and other elements that slow scanning, including the nascent peptide (see below), can enhance recognition of poor-context start codons, while placing a start codon within a structured region can decrease recognition.45–47 Finally, in bacteria, amino acids encoded in positions 3–5 can influence protein output through early ribosome drop-off,48 although it remains unclear to which extent the eukaryotic ribosome is similarly impacted.

Given the host of mechanisms that can affect start codon recognition, it is unsurprising that this step enables substantial regulation at both the gene and global levels. For instance, use of upstream ORFs (uORFs) often decreases expression of the downstream ‘‘main’’ coding region, although some notable examples, like the transcription factor GCN4, have complex regulation.49,50 Indeed, just the presence of an upstream AUG can decrease translation of the coding region, and computational models have shown that the presence and number of upstream AUGs is strongly predictive of decreased main ORF translation.27,51 In some cases, regulation of the uORF translation can even set up elegant feedback cycles, as is the case for the polyamine metabolism.52–54 Here, there is tight translational control of AZIN1 mRNA, which encodes a key regulator of the biosynthetic pathway. Under high polyamine levels, translation of the uORF induces a stall that prevents recognition of the main ORF, inhibiting the synthesis of AZIN1. However, at low polyamine levels, this stall is resolved, enabling efficient production of AZIN1 and stimulating polyamine synthesis.

Critical for our discussion here is that regulation of start codon recognition also means that a single transcript isoform can produce multiple proteoforms through upstream, in-frame start codons (which will lead to N-terminal extensions) or through leaky scanning to use downstream methionines (which will lead to N-terminal truncations). For instance, CDC20 is a key protein for mitotic exit and is regulated by the spindle assembly checkpoint. However, in humans, leaky scanning of its transcript generates an N-terminally shortened proteoform that is not regulated by the checkpoint and promotes mitotic exit even in the presence of mitotic perturbations.55

In fact, examples of N-terminal proteoforms have been known for decades.56 One classic example occurs with the yeast GRS1 gene, encoding the glycyl-tRNA synthetase.57 In S. cerevisiae, this gene encodes both the cytoplasmic and mitochondrial forms, which are produced through alternative translation initiation. Initiation from an upstream UUG creates an N-terminal extension that contains a mitochondrial targeting sequence, while initiation from the downstream AUG produces the cytoplasmic version. Another well-known example is the proto-oncogene c-Myc.58 Here, the use of an upstream, near- cognate CUG start codon leads to a small N-terminal extension. The extended and canonical forms have different activity, suggesting that the relative ratios between the two proteoforms is important; some cancers have mutations that specifically affect the extended but not canonical proteoform.59 The underlying mechanism leading to the use of the CUG start codon was only recently discovered,60 and it highlights the beauty and complexity of translation initiation. Briefly, the N-terminal extension of c-Myc contains a Pro-Pro motif. Peptide bond formation between Pro and Pro (as well as with some other dipeptide combinations) is notoriously challenging for the ribosome and requires the activity of a universally conserved elongation factor called eIF5A.61–63 Interestingly, eIF5A is the only protein in the proteome to contain a hypusine, which results from a post-translation processing event of an internal lysine and which is critical for the activity of eIF5A during translation.64,65 In the case of c-Myc, when eIF5A activity is low, ribosomes translating the N-terminal extension pause over the Pro-Pro motif, creating a queue of ribosomes and enhancing the recognition of the CUG start codon.

This second example highlights a broader principle of alternative start codon use: the kinetic competition between recognition of an upstream typically suboptimal start site and continued scanning that leads to use of a downstream one. In the case of c-Myc, decreased eIF5A activity slows scanning because of the steric block from ribosome queuing. But this is not the only such mechanism. Another example is RNA structure, which, when downstream of the poor start codon, can also enhance its recognition, leading to an N-terminal extension. For example, recent work on the neuronal pentraxin receptor highlighted the importance of RNA structure in the use of an upstream alternative CUG start codon.66 The N-terminal extension conferred different activities and localization, and altering the levels of the two proteoforms even affected learning behaviors in mice. Rather than being rare events, there are many such examples of alternative translation initiation sites,67 and recent ribosome profiling indicates that as much as 50% of genes may make use of alternative translation initiation, especially from near-cognate start codons.68

Start codon recognition can also be regulated on a global scale. For instance, decreases in eIF5A activity lead to changes in translation start sites for hundreds of genes due to ribosome queueing,60 and other cellular contexts and treatments that lead to ribosome queueing generally enhance use of start codons in a poor context.69 The relative levels of other translation initiation factors are also important. In particular, eIF1 and eIF5 have opposing roles in start codon recognition, where high levels of eIF1 enhance start codon stringency, and high levels of eIF5 decrease stringency.5,70,71 As might be expected, there is a complex network of auto- and cross-regulation between these two factors.72 The localization of eIF1 is also regulated in a cell-cycle-dependent manner.68 It is predominantly nuclear during interphase, becoming cytoplasmic during breakdown of the nuclear envelope during mitosis. In turn, its enhanced cytoplasmic levels during mitosis or mitotic arrest enforce a more stringent translation program, which then globally alters start codon usage and the balance between different proteoforms. This mechanism explains the leaky scanning of CDC20 mentioned above55,68; mitotic arrest increases cytoplasmic eIF1 and leads to more stringent start codon selection, which then favors the production of the N-terminally truncated form of CDC20 and thus enables exit from mitosis even if the checkpoint is still active.

Thus, start codon recognition is highly regulated, and different initiation sites can lead to the production of proteoforms that predominantly differ at the N terminus.68 For the best-studied examples, the relative production is biologically relevant and highly controlled, but this area remains understudied in part because of the challenges surrounding the annotation of N termini. N-terminal extensions often make use of near-cognate start codons67,68 and so may have escaped attention for annotations or experiments requiring a canonical start codon. On the other hand, N-terminal truncations are hard to detect even with empirical methods like ribosome profiling, and confident annotation requires careful experimentation. Categorizing when, where, and how these N-terminal proteoforms are made is clearly an important next step, as are molecular and computational investigations in a variety of model systems to understand the corresponding evolutionary dynamics.

THE DEGRADATIVE CONSEQUENCES OF N TERMINUS DIVERSITY

Given the diversity of proteins that a single genetic locus can generate, the key question is what it all means for protein function. The easiest to rationalize are those where proteoform differences impact inclusion of key domains or regulatory regions, as is the case for CDC20 and many other examples.15,59,73 Similarly, given that the N terminus often encodes localization signals for the endoplasmic reticulum and the mitochondria,74–76 changes can impact localization, as is the case for the yeast GRS1 gene.57 Examples of this phenomenon are widespread and can also impact localization via N-terminal modifications as well as localization to other organelles, including the nucleus.77–80 A recent study has expanded the scope and implication of such regulation.81 Approximately 19% of human genes with translated alternative isoforms exhibit differential localization, and, among these, 21.7% show changes to mitochondrial localization and are essential for proper mitochondrial function. Critically, these mechanisms are evolutionarily conserved, underscoring the importance of start codon usage in regulating protein localization.81 Further advances in predictive algorithms82 and experimental methods83 will be a powerful lens to make sense of the newly revealed proteoform diversity.

As important as these mechanisms are, the role of N-terminal proteoforms in changing protein degradation has been relatively less explored. In the following section, we will describe the fundamentals of degradation mediated by the N terminus and highlight a few examples where the combined use of RNA- and protein-focused datasets can reveal undiscovered biology.

The fundamentals of N-degrons

N-degrons are sequence elements at the very N terminus that are recognized by E3 ubiquitin ligases, known as N-recognins, and trigger protein decay. Importantly, all N-degron pathways discovered to date involve multiple E3 ligases and often require other processing enzymes, underscoring the importance of N-terminal regulation (Figure 2). Protein degradation via the N terminus is often thought to be controlled by the second-position amino acid, with some amino acids like Arg and Pro being ‘‘unstable’’ and others like Ala being ‘‘stable.’’ However, as we will discuss below, the rules controlling N-degrons and protein degradation are far more complicated than this simple heuristic, and, provided the correct context, every second-position amino acid can act as an N-degron.84

The second-position amino acids (which are often referred to as the ‘‘N-degron’’) are necessary for the decay pathways, but they are not sufficient; downstream residues play a critical role. For instance, residues immediately following the N-terminal amino acid can affect binding by the E3 ligase.85–87 In addition, as will be detailed below, other proteins are often required to generate the ‘‘active’’ N-degron via proteolytic cleavage and protein processing.88–96 These proteins themselves have their own substrate specificity. Finally, another key requirement for degradation is the cytoplasmic exposure of a proximal lysine.97 Therefore, if degron residues or lysines are inaccessible to N-recognins, then the protein will not be degraded.

The first discovered and best characterized pathway is the Arg/N-degron pathway.97 The Arg/N-degron pathway is involved in a variety of biological processes, including silencing of the integrated stress response and proteotoxic stress in aneuploid cancers, and is integral for brain and cardiovascular development.98–100 Arg/N-degrons consist of two general types. Type 1 Arg/N-degrons are characterized by positively charged residues Arg, His, or Lys at position 1 or Arg or Lys at position 2.95,101 Notably, there is a divergence in Arg/N-degron pathways between different organisms, as position 2 type 1 Arg/N-degrons are not found in yeast.90 In studies that monitor global N-terminome stability, type 1 Arg/N-degrons are among the least stable of all N-terminal residues.95 Type 2 Arg/N-degrons are characterized by the bulky hydrophobic amino acids Met, Ile, Leu, Phe, Tyr, and Trp at position 1 or at position 2 following the initiator methionine. Because all protein synthesis begins with an initiator methionine (iMet), position 1 Arg/N-degrons can only be created via proteolytic cleavage events or through post-translational modifications that add amino acids to the N terminus, which we will cover in more detail in the next section.

Both type 1 and type 2 Arg/N-degrons are targeted by UBR E3 ligases. In yeast, there is a single Arg/N-recognin, Ubr1, while in mammals, Arg/N-degrons are targeted by the UBR E3 ligases UBR1, UBR2, UBR4 (as a part of the SIFI complex, which also includes KCMF1), and UBR5.85,86,99,102–104 Arg/N-degrons can also be targeted by p62, whose recognition leads to autophagosome-mediated decay.105,106 Underscoring the complexity of the system, however, N-terminal global proteome profiling revealed that many proteins containing putative Arg/N-degrons are stable and that only 7% of Arg/N-degron substrates were stabilized upon ablation of the Arg/N-recognins UBR1, UBR2, and UBR4.95 In other words, additional elements, especially the protein sequence beyond the second position, regulate decay triggered by the N terminus.

The complex role of N-terminal processing

N-degron pathways typically require N-terminal modifications or processing to trigger decay, such as iMet removal by methionine aminopeptidases (MetAP) and acetylation (Figure 2). Recent advances in high-throughput technologies, such as massively parallel reporter assays, CRISPR screens, and N-terminal proteomics, have enabled discovery of new N-degrons and their corresponding N-recognins.90,91,94,95,107,108 They have also demonstrated the central role that N-terminal modifications play in substrate recognition, adding an additional layer of complexity. Importantly, these pathways highlight three general principles relevant for thinking about N termini. First, there is substantial crosstalk between processing, modification, and N-degron pathways. Second, the extent to which an N terminus can act as an N-degron is often determined not just by the specificity and activity of N-recognin but also by that of required and opposing modifying enzymes. Third, N-degrons often act in quality control pathways, regulating mislocalized, differentially modified, or excess subunits.

The Pro/N-degron pathway

Substrates of the Pro/N-degron pathway contain an N-terminal Pro and are targeted by the GID complex in yeast and humans.87,109,110 In budding yeast, the gluconeogenic enzymes Fbp1, Pck1, Icl1, and Mdh2 were some of the first discovered targets of the Pro/N-degron pathway and are degraded in response to glucose-rich conditions.109,111 In humans, this pathway controls cholesterol biosynthesis by targeting a key protein in the pathway, hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1.112 Adding to the complexity of targets for this E3 ligase, in yeast, GID4, which acts as one of the substrate adaptors, is also capable of targeting the bulky hydrophobic residues Ile, Leu, Val, and Phe at position 1, while another adaptor, Gid11, can also target N-terminal Thr residues.87,108 The human complex also uses multiple substrate adaptors, some of which also recognize internal degrons.113,114

N-terminal exposure of Pro is required for the N-degron pathway and requires protein cleavage. For instance, the removal of the iMet by MetAPs will create an N-terminal proline when it is encoded at the second position. Similarly, third-position Pro residues are also substrates of the Pro/N-degron pathway.88,115 Briefly, position 3 Pro N termini that are preceded by a second-position Ser or Ala can undergo iMet cleavage. This N-terminal processing then exposes an N-terminal Ala/Ser and Pro at position 2. Processing by the aminopeptidases Fra1 and Icp55 then results in an N-terminal Pro.

The Ac/N-degron pathway

Ac/N-recognins target degrons via N-terminal acetylation.116–118 N-terminal acetylation occurs co-translationally, whereby N-terminal acetyltransferases bound to the ribosome acetylate N termini as they emerge from the exit tunnel.119 Both the iMet and second-position amino acids newly exposed after iMet removal can be acetylated and targeted for destruction.117 Acetylation in and of itself does not necessarily trigger degradation, as it can also block recognition of other N-degrons and instead stabilize proteins,90,120 but its importance is likely broad, given that up to 60% of yeast N termini and 80%–90% of human proteins are N-terminally acetylated.121 Ac/N-degrons are targeted by the E3 ligases Doa10 (MARCH6 in humans) and Not4 (CNOT4 in humans).116,118 Examples of Ac/N-degron substrates include Cog1 and Hcn1 in yeast as well as RGS2 and PLIN2 in humans.116,117,122,123 Interestingly, a mutant version of RGS2 found in human patients was shown to be a target of both the Ac/N-degron and Arg/N-degron pathways. When its iMet was acetylated, the protein was targeted by the Ac/N-degron pathway; in the absence of acetylation, it was a type 2 Arg/N-degron and targeted by UBR E3 ligases.123 These examples highlight the complexity of predicting Ac/N-degrons and underscore the importance of co-translational processes in modifying the N terminus to create N-degrons.

The GASTC/N-degron pathway

N-terminal processing is essential for the recognition of proteins with position 2 Gly, Ala, Ser, Thr, and Cys residues, collectively known as GASTC/N-degrons. These are recognized by the CRL2 substrate adaptors ZYG11B and ZER1.84,91,95 Gly N-degrons were the first discovered elements in this pathway. Substrates contained an N-terminal Gly, generated either by iMet processing or internal proteolytic cleavage, and lacked additional modifications, including N-terminal myristoylation.95 Because N-terminal myristoylation is critical for anchoring Gly-containing proteins to the plasma membrane, the GASTC/N-degron pathway serves as a quality control mechanism to degrade substrates that failed to localize.

Similarly, N termini with inhibitor of apoptosis binding motifs typically contain an Ala or Ser at position 2 and are recognized by the inhibitor of apoptosis (IAP) E3 ligases BIRC2, BIRC3, XIAP, and BIRC6.124 These are GASTC/N-degrons, but they are subject to additional layers of regulation, including N-terminal processing and acetylation. For instance, N-terminal acetylation of IAP substrates inhibits recognition and degradation.124 This relationship between E3 recognition and modifications can be even more complex, as is the case with mitochondrial adenylate kinase 2 (AK2).125 Briefly, the majority of AK2 undergoes iMet processing by MetAPs, followed by N-terminal trimming by the dipeptidyl peptidases DPP8/9.125 These N-terminal modifications expose a position 4 Ser that can be recognized by IAPs and rapidly degraded. Under normal conditions, the degron-exposed AK2 is localized to the intermembrane space of mitochondria, preventing its degradation by IAP ligases. On the other hand, the acetylation of the iMet itself prevents its removal by MetAPs and consequently inhibits trimming by DPP8/9, which masks the degron and stabilizes AK2 in the cytosol while also increasing mitochondrial levels of the enzyme.125

Taken together, the GASTC/N-degron requires iMet removal (or other cleavage events) to expose the N-degron termini, but modifications like N-terminal acetylation shield the degron and so oppose recognition.91,125 These examples highlight the complexity of predicting N-degrons and underscores the importance of residues downstream of position 1 and 2 in shaping N-degron formation.

Arg/N-degrons: N-terminal cleavage events

There are now multiple lines of evidence showing that N-terminal processing also regulates Arg/N-degrons.89,90,92–94 For instance, we have recently found that position 3 Arg and Lys can be recognized by the UBR4-containing SIFI complex.92 Here, substrates first have their iMet removed by MetAPs, which shifts the position of the Arg/Lys residues to position 2, making them candidate Arg/N-degron substrates, like well-known position 2 Arg/Lys residues that follow an iMet.95 Intriguingly, genome-wide studies of N-terminal stability in budding yeast have not captured this MetAP-dependent Arg/N-degron pathway, perhaps suggesting another divergence in Arg/N-degron pathways between different species.90

In the same vein, global analysis of N-terminal stability revealed that some position 4 residues can serve as substrates of the Arg/N-degron pathway.94 Here, the N termini undergo a series of processing steps to expose the N-degron; typically, these involve predominantly small residues at position 2, a third position Pro, and Arg/N-degron residues at position 4. First, in most cases, the N-terminal Met is cleaved by MetAPs, shifting the small N-terminal residue at position 1. Next, as with AK2 described above,125 subsequent DPP8/9 cleavage exposes the N-degron. These Arg/N-degrons are then targeted by the UBR2 or UBR4 E3 ligases. Intriguingly, while DPP-dependent Arg/N-degrons are widespread, they are enriched in secretory proteins, serving as a quality control pathway to degrade these proteins when not secreted.94

Arg/N-degrons: N-terminal modifications

N-terminal modifications can also lead to the creation of new type 1 position 1 Arg/N-degrons.89,90,93,126 In many cases, these degrons arise from endoproteolytic cleavage, which generates new N termini with Gln or Asn residues. Here, the exposed N-terminal Asp/Gln is deamidated, leading to N-terminal Asp/Glu residues, and the new N terminus is arginylated by Ate1 (ATE1 in humans). Alternatively, native N termini with position 2 Asn/Gln residues undergo iMet removal, subjecting the N terminus to the same series of modifications. This cascade creates an N terminus with a position 1 Arg that can be recognized by Arg/N-recognins.90,93 Proteins with position 2 Cys residues undergo a related mechanism of arginylation and have been identified as some of the least stable N termini.95,126,127 In this pathway, the N-terminal Cys is oxidized enzymatically or chemically, recognized by ATE1, arginylated, and then degraded by Arg/N-recognins. Fascinatingly, the Arg/N-recognins UBR1 and UBR2 exclusively recognize arginylated Cys-O2 substrates (which result from enzymatic oxidation), while the SIFI complex targets Cys-O3 substrates (which result from chemical oxidation).126

As with GASTC/N-degrons, N-terminal acetylation predominantly shields Arg/N-degrons from recognition. For instance, acetylation of the N-terminal methionine can prevent the recognition of type 2 Arg/N-degrons.118,123 Similarly, acetylation of the iMet can prevent its removal for position 2 Asn/Gln residues, thus preventing Ubr1-mediated decay.90,93 More recently, comprehensive studies have highlighted the crosstalk between Ac/N-degron and Arg/N-degron pathways.96 For example, genome-wide CRISPR screens in cells depleted of the NatC complex, which acetylates the iMet of type 2 N-degrons, revealed strong genetic interactions with the Arg/N-recognins UBR2 and UBR4.96 Consistent with competition between acetylation and the Arg/N-degron pathway, NatC acetylation targets with type 2 N-degrons were depleted upon ablation of NatC subunits; expression of these same substrates was rescued upon depletion of UBR2 or UBR4. Although this study focused on N-terminal acetylation as a stabilizing modification for type 2 Arg/N-degrons, this observation appears to be more general. Acetylation of N-terminal Cys residues by the NatA complex prevents Cys oxidation, thereby stabilizing second-position Cys substrates.89 As with N-recognins, the activity of NatA was affected not just by the N-terminal residue but also by those immediately downstream of the Cys.89 Taken together, these observations underscore the complexity of predicting Arg/N-degron substrates, the role of residues beyond the second position, and the importance of N-terminal processing, modifications, and their respective enzymes in protein stability.

N-terminal diversity and implications for protein stability

Given the widespread occurrence of N-terminal extensions and the variety of N-degron contexts, a natural implication is that N-terminal extensions or truncations might create or remove an N-degron. There are a few examples of such an effect. For instance, the pim-1 oncogene makes use of an upstream CUG start codon to generate an N-terminally extended protein.73 It is interesting to note that pim-1, like Myc, contains a poly-proline stretch downstream of its CUG start codon, suggesting that its usage might also be affected by eIF5A activity.58,60 Although this proteoform and the canonical one have the same kinase activity, only the extended version can form a complex and is more stable than the canonical version.

We would argue that these small N-terminal extensions are ripe for ways to differentially regulate protein stability, and thus they may provide a more general mechanism to regulate gene output. Several lines of evidence support this idea. First, analysis of N-terminal extensions resulting from altered translation initiation revealed an enrichment for typical position 2 degrons, such as Cys and Gly.126 Second, a recent study from the Elledge lab investigated the impact of the N-terminome on reporter expression.95 We have re-analyzed their data focusing on the impact of different N termini from the same gene. Strikingly, thousands of genes show evidence of differential stability between their N termini (Figure 3A). Additional analysis of representative genes with eight or more isoforms further highlights the importance of the N terminus in regulating stability (Figure 3B). Third, although proteoform-aware mass spectrometry remains challenging, data from several studies have shown that even small differences can result in dramatically different turnover rates.128 For example, in the AN32E transcript, usage of an alternative initiation site just two codons downstream of the canonical site resulted in a protein with a 10-fold decrease in stability (Figure 3C).

Finally, in our own inspection of a variety of datasets, we noted that eIF5A provides an intriguing example of the unexplored N-terminal biology (Figure 4). UniProt annotations indicate two proteoforms of eIF5A. The proteoform driven by an upstream, in-frame AUG results in a small, 30-residue N-terminal extension to the canonical proteoform. The canonical N terminus starts with Met-Ala-Asp-Asp-Leu, is unstructured, and confers stability upon reporters, as measured by global N-terminome experiments.95 In contrast, the extended N terminus starts with Met-Cys-Gly-Thr-Gly, confers instability in those same N-terminome experiments,95 and contains the well-described N-degron Cys in its second position.

What is most intriguing is that the elements in the extended region are highly reminiscent of that in Myc. First, the extended form contains a poor start codon context, while the canonical one uses a strong context. Second, the N-terminal extension also contains a Pro-Pro motif, suggesting that this extension is likely made under scenarios of low eIF5A activity. Perhaps this extension provides a burst of extended eIF5A protein that enables the production of the stable variant but is itself degraded quickly to enable homeostatic control. Interestingly, these features in the eIF5A locus also suggests that prolonged inhibition of hypusination, the modification required for eIF5A activity,65 may have the paradoxical effect of lowering overall eIF5A protein levels due to increased use of the upstream AUG and then increased turnover of the extended form. Although much about this proposed model remains to be experimentally tested, eIF5A provides an intriguing example for the insights that emerge by integrating the different types of gene regulation.

CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

Many RNA- and protein-based regulatory pathways converge upon the N terminus. RNA-facing events, like transcription and splicing, enable the generation of multiple RNA isoforms from a single gene, which often encode different proteoforms, while translation can directly generate distinct proteoforms from a single transcript. At the same time, small changes in the N terminus can affect localization, modifications, and protein stability, in addition to commonly viewed consequences like domain inclusion. The N terminus is thus subject to a convergence of regulation, and isolated examples mentioned here point to the potential for discoveries through an RNA- and protein-aware lens.

In our view, there have been several challenges in integrating the RNA- and protein-centric perspectives. The first is one of scientific communities. RNA biologists, who are best versed in transcription, splicing, and translation, often are less familiar with the complex field of N-degrons. Conversely, the N-degron field has long been fascinated by the rules determining which N-terminal motifs mediate degradation and how these motifs are selectively recognized by N-recognonins. Such discoveries are a necessary starting point for interpreting the proteoform diversity. However, there has been less attention on the generation of alternative proteoforms, especially those generated by non-AUG start codons. For instance, despite the well-known example of CUG initiation with Myc,127 global studies of the N-terminome only included the N terminus from the canonical, AUG-initiated proteoform.95 Second, there is a large challenge in integrating available datasets and in deciding which isoforms to test. In our cross-comparison between recent ribosome profiling and global N-terminome datasets, we found surprisingly few isoforms that were present in both. In the long term, it will be also important to apply these principles to multicellular animals, disease states, and population variation.

Finally, for the purposes of this review, we focused on the mechanisms and consequences of N-terminal proteoforms, but many of these principles apply to the C terminus. A variety of nuclear processes, including splicing and 3′ end processing, can lead to alternative 3′ ends of the coding region, and some C-terminal amino acids specify localization or act as C-degrons. One example is Max, the protein partner of Myc, where alternative last exons change the inclusion of an encoded C-degron.129 We hope that this discussion sparks an increased awareness of transcription, splicing, and translation for studies of protein function, localization, and degradation.
