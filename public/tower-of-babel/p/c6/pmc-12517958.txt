Association between sex steroid hormones and obstructive sleep apnea among US adults: Data from the 2015 to 2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Qing Guo, Jiayuan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to explore the association between serum steroid hormones (testosterone, estrogen), sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) levels, and the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) among adults in the United States, as well as to evaluate the potential utility of these biomarkers for OSA risk screening. Data were collected from the 2015 to 2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, including 3331 adults aged 20 years or older. OSA symptoms were assessed via self-reported questionnaires. Serum concentrations of testosterone, estrogen, and SHBG were measured. Sample-weighted logistic regression was used to investigate associations between hormone indicators and OSA risk, with restricted cubic spline models to check for nonlinear relationships. Subgroup analyses tested the robustness of the associations. Receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve were used to evaluate the screening utility of testosterone and SHBG. Among the 3331 participants, 1674 were classified as having OSA. Univariate analysis showed that higher testosterone was associated with increased OSA risk, but after adjusting for confounders, testosterone was inversely related to OSA risk (OR = 0.69, 95% confidence interval: 0.54–0.90), with a nonlinear threshold effect. Lower SHBG levels were significantly associated with a higher risk of OSA (OR = 0.71, 95% confidence interval: 0.59–0.85), a finding that remained robust across multiple models and subgroup analyses. Receiver operating characteristic analysis showed that ln(SHBG) had limited discriminatory power for OSA screening (area under the curve, AUC = 0.595). Estrogen levels were not significantly associated with OSA risk. Lower SHBG levels are significantly associated with increased OSA risk. While its standalone value is limited, SHBG may serve as a complementary biomarker in multifactor OSA risk assessment. The testosterone–OSA relationship appears complex, suggesting clinical use should account for multiple influencing factors. Future research should integrate multidimensional biomarkers to optimize early detection and risk assessment for OSA.

1. Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common chronic sleep disorder marked by repeated partial or complete collapse of the upper airway. This condition causes brief microarousals lasting 3 to 15 seconds, which result in fragmented sleep, chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), and frequent nocturnal awakenings.[1] Clinically, OSA primarily presents as excessive daytime sleepiness, fatigue, loud snoring, reduced or absent airflow, and other related symptoms. Epidemiological studies show that the prevalence of OSA has been increasing. In the United States, about 34% of men and 17% of women are affected, with similar rates reported globally.[2] Untreated OSA is strongly associated with several serious comorbidities, such as hypertension, coronary heart disease, heart failure, arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension, stroke, sudden death, and diabetes.[3–5] Given the well-established pathological and clinical consequences of OSA, further research into its underlying causes and pathophysiological mechanisms is essential to improve clinical intervention strategies. In particular, identifying more effective and specific biomarkers may enhance early diagnosis and support stratified management of OSA.

Sex steroid hormones, including testosterone (TST) and estrogen (EST), as well as sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), are essential for maintaining sexual characteristics, cognitive function, bone and muscle development, and immune regulation.[6,7] Sex steroid hormones directly mediate various physiological processes. SHBG, as its primary regulatory protein, maintains hormonal homeostasis by binding and releasing these hormones. Sleep quality is closely linked to sex steroid hormone levels.[8,9] Testosterone synthesis is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPGA). OSA is considered a significant contributor to hypopituitary-gonadism.[10] Previous studies have found that patients with OSA generally have lower serum testosterone levels. This association remains significant even after adjusting for confounders such as age and obesity. Furthermore, treating OSA may help alleviate hypogonadism symptoms and improve sexual function.[11–13] However, some studies suggest that men with higher testosterone levels may have an increased risk of OSA. Testosterone replacement therapy (TTh) may also induce or worsen OSA symptoms.[14,15] Therefore, current evidence indicates that testosterone may be a key endogenous factor in the development of OSA, although the causal relationship remains complex. The relationship between estrogen and OSA remains inconsistent across different study populations.[16,17] Additionally, proteomic biomarker studies have shown that serum SHBG levels are negatively correlated with OSA severity in both adults and children.[18,19]

Consequently, the relationship between sex steroid hormones and OSA has been the focus of numerous studies. However, findings remain controversial, and there is a lack of large-scale, systematic studies directly examining the relationship between serum sex hormone levels and OSA. This study used data from the 2015 to 2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in the United States. The aim was to investigate the potential association between neutral steroid hormone levels and OSA in the general US population, and to provide a theoretical basis for future clinical and basic research. Because NHANES is cross-sectional, this study focuses on the correlation between sex steroid hormones and OSA rather than causal inference, which is also a common method when analyzing observational data. OSA is a multifactorial disease. Early identification and risk assessment are crucial for improving patient prognosis. The results of this study are expected to provide large-scale clinical evidence for the early identification and prevention of OSA related to sex hormone imbalance. These findings may also enhance understanding of the relationship between sex steroid hormones and OSA.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

The primary data source for this study was the NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics in the United States. NHANES is a nationally representative survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population. It collects data on demographics, socioeconomic status, dietary habits, and health-related conditions. This study used data from the 2015 to 2016 NHANES cycle, during which self-reported OSA symptoms and serum steroid hormone indicators were collected concurrently. The initial sample included 9971 participants. Participants were excluded if: questionnaire data related to OSA symptoms were unavailable; serum testosterone, estradiol, or SHBG data were incomplete; or they were under 20 years old, pregnant, or missing other covariate data. A total of 3331 participants were included in the final analysis. The specific inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Figure 1.

Flow chart of the procedure for selecting participants. OSA = obstructive sleep apnea, NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.2. OSA assessment

According to previous studies using NHANES data, participants were classified as having OSA if they answered “yes” to any of the following binary questions: experiencing excessive daytime sleepiness 16 to 30 times/mo when sleep duration on workdays was at least 7 hours; having nocturnal breathing difficulties, snoring, or wheezing symptoms 3 or more times/wk; or snoring at night 3 or more times/wk. This diagnostic criterion was first established in the NHANES 2005 to 2008 cycle and has since been validated. It has been widely used in epidemiological studies to examine the association between OSA and various health outcomes.

2.3. Assessment of sex steroid hormones

In this study, serum concentrations of TST, EST, and SHBG were measured using standardized protocols. TST and EST were quantified by isotope dilution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (ID-LC–MS/MS), following the reference method established by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Liquid–liquid extraction was used to separate analytes from binding proteins and the serum matrix, minimizing potential interferences. Mass spectrometry was performed on a triple quadrupole instrument. Testosterone was detected in positive ion mode, while estradiol was detected in negative ion mode, both under electrospray ionization. Selective reaction monitoring was used, with 13C-labeled isotopes as internal standards to ensure high accuracy and precision. This method provides high throughput and sensitivity. The detection limits for TST and EST were 0.750 ng/mL and 2.994 pg/mL, respectively. SHBG was measured using an immunoassay based on its binding to specific monoclonal antibodies, which included 2 incubation steps and a streptavidin-assisted immobilization process. Magnetic particles were then captured, and a chemiluminescence reaction occurred on the electrode. The chemiluminescent signal was detected by photomultiplier tubes and quantified using batch-specific calibration curves. The detection limit for SHBG was 0.800 nmol/L. Values below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) were replaced by LLOD divided by the square root of 2 (LLOD/√2). Overall, these methods ensured high accuracy and reliability in the measurement of TST, EST, and SHBG, providing a robust foundation for subsequent data analysis. To correct data skewness and standardize results, TST, EST, and SHBG values were log-transformed.

2.4. Covariates

Covariates included age, gender, race, educational level, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), body mass index (BMI), participation in moderate-intensity physical activity, and depression status. Participants were categorized by smoking history as nonsmokers (smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in their lifetime), former smokers (who do not smoke now but smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in the past), and current smokers (who smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and smoked every day or for several days). Alcohol consumption was classified as nondrinker, light drinker (< once per week), or heavy drinker (≥ once per week) based on drinking frequency. Hypertension and diabetes were defined by self-reported physician or healthcare provider diagnosis. Participants with a history of coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, stroke, or angina pectoris were classified as having CVD. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Participants were grouped by BMI as follows: <25 kg/m², 25 to 30 kg/m², and >30 kg/m². Participants who reported at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise per week were considered physically active. Depressive symptoms were defined as a total score of ≥10 on the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Weighted statistical analyses were performed according to the complex, multistage, stratified cluster sampling design of NHANES, using the appropriate sample weights. Descriptive analyses summarized the baseline characteristics of participants by OSA status. Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation. Multivariate logistic regression was used to examine associations between TST, EST, SHBG, and OSA. TST, EST, and SHBG were categorized into quartiles. Three stepwise regression models were constructed. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race/ethnicity. Model 3 further adjusted for educational level, marital status, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, CVD, BMI, physical activity, and depressive symptoms. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess potential heterogeneity by gender, race, age, marital status, and income. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to evaluate potential nonlinear dose-response relationships between significant variables identified in logistic regression and OSA. The models were fitted with 5 knots placed at the 5th, 28th, 50th, 72nd, and 95th percentiles of the predictor distributions. Furthermore, piecewise logistic regression was utilized to explore the threshold effect of TST on OSA. The predictive performance of TST and SHBG for OSA was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) analysis. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 3.4).

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 3331 participants in this cross-sectional study. The OSA group (n = 1674) and the non-OSA group (n = 1657) differed significantly in demographic and clinical characteristics. Compared to the non-OSA group, the OSA group had a higher proportion of males (57.1% vs 44.7%), individuals aged 40 to 59 (40.8% vs 34.7%), those with less than a high school education (13.4% vs 9.6%), and married or cohabiting individuals (69.5% vs 61.9%). Consequently, the proportion aged 20 to 39 was lower in the OSA group (31.1% vs 39.4%). The OSA group also had higher rates of hypertension (37.4% vs 28.6%), diabetes (14.6% vs 8.5%), CVD (10.5% vs 6.6%), obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²: 52.0% vs 30.7%), and current smoking (21.7% vs 17.2%). In biomarker analysis, the ln(TST) was significantly higher in the OSA group (4.62 vs 4.40), while the ln(SHBG) was significantly lower (3.78 vs 4.02); there was no significant difference in ln(EST). No statistically significant differences were found between the groups for PIR or alcohol intake (P > .05).

3.2. Association between TST, EST, SHBG, and OSA

Table 2 presents the associations between testosterone (TST), estradiol (EST), SHBG, and OSA, assessed using both continuous and categorical models. In the unadjusted model (model 1), each 1-unit increase in ln(TST) was positively associated with OSA risk (OR = 1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.02–1.17, P = .012). However, this association reversed to a significant negative relationship after adjusting for demographics in model 2 (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.47–0.83, P = .006) and persisted in the fully adjusted model 3 (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.54–0.90, P = .008). Quartile analysis of TST showed a significantly increased risk in the third quartile (Q3) in model 1 (OR = 1.94, P < .001), but this association became nonsignificant after adjustment in models 2 and 3 (P > .05). The trend test was significant for a negative association in models 2 and 3 (P = .006 and P = .013, respectively). Conversely, ln(SHBG) as a continuous variable was significantly and negatively associated with OSA risk across all models (model 1: OR = 0.54, P < .001; model 3: OR = 0.71, P = .001). The quartile analysis of SHBG demonstrated a dose-response relationship (Q4 vs Q1 in model 3: OR = 0.61, P = .008), with a significant trend test (P ≤ .002). The continuous variable ln(EST) was not statistically associated with OSA risk in any model (all P > .05), and no significant trend was observed in the quartile analysis (trend test P > .3). These results suggest that lower SHBG levels are independently associated with increased OSA risk, while the association between TST and OSA is influenced by confounding factors. No clear association was observed for EST.

3.3. Curve fitting and threshold effect analysis

Figure 2 illustrates the dose-response relationships between ln(TST), ln(SHBG) and OSA, revealing a nonlinear association for ln(TST) and a linear one for ln(SHBG). Table 3 presents the corresponding threshold effect analysis for ln(TST), where piecewise regression identified 2 inflection points at 3.144 and 5.627. When ln(TST) was <3.144, the OR was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.51–0.98, P = .037). In the intermediate range (3.144–5.627), no significant association was observed (adjusted OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 0.89–1.70, P = .221). For ln(TST) values ≥ 5.627, a significant protective effect was found (adjusted OR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.42–0.85, P = .005). The likelihood ratio test confirmed the superior fit of the piecewise model over a linear model (P = .037). The RCS analysis further corroborated this significant nonlinear association (P-overall = .002; P-nonlinear = .012). The RCS curve initially declined at low ln(TST) values, showed a mild upward trend in the intermediate range, and then decreased again for ln(TST) values exceeding 5.627, subsequently plateauing. This intermediate rise suggested a potential risk increase, though the association did not reach statistical significance, consistent with the nonsignificant finding from the piecewise model. The widening confidence intervals at both ends of the curve indicated data sparsity, which limits the robustness of inferences in these extreme regions.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

To investigate potential associations between ln(TST), ln(SHBG), and OSA, we performed a subgroup analysis of model 3 stratified by demographic and lifestyle factors (Fig. 3). For ln(TST), the association with OSA was significantly moderated by gender, marital status, household income (PIR), smoking, diabetes, and depression (all P for interaction < .05). In contrast, subgroups including age, race, and BMI did not show significant interactions (all P for interaction > .05). For ln(SHBG), the significant negative association with OSA risk was found to be consistent across all subgroups. This consistency is supported by the lack of significant interaction effects (all P for interaction > .05).

3.5. ROC curve analysis

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of ln(TST) and ln(SHBG) for OSA using ROC curve analysis (Fig. 4 and Table 4). The AUC for ln(TST) was 0.514 (95% CI: 0.494–0.534), indicating poor discriminatory ability. The corresponding optimal cutoff value yielded a sensitivity of 0.586 and a specificity of 0.515. For ln(SHBG), the AUC was 0.595 (95% CI: 0.576–0.614), also indicating limited diagnostic performance. At its optimal cutoff of 3.996, ln(SHBG) demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.670 and a specificity of 0.501. Although the AUC for ln(SHBG) was statistically superior to that of ln(TST), both markers exhibited limited standalone utility for OSA diagnosis.

4. Discussion

This study examined the associations between key steroid hormones – testosterone, estrogen, and SHBG – and OSA, utilizing data from the 2015 to 2016 NHANES cycle. Our results revealed a robust inverse association between SHBG levels and OSA risk, which persisted after adjusting for potential confounders, including age and BMI. To assess its clinical utility, ROC analysis was performed, which indicated that SHBG has limited value as a standalone screening tool (AUC = 0.595), primarily because of its low specificity. This suggests that its potential lies not in independent diagnosis but as a complementary marker within a broader, multiparameter risk assessment for OSA. Further analysis uncovered a more complex relationship for testosterone. In the unadjusted models, higher testosterone levels were positively correlated with OSA risk; however, this association was reversed to a negative correlation after adjusting for key factors such as age and BMI. Additionally, a nonlinear threshold effect was observed, suggesting that the relationship is multifaceted rather than strictly linear. In contrast, no significant association was found between estrogen levels and OSA in the fully adjusted model. Collectively, these findings provide strong evidence for the clinical significance of sex hormone dysregulation in the pathogenesis of OSA and may inform its stratified management.

The relationship between testosterone and OSA has long been inconsistent. The “association inversion” observed in our study – a positive correlation shifting to negative after full adjustment – likely stems from the interplay of factors like sex, BMI, and metabolic comorbidities. These variables can act as both confounders and mediators. For example, as a confounder, obesity influences both outcomes through independent pathophysiological pathways: obesity reduces endogenous testosterone (by enhancing its aromatization and suppressing the HPGA) while also increasing upper airway collapse via parapharyngeal fat deposition.[20] As a mediator, low testosterone promotes sarcopenia and increased upper airway collapsibility, while also promoting visceral fat accumulation. This alters the adipokine profile (e.g., leptin resistance, decreased adiponectin), which weakens ventilatory drive and exacerbates low-grade inflammation, thereby reinforcing the interplay between OSA and insulin resistance and forming a “metabolic dysfunction–respiratory deterioration” vicious cycle.[21] Since our analysis adjusted for these variables as confounders without a formal mediation analysis, this negative association warrants cautious interpretation. Similarly, Tančić-Gajić et al observed a negative correlation between AHI and testosterone in a multivariate model, but this effect attenuated with the accumulation of metabolic syndrome components.[22] Further subgroup and trend analyses revealed no stable dose-response relationship; Our data also indicated a sex interaction, with significant associations in both male and female groups. The stronger correlation in the 20 to 39 age group may reflect hormonal fluctuations and differences in OSA mechanisms specific to this age range. Socioeconomic and psychological factors further modify this relationship, as a shared sleep environment can affect OSA assessment and chronic stress can disrupt hormonal homeostasis. Mechanistically, testosterone appears to operate through an intricate network of endocrine-respiratory pathways. Testosterone deficiency not only reduces the overall mass of dilator muscles but may also directly influence molecular pathways (e.g., Akt/mTORC1) via the androgen receptor, altering muscle fiber contractile properties and function beyond its classic anabolic role.[23] Low testosterone-driven central obesity increases passive collapse both structurally, through physical compression of the pharynx, and functionally, by elevating the upper airway’s critical closing pressure.[24] Conversely, moderate physiological testosterone levels may enhance airway stability by improving muscle tone,[25] supporting anabolism,[23] and optimizing fat distribution.[26] This aligns with the slight, nonsignificant upturn in our RCS curve, suggesting a compensatory regulation insufficient to form a stable risk pattern. Further increases in testosterone, especially from exogenous supplementation (e.g., replacement therapy), could be detrimental by suppressing the HPGA,[27] increasing hematocrit,[28] or altering respiratory patterns.[29–31] Our RCS curve, however, showed a renewed risk reduction that plateaued after ln(TST) surpassed approximately 5.6, suggesting a certain protective effect may persist within the higher physiological range, though not ruling out potential risks at even higher levels. Overall, the evidence supports a “U-shaped framework”: deficiency is harmful, moderation is optimal, and excess may pose risks. Reverse causality is also plausible: CIH can downregulate the expression of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR) in Leydig cells, thereby inhibiting testosterone synthesis.[32] OSA-related inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) can induce apoptosis in testicular interstitial cells,[33] while sleep fragmentation is linked to melatonin rhythm disturbances[34] and adrenal axis dysfunction,[35] further disrupting sex hormone homeostasis. This establishes a bidirectional feedback network where OSA lowers testosterone, which in turn impacts respiratory control, explaining the high sensitivity of cross-sectional findings to confounder adjustment. In conclusion, the testosterone-OSA relationship is nonlinear, bidirectional, and co-regulated by metabolic and psychosocial factors. Clinical assessment should therefore integrate these variables, avoiding overreliance on a single metric like the AHI.

A key finding of this study is the robust inverse association between SHBG levels and OSA risk, a more consistent relationship than that observed for testosterone. This dose-response effect remained significant across all analytical models, from continuous variable to quartile analyses. Our results corroborate previous research; for instance, Mohammadi et al found significantly lower SHBG in OSA patients, particularly in males,[36] while another study reported a persistent negative correlation between SHBG and AHI in premenopausal women after multivariate adjustment.[36] The mechanistic basis for this association is likely rooted in OSA’s metabolic consequences. SHBG, primarily synthesized in the liver, regulates the bioavailability of sex hormones – particularly testosterone and estrogen – in plasma, and is highly sensitive to metabolic status, including insulin resistance and inflammation.[37] The CIH and sleep fragmentation characteristic of OSA are known to suppress SHBG synthesis, likely by activating inflammatory pathways like NF-κB and exacerbating insulin resistance.[38–40] Crucially, a Mendelian randomization study suggests SHBG is not merely a marker but a potential mediator in OSA’s pathogenesis, linking the respiratory disorder to its comorbidities, such as T2DM and hypertension.[41] Despite this strong biological link, the clinical utility of SHBG as a standalone diagnostic tool appears limited. Our ROC analysis yielded a modest AUC of 0.595, indicating poor diagnostic accuracy. This is not surprising, given SHBG’s association with numerous conditions, from metabolic syndrome to all-cause mortality,[42] which limits its specificity for OSA. Therefore, its value may lie not in independent screening but as an accessible and complementary biomarker alongside existing assessments, such as AHI, to aid in OSA screening, especially in resource-limited settings. Further studies are warranted to explore the value of integrating SHBG into multi-parameter screening strategies for OSA.

Although this study found no direct association between estrogen and OSA, existing evidence suggests the relationship is complex and highly context-dependent rather than absent. For instance, abnormal estrogen levels in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) are linked to a higher incidence of OSA.[43] Conversely, hormone replacement therapy combined with continuous positive airway pressure has been shown to improve respiratory outcomes and quality of life in postmenopausal women.[44] Such heterogeneity across studies may be explained by estrogen’s dose-dependent and stage-specific effects, which are mediated through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and synergistic hormonal pathways.[45,46] The influence of metabolic factors, which can obscure a direct hormonal effect, is particularly evident in men. In males, estradiol (E2) is primarily derived from the peripheral aromatization of testosterone, and its levels are tightly linked to BMI and SHBG. Higher E2 in obese men correlates with greater visceral fat accumulation,[47] a key driver of OSA that worsens upper airway collapsibility through both mechanical compression and systemic inflammation. This potent metabolic-hormonal interplay likely masks any independent effect of E2 on OSA, a hypothesis that requires formal mediation analysis to confirm. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of this study is a significant limitation; single, point-in-time hormone measurements are incapable of capturing the dynamic fluctuations of estrogen across circadian or menstrual cycles. This methodological constraint can lead to an underestimation of the true association, underscoring the need for future longitudinal studies with multi-time-point monitoring to elucidate estrogen’s role in OSA pathophysiology fully.

The pathogenesis of OSA is not a linear process but rather a complex, multifactorial network involving intermittent hypoxia, systemic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation.[48] Within this network, alterations in sex hormones and SHBG function as both consequences and catalysts. OSA-induced stress can disrupt hormonal balance, while these hormonal shifts concurrently impair metabolic function and amplify inflammation, thereby creating a bidirectional feedback loop that exacerbates disease severity. This complex interplay of hormonal and systemic factors underscores that the pathophysiology of OSA cannot be adequately explained by a single biomarker or a simple linear pathway.

Despite the large sample size, robust indicator measurements, and comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors, several key limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes the inference of causality, as it cannot determine whether hormonal alterations precede or result from the development of OSA. The definition of OSA in this study relied on self-reported symptoms rather than objective polysomnography-based measures like the AHI may lead to potential misclassification bias and recall bias, affecting the accuracy of results. Ideally, OSA diagnosis requires overnight polysomnography or polygraphy. Hormone levels were assessed at a single time point, failing to capture crucial intraday and long-term variations that may influence the observed associations. While we controlled for numerous variables, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as medication use or occupational exposures, cannot be entirely excluded. To overcome these limitations, future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish potential causal relationships between sex hormones and OSA. Specifically, long-term monitoring of both hormone levels and disease progression, coupled with objective sleep assessments, is crucial. Moreover, multi-center and cross-cultural studies are needed to validate the generalizability of these findings. Beyond these methodological improvements, future work should focus on 2 key areas: elucidating the precise biological mechanisms by which hormones influence OSA pathophysiology and evaluating the clinical utility of SHBG and other sex hormones as accessible biomarkers for developing cost-effective screening strategies in high-risk populations.

5. Conclusions

This study, utilizing a large sample from the NHANES, provides evidence for SHBG as an adjunctive biomarker for OSA risk assessment in a real-world setting. Clinically, SHBG testing is accessible and cost-effective. However, given its limited standalone diagnostic accuracy, its clinical utility is not as a primary screening tool but rather as an adjunctive marker. Furthermore, the complex relationship between testosterone and OSA necessitates that its clinical application be individualized, accounting for the patient’s sex, age, and comorbidities. Therefore, the value of SHBG may be best realized by integrating it with other indicators – such as hormonal profiles, genetic predispositions, and metabolic parameters – into a multi-biomarker-based early warning model for OSA, thereby enhancing risk stratification and population health management.
