Expanding visual horizons: comparative outcomes of bilateral implantation of an extended depth-of-focus versus enhanced and standard monofocal intraocular lenses
Authors: Ali Altan Ertan Boz, Mahmut Atum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePatients undergoing cataract surgery have different postoperative visual priorities. Some primarily seek excellent uncorrected distance vision and accept the need for near spectacles, whereas others aim for greater spectacle independence across distance, intermediate, and near activities. This study compared visual and patient-reported outcomes after bilateral implantation of an extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) intraocular lens (IOL), an enhanced monofocal (EM) IOL and a standard monofocal IOL.MethodsThis retrospective consecutive comparative case series included patients who underwent bilateral phacoemulsification with implantation of the same IOL model in both eyes. The evaluated IOLs were Tecnis ZCB00 (standard monofocal), Tecnis Eyhance ICB00 (EM), and Asqelio ETLIO130C (EDOF). At 6 months postoperatively, monocular and binocular uncorrected visual acuity were assessed at distance (6 m), intermediate (66 cm), and near (40 cm). Additional outcome measures included manifest refraction, binocular defocus curves, patient-reported halo and glare symptoms, spectacle independence, and patient satisfaction.ResultsA total of 197 patients were included (78 monofocal, 65 EM, and 54 EDOF). Baseline demographic and preoperative ocular characteristics were comparable across groups. Postoperative uncorrected distance visual acuity was similar among groups. Intermediate visual acuity was better in the EM and EDOF groups than in the monofocal group, with the best intermediate performance observed in the EDOF group. Near visual acuity remained limited in all groups but was better in the EDOF group than in the other groups. Defocus-curve findings were consistent with these results. Patient reported halo and glare scores were low and comparable across groups, whereas spectacle independence was highest in the extended depth-of-focus group.ConclusionIn this retrospective comparative case series, bilateral implantation of the Asqelio EDOF IOL was associated with better intermediate visual acuity and modestly better near visual acuity than implantation of the enhanced and standard monofocal IOLs, without compromising distance visual acuity or increasing patient-reported halo and glare at 6 months.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-05096-x.

Introduction

Patients’ expectations after cataract surgery vary considerably. Some patients prioritize high-quality distance vision and accept the need for spectacles for intermediate or near tasks, whereas others seek improved functional vision for daily activities, such as digital device use and reading, or wish to reduce postoperative spectacle dependence as much as possible [1, 2]. These different expectations are clinically important when choosing between standard monofocal, enhanced monofocal (EM) and extended depth of focus (EDOF) intraocular lenses (IOLs).

Standard monofocal IOLs generally provide excellent distance vision when emmetropia is targeted, but they usually offer limited unaided intermediate and near visual acuity [2, 3]. Monovision may improve near function in selected patients, but it is not suitable for all individuals because it depends on neuroadaptation and may reduce stereopsis [4]. Wider-range presbyopia-correcting IOLs can increase spectacle independence, but simultaneous-vision designs may be associated with photic phenomena such as halos and glare [5–7]. EDOF IOLs have therefore been developed to extend the range of functional vision, particularly at intermediate distances, while aiming to reduce the photic symptoms commonly associated with multifocal optical designs [8, 9].

EM and EDOF IOLs both aim to broaden functional vision beyond conventional monofocal correction, but they should not be considered interchangeable categories [10]. EM IOLs typically provide modest improvement in intermediate vision while preserving a monofocal-like visual profile, whereas EDOF IOLs are designed to extend the depth of focus more substantially and may provide better intermediate performance [10, 11].

The aim of this study was to compare visual outcomes and patient-reported experience after bilateral implantation of an EDOF IOL with those after bilateral implantation of an EM IOL and a monofocal IOL.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective consecutive comparative case series was conducted at a single center between June 2019 and January 2024. Consecutive patients who underwent bilateral phacoemulsification for age-related cataract and received the same IOL model in both eyes were eligible for inclusion. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No. 2022/294). Routine surgical informed consent had been obtained from all patients before cataract surgery.

Participants

Eligible patients had bilateral age-related cataracts. Exclusion criteria were corneal astigmatism greater than 1.0 D, axial length shorter than 21 mm or longer than 26 mm, previous intraocular or corneal surgery/pathology, and clinically significant ocular comorbidities such as glaucoma, uveitis, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, retinal disease, amblyopia, or severe ocular surface disease. Eyes with intraoperative or postoperative complications and patients who did not complete the 6-month follow-up were excluded from the final analysis.

Intraocular lenses

Three IOL models were evaluated: a standard monofocal IOL (Tecnis ZCB00, Johnson & Johnson Vision, Jacksonville, FL, USA), an EM IOL (Tecnis Eyhance ICB00, Johnson & Johnson Vision), and an EDOF IOL (Asqelio EDOF ETLIO130C, AST Products, Billerica, MA, USA). The Tecnis ZCB00 is a monofocal IOL with an aspheric anterior surface that provides − 0.27 μm of spherical aberration and a continuous 360° frosted square edge designed to reduce posterior capsule opacification [10]. The Tecnis Eyhance ICB00 is an EM IOL with a spherical posterior surface and a higher-order aspheric anterior surface. Its subtle, continuous power gradient from the lens periphery to the center extends the depth of focus and modestly improves intermediate vision [12, 13]. The Tecnis ZCB00 and Tecnis Eyhance ICB00 use the same single-piece, foldable hydrophobic acrylic platform, with a 6.0-mm optic diameter, 13.0-mm overall length, and refractive index of 1.47. The Asqelio EDOF ETLIO130C is a single-piece hydrophobic acrylic IOL with a bi-aspheric optic and phase-ring design; it also has a 6.0-mm optic diameter and 13.0-mm overall length. Its acrylic material has a refractive index of 1.50 and less than 0.5% water content. The Asqelio EDOF is implanted using the proprietary Bioli delivery system [14].

Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed by a single experienced surgeon using standard phacoemulsification and in-the-bag IOL implantation. All patients underwent bilateral implantation of the same IOL model. Surgery was performed as delayed sequential bilateral cataract surgery (DSBCS). The interval between first- and second-eye surgery was approximately 1 week in routine clinical practice. Postoperatively, all patients received a standard topical regimen of an antibiotic/steroid combination eye drop (0.5% moxifloxacin + 0.1% dexamethasone) five times daily for one week, tapered over 3 weeks.

Examinations and outcome measures

All patients underwent a comprehensive preoperative examination including measurement of uncorrected and corrected distance visual acuity, automated refraction (Tonoref III autorefractor, Nidek, Tokyo, Japan) followed by manifest refraction, slit-lamp biomicroscopy of the anterior segment, applanation tonometry, and dilated fundus examination with spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (Cirrus HD-OCT 4000, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Germany) to rule out macular pathology. Preoperative ocular biometry was performed using the IOL Master 500 optical biometer (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), and IOL power was calculated using the Barrett Universal II formula, targeting slight myopia in both eyes (approximately − 0.25 D).

Postoperative visits were scheduled at day 1, week 1, month 1, month 3, and month 6. At the 6-month visit, a detailed examination was performed that included monocular and binocular visual acuity assessments at various distances, refraction, defocus curve measurement, and patient questionnaires regarding visual phenomena and satisfaction.

Visual acuity was tested at three distances under photopic conditions (approximately 85 cd/m²) with 100% contrast optotype charts and recorded in Snellen notation then converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for analysis. The measurements were as follows:

Intermediate and near visual acuities were measured using a standardized Turkish near-reading chart by Eğrilmez et al., according to international standards for near-vision assessment [12].

Binocular defocus curves were obtained using the patient’s best distance correction, with defocus varied from + 1.00 D to − 2.50 D in 0.50-D steps [13]. Patient-reported halos and glare were evaluated using the glare/halo items of the Turkish validated NEI-RQL-42 [14]. Patients rated symptoms under night-time or low-light conditions, as reflected by the questionnaire wording and clinical interview. Spectacle independence and satisfaction with distance, intermediate, and near vision were assessed using a questionnaire modeled on the Concerto Study instrument [15].

The primary outcome measures were binocular uncorrected distance, intermediate, and near visual acuity and patient-reported satisfaction. Secondary outcomes were monocular visual acuity outcomes, corrected visual acuity outcomes, manifest refractive outcomes, binocular defocus-curve results, and patient-reported photic phenomena.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and/or Shapiro-Wilk tests, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared among groups using one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc testing or the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn-Bonferroni post hoc comparisons, depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated.

To estimate the study’s power, a post hoc power analysis for the one-way ANOVA comparing the three groups was conducted. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), an alpha of 0.05, and three groups (n = 197 total patients), the power was calculated to be 0.96, indicating an adequate sample size to detect differences.

Results

Of the 235 patients initially assessed, 197 met the eligibility criteria and completed the 6-month follow-up. Patients were excluded because of intraoperative or postoperative complications (27 patients) or loss to follow-up (11 patients). The final analysis included 78 patients in the monofocal group, 65 in the EM group, and 54 in the EDOF group. The post hoc analysis indicated adequate statistical power (0.96) for detecting intergroup differences. The mean halo/glare score on the NEI-RQL-42 subscale, where higher scores indicate fewer symptoms, was 54.5 ± 20.8 in the monofocal group, 56.3 ± 13.1 in the EM group, and 58.8 ± 17.4 in the EDOF group. Halo and glare symptoms were mild across all three groups, with no statistically significant between-group differences. When directly questioned, patients in each group reported only mild or occasional halos and glare under nighttime conditions, and none considered these symptoms severe enough to interfere with daily activities.

Mean satisfaction with uncorrected distance vision was high and comparable among the three groups (approximately 9.6–9.9/10; p > 0.05). In contrast, satisfaction with intermediate and near vision differed by IOL type. Intermediate-vision satisfaction was lower in the monofocal group (4.42 ± 1.02) than in the EM and EDOF groups (8.72 ± 0.97 and 8.89 ± 0.52, respectively; p < 0.05 for both comparisons), with no significant difference between the EM and EDOF groups (p = 0.215). Near-vision satisfaction was highest in the EDOF group (7.05 ± 0.95), followed by the EM group (5.58 ± 1.01) and the monofocal group (0.92 ± 0.67), with all pairwise comparisons reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05). Patient-reported satisfaction scores were consistent with the visual acuity findings, with higher intermediate and near satisfaction in the EM and EDOF groups than in the monofocal group.

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that bilateral implantation of EDOF IOL was associated with better intermediate visual acuity than the standard monofocal IOL and with modestly better near performance than both comparator lenses, while distance visual acuity remained similar across groups. The EM IOL also improved intermediate vision compared with the standard monofocal lens, which is consistent with the intended functional positioning of this IOL category [7, 16]. These findings are broadly in line with prior literature showing that EM IOLs improve intermediate performance relative to standard monofocal lenses and that EDOF designs can expand the functional range of vision without substantially compromising distance acuity [7, 10, 11, 16, 17].

Postoperative manifest SE and cylinder values were comparable among the three groups, indicating similar refractive accuracy and residual astigmatism across IOL types. This comparability is essential because even small residual refractive errors may influence intermediate and near visual performance, particularly in EDOF optics. Although mild postoperative myopia is commonly used to support functional near vision after cataract surgery, especially with EDOF IOLs, it must be balanced against the need to preserve high-quality distance vision [17, 18]. In the present study, a low myopic target of approximately − 0.25 D was selected for all groups, and most eyes achieved manifest refraction within ± 0.50 D of emmetropia, supporting the predictability of refractive targeting. Manifest refraction was used as the principal basis for refractive interpretation [1, 19]. The choice of manifest refraction as the basis for postoperative refractive interpretation is supported by the prospective study of Davidova et al., who compared subjective refraction with objective refraction obtained using an autorefractor, a Hartmann-Shack aberrometer, and a ray-tracing aberrometer in eyes implanted with a wavefront-shaping EDOF IOL or a diffractive simultaneous-vision IOL [20]. They reported that objective refraction obtained with modern devices was not interchangeable with subjective refraction after implantation of these presbyopia-correcting IOLs, with generally poor to at most moderate agreement between subjective and objective measurements. In the EDOF group, objective measurements showed a more myopic spherical equivalent than subjective refraction across all tested devices. These findings are clinically relevant because complex EDOF and simultaneous-vision optical designs may affect automated refractive estimates. Accordingly, objective autorefraction was not used for comparative refractive interpretation in the present study; postoperative refractive outcomes were interpreted on the basis of manifest refraction, including manifest spherical equivalent and manifest cylinder.

All three IOL types provided excellent and comparable distance vision at 6 months, with every patient achieving binocular UDVA of 0.1 logMAR or better, indicating that neither the EM nor the EDOF optical design compromised distance performance relative to the standard monofocal IOL. In contrast, intermediate visual outcomes differed according to IOL design. Although binocular UIVA was similar between the EM and EDOF groups, the superior monocular performance of the EDOF IOL suggests a greater intrinsic extension of focus. The relatively strong binocular performance observed in the EM group may be partly explained by binocular summation and neuroadaptation, mechanisms that can enhance functional visual performance after bilateral implantation [7, 21]. Overall, these findings are consistent with previous reports showing that enhanced monofocal lenses improve intermediate vision compared with standard monofocal IOLs, while EDOF lenses provide a broader functional range of vision with preservation of distance acuity [17, 19, 22, 23].

Unaided near vision remained limited across all IOL groups, as expected, because neither the EM nor the EDOF IOL is designed to provide full multifocal near correction. No patient achieved 0.1 logMAR binocular UNVA without spectacles, particularly for fine print or prolonged near tasks. Nevertheless, the EDOF group showed a more favorable near-vision profile than the other groups, with a higher proportion of patients achieving 0.3 logMAR or better binocular UNVA. These findings suggest that the EDOF IOL may provide additional support for functional near activities, but it should not be presented as an IOL that ensures spectacle-independent near vision.

The binocular defocus curve results further supported the visual acuity findings. At − 1.00 D, the EM and EDOF IOLs showed comparable performance, and both outperformed the monofocal IOL, indicating a clinically relevant improvement in intermediate vision. At higher defocus levels corresponding to nearer viewing distances (− 1.50 D to − 2.50 D), the EDOF group maintained better visual acuity than the EM and monofocal groups; however, this should be interpreted as an extension of functional vision toward near distances rather than evidence of complete near-vision correction. This pattern is consistent with the optical principle of EDOF IOLs, which broadens the range of focus, whereas EM designs typically provide more limited extension, mainly in the intermediate range. The EM outcomes observed in this study are also consistent with previous reports showing modest intermediate gains and limited benefits for near tasks [22–26].

Patient-reported halo and glare symptoms were low and did not differ significantly among the monofocal, EM, and EDOF groups. Mean NEI-RQL-42 halo/glare scores were in a similar range across the three groups, indicating generally mild or infrequent symptoms. These findings suggest that the optical modifications used to extend functional vision, including the phase-ring design of the Asqelio EDOF IOL, did not increase night-vision complaints [27]. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that EDOF IOLs can maintain an acceptable dysphotopsia profile while broadening the functional range of vision [9, 23, 26, 28, 29].

Spectacle independence and patient satisfaction were generally consistent with the objective visual acuity findings. Distance-vision satisfaction was high in all groups regardless of IOL type. Differences became more evident at intermediate and near distances. Patients in the monofocal group reported lower satisfaction for unaided intermediate vision, whereas both the EM and EDOF groups reported high satisfaction for intermediate activities, supporting the clinical benefit of these IOL designs for this range of vision. The EDOF group showed slightly higher intermediate satisfaction than the EM group, in line with its favorable intermediate acuity profile. For near vision, however, the findings should be interpreted more cautiously. Although the EDOF group reported higher unaided near-vision satisfaction than the EM and monofocal groups, this does not indicate complete near-vision correction or full spectacle independence for near tasks. Rather, the EDOF IOL appeared to provide additional functional support for occasional near activities, while reading glasses remained necessary for fine print or prolonged near work. This balanced profile—excellent distance vision, strong intermediate performance, supportive but limited near function, and low patient-reported halo/glare symptoms—is consistent with previous reports describing EDOF and EM IOLs as options that broaden functional vision while maintaining an acceptable dysphotopsia profile [8, 11, 16, 28–30].

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, non-randomized design may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding, and the single-center, single-surgeon setting may limit the generalizability of the findings despite improving procedural consistency. The 6-month follow-up was sufficient to evaluate early refractive, visual acuity, and patient-reported outcomes but does not allow assessment of longer-term neuroadaptation, capsular changes, or late satisfaction. In addition, contrast sensitivity, reading speed, mesopic visual performance, and pupil-size effects were not evaluated. The absence of a simultaneous-vision comparator also limits direct comparison with other presbyopia-correcting optical designs. Nevertheless, the study has important strengths, including the direct comparison of three IOL categories using a consistent methodology, assessment of both objective and patient-reported outcomes, and evaluation of binocular visual function. Future prospective randomized studies with larger samples, longer follow-up, and additional quality-of-vision measures are needed to confirm and extend these findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective consecutive comparative case series, bilateral implantation of the Asqelio EDOF ETLIO130C IOL was associated with better intermediate visual acuity than implantation of enhanced or standard monofocal IOLs, while distance visual acuity and patient-reported halo/glare symptoms remained comparable among groups. Near visual outcomes should be interpreted cautiously: although the EDOF group showed some supportive benefit for unaided near tasks, this effect was limited and did not indicate complete near-vision correction or spectacle independence for fine print and prolonged near work. Overall, the Asqelio EDOF IOL may be considered a reasonable option for cataract patients with high intermediate-vision demands who wish to preserve distance vision and maintain a low photic-symptom profile, if expectations regarding near vision are appropriately managed.
