Discover QTLs for the level of blood components in Shaoxing duck using GWAS and haplotype sharing analysis
Authors: Wenwu Xu, Zhenzhen Wang, Zeng Tao, Kui Li, Lizhi Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlood composition is indicative of health-related traits such as immunity and metabolism. The use of molecular genetics to investigate alterations in these attributes in laying ducks is a novel approach. Our objective was to employ genome − wide association studies (GWAS) and haplotype − sharing analysis to identify genomic regions and potential genes associated with 11 blood components in Shaoxing ducks. Our findings revealed 35 SNPs and 1 SNP associated with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) and globulin (GLB), respectively. We identified 36 putative candidate genes for the LDL trait in close proximity to major QTLs and key loci. Based on their biochemical and physiological properties, TRA2A, NPY, ARHGEF26, DHX36, and AADAC are the strongest putative candidate genes. Through linkage disequilibrium analysis and haplotype sharing analysis, we identified three haplotypes and one haplotype, respectively, that were significantly linked with LDL and GLB. These haplotypes could be selected as potential candidate haplotypes for molecular breeding of Shaoxing ducks. Additionally, we utilized a bootstrap test to verify the reliability of GWAS with small experimental samples. The test can be accessed at https://github.com/xuwenwu24/Bootstrap-test.

Introduction

Lipid components, microelements, and proteins play a crucial role in the synthesis and transportation of serum.1,2 Maintaining electrolyte balance is essential for acid–base balance and cellular homeostasis. Electrolytes in the blood are critical for human and animal health, and their imbalance can lead to many acute or chronic diseases.3 Clinical diagnostics can utilize blood components such as HDL and LDL to assess the health of individuals and animals.4 The addition of calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and other trace elements to feed can alleviate the heat stress in poultry.5 In animal breeding, blood components can serve as indirect indicators of certain economic traits. For instance, abdominal fat content in broiler chickens can be indirectly selected by abdominal fat percentage and blood VLDL levels.6 Farhat reported that serum glucose levels in fat ducks were significantly lower than those in lean ducks during fasting.7

Numerous researchers worldwide have conducted extensive research on QTLs related to the chemical composition of blood. The availability of SNPs has been consistently demonstrated, and the technology of molecular genetics is steadily advancing. Wallach, Witteman, and Kao have discovered that the expression of certain genes can impact electrolyte levels in a human serum.8–10 Candidate genes associated with blood chemistry were identified by Goor et al. in an advanced intercrossed line of chickens under heat stress.5 Other studies have also reported QTLs associated with blood components in chickens.3,11–13

In 2005, after Klein et al. successfully applied GWAS for the first time in the genetic analysis of human age − related macular degeneration, thereby revealing the potential of GAWS in the study of complex traits.14 To advance animal breeding, particularly in poultry, GWAS can be utilized to investigate the genetic basis of biomarkers. However, there is limited research on the impact of genetic factors on changes in blood composition and levels in laying ducks.

The Shaoxing duck is a highly productive breed known for its excellent egg-laying capabilities, with a feeding rate of up to 60% in China. The objective of this study was to examine the general characteristics of blood components in Shaoxing ducks and determine their genetic basis. The findings of this study may serve as a valuable reference for the breeding of Shaoxing ducks.

Materials and methods

Ducks and phenotypes

A total of 166 Shaoxing ducks from Hubei Shendan Co., Ltd. were used in our study. All ducks were sourced from a pure group, of the same generation and were housed in threE − tiered cages, with one duck per cage. The duck house was equipped with wet curtains for cooling, nipplE − style free drinking water, and a feeding machine that provided timely feeding every day. At week 66, a professional collected all blood samples using standard procedures for brachial veins. The blood was left to stand for half an hour and then centrifuged to obtain the upper serum. The auto-analyzer (HITACHI 7170, Hitachi, Japan) was used to detect the following serum indicators: albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), cholinesterase (ChE), globulin (GLB), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), total cholesterol (TC), and total protein (TP). Additionally, serum magnesium (Mg), phosphorus (P), and calcium (Ca) were also studied. Normality testing was performed using Shapiro–Wilk test to check the distribution of the studied traits. If the traits are skewed from normality, the phenotypic data were normalized by the Box-Cox transformation using R. The Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all duck care and use protocols (approval number: 2021 ZAASLA15).

Genome sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated from blood using the standard cetyl trimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The quality and quantity of DNA were examined using agarose gel electrophoresis. Paired-end libraries were generated for each eligible sample using standard procedures. Fragments were PCR amplified with 500-bp inserts, end repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to paired-end adaptors for library construction. The libraries were then submitted to 150-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform, with a mean sequencing depth of 10x for all experimental ducks, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended methods. The depth of 10X ensured the accuracy of variant calling and genotyping and met the requirements for population genetic analyses.15

Variant discovery and genotyping

The raw sequencing data was assembled to the reference duck genome (CAU duck 1.0) using Burrows-Wheeler (BWA aln).16 On average, 96.4% of the reads were successfully mapped, resulting in a final average sequencing coverage of ×10 (ranging from ×8 to ×18) per individual. The mapping details of the resequencing samples can be found in Additional file 1: Table S1, Table S2 and Table S3. Paired reads that were mapped to the exact same position on the reference genome were marked and removed by Picard MarkDuplicates (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard) to avoid any influence on variant detection. After that, Raw variants were called for individual bases using GATK’s HaplotypeCaller (version 4.0).17 This process resulted in 166 individual *.gvcf files, which were subjected to joint variant calling using GenotypeGVCFs. The VariantFiltration command was employed to exclude potential falsE − positive variant calls with the parameter ‘–filterExpression ‘QD < 2.0 ǁ FS > 60.0 ǁ MQ < 40.0 |’ for SNPs. SNPs that did not meet the following criteria were excluded: (1) a minor allele frequency >0.05; (2) maximum missing rate <0.1; and (3) only two alleles. Identified SNPs were further classified by SnpEff based on the gene annotation of the reference genome.18

SinglE − trait GWAS analysis

Based on the univariate linear mixed model (see Equation (1)), the singlE − marker association between the variants and the phenotype was tested using GEMMA (v.0.94) and is described in the following equation:19 (1)y=xβ+u+ϵ; u ∼MVNn(0,λτ−1K),ϵ∼MVNn(0,τ−1In)

All the experimental ducks are female ducks, and the breeding farms, breeding batches (the age of the ducks), and slaughter batches are the same. Therefore, gender and batch factors are not considered as fixed effects in the GEMMA model. y represented the vector of phenotypic observation, which included a column of one second; x is a vector of genotypes at each locus; β is the effect of SNPs; u is a vector of random effects following the multivariate normal distribution MVNn(0,λτ−1K), in which λ the ratio of the additive genetic variance to the variance of residual variance, τ–1 is the variance of the residual errors, and K is a kinship matrix estimated from wholE − genome sequence variants; ϵ is a vector of errors following the multivariate normal distribution (see Equation (1)) and In is an identity matrix. GenomE − wide significance thresholds were set by us using naive Bonferroni correction (0.05 divided by the number of examined SNPs). The validity of genomE − wide association studies is affected by population stratification, which is one of a variety of influences.20 Population stratification vastly affects GWAS reliability, so software R and Plink (version 1.9) were used to evaluate the population structure of the experimental populations. The Q–Q plot is a commonly used tool for scanning population stratification in GWAS studies. In this study, the Q–Q plot was constructed by R v3.6.1 software.20 PCA was used to evaluate the similarity of the genetic background the experimental ducks. PCA was generated by software Plink (version 1.9). The statistical power of a GWAS was estimated by GCTA software, with the following specific parameters: Sample size, heritability, Type 1 error rate used in the power calculation, and the variance of the SNP-derived genetic relationships (λτ–1).

This study calculated the percentage of phenotypic variation explained (PVE) by significant SNPs, utilizing the formula: (VreducE−Vfull)/Vreduce, where Vfull and Vreduce represent the residual variances in the GWAS model with and without the associated SNP loci, respectively.

Post GWA analysis

In this study, the significant QTLs were obtained through LD analysis between the most significant SNP loci and other SNPs, and based on the extent of LD between poultry genomes, the extent of LD, where r2 drops to approximately 0.4 across the genome was used as the proper distance in this study. The genes located within the significant QTL regions were considered as candidate genes. Genes located within the significant QTL regions were considered as candidate genes. The gene ontology within the confidence interval was investigated through the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID), and the biological processes related to the traits were determined (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/home.jsp).21

The lead variant was defined as the marker with the smallest P-value within a QTL. The PLINK (version 1.9) –r2 command was used to calculate the linkage disequilibrium (LD) between the lead variant and all other variants.20 Furthermore, fastPHASE was utilized with default settings to construct haplotypes within confidence intervals, and all individual haplotypes were meticulously examined to identify the shared susceptibility haplotypes.22

Bootstrap test

Bootstrap testing is a resampling technique used to estimate population statistics by replacing the original sampling data set. In this study, we utilized this technique to verify the reliability of GWAS. This method can be used to estimate summary statistics such as mean, standard deviation, confidence interval, or correlation coefficient by repeatedly taking small samples, calculating the statistic, and averaging the calculated statistics. In this study, we generated a specified number of bootstrap samples (1000), each with the same number of subjects as the original sample (166), are generated via rE − sampling with replacement. Then we conducted GWAS analysis on the newly composed 166 individuals to determine if the vulnerable regions identified in our study still exhibited strong signals, and finally counted how many times significant signals were identified in the 1000 GWAS experiments.23

The decision to use 1000 times for the resampling numbers for the bootstrapping in our study was determined partly based on the research by Li er al. And this study generated the specified number of bootstrap samples (1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, …, 10,000), each with the same number of subjects as the original sample (166), are generated via rE − sampling with replacement. In all resampled GWAS analyses (from 1000 to 10,000 times), basically, only 5% or less of the groups can detect significant signals (Additional file 1: Figure S5).

Results

Phenotype and genetic parameter statistics

Figure S2 in the additional file 1 demonstrates that the eleven phenotypes conform to a normal distribution. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics for the blood components. The estimates of SNP-based heritability and phenotypic correlations between blood components are displayed on the diagonal in Table 2. The heritability of TP, GLB, ALP, TC, and LDL, which were 0.15, 0.28, 0.37, 0.26, and 0.17, respectively, was moderate. The heritability of the ChE trait may exceed 0.7 (h2 = 0.77), which is consistent with previous studies in Pekin ducks (h2 = 0.66) and mice (h2 = 0.67–0.70).24,25 The heritability estimation of ALB, HDL, P, Mg, and Ca failed and indicated as ‘−’ in Table 2. Phenotypic correlation analyses revealed that the trait TP was strongly correlated with ALB and GLB (r = 0.70 and 0.97, respectively), and the serum concentration of Ca was highly correlated with ALB. Additionally, there were modest correlations observed between HDL and GLB, ALP and ALB, GLB and Mg, ChE and ALP, Ca and ALP, HDL and TC, and Mg and TC. Furthermore, there were correlations with other features ranging from 0.10 to 0.65.

Genome − wide association study

After quality control, a total of 6,746,746 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 166 individuals were retained for further analysis. Association tests for all eleven phenotypes were performed using a univariate linear model, and the threshold obtained by the naïve Bonferroni correction was 7.41E − 09. The results showed that no SNPs in the ALB, ALP, Ca, ChE, HDL, Mg, P, TC, and TP phenotypes surpassed this threshold (Figure 1(A–J)). However, we identified 35 SNPs that surpassed the genomE − wide significance level for LDL (Figure 2(A), Table 3), with the most significantly associated SNP being 9_25547073 (P-wald = 6.88E − 13), located at 25547073 bp within a 0.55 Mb region (25.00 Mb − 25.55 Mb) on chromosome 9. Additionally, two other quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with LDL were identified: a 1.97 Mb region spanning from 32.30 Mb to 34.27 Mb on chromosome 2 and a 0.02 Mb region spanning from 1.80 Mb to 1.82 Mb on chromosome 10. The most significantly associated SNPs in these two QTLs were 2_34241010 (P-value = 5.35E − 12) and 10_1810786 (P-value = 1.15E − 10), located at 34241010 bp on chromosome 2 and 1810786 bp on chromosome 10, respectively. A total of 36 potential candidate genes were discovered nearby the relevant loci and in the vicinity of the significant QTLs, as shown in Table 3. In addition, the most significant associated locus on chromosome 2, 2_34241010 (P_wald = 5.35E − 12) can explain 4.85% of the phenotypic variation. The most significant associated loci on chromosomes 9 and 10, 9_25547073 (P_wald = 6.88E − 13) and 10_1810786 (P_wald = 1.15E − 10) respectively, can explain 6.15% and 5.02% of the phenotypic variation. The specific results obtained were also shown in Table 3.

Post GWA analysis

Linkage disequilibrium analysis was conducted on SNPs located within significant QTLs on chromosomes 2, 9, and 10, which were selected for their association with LDL traits. The most significant association was observed with SNP 2_34241010, which was found to be in strong LD with 36 other SNPs within a 0.58 Mb region spanning from 34.20 Mb to 34.78 Mb on chromosome 2 (Figure 3(A), Additional file 1: Table S4). Similarly, SNP 9_25547073, one of 16 SNPs with substantial LD status within a 0.65 Mb region spanning from 25.00 Mb to 25.65 Mb on chromosome 9, showed the strongest association (Figure 3(B), Additional file 1: Table S4). The most significant association for LDL traits was observed with SNP 10_1810786, which was found to be in strong LD with 61 other SNPs within a 0.47 Mb region spanning from 1.36 Mb to 1.83 Mb on chromosome 10 (Figure 3(C), Additional file 1: Table S4). For GLB trait, the most significant association was observed with SNP 1_185895952, which was found to be in strong LD with 254 other SNPs within a 4.95 Mb region spanning from 182.73 Mb to 187.68 Mb on chromosome 1 (Figure 3(D), Additional file 1: Table S4).

Regional plots of GWAS signals for LDL in chromosome 2 (a), chromosome 9 (B) and chromosome 10 (C). D was the regional plots of GWAS signals for GLB.

We conducted a haplotype sharing analysis on specific intervals using linkage disequilibrium analysis, after obtaining comparable confidence intervals for LDL and GLB. As illustrated in Figure 4(A) and Additional File 2 (Table S1), we identified 299 sequences on chromosome 2 that shared a particular haplotype for LDL, which we defined as the ‘Shared-haplotype’. The mean value of the phenotype corresponding to the Shared-haplotype was 0.48, while the remaining 33 sequences constituted other haplotypes, which we defined as ‘Chaotic-haplotype’, with a mean value of 0.85. Subsequently, we employed a t-test to compare the shared and chaotic haplotypes. The results indicated that the shared haplotype on chromosome 2 significantly affects LDL (p-value = 5.94E − 07). We continued to use the same method and identified a shared haplotype on chromosome 9 and chromosome 10, respectively, as shown in Figure 4(B and C), and Additional File 2: Table S2, Table S3. The p-values were 1.93E − 06 and 0.01, respectively, when the t-test was performed on the shared haplotype and chaotic haplotype. For GLB, the confidence interval on chromosome 1 (Figure 4(D) and Additional File 2 (Table S4)) encompassed 254 loci, and we selected the loci with LD > 0.6 for haplotype sharing analysis. Based on the results, we defined a shared-haplotype of GLB with a calculated mean value of 37.96, which was shared by 288 sequences on chromosome 1. The remaining haplotypes were defined as chaotic-haplotypes without any regularity, consisting of the remaining 44 sequences, with a mean of 43.30 for those phenotypes. The t-test results of shared-haplotype and chaotic-haplotype demonstrated that the shared-haplotype has a significant impact on GLB (p-value = 4.56E − 09).

Bootstrap test

Our study has several significant limitations, including the very small sample size of our experimental population. Despite these limitations, our study provides informative data. To validate the significance of the signal on chromosome 2 for the LDL trait, we conducted a bootstrap test. The results indicated that over 950 out of 1,000 GWAS did not detect significant signals (P_wald < 7.41E − 09) in the range from 34206546 bp to 34279031 pb on chromosome 2. This finding suggests that the fluctuation in the data structure of our experimental population did not affect the GWAS (FDR < 0.05).

Discussion

Blood components are frequently used as clinical indicators in medical diagnoses and metabolism research. To date, numerous studies have focused on the genetic determinants of blood components in humans, pigs and chickens, and have reported several candidate QTLs and genes.5,26,27 However, the molecular genetic research that investigates the factors affecting blood components in laying ducks is still lacking. The development of high-density SNP array technology and ongoing improvements in genome sequencing have improved the utility of GWAS as a tool for genetic analysis of trait loci. In our study, we performed GWAS for 11 traits using a univariate linear mixed model. Based on the concentrations of enzymes, proteins, and chemicals in the blood, our research is the first to disclose chromosomal heritability and potential loci in Shaoxing ducks. Genetic parameter estimates show that ALB, HDL, P, Mg and Ca have low heritability, which is consistent with the research results of Zhu et al. We found that ChE had the highest value (0.77) by heritability estimation.24

We conducted a GWAS on the Shaoxing duck population and provided compelling evidence of SNP associations with the blood component characteristics. Most of the SNPs we identified at the genomE − wide significance level were located within known genes, revealing linkage imbalances between marker SNPs and pathogenic variants within or near genes. Although the characteristics and functions of these genes have not yet been thoroughly studied, their discovery may open up new avenues of research in the genetics of blood component quality. Our study identified 35 candidate genes, 5 of which are strongly biologically linked to LDL. Previous studied have shown that Transformer-2 alpha (TRA2A) is involved in human cholesterol metabolism. As a member of the splicing factor SR-like protein family, TRA2A is a human homolog of Drosophila derelasE − 2 (TRA-2) protein and is one of the most well-studied splicing regulators in Drosophila.28,29 Neuropeptide Y (NPY) primarily regulates energy balance, food intake, and cholesterol metabolism, and is widely expressed in both the peripheral and central nervous system. Recent research by Uusitupa et al. has shed new light on the regulation of cholesterol metabolism, revealing a correlation between a leucine (7)-to-proline (7) polymorphism (Pro(7)) in the signal peptide of NPY and elevated cholesterol levels,30 and the study of Erkkila et al. also obtained similar results.31 As LDL is the primary causative factor for atherosclerosis, and given the strong association of two out of the 35 candidate genes, namely ARHGEF26 and DHX36, with coronary artery disease (CAD), we postulate that these two genes may exert an influence on the production and metabolism of LDL, thereby contributing to the development of CAD.32,33 The most intriguing gene is Arylacetamide deacetylase (AADAC), which is located on chromosome 9. AADAC is a 45-kDa esterase that contains a typical GXSXG motif and shows significant sequence homology to hormonE − sensitive lipase in its presumed active site. Although the exact function AADAC in lipid metabolism in vivo remains unclear, it is highly probable that its transcription is regulated by PPAR due to its predominant expression in the liver and intestine.34 PPARα-activation will enhance the expression of the AADAC gene, which will increase fatty acid oxidation in rodent hepatocytes and decrease TG buildup.34

The levels of electrolytes and lipids in the blood are associated with obesity, heart disease, and other human diseases. Several candidate genetic loci related to the regulation of blood substances have been identified in human studies. Through GWAS meta-analyses, Meyer et al. have demonstrated that common CASR mutations and DGKD, GCKR, and GATA3 genes play a crucial role in serum calcium regulation.35,36 In addition, common variations in six genomic regions (MUC1, ATP2B1, DCDC5, TRPM6, SHROOM3, and MDS1 or nearby) are significantly associated with serum Mg levels. In livestock research, some QTLs related to blood electrolyte levels have also been identified. Relevant studies have shown that QTLs on chromosomes 6,37 12, 13,38 14 and 1539 have significant effects on the concentration of calciumions in pig serum. In chicken, Goor et al. found that a single QTL for ionized Ca was located on GGA26 for the measurement taken during heat.5 In a GWAS study of Peking ducks in Beijing, Zhu et al. identified six strongly associated QTLs for blood Ca2+ concentration. The most significant one was identified in KB744134.1 (P = 3.72E − 06) in the upstream region of gene RAB11B, and explained 4% of phenotypic variation. Most of the candidate genes or putative QTLs summarized above are involved in ion transport and body growth.24 In this study, no significant QTL associated with serum Ca, Mg, and P were identified, suggesting that the levels of serum Ca, Mg, and P in Shaoxing ducks may be influenced by multiple genes. Increasing the sample size in subsequent GWAS may reveal some meaningful significant loci.

Our research has identified multiple haplotypes that are strongly associated with three specific traits in egg-laying ducks. This provides valuable insights and guidance for future breeding programs. However, the relatively small sample size of our experimental population is a limitation of this study. To address this, we employed a bootstrap test to verify the reliability of our GWAS results. The test confirmed that the significant signals obtained in our GWA study were not due to chance, and therefore can be considered reliable. The method we used is available on Github at the following address: https://github.com/xuwenwu24/Bootstrap-test.

Conclusion

This study presents a GWAS analysis of 11 blood components in Shaoxing ducks, identifying a total of 36 significant SNPs associated with 35 candidate genes. Linkage disequilibrium analysis and haplotype sharing analysis revealed three haplotypes, one of which was strongly linked with LDL and another with GLB. This is the first GWAS publication on blood components in Shaoxing ducks, contributing to the limited knowledge on the levels and heritability of these components. Our findings suggest potential biomarkers that could be utilized in breeding programs for duck-related traits.
