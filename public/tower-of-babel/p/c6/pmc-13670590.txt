Association of cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose index and modified indices in predicting cardiovascular diseases incidence among population with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Zhen Zhao, Pengchong Du, Manning Jian, Yuanyuan Liu, Hongyi Pan, Qing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, characterized by the interplay of metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular dysfunction, is a key driver of cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence. The cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose (CHG) index, which integrates total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and fasting blood glucose, and its modified indices (combined with obesity indicators) have potential for CVD risk prediction. However, their role across CKM stages 0–3 remains unclear.MethodsA nationwide prospective cohort study included 5,404 participants with CKM stages 0–3, screened from 17,708 individuals screened between 2011 and 2020. CHG index and modified indices (CHG-BMI, CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR) were calculated. Logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, subgroup analysis, and sensitivity analysis were used to evaluate associations with CVD incidence and identify exploratory threshold association.ResultsDuring follow-up, 25.5% of participants developed CVD. CHG and modified indices were independently associated with increased CVD risk (all P < 0.001). In fully adjusted models, CHG as continuous data showed a 28% higher risk (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.06–1.58) of outcome, and CHG-WHtR was 54% higher (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.16–2.04). Meanwhile, the highest quartile (Q4) of CHG-WHtR was associated with a 43% higher risk (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.03–1.98) compared to Q1, while CHG-BMI (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.22–2.31) showed stronger associations. RCS analysis identified exploratory inflection points like CHG ≥ 5.15 and CHG-WHtR ≥ 2.41, above which CVD risk significantly increased (P < 0.005) and validated by bootstrap sampling. Subgroup analysis confirmed consistent associations across age, gender, and CKM stages subpopulations. Multiple sensitivity analysis supported the robustness of these associations across CKM stages.ConclusionCHG index and its modified indices, particularly CHG-WHtR and CHG-Wc, are valuable indicators associated with reported incident CVD across CKM stages 0–3. Their stage-specific associations and exploratory threshold association support their utility in risk stratification for CKM syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06295-6.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of global mortality, accounting for nearly one-third of all deaths worldwide. Its burden is dominantly driven by interconnected pathophysiological processes involving metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular damage-factors collectively encapsulated in the recently proposed cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome by the American Heart Association (AHA) [1, 2]. CKM syndrome emphasizes the systemic interplay between these components, progressing through stages 0 (no risk factors) to 3 (subclinical CVD or high-risk CKD), with early stages representing critical windows for intervention to prevent irreversible damage [2, 3]. Thus, screening diagnostic indicators and early intervention are essential to target and reduce CVD incidence in at-risk CKM populations.

Metabolic indices that integrate key markers of lipid and glucose homeostasis have emerged as valuable tools for CVD risk prediction. While indices like the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index have been widely validated, the cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose (CHG) index offers a distinct perspective by incorporating total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and fasting blood glucose (FBG). This composition uniquely captures atherogenic lipid profiles via TC/HDL imbalance and glucose dysregulation, which are two core pillars of metabolic dysfunction that drive atherosclerotic progression and renal microvascular injury. Unlike indices focused solely on insulin resistance, CHG directly links lipid metabolism (a primary driver of plaque formation) to glucose control (a key mediator of endothelial dysfunction), making it a promising marker for CKM-related CVD risk.

Metabolic indices integrating lipid and glucose homeostasis have emerged as reliable tools for CVD risk prediction. The cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, and glucose (CHG) index captures atherogenic lipid profiles and glucose dysregulation, two core pillars of metabolic dysfunction that drive atherosclerotic and renal injury [4, 5]. Distinct from insulin resistance indices, CHG directly links lipid metabolism and glucose control to vascular damage, supporting its promise as a marker for CKM-related CVD risk.

Notably, metabolic disorder rarely occurred independently, but often synergized with adiposity, particularly visceral obesity, and amplifying cardiometabolic risk. Visceral fat triggers pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative pathways that exacerbate insulin resistance, impair lipid metabolism, and damage vascular endothelium-effects that are central to CKM progression [6, 7]. Modified CHG indices, which combine CHG with obesity indicators, including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (Wc), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), may therefore better capture this synergism. Wc and WHtR, in particular, better than BMI in reflecting visceral adiposity and have been shown to enhance the association value of metabolic indices by accounting for adipose tissue dysfunction [8, 9].

Despite growing interest in CHG as a metabolic marker, its utility across the full spectrum of CKM stages remains unelucidated. Existing studies have either focused on general populations or single CKM stages, leaving gaps in understanding how CHG and its modified indices perform in early (preclinical) versus advanced (subclinical CVD) stages. Furthermore, exploratory threshold association, which is critical for defining actionable cutoffs in clinical practice, has not been explored, nor has the comparative association value of modified versus original CHG indices in CKM.

Therefore, this study utilizing a nationwide prospective cohort aimed to address these gaps by (1) evaluating the associations of CHG and modified CHG indices with CVD incidence across CKM stages 0–3; (2) identifying exploratory inflection points above which CVD risk accelerates; (3) comparing the association value of original and modified indices; and (4) validating findings via subgroup and sensitivity analysis to ensure robustness. Therefore, we seek to provide evidence for stage-specific CVD risk assessment in CKM syndrome, supporting early intervention and improved clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source of population

This was a nationwide prospective cohort study conducted as a secondary analysis using data from all five waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey in China covering 450 villages, 150 counties, and 28 provinces. CHARLS was carried out by the National School of Development at Peking University, with the baseline survey (Wave 1) conducted from 2011 to 2022 and four follow-up surveys completed to date (Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, Wave 4 in 2018, and Wave 5 in 2020). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment, conducted in accordance with the Enhanced Observational Epidemiological Research Reports (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

The study population was screened after applying rigorous exclusion criteria: (1) age < 45 years; (2) history of cardiovascular diseases at or before baseline (Wave 1); (3) missing or abnormal values of exposure indicators including CHG index and modified indices; (4) missing indicators necessary for CKM syndrome staging; (5) missing outcome reports after follow-up. Participants were followed up from Wave 2 to Wave 5 (median duration of 9 years), with outcomes assessed through regular questionnaires and clinical evaluations to track incident CVD events. CKM staging criteria

CKM stages were defined based on a combination of metabolic risk factors, kidney function, and cardiovascular status, aligned with criteria modified from recent guidelines from AHA [1, 2]. Stage 0 included individuals with no metabolic risk factors, defined as BMI < 23 kg/m², waist circumference < 80 cm (female)/<90 cm (male), fasting blood glucose < 100 mg/dL, HbA1c < 5.7%, normal blood pressure (SBP < 130 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg without medication), HDL-C ≥ 50 mg/dL (female)/≥40 mg/dL (male), TG < 150 mg/dL, eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m², and no subclinical or clinical CVD. Stage 1 was characterized by early metabolic disorder, including overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, or prediabetes, without hypertension, diabetes, CKD, or CVD. Stage 2 included participants with metabolic risk factors (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome) or moderate CKD (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²), without clinical CVD. Stage 3 was defined by high-risk CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²) or subclinical CVD (Framingham risk score ≥ 20%), without clinical CVD. The CKM staging criteria was listed (Supplementary Table S3-6).

Definition and calculation of exposure variables

The CHG index was calculated as a composite marker integrating lipid and glucose metabolism, using the formula: ln[total cholesterol (TC, mg/dL) × fasting blood glucose (FBG, mg/dL) / (2 × high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mg/dL))]. Modified indices were derived by multiplying CHG with anthropometric indicators: CHG-Wc (CHG × waist circumference, cm), CHG-WHtR (CHG × waist-to-height ratio, calculated as waist circumference (cm)/height (cm)), and CHG-BMI (CHG × body mass index, kg/m², calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)²). All blood indicators (TC, HDL-C, FBG) were measured using standard enzymatic methods after an overnight fast, while anthropometric measurements (waist circumference, height, weight) were obtained using calibrated instruments, with averages of three repeated measurements used for analysis.

Definition of CVD incidence for outcome variables

The primary outcome was CVD incidence, defined as the first occurrence of doctor-diagnosed heart disease or stroke during follow-up. CVD events were ascertained through self-reported questionnaires administered during follow-up visits. Outcome event occurrence was assessed by two standardized questions “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure or other heart problems?” and “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?”. Participants who had answered “Yes” to one of these two questions were considered to have CVD.

Missing data handling

For the handling of missing data, no multiple imputation was performed in the initial analysis. All participants with missing key data including exposures or outcomes were excluded, as detailed in the flow diagram (Fig. 1), to ensure data authenticity and avoid introducing uncertain imputation assumptions and potential artificial bias.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants were described according to CKM stages and CVD outcome status. Normality of all continuous variables was verified by Shapiro-Wilk test combined with histogram visualization before statistical comparison, and most indicators followed normal distribution or slight skewness. Given the large total sample size (n = 5404), the central limit theorem supported the application of parametric t-test and one-way ANOVA. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using One-way ANOVA or t-tests, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%), with differences assessed using Chi-square tests. The distribution of CHG and modified indices were shown through histograms and dot plots across CKM stages. We divided CHG and modified indices into four groups (Q1-Q4) according to their quartiles.

Given the fixed discrete‑wave follow‑up design without exact CVD onset dates, logistic regression was used as the primary model to evaluate associations between baseline indices and overall incident CVD during follow‑up. All participants included had complete long‑term follow‑up (median 9 years) to reduce bias from varying observation time. Cox regression was not performed due to unavailable precise event timing. Thus, the logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations of CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence, with four progressively adjusted models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, gender, and smoking status), Model 3 (further adjusted for waist circumference, height, BMI, SBP, and DBP), and Model 4 (additionally adjusted for eGFR, serum creatinine, and CRP).

The potential non-linear associations between CHG and modified indices and CVD incidence were analyzed through restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression. The RCS regression used 4 pre‑specified knots located at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the CHG and modified index distributions, consistent with standard spline modeling practice for continuous variables. The Likelihood ratio test in RCS regression was used to explore potential exploratory threshold association, with inflection points identified using Two-piecewise linear regression. Subgroup analysis were performed by age, gender, diabetes status, and CKM stage to assess consistency, with interaction terms used to test for effect modification. Sensitivity analysis excluding individual CKM stages were conducted to verify the robustness of associations. All statistical analysis were performed using R software (version 4.4.0), with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of CHG index and modified indices

The study cohort was derived from a nationwide screening of 17,708 participants, with 5,404 individuals ultimately included after excluding those with missing data, non-fasting status, baseline CVD, age < 45 years, and abnormal values. These participants were distributed across CKM stages 0 (n = 446), 1 (n = 1238), 2 (n = 1971), and 3 (n = 1749) (Fig. 1).

The baseline characteristics according to CVD incidence was shown (Table 1). Indicators in our study followed normal distribution or slight skewness. The overall cohort had a mean age of 58.4 ± 8.64 years, with 46.7% males. Significant differences across CKM stages (all P < 0.001) included older age in stage 3 (64.0 ± 7.97 years) vs. stage 1 (55.4 ± 7.38 years), a higher proportion of males in stage 3 (75.3%), and increasing physical measurements (BMI, waist circumference, WHtR). Metabolic and cardiovascular markers (FBG, TG, SBP, DBP) also rose with stage, with stage 3 showing the highest FBG (120 ± 45.7 mg/dL) and SBP (142 ± 21.3 mmHg). Figure 2 showed the distribution of CHG and modified indices (CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BMI), which increased consistently across CKM stages, with overall values of 5.32 ± 0.44, 450 ± 83.7, 2.86 ± 0.59, and 127 ± 74.3, respectively (Table 1 and Fig. 3). Associations between CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence among population with CKM stage 0–3

CHG index and modified indices (CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BMI) were divided in four groups based on their quartiles (Q1-Q4) and their distribution were shown (Table 2). Table 3 presents the associations of CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence across CKM populations in four logistic regression models, with Model 1 unadjusted and Model 2–4 adjusted with increasingly rigorous. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4, adjusting for age, gender, smoking, waist, height, BMI, SBP, DBP, eGFR, SCr, and CRP), all indices were significantly associated with increased CVD risk.

When setting CHG index and modified indices as continuous dependent variables, each 1-unit increase in CHG was associated with a 28% higher CVD risk (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.09–1.50, P = 0.002), i.e., each 1-SD increase was associated with a 14% higher risk of incident CVD (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.07–1.23, P = 0.001). Corresponding each 1-unit increase risk was observed for CHG-WHtR (54% higher risk, OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.16–2.04, P = 0.003), i.e., each 1-SD increase was 24% higher (OR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.07–1.44, P = 0.004). However, this risk was negligible for CHG-Wc (OR = 1.01), or CHG-BMI (OR = 1.01).

Then, CHG index and modified indices were converted into categorical variables by quartile. Quartile analysis showed the highest quartile (Q4) of each indice was linked to elevated risk, CHG-Wc (Q4: OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.21–2.25, P = 0.002), CHG-WHtR (Q4: OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.03–1.98, P = 0.03), and CHG-BMI (Q4: OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.22–2.31, P = 0.002) (Table 3). Thus, CHG-WHtR and CHG-BMI showing the strongest association in continuous variable and quartile analysis, respectively, while CHG and CHG-WHtR showed stable association with CVD incidence in both continuous and quartile analysis.

Additionally, we assessed the associations of CHG and its modified indices with heart disease and stroke separately. Continuous CHG, CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR and CHG-BMI maintained risk factors for heart disease in fully adjusted models. Regarding to stroke, the positive correlative trends still existed in most of the crude models, yet none of the indices reached statistical significance after full adjustment for potential confounders. Quartile analysis yielded similar results across the two subtypes of CVD (Supplementary Table S2).

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression and exploratory threshold association analysis between CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence

The restricted cubic spline plots illustrated the non-linear relationships between the CHG index and its modified indices with the incidence of CVD across CKM stages 0–3. The plots visualized the non-linear associations by plotting odds ratios against the continuous values of each index. Figure 4 showed a positive nonlinear relationship between the CHG index and CVD risk (P for overall = 0.002, P for nonlinear = 0.047). Then, the two-piecewise linear regression was used to identified significant inflection points for CHG and modified indices, supported by RCS exploratory threshold association analysis (Fig. 5). For CHG, the inflection point was 5.15; below this value, no significant association with CVD risk was observed, but above 5.15, each 1-unit increase was associated with a 48% higher risk (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.19–1.84, P < 0.001), i.e., each 1-SD increase was 19% higher (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.08–1.31, P < 0.001) (Table 4). Regarding to the bootstrap validation for threshold stability, we performed bootstrap resampling (n = 1000 iterations) to validate the stability of the inflection points for CHG and CHG‑WHtR. Bootstrap‑derived 95% CIs for the thresholds were obtained. The original inflection points (CHG = 5.15; CHG‑WHtR = 2.41) fell within the bootstrap 95% CI (CHG: 4.708–6.066, CHG‑WHtR: 2.270–3.728), confirming acceptable stability in this cohort (Table 4).

Subgroup analysis of CHG index and modified indices in predicting CVD risk in CKM populations with stage-specific analysis

Subgroup analysis was performed to explore the relationship between CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence across different subpopulations including ages, genders, smoke, comorbidities and CKM stages. It showed consistent associations across most subgroups, with limited significant interactions. For diabetes status, CHG was significantly associated with CVD risk in non-diabetic participants (OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.69–2.30, P < 0.001) but not in diabetic participants (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.78–1.64, P = 0.526, interaction P = 0.008). For age, CHG-WHtR had a stronger association in participants aged 45–60 years (OR = 3.22, 95% CI: 2.68–3.86, P < 0.001) than in those over 60 years (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.95–2.77, P < 0.001, interaction P = 0.012). Across CKM stages, CHG showed the strongest association in stage 1 (OR = 3.70, 95% CI: 1.63–8.43, P = 0.002), followed by stage 3 (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.11–1.63, P = 0.002). CHG-WHtR was significantly associated with CVD in stage 1 (OR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.10–4.19, P = 0.025), 2 (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.57–2.39, P < 0.001), and 3 (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.62–2.30, P < 0.001), but not in stage 0 (Tables 5 and 6; Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figure S1-3).

For the further analysis of CKM stage-specific effects, separated RCS plots were employed to illustrate the relationship between CHG index and modified indices with CVD incidence across different CKM stages. In CKM stage 0, none of the indices showed significant association with CVD risk (all P for overall > 0.05), reflecting the absence of metabolic risk factors in this group. For stage 1, the CHG index and modified indices exhibited significant positive associations, CHG and CHG-WHtR show linear relationships (P for non-linearity > 0.05), while CHG-BMI displays a non-linear trend with an exploratory inflection point around 194.06, above which CVD risk rises sharply (P for non-linearity = 0.042). In stage 2 and stage 3, most modified indices (CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BMI) maintain strong linear associations with CVD incidence (P for overall < 0.001, P for non-linearity > 0.05). These stage-specific patterns emphasized that CHG and modified indices, particularly CHG-WHtR, retain association value across progressive CKM stages, aligning with CKM pathophysiological progression (Fig. 7).

Sensitivity analysis excluding individual CKM stages to further clarified the robustness and stage specificity of associations between CHG index and modified indices in predicting CVD risk. Excluding stage 2 strengthened the association (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.50–2.26, P < 0.001). These results indicated that while CKM stage influenced the strength of associations, CHG and CHG-WHtR retained meaningful association value in specific subpopulations, balancing stage specificity with baseline robustness (Table 7).

Regarding to the construct overlap between Framingham risk score (FRS) and the variables related to CHG indices, we performed further sensitivity analysis by restricting the study population to CKM stages 0–2. The corresponding results showed that CHG and its indices presented similar trends both in the continuous data and quartile analysis, although the associations attenuated in the full adjusted models. In unadjusted and partially adjusted models, these indices were also positively linked to CVD risk (Supplementary Table S7).

Besides, regarding to the potential reverse causation, we excluded participants with incident CVD occurring within the early years of follow-up and taking Wave 3 as the cutoff. After excluding these early-onset CVD cases (n = 154) and remaining 5250 cases, we refitted all fully adjusted logistic regression models for CHG and its indices. The statistical significance of associations between the four indices and CVD remained consistent with our primary main results. These consistent outcomes effectively ruled out prominent reverse causation, and further confirmed the robustness of our core findings (Supplementary Table S8).

Then, collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). VIF > 3 was considered indicative of meaningful collinearity. For modified CHG indices that include BMI, waist circumference, or waist‑to‑height ratio, initially, we did not adjust for the corresponding anthropometric measure to avoid over‑adjustment and collinearity. After removing the corresponding anthropometric measures in the covariates, all VIF values in the modified models were below 3.0, confirming no critical collinearity (Supplementary Table S9).

Finally, we performed parallel analysis with CKM stages defined by the AHA-recommended PREVENT risk score (Supplementary Tables S10-S12). Baseline profiles across PREVENT CKM were summarized in Supplementary Table S10, with Stage 0 (n = 446), Stage 1 (n = 1683), Stage 2 (n = 1971), and Stage 3 (n = 1304), and a shift of 445 mild-metabolic participants from Framingham Stage 3 to PREVENT Stage 1. Supplementary Table S11 showed fewer high-CHG individuals, which were categorized in PREVENT Stage 3 relative to Framingham stratification. Supplementary Table S12 verified that CHG and its modified indices remained robust CVD predictors across all PREVENT CKM subgroups, with the largest effect size in PREVENT Stage 1 subjects.

Discussion

This nationwide prospective cohort study systematically evaluated the association of the CHG index and its modified indices (CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BMI) with CVD) incidence across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. The findings reveal that these indices are valuable predictors of CVD risk in CKM population, with modified indices showing enhanced performance. Their associations are characterized by stage specificity, exploratory threshold association, and baseline robustness, which align with the core principles of CKM syndrome management as proposed by the American Heart Association [1, 2].

Regarding to the underlying relationship between CHG and its indices with the core mechanisms of CKM syndrome, the AHA defines CKM syndrome as a systemic disorder driven by interconnected pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors, CKD, and cardiovascular dysfunction. This systemic characteristic underscores the need for biomarkers that capture multi-organ crosstalk by the CHG index and its modified indices. The original CHG index integrates TC, HDL, and FBG, reflecting three key pillars of metabolic health including atherogenic lipid profiles (TC/HDL imbalance), glucose homeostasis, and their synergistic effects on vascular damage [3, 4]. TC promotes atherosclerotic plaque formation, while low HDL impairs reverse cholesterol transport, both of which accelerate cardiovascular and renal microvascular injury [10, 11]. FBG, a marker of insulin sensitivity, links to renal hyperfiltration and endothelial dysfunction, creating a feedforward loop between metabolic derangement and kidney damage [12, 13]. Together, these components make CHG a unique indicator of the “metabolic-renal-cardiac” axis central to CKM progression.

Modified CHG indices (CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BMI) further enhance this utility by incorporating adiposity metrics. The AHA emphasizes that excess or dysfunctional adipose tissue-particularly visceral fat-is a primary driver of CKM syndrome, as it triggers pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative pathways that damage arteries, heart, and kidneys [1, 2, 5]. Our findings showed that modified indices like CHG-WHtR and CHG-Wc showed better risk prediction ability than the original CHG index, aligning with waist circumference (Wc) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) in capturing visceral adiposity. The integration between CHD and its modified indices reflected the synergistic interplay between metabolic dysfunction and adipose tissue pathology [7, 14].

Regarding to the stage-specific associations and early intervention based on the AHA guidance, the AHA stresses that CKM syndrome is progressive, with early stages (0–2) representing critical windows for intervention to prevent CVD and kidney failure. Our results made a response, CHG and its modified indices showed the strongest associations in CKM stage 1, a phase marked by early metabolic disorder (overweight, prediabetes) without overt organ damage [1, 2, 15]. This result suggests CHG indices could serve as “early warning signals” to identify individuals at risk of progressing to more severe CKM stages [16].

In stage 2 for metabolic risk factors or moderate CKD and stage 3 for subclinical CVD or high-risk CKD, modified indices especially CHG-WHtR remained robust predictors. This aligns with the AHA’s observation that metabolic disorder in later stages drive irreversible cardiovascular and renal damage. Notably, CHG indices were not associated with CVD in stage 0, where individuals lack metabolic risk factors-reinforcing that these indices reflect pathological processes rather than normal physiology, and supporting their role in targeting at-risk populations [17, 18].

Additionally, our data provided helpful implications for clinical decision-making based on exploratory threshold association analysis of CHG and its modified indices. Restricted cubic spline analysis identified critical inflection points, such as CHG ≥ 5.15 and CHG-WHtR ≥ 2.41, above which CVD risk accelerated. These exploratory inflection points provide candidate thresholds requiring validation, aligning with the AHA’s call for personalized risk stratification in CKM management. For example, stage 2 identifying individuals with CHG-WHtR > 2.41 could trigger intensified interventions like lipid-lowering therapy and weight management to mitigate risk before overt CVD develops [19]. The nonlinear relationships observed also highlight the limitations of linear risk models in capturing CKM complexity. Below these exploratory inflection points, CHG indices showed no meaningful association with CVD, suggesting that interventions below these values may have limited benefit-allowing clinicians to prioritize resources for high-risk individuals [20]. Nevertheless, these inflection points were exploratory, supported by bootstrap stability without external validation. It should not be directly adopted as clinical cutoffs until validated in independent populations.

Recent clinical studies have established that structured biomarker panels and composite scores improve acute and chronic cardiovascular risk triage, rule-out, and prognostic stratification in routine care pathways [21–24]. These reports confirm that multi-marker strategies and integrated scores exceeded single biomarkers for identifying high-risk individuals, optimizing emergency department throughput, and supporting safe early discharge in suspected acute coronary syndromes. Notably, composite indices combining cardiac, metabolic, and renal markers enhance risk discrimination beyond troponin or traditional risk scores alone. In our study, the CHG index and its modified versions (CHG-BMI, CHG-Wc, CHG-WHtR) complemented these established strategies by capturing chronic metabolic-renal-cardiovascular interplay characteristic of CKM syndrome, rather than acute myocardial injury. Whereas current algorithms focus on acute rule-out and short-term prognosis, our indices provide stage-specific associations and exploratory thresholds for long-term CVD risk stratification across CKM stages 0–3. Thus, CHG indices in our study represent an adjunctive metabolic tool for early, preclinical risk stratification, rather than a replacement for validated acute diagnostic or prognostic scores. Moreover, external validation is needed to define their incremental value in clinical practice.

Regarding to the Framingham score, since FRS comprises age, lipids, blood pressure, smoking, and diabetes, which are the factors related to CHG and its modified indices, some construct overlap exists. However, sensitivity analysis excluding the FRS component or limited to Stages 0–2 confirmed robust associations, indicating the main findings are not caused by circularity. Moreover, the AHA-endorsed PREVENT score, newly proposed in 2023 for CKM-related CVD risk prediction, was also analyzed in this study. Parallel analysis using PREVENT score-based CKM stratification further explained the discrepancy between Framingham and PREVENT risk categorization. The two scoring tools yielded consistent grouping for Stage 0 individuals and Stage 2, whereas disagreement was limited to borderline participants like mild adiposity and impaired fasting glucose. The Framingham equation, built on European white cohorts without East Asian calibration, may overestimate 10-year atherosclerotic CVD risk compared with PREVENT risk estimation. Even after this reallocation, CHG and its modified indices remained powerful CVD predictors within PREVENT CKM stages. Overall, CHG-derived composite indices exhibited stable prognostic value across both Framingham and PREVENT CKM staging frameworks, supporting their generalizability for risk stratification irrespective of the underlying cardiovascular risk algorithm.

Limitations and prospective

Our study’s strengths include its large nationwide cohort, long-term follow-up, and detailed CKM staging, which allowed for stage-specific analysis critical to CKM research [25]. By demonstrating that modified CHG indices better than the original index, we provide evidence for integrating metabolic and adiposity metrics-consistent with the AHA’s emphasis on holistic CKM assessment.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The observational nature of the study precludes causal inferences. Although extensive adjustment for confounders was performed, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out [26]. The reliance on self-reported data for some variables, such as lifestyle factors, may introduce measurement error [27]. Moreover, the study population was from a specific geographical region, and the findings may not be directly applicable to other populations with different genetic, environmental, and lifestyle characteristics [28]. In addition, although we supplemented multiple imputation and early CVD exclusion sensitivity analysis to verify result stability, all sensitivity tests were still performed based on the CHARLS single-source database without external cohort validation. Furthermore, we lacked detailed medication records related to hypoglycemic, lipid-lowering and antihypertensive treatments at baseline and during follow-up. These drugs may modify circulating TC, HDL-C and FBG levels and further change the calculated CHG index values, which could bring potential confounding to risk association analysis. Besides, the cut-off thresholds of CHG and modified indices obtained from RCS analysis are exploratory values derived from statistical fitting instead of clinical diagnostic criteria, and these inflection points need further prospective verification in independent cohorts before clinical application.

Future studies will be conducted in these directions, (1) focus on validating these findings in different ethnic and geographical populations to enhance generalizability [29], (2) validate these findings using objective CVD endpoints such as imaging or hospital records from external dataset, (3) explore how CHG indices change with CKM progression to inform longitudinal monitoring [30], (4) compare CHG and its modified indices with other metabolic markers like the TyG index to determine their complementary value in CKM risk prediction, (5) test whether targeting CHG inflection points reduces CVD incidence in interventional trials [31]. Finally, the development of risk prediction models incorporating these indices and other emerging biomarkers may further improve the accuracy of CVD risk assessment in CKM syndrome [32].

Conclusion

CHG index and its modified indices particularly CHG-WHtR effectively associate with reported incident CVD across CKM stages 0–3, reflecting the systemic interplay of metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular dysfunction central to CKM syndrome. Their stage-specific associations and exploratory threshold association support their use in early risk stratification, aligning with the American Heart Association’s goal of preventing CKM progression through early intervention. By integrating lipid, glucose, and adiposity metrics, these indices offer a practical tool to guide personalized management in the growing population affected by CKM syndrome.
