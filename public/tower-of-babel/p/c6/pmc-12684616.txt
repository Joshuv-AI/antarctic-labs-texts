Competing Risks of Cardiac and Noncardiac Mortality in Patients With Secondary Mitral Regurgitation Undergoing Transcatheter Edge‐to‐Edge Repair
Authors: Luca Esposito, Marco Di Maio, Cesare Baldi, Emilio Di Lorenzo, Michele Bellino, Angelo Silverio, Marianna Adamo, Arturo Giordano, Francesco De Felice, Carmelo Grasso, Antonio Popolo Rubbio, Paolo Denti, Cosmo Godino, Federico De Marco, Fausto Castriota, Ida Monteforte, Annalisa Mongiardo, Anna Sonia Petronio, Gabriele Crimi, Diego Maffeo, Antonio L Bartorelli, Rodolfo Citro, Gennaro Galasso, Giuseppe Tarantini, Giovanni Esposito, Corrado Tamburino, Francesco Bedogni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relative impact of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in patients with secondary mitral regurgitation undergoing mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair (M‐TEER) has been poorly investigated. We aimed to assess the competing risks and independent predictors of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in a real‐world secondary mitral regurgitation population treated with M‐TEER and included in the GIOTTO (Italian Society of Interventional Cardiology [GIse] Registry Of Transcatheter Treatment of Mitral Valve Regurgitation) registry.MethodsCompeting risks analysis was used to assess the cumulative incidence of cardiac and noncardiac mortality. Cox regression identified independent predictors of each outcome. Co‐primary outcomes were cardiac and noncardiac death at 2 years.ResultsThe analysis included 1185 consecutive patients with secondary mitral regurgitation treated with M‐TEER between January 2016 and March 2020 (median age 74 years). Two‐year cumulative incidences of cardiac and noncardiac mortality were 19% and 12%, respectively. At multivariable analysis, predictors of cardiac mortality were age (hazard ratio [HR], 1.03; P=0.002), New York Heart Association class (HR, 1.44; P=0.018), previous hospitalization for heart failure (HR, 1.67; P=0.016), hemoglobin (HR, 0.89; P=0.016), left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (HR, 1.02; P=0.025), left ventricular ejection fraction (HR, 0.98; P=0.022), and daily furosemide dose (HR, 1.19; P=0.003). Predictors of noncardiac mortality were New York Heart Association class (HR, 1.70; P=0.03), estimated glomerular filtration rate (HR, 0.98; P=0.002), and smoking habit (HR, 1.82; P=0.009).ConclusionsPatients with secondary mitral regurgitation treated with M‐TEER show a high 2‐year incidence of both cardiac and noncardiac mortality. Understanding competing risks of mortality may improve patient selection for M‐TEER.Keywords: cardiac mortality, competing risks, noncardiac mortality, secondary mitral regurgitation, transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repairSubject Categories: Valvular Heart Disease

Secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) is a strong predictor of poor clinical outcome in patients with heart failure (HF). 1 Over the past years, mitral valve transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair (M‐TEER) with the MitraClip system (Abbott) was proven to be a safe and effective therapeutic strategy in patients with severe symptomatic SMR on top of guideline‐directed medical therapy. 2 , 3 In the COAPT trial, M‐TEER significantly reduced the incidence of rehospitalization for HF and improved survival in a selected population of patients with SMR compared with guideline‐directed medical therapy alone, with a prognostic benefit that was maintained at long‐term follow‐up. Despite these favorable results, a high incidence of adverse events and mortality persisted in the device group through 5‐year follow up. 2 , 4 Patients with SMR undergoing M‐TEER represent a high‐risk clinical subset with multiple cardiac and noncardiac comorbidities, which have a significant impact on long‐term clinical outcome. 5 , 6 The presence of multiple comorbidities emphasizes the importance of exploring both cardiac and noncardiac death as distinct yet interrelated outcomes. Indeed, given the clinical complexity of these patients, their long‐term prognosis after the procedure may be influenced by multiple competing risks of mortality beyond the successful correction of SMR. Further definition of the relative contribution of cardiac and noncardiac mortality on the long‐term prognosis might be informative in clinical decision‐making, improving the selection process of patients with SMR scheduled for M‐TEER. However, the comprehensive understanding of the competing risks of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in this patient population remains inconclusive.

The multicenter GIOTTO (Italian Society of Interventional Cardiology [GIse] Registry Of Transcatheter Treatment of Mitral Valve Regurgitation) is a prospective, nationwide registry that enrolled consecutive patients with severe symptomatic MR treated with M‐TEER at 19 high‐volume Italian Centers. Therefore, it provides the opportunity to assess the relative contribution of cardiac and noncardiac death in a large real‐world population of patients with SMR. The aim of this analysis was to evaluate the competing risks and the independent predictors of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in a real‐world population of patients with SMR treated with M‐TEER.

METHODS

Study Population and Data Collection

The GIOTTO is a multicenter, single‐arm, prospective registry of patients with symptomatic MR undergoing M‐TEER with the MitraClip system in Italy. Because of the observational nature of the registry, patient selection for M‐TEER and details of the procedure were left to the discretion of individual operators. The inclusion and exclusion criteria of the GIOTTO registry have been previously described. 7 To participate in the registry, each center was required to have performed at least 15 M‐TEER procedures per year.

The present analysis included consecutive patients with symptomatic moderate‐to‐severe or severe SMR undergoing M‐TEER from January 2016 and March 2020 at 19 high‐volume Italian centers and enrolled in the GIOTTO registry.

Data were entered on an electronic online case report form, which was periodically checked to verify the quality of data. Baseline demographic, clinical, echocardiographic, periprocedural, and follow‐up data were used for the purpose of this study.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients enrolled in the registry, after receiving an oral and written explanation of the issues concerning the procedure and the study, and signed a written informed consent. In order to minimize the possibility of unintentionally sharing information that can be used to re‐identify private information, a subset of the data generated for this study is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Follow‐Up and Study Outcome Measures

Follow‐up was scheduled at 30 days, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Data were obtained by medical records or by telephone interviews of the patient, the treating physicians, or the next of kin. Death adjudication processes were conducted by experienced cardiologists at each center, who prospectively adjudicated the causes of death based on the totality of data collected.

Cardiac death was defined as any death related to HF, myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, conduction system disturbances, cardiac infections, tamponade, sudden death, device failure, or death of unknown cause (adjudicated as cardiac). The other causes of death were reported as noncardiac. Sudden death was defined as an unexpected fatal event occurring within 1 hour of symptom onset or, in unwitnessed cases, within 24 hours of the individual being last seen alive, with a presumed cardiac cause based on available clinical data. Death of unknown cause referred to cases where no specific information regarding the circumstances or cause of death was available; in absence of specific noncardiac causes, these deaths were adjudicated as cardiac‐related.

The co‐primary outcomes of the study were cardiac and noncardiac death at 2‐year follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

The normal distribution of continuous parameters was visually assessed through histograms. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean±SD and compared using the Student t test. Variables with a skewed distribution were reported as median and interquartile range in square brackets (25th–75th) and were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 test, or Fisher exact test, when appropriate.

For the competing risk analysis, noncardiac death was considered as a competing risk to cardiac death, meaning that the occurrence of noncardiac death modifies the probability of the occurrence of cardiac death and vice versa. The Fine‐Gray method was used to estimate the cumulative incidences of the study outcomes and determine the subdistribution hazard, which accounts for the competing risk of noncardiac death, rather than censoring patients who experience this type of event. 8 Univariable and stepwise multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to investigate significant associations of baseline patients' characteristics with the composite outcome. Baseline variables with P value <0.15 at univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model.

Variables were excluded from the regression model if they showed significant collinearity, defined as a variance inflation factor >5. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HR) with their 95% CI and P values.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputations with the method of chained equations. Five imputed data sets were generated and combined using Rubin's rules.

For all tests, a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed by using R version 3.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of the Study Population

Between January 2016 and March 2020, 1659 patients with symptomatic MR undergoing M‐TEER with the MitraClip system were enrolled in the GIOTTO registry. Of them, 1185 patients had SMR and were included in the present analysis. The baseline characteristics of the study population are reported in Table 1. Median age was 74.0 years, and 837 (70.6%) patients were male. Median Euroscore II and STS score were 5.7% and 3.3%, respectively. An ischemic cause of SMR was reported in 581 (49.0%) patients. DISCUSSION

Patients with SMR undergoing M‐TEER represent a vulnerable subset of patients with HF. The detrimental effect of SMR, combined with the biological frailty and comorbid status of these patients, lead to a high risk of mortality at follow‐up. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that investigates the competing risks of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in a large real‐world population of patients with SMR treated with M‐TEER.

The main findings of the study can be summarized as follows:

At 2‐year follow‐up, the study population showed a high cumulative incidence of both cardiac and noncardiac mortality (19% and 12%, respectively).

At 2‐year follow‐up, the study population showed a high cumulative incidence of both cardiac and noncardiac mortality (19% and 12%, respectively).

At multivariable Cox regression analysis, age, NYHA class, previous hospitalization for HF, hemoglobin, left ventricle end‐diastolic diameter, left ventricle ejection fraction, and daily dose of furosemide were independently associated with the risk of cardiac mortality, while NYHA class, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and smoking were independent predictors of noncardiac mortality.

At multivariable Cox regression analysis, age, NYHA class, previous hospitalization for HF, hemoglobin, left ventricle end‐diastolic diameter, left ventricle ejection fraction, and daily dose of furosemide were independently associated with the risk of cardiac mortality, while NYHA class, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and smoking were independent predictors of noncardiac mortality.

Because many comorbidities coexist in patients with HF, the risk estimation of cardiac and noncardiac death is a challenging process. 9 , 10 Considering the growing global prevalence of HF and the recent introduction of novel HF therapies that reduce cardiac mortality, the epidemiological weight of noncardiac mortality in HF is an emerging issue in cardiovascular medicine. 11 In our population, competing risks analysis showed a high incidence of both cardiac and noncardiac mortality at 2 years. Although most deaths were still related to cardiac causes, the relative contribution of noncardiac death to the overall mortality seems to be substantial. The high mortality rates reported in our population are consistent with data from previous studies. In the COAPT trial, a high 2‐year incidence of all‐cause mortality was reported in both study groups (29.1% and 46.1% in the device group and in the control group, respectively). 2 Although no data on cause‐specific mortality were provided, several subanalyses of the COAPT trial detected a significant impact of noncardiac conditions on long‐term clinical outcome. 12 , 13 , 14 A recent analysis from the MitraSwiss registry documented a lower‐than‐expected survival rate in patients with SMR after M‐TEER, independently from procedural success. 15 Thus, exploring the different behavior of the type of death in patients with SMR treated with M‐TEER might be crucial to improve risk stratification. The complexity of patients with SMR extends beyond the cardiovascular system, since noncardiac mortality acts as a significant contributor to the overall clinical risk profile. Keeping this in mind, we performed regression analyses to identify the independent predictors of cardiac and noncardiac death. Interestingly, NYHA class was independently associated with the risk of both cardiac and noncardiac mortality. Despite the limitations related to interobserver variability, NYHA class is widely used in clinical practice for assessing the severity of HF and for guiding the indications for HF therapies. However, its prognostic role in patients with SMR has been poorly investigated. 16 Higher NYHA classes are generally indicative of more advanced HF and may correlate with an increased burden of severe cardiac and noncardiac comorbidities. In a subanalysis from the COAPT trial, M‐TEER was shown to reduce clinical events consistently across all NYHA classes compared with guideline‐directed medical therapy alone, with absolute greater improvements in patients with higher NYHA class at baseline. 17 According to these data, advanced HF should not discourage per se the indication for M‐TEER. On the other hand, patients with higher NYHA classes have a greater overall clinical vulnerability that may increase the risk of malignancy, respiratory infections, and sepsis, which are reported to be the most common causes of noncardiac death in patients with HF and reduced left ventricle ejection fraction. 18 , 19 , 20 A comprehensive approach is thus needed to weigh the potential benefits of the intervention, avoiding procedures that may be futile or even potentially dangerous.

Renal function is a recognized predictor of all‐cause mortality in patients undergoing M‐TEER. 21 , 22 In our analysis, estimated glomerular filtration rate was only associated with the risk of noncardiac mortality, when considering cardiac death as a competing risk. This is consistent with previous data reporting a substantial incidence of noncardiovascular mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease, mainly related to infections and malignancies. 23

Although smoking is a major risk factor for the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases, in our analysis it emerged as an independent predictor of noncardiac death. Given the numerous toxic effects of smoking on overall health status, its impact on noncardiac mortality in this population is likely to be multifactorial. 24

In this analysis, age was an independent predictor of cardiac mortality but was not associated with the risk of noncardiac mortality. Probably, the relatively short follow‐up might explain the absence of association between age and noncardiac death. Furthermore, pathological aging rather than chronological age is usually associated with the occurrence of cardiovascular events and other noncardiovascular geriatric conditions in patients with HF. 25 The distinction between biological and chronological age might be particularly intricate in the complex framework of patients with SMR, justifying the lack of association with noncardiac mortality.

These findings have several implications. Adjudicating the type of death in patients with SMR holds significant utility, particularly in the context of refining patient selection for interventions and optimizing outcomes. The improvement of the risk stratification process might also have favorable consequences on health care policies, allowing optimization of resource allocation. Indeed, identifying specific subgroups within the SMR population who may derive the greatest benefit from M‐TEER is crucial for improving the procedure cost‐effectiveness. From a clinical research standpoint, the knowledge of the competing risks of cardiac and noncardiac death plays a crucial role, especially for sample size calculation. In clinical trials including cardiac death as an end point or as a component of the composite end point, noncardiac death is a competing risk, therefore leading to the need for a larger sample size or a longer follow‐up or both. 26 Furthermore, cardiac death is not a patient‐centered end point and might provide only partial information on the multifactorial mortality trajectory of complex patients such as those with SMR.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to its observational nature, we cannot exclude the presence of residual selection bias. Second, follow‐up data and causes of death were not adjudicated by a clinical events committee and we cannot exclude the possibility of a misclassification of the cause of death in certain cases. However, the death adjudication process was meticulously conducted by experienced physicians in a prospective fashion. Third, deaths were only adjudicated as cardiac and noncardiac in the GIOTTO registry, whereas the specific causes of death were not systematically reported. Therefore, this analysis cannot provide information on the specific causes of noncardiac death (ie, cancer, infections, respiratory disease, etc.) and on the relative contribution of these conditions to the overall mortality rates. Given this, these findings should be considered hypothesis‐generating and provide a foundation for future large‐scale prospective studies with systematic collection and reporting of detailed noncardiac mortality data in patients with SMR treated with M‐TEER. Fourth, guideline‐directed medical therapy was not achieved in all patients, especially because HF pharmacological therapies substantially changed during the enrollment period of the GIOTTO registry (ie, progressively increasing use of angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors and introduction of sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors). Therefore, we cannot exclude that these improvements in HF therapies may have influenced the relative contribution of cardiac and noncardiac death in patients with SMR. Fifth, M‐TEER was performed with the first 3 generations of the MitraClip system in the GIOTTO registry, and our findings may not be fully applicable to patients treated with the current fourth‐generation device, which was introduced after the completion of the enrollment in the registry. 27 However, despite the marked technological improvements of the fourth‐generation device, our analysis was mainly focused on long‐term clinical outcomes rather than on technical success, and we do not think that the device improvements might strongly influence the competing risks of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in this patient population.

In this analysis from the GIOTTO registry, we investigated the competing risks of cardiac and noncardiac mortality in a large, multicenter, real‐world population of patients with SMR undergoing M‐TEER with the MitraClip system. Although cardiac conditions still represent the leading causes of death in these patients, the cumulative incidence of noncardiac mortality is reported to be substantial. These findings highlight the complex interplay between cardiac and noncardiac conditions influencing the long‐term prognosis of patients with SMR treated with M‐TEER. Therefore, a comprehensive approach that considers both cardiac and noncardiac diseases is essential for achieving meaningful improvements in risk stratification and clinical outcomes.
