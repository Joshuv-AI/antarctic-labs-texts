Co-creating health system innovation with people who use drugs
Authors: Renée McBeth, Colton Sandberg, Veronica Varewny, Bethany Piggott, Asha Ajani, Sarah Auger, Denise Campbell-Scherer, Kathryn Dong, Elaine Hyshka, Cindy Srinivasan, Shanell Twan, Les Umpherville, Ginetta Salvalaggio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA polycrisis of rising drug toxicity, pervasive houselessness, pandemic-related disruptions, coloniality and climate disasters is creating and exacerbating health inequities for People Who Use/Have Used Drugs (PWUD). This confluence of intersecting health, socio-political and environmental issues highlights the need for community-driven and adaptive innovation to address inequities in complex systems of care. To inform service innovations in an inner city social service hub in Edmonton, Alberta, we co-created a process that centres PWUD in health service planning and prioritization.MethodsUsing a community-based participatory research methodology informed by complexity theory, we conducted research with PWUD using SenseMaker micro-narratives and optional arts-based asset-mapping. Academic and peer researchers co-developed the study with input from the PWUD community and collected data at social service hubs and on outreach in the community. An iterative four-phase approach to research design, data collection and analysis guided the study: (i) Pre-data collection, (ii) Formal data collection, (iii) Readjusting, and (iv) Accountability.ResultsThis methodology paper describes how our four-phase framework guided the study and promoted a dynamic and accountable approach to centering PWUD in health system innovation. Over five months, 215 PWUD participants shared narratives and rich insights into their experiences with healthcare access, harm reduction, and community support. Our results emphasise the importance of taking time to orient to each other and the community, even as a diverse team with many preexisting relationships. An iterative data analysis process allowed for adjustments in real-time to guide research focus, ensuring equity-oriented engagement with structurally vulnerable groups. Accountability began with research design, was maintained throughout data collection by creating safety for participants, and then defined the final phase of the research where we created an accessible final report and are now working with the host nonprofit partner and community members on action-oriented responses to the narratives shared.ConclusionsMeaningful engagement with PWUD in co-creating health system innovation requires relational and adaptive methodologies. The process-focused results of this study demonstrate how community-based participatory research informed by complexity theory can enable accountable healthcare innovation amidst a changing social and political landscape. We conclude with a set of recommendations for co-creation and other peer-centred approaches that prioritize PWUD voices in developing effective health services.Keywords: Community-Based participatory research, People who use drugs, Harm reduction, Sensemaking, Co-Creation, Health system innovation, Polycrisis, Complexity theory, Peer research, Structural vulnerability

Background

Despite efforts to reduce harm through mutual aid and frontline services, People Who Use/Have Used Drugs (PWUD) are facing a polycrisis of intersecting health threats, including a spike in alcohol- and drug-related deaths, health disparities, income inequality and unsheltered homelessness [1–3]. Settler colonialism structures the present crisis for Indigenous PWUD in particular as intergenerational trauma, racism, dislocation and dispossession play out in distinct and disproportionate experiences of the toxic drug crisis [4]. Across Canada, Indigenous people are overincarerated and overpoliced, and recent data indicate that in Alberta, First Nations people are eight times more likely to experience a fatal poisoning than other PWUD [5, 6]. Compounding climate-related emergencies including severe floods and wildfires have and will continue to create public health threats, disconnect PWUD from social supports and healthcare providers, disrupt access to and supply of substances, and impact mental health and substance use [7].

Edmonton, AB, a city of 1.5 million people on the Western Canadian prairies, referred to in the Cree language as (Amiskwaciwâskahikan). As documented by members of this study team in a recent survey of 499 PWUD, since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, PWUD in central Edmonton have experienced a rise in poisoning deaths, increased houselessness, and more difficulty accessing services [8]. The present drug poisoning emergency is primarily attributable to an increasingly toxic unregulated drug supply, although the COVID-19 pandemic and pre-existing structural vulnerabilities have also contributed to the changing risk environment [8]. Encampment clearing increased by 40% year over year in 2024 in Edmonton [9]. Interactions with police, bylaw officers and city crews clearing the encampments were a focus of participant narratives and witnessed by research team members during data collection. Of the inner city PWUD community that participated in the survey, 69% self-identified as Indigenous (First Nations, Métis and/or Inuit) while Indigenous people represent just over 8% of the general population [8, 10]. During this research, we witnessed the local impact of climate emergencies on PWUD, especially those who are precariously housed. Extreme cold is resulting in increasing rates of frostbite and amputations among unhoused PWUD in Edmonton [11]. Some participants had been displaced by wildfires, and our protocol had to adapt to extreme heat and smoke [12–14].

Complex crises require adapting quickly to changing circumstances. However, as highlighted by complexity theory–used to understand how to implement effective change in systems where multiple actors and processes dynamically impact each other and adapt the systems in which they interact [15]–the impact of interventions in a complex system is not easily predictable and must be designed and implemented dynamically and then evaluated to ensure benefits are reaching the intended population or community [16, 17]. From this lens, health system innovation cannot be limited to conventional healthcare spaces; it must involve community sites, services and other spaces that contribute broadly to the determinants of health and wellbeing [18].

PWUD have a long history of building mutual aid and partnerships that attend to their health and well-being [19]. When the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated health inequities faced by urban underserved populations, PWUD and their health system partners responded with innovative systems of community care [3, 20]. Formal and informal supports were disrupted, health policy and programs changed rapidly, and policy decision-makers often neglected community consultation and collaboration [21]. Many conventional health services were not prepared to respond to multiple simultaneous crises and were ill-equipped to fulfill their obligation to provide high quality and equitable access to necessary health care [22]. Some healthcare supports and short-term income assistance were introduced and then retracted without sufficient planning for transitions in care, compounding obstacles to systems navigation and access to care [21]. Promising examples of multisector and multilevel collaboration did occur, however, and were key to facilitating supports for structurally vulnerable PWUD [23]. These collaborations often centered front-line organizations, for example, in shelter and isolation facilities that co-located health and social services, and peer-led supports such as outreach to homeless encampments [21]. Throughout the pandemic, community-based and peer-led organizations continued to participate in action-oriented research—co-designing and testing health system interventions despite digital divides, limited resources and threats to health and wellbeing [1].

As highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic, community-based organizations often serve as lower-barrier health service providers, working in collaboration with local health systems. Existing research underscores the benefits of involving individuals with frontline and lived experience in developing innovative and collaborative services to address harms related to a toxic and unregulated drug supply [24]. Community-based participatory research (CBPR) has an essential role in highlighting front-line and PWUD-voiced concerns and solutions; we applied key principles of CBPR in a practical, co-designed process with academic and peer researchers to elucidate the health priorities of PWUD. Clinician-researchers, trainees, and harm reduction workers (n = 18) conducted narrative and arts-based research with PWUD who access inner city services (n = 215) over a 5-month period from July to November 2024 within facilities operated by a host nonprofit organization and its surrounding outreach areas in central Edmonton, Canada. This methodology paper describes how the research was conducted, reflects on the process, and provides guidance for community-based participatory research with PWUD. While the actual results of the study are not the focus of the paper, we provide examples from the research to illustrate the data collection and analysis processes.

The deeply localized relationships and rich context-based analysis that ground CBPR can extend timelines and limit the scale of research [25]. This can be challenging to balance with the need for timely, evidence-based responses to the evolving context. While we retained the careful collaboration essential to CBPR, we also advanced existing approaches to CBPR by incorporating complexity-oriented goals for quickly generating a large amount of rich and contextualized data.

Objectives

Enacting recognized principles of effective and ethical participatory research is both challenging and crucial given the present polycrisis exacerbating pressing health threats and underlying structural inequities and coloniality faced by PWUD. The objective of this article is to describe our process of research co-creation that directly involves PWUD in health service planning and prioritization to improve health and reduce harm.

Methods

This study was precipitated by major changes in services accessible to PWUD during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, which further destabilized the local context, aggravating the polycrisis. The objectives of the study were to document PWUD priorities for health interventions within a polycrisis state, identify existing community strengths that will help implement these interventions, and determine what other resources are needed for implementation. Our focus was on participant narratives and the self-interpreted significance of those experiences. Our research questions were “What do PWUD see as priorities to address pandemic impacts on health?” and “What community assets and strengths can be mobilized to implement PWUD-proposed health interventions?” The process-focused results highlighted here describe how we completed a university-community partnered study intended to address the impact of these changes on PWUD who regularly access inner-city services. The non-profit partner was facing a rebuild of a major service hub, which presented a timely opportunity for input and reenvisioning how the new space was to be used.

Theoretical framework

Community-based participatory research (CBPR) served as the foundational framework for the study, emphasizing co-creation by and with community at every stage to ensure an equitable and collaborative approach aligned with the study’s goals. CBPR is action-oriented and emphasizes the full participation of impacted individuals [26]. The intent of CBPR is to “mobilise local and indigenous knowledge of people based in communities of place, identity and interest” [27, p. 264]. Research on CBPR emphasises the need for “everyday ethics” in decision-making where research team members are themselves “embedded participant[s] with situated and partial relationships, responsibilities, values and commitments that frame and constrain ways of seeing, judging and acting in particular situations” [27, p. 266]. Our study design relied on existing relationships of trust between academic researchers, a community non-profit host organization and a team of research assistants including harm reduction workers and PWUD with long-standing community connections. CBPR was methodologically essential to foster co-design and to inform health system innovation with and by PWUD [28, 29]. We adopted a flexible research process highlighting relationality, community-based connection, and intersectionality.

Building from the premise that research with PWUD must be appropriately adaptive to intersecting and catalytic changes of the polycrisis and its associated loss, grief, violence, displacement and mistrust of health systems, the project was secondarily informed by complexity theory. In a complex adaptive system such as the Canadian healthcare system, overall outcomes are not easily predictable from the behaviour of individual parts, meaning a proposed change might not produce the anticipated effects [26]. In our research setting there are important factors outside the behaviour of the healthcare system, most notably the toxicity of the unregulated drug market, requiring constant adaptation as they can not be influenced or changed. This unpredictability underscores the importance of designing interventions that ensure the target population or community experiences the intended benefits [30]. Interventions must be implemented iteratively, closely monitored and adapted dynamically to minimize unintended effects and maintain the functionality of other parts of the system [28]. Equally important is the careful selection of implementation sites and personnel, as each context presents unique workflows and challenges that must be addressed when rolling out system-level protocols [28, 30]. By tailoring interventions to the specific needs of each setting and embracing an iterative process, complexity theory helps guide the development of effective, adaptive solutions in healthcare systems.

Setting

This study is part of local efforts led by the academic investigative team and host nonprofit organization to advance the field of health system innovation through an equity-oriented approach that works across sectors. The host nonprofit, Boyle Street Community Services (“BSCS”), which has been serving structurally vulnerable individuals in Edmonton for over 50 years, applied for and held the grant funds. BSCS hired peer research assistants to co-develop the research protocol, recruit PWUD participants and conduct the research, ensuring people with lived experience had space to share their voice in every phase. Two medical students supported the pre-data collection and formal data collection phases and a postdoctoral researcher oversaw day-to-day aspects of the research protocol with direction and mentorship from the host non-profit and academic principal investigator in collaboration with the academic co-leads. Our team is a mix of settler and Indigenous identities, varied in sexual orientation and gender identities, and involves people with and without lived experience of houselessness and substance use.

The study team also worked with a PWUD Community Advisory Group established in 2013 and comprised of 15 women, men, and gender diverse individuals with lived and living experience of substance use. Over half of the group’s members identify as Indigenous (First Nations, Métis, or Inuit). This group does outreach and advocacy work and serves as an advisory for academic and health practitioners on this research team, regularly providing input on staffing, design, recruitment, data collection, interpretation, and dissemination to ensure research projects equitably reflect the priorities of PWUD. Community Advisory Group members are compensated with cash honoraria and through opportunities for employment with the team.

Recruitment and data collection

Recruitment and data collection took place within four facilities operated by the host nonprofit and its surrounding outreach areas, including several local parks and a public library in central Edmonton. Peer researchers distributed a small number of recruitment flyers to community members in close proximity to the host services. Minimal recruitment was needed as we almost always had a lineup of interested participants, which came with challenges described in our results. Research assistants working in pairs conducted a single data collection session with each participant. Before commencing each session, research assistants introduced themselves, offered refreshments to participants, and reviewed the consent form in clear and accessible language.

The study employed two complementary approaches to data collection, using SenseMaker micro-narratives and arts-based asset mapping with collaging. Peer and academic researchers co-designed the asset mapping process and a data collection tool using the SenseMaker platform by Cynefin Company [16, 31].

Data collection sessions typically lasted 45 min to one hour. Other SenseMaker study protocols suggest 10 min or less to complete the survey [16], but we found that more time was needed to build rapport, establish an understanding of the study’s purpose, and respond to all the prompts. If the participant chose to participate in the arts-based asset mapping, this added an additional 15 to 20 min to the overall process. Participants were invited to start collaging immediately after the consent process, followed by the SenseMaker session.

Asset-mapping

Asset mapping was an optional component of the data collection session process where participants answered the question, “How do you care for yourself and others in the community?” or “What do you carry in your bundle to help care for yourself and others in the community?” The latter question was developed by team member SA building on the concept of personal, sacred or healing objects wrapped in a cloth or metaphorical bundle, grounded in Northern Plains Cree culture [32]. Drawing on SA’s prior arts-based research and pedagogy using collaging to articulate divergent and shared ideas, we invited participants to find magazine images and build mini-collages that answered either of the questions (an example is included in Fig. 1).

Example of a participant collage created during arts-based asset mapping in response to the question, “What do you carry in your bundle to help care for yourself and others in the community?” Participant consent to anonymous publication on file with authors. Photo by VV

Research assistants facilitated a conversation with participants, asking them about the images and words on their collages and writing the answers on small cue cards. Participants self-categorized their answers on a visual bar chart, shown in Fig. 2, structured by categories created by the research team (e.g. people, places and resources) and additional categories created by participants [33]. Research assistants recorded answers on a master list of assets and documented the collages with a photo. Participants were also invited to take their collages with them upon completion. Doing so created space to engage with the community in a multi-faceted way that opened up points of conversation with the research assistants.

Sensemaking

Sensemaking is ongoing collective reflection and understanding of a community-identified issue [34]. The opening prompt in our co-designed SenseMaker data collection tool was designed to help make sense of recent changes being faced by the PWUD community and was framed very broadly to avoid presuppositions about what issues matter most to study participants: “Tell us about a specific moment that really stands out for you over the past year, when you were caring for yourself or for others.”

After working with the participants to determine what story they wanted to share, the research assistants typed the story into the SenseMaker platform (“app”) on an iPad. Research assistants provided participants with the opportunity to read or hear their story read aloud to them and revise it in real time. These stories varied in length and content matter; an example is presented in Fig. 3.

Excerpt from a participant’s story, self-titled “I’m a human being, not a human doing.” Participant consent to anonymous publication on file with authors

After the participant confirmed they were comfortable with how the story was written, the participant then reflected on their story through answering a series of prompts with technical support from the research assistant. The follow-up prompts were co-created by academic and peer research team members and tested on an initial set of 20 people to ensure they were understandable. These prompts are structured as multiple choice questions, likert scales, triads (ternary plots) and dyads, and focused on the topic of the story, how the participant felt during and after the experience, what was threatened, sources of support and harm, whether their needs were met, as well as some demographic questions. After responding to all the prompts, the research assistant pressed a final button to submit, and the participant data became instantly available on a dashboard with patterned visuals, such as the triad in Fig. 4.

Example triad representing study participant response clustering. Figure created by Badi Jabbour and used with permission

The SenseMaker platform generates visuals that give structure to complex data, enabling multifaceted exploration of the domains being interrogated. The data are in the form of qualitative, narrative information, compositional data for the triads and categorical data for the multiple-choice questions.

The analysis process was iterative and occurred throughout the project. While data was being collected the team met weekly for the first two months, and then on an ad hoc basis. This enabled us to bring comprehensive summaries of data within each key domain to the sensemaking sessions for collective discussion and interpretation of significance. Week over week, some trends strengthened and others emerged to eventually provide a more solid understanding of PWUD needs, values and goals. This led to a deductive coding framework.

Iterative data analysis ensured there was context-relevant discussion as themes were identified [35].

After data collection was complete, the Study Coordinator and PI conducted a deep reflection of the overall data corpus including the narrative data, using the multiple choice questions, dyads, and triads to consider the groupings visualized in the patterns of narratives while reflecting on the anchoring questions of the study. Both investigators independently coded the corpus reflecting on these patterns, and reviewed these together to achieve consensus. A small number of additional codes were inductively derived resulting in expansion of the themes. These insights were returned to sensemaking sessions for member checking and verification with the entire team providing interpretation and insight on the results.

Documenting team processes

The participatory nature of the planning meant members of the research team had opportunities to incorporate their knowledge in a way that shaped the research protocol, including diverse experiences as PWUD, harm reduction workers, academics, clinicians and medical students. All study team members provided informed consent to track our collective processes of analysing the asset-mapping and SenseMaker data with insights attributed to specific team members. We made audio recordings and took notes during sensemaking sessions. Research assistants and the study coordinator recorded hand-written and electronic field notes, supported by an internal workshop on field notes with opportunities to practice. Using these field notes to document the experience of academic and peer research partners helped to frame discussion during the sensemaking sessions as we reflected on the SenseMaker and asset-mapping results.

Within a process of facilitated consensus building, participants highlighted their own field notes and reflections among the research team, and we formalized a description of our research process comprising four phases: i.a. pre-data collection phase; orienting to each other, built on a foundation of trusted relationships, ii. a formal data collection phase; bridging this trust for research connection, iii. a readjusting phase; meeting people where they are to address gaps from an equity lens, and iv. an accountability phase; continuing to make meaning from the stories and co-developing proposed interventions based on what was shared. In describing the four phases, we include observations and brief vignettes drawing on researcher fieldnotes and sensemaking sessions.

Results

Phase one: orienting to each other––pre-data collection

Co-creating the research protocol started with building familiarity and trust with one another. Even before the initial grant application, a foundation of trusted relationships was essential to the project, enabled by 20 years of collaboration between BSCS and the academic investigative team. BSCS submitted the funding proposal, gave ongoing input on research planning, participated in collective interpretation of results, managed HR and finances, and facilitated data collection at service hubs. The research protocol was guided by the academic lead and other academic researchers, including a post-doctoral researcher hired to coordinate the research. Research assistants included harm reduction workers, PWUD and medical students.

Notably, BSCS received the research grant during a time of significant upheaval. Displaced from their physical building with short notice, a major community hub for the prior 27 years, BSCS moved to a distributed model across various pre-existing and new temporary service hubs while its new building remained under construction. The impact of losing the old building, which was quickly demolished in front of the whole community, suddenly and earlier than anticipated, was operationally disruptive and widely grieved collectively. The research team, like the organization as a whole, needed to adjust the overall approach and timeline within this context.

Despite challenges, the new building was a key impetus for the study, providing an opportunity to co-create new service innovations. The welcome message in the SenseMaker tool contextualized the research as follows:

Thank you for supporting this community effort to improve services for the new okimaw peyesew kamik (Boyle Street King Thunderbird Centre) and other services. This is different than conventional surveys in that it gets you to share a short story (~ 5 min, 1–2 paragraphs) and answer some questions.

This prompt and follow-up questions in the survey aligned with the study objectives and mapped onto seven key domains of interest identified by the research team. RM had prior experience in group facilitation methods focused on co-creation, which we employed to develop the key domains [36]. We collaborated with an expert in the emerging use of SenseMaker as a method of participatory research (DCS) who helped identify question topics that would target our desired domains [37].

After reviewing and revising multiple versions, the survey was piloted with the Community Advisory Group and then revised again before launching the field research phase. Including the Community Advisory Group was a critical step in co-designing this research as they provided key feedback to ensure our questions were covering intended topics while maintaining respect and dignity for the participants. As we will explain, members of the Community Advisory Group, including several peer researchers on the study team, have tacit knowledge of context and praxis that permitted understanding of needs to go to different places to find people, how to integrate cultural practices in data collection, and how people who use different substances might appreciate certain tailored approaches to promote comfort and engagement.

In addition to developing the study procedures, our collaborative planning conversations focused on how to build trust and safety for everyone involved in the data collection sessions. While university field safety guidance included important reminders about employee and student safety, it omitted mention of participants’ well-being. Participant safety is considered exclusively under the auspices of research ethics and thus artificially carved off from the safety of non-university research team members, who are ultimately sitting together in the data collection session room. We saw safety as relational – about dynamics between the team and the community. For some team members, this is the same community where we have lived and worked for decades while others were getting newly oriented. We acknowledged our diverse positionalities and that our research ethics, safety and comfort depended on mutual and reciprocal processes. Our backgrounds informed the study design including the knowledge of Métis and Cree team members. Members of the research team participated in medicine picking with Indigenous Elders to better understand the medicines’ significance in the context of our research. In line with local neyihaw (Cree) protocols, the team offered tobacco to Indigenous participants before asking them to share their stories. Offering tobacco and other traditional medicines such as sage and sweetgrass, and providing snacks favoured by the community supported relationship-building and helped make community members feel welcomed and respected.

Respect for the time and contributions of community participants was also shown by offering a $30 stipend to Community Advisory Group members that piloted the study and to each participant.

Hiring harm reduction workers already involved in the community meant the team had built-in respect with many participants; and these prior relationships also involved challenges, as discussed below. To mitigate any potential for perceived coercion, while reviewing the consent form, we emphasized that declining participation and stopping at any time would have no impact on access to services. As additional mitigating factors, our research assistants were not the only source of possible support, and where there were pre-existing relationships, participants were given the option to complete the data collection session (and consenting) with a different team member. Not everyone on the team had the same relationships within the community. Harm reduction workers on the research assistant team asked academically situated research assistants to join them on community outreach shifts so that we could mutually observe community members’ perceptions of different research team members and vice versa. We discussed perception and judgment, acknowledging and reducing bias, and processes and systems that create differences through racialization, gendering, class and ability. Giving space to people with lived experience in co-creating the research bridged the gap between researchers and the community.

Building on the existing strengths of the community, we used BSCS service hubs as data collection session sites to create a safe, familiar, and consistent environment for participants [38]. Academic researchers also engaged in community outreach, which allowed them to learn from peers and build trust with potential study participants [29].

Phase two: bridging trust for research connection and data collection

We prepared for formal data collection with a pilot testing period. This was an intentional part of the research to allow us to adapt the data collection tool as we noticed some questions were not being interpreted as we initially thought they would be. Looking at the pilot phase data together, the research assistant team had an opportunity to see each other’s results and learn to facilitate the data collection session in a consistent way.

Pre-existing familiarity with the community enabled us to build on a foundation of trust in recruitment and data collection [39]. For example, one research assistant, LU, has been involved in the community in many different aspects, including as a community consultant and researcher, volunteer at community events, and as a resident in the community. To exemplify this foundation of trust, with their extensive community connections, LU needed to more than double their estimated travel time on the short walk from the team offices to our primary data collection hub due to inevitable greetings on route. Carefully navigating pre-existing relationships was important to ensure confidentiality and comfort of participants––as mentioned, we provided an option to complete their data collection session with a different research assistant—and the comfort of study team members. The research assistants could also ask another research assistant to conduct the data collection session if they did not feel comfortable. This was a dynamic process; occasionally, a participant and/or research assistant would determine that information shared was best handled going forward by someone without prior connection to the participant. Close relationships, while invaluable, can also involve ethical dilemmas and conflict. Relationships of trust come from demonstrated commitment to the community, including the hard work of moving through issues with care, reflexivity and professionalism.

This field note from the third day of data collection highlights reflexivity and accountability by reflecting on what was working and how to make things smoother for participants’ comfort, in this case, where both research assistants had a prior relationship with the participant:

July 18.24: Saw [XXX] today. I was very happy to give someone I know an opportunity to share his voice & make some cash. Like most, it took him a minute to get comfortable & then he had beautiful teachings. I’d like to work on introducing myself more thoroughly at the beginning. Hi, I’m VV but please call me [XXX]. Then introduce the space & topics of conversation. When it comes to consent, I will now begin asking people how to spell their names so we can have better clarity. Today, a participant only partially wrote down their name but luckily LU knew her quite well. (VV)

This field note highlights a reflexive process including polishing the introductions and consenting processes to ensure accurate record keeping and participant comfort. The optional asset-mapping with collaging provided an opportunity to begin the conversation about experiences caring for oneself and others. The conversations created comfort to elicit sharing that often became the focus of the subsequent narrative data collection.

Through the data collection phase, our research assistant team found they were simultaneously observing and experiencing some of the same challenges recorded in the data collection sessions. Interactions with police, for example, were a consistent theme in participants’ stories and researcher field notes.

Aug 6, 2024, 7:20AM: I’m turning a corner to come into [XXXX]. I saw six officers circling a woman. She was on the ground. She had a wave of defeat on her face. There was a swarm of police officers and clean up crew up and down the streets. (AA)

The description here captures felt experiences – coming around a corner, sitting on the ground, a wave of defeat. This phase of data collection was impacted by daily clearing of tents and confiscation of belongings of individuals sheltering in close proximity and participating in data collection. Research team members were not only hearing about experiences in the data collection sessions – we were embedded in the community in various ways, continuing to work shifts as harm reduction and healthcare workers, experiencing grief and loss, and participating in community life and reciprocal systems of community care.

Phase three: readjusting to Meet people where they are

Our sensemaking sessions began within one month of initiating data collection. We began these early core team analysis sessions iteratively with data collection to identify specific patterns within the dataset, as well as occasional demographic and domain gaps therein. Reflecting on the process in real-time enabled the final month of data collection to focus on equity-oriented, targeted sampling. For example, a desire to better understand the experiences of subpopulations led us to move data collection to other hubs with targeted services for youth and LGBTQ2S + community members. This equity-focused lens was important to understand the distinct and intersecting experience of structurally vulnerable subgroups that were less well documented earlier in the data collection phase.

In another instance, in a sensemaking session team members observed a smaller than expected number of individuals who primarily use fentanyl as opposed to stimulants and reasoned that this may stem from people who use methamphetamine being more likely to participate in a data collection session due to the attention regulating as opposed to sedating nature of the drug. To document the perspectives of people using fentanyl, we carried out our initial plan to incorporate mobile data collection efforts at local parks rather than relying on recruitment from only one location. Setting up alongside outreach teams from the partner organization, our mobile data collection setup involved asset-mapping and collaging outdoors on a folding table and data collection sessioning in a vehicle used by the partner organization to provide accessible healthcare and harm reduction services to PWUD.

September 11, 2024: We almost gave up on today after trying four different sites, but eventually we had a busy day of data collection sessioning and asset-mapping in MB Park. There was a firefighter memorial at the gazebo where we were supposed to meet. BP sent a text saying ST couldn’t make it into the park with the van: “There’s thousands of firefighters and family here.” I was driving and asked VV to reply. “Hang tight, we’ll try and find a spot around here. Community stories and asset-mapping conversations that day focused on decampments that had just occurred.

Phase four: accountability

Accountability, our primary team process focus in the final research phase, entails ongoing sensemaking, meaning-making based on participant narratives, and translating research data into co-designed supports. The phases are not strictly linear since the accountability of our process began in the pre-data collection phase. Recognizing that participants and researchers are situated diversely in the community requires reflexivity and relational accountability (33). One of our main focuses was vetting and continuously revisiting our research methodology and approach through the Community Advisory Group of PWUD and incorporating a peer research team. We incorporated feedback from both academic and peer researchers, maintaining alignment with community needs and values throughout the study. This ensured that the focus of the team was aligned and that all members were included in the co-development of the study.

During the project we encountered ongoing changes in the research setting that impacted participants and the research team including: changes in the environment such as severe forest fires resulted in smoke and poor air quality; in our physical location with varied availability of office space and introduction of new and mobile data collection sites; in the social-political context with displacement of individuals sheltering outdoors resulting from police and bylaw enforcement; and, in ongoing threats to participant health and wellbeing with an unpredictable drug supply and intersecting health disparities faced by PWUD.

As such, an important form of accountability was “process consent,” an ongoing form of consent through the research process [40]. For example, to meet participants where they are (our readjusting phase), we acquired a research phone that enabled participants to text or call to arrange a meeting with the research team at a place and time that worked for them. Several data collection sessions were conducted per participant preference at a public library or outdoors, where confidentiality could be impacted by others passing or stopping by during the data collection session. This elevated the need for regular check-ins with participants during the data collection session to see if they were comfortable continuing despite changes in physical context. We adapted as needed, including changing location or waiting for others to leave before continuing. Another example of process consent involved accommodating emotional or physiological changes including the need to adjust to varying levels of energy. If a participant wanted to complete the session after a rest, research assistants would offer a snack and/or remind participants they could skip some of the follow-up questions as needed to encourage better engagement for the rest of the interview. Again, regular check-ins ensured ongoing consent and good relations with participants, determining goals together by laying out options. A few participants elected to stop midway through the session, but still wanted their story included as a part of the project.

While providing information about the study, participants were informed that their narratives and optional asset mapping would be shared with other community members in a final report, which was welcomed as an empowering gesture of our team’s dedication to meaning-making from the data grounded in respect and community involvement (see Fig. 5). Both the research team and participants have described the secondary impact of the research in providing a forum for people to express their opinions about ideas for change. Community members have continued to express interest in having an ongoing program or process where people can similarly share their experiences and input.

Peer researchers took the lead on our report to community, which was created as a booklet in a “‘zine” style and highlighted the research process, key results from the data, and collages made by participants [41].

Discussion

The four-phase framework guided the study and promoted a dynamic and accountable approach to centering PWUD in health system innovation. Our results emphasise the importance of taking time to orient to each other and the community, even as a diverse team with many preexisting relationships. We participated in community outreach days with our BSCS partners, giving us better context and understanding of the community we were situated in, including the hardships as well as the rhythm and vibrancy it had to offer. We learned reciprocally from watching fellow team members respond to community needs while on outreach. We spent time on the land with Indigenous elders picking traditional medicines, and while these practices were already familiar to some study team members, this ensured that when we offered opportunities to smudge with Indigenous participants, all team members could do so with respect for cultural protocols. Taking this time to integrate into community practices enabled our team to learn cultural practices, develop rapport, and better understand the situations people may disclose during data collection.

This methodology uniquely combined SenseMaker micronarratives with arts-based asset-mapping within a process of CBPR that involved analyzing results as we captured data, and dynamically adjusting based on emerging patterns and insights generated in regular sensemaking sessions. An iterative data analysis process allowed for adjustments in real-time to guide research focus, ensuring equity-oriented engagement with structurally vulnerable groups. Adapting to meet participants where they are was essential and literal, moving data collection to spaces that are comfortable for PWUD, including youth and LGBTQ2S + PWUD.

Our adaptive approach recognized complexity within and beyond the Canadian health care system and the need for cross-sector learnings based on comprehensive informationabout PWUD-voiced priorities. By quickly and accessibly generating rich data from a large data set, complexity-oriented methods and tools such as SenseMaker enable rapid engagement of “cognitively, culturally, and experientially diverse groups” to assess the situation and contribute to decision-making in a changing environment [25, 42]. We implemented these methods through a process of co-creation that enacted and extended existing principles and best practices in CBPR and scholarly activities that engage PWUD.

Accountability began with research design, was maintained throughout data collection by creating safety for participants, and then defined the final phase of the research as BSCS and community members expect an accessible final report and action-oriented responses to the narratives shared. To this end, preliminary results have been shared with the Community Advisory group and we are now working with the nonprofit partner to translate results into codesigned supports. We have developed and shared a community report at a community gathering, and have also shared results with staff and leadership within BSCS; further knowledge sharing is planned with other local nonprofits and other policy and social service stakeholders. This kind of follow up aligns with a complexity-oriented emphasis on responding to change as well as maintaining community connection and trust.

Co-creation, a core principle of CBPR, is an essential component of effective research that includes communities typically excluded from academia, making space for insights into issues experienced first-hand and intersectional perspectives [43]. PWUD and drug user organizations have emphasized the importance of participatory research that involves PWUD at every phase, including design, data collection and implementation of proposed interventions [26, 44]. This study benefited greatly from the involvement of peer harm reduction workers, researchers and health care providers in co-developing the data collection session questions, space, and other processes. Co-creating the data collection process and building on pre-existing relationships showed respect for the community [39, 44]. Peer researchers assisted academic researchers by rewording questions with accessible language and refining the flow of data collection sessions, ensuring a logical order that improved participant experience and comprehension. Doing so helped contextualize participants’ responses within their micro-narrative during the data collection session.

Direct involvement in health system planning, delivery, research and advocacy can mutually benefit community members and health systems, but the approach to engagement determines whether community members experience positive or negative outcomes as a result of participation [24]. Involvement can lead to capacity-building for peer researchers, including providing income and skill-building. However, equitable and consistent compensation and opportunities for capacity building are critical [45]. Peer researchers in this study were paid a living wage, and the honorarium for participants was a roughly equivalent hourly rate (depending on the time taken to complete the data collection session). Training and skill-building focused on interviewing, taking field notes, First Aid, non-violent crisis intervention, public speaking, literature searches, collaborative sensemaking, documenting research experience on a resume, report writing, academic publishing and more.

Consistent with other research in this area, hiring and training peer researchers greatly increased the research team’s capacity [45]. Building from trusted relationships enabled research connections with community members who shared personal stories in the context of ongoing relationships of care, and this expanded the knowledge garnered through the research [45]. Processes of co-creation grounded in trust were essential to create a context where community members were comfortable enough to share.

Conclusions

Under conditions shaped by crisis upon crisis, astronomical loss of life, near constant displacement and entrenched colonial violence, research by and with PWUD must be as flexible as the changing picture of the problem it seeks to document, co-creating interventions that can readily change in response to needs identified by PWUD. That is, research with PWUD must be appropriately adaptive to the complexity of polycrisis. Documenting and incorporating PWUD voices in creating change is an important part of implementing effective and meaningful interventions in a rapidly adapting system. In response to the urgent need for community-centered approaches and co-design in health and social systems, we suggest a collaborative process designed and implemented with PWUD, planning and readily adjusting as needed to co-create health system innovation. This level of collaboration is only possible via a longitudinal commitment to the community-university partnership, with deliberate and mindful inclusion of co-creation activities within research grant proposals, timelines, and budgets. Because it was prioritized and adequately resourced, our collaborative process enabled the team to connect insights from complexity theory and principles of CBPR–accountability, co-creation, equitable pay, capacity-building, and building from pre-existing relationships–to research practice. An accountable, relational, complexity-oriented approach made it possible to collect rich data during a time of rapid and complex change stemming from a multidimensional polycrisis.
