Sharenting and the digital determinants of childhood
Authors: Ilona Kickbusch, Louise Holly
Published: 2026

In two earlier editorials, we argued that health promotion must lead the charge on the digital determinants of health (Kickbusch and Holly 2023) and took stock of how rapidly the concept has been taken up (Holly et al. 2025). Yet one of its earliest and most intimate expressions has gone almost unexamined within our field. Sharenting—the sharing by parents and carers of children's images and information online—has been ceded largely to lawyers and the parenting press. UNICEF addresses it chiefly as advice to parents on privacy settings and consent (Steinberg n.d.), while WHO's engagement with children's digital lives has centred mainly on gaming, online violence and adolescent mental health (World Health Organization 2026). Health promotion, despite its determinants lens and life-course horizon, has been largely silent.

The scale of this issue is not trivial. A child's digital footprint now often begins before birth, with the ultrasound posted to a feed; estimates from the Children’s Commissioner for England suggest a child may appear in some 1300 images online before the age of thirteen (Children’s Commissioner for England 2018). Much of this is ordinary affection. But sharenting is also the entry point to a commercial architecture that turns the documentation of childhood into data, content and revenue—placing it exactly where digital and commercial determinants meet, at the most formative point of the life-course.

In the decade since the term was coined, the nature and risk profile of sharenting has evolved considerably (Leckart 2012). Despite increased awareness of its risks to children’s privacy, autonomy and safety, studies indicate that large proportions of parents continue to share children's content online, often without their consent (Ferrara et al. 2024). Even where children’s faces are concealed online, algorithmic profiles can be constructed for platforms and commercial partners based on metadata and contextual cues. Professional forms of sharenting have emerged where parents monetize their children’s online presence through dedicated channels and branding (Tosuntaş and Griffiths 2024). The rising number of ‘kidfluencers’, children who create content for video sharing and gaming platforms, has raised concerns about hidden forms of child labour and violations of children’s rights (Bakioğlu 2024). Generative AI has enabled innocently shared content to be manipulated into material for child exploitation and abuse (UNICEF 2026).

Law has begun, unevenly, to respond. France's Law of 19 February 2024 wrote children's image rights into the Civil Code and allows a judge to intervene when parents disagree. The European Union's right to erasure (GDPR, Article 17) offers a cumbersome route to later deletion. In the USA, states including Illinois, California, Minnesota, and Utah have extended child labour and trust protections descended from the 1939 Coogan Law to kidfluencers. These measures share a premise that all health promoters should endorse: the child is a rights-bearing subject, not an extension of a parent's self-presentation.

But law is necessary, not sufficient, and three gaps should concern us. The first is the consent paradox: the person whose privacy is at stake cannot consent, and the guardian asked to protect it is often the discloser. The second is that the present digital architecture resists erasure—a right to be forgotten is asserted against systems built for permanence and reuse, by data brokers, by facial recognition, and now by generative models trained on scraped images, including the repurposing of ordinary photographs into synthetic abuse. The third is that regulation still targets the symptom, the individual post, rather than the political economy that rewards the exposure of childhood for engagement and income.

A health promotion lens earns its place here. Sharenting bears on children’s identity, autonomy and the right to an open future. It is also a matter of equity, particularly for those children whose medical or developmental lives are narrated to an audience, whose images are used for nefarious purposes, or who cannot exit a family economy built on their image. The digital environment is now a primary setting of childhood, as consequential as the school or the home; in the Ottawa tradition it should be safe, stimulating and enjoyable. Yet its governance is still framed largely as a matter of parental prudence and digital literacy, reprising the very individualization of structural problems that health promotion has spent four decades contesting. In fact, studies show that digital literacy may have an inverse impact on sharenting levels as parents’ privacy management skills and use of platform settings give them a false sense of security over their children’s content (Tosuntaş and Griffiths 2024).

What, then, needs to be addressed? We should resist the drift towards individual responsibility and name the digital environment as a determinant to be governed: platform accountability for the designs that commodify children and ignore their rights; default protections rather than opt-out burdens on families; and coherence across the legislative patchwork, aligned with the standard set by the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child in General Comment No. 25 (2021). It is telling that several of the strongest reforms were prompted by young people themselves—a reminder that our task is to move children from objects of disclosure to architects of their own digital lives.

Health promotion has long insisted that health is created in everyday environments. It is ever clearer that power that manifests in the most private of settings needs policy responses. We know this from efforts to tackle the many forms of violence and sexual abuse that children are exposed to behind closed doors. Sharenting is an early test of whether we will extend that insight to the digital environment in which childhood now unfolds. It deserves far more attention than it has so far received.
