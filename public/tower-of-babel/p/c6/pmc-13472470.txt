Evaluation of antibacterial efficacy of Nigella sativa seed extract on periodontal pathogens
Authors: Gholam Reza Talei, Foroogh Mirzania, Narges Ziaei, Amir Varezardi, Alireza Nazarzadeh, Amir Parvini Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Nigella sativa, a medicinal plant of the Ranunculaceae family, is widely used for its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties. This study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of N. sativa seed extract against two major periodontal pathogens: Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and Porphyromonas gingivalis.Materials and Methods:In this study, bacterial samples were collected from 30 patients with moderate-to-severe chronic periodontitis. Pure cultures of anaerobic bacteria were used to assess susceptibility to N. sativa extract and to amoxicillin–metronidazole. Antibacterial activity was evaluated using broth microdilution and agar dilution methods.Results:From the previous experiments conducted, it was determined that the extract from the plant has an inhibitory effect at a concentration of mg/mL, but not at concentrations of 4 and 8 mg/mL. Furthermore, from previous experiments, it was determined that the growth inhibition was from 14 mg/mL for A. actinomycetemcomitans and was 52 mg/mL for P. gingivalis. Furthermore, it was determined that at a concentration of 100–210 mg/mL, the growth of both pathogens was inhibited. Assessment of the FIC index for synergistic and antagonistic interactions showed synergism for the combination of N. sativa and metronidazole for the A. actinomycetemcomitans, antagonism for P. gingivalis, and no interaction with amoxicillin.Conclusion:The antibacterial property of N. sativa has positive implications and can probably be used in herbal medicine for periodontal diseases, where it can be used for the formulation of mouthwashes and toothpastes. More studies should be done.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal diseases cause inflammation, leading to the destruction and loss of tooth-supporting tissues such as gums and alveolar bone.[1] Alveolar bone contains cavities for tooth roots and supports them.[2] Periodontitis may result from systemic diseases and is a clinical manifestation of such conditions. It also appears as a chronic or aggressive form and is a major risk factor in dentistry, being a leading cause of tooth loss worldwide.[34] Tooth loss affects daily activities such as eating, speaking, and social interaction.[5] These diseases are caused by bacterial infections and present with bleeding gums, loose teeth, gingivitis, bad breath, and tooth loss.[67]

Treatment involves removing pathogens from the tooth surface and root, halting chronic inflammation, and preserving the teeth.[8910] Scaling and root planing are standard treatments but often require additional therapies,[111213] including local and systemic antibiotics,[141516] photodynamic therapy,[17] laser therapy,[1819] and chemical mouthwashes.[20] Tetracyclines and the amoxicillin–metronidazole combination are commonly used, but frequent dosing is required to achieve effective concentrations, which can reduce patient compliance and promote antibiotic resistance and side effects. Recently, traditional treatments and medicinal plants have gained attention. Shetty et al.[19] reported that garlic extract prevents the growth of oral pathogens. Chaturvedi et al.[20] showed that turmeric has analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties and is used for gingivitis and periodontitis. Other studies highlighted Aloe vera, garlic, mushrooms, and other medicinal plants.

Nigella sativa, known for its anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and immune-boosting properties, is also widely used. In this research, N. sativa seeds, with antibacterial[21] and anti-inflammatory[222324] effects, were investigated as a low-risk adjuvant treatment for periodontal diseases. N. sativa, from the Ranunculaceae family, is traditionally used to treat various ailments.[25] It contains compounds such as nigellin, nigellicine, and volatile oils[26] and grows in Asia, Southern Europe, and North Africa. Ancient Egyptians and Greeks used their seeds to treat headaches, asthma, allergies, and intestinal worms. It is also known for diuretic, menstrual-inducing, and lactation-stimulating properties and is commonly used in the Middle East for respiratory, digestive, and inflammatory conditions.[27]

Considering the high prevalence of periodontal diseases, the need for timely, low-complication treatments, and the bacterial etiology of this condition, a safe and side-effect-free therapy is desirable. Given the significant role of anaerobic bacteria in periodontal infections and the lack of studies on the effect of N. sativa on periodontal pathogens, this study was conducted to evaluate the antibacterial effect of N. sativa extract on Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis, with the hope of introducing a new therapeutic approach for periodontal disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To prepare the extract, 2 kg of N. sativa seeds were manually ground and extracted using a Soxhlet apparatus with acetone as the solvent. The purified extract was stored in the refrigerator until use.

This laboratory study involved 32 patients with moderate-to-severe chronic periodontitis who had no systemic diseases, were not taking anti-inflammatory, antibiotic, or immunosuppressive drugs, and were not pregnant. Sampling was done using two sterile paper points, placed with tweezers for 20 s into the periodontal pocket. The samples were then transferred to sterile tubes containing thioglycolate medium. The study was conducted with the cooperation of a periodontitis specialist and with patient consent.

Anaerobic bacteria were identified using morphological and biochemical tests. In addition to the target bacteria, A. actinomycetemcomitans, Prevotella intermedia, Actinomyces viscosus, and others were isolated. Samples were cultured on Brucella agar enriched with hemin, Vitamin K, and liquefied sheep blood. Colonies were identified via biochemical tests.

According to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines for the agar dilution test, at least two of the following standard bacteria should be tested: Bacteroides fragilis (ATCC25285), Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Eubacterium (ATCC29741), and Eggerthella lentum (ATCC43055). However, due to the unavailability of these strains in Iranian microbial banks and the cost and time required for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) identification, A. actinomycetemcomitans (Y4Nov99) and Porphyromonas gingivalis strains prepared by Dr. Seyed Saeed Eshraghi were used identified by PCR. Pure cultures of anaerobic bacteria were used to evaluate their sensitivity to N. sativa extract and the amoxicillin–metronidazole combination. Broth microdilution and agar dilution methods under anaerobic conditions were employed to assess qualitative effects and determine the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). For the broth microdilution method, a 100,000 μg/mL stock solution of the methanolic N. sativa extract was prepared. Then, 100 μL of Brucella broth enriched with hemin and Vitamin K1 (without horse serum) was added to each well of a 96-well plate. Serial dilutions of N. sativa extract, vancomycin, dimethyl sulfoxide (negative control), metronidazole, carvacrol, and benzamine mouthwash were added. Dilutions were made by twofold serial dilution from columns 2–10 using an 8-channel pipette. Target bacteria – P. gingivalis, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Streptococcus mutans – were added at a 0.5 McFarland standard to each well of the three test plates [Figure 1].

Sensitivity of oral bacteria to Nigella sativa extract and antimicrobial agents using the microdilution method. (a) Streptococcus mutans, (b) Lactobacillus acidophilus, and (c) Porphyromonas gingivalis

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values were determined by inoculating 10 μL of dilutions below and above the MIC onto Brucella agar [Figure 2]. The microbroth dilution method using Brucella broth enriched with hemin, Vitamin K, and 9% lysed horse blood does not yield satisfactory results for slow-growing anaerobic bacteria. According to CLSI guidelines, this method is only approved for fast-growing anaerobes like B. fragilis, and the agar dilution method is recommended for others. In this study, the positive control was a plate incubated under anaerobic conditions, referred to as a plate with good growth (spot confluent). The negative control lacked any antimicrobial agent. Average results from at least three replicate experiments were used. For the control group, the amoxicillin–metronidazole combination was applied, with MIC interpretive values of ≤8.16 μg/mL for sensitive and ≥32 μg/mL for resistant strains [Figures 3 and 4].

In the agar dilution test, positive and negative controls were also used to confirm anaerobic growth. Negative control plates showed no bacterial growth under aerobic conditions, whereas positive controls confirmed bacterial growth under anaerobic conditions on Brucella agar enriched with hemin and Vitamin K. First, the minimum effective doses of amoxicillin and metronidazole against the target bacteria were determined. Then, the effect of their combination on A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis was compared with N. sativa extract using the simplified checkerboard method. MIC values of both antibiotics were first determined by broth dilution. Then, 4–5 lower dilutions and up to twice the MIC were prepared and combined in wells. Bacterial suspensions were added as positive controls, and the wells without antibiotics served as controls. If the FIC index is <0.5, the antibiotics have a synergistic effect. A value of 1 indicates no interaction, whereas a value of 2 suggests antagonism. In the agar dilution test, positive and negative controls are necessary to confirm anaerobic conditions. In this study, the negative control showed no growth of the two bacteria on Brucella agar enriched with sheep blood, hemin, and Vitamin K under aerobic conditions. The positive control confirmed bacterial growth on the same medium under anaerobic conditions.

RESULTS

P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans were the bacterial species used in this study to qualitatively and quantitatively examine their sensitivity to N. sativa seed extract and compare the antibacterial efficiency of the extract with the effect of metronidazole and amoxicillin. A. actinomycetemcomitans is a Gram-negative bacterium that grows easily in anaerobic conditions and can infect in vivo and in vitro culture media. This bacterium is one of the pathogens of the oral cavity, which has a high resistance against the immune system, and in addition to periodontal diseases, it can also cause systemic infection and even be effective in getting rheumatoid arthritis.[2829] P. gingivalis is one of the anaerobic bacteria found in the normal flora of the mouth, which can sometimes act as a pathobiont against its host and cause a destructive effect on the patient’s oral and dental health.[30] Because it is expensive and difficult to isolate the bacteria in question from the infected patient, preprepared bacteria were used. The use of these isolated bacteria also reduced the possibility of inadvertent infections.

The results obtained from the broth microdilution method showed that N. sativa extract in the concentration range of 100–106.5 (mg/mL) was effective in destroying P. gingivalis bacteria and in the concentration range of 12.5–14 (mg/mL) was effective in killing A. actinomycetemcomitans bacteria. In the agar dilution method, the following results were obtained: N. sativa extract at concentrations of 4 and 8 mg/mL had no effect on the growth of A. actinomycetemcomitans, but at higher concentrations (14, 20, 26, 52, 100, 105, and 210 mg/mL), it prevented the growth of this bacterium. N. sativa extract in concentrations of 4, 8, 14, and 26 (mg/mL) had no effect on the growth of P. gingivalis, but in higher concentrations (52, 100, 105, and 210 mg/mL) prevented the growth of this bacterium [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

In this section, the antibacterial findings obtained from N. sativa extract are compared with previous studies and interpreted based on microbial behavior and known antibacterial mechanisms. The therapeutic effects of medicinal plants on periodontitis have been evaluated in many studies. After investigating the effects of eucalyptus extract, Nagata et al. reported its antibacterial activity against pathogens such as P. gingivalis and P. intermedia.[31] Another study showed that chewing gum containing eucalyptus reduces gum bleeding, pocket depth, and plaque accumulation.[32] Chatterjee et al. and Pai et al., in separate studies, reported that neem reduced BOP and plaque in patients with periodontitis, and in a 64-day follow-up, it showed effects similar to those of chlorhexidine.[3334]

In another study, Pai et al. point to the effect of reducing bacteria and plaque of this plant as factors that contribute to the development of periodontitis.[35] Other studies have investigated the antibacterial effects of chamomile flower, which has activity against P. gingivalis and can be used as a mouthwash in the treatment of periodontitis patients.[36] Garlic has antimicrobial activity against periodontal pathogens, including A. actinomycetemcomitans,[37] and its local use prevents the growth of A. actinomycetemcomitans and Fusobacterium.[3839404142]

Black seed has a significant effect on various microorganisms. Its extract contains several active components, among which thymoquinone and alpha-hederin are the most prominent for their antibacterial effects.[43] Studies on thymoquinone have shown that it has no effect on Gram-negative bacteria. The two bacteria used in this study are Gram-negative; however, due to their surfactant-like properties, the alpha-hederin component demonstrates effectiveness against the outer membrane lipids, depending on bacterial reproduction speed.[44]

In the current study, the antibacterial effects of black seed extract on P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans bacteria were investigated and compared with common antibiotics used in the treatment of periodontal lesions, i.e. amoxicillin and metronidazole. The results showed that black seed extract can prevent the growth of A. actinomycetemcomitans at a dilution of 14 ml/mg and P. gingivalis at a dilution of 52 ml/mg. Therefore, the local and systemic use of this herbal medicine, in the form of mouthwash or toothpaste, can play an effective role in improving periodontal diseases. The results of the study by Asiri et al. support our findings. They concluded that herbal mouthwashes have shown significant improvement in protecting oral health and can replace chemicals such as chlorhexidine, which have more side effects.[45] Singh et al. also supported the finding that Aloe vera can be a suitable therapeutic alternative to metronidazole for the adjunctive treatment of periodontitis.[46] However, the findings of Haffajee et al. and Bhat et al. are contradictory. By comparing chlorhexidine and herbal mouthwashes, they concluded that chlorhexidine is more effective in preventing the growth of periodontal pathogens and reducing plaque.[4748] Gupta et al. also found that herbal toothpastes have similar effects in controlling plaque and gingivitis compared to conventional fluoride toothpastes.[49] In this study, to compare the effectiveness of black seed extract in preventing the growth of periodontal pathogens, amoxicillin and metronidazole were used as control groups. The results showed that amoxicillin was effective at minimum dilutions of 0.25 ml/mg and 4 ml/mg on A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis, respectively, and metronidazole was effective at 8 ml/mg and 16 ml/mg, respectively. Various studies have supported this finding and considered the complementary treatment of metronidazole and amoxicillin, along with scaling and root planing, effective in improving the microbial conditions of periodontal disease.[50515253] Based on our findings, black seed extract and metronidazole have antagonistic and synergistic effects on P. gingivalis and A. actinomycetemcomitans, respectively. Furthermore, black seed extract and amoxicillin at effective concentrations on both bacteria showed no interaction. Amoxicillin and metronidazole had a synergistic effect on both bacteria. This is similar to the findings of studies by Mestnik et al., who confirmed the synergistic effect of amoxicillin and metronidazole in patients with periodontitis.[5455] Amoxicillin and metronidazole act against the peptidoglycan and DNA of bacteria, respectively. Due to their small molecular size, they act without interfering with each other, showing synergy both in vitro and in clinical use. In the simultaneous use of black seed extract and metronidazole on P. gingivalis, the alpha-hederin molecule (a large 2-ring triterpenoid saponin) crystallizes on the surface of this fast-growing bacterium and prevents metronidazole from entering, resulting in an antagonistic effect. For slow-growing A. actinomycetemcomitans, alpha-hederin can penetrate the membrane, rupture it, and allow metronidazole to enter and act on DNA, showing a synergistic effect. In the simultaneous use of black seed extract and amoxicillin on P. gingivalis, despite alpha-hederin accumulation on the surface, amoxicillin still acts near the surface (against peptidoglycan), resulting in no significant interaction. For A. actinomycetemcomitans, the extract disrupts membranes and kills some bacteria, but due to the molecule’s size, it blocks complete metronidazole entry, leading to a neutral, nonsynergistic, and nonantagonistic reaction.[5657585960616263]

CONCLUSION

In this research, the antibacterial effects of N. sativa in the treatment of periodontal infections have been investigated. The obtained results proved that N. sativa seed extract can prevent the growth of A. actinomycetemcomitans and P. gingivalis bacteria. The appropriate therapeutic effects of N. sativa seed extract, demand for herbal-based treatments, and risk of microbial resistance to common antibiotics, making it practical as an auxiliary antibacterial, and further studies should be done to produce sanitary products containing N. sativa extract like mouthwash solution and toothpaste.
