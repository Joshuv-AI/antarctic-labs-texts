An improved Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method for genome editing using CRISPR/Cas9 in elite indica rice (Oryza sativa L.)
Authors: Laxmipreeya Behera, Kailash Ch Samal, Parameswaran C, Pawan Kumar Agrawal, V Mohan Murali Achary, Manasi Dash, Ashok Mishra, Manjusha Rani, Fred Bwayo Masika, Gurunatham Sai Deekshith Goud, Mahipal Singh Kesawat, Sanghamitra Samantaray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRice feeds nearly half of the world’s population and underpins global food security. Climate change now poses a major threat to rice productivity worldwide. Genome editing has reshaped crop improvement strategies. Among these tools, the Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/CRISPR-associated protein (CRISPR/Cas) system stands out for its precision, efficiency, and scalability. However, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation efficiency is often low, particularly in indica rice varieties. Here, we optimized an Agrobacterium-mediated transformation protocol for indica rice cultivars. The method was established in Lalat and MTU-1010. Seed-derived embryogenic calli were used to introduce the thermosensitive genic male sterile (OsTMS5) gene. A CRISPR/Cas9 vector carrying a gRNA and the selectable marker hptII was used for transformation. Callus induction reached 96.87% in MTU-1010 and 93.30% in Lalat MS medium supplemented with 3 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.5 mg/L BAP. In contrast, regeneration efficiency was higher in Lalat (90.28%) than in MTU-1010 (87.51%) on MS medium supplemented with 0.25 mg/L NAA, 0.5 mg/L kinetin, and 2 mg/L BAP. In addition, PCR analysis further verifies the integration of the transgene. Subsequently, the transformation efficiency was 37.20% in Lalat and 29.62% in MTU-1010. Therefore, this protocol provides a robust platform for gene function analysis and trait editing in rice. Introduction

Plant breeding is widely acknowledged as a key strategy for incorporating desirable traits, and it has been instrumental in maintaining food security for the expanding global population [1–3]. There has been seen a sharp rise in the demand for food, vegetables, fruits, and animal products [4, 5]. Further, this situation is aggravated by significant changes in climate conditions and an increasing prevalence of both biotic and abiotic stress (Chaudhry and Sidhu 2022; Paraschivu et al. 2020). Rice (Oryza sativa L.) holds a crucial position for food security, providing a staple source of nourishment for millions of people across the globe [1, 6]. The rice genome sequencing has greatly enhanced our comprehension of gene functions and genome structure, while also accelerating the investigation of earlier unknown genes (IRGSP, 2025). This research aims to deepen our understanding of plant developmental processes, with the ultimate goal of enhancing crop performance [7]. However, the demand for rice is projected to increase significantly, with estimates suggesting it could reach 852 million tons by 2035 [8]. Furthermore, with the world population projected to reach nearly 9.8 billion by 2050, it is imperative to develop tactics to feed an expanding population [9, 10]. Moreover, rice production suffers heavy losses due to the combined impact of biotic and abiotic stress [11, 12]. To tackle these challenges, it is imperative to develop rice varieties with increased yields and enhanced tolerance to various stress [13–16]. Several techniques, such as marker assisted breeding, mutation breeding, RNA interference (RNAi), Transcription Activator-Like Effector Nucleases (TALENs), and Zinc Finger Nucleases (ZFNs) have been utilized to improve key agronomics attributes in rice [17–19]. However, a recent advancement in the field of CRISPR/Cas technique that has demonstrated efficacy in heritable genetic alterations in crops [20–23]. Genome editing involves assembling a single guide RNA (sgRNA) with Cas. The customizable sgRNA directs the Cas protein for precise cleavage of target site [24–26]. This breakthrough has created numerous new avenues for researchers, enabling them to quickly unravel the complexities of biological processes. It is predominantly employed to promote biofortification, boost yields, and enhance stress tolerance in diverse crops [27–30].

Male sterility is a plants inability to build functional pollen, anthers and male gametes [31, 32]. Developing male sterility lines presents a significant challenge for plant breeders, as these lines are essential for achieving the key goal of producing F1 hybrid [33, 34]. OsTMS5 (LOC_Os02g12290) encodes an RNase Z domain that regulates the accumulation of UbL40 mRNA. This domain is essential for RNase ZS1 enzymatic activity, which mediates the cleavage and maturation of UbL40 mRNA precursors, a process crucial for proper pollen development and the maintenance of fertility. In tms5 mutants, impaired RNase ZS1 activity prevents proper cleavage of UbL40 mRNAs, leading to their overaccumulation, particularly under heat stress. This increased level led to the producing of impaired pollen, eventually resulting in male sterility [35, 36]. This study aims to generate male sterile lines by precisely knocking out the OsTMS gene, thereby use of these lines in the development of hybrid rice varieties. In addition, while OsTMS5 is known to be essential for fertility, we hypothesized that its specific knockout would reveal downstream effects on other genes and physiological traits involved in the fertility process. The OsTMS5 line, in particular, has emerged as an excellent alternative to cytoplasmic male sterility systems. OsTMS5 line are temperature-sensitive, which affects the expression of male sterility. Over time, various methods have been employed to achieve male sterility, ranging from traditional cross-breeding techniques to advanced genetic and genomic approaches including GET [37]. GET methods, particularly gene knockout enabled by CRISPR/Cas, has demonstrated versatility and effectiveness in modifying crucial genes that govern wide range of cellular processes, such as male sterility. These valuable breeding methods offer a time efficient approach for generating novel genetic variations with beneficial alleles. They have the potential to enhance biological processes and expedite cultivar development without depending on traditional breeding methods.

Rice has been extensively studied to optimize Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and regeneration protocols. However, most of this research has focused on temperate japonica varieties, for instance, Nipponbare, which are relatively easy to transform [38–42]. Recent advancements in GETs have shown significant potential in expediting functional genomics and enhancing crop traits. However, the practical application of these revolutionary tools is impeded by low efficiency and genotype dependence in plant transformation [43]. To overcome these challenges, numerous studies have explored Agrobacterium-mediated transformation in rice using various explants, including immature embryos, shoot meristems, and embryogenic callus. These investigations have employed diverse combinations of media constituents and various strains of Agrobacterium. However, despite these efforts, the existing protocols often encounter several limitations [44]. Till now there is no efficient transformation protocol established for indica rice, in contrast to the well-developed protocols for japonica rice. Recognizing the significance of Agrobacterium-mediated transformation for crop improvement and functional genomics, this research is directed towards designing gRNA constructs to precisely edit the OsTMS5 gene in rice using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. Further, we established and optimized the stepwise procedure for Agrobacterium transformation in indica cultivars, specifically focusing on Lalat and MTU-1010. Embryogenic calli derived from the seeds were employed for the transformation of the OsTMS5 gene. Callus induction was notably high at 96.87% in MTU-1010, followed by 93.3% in Lalat. However, regeneration was more efficient in Lalat (90.28%) than in MTU-1010 (87.51%) on MS medium containing 0.25 mg/L NAA, 0.5 mg/L kinetin, and 2 mg/L BAP. Subsequently, the transformation efficiency was observed to be 37.20% in Lalat and 29.62% in MTU-1010. Therefore, this protocol holds promise as a valuable tool for elucidating the biological functions of genes, ultimately contributing to the improvement of rice.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and culture conditions

Calli were generated using mature rice seeds of the Indica cultivar MTU-1010 and Lalat, which were supplied by the National Rice Research Institute (NRRI). The seeds underwent dehusking and subsequent sterilization by washing twice for 2 min with sterilized distilled water, followed by a 1 min immersion in 70% ethanol. Subsequently, the seeds underwent treatment with a sodium hypochlorite solution, supplemented with 1–2 drops of Tween 20, for a duration of 15 min. All stages of tissue culture, including callus induction, selection, and regeneration, were carried out under controlled culture room conditions. The temperature was maintained within the range of 26 to 28 °C, and a day-night cycle was established with a photoperiod of 16 h of light and an 8 h dark. During stages necessitating complete darkness, including the callus induction, co-cultivation, selection, and regeneration, tissue culture plates were shielded by aluminum foil.

Development of a gRNA expression cassette containing the target OsTMS5 sequence

The expression cassette for rice codon optimized eSPCas9 nuclease was designed with the ZmUbiP to ensure constitutive expression, and NosT for proper termination [31]. This cassette was subsequently incorporated into entry vector 1 (pEV1). This construct, designed to target OsTM5 for knockout, included a 20-nt sequence specific to OsTM5. This construct was gathered with OsU3/OsU6 promoter, and incorporated a polymerase III terminator. Further, construct was cloned into pEV1 and pEV2, respectively (Fig. S1B). The 20 base pairs for OsTM5 was meticulously selected from the rice genome. The selection of 20 nucleotide was performed using the CRISPR Direct bioinformatic tool (https://crispr.dbcls.jp/), as detailed by [45]. The target site was synthesized by IDT, which includes two 20 nucleotide sequences and BsaI restriction sites. To create the DNA oligo-duplex, forward and reverse sequences were mixed (100 µM each) and then incubated at 42 °C for 30 seconds. This mixture heated at 95 °C for 5 min. gradually cooled to 25 °C at a rate of 0.1 °C per second. This procedure yielded a duplex DNA with four nucleotide overhangs, specifically “CAGG” and “CAAA,” at the 5’ ends. Finally, this construct including OsU3-gRNA and OsU6-gRNA were digested with the BsaI. Subsequently, the targeted duplex DNA was then inserted into these vectors, which had been pre-digested with BsaI enzyme [31].

Agrobacterium preparation and transformation

The binary vector pMDC99, carrying the hptII gene driven by the OsU6 and OsU3 promoters, was employed for transformation. For electroporation, 100 ng of the recombinant plasmid DNA (pMDC99-eSPCas9 + OsU6-20nt OsGS3-gRNA, obtained following the third LR recombination reaction) was mixed with 100 µl of competent, frozen Agrobacterium tumefaciens cells. The mixture was transferred to a pre-chilled 2 mm gap cuvette and subjected to electroporation at 1600 V for 5 milliseconds. Following electroporation, 1 ml of fresh LB medium was added to the bacterial suspension, which was then incubated at 28 °C for 1 h with continuous shaking to allow recovery. Subsequently, approximately ~ 50 µl of the transformed Agrobacterium cells were plated onto LB agar supplemented with kanamycin (50 mg/L) and rifampicin (15 mg/L). The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 48 h to enable the growth of putative transformants. The presence of the recombinant construct in the Agrobacterium colonies was confirmed by colony PCR using gene-specific forward and reverse primers (Fig. S1B).

Transformation procedure

Transfection

Isolated single bacterial colony were selected and cultured in 100 mL of Luria–Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with kanamycin (50 mg L⁻¹) and rifampicin (15 mg L⁻¹) as the primary culture medium. The inoculated broth was incubated at 28 °C for 16–18 h until the bacterial culture reached an optical density (OD) of approximately 0.6. Once this density was achieved, the primary culture was used to inoculate a secondary 100 mL culture containing the same concentrations of antibiotics. Upon reaching a bacterial density of OD = 0.6 in the secondary culture, centrifugation was performed at 6000 rpm for 6 min to obtain the pellet. Subsequently, the pellet was resuspended in resuspension medium at a ratio of 375 µL per 250 mL. The resuspension medium consisted of MS basal salts supplemented with sucrose (68 g/L), glucose (36 g/L), KCl (3 g/L), and MgCl₂ (4 g/L), with the pH adjusted to 5.2. Acetosyringone was added to a final concentration of 150 µM and was freshly prepared as a 1 M stock solution in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Following this, small pieces (2–3 mm) of callus were added to the solution, and the mixture was incubated for 25 min at 120 rpm and 28 °C. After incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and the calli were placed on sterile blotting paper before being transferred to the co-cultivation medium. The co-cultivation process was conducted in the dark at 28 °C for 48–72 h.

Washing

To enhance construct penetration, the calli were first incubated at 30–32 °C before removing excess Agrobacterium growth. They were then thoroughly rinsed with sterile distilled water 10–12 times until clear. Next, the calli were treated with 250 mg/L cefotaxime and incubated for 1 h; this treatment was repeated three times. Subsequently, the calli were washed again with water (2–3 washes at 5-minute intervals), blotted dry, and finally transferred to the primary selection medium.

Selection

During the initial selection phase, the calli were cultured on a medium supplemented with 250 mg/L cefotaxime, 50 mg/L hygromycin, and 500 mg/L carbenicillin, and incubated in the dark at 26 ± 2 °C for 15 days. After this period, the calli were transferred to a fresh secondary selection medium containing the same concentrations of antibiotics and maintained under identical conditions for an additional 10 days. Once microcalli developed, they were moved to a tertiary selection medium supplemented with 250 mg/L cefotaxime, 30 mg/L hygromycin, and 500 mg/L carbenicillin. This proliferative stage was continued for 7 days at 26 ± 2 °C in the dark to obtain well-proliferated calli.

Optimization of culture media for callus induction, plant regeneration, and acclimatization

Following surface sterilization, seeds were air-dried under aseptic conditions and placed on sterile filter paper to remove excess moisture. The dried seeds were then cultured on a callus induction medium formulated using Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal salts [46]. The callus induction medium consisted of MS basal salts supplemented with maltose (30 g L⁻¹) as the carbon source, casein hydrolysate (0.3 g/L), L-proline (0.6 g/L), and agar (0.8 g/L) as the gelling agent. The medium pH was adjusted to 5.8 prior to autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min. To optimize callus induction, ten hormonal treatments (T1–T10) were evaluated by supplementing the basal medium with varying concentrations and combinations of the auxin 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D) and the cytokinin 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP). A hormone-free MS medium was used as the control. The specific concentrations of growth regulators applied in each treatment are summarized in Table 1 (Fig. S1A and S1B).

The composition of various media employed for inducing callus formation

For each growth regulator combination, two carbohydrate sources were independently evaluated to assess their effects on callus induction efficiency. The complete composition of each treatment is summarized in Table 1. Cultures were incubated in complete darkness at 26 ± 1 °C, with each treatment comprising three independent biological replicates. Callus initiation was monitored regularly, and after 10–12 days, calli derived from mature embryos were excised and subcultured onto fresh MS medium for an additional 5–7 days to promote further proliferation. Callus induction efficiency was calculated as the percentage of explants forming callus relative to the total number of explants cultured. Callus morphology was assessed qualitatively based on texture (friable or compact), color, and nodularity, and structural features were further examined using light microscopy. Embryogenic calli were identified by characteristic morphological traits, including a compact, nodular structure and creamy-white to pale-yellow coloration. Data were recorded separately for the two rice genotypes (MTU-1010 and Lalat) and are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).

To identify the most effective regeneration protocol prior to transformation, calli from two varieties were excised from the callus induction medium and distributed across seven different regeneration media (RM). Each treatment was performed in triplicate. All cultures were incubated at 26 ± 2 °C under a 16/8-hour light/dark photoperiod. Once in vitro regeneration was initiated, the regenerated plantlets were transferred to MS basal medium to promote shoot proliferation. The regeneration response, indicated by the appearance of green spots in the different varieties, was monitored daily. Once plantlet initiation occurred, the regenerated plantlets were transplanted in MS basal media to encourage shoot proliferation. The number of regenerated shoots was recorded biweekly after transfer. To promote root elongation, the regenerated plantlets were then moved to half-strength basal MS medium, containing varied concentrations of different rooting hormones, after 10 days, sucrose was added to the medium. The regeneration efficiency was calculated as the percentage of callus samples that successfully regenerated into plantlets out of the total number of callus samples. Following this, the plantlet underwent a gentle wash with autoclaved double distilled water to thoroughly eliminate stick media, minimizing the risk of fungal infection. Subsequently, the in vitro plantlets were placed in test tubes for primary hardening (Fig. S1A and S1B). The plants were later transplanted into soil-filled pots to promote optimal growth for seed production. However, during the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, we modified the regeneration methods. Initially, we selected creamy white proliferated calli from the third selection medium and transferred them to regeneration medium 1 for 6–7 days under dark conditions. Afterward, they were transferred to RM5 following the best RM previously identified for shoot proliferation. Transformation efficiency is determined by calculating the total number of regenerated plantlets with positive hygromycin phosphotransferase II (hptII) PCR results to the total number of embryogenic calli used for Agrobacterium infection (Fig. S1A, S1B and S1C).

Evaluation of morphological and physiological characteristics during callus induction and plant regeneration

Several key traits were evaluated throughout the experiment to examine how different cultivars responded to the regeneration procedure. The evaluated traits included the day of callus induction, the number of contaminated and dead seeds, the percentage of dead seeds, the frequency and percentage of callus induction, the number of explants yielding embryogenic callus, browning rate (%), embryogenic frequency, plant regeneration frequency, and both the fresh and dry weights of calli aged between four and nine weeks.

Statistical analysis

The frequency of callus induction and plantlet regeneration was recorded across 3 replicates. Data analysis was conducted using R software version 22.0, mean differences were assessed using Tukey test.

Molecular characterization of potential transgenic plants

Leaf samples were gathered from the putative transgenic plants selected through hygromycin. The genomic DNA extracted using the cetyl tri-methyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method, as outlined by [47]. The extracted DNA underwent treatment with proteinase K and RNase. To confirm gRNA insertion in putative genome-edited (GE₀) lines of MTU-1010 and Lalat, PCR amplification was performed targeting the Cas9 and hptII genes [31]. PCR amplification of the Cas9 and hptII genes was carried out using genomic DNA isolated from GE₀ rice lines of MTU-1010 and Lalat. Each 50 µL PCR reaction contained 50 ng of rice genomic DNA, 5.0 µL of 10× PCR buffer with MgCl₂, 4.0 µL of dNTP mix (2.0 mM each; final concentration 200 µM), 2.0 µL each of forward and reverse primers specific to Cas9 or hptII (10 µM; approximately 150 ng each), 1.0 µL of Taq DNA polymerase (GeNei; 1 U), and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 50 µL. The primer pairs used for PCR amplification were as follows: Cas9 forward (5′-GTTGGGCGGTCGTTCATTCGTTC-3′) and reverse (5′-GAAGATCGGGTGCCTCTCGT-3′); hptII forward (5′-GCCTGAACTCACCGCGACG-3′) and reverse (5′-CAGCCATCGGTCCAGACG-3′). PCR amplification of the Cas9 and hptII gene targets was performed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 s, annealing at 60 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min 5 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. Amplified PCR products were resolved on 1.2% agarose gels and visualized using UV light.

Results

Design of guide RNAs for OsTMS5 in indica rice

In this study, OsTMS5 (LOC_Os02g12290) gene located on chromosome 2, was selected as the target for genome editing. For further analysis, the OsTMS5 nucleotide sequence was retrieved from the FASTA file available at (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu). The gene spans approximately 1.9 kb and contains a coding region of 909 bp. Two gRNAs were designed within the first exon of OsTMS5 to maximize knockout efficiency while minimizing potential off-target effects. The gRNAs were selected based on high on-target scores, optimal GC content (45–75%), and minimal predicted off-target sites. To facilitate cloning into the vector, BsaI enzyme restriction sites were added at the 5’ end at both DNA strands. The construct contains expression cassette of eSPCas9, labelled ZmUbiP-eSPCas9-NosT, were integrated into the pMDC99 vector using LR multiround Gateway cloning techniques. Finally, pMDC99-eSPCas9 + OsU6-OsTMS5-target1-gRNA+OsU6-OsTMS5-target2-gRNA was created. This construct was then mobilized into EHA105 strain of Agrobacterium for transformation in rice.

In this study, we optimized media formulations for callus induction, regeneration, and Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The aim was to improve regeneration efficiency from transformed embryogenic calli. Dehusked rice seeds were used as explants to induce embryogenic callus on callus induction medium (Fig. 1A-C).

Ten hormone combinations were evaluated for callus induction. Among all treatments, MS medium supplemented with 3.0 mg/L 2,4-D and 0.5 mg/L BAP (T9) yielded the highest callus induction frequencies in both cultivars, reaching 95.36 ± 0.64% in MTU-1010 and 94.10 ± 1.19% in Lalat (Fig. 2A-D; Table 1). Calli induced under T9 conditions were compact, nodular, and creamy white in appearance with characteristics indicative of highly embryogenic tissue suitable for genetic transformation (Fig. S2). Lower concentrations of growth regulators produced friable or loosely compact calli with significantly reduced induction frequencies, whereas supra-optimal auxin levels led to excessive callus compaction without a corresponding enhancement in embryogenic competence. Callus induction frequency was also evaluated in two indica rice varieties, MTU-1010 and Lalat (Fig. 2C & D). Importantly, maltose-based media consistently promoted the development of compact, highly embryogenic calli, outperforming sucrose as a carbon source (Table 1). Embryogenic calli suitable for Agrobacterium infection were obtained within 15–20 days (Fig. 1D). In contrast to previous reports that employed 3–5-week-old calli, we used younger (15–20-day-old) embryogenic calli for transformation. Subcultured calli were subsequently infected with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 carrying both gRNAs (Fig. 1E). Agrobacterium-infected embryogenic calli were co-cultivated at 28 °C for 48–72 h, followed by brief exposure to 32 °C. Calli were then washed with sterile water containing 250 mg/L cefotaxime and 500 mg/L carbenicillin to eliminate bacterial growth. The washed calli were placed on selection medium supplemented with 50 mg/L hygromycin. Surviving calli were transferred to a second selection medium with identical supplements and cultured for an additional 10 days under the same conditions. After the second selection, hygromycin-resistant microcalli were transferred to a third selection medium containing 250 mg/L cefotaxime, 500 mg/L carbenicillin, and a reduced hygromycin level of 30 mg/L (Table S1). During hygromycin selection, a proportion of embryogenic calli displayed partial browning, a common physiological response to selection pressure (Fig. 1F). Although several protocols recommend transferring only cream-white embryogenic calli, we progressed all embryogenic calli through the third selection cycle. Despite hygromycin selection pressure, 46.15% of MTU-1010 and 48.86% of Lalat co-cultivated calli maintained regeneration competence, underscoring the robustness of the regeneration system (Fig. 1G-H).

Hormonal balance is a key determinant of efficient plant regeneration

The presence and precise concentrations of growth hormones in the regeneration medium (RM) are critical determinants of regeneration frequency. To optimize regeneration efficiency, a multiple media formulations containing varied combinations of BAP, kinetin, and NAA were tested in both indica rice cultivars (Table 2). The regenerative response was markedly shaped by cytokinin–auxin interactions and genotype-specific effects.

Subsequently, selective transfer of creamy white embryogenic calli to RM 4 resulted in vigorous callus proliferation during a 5–6-day dark incubation period, prior to subsequent culture on RM 5 (Fig. 1I; Table 2). RM5 cultures were maintained under a 16 h light and 8 h dark cycle (Fig. 1J). Agro-infected calli exhibited a significantly accelerated regeneration response compared with non-infected control calli. It was observed that higher concentrations of BAP, in combination with kinetin and NAA, significantly enhanced plant regeneration (Table 2). A stepwise reduction in hygromycin concentration during regeneration and rooting further enhanced plant recovery by minimizing selection-induced stress on transformed tissues (Figs. 1L and 2E–F). The superior regeneration observed in Lalat compared with MTU-1010 may be attributed to inherent genotype-specific differences in responsiveness to the regeneration conditions.

Establishment, and acclimatization of genome-edited plants

In order for establishment and acclimatization of genome-edited plants, regenerated plantlets were carefully excised after 25–30 days of incubation using sterile forceps to minimize damage to the developing root system. Residual culture medium was meticulously removed by washing the roots under running tap water. Plantlets exhibiting well-developed shoots and roots were initially transferred to a hydroponic system (Fig. 1M) and subsequently transplanted into potting soil to achieve successful acclimatization under ex vitro conditions. Subsequently, the rooted shoots were transplanted into pots containing a mixture of soil, farmyard manure (FYM), and sand in a 1:1:1 ratio to promote further hardening (Fig. N–P). The pots were then maintained in the transgenic house for subsequent phenotypic evaluation.

Validation of transgenic plants and assessment of transformation efficiency in elite indica rice cultivars

The integration of the transgene in regenerated putative T₀ transgenic plants of both Lalat and MTU-1010 cultivars was confirmed. Using the optimized regeneration medium, a total of 17 transgenic plants were obtained for Lalat and 10 for MTU-1010. Initial screening with the hptII selectable marker identified 27 hygromycin-resistant T₀ lines. All putative transgenic lines were further validated by PCR using hptII-specific primers (Fig. 3; Fig. S3).

Discussion

Genetic transformation is a key strategy for improving economically important traits in rice and provides a powerful platform for the identification and functional characterization of genes governing complex traits. Among available methods, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation is the most widely employed approach for rice genetic engineering across laboratories worldwide. However, despite its broad applicability, gene transfer efficiency via Agrobacterium remains relatively low, particularly in indica rice cultivars. Notably, Agrobacterium-mediated transformation offers the unique advantage of delivering defined DNA fragments from specific plasmids into the host genome, enabling precise and stable genetic modification [48]. This distinctive characteristic enables the efficient insertion of single-copy DNA fragments into host cells, which can lead to more stable and predictable gene expression compared with rearranged inserts or multiple transgene copies [49]. By recognizing the importance of possessing an efficacious transformation protocol for functional genomics research, we standardized an optimized method specifically tailored for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of indica rice cultivars. Despite the high regeneration potential reported for the japonica cultivar TP-309, transformation efficiency in indica rice remains comparatively low, underscoring the need for cultivar-specific optimization [50]. In this research, we developed and optimized an Agrobacterium-mediated transformation protocol for indica rice cultivars, with specific emphasis on Lalat and MTU-1010. Embryogenic calli derived from mature seeds were employed for targeted editing of the OsTMS5 gene using a CRISPR/Cas9 construct carrying the gRNA and the hptII selectable marker. The primary objective was to enhance the efficiency of plant regeneration from transformed embryogenic calli. Dehusked mature rice seeds were used as explants to induce embryogenic callus formation on callus induction medium (Fig. 1A-C). During callus induction, embryogenic callus formation was significantly higher in the T9 medium compared with the other nine treatments (Fig. 2A-D; Fig. S2 and Table 1). Callus induction frequency was quantified for the two indica rice cultivars, MTU-1010 and Lalat (Fig. 2C & D). The regeneration potential of totipotent calli is often reduced following Agrobacterium infection and genetic transformation [44]. Therefore, this study aimed to optimize both callus induction and regeneration media compositions to enhance plant regeneration efficiency from transformed calli. Dehusked rice seeds were used as explants to induce embryogenic callus formation on callus induction medium (Table 1). Rice seeds were selected as explants due to their year-round availability. Moreover, embryogenic calli derived from rice seeds are expected to exhibit enhanced regeneration potential, as reported in previous studies [51]. Media supplemented with maltose as the carbohydrate source promoted the formation of compact embryogenic calli more effectively than sucrose-containing media (Table 1). Calli suitable for Agrobacterium infection were obtained within 15–20 days of culture (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, the subcultured embryogenic calli were infected with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 harboring the CRISPR/Cas9 construct (Fig. 1E). In contrast to previous studies that employed 3–5-week-old calli for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, this study utilized younger embryogenic calli aged 15–20 days [41, 52, 53]. Additionally, previous studies have reported that the use of early-stage calli can enhance transformation efficiency [54]. Calli from secondary cultures aged 18–30 days are commonly used as explants for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation in rice [38, 51, 55, 56]. The early scutellum imitative calli from the Nipponbare cultivar of japonica rice exhibited greater competence, achieving successful transformation after only one day of pre-culture [54]. Improved transformation efficiency has been reported in young calli compared with three-week-old calli derived from the indica rice cultivar BPT 5204 [57]. However, transformation efficiency in indica rice cultivars remains substantially lower than that observed in japonica cultivars. Both transformation and regeneration processes in indica rice are influenced by multiple factors and are highly genotype dependent. Therefore, optimizing culture conditions in conjunction with the transformation protocol is essential to achieve high-frequency transformation in indica rice. Among the various factors affecting monocot transformation, the use of vigorously proliferating embryogenic calli has been identified as a critical determinant of transformation efficiency [49]. It was observed that the regeneration frequency was higher in 6-day-old calli than in 21-day-old calli derived from the indica rice cultivar BPT 5204 [57]. In the standard rice transformation protocol, the infection step typically employs 5-day-old calli derived from the japonica cultivar Nipponbare [54]. Similarly, high transformation frequencies were observed in 6-day-old calli from the indica cultivar BPT 5204 and in 7-day-old primary calli from the indica cultivar Kasalath [57, 58].

The Agrobacterium-infected embryogenic calli were co-cultivated for 48–72 h at 28 °C. Immediately prior to washing, the calli were exposed to a temperature of 32 °C, a treatment that contributed to enhanced transformation efficiency. Pre-infection heat treatment and centrifugation of calli have been reported to improve transformation efficiency across several crops, including wheat, rice, sorghum, and maize [59–61]. Transformation efficiency in early scutellum-derived calli can be affected by multiple factors. In rice, one reported approach involves using N6 liquid medium to moisten filter paper during co-cultivation with Agrobacterium [62]. Moistening filter paper with liquid medium during explant co-cultivation has been identified as an effective strategy to regulate Agrobacterium growth. The use of filter paper during co-cultivation has also been shown to improve explant health by suppressing tissue necrosis, thereby leading to increased transformation efficiency, as demonstrated in cucumber [63]. The use of filter paper wicks during co-cultivation has been shown to limit overgrowth of Agrobacterium, thereby improving transformation efficiency in Cryptomeria japonica [64]. A resting period after co-cultivation is a common feature of numerous plant transformation protocols [65, 66]. In this work, we followed co-cultivation, embryogenic calli were thoroughly washed with sterile water supplemented with cefotaxime (250 mg/L) and carbenicillin (500 mg/L) to eliminate residual Agrobacterium. The calli were then air-dried on sterile filter paper before transfer to the selection medium (Table S1). During hygromycin selection, a subset of embryogenic calli developed a brownish phenotype (Fig. 1F). Although some protocols recommend transferring only cream-white embryogenic calli to subsequent selection stages, all embryogenic calli were retained and advanced through the third round of selection. At the third hygromycin selection stage, 46.15% of MTU-1010 and 48.86% of Lalat co-cultivated calli remained viable and competent for regeneration (Fig. 1G–H). Our results further demonstrated that both the presence and concentration of growth regulators in the RM are critical determinants of regeneration frequency. To enhance regeneration efficiency in both indica rice cultivars, systematic optimization of growth hormone composition and concentration was performed. Auxin and cytokinin combinations, including NAA, kinetin, and BAP, were evaluated at varying concentrations in the RM (Table 2). Subsequently, only healthy, cream-white calli were transferred to regeneration medium 1 and incubated in darkness for 5–6 days before being moved to the optimized RM (Fig. 1I). The highest regeneration frequency was achieved on RM-5, consisting of MS salts supplemented with 30 g/L maltose, 0.25 mg/L NAA, 2 mg/L BAP, 0.5 mg/L kinetin, 10 g/L agarose, 15 mg/L hygromycin, and 250 mg/L cefotaxime (pH 5.8) (Fig. 1J–K; Table 2). Notably, a significant improvement in plantlet regeneration efficiency was observed upon reducing hygromycin concentration from 30 mg/L in RM-4 to 15 mg/L in RM-5, with a further reduction to 10 mg/L in the rooting medium (Figs. 1L and 2E–F). Excessive antibiotic concentrations can effectively eliminate non-transformed cells but may also impair the growth and regeneration of transformed cells [67]. Initiation of direct selection immediately after co-cultivation resulted in browning of the infected calli. In contrast, the introduction of a 4-day resting period effectively eliminated residual Agrobacterium and improved the viability of transformed cells within the calli [68]. It was observed that cracking the calli before one month of culture induced phenolic exudation and tissue browning, which subsequently reduced callus proliferation and impaired somatic embryogenesis [69].

Several studies have demonstrated that, in japonica rice transformation, specific medium components particularly carbon and nitrogen sources and gelling agents, play critical roles in achieving optimal transformation outcomes [51, 52]. Hence, these medium constituents were maintained unchanged in the present protocol. Maltose was used as the sole carbon source during the plant regeneration stage, while a combination of sucrose and maltose was employed during subsequent growth phases (Tables 1 and 2; Table S1). Proline served as the nitrogen source, and agar was consistently used as the gelling agent throughout media preparation. Recognizing the pivotal role of phytohormones in plant regeneration, auxin and cytokinin concentrations in the regeneration medium were systematically optimized (Fig. 2E, F). Multiple RM formulations were developed by varying the levels of BAP, kinetin, NAA, and hygromycin, while keeping all other components constant (Table 2). Enhanced plant regeneration was achieved when elevated levels of BAP were applied in an optimal ratio with kinetin and NAA (Table 2). Notably, Lalat displayed a higher regeneration frequency than MTU-1010, likely reflecting genotype-dependent variation in regenerative competence. Auxins such as NAA are well known to promote somatic embryogenesis; therefore, initiating regeneration under dark conditions was essential for efficient somatic embryo development, given the light sensitivity of NAA. The highest plant regeneration efficiency was observed in RM-5, consistent with previous reports in diverse japonica rice cultivars [41, 52, 54].

The transformation efficiency was evaluated in both rice cultivars, with MTU-1010 and Lalat exhibiting efficiencies of 29.62% and 37.20%, respectively (Table 3). Comparable transformation efficiencies of approximately 45% have previously been reported using 18-day-old calli derived from multiple indica rice cultivars [51]. In a separate study, a transformation efficiency of 15.33% was achieved in an indica rice cultivar using 24-day-old calli [56]. Further, a transformation efficiency of 8.3% was reported for the indica rice cultivar MDU 5 [70]. Early-stage scutellum-derived calli provide multiple benefits, such as improved selection of transformed cells, enhanced transformation efficiency, reduced culture duration, and minimized somaclonal variation [54, 57]. PCR analysis was performed to evaluate transgenicity, targeting the hptII and Cas9 genes (Fig. 3; Fig. S3). Integration of the transgene was verified in the putative transgenic lines of Lalat and MTU-1010. Overall, the optimized protocol provides significant benefits, including elevated regeneration rates (87–90%) and improved transformation efficiencies (29–37%), especially in indica rice cultivars such as MTU-1010 and Lalat. Furthermore, the presented protocol employs mature rice seeds, effectively overcoming limitations associated with alternative explant sources. Mature seeds are readily available year-round and can be stored for extended periods while maintaining viability. Thus, our protocol achieved a notable transformation efficiency of approximately 37%, even when using embryogenic calli derived from mature seeds. Such an efficient transformation system is invaluable for advancing functional genomics in rice and is particularly critical for the effective application of emerging genome editing tools, including CRISPR/Cas9.

Conclusion

Genetic transformation plays a pivotal role in improving crops for value-added traits, ultimately culminating in the creation of genetically engineered transgenic or genome-edited crops. Despite substantial efforts, the task of achieving genetic transformation in rice remains challenging due to the significant variability in its reproducibility. In this work, we have optimized an efficient protocol for genetically transforming rice with the aim of producing genome-edited lines. Our study demonstrates the feasibility of achieving both high regeneration and high editing efficiency in MTU-1010 and Lalat varieties using CRISPR-Cas9, with somatic embryos via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. By utilizing the mature seed protocol, which includes all required media for callus induction, co-cultivation, selection, and regeneration, we successfully produce transgenic plants in just 94 days. The optimized medium yielded a high regeneration rate of 90.28% for Lalat and 87.51% for MTU-1010, suggesting the potential applicability of this medium to other indica rice varieties. Overall, the detailed stepwise protocol for rice transformation is user-friendly and effective in generating both transgenic and gene-edited plants, providing significant potential for crop improvement. Therefore, this study not only facilitates further exploration of additional OsTMS5 gene in the background of Lalat and MTU-1010 but also opens avenues for exploring functional genomics and molecular breeding in other indica rice cultivars through genome editing technique in the future.
