Development and validation of prognostic models for bone metastasis in Non-Small cell lung cancer based on Machine learning algorithms
Authors: Jiabin Fang, Xiaojie Yang, Lingfeng Chen, Liuying Hong, Yingqiu He, Ji Huang, Jie Lin, Nengluan Xu, Hongru Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Bone metastasis, Nomogram

Introduction

One of the most prevalent malignancies in the world, lung cancer claims the lives of around 1.76 million people yearly and causes over 2 million new instances of diagnosis [1]. The majority of lung cancer cases are of the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) type. In advanced stages of NSCLC, bone is a primary location of metastases, with approximately 30–40 % of NSCLC patients developing bone metastasis during disease progression. The median overall survival for patients with NSCLC and bone metastases often ranges from 5 to 11.5 months [2]. Although significant progress has been made in the overall management of metastatic NSCLC, bone metastasis remains a common cause of increased mortality among lung cancer patients [3].Fig. 1.

Study design flowchart.

According to earlier statistics, 17.2 % of patients with lung cancer are initially diagnosed with bone metastases [4]. In addition to causing pain, bone metastases can result in skeletal-related events (SREs), which can significantly impair a patient's quality of life. These occurrences include pathological fractures, orthopedic surgeries, bone-targeting radiation, and spinal cord compression. Tumor stage, histological type, lung cancer symptom ratings, and performance status scores are some of the factors that affect a patient's prognosis. These factors may result in different treatment outcomes, making it beneficial to fully assess individual characteristics when considering personalized treatment. However, there are currently few research on prognostic models specifically designed for individuals with lung cancer who had bone metastases at initial diagnosis. The relationship between inflammation and cancer has long been widely discussed [5]. In recent decades, numerous studies have established correlations between systemic inflammation and tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis across diverse cancer types [6]. In recent years, inflammatory markers like the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been linked to prognosis across various cancer types. This project aims to collect clinical data and systemic inflammatory markers from patients, utilizing machine learning algorithms to create a prognostic nomogram for predicting overall survival at 6, 12, and 24 months in NSCLC patients with bone metastases at initial diagnosis.

Methods

Study Population

A retrospective study was conducted on 1299 lung cancer patients who underwent high-throughput gene sequencing at Fujian Provincial Hospital between 2016 and 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically confirmed non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC); (3) initial diagnosis of lung cancer with concomitant bone metastases; and (4) availability of complete clinical data, including age, EGFR, ALK, BRAF, and KRAS mutation status, as well as information on distant metastases. The exclusion criteria were: (1) history of other or concurrent malignancies (excluding lung cancer); (2) indeterminate histological type; and (3) follow-up time less than one month after diagnosis. Ultimately, 195 patients with NSCLC and bone metastases at initial diagnosis met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study.

Clinical data

All data were extracted from the electronic medical record system of Fujian Provincial Hospital. The information gathered included patients’ sociodemographic data (sex, age, BMI, smoking history, medical history, primary site of lung cancer, histological type, TNM stage, and presence of brain, liver, or kidney metastases); clinical characteristics (pre-treatment serum indicators such as alkaline phosphatase [ALP], lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], serum calcium [Ca2+], total cholesterol [TC], carcinoembryonic antigen [CEA]); systemic inflammation indices including NLR, PLR, LMR, AAPR, SII, PIV, dNLR, PAR, MAR, PNI, and SIRI; gene mutation information including EGFR, ALK, BRAF, and KRAS mutation status; and details of first-line treatment.

Systemic inflammatory markers

Systemic inflammatory markers were derived from the gathered clinical data and encompass the following indices: LMR (Lymphocyte-to-Monocyte Ratio), AAPR (Albumin-to-ALP Ratio), SII (Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index), PIV (Pan-Immune-Inflammation Value), dNLR (Derived Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio), PAR (Platelet-to-Albumin Ratio), MAR (Monocyte-to-Albumin Ratio), PNI (Prognostic Nutritional Index), and SIRI (Systemic Inflammatory Response Index). The specific formulas are as follows: NLR = Neutrophil count (109/L) / Lymphocyte count (109/L); PLR = Platelet count (109/L) / Lymphocyte count (109/L); LMR = Lymphocyte count (109/L) / Monocyte count (109/L); AAPR = Albumin (g/L) / Alkaline Phosphatase (U/L); SII = Platelet count (109/L) × Neutrophil count (109/L) / Lymphocyte count (109/L); PIV = Neutrophil count (109/L) × Platelet count (109/L) × Monocyte count (109/L) / Lymphocyte count (109/L); dNLR = Neutrophil count (109/L) / [White blood cell count (109/L) − Neutrophil count (109/L)]; PAR = Platelet count (109/L) / Albumin (g/L); MAR = Monocyte count (109/L) / Albumin (g/L); PNI = Serum albumin (g/dL) + 5 × Lymphocyte count (109/L); SIRI = Neutrophil count (109/L) × Monocyte count (109/L) / Lymphocyte count (109/L).

Multiple imputation

For variables with less than 30 % missing data, multiple imputation was performed using the mice package to reduce bias and preserve sample size. Some clinical and biochemical indicators collected in this study had missing values, including: age (0.08 %), BMI (1.70 %), tumor size (0.00 %), neutrophils (5.49 %), lymphocytes (5.49 %), monocytes (5.49 %), platelets (5.49 %), white blood cells (5.49 %), albumin (ALB, 5.87 %), ALP (10.90 %), TC (6.57 %), LDH (6.18 %), serum Ca2+ (5.95 %), CEA (9.20 %), and gene mutations including EGFR (0.54 %), ALK (0.54 %), BRAF (0.54 %), and KRAS (0.54 %). To mitigate the impact of absent data on the outcomes, multiple imputation was conducted using the random forest method within the mice package. Furthermore, the consistency and completeness of the imputed data were evaluated to ensure that no additional bias was introduced during the data processing.

Follow-Up

Patients’ clinical status was monitored through outpatient visits, inpatient medical records, and telephone follow-ups. Comprehensive data were collected, including the date of lung cancer diagnosis, treatment details, survival status, time of bone metastasis diagnosis, and sites of metastasis (in patients with bone involvement). The observation period began at the time of lung cancer diagnosis and ended at either the last follow-up or death, whichever occurred first. Patients were followed on a monthly basis, with the most recent follow-up conducted on November 10, 2024. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from lung cancer diagnosis to death or last follow-up.

Model construction and validation

Potential variables included sex, age, BMI, smoking history, medical history, primary site of lung cancer, histological type, T stage, N stage, presence of metastases at other sites, pre-treatment serum biomarkers (ALP, LDH, serum Ca2+, TC, CEA), inflammatory indices (NLR, PLR, AAPR, SII, PIV, dNLR, PAR, MAR, PNI, SIRI), and gene mutation information (EGFR, ALK, BRAF, KRAS mutations). These variables were screened using three machine learning algorithms: LASSO regression, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest. The intersection of variables selected by all three algorithms was used as the final set of predictive factors. To graphically represent the model, a nomogram was made with rms package. The concordance index (C-index), calibration plots, decision curve analysis (DCA), and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were used to assess its performance. Bootstrap resampling was used for internal validation of the model.

Statistical analysis

All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R software (version 4.0.2). Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed using the independent Student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed variables. Depending on the features of the data, either Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test was used for comparisons of categorical data.

Baseline characteristics

For the 195 eligible patients who took part in the trial, the median follow-up period was 9.0 months. (IQR: 4–17), during which 41 patients (21.03 %) died (Fig. Supplementary Table 1). The average age was 62.8 years, and 65.6 % were male. Additionally, 26.7 % had brain metastases, 16.4 % had liver metastases, and 10.3 % had pancreatic metastases. EGFR gene mutations were present in 55.9 % of patients, and ALK mutations were identified in 6.7 % of cases.

Selection of predictive model variables

Three machine learning algorithms (LASSO regression, XGBoost, and Random Forest) were applied to independently identify prognostic variables associated with survival in NSCLC patients with bone metastases. In the LASSO regression model, 14 variables with non-zero coefficients were selected at the minimum lambda value (λ min = 0.03515). These variables included age, sex, pathological type, N stage, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, concurrent liver metastases, metastases to other sites, ALP level, serum calcium level, EGFR mutation, ALK mutation, MAR, and PNI (Fig. 2a). The XGBoost model highlighted the top 15 most influential predictors as age, SIRI, LDH, serum calcium level, BMI, MAR, PNI, PAR, TC, concurrent metastases to other sites, N stage, KRAS mutation, EGFR mutation, PIV, and dNLR (Fig. 2b). Using a random forest model with 500 trees, the top 15 predictors were identified as SIRI, age, KRAS mutation, PNI, PIV, LDH, MAR, NLR, pathological type, PAR, PLR, ALP, SII, AAPR, and serum calcium level (Fig. 2c). By intersecting the results across the three algorithms, four consistently significant prognostic variables were ultimately identified: PNI, MAR, serum calcium level, and age.

(b) The top 15 predictors were identified based on importance scores using the XGBoost model. (c) The top 15 important predictors were selected using the Random Forest model. Model construction and validation

The four selected factors were entered into a multivariate Cox regression model, which revealed that age had a p-value of 0.07, whereas PNI, MAR, and serum calcium levels were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Given that numerous prior studies have identified age as an independent prognostic factor [7,8], all four variables (PNI, MAR, serum calcium level, and age) were incorporated into the final nomogram (Fig. 3a). The nomogram achieved C-indices of 80.3 %, 73.6 %, and 71.6 % for predicting 6-month, 1-year, and 2-year survival, respectively. The corresponding AUCs were 0.865 (95 % CI: 0.800–0.931), 0.783 (95 % CI: 0.675–0.891), and 0.779 (95 % CI: 0.659–0.898) (Fig. 3b). Internal validation with 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded C-indices of 77.8 %, 71.6 %, and 69.0 % for the same time points. Furthermore, calibration curves generated from 1,000 bootstrap iterations demonstrated strong concordance between predicted and observed OS probabilities, indicating good model calibration (Fig. 3c).

Risk stratification using the nomogram

A violin plot was generated, showing that the risk scores of deceased cases were significantly higher than those of surviving cases in the cohort (Fig. 4). The median risk score of 0.96 from the model was utilized to categorize patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. The two groups' overall survival rates differed significantly, according to the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. (Fig. 5, p < 0.0001).

Additionally, given that this cohort consists predominantly of Chinese patients and has a relatively high EGFR mutation rate, we performed subgroup analyses by EGFR mutation status. The model demonstrated good discriminative performance in both EGFR mutation positive and EGFR wild type subgroups (Fig. S1). Among patients who were ALK wild type, the model likewise showed good discriminative ability (Fig. S2). We further conducted subgroup analyses by treatment modality, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (Fig. S3). The findings indicate that the model’s risk score can identify patients likely to benefit from targeted therapy or immunotherapy; however, it showed limited discriminative ability in the chemotherapy subgroup (p = 0.064).

Discussion

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [9]. Patients with bone metastases from lung cancer have a poor prognosis, with highly variable and difficult-to-predict survival outcomes. Studies indicate that approximately 80 % of patients with metastatic cancer wish to be informed of their expected survival [10]. In this study, we developed a machine learning–based predictive model for patients newly diagnosed with lung cancer and bone metastases. The model facilitates individualized risk stratification, helping clinicians identify NSCLC patients with favorable prognoses and guide treatment decisions.

Based on the results of the three machine learning algorithms, age, serum calcium level, PNI, and MAR were ultimately included as predictive factors in the model. Among them, PNI and MAR serve as markers of systemic inflammation. Consistent with previous studies, this work demonstrated that a low PNI and a high MAR were associated with poorer prognosis in NSCLC patients. PNI, defined as the ratio of serum albumin concentration to peripheral blood lymphocyte count [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]], has repeatedly been shown to predict outcomes in solid tumors. Xu et al. [17] further reported that a low PNI independently predicts worse survival in lung cancer patients with bone metastases. MAR, defined as the ratio of monocytes to albumin, has also been implicated as a prognostic marker. In a retrospective study by Zhao et al. [18], which included 195 NSCLC patients and 204 healthy volunteers, MAR levels were significantly higher in NSCLC patients compared with healthy individuals, suggesting that MAR may serve as a potential biomarker for early NSCLC detection. Serum albumin itself reflects long-term nutritional status, and its synthesis can be suppressed by both systemic inflammation [[18], [19], [20], [21]] and malnutrition [22,23]. Chronic systemic inflammation is strongly associated with progressive nutritional and functional decline in cancer patients, ultimately contributing to poor prognosis [21]. Lymphocytes, as key immune cells, play a central role in immune surveillance by inhibiting tumor cell growth, invasion, and metastasis [24]. Thus, a reduced lymphocyte count reflects impaired immune function, which is also linked to adverse clinical outcomes.

Macrophages are a vital component of the tumor microenvironment and are often polarized toward the M2 phenotype. M2 macrophages facilitate angiogenesis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby accelerating tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [25]. Elevated peripheral monocyte levels have been shown to correlate with the generation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and a high tumor burden, potentially leading to unfavorable prognosis [5]. Moreover, several studies have reported that, compared with healthy individuals, lung cancer patients exhibit significantly increased levels of circulating monocytes and monocyte-derived cytokines, and that elevated pre-treatment monocyte levels are associated with reduced survival in lung adenocarcinoma [26].

This study demonstrated that all three machine learning algorithms identified age as a significant predictive variable. In the subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis, the p-value for age was 0.07, which may reflect bias due to the relatively small sample size. Multiple studies have established that age is a significant predictive risk factor in lung cancer patients [27,28]. Elderly patients often have multiple comorbidities and diminished physiological resilience compared to younger individuals. Furthermore, with increasing age, prolonged exposure to various risk factors leads to the accumulation of genetic mutations [[29], [30], [31]]. Thus, age is recognized as an independent predictive factor in cancer patients and was included in the model of this investigation because of its clinical importance. In addition, this study also suggests that peripheral serum calcium levels are associated with patient prognosis. Hypercalcemia is one of the hallmark features of bone metastases and represents the most common metabolic complication in malignancies. Its primary mechanisms include localized osteolysis induced by tumor cells and increased renal tubular calcium reabsorption. Studies have shown [32] that hypercalcemia is a negative prognostic indicator in patients with bone metastases, with a median survival time of 10–12 weeks. This may be ascribed to its ability to induce serious consequences such as arrhythmias and acute renal failure, a conclusion consistent with our findings.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study. Although our hospital is one of the larger comprehensive hospitals in China’s Fujian Province and the included cases are reasonably representative, the single-center design makes it difficult to preclude potential selection and information biases. Second, the sample size was relatively small. During model development, we employed machine-learning algorithms for variable selection to mitigate collinearity among prognostic factors and reduce overfitting, and we conducted internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples to improve the reliability of the internal validation estimates. In the future, we will incorporate multicenter, large-sample data to further develop and validate the model.

Conclusion

The clinical prognostic model constructed based on machine learning algorithms using PNI, MAR, serum Ca2+, and age as prognostic variables demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability. As a result, it can serve as a tool for determining survival in patients with bone metastases and non-small cell lung cancer.
