Sodium bicarbonate therapy in severe metabolic acidemia: an individual patient data meta-analysis of the BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2 trials
Authors: Maxime Fosset, Joris Pensier, Matthieu Jabaudon, Laurent Bitker, Audrey De Jong, Gerald Chanques, Helena Huguet, Nicolas Molinari, Samir Jaber, Boris Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTwo randomized trials (BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2) evaluated intravenous sodium bicarbonate therapy in patients with severe metabolic acidemia but yielded inconclusive results. We performed an individual patient data meta-analysis to assess its effects on 90-day mortality and renal replacement therapy (RRT) use, and search for heterogeneity of treatment effects across prespecified subgroups.MethodsWe performed an individual patient data meta-analysis of the BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2 trials, including adults with severe metabolic acidemia (pH ≤ 7.20). Patients were randomized to receive intravenous sodium bicarbonate titrated to a pH ≥ 7.30 or no sodium bicarbonate. The primary outcome was 90-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included RRT initiation and dialysis-free days. Prespecified subgroup analyses explored treatment-effect heterogeneity by acidemia depth (pH ≤ 7.10 vs. > 7.10), severe acute kidney injury (AKI) status, and serum lactate (< 2mmol/L vs. ≥2mmol/L).ResultsA total of 1,016 patients was included (509 sodium bicarbonate, 507 control). Sodium bicarbonate therapy did not significantly reduce 90-day mortality compared to control (58.3% vs. 60.6%; risk ratio [RR], 0.96; 95%CI, 0.86–1.07; p = 0.51). However, it significantly reduced RRT initiation (34.8% vs. 50.7%; RR, 0.69; 95%CI, 0.60–0.79; p < 0.001, number needed to treat: 6.3) and increased dialysis-free days (incidence rate ratio, 1.10; 95%CI, 1.06–1.14; p < 0.001). A significant interaction was observed with acidemia depth (p-for-interaction = 0.006): in patients with pH ≤ 7.10, sodium bicarbonate reduced 90-day mortality (RR, 0.80; 95%CI, 0.68–0.93; p = 0.004); no benefit was seen with pH > 7.10 (RR, 1.05; 95%CI, 0.92–1.21; p = 0.47). No significant interactions were observed for severe AKI status (p-for-interaction = 0.11) nor serum lactate (p-for-interaction = 0.22).ConclusionsSodium bicarbonate therapy did not reduce overall 90-day mortality but decreased RRT use and suggested a mortality benefit in patients with the most profound acidemia (pH ≤ 7.10). These findings invite a reconsideration of current paradigms, suggesting timely correction of severe metabolic acidemia to avoid RRT in some patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06206-3.

Background

Severe metabolic acidemia (pH ≤ 7.20) is a frequent condition among critically ill patients, accompanying diverse conditions such as sepsis, shock, renal failure, or toxin accumulation [1]. Profound acidemia impairs cardiac contractility, triggers arrhythmias, leads to systemic vasodilation, cerebral edema, or diaphragmatic dysfunction [2]. Consequently, critically ill patients with severe metabolic acidemia experience high mortality rates, up to 50% [1, 2]. These adverse effects underscore the clinical rationale for prompt correction of severe acidemia in the intensive care unit (ICU). Beyond treatment of the underlying cause, intravenous sodium bicarbonate is a possible therapy to buffer acidemia [2]. However, its use remains controversial, owing to limited evidence and divergent clinical practices [3, 4]. Until recently, no randomized controlled trials had examined whether sodium bicarbonate therapy improves outcomes. Consequently, practice has been variable: some teams routinely administer sodium bicarbonate to raise arterial pH, whereas others avoid it except in specific situations [1, 3].

In 2018, the BICAR-ICU trial [5] provided the first large randomized data on this issue. In 389 critically ill patients with severe metabolic acidemia (pH ≤ 7.20) across 26 ICUs, sodium bicarbonate titrated to target pH ≥ 7.30 did not significantly improve the primary composite outcome of 28-day mortality or persistent organ failure compared to usual care without sodium bicarbonate. No difference of long-term patient-centered outcomes, including quality of life and 5-year mortality was found [6]. However, in a prespecified subgroup of patients with AKI, sodium bicarbonate therapy was associated with improved 28-day mortality and decreased incidence of RRT in the sodium bicarbonate group [5]. This finding suggests that sodium bicarbonate may postpone or prevent the need for renal replacement therapy in some patients by correcting acidemia, though the trial’s overall neutral result left the question of a mortality benefit unresolved [7]. Reflecting this uncertainty, current guidelines offer only a weak recommendation for using intravenous sodium bicarbonate in patients with severe acidemia (pH ≤ 7.20) complicated by shock and acute kidney injury [8].

The subsequent BICAR-ICU2 [9] randomized controlled trial targeted the AKI population. Enrolling 640 ICU patients with severe metabolic acidemia and moderate-to-severe AKI in 43 centers, BICAR-ICU2 reported no significant difference in 90-day mortality between the sodium bicarbonate and control arms [9]. However, sodium bicarbonate therapy yielded a substantial reduction in the need for RRT [9].

Both trials were potentially underpowered to detect smaller yet clinically important effects on mortality [10]. Moreover, important questions remain about heterogeneity of treatment effect, as patient subsets might derive greater benefit from sodium bicarbonate therapy [2, 10]. To address these knowledge gaps, we performed an individual patient data meta-analysis of BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2, pooling patient-level data from both trials. Our primary objective was to evaluate the effect of sodium bicarbonate therapy on 90-day mortality. Our secondary aims were to assess the need for RRT, and to conduct targeted subgroup analyses to explore treatment-effect heterogeneity, to inform the ongoing controversy and guide clinicians on the role of sodium bicarbonate in managing severe metabolic acidemia in the ICU.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This study is an investigator-initiated individual patient data meta-analysis combining two multicenter randomized controlled trials: BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2 [5, 9]. Both trials evaluated intravenous sodium bicarbonate therapy (4.2% solution) versus no sodium bicarbonate in critically ill patients with severe metabolic acidemia [5, 11]. The study design was a priori defined, with a protocol registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251229254), and conducted in accordance with PRISMA-IPD guidelines [12]. A steering committee consisting of the original trials’ investigators (BJ, SJ, NM, HH, GC, ADJ) oversaw the study design, data collection, and analysis [5, 9]. The study was approved by the local ethic committee (ref: 2025-02-205). Each contributing trial received appropriate ethics committee approvals and informed consent per local regulations. De-identified patient-level data from both trials were obtained with permission of the principal investigators and sponsors.

Eligibility criteria

We included the two available randomized controlled trials (BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2) [5, 9], both evaluating intravenous sodium bicarbonate infusion for metabolic acidemia in critically ill patients (Fig. S1). A systematic search of MEDLINE, CENTRAL, and Web of Science identified no additional eligible published randomized trials meeting the predefined criteria between 2000 and November 11, 2025 (. Two reviewers (MF, JP) independently assessed trial eligibility based on titles, abstracts, and full-text reports. Our search additionally identified two trials of sodium bicarbonate that did not meet the pre-specified eligibility criteria. The trial by Chen et al. compared two sodium bicarbonate titration strategies without a standard group [13]. The SODa-BIC pilot trial enrolled patients with moderate metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.30), a less severe eligibility threshold than the pre-specified pH ≤ 7.20 criterion [14].

In both included trials, eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) in the ICU for < 48 h with severe metabolic acidemia [5, 9]. Severe acidemia was defined by an arterial pH ≤ 7.20 in the absence of respiratory acidemia (partial pressure of CO₂≤ 45 mmHg) and a serum bicarbonate concentration ≤ 20 mmol/L [5, 9]. Patients also were required to have evidence of acute illness severity, defined as either a total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥ 4 or an arterial lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L [5, 9]. The BICAR-ICU2 trial had one additional inclusion criterion: patients must have had moderate-to-severe AKI at enrollment, defined as stage 2 or 3 AKI by KDIGO criteria [9]. In the first trial, AKI was not required for inclusion, though 49% of BICAR-ICU patients had AKI stage 2–3 at baseline [5].

Exclusion criteria were similar in both trials [5, 9]. Patients were excluded if they had primary respiratory acidemia or hypercapnic respiratory failure, or if metabolic acidemia was due to an isolated bicarbonate loss or specific treatable cause. Patients with substantial bicarbonate losses from the gastrointestinal tract or urinary tract, those with diabetic ketoacidosis, and those with toxic ingestions causing acidemia were excluded. Patients with end-stage chronic kidney disease were excluded, as patients who had received a sodium bicarbonate infusion or RRT in the 24 h before screening.

The BICAR-ICU trial was conducted in 26 ICUs in France from 2015 to 2017, enrolling 389 patients [5]. The BICAR-ICU2 trial was conducted in 43 ICUs in France from 2019 to 2023, and analyzed 627 patients [9].

Data acquisition and harmonization

Investigators provided the complete de-identified individual patient datasets after protocol registration and datasets were merged. Prior to merging, we established a unified data dictionary to ensure consistent variable definitions across studies [15, 16]. AKI was classified by comparable criteria in the two trials (AKIN in BICAR-ICU vs. KDIGO in BICAR-ICU2); these were reconciled by mapping AKIN stages to KDIGO stages for combined analyses [5, 9].

Extensive data integrity checks were performed after merging, including number of patients, baseline characteristics, and event rates in each trial’s subset [15]. There was no missing data for primary and secondary outcomes; we did not need to perform imputation. All analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis [5, 9].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 90 days after randomization (90-day mortality). Secondary outcomes were the need for RRT initiation during the index ICU stay (yes/no), and dialysis-free days through 28 days [5, 9]. RRT initiation was defined as initiation of any dialysis or hemofiltration treatment after randomization, at any time during the ICU admission. Dialysis-free days through 28 days was defined as the number of days within the first 28 days after randomization during which the patient was alive and not receiving RRT [5, 9]. For patients who died before day 28, the number of dialysis-free days was assigned as zero, consistent with standard conventions in critical care trials [5, 9]. All outcomes were determined by blinded site investigators in the original studies; no additional outcome adjudication was necessary.

Statistical analysis

We followed a one-stage individual patient data meta-analysis approach to compare sodium bicarbonate therapy versus no therapy across the two trials [17]. For dichotomous outcomes (90-day mortality and RRT initiation), we used a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) with a Poisson family and a log-link function [18, 19]. Hierarchical clusters were defined by trial and site, and a random intercept for each cluster accounted for within-cluster correlation [19]. Sites participating in both trials were treated as distinct clusters within each trial [19]. Treatment allocation was included as a fixed effect [19]. Results were expressed as risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) [19]. As sensitivity analysis, outcomes were studied as time-to-event and analyzed using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards frailty model, incorporating a random intercept for clusters (trial and site) [16]. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and results were reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95%CI [16]. Additionally, multivariable models were fitted to adjust for key prognostic covariates (age, SOFA score, and lactate) [20].

Dialysis-free days through 28 days were analyzed using a negative binomial mixed-effects model to account for over-dispersion and the non-normal distribution of the outcome [16], with a random intercept for each cluster. Results were reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95%CI. As a sensitivity analysis a multivariable model was fitted to adjust for key prognostic covariates (age, SOFA score, and lactate) [20]. The GRADE tool was used to assess the level of confidence of the results [21, 22].

Heterogeneity of treatment effects was assessed through three pre-specified subgroup analyses, chosen based on biological plausibility [23]: acidemia depth (pH ≤ 7.10 vs. > 7.10), AKI severity assessed through SOFA renal component (≥ 3 vs. < 3), and baseline lactate (< 2 mmol/L vs. ≥ 2 mmol/L). For each moderator, separate models were fitted within each subgroup using the same GLMM structure as the primary analysis [19]. In addition, interaction models including a treatment × subgroup term were fitted to formally test for effect modification. Results of subgroup analyses were presented with point estimates and 95% CIs for each subgroup category. Consistent with contemporary guidelines, we considered subgroup analyses exploratory; no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in subgroup tests, and p-values for interaction are reported without formal correction [19]. We used the ICEMAN tool to formally assess credibility of any apparent subgroup effects in terms of prior plausibility and consistency [24].

Physiological and biochemical response to treatment (arterial pH, serum bicarbonate, and serum sodium) was described using mixed-effects models with a random intercept for cluster, including fixed effects for treatment group, time (as a categorical factor), and their interaction, fitted separately overall and within acidemia-depth strata. Between-group differences were reported as adjusted means with 95% confidence intervals.

All tests were two-tailed with an α level of 0.05 for significance. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1.

Results

Two randomized controlled trials, BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2, were retrieved and included; both investigated intravenous sodium bicarbonate infusion for metabolic acidemia in critically ill patients. Individual patient data were available for both trials. The two trials were judged to be at low risk of bias using the RoB 2.0 tool [25].

After data acquisition and harmonization, the individual-patient dataset included 1,016 patients (all 389 analyzed patients from BICAR-ICU and all 627 analyzed patients from BICAR-ICU2) with complete data for the primary outcome. All patients were included in the intention-to-treat analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 66 years (interquartile range [IQR], 57–74), and 406 (39%) were female. At randomization, the median arterial pH was 7.15 (IQR, 7.09–7.18), the median bicarbonate concentration was 12.6 (IQR, 9.9–14.9) mmol/L, and the median arterial lactate was 5.7 (IQR, 3.0–10.0) mmol/L. Septic shock was the main cause of metabolic acidemia (53%). Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the sodium bicarbonate group (n = 509) and the control group (n = 507) (Table 1). Primary outcome

At 90 days, death had occurred in 297 of 509 patients (58.3%) in the sodium bicarbonate group and 307 of 507 patients (60.6%) in the control group. In the primary analysis using a generalized linear mixed model, sodium bicarbonate therapy was not associated with a significant reduction in 90-day mortality (RR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.86 to 1.07; p = 0.51, moderate certainty, Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

During the ICU stay, a total of 177/509 patients (34.8%) required RRT in the sodium bicarbonate group, compared with 257/507 patients (50.7%) in the control group. Sodium bicarbonate therapy significantly reduced RRT initiation (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.79; p < 0.001, high certainty, Table 2), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 16% (95% CI, 10% to 22%). The number needed to treat to avoid one RRT initiation was 6.3.

In sensitivity analysis, the cumulative incidence of RRT initiation within 28 days after enrollment was significantly lower in the sodium bicarbonate group, compared to the controlled group (HR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.46 to 0.67; p < 0.001, high certainty, Fig. 2B). Results remained consistent in the multivariable sensitivity analysis adjusting for age, SOFA score, and lactate (adjusted RR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.63 to 0.82; p < 0.001, high certainty).

The median number of dialysis-free days at day 28 was significantly higher in the sodium bicarbonate group compared with the control group (0 days [IQR, 0–28] vs. 0 days [IQR, 0–28]; IRR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.06 to 1.14; p < 0.001). The mean number of dialysis-free days at day 28 was 12.4 (SD, 13.3) in the sodium bicarbonate group and 11.3 (SD, 13.0) in the control group. Results remained consistent in the multivariable sensitivity analysis adjusting for age, SOFA score, and lactate (adjusted IRR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.03 to 1.11; p = 0.001, high certainty).

Heterogeneity of treatment effect for 90-day mortality

Heterogeneity of treatment effect was evaluated across three prespecified subgroups at randomization: acidemia depth at randomization (pH < 7.10 vs. ≥ 7.10), presence of severe acute kidney injury (SOFA renal score ≥ 3 vs. < 3), and serum lactate (< 2 mmol/L vs. ≥ 2 mmol/L).

A statistically significant interaction was observed between acidemia depth and sodium bicarbonate therapy regarding 90-day mortality (p-for-interaction = 0.006). In patients with a pH < 7.10 at randomization, sodium bicarbonate was associated with a significant reduction of 90-day mortality (RR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.68 to 0.93; p = 0.004, Fig. 3). In patients with a pH ≥ 7.10 at randomization, sodium bicarbonate was not associated with 90-day mortality (RR, 1.05; 0.92 to 1.21; p = 0.47). Using the ICEMAN tool for meta-analyses, the credibility of effect modification was judged high (Supplemental Material).

No significant effect modification was observed based on severe acute kidney injury status (p-for-interaction = 0.11). There was no significant difference of 90-day mortality in patients with severe AKI at randomization (RR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.76 to 1.02, p = 0.10, Fig. 3). There was no significant difference of 90-day mortality in patients without severe AKI at randomization (RR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.90 to 1.18, p = 0.67).

No significant effect modification was observed based on serum lactate at randomization (p-for-interaction = 0.22). There was no significant difference of 90-day mortality in patients with a serum lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L at randomization (RR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.83 to 1.02, p = 0.12, Fig. 3). There was no significant difference of 90-day mortality in patients with a serum lactate < 2 mmol/L at randomization (RR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.80 to 1.66, p = 0.45).

Physiological response to treatment

Arterial pH and serum bicarbonate increased more rapidly in the sodium bicarbonate group than in the control group (Figures S2 and S3). The mean between-group difference in arterial pH was 0.11 (95%CI, 0.10–0.13) at 4 h and 0.06 (95%CI, 0.05–0.08) at 24 h; the corresponding difference in serum bicarbonate was 3.8 mmol/L (95%CI, 3.2–4.3) at 4 h and 3.7 mmol/L (95%CI, 3.1–4.3) at 24 h. The separation was more pronounced among patients with a baseline pH ≤ 7.10 (between-group pH difference, 0.15 [95%CI, 0.12–0.18] at 4 h, 0.08 [95%CI, 0.05–0.12] at 24 h) than among those with a baseline pH > 7.10 (0.10 [95%CI, 0.08–0.11] at 4 h, 0.04 [95%CI, 0.02–0.06] at 24 h) (Figs. S2 and S3).

In the sodium bicarbonate group, an arterial pH ≥ 7.30 was reached in 77% of patients overall, at a median of 4 h (IQR, 4–12). Target attainment was 67% in patients with a baseline pH ≤ 7.10 and 81% in those with a baseline pH > 7.10. The median cumulative volume of 4.2% sodium bicarbonate infused during the intervention period was 500mL (IQR, 375–1000) overall; 750mL (IQR, 500–1000) in patients with a baseline pH ≤ 7.10 and 500mL (IQR, 250–1000) in those with a baseline pH > 7.10.

At its peak (12 h), serum sodium increase difference was 4.4mmol/L (95%CI, 3.7–5.2) between the sodium bicarbonate and the control groups (Figure S4). Serum sodium > 150mmol/L occurred in 10% (52/509) of the sodium bicarbonate group versus 4% (20/507) of the control group. Serum sodium > 160mmol/L was rare, observed in 0.2% (1/509) of the sodium bicarbonate group and in 0.6% (3/507) of the control group. The serum sodium increase with bicarbonate was greater in the pH ≤ 7.10 stratum than in the pH > 7.10 stratum (mean difference 4.5mmol/L [95%CI, 3.6 to 5.4] vs. 2.3mmol/L [95%CI, 1.8 to 2.8], respectively; p-for-interaction < 0.001).

Discussion

In this individual patient data meta-analysis including 1,016 critically ill patients with severe metabolic acidemia across two multicenter randomized controlled trials, intravenous sodium bicarbonate therapy did not reduce 90-day mortality compared to no sodium bicarbonate (moderate certainty). However, sodium bicarbonate led to a significant decrease in RRT initiation, with a high certainty. Moreover, significant heterogeneity of treatment effect was found with regards to baseline pH (high credibility). In patients with the profound acidemia (pH ≤ 7.10 at randomization), sodium bicarbonate therapy was associated with a significant reduction of 90-day mortality. Conversely, no significant difference was found when the baseline pH was > 7.10. The findings were robust across sensitivity analyses.

In severe metabolic acidemia, the role of sodium bicarbonate infusion as an adjunctive intervention remains an ongoing controversy. The conclusions of this study align with and extend the results of the two existing trials on sodium bicarbonate therapy in critical care [5, 9]. The most robust signal is the significant and meaningful reduction of RRT initiation. Sodium bicarbonate therapy reduced the need for RRT with a strong effect size (RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.79; p < 0.001) compared to standard care. The absolute risk difference of 16% translates into a number needed to treat of six patients to avoid one RRT indication, a clinically significant effect. Sodium bicarbonate yielded a congruent positive effect on dialysis-free days. These results were confirmed by adjustment for baseline covariates, and time-to-event sensitivity analyses.

Renal replacement therapy is frequently initiated in cases of severe metabolic acidemia refractory to medical management, with clinical protocols commonly citing a pH threshold between 7.20 and 7.15 as an indication [26, 27]. However, this threshold remains empirically derived and lacks robust validation in prospective trials [2]. Avoiding or delaying RRT in critically ill patients is a clinically meaningful objective, particularly given the associated risks and resource implications of extracorporeal support [28]. RRT, while life-sustaining, is not without harm: it may induce hemodynamic instability, accelerate loss of residual renal function, and is associated with complications such as catheter-related infections [9], bleeding, and electrolyte imbalances [29, 30]. Furthermore, early initiation of RRT has not demonstrated a mortality benefit in randomized trials of critically ill patients with AKI, and may expose patients to unnecessary procedures in the absence of urgent indications [26, 27]. In this context, the ability of sodium bicarbonate to promptly buffer acidemia and restore physiological tolerance without the need for RRT constitutes a potentially significant therapeutic benefit.

In prespecified subgroup analyses, we observed a significant interaction between acidemia severity and treatment effect, with a significant mortality reduction among patients with pH ≤ 7.10. On the other hand, no significant difference in mortality was found among patients with pH > 7.10. This heterogeneity of treatment effect was assessed as highly credible using the ICEMAN tool for meta-analyses [24]. This interaction is both biologically plausible and aligned with prior observational studies [10, 31], although it remains exploratory and should be interpreted accordingly. Severe acidemia has well-documented deleterious effects on cardiovascular, respiratory, and cellular function [4], and it is mechanistically coherent that patients with more profound acid–base disturbances may derive greater benefit from this prompt correction. In this subgroup, sodium bicarbonate may mitigate the immediate physiological consequences of acidemia, thereby contributing to improved hemodynamic stability and organ perfusion. Importantly, in the most severe settings, intravenous sodium bicarbonate can be administered rapidly at the bedside and leads to a measurable increase in arterial pH within minutes. On the other hand, RRT requires technical preparation and vascular access, leading to delays between prescription and initiation. This temporal advantage may be particularly relevant in the early management of life-threatening acidemia.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of the recently published SODa-BIC trial, an international, double-blind randomized trial of 500 patients with moderate metabolic acidemia and vasopressor-dependent shock [32]. SODa-BIC found no effect of sodium bicarbonate on the primary outcome of major adverse kidney events within 30 days (40.2% vs. 39.4%; adjusted difference, 1.2%; 95%CI, − 7.1 to 9.4), with a non-significant reduction in the use of renal replacement therapy (16.8% vs. 20.9%; adjusted difference, − 3.9%; 95%CI, − 10.6 to 2.7). Several elements distinguish SODa-BIC from the trials pooled here. First, SODa-BIC enrolled a less severely acidemic population (pH < 7.30) [32], whereas our analysis indicates that any mortality benefit is concentrated in patients with the most profound acidemia. Second, the intervention was briefer (an infusion for up to 5 h) than the sustained, pH-targeted strategy used in BICAR-ICU and BICAR-ICU2. Third, the primary outcome was a renal composite, and the trial was not powered for mortality. The non-significant reduction in renal replacement therapy in SODa-BIC is directionally consistent with the reduction observed in our analysis. Taken together, the totality of randomized evidence indicates a consistent reduction in renal replacement therapy [33] and supports the hypothesis that a mortality benefit, if present, is confined to patients with the deepest acidemia.

The absence of a demonstrable mortality difference in the overall population should be interpreted cautiously. The pooled data encompass 1,016 patients, yet the confidence interval remains compatible with substantially beneficial to harmful potential effects. A target trial emulation found a small but significant 1.9% absolute reduction of mortality [10]. Detecting such a difference with 80% statistical power in a conventional randomized controlled trial would require a sample size of approximately 5,216 patients, far exceeding the scale of existing studies. Ongoing trials will provide additional data to address this question (SRCTN14027629 and NCT05697770). In the future, an individualized treatment effect framework might help tailoring therapeutic decisions to patient-specific physiological profiles [34].

This study has several strengths. It incorporates all available randomized evidence on the question to date, includes a large and well-characterized population, and applies a prespecified and transparent statistical approach consistent with individual patient data meta-analysis methodology [12]. The harmonization of inclusion criteria and outcome definitions across trials strengthens the internal validity of pooled estimates. The use of intention-to-treat analysis, cluster adjustment, and multivariable modeling enhances the robustness of the findings. Limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the analysis includes only two trials, both conducted within the same national health system. Second, although the subgroup analysis according to pH suggests a potential differential effect, this interaction should be interpreted cautiously, as an exploratory analysis. However, the subgroups of interest were defined and registered prospectively, before data acquisition and harmonization, according to biological plausibility and prior literature [10, 31]. We limited their number to a total of three, and followed the ICEMAN guidelines to assess their credibility [24]. Finally, longer-term outcomes, including renal recovery and health-related quality of life, were not available in this analysis [6].

Conclusions

In summary, for critically ill patients with severe metabolic acidemia, sodium bicarbonate therapy did not reduce overall 90-day mortality but significantly decreased the need for RRT initiation. In patients with a baseline pH below 7.10, sodium bicarbonate therapy was associated with a significant reduction in 90-day mortality, suggesting a differential treatment effect according to the severity of acidemia. These findings invite a reconsideration of current paradigms, suggesting that timely, targeted correction of severe metabolic acidemia reduces the need for RRT initiation and may modulate the trajectory of critical illness.
