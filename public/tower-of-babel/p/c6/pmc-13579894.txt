Predicting high perceived stress in late adolescence: development and validation of a prognostic model
Authors: Pernille Bach Steen, Vita Ligaya Dalgaard, Johan Hviid Andersen, Morten Vejs Willert, Karin Biering
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimStress levels have increased among adolescents, yet tools for identifying those at high risk remain limited. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic model estimating the probability of high perceived stress in young adulthood using data collected at age 15.MethodsData were from the West Jutland Cohort Study including 2,108 Danish adolescents with complete information on the 4-item Perceived Stress Scale at ages 15 and 18. High stress at age 18 was defined as a total score of 9 or higher. Twenty-three candidate predictors were examined using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression across 100 imputed datasets. Predictors selected in ≥ 80% of imputations were entered into a pooled logistic regression model. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation under multiple imputation. Discrimination was assessed with the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and calibration with calibration-in-the-large (CITL) and slope.ResultsThree predictors at age 15 were retained: perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and self-esteem. The model showed good calibration (CITL = 0.003; slope = 1.008; expected-to-observed ratio = 0.998) and acceptable discrimination with an AUC = 0.72 (0.68;0.76). The Brier score was 0.081, indicating satisfactory overall predictive accuracy. The model showed high negative predictive values but modest positive predictive values.ConclusionsA simple model based on self-reported psychosocial measures in mid-adolescence predicted later high stress with good calibration and moderate discrimination. External validation is needed to assess generalisability and inform potential use as a low-burden decision-support tool in school health settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28328-7.

Introduction

Stress levels have risen in recent decades, posing a growing public health concern with major implications for both individual health and society [1]. Research has consistently linked stress to adverse health outcomes, including mental health disorders and mortality [2–4]. Stress also contributes to reduced quality of life and imposes substantial societal costs through healthcare use, productivity loss, and sickness absence [5]. Consequently, stress is widely recognised as a public-health challenge, and its prevention is a policy priority in several European countries.

In Denmark, surveys show a marked rise in perceived stress, particularly among younger age groups [6]. Adolescents appear especially vulnerable due to rapid biological, psychological, and social development during this period [7]. Identity formation, educational transition, and increasing autonomy may heighten stress vulnerability when coping resources are limited [8]. High adolescent stress has been associated with poorer academic performance and reduced labour-market participation in young adulthood, suggesting lasting socioeconomic consequences [9, 10].

Early identification of adolescents at increased risk is therefore crucial. Adolescence represents a key period for shaping health trajectories and coping patterns [7]. In Denmark, municipalities must offer all adolescents a school-nurse consultation before leaving compulsory school, providing an opportunity to identify emerging psychosocial risk patterns. A structured prognostic tool could support school health professionals in identifying vulnerable adolescents and tailoring preventive strategies. As stress in adolescence often persists into adulthood [11], identifying early risk may offer important preventive opportunities.

While numerous studies have examined correlates of adolescent stress, few have developed multivariable prognostic models to estimate individual-level risk. Existing youth mental-health prognostic models mainly target depression, anxiety, or self-harm [12], underscoring the need for prognostic research specifically addressing stress vulnerability.

Aim

This study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic model estimating the probability of high perceived stress at age 18 using data measured at age 15. The model is intended for potential use in Danish school healthcare to support early identification and targeted preventive action, focusing on prediction rather than causal inference.

Methods

Population and data source

Data were derived from the West Jutland Cohort Study (VestLiv), which is a population-based study including individuals born in 1989 (N = 3,681) residing in western Denmark in 2004. In 2004, 3,054 15-year-olds completed a questionnaire, and 2,400 participated again in 2007 at age 18 [13].

Outcome

Perceived stress was measured using the 4-item version of Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4), assessing the extent to which individuals appraise their lives as stressful during the past month [14]. Scores were summed, with higher values indicating greater stress. Participants with missing ≥ 1 item were excluded. The PSS-4 was dichotomised at score ≥ 9 to distinguish between low and high stress levels. This cut-point was proportionally derived from the validated PSS-10 cut-point of ≥ 24 [15], corresponding to ≥ 9.6 on the PSS-4 scale and rounded down to ≥ 9 to ensure complete case capture, which aligns with previous suggestions of ≥ 9–10 [16].

Potential predictors

Predictor selection was based on previous evidence, expert input, and feasibility in school health practice. Baseline variables captured psychological, social, and health domains, including perceived stress, depressive symptoms, self-esteem, sense of coherence, coping, subjective social status, sex, self-rated health, smoking, BMI, and social and family indicators. Owing to word limitations, only predictors retained in the final model are described below; full descriptions of all candidate predictors are provided in Supplementary Text 1.

Perceived stress at age 15 was also measured with the PSS-4 but was included as a continuous predictor [14].

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 4-item version of the Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children (CES-DC4) [17]. Exploratory analyses indicated a non-linear association between CES-DC4 scores and the log odds of high perceived stress at age 18. The measure was therefore dichotomised at ≥ 4 to improve model fit and interpretability. This cut-off is slightly higher than the ≥ 3 proposed by Fendrich et al., which would have classified approximately one-third of respondents as having depressive symptoms [17].

Self-esteem was measured using six items from Rosenberg’s self-esteem scale [18] (score 0–18), with higher scores indicating poor self-esteem.

Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) was acceptable for all scales (PSS-4: 0.62; self-esteem: 0.82; CES-DC4: 0.63).

Sample size

No formal sample size calculation was conducted, as the study used an existing cohort. Among 2,108 participants, 203 met the criterion for high perceived stress at age 18. With 28 parameters across 23 candidate predictors, the events-per-parameter ratio was ~ 7:1, slightly below the conventional 10:1 guideline [19]. The final model included three predictors (three parameters), yielding an events-per-parameter ratio of approximately 68:1, indicating an adequate statistical precision for the final model estimates.

Statistical methods

Analyses followed the TRIPOD guidelines [20] and established methodological principles for prognostic modelling [21].

Missing data

Missing data on all potential predictors were addressed using multiple imputation (MI) with chained equations with 100 iterations, incorporating both questionnaire and register-based information. Complete-case analysis would have excluded 675 participants (~ 32%), risking biased estimates and reduced precision. The outcome variable (PSS-4 at age 18) was included in the MI to preserve associations but not imputed, in line with recommended practice [22].

Statistical analyses

The linearity of continuous predictors with the logit of the outcome was assessed using the Box–Tidwell test. Non-linear predictors (p < 0.05) were categorised to improve model interpretability and stability [23]. Multicollinearity among potential predictors was evaluated using pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients.

Descriptive bivariate analyses compared potential predictors between adolescents with low and high stress levels using independent t-tests for continuous and χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

Predictor selection

Predictor selection was conducted using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression [24] applied separately to each of the 100 imputed datasets. Each model was fitted with 10-fold stratified cross-validation, using identical fold partitions across MIs to ensure comparability and prevent information leakage. The penalty parameter (λ) was chosen by the 1-standard error rule to favour parsimony and stability [25]. Predictors selected in ≥ 80% of imputations were retained for the final logistic regression model, estimated under MI. Regression coefficients and performance metrics were pooled using Rubin’s rules [26].

Internal validation

A separate k-fold cross-validation procedure (10-folds) was then used for internal validation under MI [21, 27]. Within each imputed dataset, the model was trained on nine folds and tested on the remaining fold in rotation. Cross-validated estimates of the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and the Brier score were calculated within each dataset and pooled to yield overall performance estimates.

Model performance

Calibration, discrimination, predictive accuracy, and classification performance were evaluated following recommended approaches [27, 28]. Model performance was evaluated with consideration of the outcome’s unequal distribution, acknowledging that overall accuracy measures are influenced by outcome frequency and that discrimination estimates may be less stable when few events are observed.

Calibration was assessed for the final pooled logistic regression model and re-evaluated using out-of-fold predictions from cross-validation [19, 27], estimating calibration-in-the-large (CITL), calibration slope, and expected-to-observed ratio (E: O) through logistic recalibration of observed outcomes on predicted probabilities [28]. Graphical assessment included calibration plots comparing observed and predicted event rates across deciles of predicted risk visualising the calibration curve with LOWESS smoothing, and calibration-belt plots illustrating the calibration curve across the full range of predicted probabilities with 80% and 95% confidence intervals. These complementary methods provided an overall assessment of calibration and potential optimism in the final model. Spiegelhalter’s z-test was applied to the cross-validated predictions to test for systematic miscalibration.

Discrimination and overall predictive accuracy were evaluated using cross-validation under MI, providing unbiased estimates of expected performance on new data [20, 21]. The AUC quantified discrimination and the Brier score summarised overall predictive accuracy as the mean squared difference between predicted and observed outcomes. Results were averaged across folds within each imputed dataset and then pooled across imputations to obtain overall estimates. These measures reflect the expected discrimination and overall predictive accuracy of the model in new data.

Classification performance across a range of probability thresholds was evaluated using out-of-fold predicted probabilities obtained from the cross-validation. For each threshold, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated for each threshold.

Analyses were conducted in Stata 19 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) on Statistics Denmark’s research server, with fixed seed (1234).

Predictor selection and final model

Of the 23 candidate predictors initially considered, three were retained in at least 80% of imputations in the LASSO regression (see Supplementary Table 2 for selection frequencies) and were included in the final multivariable logistic regression model: perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and self-esteem. Selection frequencies dropped markedly after these three predictors, with no remaining candidate predictors selected in more than 24% of imputations. Model performance

The model demonstrated good calibration, with a calibration-in-the-large (CITL) of 0.000, a calibration slope of 1.009, and an expected-to-observed (E: O) ratio of 1.000. The calibration plot (Supplementary Fig. 1A) showed close agreement between predicted and observed probabilities across the range of predicted risk, and the calibration-belt plot (Supplementary Fig. 1B) confirmed adequate model fit, with both the 80% and 95% confidence bands overlapping the reference line throughout the predicted-probability range.

To quantify potential optimism, calibration was then evaluated using out-of-fold predictions from the 10-fold cross-validation procedure. Cross-validated calibration remained stable (CITL = 0.003; slope = 1.008; E: O = 0.998), indicating negligible optimism and consistent agreement between predicted and observed event rates (Supplementary Figs. 2 A–B). Apparent and cross-validated calibration metrics were therefore nearly identical, confirming robust model calibration.

Discriminative ability of the final internally validated model was moderate, with a pooled cross-validated area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.721 (95% CI: 0.684–0.758). Overall predictive accuracy, summarised by the pooled cross-validated Brier score, was 0.081. Within each imputed dataset, performance metrics were averaged across folds, and the resulting estimates were combined across imputations using Rubin’s rules to obtain pooled means and 95% confidence intervals. Spiegelhalter’s z-test, applied to the pooled out-of-fold predictions aggregated across imputations, showed no evidence of miscalibration (p = 0.46).

Following internal validation, calibration and discrimination remained consistent, supporting the stability and generalisability of the final model. Given the low prevalence of high stress (9.6%), these performance measures indicate satisfactory predictive accuracy for a psychosocial outcome with a relatively low event frequency.

Sensitivity analyses using a higher threshold for high perceived stress (PSS-4 ≥ 10) showed slightly higher discrimination (AUC = 0.74) and a lower outcome prevalence (5.7%), while calibration was slightly poorer. Additional sensitivity analyses using the recommended CES-DC4 cut-off of ≥ 3 retained the same three predictors in the final model, although depressive symptoms were selected in slightly fewer imputations. The cross-validated model performance was essentially unchanged, with identical CITL, calibration slope, AUC, and Brier score estimates, and only a marginal decrease in the E: O ratio of 0.001.

Sensitivity declined as the threshold increased, while specificity and the PPV increased. At thresholds of 0.02–0.05, most adolescents were classified as test-positive, yielding high sensitivity (≥ 0.90) but low specificity (< 0.30). Between 0.10 and 0.20, sensitivity decreased from 0.58 to 0.26, whereas specificity increased from 0.75 to 0.93.

At a threshold of 0.10, ~ 28 per 100 adolescents would screen positive, with 20% true positives (PPV = 0.20). At threshold 0.20, only ~ 9 per 100 would screen positive, with 28% true positives. Across all thresholds, the NPV remained high (≥ 0.90).

Discussion

This study developed and internally validated a multivariable prognostic model estimating the risk of high perceived stress at age 18 using data collected at age 15. The final model included perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and self-esteem, and showed good calibration and acceptable discrimination (AUC = 0.72). These findings indicate that self-reported psychosocial information obtained in mid-adolescence can help identify individuals at increased risk of later high stress with reasonable accuracy. The model’s CITL and slope close to unity demonstrate strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, implying reliable individual-level risk estimates. Although the negative predictive value was high, this largely reflects the low prevalence of high stress and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the model may still have practical value in identifying adolescents with a low probability of later high stress in a population-based setting.

Research on prediction models for stress or related mental health outcomes in youth remains limited. A systematic review [12] identified several emerging models for child and adolescent mental health, but most were methodologically weak and cross-sectional. Machine-learning studies in adults have likewise identified psychosocial predictors of chronic stress [29]. In contrast, the present model used prospective data and TRIPOD-based procedures, enhancing transparency and reproducibility. Its reliance on simple psychological indicators supports feasibility in school healthcare.

Perceived stress at age 15 was the strongest predictor of later high stress. However, a model including only perceived stress showed slightly lower discrimination (AUC = 0.70) and somewhat poorer calibration compared with the full model, suggesting that depressive symptoms and self-esteem provide incremental predictive value. The three predictors may capture related but complementary aspects of psychosocial functioning. Their inclusion may therefore improve identification of adolescents at increased risk, although the predictive value remains modest. These associations are predictive rather than causal and should be interpreted accordingly.

The model’s discrimination was acceptable and comparable to other youth mental health prediction studies [12], while its calibration was strong, with predicted probabilities closely matching observed outcomes. The relatively low outcome prevalence (9.6%) partly explains the moderate AUC, as models predicting infrequent psychosocial events typically achieve modest discrimination [28]. Under such conditions, an AUC around 0.70 and a Brier score near 0.08 are considered satisfactory and consistent with previous research. Emphasising calibration over discrimination is appropriate when the model is intended for use in individual risk communication rather than case detection [28]. Sensitivity analyses using a higher threshold showed slightly higher discrimination but poorer calibration, suggesting no overall improvement in model performance.

If externally validated and further refined, the model may eventually function as a structured decision-support tool during school health consultations at the end of compulsory schooling, helping to identify adolescents who may benefit from further dialogue about stress management or referral to preventive services. The optimal threshold depends on the purpose of use, the acceptable balance between sensitivity and specificity, and available follow-up resources. The NPV was high but partly driven by the low outcome prevalence and should not be interpreted as a strong indicator of individual-level predictive performance. Predicted high-risk classifications should be interpreted with caution, as false positives are common in low-prevalence contexts. Accordingly, predicted high-risk cases should always be interpreted with caution [21]. Although this means that many screen-positive adolescents will ultimately not develop high stress, this is an expected characteristic of predictive tools for low-prevalence psychosocial outcomes and is acceptable in a preventive counselling context, where the purpose is to guide supportive dialogue rather than to provide diagnostic certainty [27]. The model’s reliance on self-reported items ensures minimal implementation burden, requiring no additional clinical testing or equipment.

Ethical considerations remain crucial. Risk communication should be handled with care to avoid stigma or unnecessary concern. Implementation should therefore be embedded within a supportive consultation setting, ensuring that model-based risk estimates inform rather than replace professional judgement.

Strengths and limitations

A key methodological strength of this study lies in its adherence to TRIPOD guidelines for the transparent development and reporting of prognostic models and the use of prospectively collected data. MI was applied to handle missing data [22] and LASSO regression was used for predictor selection to enhance parsimony and minimise overfitting [24, 27], and model coefficients were re-estimated using standard logistic regression to maintain interpretability. Internal validation through k-fold cross-validation under MI provided robust, unbiased estimates of expected performance in new data [19, 28]. This two-step approach, combining penalised and conventional regression, represents best practice in epidemiological prognostic modelling when both transparency and stability are required [19, 27].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The model was internally but not externally validated, and its generalisability to other populations or contexts remains to be established [19]. The study population was somewhat more socioeconomically advantaged than the broader VestLiv cohort and Danish adolescents born in 1989, potentially limiting representativeness. In addition, differences in the prevalence of psychiatric disorders compared with the national population may further affect generalisability and lead to underestimation of risk in more vulnerable groups.

The relatively modest internal consistency of the brief scales (PSS-4 and CES-DC4) should be considered. However, such values are commonly observed for short instruments with few items.

Although calibration was strong, the PPV was modest, indicating that not all adolescents classified as being at increased risk will later report high stress. Such misclassification is expected, as psychosocial outcomes are influenced by multiple interacting and context-dependent factors, including influences earlier in life. Dichotomising perceived stress may have resulted in some loss of information, particularly for individuals with scores close to the cut-off. Alternative outcome specifications, including ordinal or multinomial approaches distinguishing low, moderate, and high stress, may be valuable to explore in future research. While several contextual indicators were examined, they added little predictive information beyond the psychosocial measures, possibly because the effects of broader underlying constructs are already embedded in psychosocial functioning by age 15 and therefore do not appear as independent predictors. Accordingly, the model should be interpreted as a prognostic model intended to support structured risk assessment and dialogue rather than a diagnostic instrument. Physiological, behavioural, and other mental health-related factors may also have improved predictive performance but were not available in the present dataset. For example, anxiety symptoms may be important to consider in future prognostic research given their theoretical and physiological overlap with perceived stress. Future studies should therefore evaluate whether broader psychosocial, contextual, behavioural, or biological predictor sets improve model performance and generalisability. In the present study, priority was placed on achieving strong calibration to ensure that predicted risks correspond closely to observed outcomes, which is essential for trustworthy risk communication [28].

Conclusion and perspectives

This study developed and internally validated a prognostic model estimating the risk of high perceived stress in young adulthood using psychosocial data from age 15. The final model, including perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and self-esteem, demonstrated good calibration and acceptable discrimination, indicating that simple self-reported measures can inform early risk assessment. The model may have potential as a future decision-support tool in school health consultations, although external validation and further model development are needed before implementation can be considered. External validation and implementation studies are needed to evaluate generalisability, feasibility, and the effects of risk communication.
