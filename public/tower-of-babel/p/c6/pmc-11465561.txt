Investigating the Reactivity and Selectivity of [3]Cumulenes via Double-Hybrid DFT
Authors: Abdulkader Baroudi, Wejdan E Matar, Sami Tlais
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present an initial foundational theoretical investigation into the reactivity of [3]cumulenes, identifying key factors influencing the reaction selectivity using DLPNO-ωB97X-2/Def2-QZVPP. Our study demonstrates moderate to high selectivity among the three double bonds in substituted [3]cumulenes. Specifically, the [4 + 2] cycloaddition reaction is kinetically favored at the terminal π-bonds and thermodynamically favored at the central π-bond. In certain tetrasubstituted [3]cumulenes, enhanced and reversed kinetic selectivity is predicted. Our findings elucidate the reversed selectivity in tetrafluoro[3]cumulene, attributed to the reduced cumulene distortion energy, and predict similar selectivity in tetramethoxy[3]cumulene due to thermodynamic influences. Future research will focus on additional kinetic factors to enhance the stability and practical application of [3]cumulenes in chemical synthesis.

Introduction

[3]Cumulenes have received relatively limited attention within the realm of organic chemistry despite their discovery in the early 20th century.1 In the 1920s, a significant breakthrough occurred with Brand’s successful synthesis of the first tetrasubstituted [3]cumulene. Subsequently, in 1952, Schubert and colleagues achieved a groundbreaking milestone by producing unsubstituted buta-1,2,3-triene.2 This achievement, however, revealed the inherent instability of these compounds, leading to rapid dimerization at room temperature.3,4 Conceivably, owing to the instability of simpler counterparts, the majority of synthesized stable [3]cumulenes are tetrasubstituted.5−7

With its three double bonds in succession, unlike most functional groups, [3]cumulene (Figure 1) possesses two orthogonal and nondegenerate π-systems with only one of them being conjugated. The regioselectivity on such unique π-system offers unprecedented synthetic routes to complex structures.8−10 While previous theoretical studies have mainly focused on structural properties of [3]cumulene,11−19 encompassing aspects like cis–trans isomerization,20,21 theoretical studies on [3]cumulenes chemical reactions are scarce in literature. Therefore, we find it necessary to commence a systematic exploration of [3]cumulenes reactivity as a unique four-carbon functional group. In this work, we explore the [4 + 2] cycloaddition as our focal reaction, recognizing its significance as one of the most documented cycloaddition reactions for this functional group and laying the groundwork for subsequent investigations.22−31 Thus, the reactivity of monosubstituted [3]cumulenes is first explored at the three double-bond positions (α, β, and γ, Figure 1) with cyclopentadiene (CPDE), followed by the extrapolation and comparison to symmetric tetrasubstituted moieties, at high quantum mechanical level of theory.

Computational Details

All optimized structures were obtained using the Gaussian 16 program suite.32 The B3LYP-D3 exchange–correlation density functional theory (DFT) functional was utilized in conjunction with the 6-31+G(2df,p) Pople-style basis set along with the empirical D3 dispersion corrections. Wave function stability was confirmed for all selected [3]cumulenes using the standard keyword stable = opt. Harmonic vibrational analyses were conducted to verify each stationary point as either an equilibrium or a transition state (TS) structure. TS structures were further verified by IRC calculations.

For enhanced accuracy in electronic energies, high-level double-hybrid density functional theory (DHDFT) calculations were performed on the DFT-optimized geometries. It is well known that DHDFT methods yield reaction energies with a mean absolute deviation that is close to the chemical accuracy threshold.33 The ωB97X-234 DHDFT functional with the domain-based local pair natural orbital (DLPNO) approximation35,36 and the Def2-QZVPP37 basis set were used. Single-point energy calculations were executed using ORCA software.38 The electronic energies obtained with DLPNO-ωB97X-2/Def2-QZVPP were converted to Gibbs free energies at 298 K, using thermal free energy corrections derived from the B3LYP-D3/6-31+G(2df,p) level of theory. This approach was necessary because standard DFT calculations on cumulenes fail to provide precise energy values.17 The DHDFT method was found to significantly impact the DFT reaction energies with differences of up to 9 kcal mol–1.

Several conformers of cumulenes were calculated, and the lowest energy conformer was used in our study. In addition, for the reaction at the α position for monosubstituted [3]cumulenes, the barrier for endo [4 + 2] cycloaddition was found, as expected, to be lower than that of exo, and thus, endo was considered in our study. The cumulene distortion energies were obtained from the single-point calculations on the distorted cumulene structure (only) taken from its TS of cycloaddition with CPDE.

Results and Discussion

An initial insight into [3]cumulene reactivity is obtained upon the analysis of frontier molecular orbitals. A comparison of its LUMOs (out-of-plane and in-plane orbitals) with those of other π-systems with a four-carbon skeleton is performed as shown in Figure 2. Unsurprisingly, [3]cumulene LUMO(α+γ) (of its conjugated terminal π-bonds, out-of-plane orbital) is lower in energy than those of nonconjugated π-bonds (2-butene, 2-butyne, and 1,2-butadiene). In addition, [3]cumulene LUMO(β) (in-plane orbital), which corresponds to the middle double bond, has an energy close to that of 2-butene LUMO and 1,2-butadiene LUMO+1, whereas [3]cumulene LUMO(α+γ) is also lower in energy than those of 1,3-butadiene and butenyne. The enyne functional group also features two orthogonal and nondegenerate π-systems. However, it does not allow direct and simultaneous involvement of its central carbons (carbons 2 and 3) in cycloaddition reactions. This limitation confines regioselectivity in enynes solely to the alkene and alkyne regions.39 Furthermore, the in-plane alkyne LUMO(β) in enynes is unlikely to participate in reactions due to the presence of a lower energy out-of-plane LUMO(α+γ) that corresponds to the same alkyne carbons. In contrast, [3]cumulene LUMO(β) offers a third regioselectivity option at the middle double bond (β position, Figure 1), possibly through electronic alterations.

LUMO energies of different four-carbon π-systems calculated at DLPNO-ωB97X-2/Def2-QZVPP. Orbitals in blue are for conjugated π-bonds, and those in red are for nonconjugated π-bonds. MOs were generated using IboView40 software.

Accordingly, the reactivity of monosubstituted and tetrasubstituted [3]cumulenes is investigated at the DLPNO-ωB97X-2/Def2-QZVPP level of theory. Figure 3 summarizes LUMO energies of selected [3]cumulenes bearing different substituents and their [4 + 2] cycloaddition reaction barriers (ΔG‡), at the respective α, β, and γ double bonds, with CPDE. Reaction barriers presented in Figure 3 correspond to the obtained lowest energy transition state in accordance with the specified product stereochemistry shown in Figure 1. In our discussion, we use the cumulene LUMO energy level as to apparently represent the HOMO–LUMO gap between CPDE and [3]cumulene, considering that the cycloaddition reaction is normal. Consequently, the difference in gaps between cycloadditions at β and α (or γ) positions, termed Δgap, would be equal to the gap between LUMO(β) and LUMO(α+γ), i.e., Δgap = [gap(β)normal – gap(α+γ)normal] = [LUMO(β) – LUMO(α+γ)]. As expected, cycloaddition barriers are mostly lower at α and γ positions (Δgap > 0), with a trivial difference between them. Overall, ΔGα‡ and ΔGγ‡ values vary in accordance with the [3]cumulene LUMO(α+γ) relative energy levels. In contrast, our calculations reveal a low reliance of ΔGβ‡ on LUMO(β) energy levels. Additionally, Δgap values and their relevance exhibit inconsistencies with predicted reaction selectivity when comparing calculated values of ΔGα‡ and ΔGβ‡ (ΔΔG‡ = ΔGβ‡ – ΔGα‡). This is clearly illustrated in entries 2 (H3CO) and 4 (F), as shown in Figure 3. While LUMO(β) is higher than LUMO(α+γ) in both entries, ΔGα‡ is higher than ΔGβ‡ in entry 2 (ΔΔG‡ = −1.7 kcal mol–1), and ΔGα‡ is lower than ΔGβ‡ in entry 4 (ΔΔG‡ = 0.7 kcal mol–1). A substantial inconsistency is obtained for the vastly higher ΔΔG‡ value for entry 12 (tetra-CF3, 14.8 kcal mol–1) compared to that of entry 10 (tetra-Ph, 4.4 kcal mol–1), despite the relatively small difference in Δgap values (2.746 and 2.629 eV, respectively, Table 1). On the other hand, a distinctive variation in LUMO energies of entries 2 and 4 is observed relative to those of unsubstituted [3]cumulene (entry 7). Interestingly, a combined decrease in LUMO(β) and increase in LUMO(α+γ) energies resulted in significantly lower Δgap values; 1.225 and 1.371 for entries 2 and 4, respectively, versus 2.234 eV for entry 7 (Table 1). A straightforward projection of the calculated decrease in Δgap values, attributed to a single substituent only, allows for the anticipation of a major diminishing in the Δgap or even LUMO level reordering in tetrasubstituted [3]cumulenes. Indeed, LUMO(α+γ) is calculated to become higher in energy than LUMO(β) in entries 9 (tetra-H3CO) and 11 (tetra-F). However, the obtained Δgap values (−0.395 and −0.071 eV, respectively) are inconsistent with the remarkable [4 + 2] cycloaddition selectivity on the β position projected from the calculated ΔΔG‡ values for entries 9 (3.8 kcal mol–1) and 11 (9.5 kcal mol–1). Moreover, the substantial increase in the LUMO(α+γ) level observed in entry 9 is accompanied by a similar rise in the HOMO(α+γ) level (Table S1). This is due to the strong electron-donating effect of the four methoxy substituents on the conjugated α and γ double bonds. Consequently, the gap between cumulene HOMO(α+γ) and the CPDE LUMO becomes lower than that between the CPDE HOMO and cumulene LUMO(α+γ). Thus, an inverse electron-demand cycloaddition is expected to occur at the α and γ positions. As a result, a larger (and positive) Δgap = [gap(β)normal – gap(α+γ)inverse] is obtained for entry 9 (1.703 eV), providing a further major inconsistency in the reaction barriers. A similar outcome is obtained in entries 1 (inverse at the β position) and 8 (inverse at the α and γ positions) but with a much lower difference between gap values of inverse and normal cycloadditions (see Table S1). Therefore, to fully comprehend the reactivity of these π-systems, it is essential to consider factors beyond molecular orbital energies.

The cycloaddition of all selected [3]cumulenes is calculated to be highly exergonic with reaction energies (ΔG) ranging from −9.0 to −44.6 kcal mol–1 (see Table S3). Such high exergonicity suggests the improbable reversibility of the process. Moreover, the cycloaddition on the β position is significantly more exergonic, with ΔΔG ranging from −5.6 to −14.4 kcal mol–1, as shown in Table 1. This wide range of ΔG values turned our attention to the contribution of the reaction exergonicity to the discrepancies in reaction barriers (Marcus theory). This is reinforced by the consistently larger difference in intrinsic barriers (ΔΔGo‡) calculated using the Marcus equation (see Table S3) and compared to ΔΔG‡ (Table 1). Recalling that Δgap values in entries 10 and 12 are comparable, the comparison between their respective ΔΔG values (−12.7 and 0.6 kcal mol–1) is presumably the key for elucidating the substantial inconsistency in their reaction barriers (vide supra). This is supported by their lower difference in ΔΔGo‡ values (∼5 kcal mol–1) compared to the difference in ΔΔG‡ values (∼10 kcal mol–1). Moreover, the nearly equally exergonic cycloaddition at α and β positions in entry 12 suggests that the highest obtained disparity in barriers (ΔΔG‡ = 14.7 ≈ ΔΔGo‡ = 15.0 kcal mol–1) is likely due to the lack of thermodynamic contribution, i.e., kinetic factors prevail. On the other hand, the selectivity at the β position in entry 9 is mainly due to thermodynamic factors, while kinetic factors tend to balance each other out (ΔΔGo‡ = −0.1 kcal mol–1).

The penalty incurred due to the structural deformation of [3]cumulene during the progression of [4 + 2] cycloaddition at α and β positions is examined as the distortion energy (ΔGdis‡). ΔGdis‡ is calculated by subtracting the energy of the [3]cumulene-optimized structure from that of its distorted structure in the transition state (see Table S6). This is with the assumption of the comparable distortion energy of CPDE in all entries. So, the difference in cumulene distortion occurring at α and β positions is ΔΔGdis‡ (Table 1). Apart from tetrafluorinated [3]cumulene (entry 11), all obtained ΔΔGdis‡ values shown in Table 1 are positive. This implies that cycloaddition at the β position suffers from a higher cumulene distortion energy. Accordingly, the calculated intrinsic barriers are reasonably accounted for by the combined influence of two kinetic factors, ΔΔGdis‡ and Δgap, through the correlation presented in Figure 4 (R2 = 0.86). The obtained regression equation suggests that the impact of the Δgap is twice that of ΔΔGdis‡ on ΔΔGo‡. The cycloaddition preference at the β position in entry 11 (tetra-F) (ΔΔGo‡ = −6.4 kcal mol–1) is well attributed to both ΔΔGdis‡ and Δgap values being negative, with the impact of the cumulene distortion energy being dominant (−0.071 vs −4.7 kcal mol–1, respectively). This is in full agreement with the reported experimental reaction selectivity of tetrafluoro[3]cumulene with cyclohexadiene.27 On the other hand, the major deviation of tetra-H3CO (entry 9) in the correlation is due to the nature of the cycloaddition at the α position being inverse (vide supra), having its HOMO–LUMO gap significantly smaller than that of the normal one (see Table S1). Clearly, disregarding the tetra-H3CO entry (9) from the correlation results in an enhancement in the R2 value (to 0.90). In fact, entry 9 fits much better in the correlation when its Δgap for normal cycloaddition (−0.395 eV) is used in the regression equation, resulting in ΔΔGo(pred)‡ (−0.3 kcal mol–1) and ΔΔGo‡ (−0.1 kcal mol–1) being comparable. It is worth noting that the inverse cycloaddition cases of entries 1 and 8 have an insignificant impact on the correlation since they result in a very small difference in their corresponding Δgap values (normal versus inverse).

Conclusions

With all of the results above, we establish a foundation for understanding [3]cumulenes’ reactivity and highlight some of the main factors inducing their reaction selectivity. Our study demonstrates that moderate to high selectivity at β versus α and γ double bonds can be achieved in substituted [3]cumulenes. Specifically, [4 + 2] cycloaddition is kinetically more favorable at the α and γ positions while being thermodynamically more favorable at the β position. However, our results predict no significant selectivity between α and γ double bonds in monosubstituted [3]cumulenes. This paves the way for further research work on di- and trisubstituted [3]cumulenes targeting a higher selectivity between α and γ positions that can be useful in chemical synthesis. Notably, the selectivity in certain tetrasubstituted [3]cumulenes is not only enhanced but also reversed to favor the reaction at the β double bond. Using intrinsic barriers along with HOMO–LUMO gaps and [3]cumulene distortion energies as kinetic factors, we elucidated the reaction kinetic preferences. Our results explain that the observed reversed selectivity in tetrafluoro[3]cumulene is attributed mainly to the lower distortion energy required at the β double bond. Furthermore, our study predicts similar selectivity in tetramethoxy[3]cumulene due to the thermodynamic contribution lowering the reaction barrier at the β position. Further research is necessary to reveal additional kinetic factors influencing the reactivity of this unique functional group, allowing for a deeper understanding of its behavior. This will enable a greater control over its stability and enhance its use in chemical applications.
