Carnosinylation of Cardiac Antigens Attenuates Immunogenic Responses and Improves Function in Failing Hearts
Authors: Benjamin Doelling, Mamata Chaudhari, David Hoetker, Kenneth Brittian, Yibing Nong, Jonah K Stephan, Ibrahim Jouja, Helen I Janis, Thomas Mitchell, Marcin Wysoczynski, Aruni Bhatnagar, Steven P Jones, Shahid P Baba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure is associated with the accumulation of reactive lipid peroxidation‐derived aldehydes, such as acrolein, which form immunogenic protein adducts in the heart. Histidyl dipeptides, including carnosine (β‐alanine‐histidine), can covalently sequester these aldehydes. We tested whether increasing myocardial carnosine through β‐alanine supplementation reduces aldehyde stress, limits the immunogenicity of aldehyde‐modified proteins, and improves cardiac function during pressure overload‐induced heart failure.MethodsMale, wild‐type C57BL/6J mice underwent sham or transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery. β‐alanine was administered in drinking water either before TAC or beginning 4 weeks after TAC and continued for the remainder of the study. Cardiac function was evaluated by echocardiography. Myocardial histidyl dipeptides and aldehyde‐dipeptide conjugates were quantified by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry, and immune cell populations and CD4+ T‐cell activation were evaluated by flow cytometry.ResultsMyocardial carnosine and anserine levels were decreased at 3 and 8 weeks after TAC. β‐alanine administered either before or after TAC increased myocardial histidyl dipeptide levels, attenuated pathological cardiac remodeling and fibrosis, and reduced protein carbonylation in failing hearts. Carnosine binding to aldehyde‐modified proteins decreased their antigenic potential. In vitro, this reduced dendritic cell activation and CD4+ T‐cell responses. In vivo, β‐alanine pretreatment reduced the cardiac infiltration of CD4+ CD44+ effector T cells and CD11b+CD64−Ly6G+ neutrophils.ConclusionsIncreasing myocardial carnosine mitigates aldehyde stress, reduces the immunogenicity of aldehyde modified proteins and limits maladaptive immune responses, thereby preserving cardiac function during pressure overload. These findings support β‐alanine supplementation as a potential therapeutic strategy to attenuate inflammation and improve outcomes in heart failure.Keywords: aldehydes, carnosine, heart failure, inflammation, neoantigensSubject Categories: Basic Science Research, Contractile function

Heart failure is a complex pathological state that involves progressive cardiac dysfunction and significant increases in oxidative stress and inflammation. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Although oxidative injury and tissue‐damaging inflammation are prominent features of the failing heart, broad immunosuppression and antioxidant therapies have not been shown to be effective, and in some cases, may worsen heart failure by disrupting necessary reparative processes. 5 , 6 , 7 Effective and feasible strategies to target inflammation and oxidative stress are urgently needed but require a deeper understanding of the cellular and molecular processes that contribute to the pathophysiology of heart failure.

Inflammatory responses in the failing heart are triggered, in part, by the release of autoantigens which instigate nonresolving adaptive immune responses. 8 , 9 Such autoantigens can be generated by the binding of proteins to toxic lipid peroxidation derived carbonyls, such as acrolein and 4‐hydroxynonenal. 10 , 11 These carbonyls are the downstream effectors of oxidative stress that possess 2 highly electrophilic groups: an α, β‐unsaturated motif and a carbonyl group. Because of this structural motif, they react readily with nucleophilic side chains of proteins, particularly histidine and lysine. 12 , 13 Importantly, aldehyde‐modified protein retain a reactive electrophilic group that allow them to function as aldehyde modified damage associated molecular patterns (ADAMPs) capable of activating both innate and adaptive immune responses. 14 ADAMPs have been detected in the heart, 15 , 16 and anti‐ADAMP antibodies, present in patients with heart failure, are associated with protection against cardiovascular diseases. 17 However, there is no direct evidence linking ADAMPs to inflammation in the context of heart failure. Given that reactive aldehydes accumulate in the failing heart, we posit that ADAMPs contribute to inflammation in a pressure overload model of heart failure.

Reactive aldehydes in the heart are normally metabolized through several enzymatic pathways, such as aldehyde dehydrogenase and 16 aldo‐keto reductases, 15 and by conjugation with reduced glutathione, catalyzed by glutathione‐S‐transferases. 18 , 19 In addition to these pathways, reactive aldehydes are removed also via forming conjugates with histidyl dipeptides, such as carnosine (β‐alanyl‐L‐histidine) and anserine (β‐alanyl‐Nπ‐methyl histidine), which are synthesized via the enzyme CARNS (carnosine synthase) and carnosine methyl transferase, respectively. 20 , 21 Tissue levels of carnosine in both skeletal muscle 22 and heart 23 can be increased via β‐alanine supplementation, which enhances the clearance of reactive aldehydes. 24 , 25 Previous studies have shown that an increase in carnosine levels can diminish aldehyde stress during strenuous exercise, 26 protects the heart from ischemia reperfusion injury, 23 attenuates atherosclerosis, 27 and improves glycemic control in individuals with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. 28

When carnosine reacts with reactive aldehydes, the resulting carnosine‐aldehyde conjugate still retains a free electrophilic moiety capable of forming a covalent bond with the nucleophilic amino acids of proteins, generating carnosinylated proteins. 29 Although we have identified such carnosinylated proteins in aging hearts, 29 it remains unclear whether carnosine can also bind to the free aldehydic moiety of ADAMPs, thereby neutralizing their immunogenicity and modulating inflammatory responses and cardiac function during heart failure.

In the present study, we tested the hypothesis that increasing myocardial carnosine levels will sequester reactive aldehydes and carnosinylate and diminish the antigenicity of ADAMPs, thereby attenuating cardiac inflammation and dysfunction. We show that myocardial histidyl dipeptides are decreased in failing hearts. β‐alanine supplementation increases myocardial carnosine levels and sequesters reactive aldehydes, preserves cardiac function, and reduces ADAMPs antigenicity. Moreover, we show that carnosine‐mediated “capping” of ADAMPs limits CD4+ T‐cell activation, suggesting that this posttranslational modification may attenuate immune‐mediated injury in the failing heart.

METHODS

The authors declare that most supporting data are available within the article. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Animals

Wild‐type C57BL/6J and B6N.B6‐Tg (Nr4a1‐EGFP/cre) 820Khog/J (Nur77‐GFP) mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Mice were treated in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Institute of Laboratory Animal Resources, 2011) as adopted and promulgated by the National Institutes of Health. All animal experiments were performed under protocols approved by the University Committee for Animal Welfare at the University of Louisville.

Transverse Aortic Constriction Surgery and β‐Alanine Treatment

C57BL/6J male mice, 12 to 15 weeks old, were pretreated with either drinking water alone or drinking water supplemented with β‐alanine (20 g/L) for 7 days and then subjected to sham and transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgeries as described. 15 , 30 , 31 , 32 To examine the therapeutic potential of β‐alanine, mice were subjected to TAC for 4 weeks, divided into 2 groups and supplemented with either β‐alanine or water alone as mentioned. Supplementation was continued for the duration of the experiment, with water changing weekly. Briefly, following anesthesia (IP 50 mg/kg sodium pentobarbital and 50 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride), the mice were orally intubated with polyethylene‐60 tubing; and ventilated with a mouse ventilator (Hugo‐Sachs Elektronik). Analgesia (meloxicam, 20 mg/kg) was provided subcutaneously before surgery and 24 and 48 hours post surgery, as relevant. Before skin incisions, mice were also given local anesthetic (bupivacaine liposomal suspension, 5.3 mg/kg). The aorta was visualized following an intercostal incision. A 7–0 nylon suture was used to loop around the aorta between the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries. The suture was tied around a blunted 27‐gauge needle placed adjacent to the aorta to constrict the aorta to a reproducible diameter. The needle was removed, and the chest was closed in layers. The mice were extubated upon recovery from spontaneous breathing. The surgeon was blinded to mouse genotype and animal group assignment. Sham mice were subjected to the same procedure as the TAC group, except that the suture was not tied. Mice were followed for the indicated durations.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography of the left ventricle was performed using a Visual Sonics Vevo 3100 system equipped with a MX550D probe as described. 30 , 32 , 33 , 34 Serial echocardiograms were obtained at 4, 8, 12, and 16 weeks post TAC. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C throughout the procedure. We obtained 2‐dimensional images of parasternal long‐axis, short‐axis, apical 4‐chamber, and aortic arch views. End‐systolic and ‐diastolic volumes were determined from long‐axis B‐mode views, and ejection fraction was calculated as [(stroke volume)/(end‐diastolic volume)] × 100%. Left ventricular (LV) inner diameter during diastole, LV inner diameter during systole, and heart rate were determined from short‐axis M‐mode images. The sonographer was blinded to animal group assignments. The peak blood velocities before the point of constriction (V 0) and at the point of constriction (V 1) were measured via pulse‐wave Doppler at the aortic arch view 8 weeks post TAC. The estimated trans‐TAC pressure gradient (∆P) was then calculated using the Bernoulli equation ∆P=4 × (V 1 2−V 0 2), where velocities are in m/s and ∆P in mm Hg. TAC mice with pressure gradient <40 mm Hg were excluded. There were no significant differences in ∆P between supplemented and nonsupplemented mice after exclusion.

Measurement of Histidyl Dipeptides by Mass Spectrometry

Levels of histidyl dipeptides in the heart and plasma were determined using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) as described. 23 , 24 Pulverized cardiac tissue was homogenized in a solution of 10 mM HCl containing 5 μM carnosine‐d4 and 5 μM anserine‐d4 as internal standards. Homogenates were sonicated on ice, centrifuged, and collected supernatants were diluted 100× in a solution of 75:25 acetonitrile:water. Plasma samples were prepared by diluting plasma 10× in a solution of 75:25 acetonitrile: water containing 30 nM carnosine‐d4 and 30 nM anserine‐d4 as internal standards, followed by centrifugation and collection of supernatants. A 5 μL of sample was injected into a Waters Acquity ultra‐performance liquid chromatography H‐Class system equipped with a Waters Acquity bridged ethyl hybrid hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography column. Analytes were eluted using a binary solvent system consisting of 95% water, 5% acetonitrile, 10 mM ammonium formate, 0.125% formic acid (hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography A) and 5% water, 95% acetonitrile, 10 mM ammonium formate, 0.125% formic acid (hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography B). Elution began with 0.1:99.9 A:B at 0.55 mL/min, changing to 99.9:0.1 A:B over the course of 5 minutes followed by return to 0.1:99.9 A:B over 0.5 minutes. Eluates were then analyzed using a Xevo TQ‐S micro triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Transition ions used for quantification were as follows: carnosine, m/z 227 → 110; carnosine‐propanal, m/z 283 → 166; carnosine‐propanol, m/z 285 → 110; carnosine‐d4, m/z 231 → 110; N‐acetyl carnosine, m/z 296 → 110; anserine, m/z 241 → 109; anserine‐d4 245 → 109. Dipeptides were quantified by comparing peak areas to peak areas of the appropriate internal standard and heart samples were normalized to protein concentration.

Quantification of Protein Carbonyl Content

Cardiac lysates were evaluated for carbonyl content using a DNPH‐based colorimetric assay. 35 , 36 Heart tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer, sonicated on ice, and centrifuged. Protein concentration was determined by Lowry assay and samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL in diH2O. A 600 μL aliquot of diluted sample was incubated with 120 μL of 10 mM DNPH for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by an addition of 720 μL ice‐cold 20% trichloroacetic acid and 15 minutes incubation on ice. Samples were centrifuged, and supernatant was discarded and pellet washed with 20% trichloroacetic acid and centrifuged again. Supernatant was then discarded, and samples were repeatedly washed with 1 mL 1:1 ethyl acetate:ethanol and centrifuged for a total of 3 washes. After the final centrifugation, pellets were dried and resuspended in 600 μL of 6 M guanidine‐HCl. Absorbance at 400 nm was read, using 6 M guanidine‐HCl as a blank. Picrosirius Red Staining

Isolated hearts were perfused with 1 M KCl, embedded in 2% agarose and sliced using a McIlwain Tissue Chopper. Midline slices were incubated in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 hours, processed using a Tissue‐Tek VIP tissue processor (Sakura Finetek) and embedded in paraffin blocks using a Tissue‐Tek TEC 6 embedder (Sakura Finetek). An RM2255 rotary microtome (Leica Biosystems) was used to cut 4 μm‐thick sections that were mounted and dried on 25 mm × 75 mm glass slides. Slides were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by a 1‐hour incubation in 0.1% Direct Red 80 in picric acid. Slides were washed and dehydrated, and cover slips were mounted with Permount mounting media (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Stained sections were imaged using a Keyence BZ‐X800 brightfield microscope (Keyence Corporation) and images were analyzed using the BZ‐X800 Analyzer software (Keyence Corporation).

Wheat Germ Agglutinin Staining

To analyze the cross‐sectional area of cardiomyocytes, 4 μm heart sections were mounted on slides as described. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval at 100 °C in a citrate buffer: 2.4 g/L tribasic sodium citrate, 0.35 g/L citric acid, pH 6.0. The cooled sections were washed, incubated with wheat germ agglutinin working solution (1:200) for 1 hour. Sections were washed and background fluorescence was quenched by incubation with 0.1% Sudan black in 70% ethanol. Sections were washed and cover slips were mounted using VectaShield Antifade Mounting Media with DAPI (Vector Laboratories) and sealed with clear nail polish. Stained sections were imaged under a Keyence BZ‐X800 fluorescent microscope (Keyence Corporation). Cross‐sectional area of the cardiomyocytes was analyzed using the BZ‐X800 Analyzer software (Keyence Corporation).

Generation of Aldehydes and Aldehyde‐Modified Proteins

Malondialdehyde was prepared by incubating 500 mM 1,1,3,3‐tetramethoxypropane in 1 M HCl at 37 °C for 10 minutes followed by a 1:19 dilution in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) to quench the reaction. 37 Acrolein was prepared by incubating 250 mM acrolein dimethyl acetal in 100 mM HCl at 37 °C for 1 hour followed by a 1:9 dilution in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) to quench the reaction. 38 4‐HNE was purchased from Abcam and diluted to appropriate stock concentrations in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Hen egg lysozyme (HEL) was purchased from Roche and prepared at 2 mg/mL in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Cardiac tissue was homogenized in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), followed by sonication and centrifugation. Cardiac protein lysate diluted to 2 mg/mL in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) and HEL were incubated with 1 mM of either malondialdehyde (MDA), acrolein, or HNE at 37 °C for 1 hour. Following incubation, the samples were filtered using Amicon Ultra 3 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters (Sigma‐Aldrich) to remove unreacted aldehyde. The aldehyde‐protein adducts (2 mg/mL) were further incubated with either 10 mM carnosine at 37 °C or reduced with 10 mM NaBH4 at room temperature for 1 hour and filtered using 3 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters.

Western Blotting Analysis

Cardiac lysates from sham and TAC hearts were homogenized 10% (w/v) in RIPA buffer: 50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X‐100, 0.1% SDS with 1% Halt protease/phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Scientific). Lysates were centrifuged and the supernatants were analyzed via Western blotting as described previously. 39 Similarly, HEL modified with acrolein or HNE, both with and without carnosine, were immunoblotted using antiacrolein (1:1000), anti‐HNE (1:1000), and anticarnosine (1:1000) antibodies as noted followed by an appropriate HRP‐linked secondary antibody. Blots were developed using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio‐Rad) and images were captured using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio‐Rad). Blots were washed and stained for total protein content with Amido Black 10B. Chemiluminescent band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to total‐lane amido black signal.

Generation and Culture of Bone Marrow–Derived Dendritic Cells

Bone marrow–derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were prepared by incubation of bone marrow myeloid precursor cells with the growth factor GM‐CSF (granulocyte‐monocyte colony‐stimulating factor) as described before. 40 , 41 Briefly bone marrow was isolated by flushing the tibias and femurs of male, wild‐type (WT) C57BL/6J mice with RPMI‐1640. Single cell suspensions were generated by passing the bone marrow suspension through a 25 G needle, followed by a 70 μm filter. Cells were plated on untreated 10 cm plates at 2 × 105 nucleated cells/mL in BMDC media: RPMI‐1640 containing 10% heat inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 50 μM β‐mercaptoethanol, and 40 ng/mL GM‐CSF. Additional BMDC media was replaced on day 3, and half of the media was replaced with fresh media on day 6 post plating. On day 8, nonadherent and loosely adherent cells were collected and plated for further experiments. CD45+CD11b+ populations were verified as 80% + CD11c+ by flow cytometry and were thus considered BMDCs. For activation experiments, BMDCs from a single WT, naïve mouse were plated on nontreated 12‐well plates at 2 × 105 cells/mL in BMDC media and incubated with 100 mg/well of either HEL, MDA‐modified HEL, carnosinylated HEL‐MDA, or NaBH4 reduced HEL‐MDA for 6 hours. Cells were collected and stained for surface expression of CD45, CD11b, CD11c, CD80, CD86, and MHCII (major histocompatibility complex class II, I‐A/I‐E). Flow cytometry data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Splenocyte Culture and Activation

Spleens from both WT C57BL/6J and Nur77‐GFP mice were removed and placed in HBSS on ice. Splenocytes were liberated by grinding the spleen against a 70 μm strainer using the rubber end of a 5 mL syringe plunger. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500g for 5 minutes, then the pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) for 3 minutes at room temperature. Lysis was halted by adding 15 mL HBSS and the suspension was centrifuged as before. The cell pellet was resuspended and nucleated cells were counted and plated on nontreated 12‐well plates at 5 × 105 cells/mL in splenocyte media: RPMI‐1640 containing 10% heat inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 50 μM β‐mercaptoethanol. For HEL experiments, cells were treated with 200 μg/well of HEL, HEL‐MDA, carnosinylated HEL‐MDA, or NaBH4 reduced HEL‐MDA and allowed to incubate for 72 hours. For cardiac lysate experiments, cells were treated with 200 μg/well of lysate, aldehyde‐modified lysate, carnosinylated aldehyde‐lysate, or NaBH4 reduced aldehyde‐lysate for 72 hours. Cells were then collected and stained for surface expression of CD45, CD3e, CD4, and CD44 (see Table S1 for details). Flow cytometry data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 cytometer and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Quantitative Flow Cytometry of Cardiac Tissues and Mediastinal Lymph Nodes

Wild‐type C57/BL/6J mice were subjected to sham and TAC surgeries and pretreated with water alone or water containing β‐alanine as described previously. Hearts from the sham and TAC operated mice were isolated, cannulated through the aorta, and the coronary circulation was perfused with 37 °C HBSS for 5 minutes. The atria were removed, and the ventricles were finely minced using a sterile razor blade. Minced hearts were incubated in HBSS containing 1000 U/mL Collagenase II (Worthington Biochemical) for 45 minutes at 37 °C with occasional trituration using a serological pipette. 42 Digested hearts were passed through a 40 μm filter to create a single cell suspension. Isolated cells were centrifuged and resuspended in CSB (BioLegend) and nucleated cells were counted using an acetic acid/methylene blue solution (Stem Cell Technologies). Cells were liberated from isolated mediastinal lymph nodes (mLNs) by grinding the lymph nodes in CSB against a 70 μm strainer using the rubber end of a 5 mL syringe plunger. Cells from mLNs were counted using a Countess II automated cell counter (Thermo Scientific). The cells were stained by incubation with an antibody cocktail diluted in CSB for 20 minutes in the dark at room temperature followed by 2 washes with CSB. Cells were stained for surface expression of CD45, CD11b, Ly6G, CD64, CD3e, CD4, CD8a, and CD44 (see Table S1 for details). Flow cytometry data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa X‐20 cytometer and data were analyzed using FlowJo software v.10.10.0 (BD Biosciences). Cell populations were defined using the following gating strategies: Leukocytes: CD45+, CD4+ T cells: CD45+CD11b−CD3e+CD4+, CD44+ CD4+ T cells: CD45+CD11b−CD3+CD4+CD44+, CD8+ T cells: CD45+CD11b−CD3+CD8+, neutrophils: CD45+CD11b+CD64−Ly6G+, combined monocytes/macrophages: CD45+CD11b+Ly6G−CD64+, dendritic cells: CD45+CD11b+CD11c+.

Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was isolated from heart tissues using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA concentration and quality were determined using a Nanodrop 2000c Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed as described previously. 15 , 30 Briefly, cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript IV VILO kit (Invitrogen) with 1000 ng RNA as input. Polymerase chain reaction reactions were carried out in a 384‐well plate using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and a QuantStudio 5 System (Applied Biosystems) was used to perform real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (primer sequences in Table S2). Relative gene expression was normalized to the housekeeping gene Hprt and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using permutation‐based tests implemented in R (version 4.4.1) using the coin package (version 1.4.3). For single‐variable comparisons between 2 groups, a tw2o‐sample permutation t test was used and for ≥3 groups, a permutation‐based 1‐way ANOVA was performed. Permutations were performed using the oneway_test() function with an approximate Monte Carlo distribution from 10 000 permutation resamples. Following 1‐way ANOVA, pairwise comparisons were conducted between preselected pairs using the same permutation framework and P values adjusted using Sidak's post hoc correction. For samples with n<4, a parametric 1‐way ANOVA was performed followed by pairwise comparison and Sidak's post hoc correction using Prism software v. 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software).

For echocardiogram data, P values were determined using a linear mixed effects model fitted with the R package lme4 (version 1.1.36). Missing observations were handled implicitly by the mixed model framework without subject exclusion. P values for fixed effects were derived using parametric bootstrap likelihood ratio tests implemented in the R package pbkrtest (version 0.5.3) with 10 000 bootstrap resamples. Post hoc pairwise group comparisons at each timepoint were performed using 2‐sample permutation tests via the R coin package, with the approximate Monte Carlo distribution estimated from 10 000 resamples. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Sidak's post hoc correction.

For flow cytometry data, P values were determined using permutation‐based 2‐way ANOVA implemented in the R package lmPerm (version 2.1.6). Permutation was performed using probabilistic stopping with a maximum of 10 000 permutation iterations. Pairwise comparisons among preselected pairs of groups were again conducted using 2‐sample permutation tests via the coin package as described previously. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Sidak's post hoc correction.

Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. For experiments with technical replicates, BMDCs were generated from a single naïve WT mouse and plated in triplicate for each treatment group.

RESULTS

Myocardial Histidyl Dipeptide Homeostasis Is Disturbed in the Failing Heart

Previously, we showed that myocardial histidyl peptides, including carnosine (β‐alanyl‐L‐histidine) and anserine (β‐alanyl‐Nπ‐methyl‐L‐histidine), are decreased in failing hearts 43 ; however, the specific stage of heart failure at which this depletion occurs and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. To determine when histidyl dipeptides are reduced during the progression of heart failure, we subjected the WT male C57BL/6J mice to either sham or TAC surgery and measured myocardial histidyl dipeptide levels at 3 and 8 weeks post surgery using liquid chromatography–MS/MS.

We found that 3 weeks after TAC, ejection fraction remained unchanged (sham: 72 ± 5% versus TAC: 70 ± 4%); however, myocardial carnosine and anserine levels were decreased ~2‐fold compared with sham operated hearts (Figure 1A). A similar reduction (~2‐fold) was observed for 8 weeks post TAC (Figure 1B), indicating that depletion of the dipeptide's plateaus sometime between 3 and 8 weeks.

Carnosine homeostasis is regulated by synthesis via CARNS, degradation by CNDP2 (carnosinase), and cellular transport by amino acid transporters (PEPT2 [peptide transporter 2], TAUT [taurine transporter], Figure 1C). 44 To assess whether altered synthesis or transport contributes to carnosine depletion, we examined the expression of these enzymes and transporters at both the gene and protein levels. No changes were observed in the expression of CARNS, CNDP2, PEPT2, and TAUT at the mRNA (Figure 1D) or protein levels, 3 weeks post TAC (Figure 1E and 1F). In fact, CARNS expression in the TAC heart increased compared with sham despite lowered carnosine levels, although the change was not statistically significant. Overall, these findings suggests that reductions in carnosine levels are not due to altered synthesis, hydrolysis, or transport.

In failing hearts, there is a progressive increase in the generation of highly reactive carbonyls, such as acrolein, MDA, and 4‐HNE, which can conjugate with carnosine. 15 , 23 To evaluate whether aldehyde conjugation contributes to carnosine depletion, we measured the levels of carnosine‐acrolein adduct carnosine‐propanal, and its reduced form, carnosine‐propanol, as well as carnosine‐HNE and its reduced product, carnosine‐DHN in sham and TAC hearts using liquid chromatography–MS/MS. After 3 weeks of TAC, levels of carnosine‐propanal increased in TAC hearts but did not reach statistical significance (Figure 1G; P=0.09), whereas carnosine propanol remained unchanged (Figure 1H). In contrast, both carnosine‐propanal (Figure 1I) and carnosine‐propanol (Figure 1J) were significantly decreased 8 weeks post TAC compared with sham hearts. No detectable levels of carnosine‐HNE or ‐DHN were observed in either the sham or TAC hearts. Collectively, these results suggest that decreases in myocardial histidyl dipeptides precede overt heart failure and carnosine‐aldehyde conjugates generated in failing hearts may be extruded during late stages of heart failure.

β‐Alanine Supplementation Attenuates Cardiac Dysfunction and Remodeling in Response to Pressure Overload

Following our observation that myocardial carnosine decreases before the onset of heart failure, we next investigated whether increasing myocardial carnosine before the onset of heart failure could influence the progression of heart failure. Oral administration of β‐alanine, the rate limiting precursor for carnosine synthesis, enhances its synthesis and elevates intracellular levels of histidyl dipeptides. 23 , 24 Therefore, to test the effects of increased carnosine on heart failure progression, we placed WT C57BL/6J mice on either β‐alanine in drinking water (20 g/L in drinking water) or drinking water alone for 1 week before TAC surgery. The mice were placed on β‐alanine water for the entirety of the experimental period, and cardiac function was measured by echocardiography at 4 and 8 weeks after TAC (Figure 2A).

At 4 weeks after TAC, no significant differences in cardiac parameters were observed between β‐alanine treated and water‐treated TAC mice (Figure 2C through 2H); however, by 8 weeks post TAC, β‐alanine supplementation resulted in a significant reduction in LV mass compared with mice given water alone (Figure 2C). This decrease in LV mass was accompanied by an improved ejection fraction (Figure 2B and 2D), and decreases in left ventricle inner diameter at both systole (Figure 2E) and diastole (Figure 2F). Additionally, β‐alanine supplemented TAC mice exhibited a trend toward decreased end‐systolic (Figure 2G) and end‐diastolic volumes (Figure 2H), although these changes did not reach statistical significance.

Gravimetric analysis of hearts revealed a significant decrease in heart weight in β‐alanine supplemented mice compared with water alone (Figure 2I). Fibrosis area, quantified by collagen staining, showed a significant decrease in collagen deposition in the hearts of mice supplemented with β‐alanine (Figure 2J and 2K). However, cardiomyocyte cross‐sectional area was unchanged in β‐alanine supplemented mice (Figure S1). Collectively, these data indicate that β‐alanine supplementation mitigates cardiac hypertrophy and dysfunction in response to pressure overload.

Increasing Myocardial Carnosine Diminishes Carbonyl Stress in Failing Hearts

Previous work has shown that lipid peroxidation products accumulate in the failing hearts and contribute to cardiac dysfunction. 15 , 45 Histidyl dipeptides, including carnosine, bind to these reactive aldehydes. 27 , 29 Because β‐alanine is the precursor amino acid for carnosine, which can be further methylated to anserine 21 or acetylated to acetylcarnosine, 46 we performed an extensive profiling of histidyl dipeptides and their aldehyde conjugates in TAC‐operated hearts, to determine the impact of β‐alanine treatment on the removal of toxic aldehydes. In hearts from TAC mice supplemented with β‐alanine, carnosine (m/z 227) and anserine (m/z 241) levels increased significantly (~10–15‐fold) compared with hearts from TAC mice given water alone, whereas N‐acetyl carnosine (m/z 296) levels were unchanged (Figure 3A).

To assess the impact of β‐alanine supplementation on aldehyde scavenging in hearts, we measured conjugates of carnosine with acrolein and HNE: carnosine‐propanal (m/z 283) and carnosine‐propanol (m/z 285), carnosine‐HNE (m/z 383), and conjugates of anserine with acrolein: anserine‐propanal (m/z 297) and anserine‐propanol (m/z 299, Figure S2). We next examined whether carnosine‐aldehyde conjugates could be extruded into circulation by measuring histidyl dipeptide‐aldehyde conjugates in the plasma. Circulating carnosine levels were significantly increased, whereas anserine levels showed an increasing trend but did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3F). Like the pattern observed in the heart, carnosine‐propanal conjugates were significantly increased in the plasma of β‐alanine supplemented TAC mice (Figure 3G), whereas carnosine‐propanol remained unchanged (Figure 3H), suggesting that carnosine‐propanal may indeed be excreted from the heart into circulation.

To evaluate the overall impact on aldehyde stress by β‐alanine supplementation, we measured the total protein carbonyl content and found a significant reduction in the protein‐dinitrophenylhydrazone in the β‐alanine treated compared with the water‐treated TAC hearts (Figure 3E). Together, these results indicate that increasing myocardial carnosine levels reduces carbonyl stress in failing hearts by increasing the clearance of toxic aldehydes and decreasing protein carbonyl content.

β‐Alanine Supplementation After TAC Surgery Dampens Adverse Remodeling and Increases Aldehyde Scavenging

To examine the efficacy of β‐alanine, as an intervention for heart failure, WT C57BL/6J mice were subjected to TAC surgery for 4 weeks, divided into 2 groups, and supplemented with either β‐alanine (20 g/L in drinking water) or drinking water alone. Serial echocardiography performed at 8, 12, and 16 weeks post TAC (4, 8, and 12 weeks post supplementation Figure 4A) showed reductions in LV mass 12 weeks post supplementation with β‐alanine (Figure 4B), decrease in LV anterior wall thickness at both diastolic (Figure 4C) and systolic (Figure 4D) after 12 and 16 weeks of β‐alanine supplementation, and a reduction in LV posterior wall thickness at diastole after 16 weeks of β‐alanine supplementation (Figure 4E). However, there was no effect on ejection fraction. Expression of prohypertrophic genes Myh7 and Nppa was significantly lower in the β‐alanine supplemented compared with the water alone treated mice (Figure 4F).

Carnosine Binds With Aldehyde Modified Protein Adducts

The α, β‐unsaturated aldehydes possess 2 electrophilic reaction centers; one electrophilic moiety forms a covalent bond with proteins via either Michael adduct or Schiff base formation. Following Michael addition, the aldehyde‐modified protein retains another aldehyde moiety. Similarly, the Schiff base formation between aldehydes and proteins leaves a free α, β‐unsaturated imine (Figure 5A). The free electrophilic moiety in these aldehyde‐modified proteins acts as an ADAMP that activates dendritic cells and consequently CD4+ T cells. 14 , 47 Because carnosine binds with reactive aldehydes, we next investigated whether it could also bind with the free electrophilic moieties in ADAMPs forming carnosinylated ADAMPs.

The aldehyde‐modified proteins were further incubated with 10 mM carnosine, reduced with 10 mM NaBH4, or reduced with NaBH4 followed by carnosine incubation (both 10 mM). C, Modified HEL was immunoblotted and developed with anticarnosine, anti‐HNE, and antiacrolein antibodies. D, Heart lysates from mice subjected to sham, TAC, and TAC + β‐alanine supplementation were immunoblotted and developed using anticarnosine antibody. To test this, HEL was incubated with HNE (1 mM) or acrolein (1 mM) to generate ADAMPs. These modified proteins were then incubated with carnosine (10 mM), NaBH4 (10 mM), or NaBH4 followed by carnosine (Figure 5B). Immunoblotting with anti‐HNE, antiacrolein, and anticarnosine antibodies showed strong immunoreactivity of ADAMPs alone with antiacrolein and anti‐HNE antibodies, whereas ADAMPs incubated with NaBH4 showed no immunoreactivity. ADAMPs incubated with carnosine had strong immunoreactivity with anticarnosine antibodies; however, incubation of ADAMPs with NaBH4 followed by carnosine abolished carnosine immunoreactivity (Figure 5C), indicating that carnosine binds to the intact aldehyde moiety of ADAMPs.

Given that ADAMPs are elevated in failing hearts, as indicated by increased carbonyl‐protein adducts, and that carnosine can bind with the free aldehyde moiety of ADAMPs, we next assessed whether increasing myocardial carnosine during heart failure increases the formation of carnosinylated proteins. We found that carnosinylation of proteins was increased in the hearts of TAC mice supplemented with β‐alanine when compared with both sham hearts and TAC hearts (Figure 5D and 5E). Taken together, these results indicate that increasing myocardial carnosine during heart failure reduces cardiac carbonyl stress and promotes the formation of carnosinylated proteins.

Carnosinylation of Aldehyde Modified Proteins Diminishes Their Immunogenicity

Because ADAMPs activate dendritic cells and initiate adaptive immune responses, we next asked whether carnosine‐mediated modification of these aldehyde moieties alters their immunogenic potential. To test this, BMDCs were generated from WT C57BL/6J mice using GM‐CSF and then incubated with 100 μg/mL unmodified HEL, HEL‐MDA, carnosinylated HEL‐MDA, or NaBH4 reduced HEL‐MDA for 6 hours. BMDC activation was measured by flow cytometry. HEL‐MDA significantly upregulated MHCII and CD80 expression in BMDCs, whereas both carnosinylation and NaBH4 reduction abrogated these effects, implying that the binding of carnosine to aldehyde moiety could diminish the recognition of ADAMPs by BMDCs (Figure 6A through 6D).

A wide variety of reactive aldehydes are generated in failing hearts. 15 , 45 , 48 Therefore, we next examined whether the free aldehyde moieties on cardiac proteins can directly activate CD4+ T cells and whether neutralizing these aldehydes with carnosine affects CD4+ T‐cell activation. To assess this, we isolated splenocytes from naïve Nur77‐GFP mice, which express GFP (green fluorescent protein) under the control of the Nur77 promoter‐a sensitive and specific readout of T‐cell receptor signaling. 49 Heart lysate from naïve WT C57BL/6J mice was modified with either MDA, acrolein, or HNE (1 mM), followed by treatment with carnosine (10 mM) or NaBH4 (10 mM) as described in the Methods.

Taken together, these data show that cardiac aldehyde modified proteins function as neoantigens capable of activating DCs and CD4+ T cells and that carnosine effectively masks these moieties, diminishing their neoantigenic potential.

β‐Alanine Supplementation Inhibits CD4 + T‐Cell Activation and Neutrophil Infiltration in Failing Hearts

Because increasing carnosine in failing hearts scavenges reactive aldehydes, diminishes carbonyl stress, and neutralizes the neoantigenic potential of ADAMPs, we next tested whether β‐alanine supplementation affects CD4+ T cell accumulation and activation of CD4+ T cells in hearts and the heart‐draining mLNs during heart failure. Male WT C57BL/6J mice were pretreated with β‐alanine in drinking water or drinking water alone for 1 week, followed by sham or TAC surgery. β‐alanine supplementation continued for the next 4 weeks, after which single cell suspensions from the hearts and mLNs were analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 7A). In the hearts of TAC operated mice, the total number of leukocytes was significantly increased compared with sham hearts and this increase was significantly attenuated by β‐alanine treatment (Figure 7B). CD4+ T cells were increased in TAC hearts compared with sham and this was attenuated with β‐alanine supplementation but did not reach statistical significance (Figure 7C). We found that CD4+CD44+ T cells were increased in TAC hearts and significantly reduced in β‐alanine treated TAC hearts (Figure 7D). Additionally, CD8+ T cells and neutrophils were also increased in TAC hearts and reduced upon β‐alanine treatment (Figure 7E and 7F). In contrast, monocytes and macrophages remained unchanged between the sham and TAC hearts (Figure 7G). In all cell populations, β‐alanine supplementation had no effect on cell numbers following sham surgery.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the impact of increasing myocardial histidyl dipeptides on pathological cardiac remodeling. Our data show that histidyl dipeptides carnosine and anserine are depleted before the onset of overt heart failure, likely because they are consumed through conjugation with reactive aldehydes. Pretreatment with the carnosine precursor β‐alanine increased myocardial carnosine levels, prevented cardiac dysfunction, and increased the removal of toxic aldehydes from heart in a pressure overload‐induced model of heart failure. Similar to pretreatment, β‐alanine administration after 4 weeks of TAC attenuated cardiac remodeling and increased myocardial carnosine levels and removal of toxic aldehydes from failing hearts. We further demonstrate that carnosine binds not only with free aldehydes, thereby decreasing aldehyde stress, but also with the free electrophilic group of aldehyde‐modified proteins, diminishing their antigenic potential and inhibiting CD4+ T‐cell activation during heart failure. These findings suggest that the aldehyde‐modified proteins generated in failing heart act as neoantigens, triggering innate and adaptive immune responses. Collectively these studies suggest that scavenging toxic aldehydes with carnosine either directly or through β‐alanine supplementation may represent a novel and promising strategy to mitigate aldehyde stress and ADAMP‐induced inflammation and associated dysfunction in the failing heart. These findings support a model in which carnosine links aldehyde detoxification to suppression of immune activation in the failing heart.

Depletion of Myocardial Carnosine in Failing Hearts

Histidyl dipeptides, namely carnosine and anserine, are abundant in tissues with high glycolytic activity such as the heart and skeletal muscle, 23 , 50 with concentrations ranging from 0.1to 1 mM in healthy vertebrate hearts. 23 , 51 Previous work in hypertrophic and failing hearts has reported decreased myocardial carnosine following 8 weeks of TAC. 43 Targeted metabolomic analysis of serum in patients with heart failure has similarly shown that carnosine as well as its constituent amino acids, β‐alanine and L‐histidine, were decreased. 52 Using the TAC model, which recapitulates the gradual transition from compensatory hypertrophy to decompensated heart failure, we found that myocardial carnosine levels decreased before the onset of overt heart failure, and this decrease in carnosine did not change further during the progression of heart failure. Although depletion of carnosine in heart failure has been reported, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Carnosine homeostasis is maintained by enzymes (CARNS, CNDP2) and transporters (PHT1 [phosphate transporter 1] and TAUT). 44 We found no changes in the expression of these regulators either at the onset of hypertrophy or in the failing heart, suggesting that altered synthesis or transport is likely not responsible for the observed depletion. Instead, we propose that carnosine levels are decreased as the dipeptide is consumed by binding with unsaturated aldehydes. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed an increase in the carnosine‐acrolein conjugate, carnosine‐propanal, during early remodeling, followed by a decline during overt heart failure.

Previous work has shown that conjugates of carnosine with aldehydes are excreted largely in urine. 25 , 29 Therefore, changes in the levels of these conjugates suggest that these dipeptides are consumed by binding with reactive aldehydes to form conjugates that may subsequently be extruded in the urine. However, because urinary levels of these conjugates were not measured in this study, further work is required to confirm this possibility. Other contributors to decreased carnosine could include altered activities of CARNS and CNDP2, enzymes involved in carnosine synthesis or degradation, or changes in β‐alanine availability. Anserine levels were also decreased during the progression to heart failure, although no anserine‐aldehyde conjugates were detected, suggesting that binding with reactive aldehydes is unlikely to contribute to their decrease. Anserine is synthesized by methylation of carnosine by carnosine methyltransferase. 53 The concurrent reduction in both carnosine and anserine suggests that depletion of carnosine may limit anserine bioavailability for methylation; however, further studies are needed to clarify whether this reflects substrate limitation or changes in enzyme activity or both. 54

Preservation of Cardiac Function and Decrease in Aldehyde Load by β‐Alanine Feeding

Increasing myocardial levels of carnosine has been shown to protect against myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury. 23 More recent work has reported that rats subjected to myocardial infarction and supplemented with β‐alanine plus histidine and subjected to exercise training had improved running distance and time to exhaustion compared with rats trained with exercise only; however, no benefit on cardiac function was observed by β‐alanine and histidine supplementation. 55 In contrast, our study reports the benefit of β‐alanine both as pre‐ and posttreatment in ameliorating the decline in cardiac function during the late stages of heart failure.

β‐alanine pretreatment prevented contractile dysfunction, reduced LV wall thickness and normalized heart weight/tibia length. Furthermore, posttreatment with β‐alanine attenuated the progression of pathological hypertrophy, by decreasing the LV mass, LV diameter at systolic and diastolic, expression of hypertrophic genes Myh7 and Nppa compared with the untreated TAC mice, highlighting the therapeutic benefits of β‐alanine. Nevertheless, discrepancy with previous studies may be attributed to differences in dosage and disease models. Notably, in our study, we used 8 g/kg of β‐alanine, which increased the myocardial carnosine ~3‐fold compared with the 0.25 g/kg of β‐alanine used by Stefani et al., 55 which did not elevate myocardial carnosine levels.

Extensive evidence from human and animal studies of heart failure shows progressive increases in tissue levels of lipid peroxidation products, such as 4‐HNE and acrolein in failing hearts. 15 , 56 , 57 , 58 These reactive aldehydes can cause contractile and mitochondrial dysfunction in chronic heart failure 16 and are directly correlated with impairment of LV contractility in patients with heart failure. 59 Consistent with these observations, we found a substantial increase in the cardiac aldehyde load, characterized by increased DNPH derivatization of proteins in failing hearts. Importantly, increasing myocardial carnosine either before the onset of TAC or after TAC enhanced the removal of aldehydes, particularly acrolein that was extruded as a carnosine‐propanal conjugate, thereby attenuating the aldehydic burden during heart failure. Although anserine levels were also increased following β‐alanine treatment, and anserine can bind lipid peroxidation products, 60 no detectable levels of anserine‐aldehyde conjugates were observed either in the heart or plasma of β‐alanine fed TAC mice. Because carnosine is the predominant histidyl dipeptide present in humans, whereas anserine is more abundant in rodents, 51 our findings that carnosine effectively removes toxic aldehydes from failing hearts, closely parallel the anticipated response in human heart failure.

Cardiac fibrosis is a hallmark of pathological remodeling. In failing hearts, resident fibroblasts transition to myofibroblasts phenotype, characterized by increased collagen deposition, secretion of inflammatory cytokines and elevated expression of α smooth‐muscle actin. 61 Recent work by Monroe et al., showed that reactive aldehyde 3,4‐dihyroxyphenylglcoaldehyde forms, 4‐dihyroxyphenylglcoaldehyde–protein adducts that stimulate fibroblast proliferation and induce profibrotic gene expression in cardiac fibroblasts. Importantly, carnosine treatment blunted these profibrotic responses. 62 Consistent with these findings, our study shows β‐alanine treatment attenuated cardiac fibrosis in TAC operated mice hearts, suggesting that enhanced aldehyde scavenging by carnosine mitigates fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling. Collectively, these data indicate that enhancing myocardial carnosine by supplementing β‐alanine removes toxic aldehydes and impedes the progression of heart failure. 16

Capping of Aldehyde Modified Proteins and Impact on Immunogenicity

Lipid peroxidation products possess 2 highly electrophilic centers. One electrophilic groups can bind cysteine, histidine, and lysine residues of proteins via Schiff base formation or Micheal addition followed by Amadori rearrangement, generating a range of aldehyde modified protein adducts. 12 , 14 These aldehyde modified proteins are not end products; they are intermediates that can bind with free thiols via a Michael‐type addition forming glutathiolated protein adducts. 12 Because the aldehyde‐modified proteins still retain at least 1 electrophilic moiety, and carnosine binds to electrophiles through the terminal or imidazole amines, we expected carnosine to bind and carnosinylate the free aldehyde moiety of aldehyde modified proteins. Indeed, our observations show that the incubation of HNE and acrolein modified HEL with carnosine increased the immunoreactivity with anticarnosine antibody, whereas this immunoreactivity was abolished in the aldehyde‐modified HEL reduced with sodium borohydride. These findings suggest that carnosine becomes incorporated into aldehyde modified proteins by binding to the remaining electrophilic moiety.

Previously, we reported that carnosinylated proteins accumulate in aged and ischemic heart tissues 29 ; however, to the best of our knowledge, whether the carnosinylation of aldehyde‐modified proteins could be influenced by heart failure conditions or altered by increasing carnosine levels has not been reported. The present study therefore reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism by which increasing myocardial carnosine in the failing heart promotes protein carnosinylation and may reduce the neoantigenic potential of aldehyde modified proteins.

Aldehyde‐Modified Damage Associated Molecular Patterns and the Role of Carnosine in Modulating Adaptive Immunity

Extensive evidence shows that dying cardiomyocytes from failing hearts release multiple DAMPs, which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors and activate innate and adaptive immune responses; however, many of these DAMPs can have both protective and harmful effects. For example, intracellular S100 proteins, HSP60 (heat shock protein 60), HMGB1 (high‐mobility group box 1), mitochondrial DNA, and isolevuglandin‐modified proteins released from failing hearts are all recognized as DAMPs. 9 , 17 , 45 , 63 HSP60 is essential for normal mitochondrial and cardiomyocyte homeostasis. 55 In contrast, differential effects of HMGB1 inhibition have been observed during ischemia–reperfusion injury in the heart. 8 , 64 , 65 Inhibition of HMGB1 protects heart against ischemia–reperfusion injury, 66 whereas administration of HMGB1 improves ejection fraction following myocardial infarction. 67 Isolevuglandin‐modified protein adducts lead to CD4+ T‐cell activation and promote heart failure. 45 This duality highlights the need to distinguish harmful DAMPs from those DAMPs that may have benign or protective functions.

Aldehyde‐modified protein adducts have long been identified as triggers of innate and adaptive immune responses in several cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, ischemia reperfusion, 23 diabetic caridiomyopathy, 68 and exposure to environmental contaminants. 69 , 70 The free electrophilic group of aldehyde‐modified proteins causes them to act as neoantigens, or ADAMPs. These ADAMPs are taken up and presented by dendritic cells, leading to the activation of T cells. 69

The results of our present study show that model ADAMPs (HEL‐MDA) activate DCs, as indicated by increased expression of MHCII and costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86. We further found that the incubation of HEL‐MDA with whole splenocyte populations isolated from Nur77GFP transgenic T‐cell reporter mice mice increased GFP expression in CD4+ T cells, whereas reduction of the aldehyde moiety by sodium borohydride diminished DC and CD4+ T‐cell activation. These findings are aligned with previous observations that the aldehyde moiety specifically causes aldehyde‐modified proteins to behave as immunogenic antigens. 14

Our results show that aldehyde‐modified heart lysate, which mimics failing hearts enriched with ADAMPs, and homogenates from hypertrophic TAC hearts upregulated GFP expression in CD4+ T cells from Nur 77GFP splenocyte populations. These findings support our model that ADAMPs generated in failing hearts are presented by DCs, which ultimately activate CD4+ T cells during heart failure.

Our findings also highlight the pathophysiological significance of protein carnosinylation. Capping of the free aldehyde moiety by carnosine of HEL‐MDA, aldehyde‐modified heart lysate, or ADAMP‐containing heart failure lysate diminished the GFP expression of CD4+ T cells from Nur77GFP mice. Although we did not identify the specific ADAMPs generated in failing hearts that trigger immune responses, further studies are needed to identify the precise epitopes responsible for this inflammatory activation. Nevertheless, our data strongly suggest that ADAMPs generated in failing hearts act as neoantigens that activate DCs and ultimately CD4+ T cells. Importantly, we report that posttranslational modification of ADAMPs by carnosine diminishes signaling between the DCs and CD4+ T cells. Therefore, this posttranslational modification of ADAMPs by carnosine could represent an effective mechanism that dampens DC‐mediated and CD4+ T‐cell activation in failing hearts.

Diminished CD4 + T Cell Activation and Neutrophil Infiltration in Failing Hearts by β‐Alanine Feeding

Several studies using the TAC model of heart failure have demonstrated that DCs, neutrophils, and CD4+ T cells infiltrate the left ventricle and contribute to the pathogenesis of heart failure. 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 DCs also accumulate in the mLNs that drain the heart. 45 Within the left ventricle, these accumulated DCs promote adverse remodeling by presenting DAMPs and activating CD4+ T cells, which then infiltrate the myocardium. Notably, depletion of BMDCs significantly attenuates LV fibrosis and hypertrophy in response to TAC. 75 Similarly, studies using T‐cell deficient mice show recombinase‐activating gene null, 71 T‐cell receptor null, 72 and MHCII−/− 71 mice do not develop adverse cardiac remodeling in the onset of TAC. These findings collectively suggest a critical role for DC–T‐cell interactions in mediating cardiac inflammation and tissue remodeling.

Oxidized proteins generated in response to TAC or hypertension can serve as antigens presented by DCs, leading to T‐cell activation. 45 , 76 We found that TAC promotes the accumulation of DCs and CD4+ T cells in the mLNs and heart, respectively. This increase parallels the rise in aldehyde stress, implicating ADAMPs as potential triggers for DCs and CD4+ T‐cell activation. Our results show that the population of effector T ‐cells (CD4+ CD44+ T cells) increases significantly in response to TAC and that β‐alanine treatment reduces this accumulation. Moreover, β‐alanine supplementation also attenuated TAC induced neutrophil infiltration. Previous studies have shown that lipid peroxidation products promote neutrophil chemotaxis 77 and that CD4+ T cells prolong neutrophil survival time, 78 suggesting multiple mechanisms driving neutrophil accumulation in failing heart. Therefore, our findings that β‐alanine supplementation increases sequestration of lipid peroxidation products and reduces activated CD4+ T cells in failing hearts suggests a combined effect that contributes to decreased neutrophil accumulation. Together, these findings support the hypothesis that increasing myocardial carnosine through β‐alanine supplementation enhances the sequestration of reactive aldehydes and promotes carnosinylation of ADAMPs in the failing heart. This process likely reduces aldehyde stress, attenuates the neoantigenicity of ADAMPs, and consequently mitigates the maladaptive immune response.

CONCLUSIONS

Although oxidative stress is a well‐established contributor to the pathogenesis of heart failure, treatment with antioxidants has had limited clinical success in treating cardiovascular disease. 79 , 80 Our data suggest that increasing myocardial carnosine can attenuate heart failure through binding with free aldehydes, which are key downstream effectors of oxidative stress. In addition, carnosine binds the aldehyde moiety of ADAMPs, providing a novel mechanism to dampen immune activation during heart failure.

Importantly, because myocardial carnosine levels can be safely and effectively increased through oral administration of β‐alanine, 23 our findings could be of significance also in decreasing the progression of other major cardiovascular diseases including atherosclerosis, where ADAMPs promote macrophage conversion to foam cells and increases the expression of TLRs (toll‐like receptors) in macrophages. 81 , 82 Similarly, elevating myocardial carnosine via β‐alanine treatment may reduce the immunogenicity of ADAMPs in diabetic cardiomyopathy, which is marked by excessive accumulation of advanced glycation end products and a shift of T cells toward proinflammatory phenotype that contributes to disease progression 68 . 83 , 84 Collectively these findings suggest that increasing myocardial carnosine represents a promising intervention to reduce inflammation and preserve cardiac function in cardiovascular diseases.

Study limitations

This study was performed in male mice. In human skeletal muscles, carnosine concentrations are 30% to 80% higher in men compared with women, 54 , 85 , 86 similarly, we have found that both carnosine and anserine levels are 4‐5‐fold lower in female murine hearts compared with male murine hearts at baseline (data not shown). Given that myocardial levels of carnosine in females are lower compared with males, we reason that increasing myocardial carnosine in female failing hearts might have a higher impact on the cardiac function during heart failure. Previous studies using orchiectomized male mice and testosterone‐supplemented female mice have shown that androgens influence muscle carnosine levels. 87 Therefore, understanding the complex sex‐dependent role of carnosine in cardiac function represents an important area of study requiring an in‐depth independent investigations in the future.

Another limitation of the study is that β‐alanine supplementation increases carnosine levels in the brain and skeletal muscle. Therefore, whether the beneficial effects of β‐alanine on heart failure outcomes are entirely cardiac‐specific remains unclear. Further studies using cardiac‐specific overexpression or deletion of Carns will be necessary to determine how altering the myocardial carnosine affects ADAMP formation and inflammatory response under heart failure conditions. 23 , 39 In addition, the specific ADAMPs generated in failing hearts and the core peptides responsible for their immunogenicity remains to be identified and the extent of their immunogenic potential requires further investigation.
