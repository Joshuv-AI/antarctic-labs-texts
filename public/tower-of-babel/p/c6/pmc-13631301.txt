MANF in the lateral septum dynamically regulates endoplasmic reticulum function in chronic stress response
Authors: Jinmei Ye, Cong Duan, Huibing Pei, Jie Weng, Weidi Wang, Jiashuo Xu, Yuan Li, Tifei Yuan, Daihui Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder (MDD) is primarily influenced by chronic stress as an environmental risk factor, yet the precise temporal mechanisms governing disease initiation and progression remain largely undefined. This study systematically characterized the dynamic molecular adaptations within the lateral septum (LS), a limbic hub crucial for stress processing, across the critical transition from acute to chronic stress exposure. Through longitudinal transcriptomic analysis, we identified a biphasic dynamic response in the LS, initial widespread gene upregulation during acute stress phases progressively shifted to predominant downregulation as stress persisted. This temporal reprogramming was characterized by persistent activation of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress pathway, particularly involving the unfolded protein response. Most notably, the ER stress-related factor mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF) exhibited a striking temporal pattern, showing rapid upregulation during acute stress, followed by sustained suppression throughout the chronic stress phases. Functional investigations established that LS-specific MANF knockdown directly induced ER dysfunction, impaired structural and functional synaptic plasticity, and promoted depressive-like behaviors. Conversely, MANF overexpression effectively rescued these pathological phenotypes. Our findings illuminate the crucial temporal dimension of LS transcriptomic reorganization in MDD pathogenesis and identify MANF as a pivotal regulator bridging ER stress adaptation to neural circuit dysfunction, providing a mechanistic foundation for early intervention strategies and biomarker development.

Introduction

It is anticipated that by the year 2030, major depressive disorder (MDD) will become the leading factor contributing to the global burden of disease, marking it as a significant global health crisis [1, 2]. Characterized by high incidence, disability, and recurrence rates, MDD patients face a suicide risk 20 times higher than the general population, accounting for nearly half of global suicide cases [3, 4]. Its clinical heterogeneity, unclear biological mechanisms, and lack of objective diagnostic markers result in low treatment efficacy [5]. Chronic stress is a key driver of MDD pathogenesis, as prolonged exposure disrupts central nervous system (CNS) responses to environmental threats, triggering maladaptive emotional, cognitive, and behavioral changes [6–8]. Traditional investigations in depression neuroscience have primarily focused on molecular profiling at disease endpoint stages, only limited number of studies pay attention to time points during the progression of depression. Moreover, these studies focus on time points after the onset of depression and predominantly within a network of established emotional processing regions, comprising the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala[9, 10]. However, these stress-induced neural dysregulations, spanning from acute to chronic pathological remodeling, underlie MDD progression, highlighting the urgency of mapping stress-related molecular pathways for early intervention strategies.

The lateral septum (LS) is a critical hub for integrating external stressors and internal states to regulate emotion and social behavior [11, 12]. It responds robustly to acute stress [13] and, owing to its dense neuromodulator receptors and limbic connectivity, mediates chronic stress-induced psychopathology like depression [14–17]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that chronic stress paradigms, such as chronic social defeat stress (CSDS) and chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS), result in hyperactivity in LS neurons and drive depressive-like behaviors in rodents [18, 19]. Furthermore, bidirectional manipulation of specific LS neuronal subtypes (e.g., GABAergic somatostatin-positive neurons) can directly modulate depressive phenotypes, establishing a causal role for the LS in the stress-to-depression transition [15, 20]. Despite these advances, the early dynamic molecular mechanisms governing the maladaptive shift from acute stress responses to chronic pathological states remain poorly characterized, significantly limiting the development of early intervention strategies for stress-induced MDD.

This study establishes the LS as an essential node in the progression from stress to MDD. Longitudinal transcriptomic profiling revealed a biphasic dysregulation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress pathways: a transient, adaptive upregulation triggered by acute stress, and its progressive collapse during chronic stress. Central to this transition was mesencephalic astrocyte-derived neurotrophic factor (MANF), which exhibited a distinct temporal pattern, sharply upregulated initially then suppressed chronically. Functionally, LS-specific MANF knockdown induced ER dysfunction, impaired synaptic plasticity, and exacerbated depressive-like behaviors, whereas MANF overexpression rescued these deficits. These findings define MANF as a dual-phase regulator of stress adaptation, whose chronic suppression is pivotal in the transition to pathology. Our results position MANF-driven ER stress dynamics not only as a potential biomarker for maladaptive early stress responses, but also as a promising target for therapeutic interventions aimed at blocking the development of depression.

Methods and materials

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice, procured from GemPharmatech, were kept in groups of five per cage for a week before the experiments. Retired CD-1 male breeders, about six months old, from GemPharmatech, were housed alone. Housing conditions included a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00) and a room temperature maintained between 22 °C and 25 °C. With ad libitum access to food and water, all mice were handled under procedures approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee and in compliance with the Shanghai Mental Health Center’s guidelines.

Sample sizes were determined based on standard practices in preclinical neuroscience and previous publications to ensure adequate statistical power. Mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups using a random number table. Investigators were fully blinded to group allocation during all behavioral tests and morphological quantifications. No animals or tissue samples were excluded from analyses. To minimize the number of animals used (in compliance with the 3 R principles of animal experimental ethics) while ensuring the consistency of experimental subjects and reducing inter-individual variability, the same animals were used for multiple behavioral tests, with strict control of the time interval between tests to avoid confounding effects.

Chronic social defeat stress (CSDS)

The chronic social defeat stress protocol involved placing a male C57BL/6 J mouse into the cage of an aggressive CD-1 resident mouse within the latter’s home cage, repeated for 10 consecutive days. The defeated mouse was then placed across a plexiglass barrier until the next day’s session [21]. All mice were sacrificed 24 h after the last stress session or behavioral test. Detailed experimental descriptions and methodologies are provided in the supplementary materials.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral tests were performed during the light phase. Mice were habituated to the testing room for at least 2 h before testing. Experimenters were blinded to group allocation. Specifically, a minimum interval of 24 h was maintained between any two consecutive behavioral tests to prevent stress accumulation and habituation effects.

Social interaction test (SIT)

Following the social defeat phase, an SI testing phase is carried out to evaluate the social behavior of C57BL/6 J mice. During this phase, the mice are positioned in an open-field arena equipped with a wire mesh enclosure that houses a new aggressive CD-1 mouse. The duration spent by the C57BL/6 J mouse in the interaction zone was recorded under two conditions: with the target (CD-1) mouse present and with it absent. Their actions were captured by a video tracking system located directly overhead with Topscan (CleverSys) and analyzed with Noldus. The social interaction ratio (SI ratio) was defined as the quotient of the interaction time with the target mouse present to the interaction time with it absent.

Forced swimming test (FST)

The FST in mice involves placing the mice in a cylinder filled with water, the temperature of which is maintained at 25 °C. The mice undergo observation for 6 min via a video tracking system, and the duration of immobility, swimming, or attempts to climb the walls is recorded. Immobility is considered a measure of the passive stress response, such as learned helplessness or depression. In the final 4 min, immobility time was analyzed by Noldus.

Open field test (OFT)

Each mouse was individually introduced to the center of an open-field arena (40 × 40 × 30 cm) to initiate the test. It was then allowed 15 min to freely explore the area under low - light conditions. The behavioral activities of the mice were recorded using a ceiling-mounted video tracking system running the Topscan software (CleverSys). Subsequently, the Noldus system was used to quantify both the time spent by each mouse in the central zone and the total distance traveled.

Acute stress

The acute stress methods are described as follows: (1) Sudden tail suspension (TS). During the test session, we quickly suspended the tails of the test mice within 5 s to minimize interference from unrelated stimuli. The suspension height was 20–30. (2) Unexpected forced swimming (FS). The mice were swiftly transferred to a water-filled cylinder. The water temperature was consistently maintained at 25 °C.Each test lasted for 5–10 s. (3) Unexpected social defeat stress (SDS). Put the mouse, as an intruder, into the cage where CD-1 is resident and observe the attacking behavior of CD-1. Take the mice out of the cage after one or two effective attacks (lasting more than 5 s).

Bulk RNA-seq

The mice were humanely euthanized 24 h following the experimental procedure. The LS tissues were meticulously dissected rapidly, and promptly cryopreserved on dry ice. For each individual specimen, the tissue samples collected from a pair of mice were pooled. Three biological replicates were included in each experimental group to ensure the findings were robust and reproducible. Sequencing was carried out utilizing the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, which produced 150 base-pair (bp) paired-end reads. To pre - process the sequencing data, Fastp [22] was utilized to eliminate low-quality raw fastq reads. Alignment of the high-quality reads to the reference genome was performed using HISAT2 [23]. After that, FPKM [24] and read counts were calculated. For differential expression analysis, Statistically significant genes/transcripts were identified using DESeq2 based on the thresholds of |FC | > 1.2 and P < 0.05 [25]. A thorough and detailed delineation of the protocol for RNA isolation, library preparation, and transcriptome sequencing is presented in the supplementary materials.

qRT-PCR

After administering isoflurane for anesthesia and sacrificing the animals, the LS was isolated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN). For all the RNA samples under examination, the 260/280 absorbance ratios were measured and found to lie within the range of 1.8–2.1 (NanoDrop2000), indicating good RNA purity. PrimeScript was used to carry out reverse transcription (Takara, RR036A). qPCR amplification was conducted on an Applied LightCycler480 System using SYBR green (Takara, RR820). The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification. Each cycle involved denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, coupled with annealing and extension at 60 °C for 60 s. A dissociation curve analysis was subsequently conducted by gradually heating the samples to 95 °C to confirm the specificity of the amplification and the presence of a single product. The resulting data were analyzed via the ΔΔC(t) method, comparing the C(t) values across the tested conditions. All related primers are listed in the supplementary materials.

Western blot (WB)

Once the animals were put under with isoflurane and euthanized, the LS was quickly dissected. Using a tissue homogenizer (Servicebio, KZ-II), tissues from the LS were homogenized for 1 min in M-PER Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 78501) to extract total protein. The brain homogenate was then subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. Following this, the membrane fraction was obtained using the Membrane and Cytosol Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0033) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the protein concentration of the resultant extracts was determined employing a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit. (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225). Finally, 12–15% SDS-PAGE gels were used to separate the protein samples. Antibodies specific to MANF (Affinity Biosciences, DF12836, 1:1000), HSPA5 (NOVUS, NBP1-06274, 1:3000), PSD95 (Servicebio, GB11277, 1:100), and β-actin (Bioworld Biotech, AP0060, 1:8000) were utilized. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH).

Immunochemistry

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane; subsequently, transcardial perfusion was performed with ice-cold saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Next, brains were carefully harvested, post-fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C, and then transferred to 30% sucrose in Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS). After being sectioned coronally (40 μm) on a Leica CM1950 microtome, the brain slices were stored at 4 °C. They were then washed three times in PBS, followed by a 1 h block at room temperature in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100, 1% BSA, and 10% goat serum. Overnight incubation with primary antibodies was carried out at 4 °C. After primary antibody incubation, three washes with PBS (supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100) were performed. A subsequent 1 h incubation at 37 °C was carried out with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, 1:1000). Following nuclear counterstaining with DAPI, image acquisition of representative fields was conducted on an Olympus confocal microscope. The immunofluorescence assay used the following primary antibodies: anti-c-Fos (Abcam, ab190289, 1:1000); anti-MANF (Sigma-Aldrich, SAB3500384, 1:50); anti-HSPA5 (Affinity Biosciences, AF5366, 1:200). The number of DAPI-positive cells was quantified to define total cellular density within the LS. The number of cells double-labeled for target protein (c-Fos/MANF/HSPA5) and DAPI was counted, and the density of double-positive cells was calculated and expressed as the number per 0.5 mm² in the corresponding brain region. All quantifications were performed in a blinded manner.

Transmission electron microscopy

Animals underwent transcardial perfusion and were sacrificed for transmission electron microscopy. Fixation of brain tissues was performed using PBS containing 1.25% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde. The samples were subsequently treated with 1% OsO4 in phosphate buffer for 2 h for post-fixation. Following dehydration and resin embedding, the <1 mm³ tissue blocks were sliced into 60–80 nm ultrathin sections. These sections were subjected to staining with 2% uranyl acetate, and image acquisition was conducted on a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700). Systematic random sampling was employed to capture ultrastructural images across multiple non-adjacent brain sections. The relative area occupied by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) profiles was quantified as the proportion of cytoplasmic area using ImageJ software (NIH), according to a previously established protocol [26]. In brief, regions of interest (ROIs) were manually delineated along the ER membrane within each sampled field, and ImageJ was used to compute the corresponding ER area. For each experimental group, 3 biological replicates were analyzed, with at least 10 randomly selected, non-overlapping fields quantified per sample in a fully blinded manner. Synapse number and postsynaptic density (PSD) thickness were similarly assessed in a blinded fashion using ImageJ software (NIH), following a validated protocol [27]. For each sample, no fewer than 10 random non-overlapping fields were examined, with 3 biological replicates included per group. A stereological method was not applied; however, systematic unbiased sampling was implemented to ensure representative and reliable quantification.

Stereotaxic surgery

Wild-type mice were subjected to isoflurane anesthesia and securely positioned in a stereotaxic instrument (RWD Life Science). A bilateral injection of 0.4 μL virus was bilaterally infused over 5 min into the lateral septum (LS), targeting the stereotaxic coordinates from bregma: AP + 0.97 mm, ML ± 0.38 mm, DV −3.42 mm, using a 33-gauge Hamilton needle. The needles were retained in place for an additional 10 min post-infusion.

For fiber photometry, AAV vectors expressing jGCaMP7s, PFD-rAAV-hSyn-jGCaMP7s-WPRE-hGH polyA, were delivered (5.13 × 10¹² vg/mL). Optic fibers suitable for long-term implantation, made with a 200 μm core and 0.22 NA, were inserted through ceramic zirconia ferrules and placed in the LS. The cranial implant was securely anchored to the skull using light-curing composite resins. Following surgical placement, the incision site was meticulously closed with sutures and prophylactically treated with topical antibiotics to mitigate infection risks. Optogenetic stimulation protocols and fiber photometry experiments were initiated after a 3-week postoperative recovery period to ensure proper tissue healing and stable integration of the optical interface. Detailed procedure for optical fiber implantation was performed as described [28].

For knockdown of MANF, adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors expressing MANF were delivered, AAV-U6-shRNA (MANF)-CMV-mCherry-SV40 polyA (5.21 × 10¹² vg/mL) or rAAV-U6-shRNA(scramble)-CMV-mCherry-SV40 polyA (5.47 × 10¹² vg/mL). For overexpression of MANF, AAV vectors expressing MANF were delivered, rAAV-CMV-MANF-P2A-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (5.09 × 10¹² vg/mL) or rAAV-CMV-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (5.09e+12vg /mL). For knockdown of HSPA5, AAV vectors expressing HSPA5 were delivered, AAV-U6-shRNA2 (HSPA5) -CMV-mCherry-polyA (5.29 × 10¹² vg/mL) or rAAV-U6-shRNA (scramble)-CMV-mCherry-SV40 polyA (5.47 × 10¹² vg/mL). For chemogenetic activation of LS neurons, AAV-hSyn-hM3D(Gq)-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-polyA (5.27 × 10¹² vg/mL) or control AAV-hSyn-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-polyA (5.18 × 10¹² vg/mL) was injected bilaterally into the LS. For chemogenetic inhibition of LS neurons, AAV-hSyn-hM4D(Gi)-EGFP-WPRE-hGH-polyA (5.23 × 10¹² vg/mL) or control AAV-hSyn-EGFP-mCherry (5.44 × 10¹² vg/mL) was infused bilaterally into the LS. For this study, all viruses were sourced from BrainVTA Co., Ltd., located in Wuhan, China. To ensure strong viral expression, viral injections were administered at least 3 weeks prior to behavioral testing.

Drug administration

For chemogenetic activation or inhibition, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) (LKT, c4759) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Ambeed, A1458605) to prepare a 10 mg/mL stock solution, which was further diluted 1:100 in 0.9% sterile saline before use. CNO was administered by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 1 mg/kg body weight 1 h before the onset of behavioral testing. All control mice received an equivalent volume of vehicle solution (DMSO diluted 1:100 in saline) following the same injection schedule.

Fibre photometry

Fiber photometry was performed following an established protocol [29]. Mice underwent a 3-day habituation to the patch cord following a 3-week recovery from surgery. A Fiber Photometry System (RWD Life Science) connected with a 200 μm, 0.37 NA fiber-optic patch cord (Inper) was used to record calcium fluorescence in the LS during acute stress exposure. GCaMP7s signals were measured at 470 nm, with 415 nm used for autofluorescence correction. In every trial, the light power at the fiber tip remained within the range of 40–80 μW. After a 5 min habituation period, recordings included a 2 min baseline followed by a 5 s acute stress exposure. Using custom MATLAB scripts, signals were analyzed, and ΔF/F was calculated as the percentage change relative to the baseline mean fluorescence. Video frames were synchronized with signal acquisition to temporally align behavioral data with fluorescence traces. LS neuronal responses were quantified as the average ΔF/F during the 2 s pre-event and 6 s post-event periods.

Statistics

All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality and variance homogeneity were verified prior to all parametric statistical tests. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism10 and MATLAB, with all tests being two-tailed. Statistical analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test for paired samples and the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between two unpaired groups. For multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All detailed statistical results can be found in the corresponding figure legends. A significance threshold (α) of 0.05(two-tailed) was established a priori for all tests, with the following notations: ns (not significant, P ≥ 0.05), * (P < 0.05); ** (P < 0.01); *** (P < 0.001); **** (P < 0.0001).

Results

The lateral septum serves as a core hub in acute and chronic stress responses

To identify brain regions critical for chronic stress responses in depression, we integrated a 10-day CSDS model with c-Fos mapping (Fig. 1A). We next characterized LS responses to acute stressors. Using fiber photometry in mice expressing the calcium indicator GCaMP7s in the LS (Fig. 1D-E), we found all challenges—tail suspension (TS), forced swimming (FS), and social defeat stress (SDS)—elicited rapid and significant increases in LS Ca²⁺ activity (Fig. 1F–K), aligned with previous reports of LS reactivity to environmental stimuli [13, 30]. Notably, the most substantial response (ΔF/F) was observed during SDS, specifically during the attack episodes by an aggressive CD-1 mouse (Figs. 1J, K), exceeding the ~20 and ~25% increases seen in TS and FS, respectively (Figs. 1F, G, H, I). These findings establish that the LS neuronal activity is strongly modulated by acute stressors, with social threat being a particularly powerful activator.

To confirm the causal role of LS neuronal activity in depression-like behaviors, we performed bidirectional chemogenetic manipulations. In WT mice, activation of LS neurons via hM3D(Gq) was sufficient to induce depression-like behaviors, as evidenced by increased immobility in FST and TST, reduced center time in OFT (Supplementary Fig. 1G–J), but without alteration in general motor activity (Supplementary Fig. 1K). In CSDS mice, inhibition of LS neurons via hM4D(Gi) significantly reversed the depression-like phenotypes, including deficits in social interaction, and FST immobility (Supplementary Fig. 1L–O), without a significant difference observed in total locomotor distance (Supplementary Fig. 1P). These findings suggested that aberrant activity of LS neurons is both necessary and sufficient to drive depression-like behaviors.

Temporal transcriptional dynamics in the LS underlie depressive-like behavior development

To delineate the dynamic progression of pre-depressive stress states, we systematically analyzed temporal patterns of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the CSDS group compared to controls. Notably, while KEGG pathway analysis revealed enrichment profiles exhibited temporal specificity, the persistent alterations of “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” pathways emerged as a hallmark throughout all experimental phases (Fig. 2E-H). A biphasic regulation pattern was evident, with initial upregulation on Day 1 (Fig. 2E) followed by progressive downregulation during subsequent phases (Days 4–10; Fig. 2F-H). This transition from compensatory activation to exhaustion of ER stress pathways represents a fundamental molecular shift in stress adaptation.

Gene ontology (GO) analysis of ER-related biological processes corroborated this biphasic regulation pattern. Acute stress (Day 1) activated unfolded protein response (UPR) mechanisms, while later time points exhibited sustained suppression of protein folding chaperones and ER structural components (Fig. 2I-L, Supplementary Fig. 2M-P). The early upregulation phase was characterized by enrichment in “response to unfolded protein,” “ER stress response,” and “chaperone binding,” reflecting initial adaptive efforts. By Day 4, these pathways showed significant downregulation, particularly affecting “protein refolding” and “ER chaperone complexes,” indicating progressive failure of proteostatic mechanisms.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further detailed this biphasic ER dynamic: acute stress (Day 1) upregulated chaperone complexes and endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi intermediate compartment components, while Days 4–10 showed progressive attenuation of proteostasis mechanisms, particularly affecting protein refolding systems (Supplementary Fig. S3A-M). The suppression of “misfolded protein binding” and “protein refolding” pathways from Day 4 onward suggests a collapse in the quality control systems that normally maintain proteostasis under stress conditions.

Collectively, our results capture a stress-phase-dependent transcriptional dynamics in the LS, transitioning from acute compensatory upregulation to chronic adaptive suppression of ER pathways, thereby providing a mechanistic framework for how chronic stress disrupts cellular homeostasis and produces depressive-like phenotypes. The temporal coupling between transcriptional dynamics and behavioral manifestations suggests a causal relationship between molecular adaptation failures in the LS and the pathogenesis of depression.

Temporal regulation of MANF coordinates dynamic ER stress networks in LS stress response

To identify core molecular changes during chronic stress, we integrated DEGs across four CSDS phases (Days 1/4/7/10) and identified eight genes persistently differentially expressed at all stages, including Egr1, Manf, Hspa5, Hspb1, Ndn, Plod1, Sdf2l1, and Thbd (Fig. 3A). Among them, Manf and Hspa5 are two key components of ER stress. To further determine whether the expression patterns of these candidates aligned with the biphasic regulatory model, we utilized Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) analysis on the chronological datasets in the order of first, forth, seventh, and tenth Day. This screening process yielded 13 significant co-expression modules, for which temporal expression trajectory plots were systematically generated (Supplementary Fig. 4A and S5A-M). Our time-resolved gene interaction network analysis using Cytoscape [33] across four distinct temporal stages demonstrated MANF’s persistent centrality (Fig. 3C-F). Notably, sustained Manf-Hspa5 co-regulation across all time points (Fig. 3C-F), consistent with prior studies that MANF orchestrates proteostasis through direct modulation of HSPA5 (BiP) activity [32, 34]. Moreover, both genes exhibited remarkably coherent expression patterns during this temporal progression, suggesting potential functional coordination within the regulatory network of our chronic stress model. Apart from Hspa5, numerous genes involved in ER stress coherently interacted with Manf. After 1 day of stress, Manf hub coordinates ER stress activation, with 20 of 26 gene interactors upregulated, such as Xbp1, Dnajb11, Serpinh1, Pdia4, and Pdia (Fig. 3C). Most interactors exhibited downregulation at the following three time points. Those involved in ER stress included Hspa1a, Hspa1b, and Hsph1 (Fig. 3D-F). This biphasic MANF interactome dynamics, characterized by early network mobilization, mirrors its transcriptional trajectory and the ER stress phase transition, solidifying MANF’s role as a molecular gatekeeper governing the transition from acute ER stress adaptation to chronic proteostasis failure in LS.

Temporal dysregulation of MANF-ER stress axis underlies synaptic remodeling in chronic stress-induced depression

We next validated these molecular findings at protein and structural levels. At the mRNA level, the expression trends of Manf and Hspa5 showed a transient increase on the first day of CSDS, followed by a continuous decline (Fig. 4A-B, Supplementary Fig. 6A-H). The immunofluorescence results revealed temporally distinct decreases in protein levels of MANF and HSPA5, with HSPA5 reduction (Day 4) emerging earlier than MANF depletion (Day 7) (Fig. 4C-F, Supplementary Fig. 6I-P). Western blot analysis confirmed the decrease of both proteins on Day 10 (Fig. 4G-H), a time point coinciding with robust depressive-like behaviors. The delayed protein-level changes relative to mRNA fluctuations suggest the involvement of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that warrant further investigation. Given the established link between chronic stress and ER dysfunction [35], along with MANF’s crucial role in ER stress response [36], we examined how prolonged stress impacts ER homeostasis in the LS. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) documented progressive luminal expansion in CSDS mice (Fig. 4I-J, Supplementary Fig. 6Q-S), providing structural evidence of ER stress. Molecularly, chronic stress suppressed multiple unfolded protein response (UPR) markers (ATF6, IRE1α, XBP1, ATF4) and related chaperones (Fig. 4L-P, Supplementary Fig. 6U-X). Meanwhile, TEM analysis showed a significant reduction in synapse number and postsynaptic density (PSD) thinning with temporal progression in CSDS mice (Fig. 4Q-S), which was corroborated by a marked decrease in PSD95 protein levels (Fig. 4T-U). in line with the documented correlation between depression-related proteostatic failure and synaptic pathology [37], our results suggest that chronic stress-induced disturbance in MANF-ER stress axis contributes to synaptic dysfunction. Furthermore, observed alterations in mitochondrial morphology, such as shrinkage (Fig. 4K), suggest that the organellar dysfunction extends beyond the ER, implicating aberrant inter-organelle contacts in the pathophysiology of chronic stress, as reported previously [35, 38, 39]. This further suggests broader organellar dysfunction in chronic stress.

Discussion

Our study reveals the LS as a chronologically sensitive hub for stress adaptation, undergoing stage-specific transcriptional remodulation during depression progression. We identify a biphasic ER stress response, initial UPR activation followed by sustained proteostasis failure, as a hallmark of chronic stress pathophysiology. Central to this process, MANF acts as a temporal gatekeeper. Its early upregulation coordinates adaptive responses, while its subsequent depletion drives ER dysfunction and dysregulation from HSPA5. This MANF-dependent trajectory directly culminates in synaptic destabilization, evidenced by PSD95 loss and ultrastructural decay. The spatiotemporal alignment of molecular dysregulation, cellular pathology, and behavioral phenotypes establishes a causal cascade from proteostatic failure to neural impairment (Fig. 6H).

ER stress as a unifying node in temporal dynamics of stress maladaptation in the LS

Converging evidence suggests that ER stress and misfolded protein accumulation are key components of MDD pathology. Under physiological conditions, molecular chaperones and quality control mechanisms maintain proteostasis through precise protein folding and targeted degradation of misfolded proteins via proteasomal, lysosomal, and autophagic pathways [40]. When ER homeostasis is disrupted, the unfolded protein response (UPR) is triggered, which orchestrates recovery through enhanced folding capacity, increased degradation, or ultimately, apoptosis [41, 42]. Under prolonged stress conditions, however, persistent ER stress generates misfolded proteins that overwhelm these adaptive mechanisms, leading to UPR dysfunction [43]. While previous studies have documented ER-related gene alterations in MDD patients [36, 43] and demonstrated that ER stress mitigation ameliorates depressive-like behaviors in rodents [20, 35], the temporal dynamics of ER stress progression throughout chronic stress exposure remain largely unexplored. Our study addresses this critical gap by implementing a longitudinal systems biology approach specifically within the LS, enabling comprehensive characterization of ER stress as a persistent feature, rather than merely a terminal event, across the entire stress adaptation continuum.

MANF as a multifunctional proteostasis integrator

MANF is a protein that can be induced by ER stress and is crucial for preserving protein homeostasis. Although the precise regulatory mechanisms require further elucidation, evidence suggests ATF6 and XBP1 activate the MANF promoter under stress conditions [34, 44]. Intracellularly, MANF binds to the nucleotide-binding domain of HSPA5 when it is in the ADP-bound state, triggering downstream UPR signaling [45]. Beyond its roles in ischemic and Alzheimer’s disease, MANF dysregulation has been linked to mood disorders, though the neural mechanisms remain unclear [46, 47]. Our study demonstrates reduced MANF levels in the LS of mice with stress-induced depression-like behaviors, and reveals its involvement in depressive progression via ER stress-mediated impairment of neuroplasticity, suggesting a therapeutic potential.

Our data also provide a valuable resource for contextualizing depression-related transcriptional networks. Beyond identifying novel targets, we observed conserved gene expression patterns across pre-depression and depression stages (e.g., Egr1, Hspb1, Ndn, Plod1, Sdf2l1, Thbd). While Egr1 [48] and Hspb1 [49] have been previously associated with depression, the roles of Ndn, Plod1, Sdf2l1, and Thbd in chronic stress represent novel findings. However, the precise functions of these genes throughout stress adaptation require further investigation. Several key questions remain unresolved, particularly regarding MANF’s cell-type-specific effects within LS subpopulations and non-neuronal cells, as well as the structural basis of its interaction with HSPA5. Addressing these questions will be essential for fully understanding MANF’s integrative role in proteostasis and its therapeutic relevance for MDD.

From organelle dynamics to translational opportunities

Our study extends the established link between ER-mitochondria contact sites and depression to the LS, demonstrating that chronic stress induces progressive ultrastructural alterations in organelle architecture, including disrupted inter-organelle distances and mitochondrial morphological abnormalities (Fig. 4K). Unlike prior research focused on terminal disease stages, our temporal analysis captures these dynamics across the stress continuum, revealing their progression from early adaptation to pathological states. These findings position organelle homeostasis as a promising interventional target, suggesting that modulating ER-mitochondria interactions during stress exposure may confer resilience against depression pathogenesis.

The molecular regulators of these organellar changes also offer novel translational avenues. Organelle-associated proteins may serve as biomarkers for stratifying depression risk or monitoring disease progression [50]. While quantifying subcellular dynamics in vivo remains technically challenging, emerging methods, such as in situ cryo-electron tomography and organelle-specific biosensors, present opportunities to bridge this gap. Our longitudinal LS gene atlas further provides a molecular framework for validating stage-specific targets. Coupled with advanced computational approaches, including machine learning and AI-based modeling, these resources bring us closer to achieving predictive diagnostics and targeted interventions for stress-related psychiatric disorders.

Limitations

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, the bulk RNA-seq approach, while valuable for capturing population-level dynamics, cannot resolve cell-type-specific contributions within the heterogeneous LS. Thus, the observed molecular changes in MANF and ER stress pathways may originate from distinct, unidentified cellular subpopulations. Second, while we established MANF’s functional importance, its actions as a secreted factor likely involve paracrine signaling to glial cells (e.g., astrocytes and microglia), a non-cell-autonomous dimension that remains unexplored. Finally, to establish MANF’s translational potential as a biomarker, its biphasic dynamics must be validated in human MDD cohorts to confirm their conservation across species. These points do not undermine our core findings but instead delineate productive avenues for future research.
