Comparative evaluation of the effect of various transdermal patches and oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs on postoperative endodontic pain and quality of life – A placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial
Authors: Balaram Alagappan, Sathish Sundar, Natanasabapathy Velmurugan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the efficacy of preemptive ketoprofen transdermal patch (KTP) administration against oral ibuprofen in reducing postoperative endodontic pain following single-visit endodontic treatment of mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and apical periodontitis.Materials and Methods:Patients fulfilling the eligibility criteria were randomized into four groups. Group 1 received oral ibuprofen; Group II received a placebo oral tablet, Group III received a KTP, and Group IV received a placebo patch 1 h before commencement of root canal treatment. Pain scores were evaluated at baseline, 4, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after the treatment. Oral Health-related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) was assessed at baseline and 2 weeks after the treatment.Results:All the groups had a significantly lower incidence of postoperative pain and improved OHRQOL. At 4 h, the pain level was significantly lower with an oral ibuprofen tablet, and at 12 and 24 h, it was significantly lower with transdermal ketoprofen. There was no significant difference in the OHRQoL among the four groups.Conclusion:Oral ibuprofen is most effective at the 4-h interval, whereas transdermal ketoprofen has the best pain reduction in the 12–24-h window due to its sustained-release pharmacokinetics. A ketoprofen patch is a promising alternative for postoperative pain management.

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative pain is a common yet undesirable consequence of root canal treatment (RCT), with an incidence of 25% – 69% within the first 24 h.[12] Strategies to minimize postoperative pain include using preemptive/postoperative analgesia, the crown-down technique of canal preparation, reciprocating files, special irrigation apparatus, number of visits,[3] and occlusal reduction.[4]

Oral pre-emptive analgesic administration, such as ibuprofen, is one of the most convenient options for pain mitigation. With ketoprofen as an alternative administered transdermally, it overcomes the disadvantages[5] of the oral route. It is a controlled delivery system that works over a long duration.[6] with better patient compliance. The efficacy of transdermal ketoprofen for pain management in single-visit endodontics of molars is still inconclusive.

Earlier studies have ignored the use of suitable negative controls, such as a placebo transdermal patch (PTP), for their transdermal patch interventions. In the present study, a PTP and a placebo oral tablet (POT) group were used as negative controls for the two routes of administration, respectively.

This prospective randomized clinical trial aimed to evaluate the effect of pre-emptive transdermal patch administration compared to pre-emptive oral analgesic administration on post-operative pain and oral health-related quality of life following single-visit RCT in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis.

The null hypothesis was that there was no difference in the effect of preemptive oral and transdermal administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs on postoperative pain level and oral health-related quality of life following single-visit RCT in mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and apical periodontitis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Review Board (MADC/IEC-II/029/2023) of Meenakshi Academy of Higher Education and Research approved this clinical trial protocol. It was also registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2024/11/076805), with the protocol in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the World Medical Association’s Code of Ethics. The sample size was estimated using a pilot trial, accounting for a 20% dropout rate, and calculated as N = 80 (20 participants per group). This was designed as a multiple-arm, randomized, placebo-controlled, triple-blinded clinical trial carried out at a single center.

The inclusion criteria were patients aged 18–60 years with mandibular molars exhibiting symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis, with a baseline visual analog scale (VAS) score of >5 and a Peri-apical Index (PAI) score of <2. The exclusion criteria were systemic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, and epilepsy), crown root fractures, open apices, pulpal necrosis, pregnancy, lactating patients, and consumption of analgesics, steroids, and antibiotics within the past 24 h, with a history of allergy to local anesthetics and other medications.

140 patients were screened for eligibility by thorough clinical and radiographic examinations, followed by sensibility testing. 96 qualified patients (VAS >5, <8) were selected [Figure 1: CONSORT Flowchart]. Allocation concealment was achieved with the sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes method. Apart from the patient and the operator being blinded, the statistician was also blinded to reduce bias in the interpretation of the results. A nursing assistant unrelated to the trial administered the drug in a sealed envelope to the patients 1 h before the clinical procedure. A calibrated postgraduate performed a single-visit RCT, which two experienced endodontists further assessed.

Under local anesthesia (Lignox 2% with 1:80,000 epinephrine) and rubber dam isolation, the traditional access opening was done. Working length (WL) and patency were verified using a size 10 K-File. Cleaning and shaping were completed 0.5 mm short of the WL. Canal enlargement was performed up to F2 (25/0.08) in the mesial canals and F3 (30/0.09) in the distal canals using Protaper gold files (Dentsply Sirona, USA). Final irrigation was done with 3% sodium hypochlorite and 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Sonic activation) using a 30-G side-vented needle placed. A final flush with 0.9 % physiological saline was done. The canals were then obturated with bioceramic sealer (Bioroot RCS, Septodont, France) and gutta-percha. Entrance filling was done with composite, and occlusion reduction with an occlusal relief of 1 mm was ensured.

The primary outcome measure of postoperative pain was assessed immediately after postendodontic restoration and at 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hrs respectively, using the 100 mm VAS. Electronic (text messaging) and written forms were used to assess VAS. Patients were given a printed handout to record their pain score. They were instructed on the different pain intensities using the VAS Scale to help them fill out the forms. Patients were asked to return at 72 h for a review and to submit their pain score forms. The data from both methods were compared, and the method that provided pain scores at all intervals was used for the final analysis. Ketorolac tromethamine 10 mg (Ketorol-DT, Dr. Reddy’s Laboratories Ltd., India) was prescribed as an escape medication. Patients who consumed rescue medications were excluded from further analyses. Patients excluded from the trial were treated in two visits with intracanal medicament. The presence or absence of pain postoperatively (percentage) was noted. The primary outcome measures were incidence and intensity of postoperative pain. The influence of gender, age, and type of tooth on postoperative pain was evaluated as secondary outcomes.

The Oral Health Impact Profile - 14 (OHIP-14) Questionnaire was used to assess the patient’s quality of life pre- and postendodontic treatment. After 2 weeks, full coverage restoration was delivered, and scores were retaken.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using STATA statistical software (Stata Corp., College Station, Texas, USA, Version 17). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and one-way analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables. Dunn’s post hoc test was applied for pairwise differences, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for group differences. The Friedman test was used for intragroup changes with pairwise timepoint assessments using the Eisinga test [Supplementary Tables 1-7].

RESULTS

Ninety-six patients (24 patients in each group) received single-visit endodontic treatment. Out of the 96 patients, 80 were included for the final analyses [Table 1 for baseline characteristics]. An intragroup comparison revealed a significant reduction in mean pain intensity and a substantial decrease in post-operative pain over the 72-h time interval following single-visit endodontic treatment compared to the baseline (P < 0.05) [Supplementary Tables 1-7]. Intergroup comparison revealed that IOT was the most effective at the 4-h interval [Table 2 and Graphs 1,2] (P < 0.001 compared to placebo and P = 0.005 compared to KTP). At 6 h, both the active drug groups were equally effective. At the 12- and 24-h time intervals, the KTP group was most effective in pain control (P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in mean pain intensity among the four groups at the 48-h and 72-h intervals. The OHIP score showed a marked reduction across all four groups with no significant intergroup differences [Graph 3]. Logistic regression analysis revealed that neither age nor gender had a significant influence on the pain levels. The odds ratio for IOT was 0.01 at 4 h and 0.04–0.13 for KTP at the 12- and 24-h time intervals, indicating their superiority in pain mitigation at these time points.

DISCUSSION

This trial was done to assess the efficacy of transdermal ketoprofen as an alternative analgesic for postendodontic pain following single-visit endodontics. Oral ibuprofen (400 mg) and transdermal ketoprofen (30 mg) were used as the interventions, as both are propionic acid derivatives. The placebo counterparts (oral glucose tablets and a PTP) for both routes of administration ensured proper blinding and eliminated bias in the trial.[7] In India, the commercially available dosage of KTP is 30 mg and has been used as a comparator. Ketoprofen is effective at doses as low as 25–50 mg, whereas ibuprofen typically requires 200–400 mg for similar analgesic effects.[8]

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first clinical trial in endodontics to use a placebo counterpart for the transdermal intervention. Achieving actual patient blinding is challenging in cases where two drug administration routes are compared. A novel methodology has been implemented to ensure true patient blinding. For the interventional groups (IOT and KTP), co-administration of an oral placebo and a placebo patch was done, so the patient was unaware whether the patch or the oral drug benefited them. Size matching of the placebo and ketoprofen patches (7 mm × 10 mm) and the oral tablets further enhanced the blinding protocol. This study included mandibular molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis and symptomatic apical periodontitis, as they are anatomically complex.

As shown in previous studies, an occlusal reduction of 1 mm was performed on both the functional and non-functional cusps to reduce postoperative pain.[9] In this trial, oral and transdermal analgesics were administered 1 h before endodontic treatment to standardize timing, regardless of each drug’s plasma peak, in line with similar studies. Matrix-type transdermal patches used in this study have controlled release, reduced overdose risk from patch damage, lower abuse potential, and stable plasma drug levels.[1011] In this trial, the transdermal patches were applied to the upper arm (medial deltoid) on the side of the affected quadrant, the site closest to the tooth.[12]

If the patient experienced severe pain or pulp was necrosed on access opening, the patients were excluded and managed in 2 visits by using supplemental anesthesia techniques.

Post-operative pain is most intense between 4 and 48 h after endodontic treatment and begins to decline significantly after this period,[13] gradually decreasing over 72 h.[14] This formed the basis for postoperative pain assessment at the 4-, 6-, 12-, 24-, 48-, and 72-h intervals. Loss to follow-up and data loss were minimized by collecting scores electronically and in person (10% did not return with the pain diary sheet), with the majority submitting their pain scores via phone.

The results however in line with earlier studies of similar nature, wherein both ibuprofen oral tablet (IOT) and ketoprofen transdermal patch (KTP) significantly reduced the mean postoperative pain over the 72-hour time frame when administered as a single dose preemptively, The reduction in pain intensity was gradual following single visit endodontic treatment, throughout 4 h to 72 h postoperatively, in all four groups compared to their baseline VAS score. KTP and oral ibuprofen were equally effective in pain reduction over 48 h when used as postoperative analgesics for molars with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis[15] in a study. At 48 h, the KTP was superior to both transdermal and oral diclofenac in reducing postoperative pain following endodontic treatment.[16] Direct comparisons with previous studies cannot be made, as the present study employed preemptive single-dose administration, in contrast to the repeat dosing used in previous studies.[1617]

The findings of this study can be attributed to the pharmacokinetic differences between the two analgesic administration methods. Ibuprofen is rapidly absorbed after oral administration, with peak plasma concentrations (Cmax) typically reached within 30–90 min, and an oral bioavailability of 80%–100%. The drug delivery process via a transdermal patch relies on percutaneous absorption through the skin’s three primary layers: The epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous tissue (hypodermis). Once the transdermal patch is applied, the drug traverses the concentration gradient, creating a reservoir within the stratum corneum. Afterward, the drug penetrates deeper into the skin, gets absorbed into the local capillaries, and ultimately reaches the systemic circulation.[10] The difference in the onset of action of the two routes of administration could be the reason for the significantly reduced pain score in the IOT group compared to the KTP group (P < 0.005) and placebo groups (P < 0.001) at the 4-h interval. In the immediate postoperative period, as local anesthesia gradually weans off, the IOT group will have achieved potent analgesia due to the rapid onset of action. While in the KTP group, the analgesic effect would have taken more time, similar to the results seen with the oral route of analgesic administration, as seen in a previous study.[12]

At the 6-h interval, there was no significant difference in the mean pain scores between the IOT and KTP, although both groups were significantly better than their placebo counterparts (P < 0.001). However, the mean pain score of KTP was less than that of IOT. At this time frame, ketoprofen from the KTP exerts its analgesic effects by achieving therapeutic plasma concentration, which explains why there was no significant difference at this interval with IOT Also, the elimination half-life (time taken for half of the initial concentration of the drug to be eliminated) of oral ibuprofen (400 mg) is about 2.04 h,[18192021] which explains why at the 6-h interval, the mean pain score was slightly higher than KTP. Considering both the onset of action and peak plasma concentration of ibuprofen in the oral route and ketoprofen in the transdermal route, it can be inferred that during the time KTP begins to produce its analgesic effects, the IOT reaches its maximum drug concentration, producing profound analgesia at the 4-h interval. In contrast, KTP exerts its therapeutic effects after 4 h.

At the 12-h interval, the ketoprofen patch (KTP) was significantly more effective than the placebo (P < 0.001) and IOT (P < 0.007). This may be attributed to ketoprofen’s zero-order kinetics and additional central pain suppression.

The elimination half-life of transdermal ketoprofen is around 22 h,[22] which explains why the KTP group had a lower mean pain score at the 24-h interval. However, it was not statistically significant compared with IOT By 48 and 72 h, there was no significant difference between the four groups, which is likely due to the resolution of inflammatory mediator levels and healing that occurs following endodontic therapy, resulting in reduced peripheral sensitization and improved central pain modulation.

Patients in the POT and PTP groups experienced significantly more postoperative discomfort within the 24-h time interval, likely because the activity of pro-inflammatory mediators was mitigated in the analgesic groups, which contain active drug molecules that inhibit pain pathways. Definitive dental treatment, not medication, reduces the pain associated with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis,[23] and this is the justification for pain reduction in the placebo groups over 72 h. The natural decrease in pain following the inflamed pulp removal is expected following RCT in symptomatic irreversible pulpitis with apical periodontitis.[24]

The oral route of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs administration has a shorter duration of action, and it requires frequent intake of the oral analgesic at regular time intervals to effectively reduce the postoperative pain. Patient compliance and repeat dosing determine the efficacy of the oral route. In contrast, the ketoprofen patch is administered only once, offering better compliance and comfort. In the present study, although only a single dose of premedication was administered, the OHRQOL (Oral Health-related Quality of Life) was slightly better in the KTP group than in the IOT group. Still, this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05).

Six patients (three from the PTP and three from the POT groups) took escape medications in the present study. They needed the medication between 6 and 12 h apart, possibly due to a transient spike in the pro-inflammatory mediators and the wearing off of the local anesthetic.

None of the patients from the KTP group reported any side effects. However, two patients from the IOT group reported gastric discomfort. Systemic side effects associated with the KTP are rare, whereas local side effects include skin reactions at the site of drug administration, such as burning, itching, and dry skin.

This trial’s strengths include block randomization, allocation concealment with blinding, a placebo control for the transdermal groups, and the elimination of confounders through standardized protocols. Only patients with moderate pain were included in this study, and pulpal necrosis was excluded; therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated. Future studies with different preemptive administration times and other formulations must be assessed for endodontic surgery and necrotic teeth with lesions.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this randomized clinical trial, at the 4-h interval, oral ibuprofen was most effective, while the transdermal ketoprofen group showed better results at the 12- and 24-h intervals. The transdermal route provided prolonged pain relief, whereas the oral route offered short-term relief in this study. Ketoprofen, when administered as a transdermal patch, is a suitable alternative to ibuprofen for managing endodontic pain.
