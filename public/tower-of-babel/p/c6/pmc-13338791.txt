Association between the A1 Segment Asymmetry and Severe Clinical Presentation in Ruptured Anterior Communicating Artery Aneurysms
Authors: Hiroki KARITA, Shunichiro MIKI, Yoshiro ITO, Takuma HARA, Satoshi MIYAMOTO, Kazuya UEMURA, Eiichi ISHIKAWA, Yuji MATSUMARU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The asymmetry of the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery has been implicated in the development and rupture of anterior communicating artery aneurysms; however, its relationship with the severity of subarachnoid hemorrhage remains unclear. We retrospectively analyzed 114 patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage due to ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysms (mean age, 64.2 ± 13.9 years; 56 males, 58 females) treated at 2 institutions between January 2014 and March 2024. Patients were categorized into Symmetric and Asymmetric A1 groups, with asymmetry defined as a diameter ratio of ≥2 between sides. Among the 114 patients, 72 (63.2%) and 42 (36.8%) had symmetric and asymmetric A1 segments, respectively. The Asymmetric A1 group demonstrated significantly larger aneurysm size, a higher incidence of World Federation of Neurosurgical Surgeons grade IV-V, and more frequent intracerebral hemorrhage. Surgical clipping was more frequently performed in the Asymmetric A1 group (p = 0.04), likely due to associated intracerebral hemorrhage. The A1 diameter ratio was negatively correlated with aneurysm angle (r = −0.27, p < 0.01) and positively correlated with aneurysm length (r = 0.28, p < 0.01). Multivariate analysis showed an association of A1 asymmetry with severe clinical presentation but not with functional outcome. The effect of A1 asymmetry on severity was attenuated after adjustment for intracerebral hemorrhage, the strongest predictor of both severity and outcome. These findings suggest that in ruptured anterior communicating artery aneurysms, A1 asymmetry is associated with larger aneurysms, more severe subarachnoid hemorrhage, and higher rates of intracerebral hemorrhage.

Introduction

Variations in the circle of Willis, especially the asymmetry between the left and right sides, are common anatomical variants found in approximately half of the general population.1) Variations in the circle of Willis increase the risk of aneurysm formation due to hemodynamic factors.2,3) Among these variations, asymmetry in the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery (A1) is a normal anatomical variant found in 2%-22% of cases.4,5) A1 asymmetry is closely related to the development of aneurysms in the anterior communicating artery (AcomA), which is believed to be due to hemodynamic mechanisms.6,7) Reviews suggest that asymmetric A1 increases the likelihood of aneurysm formation by approximately 8 times.3) Additionally, AcomA aneurysms tend to be larger in maximum diameter in individuals with asymmetric A1.8) Moreover, A1 asymmetry is associated with a two-fold increased risk of rupture,3) and treatment can often lead to ischemic complications, potentially affecting prognosis.9-11) However, the severity and neurological prognosis of subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) in individuals with asymmetric A1 are poorly understood.

In this study, we focused on A1 asymmetry in the context of ruptured AcomA aneurysms. This retrospective study aimed to clarify the characteristics of ruptured AcomA aneurysms with asymmetric A1. Additionally, we analyzed the effect of A1 asymmetry on severity and prognosis in ruptured AcomA aneurysms.

Materials and Methods

Patient population

We retrospectively analyzed all consecutive cases of SAH due to ruptured AcomA aneurysms treated at 2 institutions between April 2014 and April 2023. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: 1) having undergone computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or digital subtraction angiography (DSA) at the time of admission; 2) having undergone surgical clipping or coil embolization for prevention of re-rupture; and 3) having detailed records available until discharge for follow-up. A total of 116 cases of SAH due to a ruptured AcomA aneurysm that met the inclusion criteria were identified. Two cases that were transferred before treatment were excluded.

Determination of the A1 segment asymmetry

The mean values of the proximal and distal segments of A1 were measured (Figure 1A). Patients were categorized into Symmetric and Asymmetric A1 groups, with asymmetry defined as a diameter ratio of ≥2 between sides.8) The diameters of A1 were measured by 2 neurosurgeons (H.K. and S.M.). The measurement modalities were CTA in 91 cases (79.8%), MRA in 19 cases (16.7%), and DSA in 4 cases (3.5%).

Method for measuring aneurysm morphology.

A) The vascular diameter of A1 is measured at the proximal and distal segments, and the average value is calculated. B and C are enlarged views of the dotted box in A.

B) Dome, neck, height, and length are defined as shown in the figure. The neck is the baseline where the aneurysm connects to the vessel. The dome is a bulging part of the aneurysm. Length is the straight-line distance from the center of the neck to the tip of the aneurysm. Height is the perpendicular distance from the neckline to the tip of the aneurysm.

C) The angle (θ) of a cerebral aneurysm is defined as the angle between the long axis of the vessel at the base and the long axis of the aneurysm length.

Data management

These data were collected from the SAH database based on electronic medical records from each hospital. The hospital's institutional review board approved the study (R05-101). The collected data included patient's age, sex, smoking history (past and current), hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, family history of SAH, anti-thrombotic therapy, premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS), A1 and aneurysm morphology, World Federation of Neurosurgical Surgeons (WFNS) grade, modified Fisher CT scale, intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), treatment methods, post-treatment complications, cerebral vasospasm, delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), shunt surgery for hydrocephalus, and mRS score at discharge. WFNS grade was dichotomized as I-III and IV-V, and mRS score was dichotomized as 0-3 and 4-6. ICH was defined as a parenchymal hematoma identified on initial CT, contiguous with the rupture site, and not attributable to other etiologies. Post-treatment complications were defined as ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke on post-treatment imaging. Cerebral vasospasm was defined as a narrowing of 33% or more in the main cerebral arteries compared with before treatment, as observed on DSA, CTA, or MRA. DCI was defined as new focal neurological deficits or a decline of at least 2 points on the Glasgow Coma Scale, persisting for ≥1 hour, not occurring immediately after aneurysm occlusion, and not attributable to other identifiable causes based on clinical evaluation, neuroimaging, or laboratory studies.12)

The geometry of aneurysms

The geometry of the aneurysm was measured based on 3-dimensional images obtained at the angle that provided optimal visualization of the aneurysm. The definitions of height, length, neck, and dome of the aneurysm are shown in Figure 1B. The dome/neck and aspect ratio were measured to evaluate aneurysm morphology. The aspect ratio was defined as the ratio of height to neck. These definitions were obtained from the literature.8) Furthermore, the angle θ of a cerebral aneurysm was defined as the angle between the long axis of the vessel at the base and the long axis of the aneurysm length (Figure 1C).13)

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are expressed as mean (standard deviation), while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. We selected the appropriate statistical test, either Student's t-test or Fisher's exact test, to examine univariate associations between clinical variables and dichotomized functional outcomes. Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the associations between vascular diameter ratios and aneurysm angle as well as aneurysm size.

Subsequently, univariate analyses were performed for factors associated with severity and prognosis. Univariate analyses for factors associated with severity and prognosis are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Factors with p < 0.05 and without significant inter-variable correlation were used in multivariate logistic regression analysis. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors. A variance inflation factor greater than 5 indicated substantial collinearity (Supplementary Table 2). The primary analysis was designed to evaluate the total effect of A1 symmetry on WFNS and mRS. Multivariable generalized linear models with a binomial distribution and logit link function were constructed, including A1 symmetry and baseline covariates. ICH and shunt dependency were not included in the primary model because they were considered potential mediators in the causal pathway. Secondary analyses were performed to assess whether the association between A1 symmetry and outcomes persisted after accounting for ICH and shunt dependency. These variables were additionally included in the multivariable models as potential mediators. SPSS (version 27; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all analyses. All statistical tests were considered significant for differences with p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 114 patients with SAH due to ruptured AcomA aneurysms were included. The cohort comprised 56 male (49.1%) and 58 female (50.9%) patients, with a mean age of 64.2 (13.9) years. Of the 114 patients, the Symmetric A1 group included 72 (63.2%), and the Asymmetric A1 group included 42 (36.8%), 5 of whom had anaplastic A1. Patients' characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Age, male sex, medical history, smoking history, family history of SAH, antithrombotic therapy, and premorbid mRS 3-5 did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. The Asymmetric A1 group had a significantly higher proportion of WFNS grade IV-V than the Symmetric A1 (61.9% vs. 22.2%, p < 0.01). The initial severity of SAH, as measured by the modified Fisher CT scale, showed a significantly higher proportion of modified Fisher grade 4 in the Asymmetric A1 group than in the Symmetric A1 group (52.4% vs. 22.4%, p < 0.01). Additionally, the Asymmetric A1 group had a significantly higher proportion of ICH than the Symmetric A1 group (47.6% vs. 5.6%, p < 0.01), and 16 of 20 patients with ICH (80.0%) showed contralateral ICH. More patients in the Asymmetric A1 group underwent clipping than in the Symmetric A1 group (50.0% vs 30.6%, p = 0.04). Post-treatment complications, defined as hemorrhage or cerebral infarction on the day following aneurysm treatment, were observed in 16.7% of the Symmetric A1 group and 11.9% of the Asymmetric A1 group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.49). Cerebral vasospasm occurred in 36.8% of patients overall. The incidence did not differ significantly between A1 ratio groups (34.7% in the Symmetric A1 group vs. 40.5% in the Asymmetric A1 group; p = 0.55). DCI occurred in 23.6% of patients in the Symmetric A1 group and 26.2% in the Asymmetric A1 group, with no significant difference between them (p = 0.76). The proportion of individuals who underwent shunt surgery for postoperative normal pressure hydrocephalus did not differ between the groups (33.3% vs. 20.8%, p = 0.14). The proportion of patients with mRS 4-6 at discharge in the Asymmetric A1 group was significantly higher than the Symmetric A1 group (54.7% vs. 26.4%, p < 0.01).

The geometry of aneurysms was compared between the 2 groups (Table 2). The Asymmetric A1 group showed significantly larger dome (5.24 vs. 4.09 mm, p = 0.04), neck (3.39 vs. 2.78 mm, p = 0.04), height (5.70 vs. 4.39 mm, p < 0.01), and length (6.39 vs. 4.80 mm, p < 0.01) measurements than the Symmetric A1 group. However, no significant differences were observed in the aspect ratio (1.79 vs. 1.69, p = 0.50) and dome/neck ratio (1.57 vs. 1.55, p = 0.86) between the groups. The angle θ was smaller in the Asymmetric A1 group than in the Symmetric A1 group (57.7° vs. 73.3°, p = 0.02). In the Asymmetric A1 group, aneurysms had a similar shape to those in the Symmetric A1 group; however, they were larger in dimension and directed toward the contralateral side. Pearson's correlation analysis revealed a negative correlation between the mean A1 diameter ratio (wide/narrow) and aneurysm angle (r = −0.27, p < 0.01) (Figure 2A) and a positive correlation with aneurysm length (r = 0.28, p < 0.01) (Figure 2B).

Discussion

Ruptured aneurysms in the Asymmetric A1 group showed a higher incidence of contralateral ICH and a higher proportion of severe grades than those in the Symmetric A1 group. They were larger in size and directed toward the contralateral side. The A1 diameter ratio was negatively correlated with aneurysm angle and positively correlated with aneurysm length. Images of a typical case involving an aneurysm and ICH associated with an asymmetric A1 segment are presented in the Supplementary Figure.

The asymmetric A1 is a common anatomical variation, with reported incidence rates ranging from 24% to 90%, depending on the definition used.6,7,14) In our study, the incidence was 37%. Aneurysm size in the Asymmetric A1 group was significantly larger than that in the Symmetric A1 group, consistent with previous reports. AcomA aneurysms are more prevalent and larger in patients with asymmetric A1 segments than in those with symmetric A1 segments.1,8,15) A previous study using Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated significantly higher blood flow in the dominant internal carotid artery in patients with asymmetric A1.16) Furthermore, investigations into wall shear stress have shown that stress at the AcomA junction is significantly elevated in patients with asymmetric A1 compared to those with symmetric A1.17,18) These hemodynamic changes―namely, increased flow velocity and wall shear stress―may promote aneurysm formation, growth, and rupture at the AcomA, potentially contributing toward ICH.19) Notably, asymmetric A1 is associated with a two-fold increased risk of aneurysm rupture.3) Despite these findings, the clinical course of SAH associated with asymmetric A1 remains poorly understood.

This study demonstrated that the effect of A1 asymmetry on clinical severity may be mediated through the occurrence of ICH rather than representing an independent determinant of severity. Unlike clinical severity at presentation, clinical outcome was not independently influenced by A1 asymmetry. Clinical outcome appears to be determined by age, premorbid mRS, and ICH. Additionally, a positive correlation was observed between aneurysm size and the A1 diameter ratio, consistent with previous studies.1,8,15) The aneurysm angle showed a negative correlation with the A1 diameter ratio, suggesting that the aneurysm's long axis tends to orient toward the contralateral side. This anatomical orientation may explain the higher frequency of bleeding on the contralateral side of the dominant A1. A sharper aneurysm angle correlates with increased blood flow and a higher likelihood of rupture.20,21) Overall, our results indicate that asymmetric A1 influences both the geometry and orientation of AcomA aneurysms, further increasing the risk of contralateral ICH and worsening clinical severity.

The size of an aneurysm and its rupture rate are correlated. Furthermore, aneurysm rupture risk varies by location, with AcomA aneurysms exhibiting a higher risk of rupture than other aneurysms.22,23) Reportedly, even small aneurysms are more likely to rupture in asymmetric A1 segments according to machine learning models.24) Moreover, in small ruptured AcomA aneurysms, asymmetric A1 segments contribute to rupture.25,26) In this report, ruptured AcomA aneurysms associated with asymmetric A1 segments were significantly larger than those with symmetric A1 segments. Because this study compared aneurysm size at the time of rupture, it is plausible that aneurysms associated with asymmetric A1 segments may rupture even when small. No prior study has evaluated aneurysms based on their location. This study explored how the morphology of the parent vessel affects aneurysm shape and severity. Although retrospective in nature, the findings underscore that treatment indications for unruptured AcomA aneurysms with asymmetric A1 segments may need to be considered separately from those for other aneurysms. These results may provide important insights for future treatment guidelines concerning unruptured AcomA aneurysms.

The limitations of this study warrant further consideration. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at 2 institutions with a small sample size. Second, several examination modalities were used to evaluate vessel diameter and aneurysm morphology, which may have introduced measurement variability. Although different modalities were used, measurement methods were standardized to reduce errors. Third, the treatment methods for aneurysms differed and may have influenced functional outcomes at discharge. Fourth, consecutive cases of SAH due to ruptured AcomA aneurysms were analyzed. Aneurysm morphology was assessed using acute post-rupture imaging and may have been influenced by post-rupture hematoma and cerebral edema. To evaluate true aneurysm morphology, verification in unruptured aneurysms is necessary. Finally, the angle formed by the A1 segment and the aneurysm may be related to severity and clinical prognosis. However, because the sample size was small, it was not incorporated into the analysis.

Conclusion

Ruptured AcomA aneurysms with asymmetric A1 segments have significantly worse clinical severity, largely mediated by a higher incidence of ICH, often contralateral. Aneurysms are larger and have smaller angles than those with symmetric A1 segments.
