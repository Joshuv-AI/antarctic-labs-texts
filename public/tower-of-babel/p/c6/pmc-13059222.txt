Palliative care needs among residential aged care facility residents referred to an Australian hospital-in-the-home service: a descriptive retrospective cohort study
Authors: Angela Zeng, Aaron B Wong, Seok M Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough the majority of those living in residential aged care facilities (RACFs) prefer to remain in their facility if their health deteriorates, many do not end up achieving this. Hospital-in-the-home (HITH) services have been increasingly used to support RACFs by providing acute, palliative, and supportive care to residents on-site. Understanding the reasons for RACFs engaging HITH services can help identify unmet needs and guide improvements in community-based end-of-life care. This study aimed to characterise the palliative care needs of RACF residents admitted to an Australian HITH service and describe the resources and interventions provided to meet these needs.MethodsA single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at a large metropolitan hospital in Melbourne, Australia. We included RACF residents with palliative care needs admitted to this hospital’s HITH service between July and December 2023. Data were extracted from electronic health records and analysed using descriptive statistics.ResultsThere were 142 separate admissions for 135 patients. The patient cohort had a mean age of 88.2 years, were predominantly female (55.6%), frail (median Clinical Frailty Score 7), highly comorbid (median Charlson Comorbidity Index 7), and functionally dependent (median Australia-modified Karnofsky Performance Status 30). Dementia or cognitive impairment was present in 75.6%, and 53.3% had delirium at referral. Most referrals (80.3%) originated from RACFs, with 42.3% occurring after-hours. The most frequent referral reason was for acute deterioration (74.6%). Over a quarter (28.9%) of patients had three or more concurrent symptoms at time of referral. The most common interventions included palliative care discussions (88.0%) and anticipatory medication charting and provision (75.4%). A quarter (25.9%) died in their RACF during admission, which was aligned with their location of choice. Specialist palliative care input was sought in 31.7% of cases, predominantly for care coordination (87.6%). Most (81.5%) of this cohort were deceased within 12 months.ConclusionsThis study highlights key gaps in care delivery for RACF residents with palliative care needs and how HITH can support care in this setting. Background

Approximately 185,000 Australians aged over 65 live in residential aged care facilities (RACFs) [1]. With increasing life expectancies globally, there is a greater prevalence of chronic disease and multimorbidity, leading to increasingly complex health care needs —often compounded by increased frailty, cognitive and functional impairment [2, 3]. Many RACF residents live with non-cancer progressive illnesses such as organ failure or dementia. These often follow less predictable trajectories, where individuals may experience a more gradual pace of decline with higher degrees of functional dependence, in which some end-of-life signs and symptoms might be present, without approaching imminent death [2, 4, 5]. This gradual downwards trajectory can be punctuated by periods of acute illness episodes which can prove increasingly fatal in the setting of progressively declining reserves, until an acute event (such as pneumonia) leads to death [2, 6]. For some of these residents, a simultaneous care approach, which integrates palliative care with active treatments aimed at managing reversible conditions, optimising health, preventing exacerbations, and restoring function, may be aligned with their goals [2, 4].

Receiving end-of-life care (EOLC) in one’s preferred place is an important component of a “good death” [7], and most Australians express a preference to die at home [8]. Among RACF residents, a recent publication reported that 97% wished to remain in their facility if their health were to deteriorate [9]. Despite this, a significant proportion of residents do not receive EOLC at their RACFs. Data from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare indicated that more than a quarter (28.5%) of residents were on hospital leave from their facilities within 30 days of their death, with half of these individuals then dying while in hospital [10].

Decisions regarding hospital transfers for RACF residents in the end-of-life period are complex, and influenced by various factors including uncontrolled symptoms, general practitioner (GP) availability, nursing staff levels and expertise, and advance care plans [11, 12]. Observational studies have estimated that 19–67% of hospitalisations for RACF residents are potentially unnecessary [13], and hospitalisations near the end of life remains common [14]. Hospital transfers for residents nearing end of life may not be aligned with their preferences and may be associated with discomfort and unnecessary medical interventions [12, 14, 15]. For residents with palliative care needs, hospitalisation does not necessarily improve symptom management or quality of life. The act of transfer to hospital itself also introduces an increased risk of complications and mortality, especially when performed outside regular business hours [12, 16].

The Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety found that RACFs do not always provide adequate support for dying residents [17]. An Australian-based cohort study estimated that up to 92% of RACF residents had potential palliative care needs prior to death, even though only 6% were assessed as requiring palliative care using the funding-based aged care assessment [18]. With no nationally consistent data collection or methodology to quantify need for palliative care in Australia, there is limited insight regarding palliative care needs (or unmet needs) and service requirements within RACFs [18–20]. Furthermore, there remains a reliance on acute care settings to provide EOLC, rather than investment in approaches to reduce this dependence [12].

In Victoria, Australia, community palliative care (CPC) services are delivered by geographically defined providers and offer holistic specialist palliative care, including practical support, information, and access to specialist palliative medical review and assessment in patients’ homes, including RACFs [21]. Local specialist palliative care services can support RACFs through visits for complex issues or providing remote clinical support [22]. However, variations and limitations exist in their care models, operating hours, eligibility criteria and capacity. In response, some metropolitan health services have introduced rapid-response hospital outreach specialist palliative care services to provide same-day assessment; however, these services are limited in focus to specialist palliative care and do not provide concurrent acute treatments [23–25]. As supplementary information, we have included Supplementary Table 1 that illustrates current services that are available to RACF residents for providing acute and palliative care [21, 26–30].

Residential In-Reach (RIR) programs and hospital-in-the-home (HITH) are hospital outreach services that have been utilised to deliver acute, palliative and supportive care for RACF residents [31, 32]. RIR programs, staffed by hospital-based doctors and nurses, primarily aim to provide rapid assessment and management to RACF residents, through telephone support or on-site assessments, to avoid unnecessary emergency department presentations [31]. In comparison, HITH manages actual or potential acute symptoms within the RACF which would otherwise require inpatient admission [32, 33]. Previous studies have shown that utilising HITH services in the RACF setting have been shown to reduce unnecessary hospital transfers and emergency department presentations and promote better quality end of life care (EOLC) within the home, aligning with the wishes of many residents [33]. We hypothesise that engagement with HITH services reflect the limitations of the current primary care framework to adequately address acute healthcare and palliative care needs within RACFs. Understanding the reasons for HITH engagement is essential for identifying gaps in care and improving service delivery for this vulnerable population nearing the end of life.

Methods

Study design and objectives

This single-centre, retrospective cohort study was conducted at a major metropolitan hospital in Melbourne, Australia, over a six-month period from July 1, 2023, to December 1, 2023. The primary aim was to describe the palliative and supportive needs of RACF residents admitted to a HITH service. The secondary aim was to characterise the interventions and resources used to meet these needs. This study was approved by the Royal Melbourne Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (QA2023076).

Study setting

The HITH service provides geriatric outreach care to approximately 2300 residents of 37 local RACFs, operating as an acute admission bed-substitution model that delivers hospital-level care to residents within their facilities. It is a multidisciplinary service comprising medical, nursing, and allied health practitioners, delivering in-person and virtual care daily from 08:00 to 20:00, with capacity for telehealth assessments and escalation to in-hospital treatments 24 h a day, 7 days a week. Referrals are accepted directly from community services, e.g. GPs, RACF staff or RIR services, as well as via secondary hospital services such as emergency departments and hospital inpatient wards. At our hospital, the RIR service provides initial assessment and acute management to RACF residents. If ongoing hospital-level care is required beyond the initial RIR episode, patients are referred to the HITH service, which assumes responsibility for delivering continued treatment and monitoring within the RACF. The HITH service also works closely with the hospital’s specialist palliative care outreach team, who provide an additional consultative service to support patients and their carers with complex decision-making, goal-setting, symptom management, and urgent linkage with CPC services.

Data collection

Data from eligible cases was stored in a password-protected REDCap database. The electronic medical records (EMR) for each of these patients was then manually reviewed and used to obtain the following data.

We collected data on demographics, linguistic background, primary life-limiting illness, and comorbidities based on the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), as well as additional conditions not included in the CCI but relevant to an older population, including osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, visual impairment, movement disorders, and delirium. Frailty was assessed using the Canadian Study of Health and Aging Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS, 1 = very fit to 9 = terminally ill), which is widely validated in older adults and predictive of mortality, functional decline and adverse clinical outcomes [34]. Functional status was measured with the Australia-modified Karnofsky Performance Status (AKPS, 0 = dead to 100 = normal activity), a validated tool in palliative care settings [35]. We also recorded referral symptoms, availability of anticipatory palliative care medications at time of referral (subcutaneous analgesic, anxiolytic, anti-emetics and anti-secretory agents), time from referral to the initial response by the hospital outreach service (via RIR or HITH, whichever occurred first), and information regarding the patients’ advance care plans.

Outcomes

Outcome data was collected from EMR. The primary outcomes focused on characterising the palliative care needs of RACF residents at the time of referral. These included referral reasons, referral source, timing (during or outside of standard business hours of Monday to Friday, 08:00 to 17:00), the presence of symptoms, and the availability of anticipatory palliative care medications.

Secondary outcomes included the interventions and resources required to address both palliative and acute healthcare needs during the HITH admission. This included length of stay (LOS), interventions provided during the admission, as well as the frequency and type of clinician contacts (HITH medical or specialist palliative care). Clinician contacts were defined as a two-way interaction between healthcare staff and the patient, family, or carers (including RACF staff or GPs). For patients receiving specialist palliative care input, data on referral reason and interventions provided were collected.

We collected outcomes following HITH admission, including mortality during admission and at 12 months. For those who died, we recorded the cause and whether the location of death aligned with documented patient or family preferences. For discharged patients, we noted any increase in support services. For those readmitted, we captured the reason and eventual outcome.

Data analysis

Data was analysed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables with normal distribution, such as age, were summarised using means and standard deviations. Non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles). Categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies with percentages.

Discussion

Our study provides valuable insights into the palliative care needs of RACF residents, highlighting the complexities of managing this vulnerable population. The cohort in this study were characterised by advanced age (mean 88.2 years), frailty (median CFS 7), significant comorbidity (median CCI 7), and high functional dependency (median AKPS 30). A substantial proportion of referrals (42.3%) and clinical reviews (51.8%) occurred after hours, with most (80.3%) referrals initiated by RACF staff. The most common reason for referral was acute clinical deterioration (74.6%), followed by requests for a trial of active treatment (34.5%). Many patients had multiple symptoms at the time of referral, with dyspnoea (58.5%) and drowsiness (50.7%) being the most frequent. HITH provided a range of interventions in response to these needs, including palliative care discussions and education with families and patients (88.0%), palliative care medication charting and supply (75.3%), and symptom management (51.4%). Specialist palliative care input was sought for 31.7% of admissions, primarily for care coordination (86.7%), and one-third (36.6%) of patients were discharged with additional CPC support.

Despite clear evidence of palliative care needs, engagement with community-based palliative care services prior to HITH referral was limited — only 5.9% of residents had existing CPC input at the time of referral and anticipatory palliative care medications were available in just 28.7% of cases. HITH was responsible for initiating or supplying anticipatory medications in 75.4% of admissions. Multiple factors likely contribute to the low availability of anticipatory medications in this setting. Although the proactive prescribing of these medications is recommended in countries such as Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom to optimise symptom control and ensure timely access, standardised practices remain inconsistent and poorly documented outside the United Kingdom [36–39]. In Australia, timely access to these medications may be limited, especially as after-hours pharmacy services are often limited – a problem more pronounced in rural and remote areas [40]. Furthermore, while some RACFs may have an onsite ‘imprest’ medication system with emergency anticipatory medications, establishing such systems involves multiple administrative steps, regulatory requirements, and associated costs, which may limit uptake [41, 42]. Additionally, anticipatory prescriptions include controlled drugs such as opioids and benzodiazepines [39]. These must comply with strict regulations for storage [43], which not all facilities are resourced or trained for. Finally, even when medications are available, RACF nursing staff may lack confidence in administering them or feel emotionally burdened by the responsibility particularly when they have limited training or experience [44]. Despite these factors, evidence shows that anticipatory prescribing is perceived by healthcare workers to be effective for symptom control, reduce unplanned hospital admissions, and provide reassurance to all involved [39]. It should therefore be prioritised as a core component of high-quality EOLC in RACFs. Strengthening anticipatory prescribing practices through clearer protocols, improved access pathways, and targeted staff education may address some of these barriers and ensure that residents receive timely, appropriate symptom relief.

However, addressing anticipatory needs alone is insufficient, as timely identification of the end-of-life period remains a challenge. As previously discussed, individuals with non-cancer progressive illnesses, which is the case for many RACF residents, often have trajectories marked by a more gradual pace of decline, in which some end-of-life signs and symptoms might be present, punctuated by acute illness episodes that may ultimately lead to death [2, 4–6]. In our cohort, dementia was the most common life-limiting condition (61.5%) followed by heart failure (14.1%), whereas cancer accounted for only 5.9%. Evidence consistently shows that individuals with advanced dementia are less likely to receive appropriate palliative care, due to a combination of factors such as limited staff awareness and training, under-recognition of symptoms due to impaired communication in the setting of cognitive impairment, and reduced engagement with specialist palliative care [4, 6, 45]. This disparity is highlighted by an Australian study of over 5,000 individuals who died from dementia, which found that only 6% received community-based palliative care, compared to 26% of all people who died and 50% of those who died from cancer [46]. The Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety has similarly highlighted this systemic gap, stating: “Too few people receive evidence-based end-of-life and palliative care, and instead experience unnecessary pain or indignity in their final days” [17].

Notably, 81.5% of the cohort in this study were deceased within 12 months of admission to our HITH service, highlighting the prognostic significance of their clinical presentation. The patients in our cohort met criteria on prognostic tools such as the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT™), which, if applied earlier, may have identified a high risk of deterioration and death [47, 48]. Furthermore, symptoms frequently observed in our cohort – such as dyspnoea, anorexia, and delirium – are commonly recognised as components of palliative prognostication tools and are associated with a poorer prognosis [49]. These clinical characteristics suggest indicators that could assist in identifying RACF residents transitioning into the end-of-life period, enabling earlier advance care planning and timely palliative care interventions. An admission to a HITH service, whilst in a RACF, should therefore prompt healthcare providers to consider the resident’s overall illness trajectory and prognosis. These findings highlight the opportunity for earlier recognition of RACF residents who are at risk of dying, to facilitate anticipatory planning, symptom management, and integration of palliative care alongside treatment for advanced comorbidities.

Our findings also highlight significant gaps in advance care planning and goals-of-care discussions in the community setting. Palliative care discussions with the patient or family were required in 88.0% of admissions, suggesting these conversations had either not occurred prior to HITH involvement or had not been sufficiently detailed to guide care during the episode of acute deterioration. This may reflect the challenges in recognising which residents are approaching the end-of-life phase as well as a misalignment between care needs and timely access to palliative care support. Although most (88.8%) patients had a documented preference of care for limited active treatment at their RACFs, accessing timely medical support remained challenging. The substantial proportion of after-hours referrals and clinical reviews found in this study reflects this cohort’s unpredictable timing of deterioration. Access to timely medical support in RACFs is often limited, as GPs, who are typically the first point of call, may not be readily available, particularly after-hours [17, 50, 51]. In the absence of timely medical care, RACFs frequently rely on hospital emergency transfers [17, 51]. To address this, telehealth assessments have become increasingly utilised in RACFs, especially since the COVID-19 pandemic, due to its ability to facilitate timely healthcare delivery [52, 53]. However, notable limitations still include the inability to perform comprehensive physical examinations, as well as challenges in virtual interactions for patients who have cognitive, visual, or hearing impairment [52–54]. The Victorian Virtual Emergency Department (VVED) is one such model, which typically provides a one-off review [55], and ongoing longitudinal care must be provided by other services. In our study, 16.2% of patients were referred for follow-up after a VVED review in a RACF, highlighting that virtual assessment alone may be insufficient to meet the complex and evolving needs of this population.

Nearly half (44.4%) of the patients in our study were from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds. Existing literature indicates that sociodemographic factors such as advanced age and speaking a language other than English are associated with reduced likelihood of being assessed for palliative care needs [18]. This suggests the presence of potentially unmet needs and disparities in care for these populations. Several barriers may contribute to these inequities, particularly among CALD communities, including cultural, linguistic, systemic, and structural factors [56]. Further research is required to inform more equitable and culturally sensitive models of care.

In Victoria, Australia, several models exist for delivering palliative care within RACFs; however, there remains considerable variation in practice, resourcing, and availability [57]. Many of these models involve specialist palliative care clinicians working collaboratively with RACF staff and GPs through approaches such as case management, mentorship programs, specialist in-reach services, shared care, outreach, family case conferencing, and ‘needs rounds’ [11, 45, 50, 58]. Specialist palliative care ‘needs rounds’ are monthly triage meetings between RACF and specialist palliative care clinicians which focus on identifying residents at risk of dying with a high symptom burden, and have been associated with more proactive, anticipatory prescribing based on symptoms and improved advance care plan documentation [12, 45, 58]. In our study, 31.7% of admissions received input from specialist palliative care during their HITH admission, primarily for care coordination and medication advice or adjustment. Overall, 36.6% of patients had ongoing CPC support arranged. These findings highlight the need for greater specialist palliative care involvement for this population and suggest that, while such input is often targeted and valuable, it remains underutilised in the broader delivery of care for RACF residents with complex needs. Furthermore, although multiple community-based and hospital outreach services are available to provide palliative care for RACF residents, referral and care processes must be streamlined and coordinated to avoid duplication or gaps, ensuring patients receive tailored timely and comprehensive care.

Our study underscores the value of a holistic approach to the care of frail older residents with advanced and often multiple chronic conditions. High-quality EOLC includes not only symptom management but also the provision of active treatment for acute and potentially reversible conditions, when aligned with the individual’s goals and values. A third of our cohort (34.5%) received active treatment in conjunction with symptom management in their presentation. Moving forward, improvements are needed in the early identification of RACF residents with potential palliative care needs, as well as services that can provide timely delivery of care that aligns with their goals and clinical situation. Key priorities include improving access to after-hours support, planning for and initiating appropriate active treatments, and developing advance care plans that clearly outline individuals’ preferences including decisions not to pursue active treatment.

While this study offers novel insights into the palliative care needs of RACF residents and highlights important service gaps, several limitations must be considered. As a retrospective review, this study relies on the accuracy and completeness of clinician documentation. It was conducted within a single hospital catchment area in northwest Melbourne, Victoria, which may limit generalisability to other locations due to differences in baseline characteristics, sociocultural demographics, and service models. Additionally, this study’s observational, single-centre cohort design limits direct comparisons with residents managed through other pathways. Furthermore, we did not quantify other measures such as care quality, symptom intensity and control, or the psychosocial and spiritual care needs of this cohort, which are also important components of EOLC [7]. Future prospective studies are needed to explore these aspects in more detail, as well as to examine caregiver and family-related outcomes, barriers to equitable, timely and high-quality palliative care access, and opportunities to optimise service integration, coordination, and communication. Nevertheless, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of the palliative care needs of RACF residents, particularly within culturally and linguistically diverse populations that are often under-represented in the literature. By examining the interface between RACFs and HITH services, it provides novel insights into how acute and EOLC can be better delivered in the community. This study also highlights some of the reasons for HITH referral and how these services can bridge care gaps when existing aged care, primary care, and community palliative services are inadequate. These findings may inform care models in other health systems facing similar challenges in providing equitable and timely person-centred approaches to EOLC in RACFs.

Conclusions

This study highlights the substantial palliative and supportive care needs of RACF residents admitted to an Australian HITH service and demonstrates how HITH can support this cohort in receiving palliative care in the RACF setting. Despite high clinical vulnerability, significant gaps were identified in proactive palliative care engagement and timely access to community-based services. High after-hours demand, symptom burden, and limited availability of anticipatory medications highlight gaps in advance care planning and timely care access. Earlier identification of residents at risk of dying is essential to support effective symptom management, timely medication review, and meaningful goals of care discussions. The common use of concurrent active and symptom-focused treatments underscores the need for integrated, individualised care. Future models of care must be flexible and responsive, ensuring goal-concordant care that integrates palliative support with appropriate, non-burdensome interventions.
