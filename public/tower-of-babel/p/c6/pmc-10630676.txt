Protocol for establishing a protein interactome based on close physical proximity to a target protein within live budding yeast
Authors: Janhavi A Kolhe, Neethu L Babu, Brian C Freeman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for establishing a protein interactome based on close physical proximity to a target protein within live yeast cells. We describe steps for capturing both transient and stable binders by integrating a non-natural amino acid. We detail procedures for employing a site-directed method for labeling the surface that mediates protein associations and uncovers the binding sites on the interactors. Combined with mass spectrometry, our approach proves valuable in discovering binding partners and constructing a comprehensive protein-interaction network.Subject areas: Model Organisms, Molecular Biology, Protein Biochemistry

Before you begin

The phenylalanine derivative p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (Bpa) initially was shown to be a useful site-specific UV-induced cross-linker in a synthetic polypeptide.1 The inclusion of a modified aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, which enables the incorporation of Bpa into the amber stop codon UAG, allowed site-specific incorporation of Bpa into a target protein expressed in live Escherichia coli.2 When irradiated with 365 nm UV light the carbonyl group of Bpa reversibly forms a diradical and this triplet state can react with the C-H bonds of nearby amino acids (within ∼6 Å) to form a covalent adduct3 (Figure 1). If C-H bonds are close, the triplet state will revert back to the ground state yet can be reactivated by further UV light exposure. As Bpa exhibits minimal side reactions with solvents, it is particularly useful for in vivo applications. Here, we describe a method that exploits these beneficial Bpa characteristics, standard Multidimensional Protein Identification Technology (MudPIT) mass spectrometry, and a combination of bioinformatic algorithms to establish a high-throughput assay for building intricate physical interaction networks for target proteins (TPs) of interest. We recently applied this approach to the highly abundant molecular chaperone Hsp90 to create a large interactome (∼1100 hits), reveal the surfaces on Hsp90 mediating the interactions and provide insights into the features recognized by Hsp90 on the associated partner/client proteins.4

Mechanism of Bpa-mediated protein cross-linking

In the presence of UV light (365 nm), p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (Bpa) will reversibly form a diradical capable of reacting with amino acid C-H bonds within ∼6 Å to form a covalent adduct. The yellow shape represents the target protein (TP) with translation-incorporated Bpa and the green shape represents a TP interaction partner protein.

Yeast plasmid shuffling method

To interrogate the protein products of essential genes, a commonly used yeast genetic strategy is plasmid shuffling. First, the essential open reading frame (ORF) is cloned into an expression vector with a URA3 marker and the endogenous TP ORF is knocked out using standard methods.13 Next, the yeast is transformed with an expression vector for the TP-Bpa variant and pSNR-tRNA-pBpaRS plasmid, which is a vector for expressing the tRNA that allows Bpa to be incorporated into the engineered Amber stop codons while the TP is being translated. Finally, the transformants are grown on 5-FOA selectable media that induces loss of the URA3-marked plasmid thereby leaving the TP-Bpa variant as the sole source of the TP.

Mass spectrum of cross-linked peptides

Cross-linked peptide pairs are identified in the MudPIT spectra using the MeroX algorithm.9,16 The recognized pairs are further filtered by visually scanning the pairs for the presence of b and y ions from each peptide. The TP peptide will contain an “X” that represents the Bpa moiety created while running MeroX. For illustrative purposes, we highlight the b and y ions from the captured interaction partner protein in an idealized MS/MS spectrum free of noise and containing all of the b and y ion fragments (right). Ionization at the N-terminal of the peptide results in the formation of b ions and ionization at the C-terminal results in the formation of y ions.

Expected outcomes

Successful completion of the described protocol will yield a list of potential TP interactors. For the hits identified using an engineered Bpa site, the interaction site on the TP will be known (i.e., ∼6 Å from the Bpa site) since the Bpa was engineered at that site. Employing a series of Bpa derivatives spanning the surface of your TP can be useful for delineating how the different surface regions/domains interact with various proteins. If you exploit the non-Bpa wild type TP to capture stable interactors, then your final dataset also will provide insights into the nature of the interaction between the different factors (i.e., stable vs. transient). Of course, it is important to validate these interactors with further tests of both the physical (e.g., 2-hybrid) and functional (e.g., does the binding partner alter the activity of the TP or vice versa) sense. Significantly, the described Bpa-mass spectrometry procedure effectively creates an intricate interactome that provides an in-depth understanding of what your TP physically associates within live cells.

Limitations

While the Bpa tactic has a number of advantages, it is limited by the relatively short reach (∼6 Å) of the Bpa moiety.2 From one perspective, the short distance is advantageous since spurious interactions are less likely to be captured. Yet, it also limits the surface area covered and therefore potential transient interactors are likely to be missed since they won’t be in proximity to a UV-activated Bpa. Although this problem can be minimalized by using a large number of engineered Bpa sites, an increasing number of Bpa variants will drive the financial costs higher. In our experience with the Hsp90 molecular chaperone we used 5 variants covering only ∼2% of the surface area of Hsp90, yet we were able to identify ∼1100 interactors that were, in select cases, functionally validated.4

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Faint or low abundance cross-linked products (i.e., higher molecular weight species above full-length TP) are not detected in the immunoblot (related to Step 25).

Potential solution

Increase exposure time of the immunoblot – faint or low abundance cross-linked products are missed due to not enough exposure time of the blot since the wild type band for TP is often much more intense than the lower abundant cross-linked species.

Problem 2

The concentration of the purified sample is too low (related to Step 18).

Potential solution

Increase the sample size by either purifying sample replicates in parallel and combining or processing a larger culture of TP-Bpa expressing yeast (please see below for the upscale procedure). The larger sample size can be concentrated by either using a microconcentrator or by TCA precipitation.

Problem 3

UV bulbs might lose intensity over time (related to Step 22).

Potential solution

Test UV bulbs – use a UV light meter to test the intensity of the 365 nm UV bulbs. The recommended UV energy dosage is 120 mJ/cm2.

Problem 4

The time course for cross-linking is not sufficient (related to Step 23).

Potential solution

Cross-linking of proteins is dependent on protein structure, location of the protein, and neighboring residues. Thus, a single duration of cross-linking cannot be applied to different types of TPs and a longer time course should be used to find the optimal cross-linking time.

Problem 5

The assay and reagent used for the detection of cross-linked products aren’t sensitive. (related to Step 36).

Potential solution

The assay and reagents used to detect the cross-linked products containing the TP can vary in strength. Therefore, the use of sensitive reagents (RUBY stain and/or high-quality, specific antibody) is important.
