Clinical and Radiological Efficacy of Short-Segment In Situ Fixation vs. Long-Segment Fixation and Corpectomy in Young Patients With Traumatic Thoracolumbar Burst Fractures
Authors: Yoseb Oh, SungHwan Hwang, Shin Won Kwon, Hyung Jin Shin, Sunho Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study compared the outcomes of short-segment in situ fixation (SSF), long-segment fixation (LSF), and corpectomy for thoracolumbar burst fractures to identify the optimal fixation method.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 67 patients grouped into SSF (n=24), LSF (n=22), and corpectomy (n=21). Clinical and radiological parameters were assessed, including pre- and postoperative hemoglobin, numeric rating scale scores, American Spinal Injury Association scale grades, anterior vertebral body height (AVBH), spinal canal encroachment, Cobb angle, local kyphotic angle, operative time, and blood loss.ResultsPreoperatively, the corpectomy group demonstrated significantly lower AVBH (49.7%) and greater spinal canal encroachment (64.9%) compared with the SSF (62.6% and 39.9%) and LSF (58.4% and 43.0%) groups (p=0.006 and p<0.001, respectively). Postoperative outcomes were comparable among the three groups. Postoperative AVBH was 19.7 mm, 20.0 mm, and 20.5 mm in the SSF, LSF, and corpectomy groups, respectively (p=0.801). Likewise, postoperative local kyphotic angles were 9.4°, 10.8°, and 7.8°, respectively, showing no statistically significant differences (p=0.499). Effective spinal canal decompression was achieved in all groups. However, the corpectomy group required significantly longer operative times (mean, 343.1 minutes; p<0.001) and experienced greater intraoperative blood loss (mean, 1,634.2 cc; p=0.054).ConclusionSevere preoperative AVBH loss (≤54.93%) and spinal canal encroachment (≥54.39%) may serve as practical thresholds for selecting corpectomy to achieve adequate decompression. In contrast, when these parameters are within acceptable ranges, SSF may be preferred in younger patients as it is less invasive, requires shorter operative time, and provides outcomes comparable to LSF.

INTRODUCTION

Thoracolumbar fractures constitute a substantial proportion of spinal trauma cases, often requiring surgery to achieve stability, restore alignment, and facilitate decompression, when indicated. However, determining the optimal fixation strategy remains controversial due to the heterogeneity of injury patterns, patient-specific factors, and the diverse range of available surgical techniques.

In managing burst fractures, the choice between surgical intervention and conservative treatment is primarily guided by established classification systems, including the Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS)15) and the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen Spine Thoracolumbar Spine Injury Classification System.25) These systems provide a structured framework to guide clinical decision-making based on the severity and characteristics of the injury.

Once surgery is indicated, spine surgeons must choose between open and minimally invasive surgery, a decision that remains subject to clinical debate.1822) In surgical planning, further complexity arises in determining the optimal extent of fixation, whether short- or long-segment.171720) Short-segment in situ fixation (SSF) provides benefits such as motion segment preservation, reduced operative time, and decreased blood loss; however, it may provide less biomechanical stability. Conversely, long-segment fixation (LSF) offers enhanced biomechanical stability but carries the risk of increased surgical invasiveness and a higher risk of complications, including adjacent segment degeneration. In more severe cases, corpectomy may be warranted for direct decompression by removing fractured vertebral body fragments, though this also increases surgical complexity and morbidity.

Given these varied options, comparative evidence-based data is needed to guide the selection of the most appropriate surgical strategy. Therefore, we aimed to compare clinical and radiological outcomes among patients who underwent SSF, LSF, and corpectomy. By focusing on cases managed through open surgical approaches, we aimed to provide further insight into the relative efficacy, complications, and biomechanical outcomes of these fixation techniques, thereby contributing to a more informed and individualized approach in the surgical management of thoracolumbar burst fractures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Armed Forces Capital Hospital approved this study (2024-02-004-008). We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent surgery for traumatic thoracolumbar spine fractures at our institution between June 2012 and July 2024.

The use of retrospective data in this study posed minimal risk to participants. The IRB waived the informed consent requirement due to the impracticality of obtaining consent from all individuals and the potential for significant bias and compromise of the study’s validity if only consented patients were included in the results.

Patient enrollment

We included patients with sustained traumatic thoracolumbar fractures and a TLICS score of ≥4. Our inclusion criteria were traumatic fractures without multiorgan trauma, absence of osteoporosis or scoliosis, no previous spinal fusion surgery, and an age under 55 years.

We excluded patients with multiorgan trauma, osteoporosis or scoliosis, those lost to follow-up within 3 months, and patients older than 55 years of age.

Of the 87 patients, 67 patients met these criteria and were included in this study. Their data were retrospectively analyzed using information obtained from the electronic medical records and picture archiving and communication system.

Surgical procedure and postoperative management

Although multiple surgeons participated in the procedures conducted at the Armed Forces Capital Hospital over the 12-year study period, the majority of surgical decisions, including determination of fixation levels, were made by two experienced neurosurgeons (including the corresponding author, SungHwan Hwang, MD). Both surgeons had individually performed more than 300 spinal procedures, and surgical strategies were based on empirical practices acquired at their respective training institutions.

The trans-pedicular screw was inserted under C-arm guidance, and if necessary, laminectomy was performed to decompress the canal. Thereafter, posterior lateral fusion was performed.

The primary distinction between SSF and LSF is the number of segments involved and the use of in situ fixation. SSF includes three segments, while LSF involves more than three segments. In SSF, pedicle screws are inserted to the fracture site and at the adjacent upper and lower vertebral levels. SSF facilitates kyphosis correction, vertebral height restoration, and spinal canal decompression using the principle of ligamentotaxis. This is primarily achieved through positional reduction and instrumentation-based distraction, generating tension on the posterior longitudinal ligament, helping to reduce retropulsed bony fragments.9) In contrast, the fracture site in LSF was decompressed without direct fixation and the upper and lower levels were fixed. Corpectomy was performed in cases where severe canal encroachment caused by retropulsed bony fragments rendered the posterior approach alone insufficient for adequate reduction. All corpectomy procedures were performed in the lateral decubitus position using a retroperitoneal approach. Two different surgical approaches were employed based on the graft material used. The first method involved anterior support using a femoral neck graft without a posterior approach. The second method combined a posterior approach with the use of an artificial, expandable cage as the graft material (FIGURE 1).

As in other studies from the Armed Forces Capital Hospital, patients at out institution were encouraged to ambulate on the day of surgery13) unless contraindicated by severe neurological deficits or associated orthopedic injuries. A lumbar-sacral orthosis was prescribed postoperatively for 6 months in cases involving instrumented fusion.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical indicators included patient age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), fracture level, operation time, numeric rating scale (NRS) (preoperative, postoperative day (POD) 1, and POD 7), amount of intraoperative transfusion, hemoglobin (Hb) levels (preoperative and postoperative), American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) scores (preoperative and postoperative), TLICS score, postoperative infection, and implant failure.

Radiologic evaluation

Radiological evaluations were performed using computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging at three-time points: preoperative, immediately after surgery, and at follow-up (3–9 months postoperative). The analyzed radiological parameters included anterior vertebral body height (AVBH), degree of spinal canal encroachment, lumbar lordosis, Cobb’s and local kyphotic angles, and the presence of vertebral fusion. AVBH (mm) was defined as the anterior height of the fractured vertebral body, while AVBH (%) was calculated as twice the anterior height of the fractured vertebral body divided by the sum of the anterior heights of the adjacent upper and lower vertebral bodies.21) Spinal canal encroachment (%) was calculated from axial images as the percentage of remaining canal space subtracted from 100%. Spinal canal encroachment (%) was determined by dividing twice the canal length at the fracture level by the sum of the canal lengths at the upper and lower adjacent levels.11) The local kyphotic angle was measured on lateral radiographs using the Cobb method, defined as the angle between the superior endplate of the vertebral body above the fracture and the inferior endplate of the vertebral body below the fracture17) (FIGURE 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared among the three groups using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) after verifying the normality of the data distribution. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test to identify specific group differences. Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons for categorical data were conducted using Bonferroni correction when significant differences were identified. The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation or frequencies (percentages). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was also performed to identify optimal cutoff values for preoperative ABVH (%) and spinal canal encroachment (%) to predict the need for corpectomy. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value <0.050.

Interclass and intraclass correlations

To assess reliability, all measurements were performed twice at intervals of more than 2 weeks by one neurosurgeon (YSO) and twice by another neurosurgeon (SHH). Both surgeons were blinded to patient information. The intraobserver and interobserver intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated for the mean values.

RESULTS

Demographics and injury characteristics

This study included 67 patients in this study. Some patients did present with concomitant limb fractures, but no cases involved severe polytrauma. Despite the unique characteristics of the military environment, 63 out of the 67 patients with traumatic spinal injuries had injuries due to slips and falls, and four due to traffic accidents. Therefore, the mechanisms of injury do not differ significantly from those observed in the civilian population. While some patients presented with concomitant fractures of the upper and lower extremities, none had multiorgan trauma or required additional surgical interventions for internal organ injuries during hospitalization.

The patients were categorized into three groups: SSF (n=24), LSF (n=22), and corpectomy (n=21) (FIGURE 3). The mean age was similar across the groups (SSF: 24.4±7.4 years; LSF: 25.1±8.7 years; Corpectomy: 27.0±12.1 years; p=0.643). Similarly, height, weight, and BMI were comparable among the three groups (p>0.050 for all) (TABLE 1). The most commonly affected level was L1 (26.9%), followed by L2 (23.9%) and L3 (17.9%). Postoperative Hb levels were significantly higher in the SSF group (12.4±1.4 g/dL) than in the LSF (11.0±2.0 g/dL; p=0.012) and corpectomy groups (11.0±1.3 g/dL; p=0.017). The corpectomy group had higher intraoperative transfusion; however, this was not statistically significant (p=0.107). Implant failure at 6 months occurred in one case in the SSF group and two in the corpectomy group, but this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.334).

Pain scores varied significantly among the groups. Preoperative NRS scores were highest in the corpectomy group (5.5±2.4), and significantly greater than those in the LSF group (4.0±1.6; p=0.042). On POD 7, the corpectomy group showed significantly higher NRS scores (2.9±1.6) compared to SSF (1.8±0.8; p=0.024).

Preoperatively, neurological status, as measured by the ASIA scale, was significantly lower in the corpectomy group (3.8±1.2) than in the SSF (4.5±0.9; p = 0.018) and LSF groups (4.6±0.5; p=0.023). Postoperatively, the corpectomy group continued to show significantly lower ASIA scores (4.0±1.1) than SSF (4.7±0.8; p=0.037) and LSF (4.7±0.5; p=0.016).

The corpectomy group had the longest length of hospitalization (108.4±103.8 days) than the SSF (76.8±58.2 days) and LSF group (88.0±50.7 days); however, the difference was not statistically significant.

ROC curve and cutoff values

In the one-way ANOVA, preoperative ABVH (%) and spinal canal encroachment (%) were identified as significant predictive factors for corpectomy. ROC curve analysis revealed an area under the curve of 0.726 for preoperative ABVH (%) and 0.776 for spinal canal encroachment (%), both statistically significant. The optimal cutoff value for preoperative ABVH (%) was 54.93%, demonstrating a sensitivity and specificity of 60.9% and 61.1%, respectively. For spinal canal encroachment (%), the optimal cutoff value was 54.39%, with a sensitivity of 66.7% and specificity of 67.4% (FIGURE 4).

Intraobserver and interobserver reliabilities

For all radiological parameters, the intra-rater correlation coefficient was greater than 0.86, and the inter-rater correlation coefficient was greater than 0.81. Consequently, the mean values of the four measurements were used for this study.

DISCUSSION

When managing patients with traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures, the first step is to determine whether surgery is necessary. If surgery is indicated, the next important step is to decide on the appropriate extent and method of fixation.

Corpectomy was considered in cases with high spinal canal encroachment (%) or low AVBH (%). In other cases, the choice was between SSF and LSF. After this selection, the next consideration is whether to perform in situ fixation. Several studies have reported that in situ fixation yields favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes167101217182023); however, it may not provide sufficient support for screw insertion when the pedicle at the fracture level is severely compromised. In such cases, in situ fixation is not feasible, and LSF may be required as an alternative. The subsequent decision involves whether to perform fusion or proceed with percutaneous fixation. Studies have reported mechanical complications, such as screw loosening, when fixation is performed without fusion. These failures are primarily due to the inability of fixation alone to withstand continuous mechanical stress and physiological weight-bearing forces.2327) One study also found that kyphotic correction was better maintained in patients with high load-sharing scores.26)

Given this background, we aimed to evaluate and compare the clinical and radiological outcomes of three surgical techniques, SSF, LSF, and corpectomy, for treating traumatic thoracolumbar fractures. Our findings indicate that all three methods achieved comparable radiological outcomes in terms of vertebral height restoration, kyphotic angle correction, and spinal canal decompression. However, SSF offered distinct advantages, including reduced surgical invasiveness, shorter operative time, and improved perioperative parameters.

AVBH and kyphotic correction were successfully restored postoperatively across all groups, and not statistically significant differences were observed. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that SSF and LSF can achieve satisfactory biomechanical alignment in thoracolumbar fractures.16720)

However, the significantly higher preoperative AVBH deficit and spinal canal encroachment were significant predictive factors for corpectomy, as revealed by one-way ANOVA. Consistent with previous studies, these results suggest that the extent of vertebral body height loss and the degree of spinal canal compromise are crucial factors to consider when deciding on the need for corpectomy.1619)

The ROC curve analysis confirmed that preoperative ABVH (%) and spinal canal encroachment (%) are significant prognostic indicators. Specifically, cases with a preoperative ABVH (%) of ≤54.93% or spinal canal encroachment (%) of ≥54.39% were considered for corpectomy. These thresholds generally align with previously reported findings, suggesting that in such scenarios, corpectomy may be a more appropriate surgical option than SSF or LSF.

Despite achieving superior canal decompression, corpectomy was associated with increased operative time, greater intraoperative blood loss, and a more pronounced Hb decline. Furthermore, the longer hospital stay and slower pain reduction observed in the corpectomy group likely reflects the more severe baseline pathology in these patients, as indicated by higher TLICS scores and lower preoperative ASIA grades.

SSF demonstrated comparable radiological outcomes to LSF and corpectomy while offering several perioperative advantages, including shorter operative duration and less intraoperative blood loss. These results support the use of SSF as a minimally invasive yet effective treatment option, particularly for younger or military patients with good bone quality and moderate spinal canal encroachment, as it may better preserve spinal mobility and facilitate earlier functional recovery.

Neurological recovery, as assessed by the ASIA scale, improved in all groups postoperatively; however, the degree of recovery was limited in the corpectomy group. This likely reflects the greater preoperative neurological impairment in these patients rather than an inherent limitation of the surgical approach. Similarly, the higher postoperative pain scores in the corpectomy group could be attributed to the more extensive surgical exposure and manipulation required for anterior column reconstruction.

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design and relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the choice of surgical technique was not randomized rather, it was based on injury severity and the surgeon’s discretion, which introduces potential selection bias. Third, due to the characteristics of the military population, many patients are discharged from service after spinal surgery. This led to a relatively short follow-up period of 3–9 months, which might be insufficient to fully evaluate long-term outcomes such as fusion status, hardware longevity, or adjacent segment degeneration. Fourth, the patient population is limited to young, physically active males due to the nature of the military hospital setting. This may affect the applicability of the results to a broader population. Bone mineral density could not be routinely assessed in acute trauma cases; however, most patients were presumed to have relatively high bone density. Fifth, all patients in this study underwent fusion surgery; however, recent studies suggest that in cases of burst fracture, fixation alone may yield outcomes comparable to those of fusion.4581424) Therefore, further research is needed to determine whether similar results can be achieved between SSF and LSF without fusion.

Nevertheless, the strength of this study lies in its direct comparison of three common surgical strategies under consistent institutional protocols, offering valuable insights into their relative efficacy and limitations.

CONCLUSION

SSF offers an effective and less invasive alternative to LSF or corpectomy for thoracolumbar burst fractures. It provides comparable outcomes with reduced operative time and blood loss. However, corpectomy should be considered when preoperative ABVH (%) is ≤54.93% or spinal canal encroachment (%) is ≥54.39%.
