Performance of the SVEAT (Symptoms, Vascular Disease History, Electrocardiography, Age, and Troponin) score for predicting 90-day major adverse cardiac events in patients with acute chest pain
Authors: Ali Ayvaz, Akkan Avci, Mustafa Oguz Tugcan, Muhammed Mert Can, Ahmet Burak Urfalioglu, Erdem Aksay, Yeliz Simsek, Sadiye Yolcu Seven, Begum Seyda Avci, Ecem Basak Kaya, Aysegul Sancaktar, Mehmet Bankir, Ramazan Guven

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the emergency department, chest pain is a common presentation, and the primary clinical challenge is the early identification of patients at risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE). This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the SVEAT (Symptoms, Vascular Disease History, ECG, Age, and Troponin) score in predicting 90-day MACE in patients presenting with acute chest pain.MethodsThis study included 348 patients presenting with acute chest pain. Serial high-sensitivity troponin I (HsTn-I) measurements were obtained at admission and 2 h later. Electrocardiographic and clinical data were recorded, and the SVEAT, HEART, and TIMI scores were calculated. Patients were followed for 90 days for MACE occurrence.ResultsMACE occurred in 7.2% of patients during the 90-day follow-up. The second high-sensitivity troponin I (HsTn-I) measurement was significantly associated with MACE (p = 0.007). The SVEAT score demonstrated the highest discriminative performance (AUC = 0.806), compared with the HEART score (AUC = 0.756) and TIMI score (AUC = 0.753). At the optimal cut-off value of 4, the SVEAT score showed a sensitivity of 0.56 and a specificity of 0.86. In comparison, both the HEART and TIMI scores (cut-off = 3) showed higher sensitivity (0.72), with specificity values of 0.72 and 0.63, respectively.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.ConclusionThe SVEAT score may have potential as a rule-in tool for predicting 90-day MACE in emergency department patients presenting with acute chest pain, due to its higher specificity; however, this is accompanied by relatively low sensitivity, which limits its utility as a standalone screening tool.Keywords: Chest pain, Acute coronary syndrome, SVEAT score, Major adverse cardiac events, Emergency department

Introduction

Chest pain is one of the most common reasons for emergency department visits and may arise from conditions ranging from benign causes to life-threatening diseases [1, 2]. The primary goal in emergency care is to rapidly identify patients at high risk of mortality or morbidity while safely ruling out serious conditions in low-risk individuals [3, 4].

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) represents one of the most important causes of chest pain and may lead to serious outcomes when diagnosis is delayed [5, 6]. In patients presenting with suspected ACS, initial electrocardiography and baseline high-sensitivity troponin I (HsTn-I) measurements may not be sufficient to reliably exclude the diagnosis, often leading to prolonged observation or hospital admission [7–9]. To support decision-making in patients with suspected ACS, several risk stratification tools, including TIMI, HEART, GRACE, T-MACS, and EDACS, have been developed. However, their ability to accurately identify truly low-risk patients with suspected ACS, particularly those with atypical symptoms or non-specific findings, remains limited [10, 11].

The SVEAT score is a recently developed risk stratification tool that incorporates clinical features, vascular history, electrocardiographic findings, age, and high-sensitivity troponin I levels. Although it has shown potential for improving risk stratification in acute chest pain patients with suspected ACS, evidence regarding its performance in diverse populations and over longer follow-up periods remains limited [12–15]. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the predictive performance of the SVEAT score for 90-day major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in patients presenting to the emergency department with acute chest pain, focusing on prognostic risk stratification rather than diagnostic confirmation of acute coronary syndrome at presentation.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This investigation was conducted as a prospective, single-center, observational cohort study in the Department of Emergency Medicine at Adana City Training and Research Hospital. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the hospital’s Scientific Research Ethics Committee during its 7th meeting held on 7 November 2024 (Decision No. 212). All study procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was secured from each participant prior to enrollment. The final study cohort consisted of 348 patients who presented to the emergency department with acute chest pain between 1 December 2024 and 30 September 2025.

Sample size estimation was performed during the study planning phase using G*Power software (version 3.1). The calculation was based on the primary endpoint of 90-day major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and aimed to ensure adequate statistical power for evaluating the prognostic performance of the SVEAT, HEART, and TIMI scores in emergency department patients presenting with acute chest pain. An a priori two-tailed analysis was performed using a significance level (α) of 0.05, statistical power of 85% (1 − β), an allocation ratio of 1:1, and a medium effect size assumption corresponding to Cohen’s d = 0.50. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 502 participants. Since the study was designed to evaluate prognostic performance in a real-world cohort, this calculation was intended to provide adequate overall statistical power rather than to serve as a dedicated ROC curve–based or AUC comparison sample size analysis.

A total of 502 consecutive patients presenting to the emergency department with acute chest pain between 1 December 2024 and 30 September 2025 were screened for eligibility. All patients presenting with chest pain were initially assessed. Adult patients aged 18 years or older who presented with chest pain lasting longer than five minutes as their primary symptom were considered eligible for inclusion.

During the screening phase, patients were excluded if chest pain was trauma-related (n = 95), if 90-day MACE data were unavailable (n = 28), if the patient was pregnant (n = 8), or if the evaluation process could not be completed because of refusal to participate or protocol-related deviations during follow-up (n = 23).

After applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 154 patients were excluded, resulting in a final study cohort of 348 patients. The patient selection process was summarized in Fig. 1 using a flow diagram.

Flow diagram showing patients included in and excluded from the study

Patient information was prospectively obtained by the attending emergency physician using a standardized data collection form. Recorded variables included demographic data such as age and sex, along with vital signs measured at presentation, including heart rate, arterial blood pressure, respiratory rate, body temperature, peripheral oxygen saturation, and Glasgow Coma Scale score. Symptom characteristics were systematically documented for all patients. In addition, a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was performed at the time of presentation, and ECG findings were evaluated for ST-segment deviations, T-wave abnormalities, and the presence of bundle branch block. Electrocardiograms were independently interpreted by a cardiology specialist. Electrocardiographic findings used for risk score calculations were based on the cardiologist’s assessment.

As part of the laboratory evaluation, HsTn-I levels were obtained for all patients at two time points: at initial presentation and two hours thereafter. HsTn-I concentrations were measured using the Beckman Coulter Access HsTn-I assay on the UniCel DxI 800 Access Immunoassay System analytical platform. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the assay-specific 99th percentile upper reference limit (URL) was 16 ng/L. Sex-specific thresholds were not used in the present study. The coefficient of variation (CV) at the 99th percentile was < 10%. HsTn-I elevation was defined as values > 16 ng/L according to the institutional laboratory cut-off.

The SVEAT score was calculated at the time of emergency department presentation based on symptoms, history of vascular disease, electrocardiographic findings, age, and HsTn-I levels.

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of 90-day MACE, defined as a composite of myocardial infarction, urgent coronary revascularization, cardiovascular death, ventricular arrhythmia, cardiogenic shock, atrioventricular block, or sudden cardiac arrest. Assessment of MACE occurrence at follow-up was performed through review of electronic health records (e-Nabız system) and supplemented, when necessary, by telephone contact.

In addition, the diagnosis of ACS during the index visit was assessed based on objective clinical data, including electrocardiographic findings, serial HsTn-I measurements, and physician evaluation, in accordance with guideline-based criteria. However, ACS diagnosis was not used as an inclusion criterion, as the study was designed to reflect an undifferentiated emergency department chest pain population.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]) according to distribution characteristics. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. When comparisons involved three or more groups, non-parametric variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

The discriminative performance of the SVEAT, HEART, and TIMI scores for predicting 90-day MACE was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio, and negative likelihood ratio were subsequently calculated for each scoring system. Formal pairwise comparison of ROC curves was performed using the DeLong test. Confidence intervals for diagnostic performance measures were calculated using asymptotic methods. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study cohort had a mean age of 51 ± 16 years. Age did not differ significantly between patients who experienced MACE and those who did not (p = 0.78). Male patients constituted 60.1% of the overall population; however, sex was not significantly associated with the occurrence of MACE (p = 0.089). In addition, no significant associations were identified between MACE development and the presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, or chest pain characteristics. In contrast, elevated values observed in the second HsTn-I measurement were significantly related to the development of MACE (p = 0.007) (Table 1).

Discriminative performance of the three risk stratification scores was assessed by ROC curve analysis and quantified using the AUC. Among the evaluated tools, the SVEAT score showed the highest ability to differentiate outcomes, achieving an AUC of 0.806 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.717–0.895). The HEART score demonstrated moderate performance with an AUC of 0.756 (95% CI: 0.661–0.852), while the TIMI score yielded an AUC of 0.753 (95% CI: 0.649–0.856) (Table 2).

When optimal threshold values were applied, a cut-off of 3 for both the HEART and TIMI scores produced a sensitivity of 0.72 (95% CI: 54.4–89.6). In comparison, the SVEAT score demonstrated a lower sensitivity of 0.56 (95% CI: 36.5–75.5) at a cut-off value of 4. Despite this, the SVEAT score achieved the highest specificity at 0.86 (95% CI: 0.82–0.90). Positive likelihood ratios were highest for the SVEAT score (3.933; 95% CI: 2.81–5.23), followed by the HEART (2.58) and TIMI (1.97) scores. Negative likelihood ratios were 0.51 (95% CI: 0.15–0.60), 0.39 (95% CI: 0.10–0.62), and 0.44 (95% CI: 0.25–0.80) for the SVEAT, HEART, and TIMI scores, respectively (Table 3).

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the SVEAT score demonstrated higher discriminative performance for predicting 90-day MACE, with an AUC of 0.806 compared with the HEART (AUC = 0.756) and TIMI (AUC = 0.753) scores. Visual inspection of the ROC curves suggested a more favorable balance between sensitivity and specificity across a range of threshold values.

Formal comparison of ROC curves using the DeLong test showed that the AUC of the SVEAT score was significantly higher than that of the HEART score (AUC difference = 0.050, p = 0.030) and TIMI score (AUC difference = 0.053, p = 0.016). No significant difference was observed between the HEART and TIMI scores (AUC difference = 0.004, p = 0.851).

Discussion

In our study, the absence of significant differences in age, sex and traditional cardiovascular risk factors between patients who did and did not experience MACE suggests that demographic and static clinical variables are not particularly useful for predicting short-term major cardiovascular events. This finding is consistent with AHA/ACC guidelines and prospective studies emphasising that dynamic clinical and biochemical parameters are more important than classical risk profiles for short-term risk assessment [2, 3]. Although the initial HsTn-I level was not associated with MACE, the significant association between elevated values in the second HsTn-I measurement and MACE (p = 0.007) highlights the importance of biomarker kinetics in short- to mid-term risk prediction. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that serial measurements provide more prognostic information than single measurements [16, 17]. In this respect, our study suggests that biomarker dynamics may be more important than demographic characteristics for early risk stratification.

One of the main contributions of this study is the evaluation of the predictive performance of risk scores for 90-day MACE, whereas most previous studies have focused on shorter follow-up periods such as a 30-day follow-up period [13, 18, 19]. In this study, the SVEAT score demonstrated higher discriminative performance (AUC = 0.806) compared with the HEART (AUC = 0.756) and TIMI (AUC = 0.753) scores; however, this difference may not represent clinically significant superiority. While the SVEAT score showed higher specificity (86%), its relatively low sensitivity (56%) limits its utility as a rule-out tool in acute chest pain populations. The positive likelihood ratio (3.93) indicates only moderate diagnostic utility for ruling in disease, whereas the HEART score demonstrated better rule-out performance based on its lower negative likelihood ratio (0.51). Therefore, the overall diagnostic advantage of the SVEAT score over established scores appears limited and should be interpreted with caution. Although these differences were statistically significant in the DeLong analysis, the absolute differences in AUC were small (approximately 0.05), and the higher AUC of the SVEAT score should be interpreted cautiously, as statistical significance does not always indicate a clinically meaningful improvement in decision-making.

This finding is consistent with the results of a prospective observational study by Gol et al., one of the most recent and comprehensive investigations to evaluate the clinical performance of the SVEAT score [13]. In their study of 809 patients, Gol et al. reported that the SVEAT score significantly outperformed the HEART score in predicting 30-day MACE, with respective AUC values of 0.916 and 0.856 [13]. Although the AUC values in our study were lower, the SVEAT score still outperformed the HEART and TIMI scores, which enhances the originality and clinical relevance of our findings.

Analysis of diagnostic performance showed that the HEART and TIMI scores had higher sensitivity (72%), whereas the SVEAT score demonstrated higher specificity (86%). The higher positive likelihood ratio of the SVEAT score (3.93) suggests better ability to identify patients with MACE within this cohort, whereas the HEART score showed a lower negative likelihood ratio (0.39), suggesting better rule-out performance [18, 19]. The TIMI score, on the other hand, has been shown in the literature to perform more poorly in terms of both specificity (63%) and likelihood ratios, falling behind the HEART and SVEAT scores when used on heterogeneous emergency department chest pain populations [20].

The fact that 92.8% of patients did not experience MACE during the 90-day follow-up period, and that urgent coronary revascularisation was the most frequently observed event (5.2%), indicates that the risk of serious cardiac events in the medium term is relatively low, and that these events are predominantly related to ischemic processes. This is consistent with the 6.1% MACE rate reported by Gol et al. during a 30-day follow-up period [13]. Furthermore, the low incidence of life-threatening events, such as acute heart failure (1.4%) and ventricular arrhythmia (0.6%), supports the idea that serious adverse events are uncommon among patients categorised as low risk by risk scores [21, 22]. The majority of baseline electrocardiograms being normal (77.9%) suggests that ECG alone may be insufficient for comprehensive risk stratification. Multicomponent risk scores, such as SVEAT, may help identify patients at high risk of a major adverse cardiac event within a 90-day period [13, 23, 24]. However, the relatively low number of observed events limits the statistical power of these findings. Larger multicenter studies are required before firm conclusions regarding clinical implementation can be drawn.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The single-center, observational design may limit the external validity of the results. In addition, MACE evaluation was confined to a 90-day follow-up period, preventing assessment of longer-term cardiovascular outcomes. The analysis was also restricted to baseline and second HsTn-I measurements, limiting evaluation of more frequent serial measurements.

Although a priori sample size estimation indicated 502 participants, only 348 patients were included. Therefore, the study may have been underpowered for certain outcomes.

Another important limitation is the relatively small number of observed MACE events (n = 25), which may have reduced the statistical power of the analyses and the precision of diagnostic performance estimates, including sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, and ROC-based measures.

Because baseline data for excluded patients were incomplete, a formal comparison between included and excluded groups could not be performed, which may introduce potential attrition bias.

Finally, ACS diagnosis was not used as an inclusion criterion; although objective clinical data were used during index evaluation, the study population represented an undifferentiated chest pain cohort rather than confirmed ACS patients. Calibration analysis and decision curve analysis were not performed, which limits evaluation of model calibration and net clinical benefit.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that demographic variables and traditional cardiovascular risk factors may have limited value in predicting 90-day MACE in patients presenting to the emergency department with chest pain. In contrast, serial HsTn-I measurements and multidimensional risk scores demonstrated better prognostic performance. The SVEAT score showed the highest discriminative ability and may serve as a rule-in tool for MACE due to its higher specificity. However, the limited number of events.

restricts the strength of these conclusions, and further prospective studies are required for validation.
