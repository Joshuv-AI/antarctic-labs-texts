Impact of eccentric cycling in coronary rehabilitation program: a pragmatic randomized controlled trial versus conventional rehabilitation
Authors: Delphine BESSON, Amadou-Khalilou SOW, Isabelle FOURNEL, Anaïs GOUTERON, Aurélie GUDJONCIK, Jean M. CASILLAS, Paul ORNETTI, Davy LAROCHE
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThis randomized controlled trial examined the feasibility of adding eccentric exercise to a conventional cardiac rehabilitation program (CCRP) for coronary heart disease patients.METHODSNinety-three patients were randomly assigned to either the MIX group (eccentric ergometer + CCRP) or the CON group (concentric ergometer + CCRP) for 7 weeks. Training effectiveness was assessed based on “good responders” showing improved functional capacities, such as 6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance and maximal voluntary contraction of the plantar flexors (ankle MVC). Safety was monitored with a visual analog scale for muscle soreness, perceived exertion, and heart rate during training.RESULTSThe proportion of good responders was similar between groups (26% in MIX, 29% in CON, P=0.744). Both groups improved in 6MWT (CON: 12.6%, MIX: 16.14%) and ankle MVC (CON: 15.5%, MIX: 11.30%), with no significant differences. Exercise tolerance did not differ significantly between the groups, but perceived effort was significantly lower in the MIX group (P<0.0001) compared to the CON group.CONCLUSIONSIntegrating eccentric exercise into cardiac rehabilitation is safe and well-tolerated. Nevertheless, this study did not find significant advantages over conventional programs for coronary heart disease patients. Further research should explore specific patient groups or conditions where eccentric exercise may be more beneficial, emphasizing personalized prescriptions and gradual workload progression for better cardiac rehabilitation outcomes.

Coronary heart disease represents a significant global health burden leading to frequent cardio-respiratory disabilities and considerable midterm mortality rates. In the subacute phase after a major coronary event, international guidelines promote cardiac rehabilitation (CR) programs in order to enhance functional recovery and avoid recurrence through a combination of adherence to a treatment plan and adoption of a healthy lifestyle.1 Numerous studies have highlighted the beneficial effects of CR programs on physical capacity, quality of life, probability of recurrent events, and hospitalization or mortality.2, 3 The conventional CR is designed to facilitate the restoration of functional, muscular and cardiorespiratory capacities in patients with coronary artery disease, promoting their reintegration into daily life with a focus on achieving optimal autonomy. Typically, CR programs involve tailored aerobic training of various intensity, including continuous or intermittent aerobic endurance training utilizing concentric (muscle fiber shortening) ergometers, potentially supplemented by resistance training using body weight, small equipment or weight machines.4 However, concentric training can induce considerable cardiovascular constraints, in particular heart rate increase, in patients already weakened by their condition.

In recent decades, approaches using eccentric (muscle fiber lengthening) exercise have been suggested to reduce existing constraints and to improve muscle workout.5 Eccentric muscular loading or braking is a common human muscular activity in activities of daily life, such as lowering to the floor, going downstairs or any movement that resists gravity. Several studies have focused on specific approaches using eccentric ergometer training,6, 7 wherein dynamic energy is provided by the device, opposing the movement by generating a negative joint torque.8 Comparative analyses between eccentric and concentric ergometer training have shown that eccentric exercise induces beneficial effects.9 Specifically, it leads to functional and structural changes in locomotor muscles, resulting in increased strength and muscle fiber size.10 The benefits seen with eccentric training were obtained with lower cardiovascular, respiratory, and metabolic demands but with the same mechanical power as concentric cycling.11-13 Nevertheless, whatever the population, individuals unaccustomed to eccentric exercise can potentially experience muscle damage and delayed pain, commonly known as “delayed-onset muscular soreness” (DOMS).10, 14 To date, the most useful preventive strategy consists of repeated sessions involving submaximal eccentric contractions, progressively increasing the intensity over the course of the program,11-13 thus obtaining the benefits of eccentric exercise and minimizing any adverse effects.

The existing findings have been obtained in cohorts of healthy15, 16 volunteers and patients with chronic cardiorespiratory disease,6, 9 and the approach seems particularly well-suited for cardiac populations. However, few studies were designed with dedicated protocols comparing eccentric versus concentric training.5, 17

Hence, to validate the potential interest of eccentric training in cardiac rehabilitation, we thought it would be interesting to include eccentric exercise in a conventional CR program and then to compare the resulting mixed eccentric ergometer/conventional rehabilitation program (MIX) to a concentric ergometer/conventional rehabilitation program (CON), as was previously done in a sample of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or coronary heart disease.5, 18 The combination of eccentric and concentric exercise training could potentiate the beneficial effects of conventional CR by increasing tolerance due to the lowered effort required by the cardiorespiratory system during exercise, while at the same time increasing muscular capacity.

The aims of the current study were to determine the percentage of good responders and to compare the effectiveness of a mixed CR program (i.e. combining concentric and eccentric exercises) versus conventional exercise (only concentric) in patients with coronary heart disease referred to a rehabilitation center. Additionally, we wanted to compare safety between a mixed and conventional program in this population. We hypothesized that the integration of concentric and eccentric exercises both enhances functional outcomes and ensures the safety of patients taking part in the rehabilitation process.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This randomized controlled open-label single center trial was carried out in outpatients with coronary disease referred to a CR program at the Dijon Bourgogne University Hospital (France).

The ENERGETIC study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Est 1, July 6, 2018) and authorized by the French National Agency for Drug Safety. This study has been registered in the Clinical Trials database (reference NCT02156245).

All participants included in this trial provided informed written consent. They then had a standard evaluation for CR referrals, including an initial cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET). After the conventional evaluation, patients were included in this study and started a CR program of 20 sessions, 3 times per week, following the recommendation of French Society of Cardiology.19 Randomization was performed online by the investigating physician using Tenalea software (Netherlands, Kanker Institute). Allocation was based on minimization considering age, gender and body mass index. Patients were randomized to the CON group or the MIX group with a 2:1 ratio. This ratio was chosen for organizational reasons due to the availability of only one eccentric cycle ergometer.

Initial and final evaluations were scheduled in the 15 days before the start (M0) and the 15 days following the end of the program (M2), respectively. They included conventional cardiopulmonary exercise tests and functional evaluation with 6MMT and maximal strength. Evaluation and training processes are summarized in Figure 1. Exercise was stopped when there was an abnormal heart rate elevation (maximal heart rate during CPET) or blood pressure (systolic blood pressure >200 mmHg), or when the patient described functional pain (VAS >5) or chest pain or feeling ill.

Description of evaluations

Conventional cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET)

The initial and final CPETs were performed using a conventional electronically braked cycle ergometer. Heart rate was monitored by a 12-lead ECG system and VO2 was analyzed with continuous assessment of breath-by-breath gas exchange using a portable device (K5, Cosmed, Rome, Italy).

The workload was started at 20 watts and then increased every minute by 10-watt increments. The first ventilatory threshold (SV1) was determined by the Wasserman method.20 The VO2 peak was defined as the highest value during the last 30 seconds of the stress test.

6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT)

The 6MWT at a self-selected speed was performed on a flat 50-meter-long indoor walking track that the patient was familiar with. After a rest period, patients were instructed to walk for 6 minutes at their freely-chosen walking speed. Patients were told the elapsed time every 2 minutes by the same trained operator who was walking approximately one meter behind. The test result was expressed in meters and walking speed was calculated in meters per second. The parameters monitored during the test were continuous assessments of breath-by-breath gas exchange using the same portable device as for the CPET, and continuous heart rate monitoring (HRM3-SS, Garmin, Olathe, Kansas). The mean VO2 during the last 30 seconds of the 6MWT was retained as the reference value for the assessment of aerobic capacity.21

Maximal isometric voluntary contraction

Maximal isometric voluntary contraction of the knee extensor (Knee MVC) and the ankle (Ankle MVC) were measured by an isometric maximal voluntary contraction of the right lower limb using a dynamometer (S4Pro, Biodex Medical Systems, New York, NY, USA). Patients warmed up for 10 minutes on a cycle ergometer and were then familiarized with the test device and procedure. The test required three maximal voluntary isometric contractions in a seated position. Quadricep isometric maximal voluntary contraction was performed at a knee angle of 90° (0° corresponding to full knee extension). Plantar flexor isometric maximal voluntary contraction was performed at an ankle angle of 90° (0° corresponding to full plantar flexion). A strap was placed around the foot to secure it firmly to the pedal. Each contraction was followed by a 30-second rest period. The highest isometric torque (N.m) of the three trials was selected.

Participants

We included patients aged 40 to 75 years with coronary heart disease and referred to a CR program for the first time.

The main exclusion criteria were: heart failure with altered ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction <45% evaluated with echocardiographic Simpson method), severe obstructive cardiomyopathy, severe aortic valve stenosis, severe heart rhythm disorders or conduction disorders not corrected by a pacemaker and detected during the initial stress test; cardiac intracavitary thrombus, severe pulmonary artery hypertension (>70 mmHg), recent history of venous thromboembolism (previous 3 months), heart transplant, associated medical condition that could substantially affect functional capacities (e.g. non-stabilized metabolic disorders such as chronic kidney disease, major asthenia linked to a severe non-stabilized disorder such as neoplasia, systemic disease, etc.), physical disorder of the lower limbs that could impair training, whether neurological (central or peripheral), arterial (in particular, peripheral artery disease with a systolic index <0.6), or orthopedic (degenerative or inflammatory rheumatoid arthritis), and the consumption of corticosteroids, anabolic steroids or L-carnitine.

Data were collected prospectively in case report form and stored in the database of the Clinical Investigation Center (CIC) of the Dijon Bourgogne University Hospital.

Intervention

For both groups: shared training program

All patients had 20 sessions of a shared training program corresponding to the conventional CR except for cycling ergometer (usually concentric, but depending on randomization arm in the study). Each session involved 3 training days per week for 7 weeks (Figure 1) and included the following activities (repeated every week):

Day 1 (3 * 22 minutes of exercise): 1) 22 minutes of exercise on cycling ergometer (concentric or eccentric depending on group); 2) 22 minutes of exercises on ergometer (arm ergometer, treadmill, rowing machine or elliptical trainer); 3) 22 minutes of exercise on cycling ergometer (concentric or eccentric depending on group). Five-minute warm-up and 2 minutes of active recovery was included in each 22-minute session.

Day 2 (60 minutes of exercise): strengthening exercises on a fitness machine or with accessories (elastics, halters…).

Day 3 (3 * 22 minutes of exercise): 1) 22 minutes of exercise on ergometer (arm ergometer, treadmill, rowing machine or elliptical trainer); 2) 22 minutes of exercise on cycling ergometer (concentric or eccentric depending on group); 3) 22 minutes of exercise on ergometer (arm ergometer, treadmill, rowing machine or elliptical trainer). Five-minute warm-up and 2 minutes of active recovery was included in each 22-minute session.

Cycling ergometer use for the CON group

On days 1 and 3, patients used a concentric cycle ergometer (Kardiomed 521, Kardiomed®, Serbia). Patients from the CON group did a total of 66 minutes per week of concentric cycle exercise. The workload was adjusted to obtain the heart rate target corresponding to the first ventilatory threshold determined during the initial CPET.

Cycling ergometer use for the MIX group

On days1 and 3, patients used an eccentric cycle ergometer (Tech Med TMS, 89290 Champs-sur-Yonne, France). Patients from the MIX group did a total of 66 minutes per week of eccentric cycle ergometer exercise. The pedal speed was set at 15 revolutions per minute and the patients were instructed to adjust the pedal resistance by limiting the rise of their heart rate to that corresponding to the first ventilatory threshold determined during the initial CPET. Then, the resistance was increased every 3 sessions, provided that the perceived effort did not exceed 11 on the 6-20 Borg Scale (with 6 rated as “very, very light” and 20 as “extremely hard”),22 in accordance with previous work that had shown the effectiveness and good tolerance of low intensity ECC training on patients with cardiovascular disease.23

Outcome measures

The primary objective of this superiority trial was to compare the improvement in functional capacities between the MIX group and the CON group. Building upon prior research involving cardiac patients,24 a composite primary endpoint was utilized to quantify both locomotor and muscular capabilities. To ensure clinical relevance, it was imperative to detect a minimum clinically significant improvement in both the distance covered during the 6MWT and the strength of the plantar flexor muscles to validate the efficacy of the MIX protocol. Consequently, patients were categorized as “good responders” if they demonstrated a minimum increase of 10% in the distance covered during the 6MWT and a 15% improvement in plantar flexor strength, in accordance with the findings of Gremeaux et al.24 We hypothesized that 20% of patients in the CON group and 50% of patients in the MIX group would have improved results for the 6MWT and the Ankle MVC.

Efficacy secondary outcomes included:

change in functional capacity, including the distance walked during the 6MWT, the maximal power during CPET and maximal isometric contraction measurement of plantar flexion and knee extension measured between baseline and M2;

change in functional capacity, including the distance walked during the 6MWT, the maximal power during CPET and maximal isometric contraction measurement of plantar flexion and knee extension measured between baseline and M2;

change in physiological capacity, including VO2 and heart rate measurements during CPET and 6MWT between baseline and M2;

change in physiological capacity, including VO2 and heart rate measurements during CPET and 6MWT between baseline and M2;

progression in the workload for the eccentric and concentric ergometers during training;

progression in the workload for the eccentric and concentric ergometers during training;

safety during sessions: heart rate was collected at rest before each session and at the end of each exercise. Patients used a visual analogue scale (VAS 0-10, min-max) to assess musculoskeletal pain, and the Borg scale was assessed at the end of each exercise to quantify the rate of exertion. Training sessions were cancelled if patients described muscle pain VAS >4 before training. Side effects were gathered for each session and for all patients.

Sample size

The expected percentage of responders in the CON group was 20% versus 50% in the MIX group. Under these assumptions, associated with an alpha risk of 5%, a power of 80%, the inclusion of 2 patients randomized in CON group for 1 patient randomized in MIX group and a possible loss of follow-up, 84 analyzable patients were required (62 in the CON group and 31 in the MIX group).

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis was performed on complete data, with an intention-to-treat analysis under the assumption of maximum bias for patients lost to follow-up (i.e. patients lost to follow-up were considered to be non-good responders in the MIX group, and good responders in the CON group). The proportion of good responders was compared between groups using the Chi2 test. A sensitivity analysis was conducted on patients with complete data. Secondary analyses were also performed on complete data. Comparisons between groups relied on Chi2 test or Fisher’s Exact Test for qualitative variables, and Wilcoxon or Student’s t-test for quantitative variables, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using R4.1.0. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To obtain an overall estimate of the central trends of the maximum power developed during the 7 weeks for each group, we calculated the overall mean for each group.

Results

Among 370 patients with coronary heart disease referred for a CR program at our rehabilitation center, 93 were eligible and agreed to participate in this study; 31 patients were allocated to the MIX group and 62 patients to the CON group. In each group, three patients did not have a final evaluation (one was lost to follow-up and two for medical reasons in the CON group, and two were lost to follow-up and one for medical reasons in the MIX group). Efficacy

The proportion of good responders did not significantly differ in the two arms in the intention-to-treat analysis (26% in the MIX group (8/31) and 29% in the CON group (18/62), P=0.744) or in complete data analysis (29% in the MIX group (8/28) and 25% in the CON group (15/59), P=0.756).

There were no significant differences in age, sex, and BMI between good responders and non-good responders. The mean walked distance increased by 22.3% between M0 and M2 for good responders and by 10.7% for non-good responders (P<0.001), and ankle flexor strength increased by 33.73% for good responders and by 7.1% for non-good responders, P<0.001 (Table II).

Other efficacy outcomes

Distance for the 6MWT

The mean walked distance for the 6MWT significantly increased in both groups between M0 and M2, with an average relative change of 12.6% (95% CI: [9.6; 16]) for the CON group and 16.14% (95% CI: [11; 22]) for the MIX group, but without any significant differences between CON and MIX groups (Supplementary Digital Material 1: Supplementary Table I).

Maximal isometric voluntary strength and maximal power

Maximal isometric plantar flexor strength significantly increased in both groups between M0 and M2, with an average relative increase of 15.5% (95% CI: [9.6; 21] for the CON group and 11.30% (95% CI: [2.3; 20]) for the MIX group), with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.522). There was no significant difference between groups regarding the change in maximal isometric knee extensor strength (P=0.679), although a significant increase was observed in the CON group only (8.42%, 95% CI: [4.4; 12] for the CON group and 5.8%, 95% CI: [-0.08; 12] for the MIX group).

Maximal power during the CPET significantly improved in both groups (22.7%, 95% CI: [18; 28] for the CON group and 14%, 95% CI: [9; 19] for the MIX group, P=0.053) (Supplementary Table I).

Change in cardiorespiratory parameters during CPET and 6MWT

During the CPET, the relative change in VO2 was significantly higher for the CON group than for the MIX group (+14.38% [95% CI: 9; 20]) versus 2.3% [95% CI: -4; 8.6] respectively, P=0.006).

On the contrary, the change in heart rate was not significantly different between or within groups (0.02% [95% CI: -2.5; 2.5] versus 1.12% [95% CI: -2.7; 4.9] respectively for CON and MIX groups, P=0.368) (Supplementary Table I).

Change in workload during training

The overall mean of the maximum power developed during the program was significantly lower in the CON group (90.7±26.5 watts) than in the MIX group (168±66.93 watts, P<0.001) (Figure 3). The power developed during the training increased progressively from week 1 to week 7 for both groups with a ratio 2.3 times higher in the MIX group than the CON group (229±53 watts compared to 99.1±26, P<0.001) in the seventh week.

The relative change in heart rate over the study period was significantly lower for the MIX group than for the CON group (Figure 3). During the seventh week, on average, the relative change in heart rate was 5 times higher in the CON group (74.1% [95% CI 67; 81] versus 15.63% [95% CI 10; 21] in the MIX group, P<0.001).

Safety outcomes

Rate of perceived exertion and pain

Effort was perceived as being significantly lower for the MIX group than the CON group, and the mean rate of perceived exertion was always <10 for the MIX group. This result was consistent throughout training (P<0.0001 for each week). Patients from the MIX group tended to describe less muscle pain in the legs than those from the CON group, with significant differences at the end of CR (week 5, P<0.001 and week 6, P<0.003) (Figure 4).

—Mean and standard deviation of rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with Borg Scale (A) and number of pain >4 with Visual Analogic Scale (VAS; B) during the seven weeks of training between concentric and concentric/eccentric group. *Significant differences (P<0.05).

Tolerance outcomes

CR was never stopped for heart rate or blood pressure abnormalities. During the study period, nine serious adverse events were reported (6/62 in the CON group and 3/31 in the MIX group), not medically related to the study. Among the 1241 training sessions, 112 adverse events occurred during cycling training sessions, with no significant difference between the two groups (10% in the CON group versus 7% in the MIX group, P=0.114). Among these events, 98 were related to musculoskeletal issues, 11 to cardiorespiratory problems and 3 to other conditions. Discussion

In this study, we hypothesized that adding eccentric exercises to a conventional cardiac rehabilitation program for coronary heart disease patients could improve their functional capacities compared to the conventional approach. Our hypothesis was based on previous studies25, 26 in which significant physical improvements were observed following eccentric training versus concentric training. The results did not confirm our hypothesis seeing as the similar results obtained with the MIX group and the CON group in terms of percentage of responders (26% versus 29% respectively), and the results that were clearly lower than expected (26% in our trial versus 50% expected). Furthermore, no different evolution between groups for functional and physiological capacities has been found during initial and final evaluations. However, participants in the MIX group reported higher workload along session and better safety during training.

This study presents some limits. First, considering the initial hypothesis, the study was designed as a superiority trial. However, the observed difference between the groups is substantially smaller than anticipated and not significantly different. These results need to be confirmed by a non-inferiority trial in order to conclude the non-inferiority of MIX versus CON. Secondly, eccentric exercises were not the main activity during training, so the effects of this type of exercise could be masked. However, our main objective was to evaluate the contribution of eccentric exercise as a complement to conventional training, and we wanted to go a step beyond by studying the integration of eccentric exercise directly to the CR program. The standard-of-care practices were therefore maintained within the rehabilitation program while substituting concentric cycling exercises with eccentric ergocycle exercises. Overall, cycling (both MIX and CON group) accounted for 34% of the total exercises time during rehabilitation. This duration was shorter than in other studies, which could have led to effects that were reduced compared to other studies that used only eccentric versus only concentric exercise.17, 27 While eccentric training is an interesting approach for patients with chronic disease, cycle ergometers are perhaps not the most effective means of implementing this type of movement. Eccentric cycling is a good approach which can be easily compared with concentric cycling, but other types of eccentric exercises can also be used, such as stair descent exercises24 or resistance training.7 These exercises complement usual care while avoiding the financial cost of an eccentric ergometer. Finally, patients included in this study may have a lower comorbidity burden compared with patients usually found in cardiac rehabilitation.

Over the last thirty years, numerous studies have reported improved muscle strength or functional capacities after eccentric training, both in healthy subjects15, 16, 28 and in patients with chronic diseases.5, 24, 25, 27, 29 These observations were a convincing basis to justify implementing eccentric training in patients with chronic respiratory disease or muscle deconditioning.11, 30Previous studies found improvements in knee extensor strength5, 24, 25 and less frequently in ankle flexor strength.23, 25 Gremeaux et al. and Casillas et al. also found a significant different evolution between eccentric and concentric group for ankle flexor strength in favor of eccentric exercise. We decided to assess changes in plantar flexor muscle strength given that this muscle play a major role in the propulsive phase of human locomotion by combining this criterion with changes in walking distance at 6MWT. Our knowledge, few studies dealt with the evolution of functional capacities with the 6MWT after a cycle training but none of them found significant difference of distance23, 25-27 between eccentric and concentric. Finally, the results of our study were consistent with previous studies for the evolution of distance during 6MWT but not for the muscular strength of ankle flexor.

Some results are conflicting with previous studies. Unlike Steiner et al., we did not find a greater increase in knee extensor strength after training using the eccentric bike. Results concerning maximal power during CPET was also different than Meyer et al. although results about VO2 pic during CPET was same. Several hypotheses may be envisaged. First, eccentric training was not associated with the changes of aerobic metabolism.29, 31 This result was confirmed with the CPET, during which there was a significant increase in maximal power but without an increase in VO2. Secondly, all evaluations used concentric (CPET) or isometric (ankle or Knee MVC) contractions. Thirdly, some patients included in this study might not have improved their capacities because their initial abilities were not significantly altered, and because of their limited comorbidities, especially musculoskeletal.

One could expect that because the training workload in the MIX group was on average 2.3 times higher than that of the CON group, the MIX group would have significantly better outcomes. This was not the case, which leads us to suppose that the workload and/or the number session were not probably sufficient although duration of our training or sessions was similar than other studies.5, 17, 18, 25 Lewis et al. proposed a fixed workload of 60% of concentric peak power during 8 weeks and found similar results to ours, probably for the same reasons. As far, some of studies that found more convincing results used a higher workload increment than ours, with significantly higher end-of-training loads.17, 32 One of the aims was to limit DOMS, then the limitation of the workload seemed to be important for preserving patients.28 Some studies that compared concentric versus combined concentric/eccentric exercise also found similar results with no further improvements in exercise capacity (distance during the 6MWT) in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease18 and when the workload was 69% of the maximal workload. Steiner et al.5 integrated eccentric work into a standard comprehensive rehabilitation program including light calisthenics, stretching and relaxation, identical for all subjects. The increase in eccentric exercise resulted in a level corresponding to 60% of the peak oxygen uptake. These studies suggest: 1) that the workload was not sufficient to improve patient outcomes, or 2) that eccentric training would be more beneficial for patients who have experienced a significant loss of functional capacity. These hypotheses need to be confirmed by further studies using the same procedure with either a higher workload of eccentric exercises or patients with lower functional capacities. Adding eccentric exercise to the clinical routine is essential to promote its use, as highlighted by a recent prospective protocol comparing the combined use of eccentric and concentric cycle ergometers versus concentric ergometer only over a 5-week period.33

Our study is the first to report that the personalized progressive increase in workload intensity with the eccentric ergometer is well tolerated by coronary disease patients during a 7-week rehabilitation program. The progressive approach made it possible to minimize side effects (leg muscle pain and associated inflammation) and to use higher workloads for prolonged periods.11, 13 On the other hand, the workload was regulated according to perceived exertion for the MIX group and heart rate for the CON group. The fact that side effects were infrequently reported, in comparison with the total number of training sessions, evidenced that these markers were appropriate, which is consistent with our previous work13, 23, 34 and may be associated with good adaptation of contractile muscle properties.35 These eccentric exercises induced a higher workload, less pain, lower cardiorespiratory load and a lower rate of perceived exertion in the MIX group than in the CON group. These results have been reported in several previous studies.11, 17, 30 Overall, the evidence indicates that it is possible and feasible to incorporate eccentric exercise as an alternative or a complementary activity in routine rehabilitation programs.

Conclusions and perspectives

In our study, we wanted to go a step beyond by studying the addition of eccentric exercise to the usual CR program. To our knowledge, few studies have made this type of comparison, and our study was the first in the form of a randomized controlled trial for patients with coronary heart disease. The MIX program appears to be an appropriate complementary approach for this population. The difference in results between previous studies and ours, which may be due to a dose effect or to the initial functional capacities of the participants, warrants further evaluation. Eccentric cycling might be more beneficial in deconditioned populations, particularly those who suffer from muscular or cardiovascular deficits (especially heart failure). It may be of interest to start CR programs with eccentric exercise in order to improve muscle strength without significant cardio-respiratory strain, and then to gradually integrate the concentric approach. The balance between eccentric and concentric activities should be tailored to cardio-respiratory limitations and specific patient needs. Finally, we found that the rate of perceived exertion seems well indicated to minimize adverse events and to standardize the monitoring of effort during eccentric exercises. In order to prevent muscle damage, the workload needs to be increased incrementally and adjusted on an individual basis.
