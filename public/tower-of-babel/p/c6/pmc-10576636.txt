Intratracheal recurrence of thymoma 7 years after complete resection
Authors: Kaoru Fukuyama, Yasunobu Funakoshi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 66-year-old woman with a massive anterior mediastinal tumour was diagnosed with type B2 thymoma. After 2 courses of chemotherapy, extended thymectomy and partial resection of the lung and large vessels were performed, and the tumour was completely resected. The patient received no postoperative therapy. Seven years after the surgery, follow-up chest computed tomography showed a papillary soft tissue tumour in the trachea almost obstructing the airway. Intratracheal tumour resection was performed under rigid bronchoscopy, and the pathological diagnosis of intratracheal recurrence of thymoma was made. The patient received postoperative radiotherapy and has remained alive without disease progression 9 months after the second operation.Keywords: Thymoma, Intratracheal recurrence, Rigid bronchoscopy

INTRODUCTION

Although thymic epithelial tumours are most likely to recur in the pleura (59.9%), lungs (19.8%) and local sites (16.0%) [1], endobronchial recurrence or growth is uncommon [2]. There are few reports of tracheal invasion of thymoma [3]. This report describes the successful treatment of intratracheal recurrence of thymoma by tumour resection under rigid bronchoscopy and radiotherapy.

CASE REPORT

In this case, treatment conformed to the guidelines, and informed consent was obtained from the patient.

A 66-year-old woman with persistent facial oedema was referred to our hospital. Chest computed tomography (CT) showed a homogenously enhancing mass in the anterior mediastinum with superior vena cava stenosis (Fig. 1A). A CT-guided needle biopsy was performed, and a diagnosis was made of type B2 thymoma of the WHO classification, stage III according to the Masaoka staging. After 2 cycles of chemotherapy, extended thymectomy combined with partial resection of the right upper lobe, superior vena cava and right brachiocephalic artery was performed (Fig. 2A). Although the tumour adhered firmly to the surrounding organs, the surgical margin was tumour free according to the intraoperative frozen-section and postoperative pathological diagnoses. Postoperatively, bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve paralyses occurred, but the left-sided paresis improved spontaneously 2 months after surgery. Because of such complications, she received no postoperative therapy.

Enhanced computed tomography images showing (A) 10-cm, enhanced mass in the anterior mediastinum before treatment, (B) a slight bulging shadow on the posterolateral wall, which was not remarkable, in the trachea 5 years postoperatively and (C) increased size and protrusion into the trachea 7 years postoperatively.

(A) Intraoperative findings of initial surgery showing reconstruction of the superior vena cava and right brachiocephalic artery using polytetrafluoroethylene grafts, after tumour resection. (B) Bronchoscopic examination showing a polypoid lesion almost obstructing the trachea, (C) successfully resected under rigid bronchoscopy by the core-out technique.

Postoperative follow-up chest CT showed an enlarged papillary tumour in the trachea with the complaint of dyspnoea (Fig. 1B and C). Flexible bronchoscopy 7 years after the initial surgery showed a polypoid tumour in the trachea causing 75% stenosis. Tracheal resection was not performed due to the history of bilateral recurrent nerve paralyses, severe adhesions involving artificial vessels and her physical condition. Intratracheal tumour resection was then performed under rigid bronchoscopy, without airway stenting (Fig. 2B and C). Pathological examination showed intratracheal recurrence of thymoma. The patient was treated with irradiation (60 Gy) and is now alive 9 months after the second operation without re-growth.

COMMENT

In this case, the patient had intratracheal recurrence 7 years after complete resection of an invasive thymoma. The most frequent recurrence site of thymoma is the pleura (59.9%), followed by the lungs (19.8%) and local sites (16.0%), with bone, liver, pericardial and brain metastases being uncommon [1]. In particular, recurrence or invasion in the trachea or bronchus is extremely rare, and few cases have been reported [2]. In addition, tracheal invasion of thymoma is also rare, and there are only a few reports in which untreated primary lesions infiltrated the trachea [3, 4].

In the present case, the tumour recurred only in the tracheal lumen, which differs from the usual local recurrence. The intratracheal tumour required surgical resection because of impending central airway stenosis. Tracheal resection is rarely performed for thymoma because it is highly invasive [3]. In this case, minimally invasive treatment, intratracheal tumour resection under rigid bronchoscopy and postoperative radiation (60 Gy), was performed. A repeated tumour resection under rigid bronchoscopy is planned if it were to re-grow.

There are few reports of bronchoscopic treatment for thymoma. Kawaguchi et al. [5] resected a tumour with a snare under flexible bronchoscopy, and Tassi et al. [4] resected only the intratracheal lesion under rigid bronchoscopy without re-growth for 15 months after surgery, while the main tumour was left in the mediastinum. Airway stenting was considered unnecessary because of the slow progression of the thymoma.

In conclusion, although intratracheal recurrence of thymoma is uncommon, tumour resection under rigid bronchoscopy and postoperative radiation could be an effective treatment option.
