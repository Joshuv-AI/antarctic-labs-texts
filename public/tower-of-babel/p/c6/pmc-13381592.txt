Development of Ultrasound Microbubbles and Phase Shift Microbubbles in Theranostics
Authors: Emmanuelle J. Meuillet, Evan C. Unger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUltrasound is widely used for diagnostic imaging and therapy. Microbubbles (MB) are effective reflectors of ultrasound and cavitation enhancers, hence their roles in diagnostic and therapeutic ultrasound. MB must be of controlled size, concentration, biocompatible, and stable for biomedical use. Because of low water solubility and high molecular weight, fluorinated gases are generally used to make MB for biomedical applications. Lipids are a viable approach to stabilize MB for use as ultrasound contrast agents. MB of gas can be condensed into nanodroplets or Phase Shift Microbubbles (PSMB). PSMB can remain condensed following vascular administration, but upon application of ultrasound, can reform MB for use as cavitation nuclei. Because of their small size, PSMBs permeate thrombus and tumor microvasculature. Studies have shown effective sonolysis and tumor ablation with PSMB. Lipid‐based systems provide robust opportunities for stabilizing MB and PSMB. Improved lipid coating materials are currently in clinical development for novel ultrasound contrast agents. Using the building blocks of lipids and fluorocarbon gases novel platforms are under development for theranostics—a blending of therapy and diagnostics, referring to an integrated medical approach that combines diagnostic imaging and targeted therapy to personalize patient treatment. For example, a theranostic agent might detect a tumor through imaging while simultaneously delivering a therapeutic drug or radiation to the tumor site. This dual functionality enables precise disease characterization, treatment monitoring, and optimized therapeutic outcomes, improving efficacy and reducing side effects. In addition, MB or nanobubbles can be used for oxygen delivery, and these applications will also be reviewed.

Introduction

A dear high school friend, after entering college, developed a brain tumor. My friend underwent brain surgery, radiation and chemotherapy, this in the 1970s. He survived, but was left with double vision, ataxia and other disabilities. Later in his 50's he developed dementia and died. As a college student, as I saw my friend suffer through his illness, I thought of targeted therapy. Why couldn't we develop technology to destroy the tumor non‐invasively, without the need for cutting open the skull and surgically incising the brain, avoiding damage to normal brain tissue? I had seen the movie, “Fantastic Voyage,” a film in which a submarine and its crew is shrunk to microscopic size, injected into the bloodstream and travels to the brain to repair neurological damage to a scientist's brain. While the film is silly and far‐fetched, in medicine now, we are journeying on a fantastic voyage in our ability to diagnose and treat disease far more non‐invasively than in the past. We have ultra‐sensitive imaging techniques comprising computed tomography, nuclear medicine, optical imaging and ultrasound. Our knowledge of the human genome and molecular underpinnings of disease has unleashed new targeted drugs.

While radiation has been the mainstay of therapy for energy‐mediated treatment of disease, there are now multiple treatment paradigms employing different kinds of energy. Radiofrequency energy can be used to heat tissues and activate nanoparticles. Magnetic energy can be used to guide or attract particles and cause local heating. Optical energy can be used to activate fluorophores for tumor treatment. Ultrasound (US) energy can be used to heat and/or coagulate tissues. Microbubbles (MB) and other acoustic active materials can be used synergistically with US for localized therapy (Rix et al. 2018; Stride and Coussios 2019). Compared to radiation, US has the advantage that it uses non‐ionizing radiation. Compared to optical techniques, US has the advantage of much greater tissue penetration. Much of this review is centered upon the use of MB/acoustically active carriers for use with US for diagnosis (Stride and Coussios 2019) and therapy to realize the fantastic voyage combining nanotechnology with image‐guided therapy. As a starting point, we will review a class of nanoparticles, useful in drug delivery and potentially in image‐guided therapy, liposomes. Liposomes served as a starting point for the development of acoustically active carriers.

Liposomes in Diagnostic Imaging

We start this review with liposomes. Why liposomes? This is because in my early research career, liposomes, or lipid vesicles, were an important starting point on my research journey that ultimately led to lipid‐coated MB. Accordingly, we start with a review of liposomes. Liposomes are spherical vesicles composed of one or more lipid bilayers, typically made from phospholipids, that can encapsulate aqueous solutions (Allen and Cullis 2013; Bangham et al. 1965; Torchilin 2005). These structures, ranging from nanometers to micrometers in size, mimic cell membranes, making them valuable in drug delivery, cosmetics, and research. Their amphiphilic nature allows them to carry hydrophilic substances in their core and hydrophobic molecules within their bilayers, enabling targeted delivery of drugs or nutrients. Liposomes are biocompatible, biodegradable, and can be engineered for specific functions, such as controlled drug release or enhanced stability, but challenges like scalability and cost remain in their widespread application.

Liposomes have been developed as contrast agents in medical imaging due to their biocompatibility, ability to encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic substances, and customizable surface properties. In contrast‐enhanced imaging techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and US, liposomes can be engineered to carry imaging agents like gadolinium (Gd) for MRI or iodine for CT, enhancing the visibility of specific tissues or pathological sites (Mulder et al. 2006; Rabin et al. 2006; Torchilin 2005; Yan et al. 2013). Moreover, their surfaces can be functionalized with targeting ligands, enabling selective accumulation in tumors or inflamed tissues, which improves diagnostic accuracy and reduces systemic toxicity. Their versatility and capacity for targeted delivery make liposomes valuable tools in both diagnostic and theranostic applications. No Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved liposomal MRI contrast agents or radionuclide diagnostics exist yet; however, they have shown promise in clinical trials and preclinical models.

We prepared and tested liposomes entrapping Gd chelates as MRI contrast agents (Unger et al. 1989). We showed that water flux across the liposome bilayer was necessary for contrast enhancement. Water flux was greater across lipid membranes composed of unsaturated lipid systems. Water flux was diminished across saturated membrane systems and systems containing cholesterol. Relaxivity or 1/T1/mmol Gd correlated with the surface area to volume of the liposomes entrapping the Gd (Figure 1 from reference (Tilcock et al. 1990)). In other words, smaller liposomes were more effective as contrast agents. MR imaging in rat liver tumor model showed that Gd‐diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) liposomes significantly improved detection of hepatic metastases (Figure 2 from reference (Unger et al. 1989)).

We performed a pharmacokinetic study of Gd encapsulated in diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) and 1,4,7,10‐tetraazacyclododecane‐1,4,7,10‐tetraacetic acid (DOTA) liposomes (Unger et al. 1991). Highest uptake was in liver and spleen. There was divergence of clearance kinetics of 153Gd and 14C‐14 labeled DTPA but not for 153Gd and 14C‐14‐labeled DOTA. This is consistent with known equilibrium constants for Gd‐DTPA and Gd‐DOTA; that is, Gd is more likely to dissociate from the linear than the macrocyclic chelate. In hepatic liposomal clearance the Gd‐chelate is subjected to slower clearance than the renal route as well as the intracellular milieu in hepatocytes or Kupffer cells designed to detoxify and excrete molecules. Our study on clearance of liposomal Gd chelates suggested that decomplexation of Gd‐DTPA might occur in certain environments. Patients with renal failure have decreased renal clearance of Gd chelate, prolonged retention and increased hepatobiliary clearance (Sieber et al. 2008). Decomplexation of Gd has been shown in patients. The condition of Nephrogenic Sclerosing Fibrosis (NSF) has been shown in patients with Gd retention. The clinical presentation of NSF resembles scleroderma (Cowper et al. 2000; Kuo et al. 2007; Marckmann et al. 2006; Thomsen 2006). Now that this is understood, Gd contrast agents are used which minimize retention of metal ion.

In addition to MRI, liposomes have been tested as contrast agents for x‐ray and computed tomography, optical imaging, and in nuclear medicine.

In 1989, I attended the Contrast Media Research (CMR) meeting in Sydney, Australia to present some of my work on paramagnetic liposomes. There was a presentation on albumin‐coated MBs, then referred to as Albunex, as an ultrasound contrast agent. At the time it did not seem very exciting to me as it seemed to be only a right heart agent and not to give robust opacification of the left ventricle. However, there was a well‐respected Wall Street analyst at the conference who seemed enthusiastic about Albunex. I thought to myself, I can make gas‐filled liposomes, and ever since, this has been the focus of my research, although as I subsequently learned, these were not true gas‐filled liposomes.

Although liposomes have not emerged as clinical contrast agents, one area of robust development, however, has been for lipids in US contrast agents (Unger et al. 1992). After x‐ray, diagnostic US imaging is the most common form of imaging. Diagnostic US applies high‐frequency, pulsed US into the body and processes images from the US signals reflected within the body and received by the transducer. US energy is bounced back by reflective acoustic interfaces. Because MB are highly compressible, low density compared to blood or tissue, MB are highly effective reflectors of US (Stride and Coussios 2019). Depending upon the US imaging regime that is used, US can detect a single MB, making it a strong candidate for molecular imaging (Dayton and Ferrara 2002; Errico et al. 2015; Lindner 2004). These references demonstrated that individual MBs can be localized and tracked to create super‐resolution vascular maps.

Ultrasound and Microbubbles

Ultrasound (US) is used not only for diagnostic imaging but also in therapy (Rix et al. 2018). High‐energy US can be used to heat or coagulate tissue or with cavitation. MBs lower the cavitation threshold by 2–3 orders of magnitude compared to fluids without MB. Cavitation can be used for sonothrombolysis, non‐thermal tumor ablation, sonoporation for drug or gene delivery, or for other applications. MB have extensive applications as theranostics to treat various diseases.

The term ultrasound enhancing agent (UEA) was formerly adopted by the American Society of Echocardiography to describe ultrasound contrast agents. Hence, we will use this term in the remainder of this manuscript. UEA are typically gas‐filled MBs encapsulated by a stabilizing shell made of lipids, proteins, or polymers, designed to enhance the echogenicity of blood and tissues during US imaging (Cosgrove 2006; de Jong et al. 2002; Ferrara et al. 2007; Fritz et al. 1997; Metzger‐Rose et al. 1997; Metzger‐Rose et al. 1996; Stride and Saffari 2003; Unger et al. 2014; Unger et al. 1994). These MB oscillate in response to US waves, producing strong acoustic signals that improve the contrast between blood vessels and surrounding tissues, thereby enabling more precise visualization of vascular structures and perfusion. UEA are especially valuable in cardiology, oncology, and liver imaging, aiding in the detection of tumors, assessment of organ function, and monitoring of therapeutic response. Their safety, real‐time imaging capability, and potential for molecular targeting make them a powerful tool in both diagnostic and interventional US applications.

Fluorocarbon gases, such as perfluoropropane and perfluorobutane, are commonly used as the core component in UEA due to their low solubility in blood and high stability, which prolongs the circulation time of MB in the bloodstream (de Jong et al. 2002; Ferrara et al. 2009; Klibanov 2006; Stride and Saffari 2003). These inert, non‐toxic gases enhance the acoustic response of the MB by providing strong impedance mismatch with surrounding tissues, resulting in increased echogenicity during US imaging. Their chemical stability helps maintain the integrity of the MB under physiological conditions, allowing for clearer, more sustained imaging of vascular and tissue structures. Additionally, the low diffusivity of fluorocarbon gases reduces premature dissolution, making them ideal for targeted imaging and emerging theranostic applications where prolonged contrast is required.

The stability of MB used as UEA largely depends on the composition of their outer shell, which acts as a protective barrier for the gas core and influences their acoustic properties, circulation time, and biocompatibility (Chomas et al. 2000; de Jong et al. 2007; Ferrara et al. 2007; Klibanov 2006; Stride and Saffari 2003). Common coating agents include lipids, proteins, and polymers. Lipid‐based shells, often made from phospholipids, provide flexibility and good biocompatibility, allowing MB to oscillate effectively under US without rupturing. Protein shells, such as those made from albumin, were among the earliest materials used in clinical contrast agents. Polymer‐coated MB, which may include materials like polyethylene glycol (PEG), offer increased durability and reduced immune recognition, enabling longer circulation times and potential for functionalization with targeting ligands (Christiansen et al. 2003; Ferrara et al. 2009; Klibanov et al. 2010; Stride and Saffari 2003). Each coating material presents a trade‐off between stability, responsiveness to US, and ease of functionalization, allowing tailored design of MB for specific diagnostic or therapeutic applications (Schutt et al. 2003).

Ultrasound enhancing agents.

Although I initially thought that I was making gas‐filled liposomes, I later learned from electron microscopy and other studies, that lipid‐coated microbubbles are not coated by a bilayer of lipid, but rather, by a monolayer of lipid. Lipid monolayer systems consist of a single layer of lipid molecules arranged at an interface, typically between air and water or oil and water, with their hydrophilic heads oriented toward the aqueous phase and hydrophobic tails extending inward (Adamson and Gast 1997; Kaganer et al. 1999; Knobler and Desai 1992; McConnell 1991; Möhwald 1990). Applications of lipid monolayer systems include biosensors, drug delivery systems, as a tool for studying cell membrane dynamics and as coating materials for UEA. The structure of lipid monolayers is tunable by varying lipid composition, surface pressure and environmental conditions.

The fluorocarbon gases used in UEA are highly hydrophobic. They are lipophobic as well, but much more hydrophobic than lipophobic. Because of hydrophobicity, the lipids stabilizing the MBs arrange in a monolayer rather than bilayer phase surrounding the MB (Borden and Longo 2002; de Jong et al. 2007; Kwan and Borden 2012). We developed Perflutren (phospholipid‐coated perfluoropropane), and electron microscopy confirmed a monolayer configuration of the coating phospholipid.

UEA stabilized by lipids are most used in clinically approved formulations due to biocompatibility, flexibility, and ability to form stable MB shells. These lipid‐shelled MB typically consist of phospholipids, which self‐assemble into a monolayer around a gas core of sulfur hexafluoride, perfluoropropane or perfluorobutane, creating a stable interface that is highly responsive to ultrasound waves. The fluid nature of lipid shells allows the MB to oscillate efficiently under acoustic pressure, enhancing echogenicity and providing strong contrast in vascular and tissue imaging (Chomas et al. 2001; de Jong et al. 2007; Ferrara et al. 2007; Marmottant et al. 2005; Stride and Saffari 2003). Additionally, lipid‐based MBs have been functionalized with polyethylene glycol (PEG) to prolong circulation time or with targeting ligands for molecular imaging. Their stability in the bloodstream and customizable surface properties make lipid‐shelled MB highly versatile tools for both diagnostic imaging (Schutt et al. 2003) and emerging therapeutic applications such as drug and gene delivery.

Echocardiography with UEA is a non‐invasive imaging technique that enhances the visualization of cardiac structures and blood flow. MB agents improve the echogenicity of blood, allowing clearer delineation of heart chambers, valves, and myocardial perfusion (Mulvagh et al. 2008; Senior et al. 2009). This method is particularly valuable for patients with suboptimal US windows, such as those with obesity or lung disease, where standard echocardiography may yield poor images. The contrast agents are generally safe, with minimal side effects, and do not require ionizing radiation, making them ideal for assessing conditions like heart failure, coronary artery disease, or intracardiac shunts. This technique significantly improves diagnostic accuracy and guides clinical decision‐making in cardiology.

In radiology, UEA are particularly valuable for characterizing focal lesions of the liver or kidney, detecting vascular abnormalities, and monitoring therapeutic interventions, offering a safer alternative to contrast agents used in CT or MRI due to their lack of nephrotoxicity and non‐reliance on ionizing radiation. The transient nature and high sensitivity of UEA make them an essential tool for improving diagnostic precision in US imaging (Schutt et al. 2003).

When we developed Perflutren we referred to it as MRX‐115, ImaRx Pharmaceutical being our company at that time developing Perflutren (Fritz et al. 1997; Metzger‐Rose et al. 1997). The number 115 referred to the 115th lipid mixture that we tested for stabilizing perfluoropropane MB. We tested MB size, concentration, acoustic reflectivity and stability to acoustic impedance of US. MRX‐115 had the best performance. There were three lipids in the formulation, in the ratio of 82 mol% dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC), 10 mol% dipalmitoylphosphatidic acid (DPPA) and 8 mol% dipalmitoylphosphatidylethanolamine‐PEG‐5000 at a total lipid concentration of 0.75 mg/mL. Perflutren has a high MB concentration, typically around 1.2 × 1010 MB per mL.

For clinical enhancement of the blood pool on echocardiography, a typical dose involves injecting or infusing 0.1–1 mL of this MB dispersion, which corresponds to approximately 109 to 1010 MB per dose (Kaufmann et al. 2007; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Main et al. 2007; Unger et al. 2014). The mean diameter of Definity MB ranges from about 1.1 to 3.3 μm, with a commonly cited average of around 1.0–2.7 μm depending on the measurement technique and study (Goertz et al. 2007; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.). The measured size depends upon the technique used for measurement. A mean diameter of 1.0 ± 0.83 μm has been reported when measured with a laser light obscuration and scattering device (Accusizer 780A). An acoustic sizing study at 25 MHz reported a mean diameter of 2.5 ± 0.9 μm. Another study comparing Perflutren to other agents noted a mean diameter of 2.7 ± 2.0 μm. The maximum MB diameter is specified as up to 20 μm, though the vast majority are much smaller to ensure safe passage through capillaries. Perflutren MBs are somewhat polydisperse, having a broad size distribution compared to monodisperse agents produced in controlled laboratory settings. The standard deviation of the size distribution is relatively large, reflecting variability. One study reported a size distribution with a mean of 2.7 μm and a standard deviation of 2.0 μm, indicating some variability compared to monodisperse agents (e.g., 3.7 ± 0.2 μm for lab‐made MB). Another study noted a size distribution of 1.0 ± 0.83 μm, suggesting a slightly tighter distribution but still polydisperse. The mode diameter (most frequent size) for Perflutren MB typically range from 1 to 4 μm in diameter, making them smaller than red blood cells (~7–8 μm), which allows them to flow easily through the microcirculation. A small fraction may exceed this range, but > 98% of MB are specified to be under 10 μm to prevent capillary occlusion.

Perflutren MB are produced via mechanical agitation (e.g., using a VialMix device). After agitation, the Perflutren MBs are to be used within 12 h post agitation (Kaufmann et al. 2007; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Senior et al. 2009; Wei et al. 2008). The shelf‐life of the Perflutren product is 2 years with refrigerated storage ((EMA) et al.; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Senior et al. 2009; Wei et al. 2008).

Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) is a UEA approved by the FDA for specific uses in echocardiography. The approved label claims for Definity, based on the most recent information available, are as follows:

Indication for Use

Definity is approved for use in patients with suboptimal echocardiograms to opacify the left ventricular chamber and to improve the delineation of the left ventricular endocardial border (Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Mulvagh et al. 2008; Porter et al. 2014; Senior et al. 2009). This indication specifically targets enhancing the visualization of the left ventricle in cases where standard echocardiography yields technically difficult or suboptimal images, which can occur in up to 15%–20% of echocardiograms (Kaufmann et al. 2007; Main et al. 2007; Mulvagh et al. 2008; Senior et al. 2009).

Label Updates

In 2017, the FDA removed the contraindication for use in patients with known or suspected cardiac shunts, expanding access to Definity for such patients based on safety data (FDA information et al., n.d.; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.). The label includes warnings about rare but serious cardiopulmonary reactions and potential hypersensitivity reactions, such as anaphylactoid reactions, though these are uncommon (e.g., allergic reactions in 0.009% and anaphylactic reactions in 0.004% of patients) (FDA Drug Safety Communication: Updated information on contrast agents for ultrasound imaging (Definity et al.) 2017; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Main et al. 2014). The safety and efficacy of Definity for exercise or pharmacologic stress testing have not been established, and this remains an off‐label use; published studies, however, suggest clinical benefit. Definity is not approved for myocardial perfusion imaging or other non‐cardiac indications in the United States, but there are published studies that suggest clinical utility (Capdeville et al. 2023; Kaufmann et al. 2007; Senior et al. 2009; Wei et al. 1998).

Off‐label Uses

Off‐label uses (e.g., liver or tumor imaging) have been explored in research and are used off label in regions like Europe and Canada (Burns and Wilson 2006; Dietrich et al. 2012; Dietrich et al. 2020; Quaia 2007). Definity is marketed as Luminity in Europe. Published studies show efficacy for detecting and characterizing tumors of the liver and kidney. Studies also show that US contrast is useful for local ablation procedures to improve visualization of vascularity and ablation margins. Phospholipid‐coated sulfur hexafluoride MBs, marketed as SonoVue in Europe and Lumason in the US, are approved for cardiac and radiology indications (Dietrich et al. 2012; Inc et al., n.d.; Piscaglia and Bolondi 2006; S.p.A. et al. 2002).

Definity is administered intravenously as a bolus or infusion, with a maximum total dose of two vials (4.0 mL). A popular way to administer Definity is to dilute 1.3 mL of activated product with 8.7 mL of normal saline in a syringe total volume then = 10.0 mL. Thereafter, the product is injected in small boluses (e.g., 0.5–2.5 mL) or slow IV pushes to dial in the appropriate level of contrast for optimal imaging (Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Mulvagh et al. 2008; Porter et al. 2014; Wei et al. 2008).

Safety monitoring is recommended for patients with pulmonary hypertension or unstable cardiopulmonary conditions, but the requirement for a 30‐min post‐administration monitoring period was removed in 2008 for most patients (Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; Main et al. 2014). The therapeutic index, safety factor, or ratio of the maximal tolerable dose divided by the diagnostic dose is probably greater for US contrast than for x‐ray or MR contrast agents (Mulvagh et al. 2008; Piscaglia and Bolondi 2006; Quaia 2008; Unger et al. 2014). There is no ionizing radiation with US, and US is cheaper than CT or MR scanning and more widely available. Recently, however, publications have shown unexpectedly high nociceptive serious adverse events in patients with Sickle Cell Disease (mainly seen as back or flank pain) upon administration of Definity (D'Amico et al. 2020; Lantheus Medical Imaging. Definity (perflutren lipid microsphere) [Prescribing Information]. North Billerica, n.d.; U.S. Food and Drug Administration, n.d.). Occasional back pain has been described in other patients administered Definity, but at a much lower rate than in Sickle Cell Disease (SCD). It has been published that the back pain is likely related to renal retention of MB and activation of the inflammatory cascade (D'Amico et al. 2020; Skyba et al. 1998; Unger et al. 2014).

Perflutren MBs contain three lipids, one of which is DPPA. DPPA is a signaling molecule and its hydrolysis product, lyso‐phosphatidic acid (lyso‐PA), is an even more potent signaling molecule triggering inflammation (Brindley and Pilquil 2009; Choi et al. 2010; Moolenaar et al. 2004; Yung et al. 2015). When administered to C57BL/6 mice, there is retention of Perflutren (Manuscript in preparation to be subitted to Ultrasound Med Biol (Meuillet et al. 2026)). The renal retention of Perflutren in this model could be due to the anionic (negative) charge of the Perflutren MBs or the presence of PA or its breakdown product lyso‐PA. Given the side effects and severe adverse events (SAE) caused by Perflutren in SCD patients, we sought to understand the reason for renal retention and to eliminate renal retention and possible related side effects.

We have designed a new neutral MB referred to as MVT‐100 (Manuscript in preparation to be submitted to Ultrasound Med Biol). In MVT‐100, the anionic lipid DPPA is substituted with the neutral lipid, dipalmitoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DPPE). Three lipids are necessary for stabilization of Perflutren. When MB are made with only two of the lipids, excluding DPPA, the resultant MB, count is markedly reduced and the MB are unstable, despite adjusting the overall lipid concentration to be 0.75 mg/mL, the same as in Perflutren. Upon doubling the lipid concentration to 1.5 mg/mL, the MB count is still less than native Perflutren, despite doubling the lipid concentration, and the resultant MB are unstable to dilution.

DPPC is a cylinder‐shaped lipid, and DPPE‐MPEG‐5000, due to the large PEG group bound to the head of the lipid, is a cone‐shaped lipid. To stabilize MB, a third lipid is needed: an inverted cone‐shaped lipid, DPPA (Figure 3) (Arroyo Olarte and Gupta 2014). DPPA and DPPE have the same inverted cone shape, as shown in Figure 3. While DPPA is a signaling molecule, phosphatidylethanolamine is the second most common lipid in the human body after phosphatidylcholine. The relative abundance and distribution of each of the lipids (conical, inverted conical, or cylindric) will determine the shape and curvature of the membranes (Janmey and Kinnunen 2006).

The anionic lipid, DPPA, is the most hydrolytically unstable lipid of the three lipids in the Perflutren formulation and its breakdown products accelerate the hydrolysis of the other three lipids. We have shown that the average percent lipid loss from Perflutren and MVT‐100 after incubation at 40°C for 31 days. Almost 25% of DPPA is lost due to hydrolysis and the loss of DPPE‐PEG‐5000 and DPPC range from 15% to 20%. By comparison, there is less than 5% lipid loss in MVT‐100 which includes DPPE instead of DPPA.

Phosphatidic acid and its hydrolysis product are potent mediators of inflammation. When DPPA hydrolyzes it forms Lyso‐PA and PA. Lyso‐PA is a potent signaling molecule for fibroblast migration, thereby regulating the inflammatory response (Lim et al. 2003; Pradère et al. 2008). We hypothesize that inflammation in the kidney is responsible for the back pain, occasionally seen as a side effect in administration of Definity. A study was performed in wild type mice. Definity and MVT‐100 were tested for renal retention. On delayed imaging of the kidneys, Definity increases signal intensity > 2.5× greater than MVT‐100 (reflecting renal retention). We hope to show in clinical studies that MVT‐100 causes less back pain than Perflutren.

Microbubbles as Targeted Agents for Molecular Imaging

As noted above, US can detect a single MB. MBs have been labeled with antibodies, peptides and other ligands for molecular targeting. Since MB are micron‐sized structures, this may limit US molecular imaging to targets accessible via the vasculature, although in some cases MB may be internalized by cells. We hypothesize that a neutral surface MB, such as MVT‐100 will serve as a better platform for development of molecularly targeted MB than charged, for example, anionic MB.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is the most common type of pancreatic cancer, accounting for about 90% of cases (Kleeff et al. 2016; McGuigan et al. 2018; Rawla et al. 2019). It originates in the ductal cells of the pancreas, which produce digestive enzymes. PDAC is aggressive, often diagnosed late due to vague symptoms, and has a poor prognosis, with a 5‐year survival rate of around 12% (Kleeff et al. 2016; McGuigan et al. 2018; Rawla et al. 2019). Approximately 60,000 new cases of PDAC arise annually in the U.S., with global rates rising. PDAC arises from pancreatic ductal cells, often progressing from precancerous lesions (e.g., pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (American Cancer Society. Cancer Facts and Figures 2024; Kleeff et al. 2016; Rawla et al. 2019; Siegel et al. 2024)). The Symptoms associated with PDAC are usually nonspecific—abdominal pain, weight loss, jaundice (if bile duct obstructed), new‐onset diabetes, or digestive issues. The diagnosis of PDAC is typically via imaging (CT, MRI, or endoscopic US) and biopsy (Conwell et al. 2014; Kleeff et al. 2016; Tempero et al. 2024; Vincent et al. 2011). CA 19‐9 is a blood marker but lacks specificity (Ballehaninna and Chamberlain 2011; Goonetilleke and Siriwardena 2007; Locker et al. 2006; Tempero et al. 2024). Unfortunately, PDAC is usually diagnosed at stage III or IV due to late presentation, limiting surgical options. Surgery is presently the only curative option, but only 15%–20% of patients are candidates for resection (e.g., Whipple procedure). Even with surgery, recurrence is common, however. Median survival is only 6–12 months for patients with metastatic disease and 2–3 years post‐resection for patients treated surgically (Kleeff et al. 2016; McGuigan et al. 2018; Tempero et al. 2024; Vincent et al. 2011). High‐risk individuals (e.g., BRCA2 carriers) may benefit from surveillance, but no universal screening exists. Liquid biopsies may afford early detection but have not yet been developed (Canto et al. 2018; Cohen et al. 2018; Goggins et al. 2020).

Early detection of PDAC while it is still curable with surgery could fundamentally improve outcomes. Thy‐1 (CD‐90) is expressed on PDAC cells and PDAC‐associated tumor microvasculature but not on normal cells in the pancreas and not in pancreatitis (Bam et al. 2020; Grzesiak et al. 2007; Zhu et al. 2014). We coupled a single chain antibody to Thy‐1 to neutral MB and performed imaging in vivo. Thy1 US molecular imaging, MBThy1‐scFv was tested in the transgenic mouse model (C57BL/6J − Pdx1‐Cretg/+; KRasLSL‐G12D/+; Ink4a/Arf−/−) of PDAC and in control mice (C57BL/6J) with l‐arginine–induced pancreatitis for normal pancreas (Bam et al. 2020). Molecular imaging with US showed that we could detect millimeter‐sized tumors in the mice. There was no cross‐reactivity with pancreatitis, indicating that the Thy‐1 targeted agents held potential for detecting early stage PDAC. Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women globally, with approximately 2.3 million new cases diagnosed in 2020 (WHO data). In the U.S., about 1 in 8 women (13%) will develop invasive breast cancer in their lifetime, with an estimated 300,590 new cases in 2023 (American Cancer Society). Incidence increases with age, peaking in women aged 50–70, but younger women and men (though rare, ~1% of cases) can also be affected. Risk factors include genetics (e.g., BRCA1/2 mutations), hormonal factors, lifestyle, and family history (https://www.who.int/news‐room/fact‐sheets/detail/breast‐cancer). Mammography is used as the primary screening tool, detecting abnormalities like masses or calcifications. Sensitivity is ~85%–90% but lower in dense breasts. US is used to differentiates solid vs. cystic lesions, often used in younger women or as a follow‐up to mammography. MRI has high sensitivity (~95%–100%) for high‐risk patients (e.g., BRCA carriers) or staging cancer. Imaging‐guided biopsies (e.g., US or stereotactic) are used to confirm diagnosis. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) typically presents as microcalcifications without a palpable mass, while fibroadenomas are palpable, well‐defined masses. US distinguishes fibroadenoma's solid, benign features from DCIS's ductal or calcific patterns. Biopsy is definitive for diagnosis, as imaging alone cannot always rule out malignancy.

Approximately 40%–50% of women aged 40 and older in the U.S. have dense breasts, classified as heterogeneously dense (category C) or extremely dense (category D) on mammograms, per the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI‐RADS) (D'Orsi et al. 2013; Mandelson et al. 2000; Sprague et al. 2014; U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). National Dense Breast Reporting Requirements. Final Rule, n.d.). This prevalence is higher in younger women (50%–60% for ages 40–44) and leaner women, and lower in older (20%–30% for ages 70–74) or obese women. Dense breast tissue reduces mammography sensitivity, as both dense tissue and cancer appear white on mammography, masking abnormalities: Sensitivity of mammography drops to ~30%–50% in extremely dense breasts (category D) compared to ~85%–90% in fatty breasts (category A). Cancers are missed in ~40% of dense breast cases. Dense breasts increase breast cancer risk 4–6 times compared to fatty breasts, with interval cancers (detected between screenings) up to 17.8 times more likely. Dense breast tissue obscures microcalcifications and masses, complicating detection of DCIS or invasive cancers. In the setting of dense breasts, it is difficult to distinguish between fibroadenoma, a benign condition, and DCIS (Berg et al. 2004; Boyd et al. 2007; Carney et al. 2003; Pisano et al. 2005).

MB targeted to breast cancer may enable detection of breast cancer in the US in patients with dense breasts and differentiation of fibroadenoma from DCIS. B7‐H3 (CD‐276), a transmembrane glycoprotein, is expressed in DCIS but not in fibroadenoma (Cimino‐Mathews et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2020; Loos et al. 2010; Zang et al. 2007). We coupled an affibody to B7‐H3 to MB. The images below show selective enhancement of breast cancer in a murine breast cancer model (Figure 5). A UEA targeted to B7‐H3 could potentially detect early‐stage breast cancer in patients with dense breasts. Further preclinical and clinical development of such an agent may be warranted.

Therapeutic Potential of Microbubbles

Microbubble‐enhanced cavitation offers promising therapeutic applications in medicine, leveraging the controlled oscillation and collapse of MB under US to achieve targeted effects (Ferrara et al. 2009; Kooiman et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2009; Stride and Coussios 2010). Some of the potential applications of MBs with US in therapy are depicted in Figure 7. For most applications, MB are administered intravenously, and US is applied to the target region. However, MB can be administered interstitially or intra‐arterially to target specific tissues. Mechanical effects of MB with US can be obtained with stable or inertial cavitation, microstreaming and the radiation force of US. Figure 8 depicts stable (a) and inertial cavitation (b). During stable cavitation, the oscillation is moderate and reversible—the bubble expands and contracts rhythmically without collapsing. The radius oscillation typically ranges from: Rt=R01±ΔR where R0 is the equilibrium radius and ΔR is the fractional change. The fractional change in MB radius depends upon the acoustic pressure. At low acoustic pressures (≈0.1–0.3 MPa (MPa)): ΔRR0≈10%–30%. At moderate acoustic pressures (≈0.3–0.6 MPa): ΔRR0≈50%–100%. So a 2 μm MB might oscillate between roughly 1 μm and 3–4 μm in diameter (Kooiman et al. 2020; Postema and Gilja 2011).

Ttherapeutic applications of microbubbles and ultrasound.

In stable cavitation, MB oscillates back and forth, increasing in diameter during the rarefaction phase of insonation and decreasing in diameter during the positive phase. In inertial cavitation, the bubble expands and collapses catastrophically.

In inertial cavitation, the expansion can be > 3 × −10× the original radius, followed by violent collapse. In contrast, stable cavitation remains within about 1.5 × −2× the equilibrium size (Kooiman et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2009; Stride and Coussios 2010). Lower frequencies (e.g., 1 MHz) favor larger excursions for a given pressure (Kooiman et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2009; Stride and Coussios 2010). Stiffer shells coating the MB reduce oscillation amplitude. Higher static pressures, for example, in the arterial circulation, or within a tissue, reduce oscillation amplitude (de Jong et al. 2007; Qin et al. 2009; Stride and Coussios 2010). As MBs undergo stable cavitation, diffusion of gas and loss of integrity of the bubble shell leads to bubble dissolution (Borden et al. 2005; Ferrara et al. 2007; Klibanov 2006; Qin et al. 2009; Stride and Saffari 2003). In inertial cavitation the MB are destroyed yielding daughter MB fragments that then dissolve.

The biological effects of US/MB interactions can be used to enhance perfusion in tissues, dissolve clots and enhance vascular and cell membrane permeability (Ferrara et al. 2007; Hitchcock and Holland 2010; Kooiman et al. 2020; Skyba et al. 1998; Unger et al. 2014). In the heart this can be used to dissolve thrombi, stimulate local nitric oxide release and increase blood flow (Miller and Song 2002; Porter and Xie 2001; Skyba et al. 1998; Xie, Slikkerveer, et al. 2011; Xie et al. 2009). In the brain MB/US stable cavitation can be used to transiently open the blood brain barrier (BBB) (Choi et al. 2007; Hynynen et al. 2001; Hynynen et al. 2006; Sheikov et al. 2008). In cancer treatment, sonoporation can be used to increase permeability and drug delivery to tumor (Escoffre and Bouakaz 2016; Kooiman et al. 2020; Liang et al. 2010; Meijering et al. 2009; Ting et al. 2012; Treat et al. 2012). High intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) can use MB to augment tumor ablation (Hu et al. 2007; Xu, Fowlkes, Ludomirsky, et al. 2005). In regenerative medicine, MB can be used with US in gene and cell therapy to treat a variety of organs including muscle (Fujii et al. 2009; Shohet et al. 2000; Suzuki et al. 2011; Taniyama et al. 2002). In drug delivery, MB can be co‐administered with drugs wherein sonoporation increases tissue permeability to drugs. MBs can also be loaded with therapeutic agents and guided to specific tissues, where US‐induced cavitation disrupts cell membranes (sonoporation), enhancing drug uptake in conditions like cancer or cardiovascular disease. MB inertial cavitation can ablate tissue in cancer treatment. Both stable and inertial cavitation have been used to break down blood clots. Stable cavitation increases permeation of lytic drug into clot. Inertial cavitation can break down clots directly. Bioeffects may be greater with inertial than stable cavitation and in either case, cavitation should be monitored during clinical development. Stable cavitation aids in hyperthermia for cancer therapy by generating localized heat, whereas inertial ablation may be used for tissue ablation (Hynynen 2008) (Coussios et al. 2007; Kennedy 2005; Miller et al. 2012; Leighton 1994; Xu, Fowlkes, Rothman, et al. 2005). These applications benefit from the non‐invasive nature of US and the biocompatibility of MB, though precise control of acoustic parameters to ensure safety and efficacy (Fujii et al. 2009; Shohet et al. 2000; Suzuki et al. 2011; Taniyama et al. 2002).

Drug Delivery With Microbubbles

Both stable and inertial cavitation have been explored for drug delivery with US and MB (Ho et al. 2021; Unger et al. 2014). As discussed above, cavitation in the context of UEA involves the dynamic behavior of MB under acoustic pressure waves. When exposed to US, gas‐filled MB oscillate, expand, and contract due to the alternating high‐ and low‐pressure phases of the sound wave. At low acoustic pressures, MBs exhibit stable cavitation, oscillating linearly or nonlinearly, which enhances US imaging by scattering sound waves. At higher pressures, inertial cavitation can occur, where MB rapidly collapse, generating localized shock waves, microjets, and heat. This phenomenon is critical in applications like targeted drug delivery and sonoporation but must be carefully controlled to avoid tissue damage. The physics of cavitation depends on factors like bubble size, US frequency, and medium viscosity, making it a key mechanism in optimizing MB performance in medical imaging and therapy (Church 1995; Coussios and Roy 2008; Marmottant et al. 2005; Qin and Ferrara 2007; Leighton 1994).

How can drug delivery be accomplished with MB? MB can be co‐administered with drugs and sonoporation can be exploited to increase local vascular and cellular permeability (Ingram et al. 2020; Suzuki and Klibanov 2016; Unger, Hersh, Vannan, Matsunaga, et al. 2001). MB are being explored in clinical trials to open the Blood Brain Barrier with US (Chen et al. 2022; Chien et al. 2022). Applying US to the brain without MB requires high intensity US, which may heat and/or damage the brain. However, administering MB with the appropriate parameters of US, employing stable cavitation of MB, enables the BBB to be opened, reversibly without damaging the brain. This process is being used to deliver therapeutics to the brain such as antibodies for Alzheimer's disease and chemotherapeutics to treat cancer (de Koning et al. 2025; Sharma et al. 2022).

Highly active therapeutics, such as some chemotherapeutics and gene‐based medicines (DNA, RNA and si‐RNA) can potentially be incorporated into MB. We suspended paclitaxel in soybean oil and added it to an aqueous suspension of phospholipids in vials. The headspace of the vials was replaced with perfluorobutane gas; the vials were sealed, and they were agitated at 4200 rpm on a shaking device. The resulting lipospheres containing paclitaxel were studied for concentration, size, acute toxicity in mice, and acoustic activity and drug release with US (Unger, McCreery, Sweitzer, Vielhauer, et al. 1998; Unger, McCreery, Sweitzer, Shen, and Wu 1998). Lipospheres containing Sudan black dye were produced to demonstrate the acoustically active liposphere (AAL)‐ultrasound release concept. AALs containing paclitaxel had a mean particle count of approximately 1 × 109 particles per mL and a mean size of 2.9 μm. Acute toxicity studies in mice showed a 10‐fold reduction in toxicity for paclitaxel in AALs compared with free paclitaxel. The AAL reflected US as a contrast agent. Increasing amounts of US energy selectively ruptured the AALs and released the paclitaxel (Figure 9) (Unger, McCreery, Sweitzer, Caldwell, and Wu 1998).

Sonothrombolysis

Vascular thrombosis is the formation of blood clot (thrombus) within blood vessels that obstruct blood flow and can lead to serious complications. It can occur in arteries or veins, often triggered by factors like vessel injury, stagnant blood flow, or hypercoagulability (increased clotting tendency) (Esmon 2009; Mackman 2012; Wolberg et al. 2012). Arterial thrombosis may cause heart attacks or strokes (Libby et al. 2019) (Jackson 2011; Mackman 2008), while venous thrombosis, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), can lead to pulmonary embolism if the clot dislodges. Risk factors for thrombosis include smoking, obesity, surgery, or genetic predisposition. Treatment typically involves anticoagulants, thrombolytics, or, in severe cases, surgical intervention to restore blood flow. Globally, there are about 18 million deaths each year due to heart attack and 12 million deaths due to stroke (“Global burden of 288 causes of death and life expectancy decomposition in 204 countries and territories and 811 subnational locations, 1990–2021: a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021” 2024; https://www.who.int/news‐room/fact‐sheets/detail/cardiovascular‐diseases‐(cvds)) (Mensah et al. 2023). Pulmonary emboli (PE) and DVT, or venous thromboembolism are responsible for about 1 in 4 deaths worldwide (Raskob et al. 2014; Wendelboe and Raskob 2016). How can US and MB be used to treat these conditions?

US MB sonothrombolysis is a minimally invasive technique that enhances the breakdown of blood clots by combining US waves with contrast MB (Culp et al. 2004; Hitchcock and Holland 2010; Xie et al. 2013). Both stable and inertial cavitation can be used in sonothrombolysis. Stable cavitation generates localized mechanical forces such as microstreaming that will improve permeation of lytic drugs such as tPA into the clot to hasten thrombolysis (Datta et al. 2006; Hitchcock and Holland 2010; Xie et al. 2009). Inertial cavitation may fragment the clot directly (Culp et al. 2004; Datta et al. 2006; Everbach and Francis 2000). Bioeffects from inertial cavitation may be greater than from stable cavitation, so it must be monitored closely. Sonothrombolysis holds significant promise for treating conditions like deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, ischemic stroke, and microvascular obstruction (MVO) associated with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), offering faster recanalization and reduced systemic side effects compared to conventional thrombolytic therapy. Ongoing research continues to optimize parameters such as US frequency, bubble formulation, and drug co‐delivery to maximize.

We did a number of studies, spanning over a period of many years, in an attempt to develop MB enhanced sonothrombolysis (Culp et al. 2012; Mohammed et al. 2024; Molina et al. 2009; Porter et al. 2010; Roos et al. 2014; Schumann et al. 2002; Tsutsui et al. 2006; Unger et al. 2000; Unger 2006; Unger, McCreery, Sweitzer, Vielhauer, et al. 1998; Wu et al. 1998; Xie et al. 2013; Xie, Everbach, et al. 2011; Xie et al. 2005). An example of MB enhanced sonothrombolysis is an experimental study performed in dogs with thrombosed arteriovenous grafts (Xie et al. 2005). In these studies, thrombi were created in the grafts and sonothrombolysis was performed with either low (188 kPa) or high intensity (958 kPa) US applied to the skin overlying the graft during infusion of the MBs. US creates acoustic streaming which can be used to improve permeation of lytic drug into clot to hasten clot lysis. The EKOS US catheter has been developed to treat DVT and PE with US and lytic drug, for example, tPA. The EKOS catheter is advanced into the thrombus and US energy is applied to the thrombus while tPA is infused into the thrombus. On its own, the catheter US has no or little effect on clot dissolution but increases permeation of tPA into thrombus and increases the rate of clot lysis compared to infusion of lytic drug without US. Lytic drug is generally necessary for the EKOS catheter to be effective. Administration of MB has been tested with infusion through the EKOS catheter with administration of the lytic drug urokinase in a porcine model of peripheral arterial occlusion. MB administration increased the rate of clot lysis (Doelare et al. 2024).

Sonothrombolysis treatment of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) has been studied in animals and humans (Culp et al. 2004; Xie et al. 2009). This technique uses US combined with MB contrast agents to enhance thrombolysis and improve coronary blood flow in patients with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (Mathias Jr. et al. 2019). The mechanism of sonothrombolysis comprises application of high mechanical index US impulses, combined with intravenous MB infusions to induce inertial cavitation, which mechanically disrupts thrombi in coronary arteries and microvasculature. This can restore epicardial and microvascular flow, even when epicardial recanalization is not fully achieved. The process involves MB oscillations that create shear stress and microjets, facilitating clot dissolution without necessarily requiring prolonged US exposure.

Preclinical studies in pigs (normal and hyperlipidemic) demonstrated that HMI US with MB improved peri‐infarct myocardial blood flow and reduced infarct size, even without epicardial recanalization. In hyperlipidemic pigs, epicardial recanalization reached 100% with US + MB, compared to 50% without US. Long‐pulse‐duration US (20 μs) was more effective for epicardial recanalization than short‐pulse (5 μs), but both improved microvascular flow (Xie et al. 2013). In 2016 a study was reported in the Journal of American College of Cardiology, A randomized trial (NCT02410330) conducted from May 2014 to September 2015 involved 30 STEMI patients (Mathias et al. 2016). Twenty received high mechanical index US with MB infusion before and after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), while 10 received low mechanical index imaging for perfusion assessment. The HMI group showed improved microvascular flow and reduced microvascular obstruction, with no significant safety concerns. In a larger study of 50 anterior STEMI patients (Mathias et al. 2019), post‐PCI sonothrombolysis reduced microvascular obstruction acutely but did not significantly reduce infarct size at 48 h. However, at 6–8 weeks, the sonothrombolysis group showed improved left ventricular ejection fraction (vs. control), suggesting better long‐term systolic function (Mathias Jr. et al. 2019). However, a recent study has shown that sonothrombolysis had no benefit in patients with persistent ST elevation post angioplasty (El Kadi et al. 2024). It may be that clot in such cases is organized and resistant to MB sonothrombolysis.

The Transcranial Ultrasound in Clinical SONothrombolysis (TUCSON) trial was a phase I‐II, randomized, placebo‐controlled, dose‐escalation study evaluating the safety and efficacy of sonothrombolysis using perflutren MB (MRX‐801) and transcranial Doppler (TCD) US as an adjunct to intravenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) in patients with acute ischemic stroke and proximal intracranial occlusions (Molina et al. 2009). Thirty‐five patients with acute ischemic stroke were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive either MB infusion (Cohort 1: 1.4 mL; Cohort 2: 2.8 mL) with continuous 2‐MHz TCD insonation with tPA with brief TCD assessments (control group). The primary endpoint was symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) within 36 h after tPA administration. Secondary endpoints included recanalization rates and functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score at 3 months). In Cohort 1 (1.4 mL MB) no sICH was observed in the 12 patients or the 12 controls, indicating that the lower dose was safe when combined with tPA and US. In Cohort 2 (2.8 mL MB) 3 of 11 patients (27%, 2 fatal) experienced sICH, a statistically significant increase compared to controls (p = 0.028). Cases of sICH in the TUCSON Trial are shown in Figure 14. This raised safety concerns, leading to the trial's premature closure. While the trial suggested that the higher dose (2.8 mL) may increase the risk of intracranial hemorrhage, hypertension, time post stroke and other factors may have increased the rate of sICH in the cohort receiving 2.8 mL of MB. Patients in both cohorts treated with microspheres and TCD showed a trend toward higher recanalization rates compared to controls. The TUCSON trial noted that recanalization and clinical recovery rates tended to be higher in the treatment groups. An exploratory analysis suggested that higher estimated in vivo acoustic peak rarefaction pressure was associated with better recanalization and functional independence (mRS 0–1 at 3 months) in 20 patients with eligible CT scans (Molina et al. 2009).

sICH is a complication associated with administration of tPA to treat acute ischemic stroke. In the TUCSON trial, the cluster of sICH in the second cohort suggested that sonothrombolysis with MB increased the risk of bleeding. Dr. Culp's group at the University of Arkansas performed sonothrombolysis experiments in rabbit stroke models and showed that MB sonothrombolysis with tPA did not increase but instead decreased the rate of hemorrhage in this model (Brown et al. 2011). Furthermore, research conducted by Culp's group after the TUCSON Trial showed that MB sonothrombolysis in acute ischemic stroke could be performed without lytic drug. Because of time‐window post stroke and contraindications to lytic drug therapy, most stroke patients are not candidates for tPA administration. MB sonothrombolysis may afford a treatment option for stroke patients who are not candidates for lytic drug therapy. Further research appears to be warranted (Culp et al. 2003).

Opening Blood Brain Barrier With MB and Ultrasound

US combined with MB is a non‐invasive technique to temporarily open the blood–brain barrier (BBB), enabling targeted drug delivery to the brain (Hynynen et al. 2001). MB, injected intravenously, oscillates or collapses under low‐intensity focused US, creating mechanical stress that transiently disrupts tight junctions in the BBB. This allows drugs, genes, or therapeutic agents to pass into the central nervous system, which is typically restricted by the BBB. The method is promising for treating neurological disorders like Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, or brain tumors, offering precise targeting and minimal systemic exposure. Safety depends on optimizing US parameters to avoid tissue damage, with clinical trials showing reversible BBB opening and good tolerability (https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/industry‐reports/high‐intensity‐focused‐ultrasound‐market‐100723; Hynynen 2008; Kennedy 2005; Lipsman et al. 2018).

Phase Shift Microbubbles

MB are micron‐sized structures, wherein the gas of the MB is stabilized by surfactant or other wall‐forming material. In phase shift microbubbles (PSMB) the gas is condensed within the structure. Figure 15 shows electron micrographs of MVT‐100 MB (Microvascular Therapeutics Inc.) and PSMB derived from MVT‐100 MB. As shown in the electron micrographs, PSMB are < 1/10th the diameter of parent MB. PSMB are sometimes referred to as nanobubbles and nanodroplets. In classic nanobubbles, these are sub‐micron sized MB wherein the gas entrapped is within the gaseous state. In nanodroplets, the interior gas may be condensed into liquid, as in PSMB, but the liquid entrapped therein may have such a high boiling point that conversion back to gaseous state is generally not practical. PSMB generally are prepared from the gases comprising perfluoropropane (boiling point −34°C), perfluorobutane (boiling point −1.7°C), and perfluoropentane (boiling point 29°C). Preparation of PSMB is generally accomplished by decreasing the temperature of the MB suspension and application of pressure to achieve condensation.

Electron micrographs of MVT‐100 Microbubbles (left) and PSMB prepared from MVT‐100 (right). The PSMB are < 1/10th the diameter of the parent MB.

The mechanical index or US power necessary to convert PSMB back to MB occurs via acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV). ADV depends upon the type of gas entrapped therein, the size of the PSMB, the frequency of the US, and acoustic power. The Mechanical Index (MI) is described by the equation, MI = Pr/√f, where Pr is the peak rarefactional pressure (MPa) and f is the US frequency (MHz). MI quantifies the likelihood of cavitation and is critical for ADV. A higher MI is needed to convert PSMB of perfluoropentane > perfluorobutane > perfluoropropane back into the parent MB (Aliabouzar et al. 2019). After conversion of PSMB back into MB, these can be used for US imaging and stable or inertial cavitation. Because of the small size, PSMBs offer advantages for sonothrombolysis, drug, and gene delivery.

Phase Shift Microbubbles and Their Applications

PSMB are emerging as powerful theranostic agents that integrate therapeutic and diagnostic functions within a single platform. Comprising a volatile perfluorocarbon core encapsulated by a lipid or polymer shell, PSMBs exist initially as nanoscale liquid droplets. Upon exposure to external energy sources such as US, they undergo a liquid‐to‐gas phase transition, expanding into MB that enhance imaging contrast and facilitate site‐specific therapeutic delivery. This unique phase‐change capability enables deep tissue penetration in their nano‐form, followed by localized activation for enhanced US imaging and targeted drug release. As a result, PSMBs hold significant promise for non‐invasive, image‐guided therapies in oncology, cardiovascular diseases, and other pathologies, making them a versatile tool in precision medicine.

While encouraging results have been obtained with MB in sonothrombolysis, there are limitations. MB are micron‐sized structures, for example, 2–3 μm in diameter. While clots are porous structures, the clot permeability decreases as clots age. MB sonothrombolysis has not been effective in hard clots. In a clinical study of sonothrombolysis in acute MI, the treatment was ineffective in those patients in whom ST elevation persisted post‐sonothrombolysis. It is hypothesized that these patients had hard clots in the regions of microvascular obstruction. If nanoscopic acoustically responsive materials could be engineered, this might improve clot penetration and sonothrombolysis. PSMBs afford nanoscopic potential. We prepared PSMB from MVT‐100 MB and tested them in models of vascular thrombosis. Figure 16 depicts permeation of vascular thrombus by PSMB (from (Unger et al. 2014)). Application of US activates PSMB back into MB and cavitation of MB achieves sonothrombolysis.

PSMB in Oncology

MB are emerging as powerful agents in tumor ablation strategies, particularly when combined with focused US to enhance the precision and efficacy of cancer treatment. When MB are exposed to HIFU, they undergo violent oscillations or collapse, a phenomenon known as inertial cavitation, which generates localized heat and mechanical forces capable of disrupting tumor cells and vasculature. This enhances the thermal and mechanical effects of ablation, allowing for more efficient tumor destruction with lower energy input and reduced damage to surrounding healthy tissue. Additionally, MB can be engineered to carry therapeutic agents or targeting ligands, enabling site‐specific drug delivery that can be triggered by US. This dual role of MB in both enhancing ablation and delivering therapy positions them as a promising tool in image‐guided, non‐invasive cancer treatments.

Because of their much smaller diameter, PSMBs accumulate much more effectively in tumor microvasculature. Studies were performed in the F98 rat glioma model with HIFU (Bryant et al. 2008). Figure 21 shows MR images of rats with gliomas pre‐ and post‐HIFU treatment. MB only produced ablation around the edges of the tumor. Non‐thermal ablation with PSMB produced homogeneous ablation in the tumor vasculature (Odeen and Jensen 2025).

MR images of rat gliomas left panels T2W MR images. Right sided images, T2*W MR images post HIFU treatment. MB only produces ablation around edges of tumor, whereas PSMB afford ablation throughout substance of tumor. From Odeen and Jensen (2025).

In summary, preclinical studies of PSMB in oncology indicate that these nano‐sized particles afford much more effective tumor ablation than MB. The mechanism of action that we have employed is non‐thermal using cavitation. This has potential as stand‐alone therapy and as combination therapy with chemo, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy. Work is underway to take PSMB FUS into clinical trials.

PSMB in Neurology

Because of small size, PSMB might more easily cross the blood brain barrier (BBB) for therapy or to deliver drugs, genes, or other agents to the CNS. We prepared PSMB with bioconjugates targeted to tau and amyloid. In vitro, we showed that we could dissolve tau aggregates with tau targeted PSMB and US. In vivo experiments were performed in Alzheimer's mice. PSMB did not cross the BBB initially. However, after administering MB and applying US to the mice brains, we showed that we could open the BBB by fluorescence and Gd contrast enhancement. After BBB opening, we showed that fluorescent labeled PSMB entered the brain tissue. A second sonication was performed to solubilize amyloid plaques. Further work is needed to explore the potential of this technology. Figure 22 depicts Tau targeted PSMB and the in vivo experimental study that is ongoing. Microbubbles/Nanobubbles for Oxygen Delivery

In the foregoing, all our discussion on MB and PSMB involved the use of US. In this section, we will discuss MB or nanobubbles as oxygen carriers without US. Tissue hypoxia is a central pathophysiologic mechanism underlying many of the leading causes of death worldwide, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, cancer, sepsis, trauma, ARDS, and chronic lung disease (Semenza 2012). Gases can carry 1000× as much oxygen as liquids (West 2016). Dodecafluoropentane has a boiling point of 29°C, less than physiologic. A company, Sonus, previously tried to develop dodecafluoropentane emulsion (DDFPe) as a UEA, referred to as EchoGen at that time (Fontanilla Echeveste et al. 2024). Because of surface tension, DDFPe remained mainly condensed, rather than forming MB, necessary for US contrast. Sonus converted DDFPe to MB by employing negative pressure in the syringe, hypobaric activation, prior to injection, to create MB (Forsberg et al. 1998). When it came time to file the New Drug Application (NDA), the FDA felt that hypobaric activation was a terminal, uncontrolled manufacturing step and did not approve the contrast agent.

We discovered that DDFPe had superior properties for oxygen delivery and obtained ownership of DDFPe and the preclinical and clinical regulatory documents related thereto. Low doses of DDFPe reverse hypoxia and improve outcomes in preclinical models of stroke, myocardial infarction, traumatic brain injury, respiratory distress, sickle cell disease, and cancer (Arthur et al. 2017; Brown et al. 2014; Culp et al. 2015; Graham et al. 2019; Johnson et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2019; Mosier et al. 2023; Murphy et al. 2025; Strom et al. 2014). Figure 23 shows that DDFPe carries far more oxygen than two other high boiling point fluorocarbons (prepared at the same concentrations as DDFPe) that were previously tested as oxygen therapeutics (Johnson et al. 2009). Furthermore, DDFPe carries proportionally more oxygen at physiologic temperatures, above the boiling point of DDFP.

Most solid tumors have roughly 1/10th the oxygen concentration of the parent tissue (Höckel and Vaupel 2001). A major mechanism of radiotherapy is creation of singlet oxygen radicals from oxygen within water in tumor tissue (Azzam et al. 2012; Graham and Unger 2018). If a tumor is hypoxic, and most are, it requires a three‐fold higher dose of radiation to kill the tumor cells than in the setting of normoxia (Gray et al. 1953). Because of toxicity caused by radiation to normal tissues, it is generally not possible to increase the dose of radiation to compensate. Most chemotherapeutic drugs work better in the setting of normoxia and tumor hypoxia is a potent barrier to immunotherapy as well. NuvOx Pharma completed a Phase IIa trial in primary glioblastoma (GBM) in association with chemoradiation. Patients received a dose of DDFPe IV prior to each of 30 radiation treatments over a 6‐week period (Lickliter et al. 2023). TOLD MRI (T1W Oxygen Level Dependent) MRI confirmed reversal of tumor hypoxia and patients had a 5‐month increase in overall survival (OS) and a 40% increase in Progression Free Survival (PFS) (Lickliter et al. 2023). Currently the average OS of GBM patients treated with surgery and chemoradiation is only about 14 months, so the 5‐month increase is potentially significant (Stupp et al. 2005).

NuvOx Pharma Inc. has completed enrollment in a Phase IIb trial in GBM. In this study, 93 subjects were randomized 2:1 (study drug versus placebo) in association with standard of care chemoradiation (https://clinicaltrials.gov/search?cond=Glioblastoma%20Multiforme&term=nuvox&intr=ddfpe). The primary endpoint is PFS and the secondary endpoint is OS. This is the first time that an oxygen therapeutic has been tested in a randomized clinical trial.

In ischemic stroke, blockage of blood vessels leads to ischemia and neuronal cell death. In stroke, there is a penumbra, a region where neurons are still alive, generally shifting to anaerobic glycolysis. Neurons in the penumbra region will gradually die but can be saved if reperfusion is obtained quickly enough. Reperfusion, with a lytic drug (e.g., tPA) or mechanical thrombectomy (in large vessel occlusion stroke), however takes time, time during which neurons continue to die. We completed a randomized, placebo‐controlled dose escalation trial in acute ischemic stroke, in which all patients were treated with standard‐of‐care reperfusion therapy as clinically indicated. Patients were administered three doses of DDFPe, 90‐min apart (the terminal half‐life of DDFPe = 90‐min, clearing via exhalation). Patients treated with three doses of 0.17 mL, 2% w/vol DDFPe, had significant improvement in the functional endpoint of the modified Rankin Scale at 30 and 90 days (see Figures 25 and 26).

Regulatory Considerations

Contrast agents are currently regulated as drugs by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), but this bears some discussion. The Genus Medical Technologies LLC versus FDA case, decided by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in April 2021, centered on the FDA's classification of Genus's barium sulfate contrast agent, used for imaging the gastrointestinal tract, as a drug rather than a device. The FDA historically regulated most contrast agents as drugs, even those meeting the device definition, citing its discretion under the Food Drug and Cosmetic Act. Genus challenged this, arguing that its barium product met the statutory definition of a device and should be regulated as such. The filing fee for a Class I medical device via 510(k) is about $26 K in 2025 versus $3,242,026 for a drug requiring clinical data and $1,621,013 without clinical data (https://www.fda.gov/industry/fda‐user‐fee‐programs/prescription‐drug‐user‐fee‐rates‐fiscal‐year‐2025).

The Genus decision clarified that the FDA must regulate contrast agents meeting the device definition (no chemical action or metabolization, e.g., barium sulfate) as devices, not drugs, limiting the agency's discretion. This challenged the FDA's long‐standing practice of classifying all contrast agents as drugs, potentially reducing regulatory burdens for manufacturers. However, Congress's 2022 Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022 (FDORA) legislation reversed this for contrast agents, mandating their regulation as drugs.

For a new contrast agent to enter human clinical testing it must generally undergo acute and multi‐dose pharmacology/toxicology in two species and the studies must be performed under Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) conditions. For human studies the agent must be manufactured under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions. Generally, an Investigational New Drug (IND) is submitted to the FDA prior to conducting human testing. The IND includes information from pre‐clinical pharm/tox studies, CMC data and the proposed clinical trial protocol. CMC stands for Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls. It refers to the section of regulatory submissions that details the composition, manufacturing process, quality control, and stability of the contrast agent. CMC ensures that the product is consistently produced to meet safety, efficacy, and quality standards for use in clinical trials.

Generally, the contrast agent is tested in Phase I clinical trials in healthy volunteers for a first in man study. Then after demonstrating safety and determination of dose, the agent is tested in a larger number of subjects in Phase II studies and thereafter in one or more Phase III studies. When we developed Perflutren (now marketed as Definity), we completed Phase III trials in echocardiography and in radiology US imaging of the liver and kidney. The FDA approved the label claims for echocardiography but rejected the claims for radiology US. We showed unequivocally that we could increase detection of and characterization of kidney and liver pathology, but the FDA said that we did not show that we changed outcomes in treatment of kidney and liver pathology and hence denied approval for these indications.

Phase 0 studies, or micro‐dosing studies, might be applicable to contrast agents but generally are more pertinent to early studies of radionuclide agents. To qualify for a Phase 0 study, the agent must be administered at less than 1/100th of the maximum tolerated dose from animal studies and not more than a dose of 100 micrograms. To enter Phase 0, limited pharm/tox studies are required, perhaps only acute, single dose in one species. The drug product must still be manufactured GMP, and the IND package is more limited than for a standard IND application.

In the context of clinical trials and medical regulation, a medical device is defined by regulatory agencies like the FDA or the European Medicines Agency (EMA) as an instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or other similar or related article, including a component part or accessory, which is intended for use in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease in humans or animals, or intended to affect the structure or any function of the body of humans or animals, and does not achieve its primary intended purposes through chemical action within or on the body (unlike a drug) or by being metabolized. As noted above, contrast agents might meet this definition of device, but under FDORA legislation are determined to be drugs. What about MB or phase shift MB being used for therapy—not for imaging? Under certain circumstances, MB or phase shift MB may be regulated as devices rather than drugs. Regulation as a device affords lower fees for approval and potential for shorter and less expensive clinical trials. Yet more upfront manufacturing and biocompatibility studies are required prior to testing in humans as part of the Investigational Device Exemption (IDE).

It allows the applicant to rely partially on existing data (such as published literature or FDA's findings for a previous drug), reducing time and cost compared to a full 505(b)(1) NDA (https://www.fda.gov/drugs/cder‐small‐business‐industry‐assistance‐sbia/small‐business‐assistance‐frequently‐asked‐questions‐regulatory‐process‐over‐counter‐otc‐drugs). Generics are approved under the Abbreviated New Drug Application (ANDA) process, authorized by Section 505(j) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and will be much less expensive to develop than new drugs. Currently there are no generic UEAs in the US. Verbal communication indicates that several companies in China are currently developing generic versions of Perflutren.

Arguably the most exciting developments with UEA are in the field of theranostics. In theranostics, UEAs will be developed not only for imaging but also for therapeutic indications. While it is expensive to develop a diagnostic contrast agent, as discussed above, it is generally even more expensive to develop a therapeutic agent. While the total cost for developing a contrast agent may be from $100 to $200 M, the cost for developing a therapeutic may range from $300 M to $2.6B (avg~$1.3B) (DiMasi et al. 2016).

Therapeutic drugs are priced much more highly than diagnostic imaging contrast agents, in part to recompense the higher expense of development. It is difficult to charge a higher price for an already approved drug when it is then developed for a new indication (Adida 2024). If we consider sonothrombolysis of MVO associated with acute MI, to develop Perflutren for this indication, will be expensive, and require large clinical trials. It may not be possible to charge a higher price for the same MB product for the therapeutic indication than for the already approved diagnostic indication. New, more effective agents, for example, PSMBs, specifically developed for therapeutic indications, however, may be developed as therapeutic agents and command higher price points to justify the expense for such development.

Conclusions and Future Directions

New, improved MBs, including MVT‐100, are under development. The neutral surface chemistry of MVT‐100 enables condensation to form PSMB. PSMB are more effective for sonothrombolysis and sonoreperfusion than MB. Likewise, PSMBs outperform MBs for tumor ablation with FUS in cavitation‐mediated tumor ablation. Nanobubbles (e.g., DDFPe) are under development as an oxygen therapeutic in cancer, stroke, and respiratory distress. Progress will need to be made within the regulatory framework in the US requiring demonstration of safety and efficacy to gain clinical approval. MB/nanobubbles/PSMB afford great potential to advance the field of theranostics to afford less invasive, more efficacious, and cost‐effective treatment of many different diseases. Phase shift microbubbles/nanobubbles are predicted by the authors to become important tools in our fantastic voyage to conquer disease.
