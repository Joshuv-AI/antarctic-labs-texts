Prospects of training system and deficit irrigation for increasing yield and water use efficiency of pitaya (Hylocereus spp.) in the Mediterranean region
Authors: Harun Kaman, Hamide Gübbük, Lokman Altinkaya, Recep Balkiç, Mustafa Ünlü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is well known that the water consumption of some tropical fruits is high. However, in the world and especially in agricultural areas, where the Mediterranean climate prevails, drought is defined as one of the most important abiotic stress factors that affect product patterns. For this reason, this research chose the pitaya (Hylocereus spp.) plant, which has relatively low water consumption among tropical fruit species. Three different training (Control [Pole], T and Inverted U) systems with different deficit irrigation (combined with FULL = 100%; DI75 = 75%, DI50 = 50% and DI25 = 25%) treatments in the cultivation of pitaya under greenhouse conditions; their effects on the yield, evapotranspiration, water use efficiency, irrigation water use efficiency, yield response factor and some quality parameters (fruit weight, length and width, fruit flesh hardness and water-soluble dry matter) were examined. The research was carried out in Antalya province, Turkey between 2018 and 2021 and ‘Vietnamese Jaina’ was used as a cultivar. Irrigation applications were carried out using drip irrigation method and the amount of irrigation water to be used in the control (FULL) treatment was calculated based on measurement values taken from the A-Class evaporation pan. The research findings revealed that the amount of irrigation water varied between 27.63 mm and 83.80 mm, and the fruit flesh value ranged between 79.36 kg cm−2 and 105.53 kg cm−2. In general, as the irrigation water level decreases, a decrease in fruit flesh values is also recorded. In addition, it was found that different irrigation water levels affect yield. The average yield values were found as DI75 (7169.60 kg ha−1) > FULL (5783.90 kg ha−1) > DI50 (4737.00 kg ha−1) > DI25 (4376.30 kg ha−1) ranking from high to low. Yield varied according to the training system, with the highest value recorded in the Pole (Control) System (5625.30 kg ha−1) followed by Inverted U System (5594.90 kg ha−1) and T System (5330.00 kg ha−1). As an important finding for yield values, it was found that the treatment DI75 irrigation has a general superiority over the other irrigation treatments. Based on the research findings, considering the yield and quality parameters, a Pole (Control) System and an Inverted U System were recommended as a training system, respectively, along with the irrigation treatment (DI75), in which 75% of the water needed by the plant is given in greenhouse conditions considering the evaporation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07442-5.Keywords: Water use efficiency, Water-yield relations, Climate change, Drought resistance, Fruit yield

Introduction

It is predicted that Turkey would be affected by climate change as it is all over the world. The increase in temperature in recent years, and water scarcity due to the irregular rainfalland extreme storms could be given as the most important problems caused by global climate change. Therefore, it is expected that scientific studies conducted in the regions suffering from water shortage on species that are already drought-resistant, have high added value and have a chance of export, which will then make a significant contribution to the sustainability of agriculture and natural resources. Trivellini et al. [1] reported that pitaya is a potentially important species for cultivation in Mediterranean countries as it has a very low water consumption and good adaptation to high temperatures. The studies conducted across the world on pitaya are seen to have been conducted in the last 20 years. Mizrahi [2] reported that very limited studies were conducted on pitaya until 1994; however, the number of total studies on this species in 2015 was found to have increased up to 284 in the literature.

Pitaya is a species whose cultivation has started to become widespread in the tropics and subtropics around the world and whose originates from tropical America [3]. Although pitaya is cultivated in open fields under tropical conditions, in many subtropical regions it is grown in greenhouses or under shade nets. In hot summer regions such as Israel, cultivation is carried out under net to protect the plants from the damage of excessive sunlight [4]. The first adaptation study on the cultivation of pitaya species in Turkey started open-field in Antalya province in the last ten years. However, plants were damaged by cold in the winter months and by excessive heat in the summer months in the first year. Nowadays, pitaya cultivation has started to be common in greenhouse conditions in the Mediterranean and Aegean Regions of Turkey.

The research on water-yield relations in many plant species and varieties adopted different approaches in the determination of irrigation water to be used. One of these approaches is to use an A-Class evaporation pan. Several studies have estimated irrigation requirements using Class A pan evaporation data [5–7]. On the other hand, the number of studies conducted on the irrigation of pitaya have been very limited. However, Mizrahi and Nerd [4] emphasized that the effect of irrigation regimes is extremely important for plant development and fruit quality in pitaya. As the majority of the roots of the pitaya are at a depth of 15–30 cm in the soil, irrigation is required to prevent the soil from complete drying [8]. Irrigation is important to prevent cracking during the fruit development stage as well as the plant growth and development. Nerd et al. [3] reported that vegetative growth is slower as irrigation water salinity promotes water deficiency in plants. Pitaya is a crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) plant. For this reason, their stomata remain open during the night, and evaporation has been reported to be less as the CO2 uptake occurs during the night [9]. In addition, as the plant has a succulent structure, there are no leaves and the presence of a waxy layer on the stem also increases the ability of plants to adapt to adverse conditions [10]. Mizrahi et al. [11] reported that pitaya and Indian figs use 10% of the water consumed by citrus fruits, peaches, avocados and pears. On the other hand, Nerd and Neumann [12] found that the development of the pitaya is better under well-watered conditions. In another study on irrigation, the effect of saline water on the development of pitaya was examined, and it was reported that the roots of pitaya were as sensitive as the shoots to saline effects [13]. A review of the literature on pitaya cultivation suggests that research on deficit irrigation regimes in greenhouse production under subtropical conditions is still lacking. Therefore, the results of this study will shed light on the deficit irrigation regime and will make an important contribution to water management in the future.

So far, the studies conducted on pitaya in general have been quite limited to the adaptation, fertilization biology and reproduction of the species. In addition, considering the studies conducted on pitaya, there has not been any research in which the training system and deficit irrigation treatments have been done together. For this reason, there are still many issues to be examined, including cultural practices, the adaptation of different irrigation regimes (deficit irrigation), and training systems. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the effects of three different training systems (Pole, T, and U) and four different irrigation (100%, 75%, 50% and 25%) levels on fruit yield and quality, water consumption, water use efficiency, irrigation water use efficiency and principal component analysis in the pitaya species.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

This research was carried out between 2018 and 2021 in a plastic-covered greenhouse at the Research and Application Land of Akdeniz University Faculty of Agriculture, which is 54 m above sea level. Greenhouse dimensions: span = 8 m; bay spacing = 3 m; post spacing = 3 m; side‑wall height = 3 m; ridge height = 5 m; double‑ridge ventilation. UV + IR co-extruded plastic with a thickness of 200 microns was used as the greenhouse cover.

The ‘Vietnamese Jaina’ pitaya cultivar, which is widely grown in Turkey, was used as plant material in the research. After rooting, plants were transferred to steel 20-litre-pots containing 2/4 soil, ¼ peat, and ¼ pearlite [14]. The pots were placed in the greenhouse in such a way that there are 2 m distances between the rows and 1.5 m space above the rows. As a training system, the Pole System, which is widely used in Turkey, was used as a control unit. Apart from the control (Pole system), two different training systems, T and Inverted U, have also been studied in the research (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Installation of greenhouse and training systems

Four irrigation treatments were tested (Supplementary Fig. 2): (1) Full amount of irrigation water was applied uniformly on the plant-root zone; (2) DI75, 75% of the water of the FULL was applied; (3) DI50, 50% of the water of the FULL was applied; and (4) DI25, 25% of the water of the FULL was applied.

An A-Class evaporation pan was placed in the greenhouse and the amount of irrigation water applied to the control (FULL) treatment was calculated using the evaporation measurement results obtained from Eq. 1 [15].

I refers to irrigation water (L plant area−1); kp and kc, respectively refers to the evaporation pan and crop coefficient (1 was taken); Ep refers to the total evaporation measured with the A-class evaporation pan corresponding the irrigation intervals (mm) and A refers to plant (pot) area (m2).

Irrigation applications were carried out by drip irrigation method. After the transportation of the pots, a main drip irrigation unit, a side pipeline, water distribution pipes and laterals were installed in the greenhouse. Irrigation pipes were placed in the root zone of the plant using octopus-type drippers with a dripper flow rate of 2 L h−1 assuring one outlet to each pot. Irrigation applications were planned as once every 10 days in winter, once a week in spring and twice a week in summer.

Fertilization and pruning

Each year, a total of 135 g N, 180 g P₂O₅, and 62.5 g K₂O was applied per plant in split applications at different times [16]. According to the cultivation systems, when the plants reached 180 cm in height, their tops were cut off and the formation of side branches was encouraged. In the first year, 4 or 5 of the side branches were left and the remaining branches were pruned. Later, when the growing branches reached 1 m, the end parts were cut off, and plants were encouraged to fruit set. Also 3 times a year; thickly growing and dried branches were removed allowing the remaining branches for better ventilation, good sunshine and healthier development of the plant [17].

Soil water content

Soil water content was monitored throughout the growing season. In addition, at the beginning and end of the research, the pots were weighed and the water budget was calculated. In cases where there is water leaking under pots during irrigation, the amount of water drained was measured and recorded.

PR2 Profile Probe access tubes were installed, mid-way between the drippers and the plant roots. The access tubes were used to measure soil water content distribution profiles, before and after irrigation, using a PR2 Profile Probe. According to the method specified by Köksal et al. [18], and Kaman and Özbek [19], firstly, the calibration of the PR2 method was made within 30 cm of soil depth. Then, the water content in the soil was monitored by the PR2 method under three different soil water content conditions.

Yield

During the vegetation period, the weights of the harvested fruits were calculated by weighing them on a precision scale. The yield value obtained per plant was calculated by multiplying the yield value by the number of plants per decare. For this experiment, a total of 252 pots were placed in a 0.1 ha (1 decare) greenhouse, with one plant grown in each pot. In commercial production, however, four plants are typically grown per pot. To ensure comparability with commercial standards, the yield per plant in all treatments was calculated as if each pot contained four plants. Accordingly, yield estimations were based on 252 pots × 4 plants (1008 plants) per decare.

Pomological and biochemical analysis results

The weights of the harvested fruits (g fruit−1) were calculated by weighing them on a precision scale. The height of the fruits was calculated by measuring them with a digital caliper sensitive to 0.01 mm (mm fruit−1). The widest diameter perpendicular to the fruit axis (mm fruit−1) was measured with a digital caliper. Fruit flesh hardness was determined by measuring using the Brookfield texture device with an 11 mm tip [22]. The total amount of soluble solid content (SSC) (%) was determined with the help of a refractometer in a 5 ml sample taken from the fruit.

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to better visualize the relationships among the variables. The data were evaluated with three replications, and the PCA analysis was performed using the XLSTAT (NY, USA) software [23].

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted in a factorial randomized block design with three replications, each consisting of seven pots. Within the greenhouse, the training systems were arranged to form the block structure. Data was analyzed using the XLSTAT software, applying a two-way ANOVA to evaluate the main effects of training system and irrigation as well as their interaction (training ×irrigation). The significance of treatment mean differences was tested at p < 0.05 and p < 0.001 levels. Prior to the analysis, assumptions of homogeneity of variances and normality were tested. Multiple comparisons were performed using the LSD test.

Results

Irrigation water and evapotranspiration (ET)

During the research, the total measured evaporation value was calculated as 1884.6 L (in a cumulative manner) (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the temporal change of the cumulative state of irrigation applications can be seen in Fig. 1. The first irrigation application was started to be 0.52 L per pot and plant and irrigation application was carried out 162 times. During the first 17 irrigation applications, a total of 8.84 L of irrigation water was applied to all pots/plants in equal amounts in a cumulative manner (Fig. 1). Then following the 18th irrigation application, deficit irrigation (DI75, DI50 and DI25) applications were started together with FULL irrigation. The difference between FULL and deficit irrigation (DI75, DI50 and DI25) applications was found to be temporal and as expected (Fig. 1).

Irrigation water amounts (mm) were calculated from 162 irrigation applications. Under all training (Pole (Control) System, T System and Inverted U System) systems, irrigation water amounts were realized as 83.80, 65.07, 46.35 and 27.63 mm respectively in FULL, DI75, DI50 and DI25 treatments (Please refer to Supplementary Table 1). During the research, there was no water loss by drainage in DI75, DI50 and DI25 treatments under the Pole (Control) System. Similarly, there was no water loss by drainage under the DI50 treatment under the Inverted U System and the DI25 treatment under the T System. Considering that 162 irrigation applications were carried out during the research, the values regarding the possible drainage water (mm) column in in Supplementary Table 1 were recorded as essentially a very low amount of values. Although these values are too small to be included in the calculation (for example, on average, they ranged from 0.02% to 0.51% for an irrigation application), they were still taken into consideration for a precise calculation. plant evapotranspiration (ET, mm) values were calculated with water budget equation.

Under all training systems (Pole [Control] System, T System and Inverted U System), the relationships between the yield (kg da−1) and the amount of irrigation water (mm) for FULL, DI75, DI50 and DI25 treatments were found (Fig. 3). In this study, when the amount of irrigation water required by the plant was classified as 25% (DI25), 50% (DI50), 75% (DI75) and 100% (FULL), an increase in yield values was also observed when the amount of irrigation water was increased up to 75% in general.

Relationship between yield (Y, kg da−1) and irrigation water (mm) under different training systems for pitaya

The yield values of the irrigation water amount at the level of 75% (DI75) and 100% (FULL) remained statistically the same in the Pole (Control) System (Table 1; Fig. 3). As a result, it was found that 75% (DI75) of the amount of irrigation water required by the plant is more advantageous than other treatment levels (25% [DI25], 50% [DI50] and 100% [FULL]) (Table 1; Fig. 3).

In terms of training systems, the Pole (Control) System and the Inverted U System, which have a similar attitude in the WUE (kg m−3) values, as well as in the yield values (Table 1), have statistically given better results (Table 1). Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) analysis was used to show the relationships between the examined criteria and the applications. The relationship between the training systems and the examined criteria is shown in Fig. 6a. All the criteria examined according to the training systems showed a strong relationship with each other, PCA components explained a total of 100% variance in two factors (PC1 88.46%, PC2 11.54%). Pole (P) and inverted U (U) training systems affected the examined criteria more than T training system (T).

Discussion

In parallel with the increase in the amount of irrigation water (Fig. 1), an increase in the soil water content is also expected (Fig. 2). As a result, depending on the amount of irrigation water applied in the research, the temporal change in soil water content, as expected in all training systems, was found to be as in FULL >DI75 >DI50 >DI25 ranking from high to low (Fig. 2). The results of many studies conducted on different plant species have also indicated, as expected, an increase/decrease in soil water content in parallel with the increase/decrease in the amount of irrigation water ([7, 15]).

The yield values of the irrigation water amount at the level of 75% (DI75) and 100% (FULL) remained statistically the same in the Pole (Control) System (Figs. 1 and 2; Table 1). However, in particular, if the amount of irrigation water increases up to 100% (FULL) in the T System and the Inverted U System, a decrease in the yield value was found compared to the 75% (DI75) treatment (Fig. 3). Moderate levels of water deficit are known to suppress excessive vegetative growth while simultaneously enhancing water-use efficiency, since mild stress conditions tend to restrict transpirational water loss to a greater extent than carbon assimilation [24]. Accordingly, applying approximately 75% of full irrigation appears to provide an optimal compromise between sustaining essential physiological functions and promoting assimilate partitioning toward reproductive development. In line with this, deficit irrigation strategies have generally been reported to be most effective when irrigation levels remain relatively high, typically within 60–100% of crop evapotranspiration [25]. As a result, it was found that 75% (DI75) of the amount of irrigation water needed by the plant is more advantageous than the other irrigation levels (FULL, DI50 and DI25: 100%, 50% and 25%) (Figs. 1, 2 and 3; Table 1). Yield decreases could be observed in DI50 and DI25 (50% and 25%) treatments in which the least irrigation water is used (Figs. 1, 2 and 3; Table 1). Similarly, Mizrahi et al. [11] found that yield values also decreased with a decrease in the amount of irrigation water in the pitaya plant. In this research, irrigation treatments were statistically listed from high to low as DI75 >FULL >DI50 >DI25 treatments when yield values were considered (Table 1). The tendency of decrease in yield values in the other irrigation treatments (DI50 and DI25) except FULL irrigation treatment was found to be similar to the findings of Mizrahi et al. [11].

Yield and quality characteristics, varieties, and ecological conditions vary according to cultural practices and harvest time in pitaya cultivation [26]. Therefore, yield is influenced by many factors. The results of this study have shown differences in yield values according to irrigation treatments and training systems. When the effect of irrigation treatments on the yield per plant was examined, the DI75 treatment was found to have given the best result (Table 1; Fig. 6a). The highest yield was obtained under the DI75 treatment (Table 1). Among the training systems, the Pole System (Control) was found to have given the best result, and this was followed by the Inverted U System (Table 1).

Pitaya starts to produce fruit in the second year after planting, and the maximum yield can be reached after 5 years [27]. When the full yield age is reached, the yield value could reach up to 20–30 t ha−1. Mizrahi and Nerd [4] reported that the yield in Israel reached up to 40 t ha−1. Mizrahi [28] found that the yield efficiency of the Pole System made of concrete varies between 20 and 45 t ha−1 However, only one study was found to compare the training systems [28]. This study compared the Pole, T bar and V systems, in our study, the Pole System was found to have increased plant growth and development by 17–38%, flower bud formation by 15–36%, fruit number by 15–36% and fruit weight by 24%, respectively, compared to the T bar and V System. Based on the findings of this study, the yield values could be claimed to have remained among the average values for the beginning as the cultivation was performed soil-free and as the plants were 2.7 years old.

As with the yield (Table 1) and WUE (Table 1) values, as an important finding in IWUE values, it can be concluded that the DI75 irrigation treatment is generally more advantageous than the other irrigation treatments (FULL, DI50, DI25) (Table 1). As an application that could save 25% of irrigation water in arid and semi-arid regions where water is scarce and expensive, the treatment of DI75 was found to have given better results (Table 1). Regarding the yield (Table 1) and WUE (Table 1) values in the training systems, the Pole (Control) System and the Inverted U System, which statistically exhibit a similar attitude in IWUE (kg m−3) values, were found to be superior (Table 1).

The fruit weight values, which vary according to irrigation treatments, varied between 225.08 g fruit−1 and 260.31 g fruit−1 (Table 2). The fact that the fruit weight was found to be lower in the FULL treatment was because the number of fruits was higher in the FULL treatment. ElObeidy [29] reported that the fruit weight in pitaya is between 150 g and 600 g, although it varies according to species and varieties.

As a result of the research findings, the fruit length values varying according to training systems varied between 106.46 mm fruit−1 and 117.40 mm fruit−1, and the fruit length values varying according to irrigation treatments varied between 112.25 mm fruit−1 and 115.31 mm fruit−1 (Table 2). Fruit length values were found to be higher than those of Jiang et al. [30]. This may be due to the difference in species, growing environment and cultural practices.

Considering the water consumption of pitaya, irrigation methods that will save irrigation water, such as drip irrigation, should be preferred in its cultivation. Panigrahi et al. [31] reported that soil water sensor-based drip irrigation increases yield by using less water in bananas, and that product quality and water efficiency increase under sensor-based irrigation, so the automatic drip irrigation method could be a better option for banana cultivation in water-scarce regions. For this reason, it is important to examine soil water sensor-based drip irrigation in pitaya cultivation, where adequate research on irrigation has not been conducted.

A study involving deficit irrigation or training system applications for pitaya has not been found, nor have PCA analysis results related to this topic been reported. However, PCA analysis has been applied to some fruit and vegetable species in relation to deficit irrigation. Yavuz et al. [32] investigated the effects of deficit irrigation practices on yield, yield parameters, and quality traits in grafted and non-grafted watermelon varieties. Specifically, as a result of the PCA analysis, they reported that, similar to our study, deficit irrigation practices DI100 and DI75 were prominent in yield parameters such as yield, fruit weight, fruit length, and fruit width. Additionally, the highest dry matter content was obtained from the DI35 and DI0 deficit irrigation treatments. Our findings regarding dry matter content are consistent with those of Yavuz et al. [32], as the study reported that the highest dry matter content was obtained from the lowest irrigation amount (DI25). In a different study, Wang and Xing [33] investigated the effects of different fertilizer doses combined with deficit irrigation on yield and quality parameters in tomatoes. According to the PCA analysis conducted on all criteria, the researchers reported that the highest fertilizer dose applied with the DI75 deficit irrigation method yielded the best results. Our findings are also consistent with the results of Wang and Xing [33]. This highlights the importance of proper water usage rather than excessive irrigation in cultivation practices.

Conclusion

Research findings suggests that irrigation water should be given at a rate of 100% considering the evaporation until plants complete their vegetative development in the pitaya and 75% (DI75) irrigation water should be given considering the evaporation at the time when plants are at the generative period. In addition, across training systems, the DI75 irrigation treatment was found to be generally superior over the other irrigation treatments (FULL, DI50, DI25), especially in terms of yield value from the parameters studied. Thus, FULL irrigation can be reduced by 25% without compromising yield. Among the examined parameters, the Pole (Control) System and Inverted U System were recommended as the training system, respectively.

In addition to this, considering water cost under FULL, DI75, DI50, and DI25 irrigation conditions, water savings, along with yield and quality, are very important in the economic analysis. As a matter of fact, DI75, which has 25% less water than FULL, will also have 25% less irrigation water costs than FULL.
