Beliefs on the ability to regulate negative emotions mediates the effects of competitive anger on task-focused self-efficacy in taekwondo athletes
Authors: Luca Bovolon, Maria Rosaria Nappa, Salvatore Chiodo, Francesca Greco, Katia Folino, Maria Grazia Tarsitano, Maurizio Bertollo, Gian Pietro Emerenziani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-efficacy (SE) refers to an individual's belief in their own ability to successfully execute specific tasks and produce desired outcomes. The focused SE on specific sports tasks has been shown to impact athletic performance by interacting with other self-evaluation processes, like self-efficacy beliefs in emotion-related regulation and effective emotion regulation. This study aims at investigating the mediating effect of SE in regulating negative affect in the relationship between anger and taekwondo task-focused SE. Accordingly, the study aims to adapt and validate the Roundhouse Kick Self-Efficacy Scale (RKSES) in Italian, which assesses taekwondo task-focused SE in athletes.In study 1, 180 Italian taekwondo athletes (Mage = 27.6, SD = 12; 89.4 % black belts; 69 women) were sampled to validate the Italian version of the RKSES, which presented a good fit of the data (p = .031), internal consistency (α = .916), and concurrent validity (r = .561). Study 2 involved 357 Italian taekwondo athletes (Mage = 28.4, SD = 11.87; 87.4 % black belts; 147 women), and collected self-reports on regulating negative emotions SE, competitive anger and aggression, perceived physical ability, and taekwondo task-focused SE. Analysis of covariance revealed multiple differences between age-related categories in terms of competitive anger (p < .001) and aggressiveness (p = .041), and between gender groups in terms of competitive aggressiveness (p = .002), negative emotion regulation SE (p = .024), and perceived physical abilities (p < .001). Mediation analysis indicated that negative emotion regulation SE partially mediated the effects of competitive anger on task-focused SE (p < .001).Findings suggest a mediating role of negative emotion regulation SE in the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE, emphasizing the importance of taking an instrumental approach to emotion regulation, and advocates for the use of practical tools to assess task-focused SE.Keywords: Sport-focused self-efficacy, Regulatory emotional self-efficacy, Competitive anger, Combat sports

Introduction

In sports, athletes face both physical and mental challenges to reach peak performance. Self-efficacy (SE) beliefs are an often-overlooked factor that can significantly influence athletes' performance [[1], [2], [3]]. SE beliefs refer to an individual's belief in their own ability to successfully execute specific tasks and produce desired outcomes [4]. These beliefs are shaped by past and similar experiences, observed performances of others, social persuasion, and physiological conditions [4]. According to Bandura's recommendations [1,4,5], SE has been investigated referring to specific tasks, rather than ones that assess global expectations of performance. Specifically, sports focused SE concerns athletes' confidence in their ability to execute specific skills, techniques, or strategies required by their own sport [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Moritz et al. [10] underlined a positive association between SE beliefs and sport performance. Athletes with high SE beliefs tend to approach challenges with confidence, persistence, and a belief in their ability to overcome obstacles [11]: they are more likely to set challenging goals, exert greater effort, and persevere through setbacks. In contrast, athletes with low SE beliefs may doubt their capabilities, experience heightened anxiety, and exhibit a lack of assertiveness in crucial moments [12]. General, and task-focused SE beliefs play a vital role in athletes' performance outcomes by affecting their thoughts, emotions, and behaviors [13].

Thus, comprehending antecedents and dynamics of SE beliefs seems to be crucial to design effective interventions to enhance athletes' self-beliefs, optimize their performance, and foster a positive athletic environment [13,14]. However, SE beliefs do not operate in isolation [4,15]. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that SE beliefs impact athletic performance by interacting with emotional SE and effective emotion regulation strategies [16,17]. Indeed, positive and negative affect, and the relative skills to manage them, are psychological variables strongly associated with athletes’ SE belief [[18], [19], [20]].

Emotional self-regulation refers to automatic or deliberate actions to initiate, maintain, or change one or more components of an emotional response or the situation in which emotional experiences take place [[21], [22], [23]]. In sport contexts, emotional self-regulation refers to the ability of athletes to effectively manage and control their emotions during sport-related situations: it involves understanding, recognizing, and modulating one's emotional responses to optimize performance decision-making e.g., Refs. [24,25]. Literature underlined that emotion regulation not only aims to promote pleasure while decreasing unpleasant emotions, but it also upregulates pleasant or unpleasant emotions to achieve a particular goal i.e., instrumental emotional self-regulation; [26], and it seems to be particularly relevant to sport performance [24,27,28]. Furthermore, Lane et al. [24] observed that athletes who believe that anxiety or anger improve performance also reported high anger before performance.

In regard to anger, it is important to recognize that it is a natural response to various stimuli e.g., Ref. [29], and it may not always be detrimental to performance [27]. Athletes may experience anger in response to perceived injustices, mistakes, or challenges they face [30], and it can fuel determination, motivation, and intensity to help athletes to push through obstacles and perform at their best [31]. Conversely, uncontrolled anger could negatively interfere with athletes' performances via impairments of attention, cognitive processing, and decision-making [32,33]. Anger can be expressed in different ways in relation to specific types of sport, and competitive categories [34]. Moreover, scientific studies focused on the role of anger in martial arts and combat sports, with the most recent reviews and meta-analysis informing of contrasting results [35,36]. Studies demonstrated that training in martial arts seems to reduce levels of anger and aggression via meditation, philosophy or kata; other authors highlighted opposite tendencies or even no association between martial arts practice and anger and aggressiveness [37,38]. Furthermore, other works suggested that anger is linked to successful performance [31,39] and that it could be perceived as useful by martial arts athletes [40,41]. The aggressive nature of the sport might promote anger and violent behaviors [42,43], and there seems to be an association between individual levels of anger and aggressiveness and their predisposition to work in martial art and combat sport [44]. Yet, previous evidence also pointed out the role of martial arts as a specific sporting culture, distinguished from other physical activities, in which practitioners can learn specific tools to manage emotions [45,46]. In particular, taekwondo is a globally popular example of martial arts that could positively influence affective and cognitive processes via specific self-monitoring and self-regulating strategies acquired through training [[47], [48], [49]]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of conducting in-depth investigations into how individuals' features might interact with the unique aspects of martial arts training. Further, understanding how athletes' characteristics may affect their beliefs about their ability to self-regulate may inform practitioners to tailor intervention strategies. Furthermore, a positive relationship between SE and sport performance [10] would benefit from practical tools that reliably assess SE, especially when competitive sports contexts are being considered. Therefore, the main aim of the current study is to investigate the interplay between anger, beliefs on the ability to regulate negative affect, and sport tasks' focused SE among Italian taekwondo athletes (Study 2). To this purpose, we adapted a specific tool to analyze task-focused SE in taekwondo (Study 1). Based on previous research findings, and considering the martial arts context, we hypothesized a mediating effect of SE in regulating negative affect in the relationship between anger and taekwondo task-focused SE. Specifically, we expect that anger is negatively associated with SE in regulating negative affect, and that SE in regulating negative affect is positively related to taekwondo tasks-focused SE. As discussed above, SE beliefs connected to specific domains of individual functioning do not operate in isolation, and the perception of being able to effectively manage one's negative emotion regulation SE could be positively associated with the task-focused SE [15,16]. However, in conditions of stress or specific circumstances, anger can be perceived as uncontrollable or not goal-oriented, lowering their expectations of being able to manage them. Therefore, SE beliefs in modulating the expression of negative affect may also differ based on the individual emotional state [50,51].

Study 1

The purpose of study 1 was to adapt and validate the Roundhouse Kick Self-Efficacy Scale [52] in Italian. The adapted scale will be a valid tool to assess athletes’ SE beliefs on taekwondo-specific skills, associated with the main roundhouse kick technique, in Italian language and cultural contexts.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

One-hundred and eighty Italian Taekwondo athletes (69 women) aged 12 to 63 (Mage = 27.6, SD = 12) agreed to take part in this study. Participants were recruited via online advertisements on the main social media channels of the Italian Taekwondo Federation (FITA), and were provided with a link to access an online survey. To be included in the study, participants had to be 12 years old or above, be a member of FITA as a practicing athlete, and hold at least a yellow belt in Taekwondo.

The majority of the athletes in our sample held a black belt in taekwondo (89.4 %). Athletes averaged 3.48 (SD = 1.46) training sessions per week, with a mean training time of 1.84 h per training session (SD = .67). Referring to the main FITA categories to which athletes are assigned based on their year of birth, 43.3 % (n = 78) of the participants competed in the age group 18–24, 8.3 % (n = 15) in age group 12–17, 11.7 % (n = 21) in age group 25–30, 25 % (n = 45) in age group 31–46, and 11.7 % (n = 21) in age group over 47. The present sample size ensured a person-to-item ratio of 10:1 [53]. All participants signed the written informed consent approved by the Ethics Committee of the region Calabria (Italy) (Register entry N. 86).

Materials

Roundhouse Kick Self-Efficacy Scale (RKSES): The RKSES [52] utilizes a Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not capable) to 5 (Totally capable) to measure specific SE beliefs for the roundhouse kick, and it is composed of a total of 12 items. The scale was shown to reliably predict kicking performance.

Perceived Physical Ability Scale (PPAS): The Italian version of the PPAS [54]; adapted from Ref. [55] measures participants' beliefs on their physical abilities (i.e., strength, speed, and agility) in 10 items (e.g., ‘Can you react quickly?’) to rate on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Yes, very much’) to 5 (‘No, not at all’). Cronbach's alpha (α = .816) suggested a good internal consistency for our sample.

Statistical analyses

The Italian version of the Roundhouse Kick Self-Efficacy Scale (RKSES-IT) was back-translated from the original version, which measured specific SE beliefs for the roundhouse kick with 12 items [52]. Data were screened for extreme and missing values (<1%). An Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) with maximum likelihood method of extraction was performed on the RKSES-IT to explore the factor structure of the scale. Oblimin rotation was applied to identify any meaningful components, and the number of factors to extract was based on parallel analysis [56], and later confirmed by eigenvalues [57] and Scree plot scrutiny [58]. Loading minimum threshold was set at .3. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sample adequacy and Bartlett's test of sphericity were used to test for the sample's suitability for factorialization. KMO values > .08 and significant p-value on the Bartlett's test (p < .05) indicate an acceptable factorability of the data [59,60]. Goodness-of-fit indexes were used for the evaluation of the given model, in particular chi-square (χ2), normed chi-square (χ2/df), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis fit index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). As a general rule, CFI and TLI values > .90, SRMR and RMSEA values < .08, and χ2/df values < 5 indicate an acceptable fit for the model [[61], [62], [63], [64], [65]]. A Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with maximum likelihood method of extraction was performed on the RKSES-IT to confirm the factor structure of the EFA. CFA models were estimated via a maximum likelihood method, with standard errors and a mean-adjusted chi-square test statistic that is robust to non-normality [66,67]. Goodness-of-fit indexes were used to evaluate the model. Both Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega were used as a measure of reliability in terms of internal consistency, and values > .70 were considered acceptable [68,69]. Finally, concurrent validity was examined using Pearson product moment correlation (Pearson r) between the RKSES-IT and the PPAS.

Results

Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sample adequacy (overall MSA = .91) and Bartlett's test of sphericity (p < .001) confirmed the sample's suitability for factorialization. An item inspection was performed on the original RKSES scale [52] on the basis of content analysis on each item [70]. An expert was consulted to review the scale, analyze the content validity, and evaluate items for clarity, grammar, face validity, and redundancy [71]. A final 6-item solution was proposed and explored via EFA to determine the factorial structure and examine its internal reliability. The model was estimated using maximum likelihood method (MLM) for factorial extraction and oblimin rotation was applied to identify meaningful components. Based on parallel analysis, 1 single factor was suggested, with item loadings ranging from .71 to .90 (Table 1). The selection of 1 factor was also confirmed by eigenvalues and scree plot examination. The total variance explained by the items was 66 %, and the model fit measures suggested an acceptable fit (χ2 (9) = 17.8, p = .036; χ2/df = 1.99, TLI = .979, RMSEA [90%CI] = .074 [.018–.125]). A CFA with MLM and oblimin rotation was then run to validate the given factorial structure derived from the EFA. The CFA fit indices suggested an acceptable fit (χ2 (9) = 18.35, p = .031; χ2/df = 2.039, CFI = .987, TLI = .979, RMSEA [90%CI] = .076 [.022–.126], SRMR = .027). The reliability of the scale, as investigated by the internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach's alpha), indicated good scale reliability (α = .916). This was also supported by McDonald's omega (ω = .919). Finally, concurrent validity, as assessed by correlation analysis between the present scale and the PPAS, indicated a strong correlation between the two scales (r = .561). Analyses were performed on R-Studio for R (v. 4.3; RStudio Team, 2020). The full RKSES-IT scale is reported in the supplementary materials (Supplementary File 1).

Study 2

Study 2 explored the mediating role of athletes’ negative emotions regulation SE on the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE. Additionally, differences in competitive anger and aggressiveness, emotion regulation SE, task-focused SE, and perceived physical abilities were analyzed between age-related competitive category and gender.

Materials and methods

Participants

An a-priori power analysis was conducted via the G∗Power software program (version 3.1) to determine sample size. Considering a power of 80 % and a significance threshold of .05, the power analysis indicated a required sample size of 304 to detect small-to-medium effect sizes (Cohen's f = .20) [72]. Thus, a sample of 357 Italian Taekwondo athletes (147 women) aged 12 to 63 (M = 28.4, SD = 11.87) were enrolled for Study 2 following same recruitment process and inclusion criteria described in Study 1. The majority of the athletes held a black belt in taekwondo (87.4 %). Athletes averaged 3.61 (SD = 1.58) training sessions per week, with a mean training time of 1.84 h per training session (SD = .69). Referring to the FITA categories, 46 % (n = 164) of the participants competed in the age group 18–24, 8.4 % (n = 30) in age group 12–17, 11.5 % (n = 41) in age group 25–30, 22.1 % (n = 79) in age group 31–46, and 12 % (n = 43) in age group over 47.

Measures

The following questionnaires were administered to the participants:

Regulatory Emotional Self-Efficacy Scale: SE in regulating negative affect were assessed by means of the RESE scale [73]. The scale is further divided into two subscales. The first scale is made up of four items assessing the participants' perceived ability to express positive affect (i.e., liking and affection towards others, enthusiasm and enjoyment, and satisfaction after personal accomplishments), as measured on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not well at all) to 5 (very well) (e.g., ‘How well can you express joy when good things happen to you?’). The second scale measured the participants perceived ability to regulate negative affect in the face of anxiety-arousing threats, anger provocation, rejection and disrespect, and the ability to control worrying or anxiety when things go wrong, on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Not well at all’) to 5 (‘Very well’), and was constituted by 15 items (e.g., ‘How well can you keep from getting discouraged by strong criticism?’). Cronbach's α was .916 for the first scale, and .928 for the second scale, both suggesting good internal consistency for our sample.

Competitive Aggressiveness and Anger Scale (CAAS): Athletes' levels of aggressiveness and anger were measured using the Italian version of the CAAS [74,75]. The scale is composed of two subscales, one assessing anger via 6 items (e.g., ‘I feel bitter towards my opponent if I lose’), and the other assessing aggressiveness via 6 items (e.g., ‘I use excessive force to gain an advantage’). Cronbach's α was .809 for anger, and .659 for aggressiveness, suggesting good internal consistency for the anger scale and acceptable (though, not ideal) for the aggressiveness scale.

Perceived Physical Ability Scale (PPAS): The Italian version of the PPAS [54,55], and it measures participants' beliefs on their physical abilities (i.e., strength, speed, and agility) in 10 items (e.g., ‘Can you react quickly?’) to rate on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Yes, very much’) to 5 (‘No, not at all’). Items 1-3-5-7-9 are reverse-score items. Again, Cronbach's alpha (α = .825) suggested a good internal consistency for our sample.

Roundhouse Kick Self-Efficacy Scale - Italian version (RKSES-IT): The RKSES-IT was adapted from the original version [52]. The scale measures taekwondo athletes' self-efficacy beliefs on their roundhouse kick in 6 items (e.g., ‘Kicking from execution distance with good execution time’), and athletes' beliefs are measured on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not capable) to 5 (Totally capable). The scale demonstrated good internal reliability in terms of internal consistency as measured by Cronbach's alpha (α = .930) and McDonald's omega (ω = .932).

Statistical analyses

Student's t-test was used on the mean scores of the number of training sessions to detect any differences between genders, and an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to determine differences between FITA categories (i.e., 12–17; 18–24; 25–30; 31–46; over 47) in terms of number of training sessions per week [76]. Finally, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to determine differences between groups (i.e., age-related competitive category and gender groups) in terms of competitive anger and aggressiveness, emotion regulation SE, task-focused SE, and perceived physical abilities. For each ANCOVA, the number of training sessions per week was selected as covariate. Specifically, 6 different ANCOVAs were performed with category as factor (5 levels: 2–17; 18–24; 25–30; 31–46; over 47), and the remaining 6 were performed with gender as factor (2 levels: male, female). Effect sizes for significant effects are reported as partial eta squared (η2p), with .01 reflecting a small effect, .06 a medium effect, and .14 a large effect [77,72]. Critical α was set at .05 for significance level, and all post hoc analyses were Tukey HSD corrected. Finally, mediation analysis, by means of regression analyses of total effect, direct effect, and indirect effect, was performed to examine whether athletes' perceived ability to regulate their negative emotions mediated the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE beliefs. Indirect effect was tested via percentile bootstrap estimation approach with 5000 samples, implemented with PROCESS for R Version 4.3 [78,79]. Bootstrap 95 % confidence intervals that do not contain zero indicate a significant indirect effect at p < .05. Analyses were performed on R-Studio for R (v. 4.3).

Results

Descriptives for each scale are reported in Supplementary File 2. Data were screened for outliers, normal distribution, and missing values [80]. Values with a z-score greater than 3.29 were considered extreme outliers and were removed (<5 %), while missing values (<1 %) were excluded from subsequent analyses via pairwise deletion [81]. A logarithmic transformation was applied to the data due to abnormal distributions [82,83]. Student's t-test was performed to test differences between genders in the number of training sessions per week. The t-test did not indicate any significant difference between genders (p = .505). Conversely, the ANOVA performed to detect whether the categories differed in terms of number of training sessions per week revealed a significant main effect of category (F (4, 349) = 2.804, p = .026, η2p = .03). The post hoc analysis revealed that category 4 (M = 3.285) trained significantly fewer times per week than category 2 (M = 3. 863, p = .048).

Differences between FITA categories

A series of 6 analyses of covariance were performed to investigate the possible differences in scores between FITA categories. The ANCOVA testing for potential effects on competitive anger showed a significant main effect of category (F (4, 348) = 8.013; p < .001, η2p = .09). Post hoc tests revealed that category 3 (M = 1.70, p = .014) and 4 (M = 1.70, p = .003) reported lower scores of competitive anger than category 1 (M = 2.24), and category 5 (M = 1.45) reported lower scores than category 1 (p < .001) and 2 (M = 1.92, p < .001). The ANCOVA testing for potential effects on competitive aggressiveness showed a significant main effect of category (F (4, 348) = 2.528; p = .041, η2p = .03). Post hoc tests revealed that category 4 (M = 1.24, p = .043) and 5 (M = 1.24, p = .031) reported higher scores of competitive aggressiveness than category 1 (M = 1.48). The ANCOVAs testing for differences between categories on negative emotion regulation (p = .099), roundhouse kick self-efficacy (p = .244), positive emotion regulation (p = .250), and general self-efficacy (p = .221) did not reveal any significant differences between categories.

Differences between genders

A series of 6 analyses of covariance were performed to investigate the possible differences in scores between gender categories. The ANCOVA testing for potential effects on competitive aggressiveness showed a significant main effect of gender (F (1, 354) = 9.577; p = .002, η2p = .03). Post hoc tests revealed that males (M = 1.34) reported higher scores of competitive aggressiveness than females (M = 1.22, p = .002). The ANCOVA testing for potential effects on negative emotion regulation showed a significant main effect of gender (F (1, 354) = 5.132; p = .024, η2p = .01) and of the covariate (F (1, 354) = 14.531; p < .001, η2p = .04). Post hoc tests revealed that males (M = 3.25) reported higher scores of negative emotion regulation beliefs than females (M = 3.07, p = .024). The ANCOVA testing for potential effects on perceived physical abilities showed a significant main effect of gender (F (1, 354) = 14.850; p < .001, η2p = .04) and of the covariate (F (1, 354) = 37.569; p < .001, η2p = .10). Post hoc tests revealed that males (M = 4.01) reported higher scores of perceived physical abilities than females (M = 3.84, p < .001). The ANCOVAs testing for differences between genders on competitive anger (p = .268), roundhouse kick self-efficacy (p = .065), and positive emotion regulation (p = .186) did not reveal any significant differences between gender categories.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether negative emotion regulation SE mediates the effects of competitive anger on task-focused SE. Results indicated that competitive anger significantly predicted negative emotion regulation SE (B = −.268, SE = .054, 95%CI [-.374; −.162], β = −.256, p < .001), and negative emotion regulation SE significantly predicted task-focused SE (B = .442, SE = .050, 95%CI [.344; .539], β = .439, p < .001). Approximately 20 % of the variance in task-focused SE was accounted for by the predictors competitive anger and negative emotion regulation SE (R2 = .199).

The total effect of competitive anger on task-focused SE was significant (B = .144, SE = .055, 95%CI [.036, .253], β = .137, p < .009). Furthermore, when partialling out the effect of the considered mediator, the direct effect of the predictor competitive anger on task-focused SE remained statistically significant (B = .263, SE = .052, 95%CI [.161, .365], β = .250, p < .001), indicating that the mediation by the negative emotion regulation SE is a partial mediation. Indeed, the indirect effect, as tested by a percentile bootstrap estimation approach with 5000 samples, implemented with PROCESS for R Version 4.3 [79], reported that negative emotion regulation SE significantly mediates the effect of competitive anger on task-focused SE (B = −.118, SE = .026, 95%CI [-.173, −.072], β = −.112). Thus, 31.1 % of the effect of anger on task-focused SE can be explained by the indirect effect (see Fig. 1).

Discussion

The current study aimed to investigate the role of one's beliefs in their ability to efficiently regulate negative emotions in the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE. To accomplish this, we first adapted and validated a short scale to assess athletes' task-focused SE beliefs.

The apparent rise in popularity of short scales could be explained in terms of their potential to reduce assessment time and related costs, therefore offering a more parsimonious alternative when time and financial resources are limited. Despite the advantages attributed to the use of short scales, certain critiques have been advanced concerning an inevitable loss in psychometric quality [84,85]. Yet, previous works have argued that a complete rejection of short scales is not justified, and that a consideration of the objective of the assessment is paramount in determining which format (i.e., long or short version) ought to be used [86]. Nevertheless, our scale maintained good reliability and concurrent validity, and its item selection and reduction was supported by the scale's factorial structure being in line with the original scale [52]. Therefore, as a complete understanding of task demands is essential to accurately judge one's capabilities to perform such a task [4,87], the present scale may offer a practical and reliable tool to assess Italian athletes' SE on the roundhouse kick.

Moreover, Study 2 looked at how individual characteristics, such as genders and age categories, modulate the levels of our variables of interest. Specifically, the analyses of variance highlighted differences between age groups in competitive anger and aggressiveness, with older athletes displaying lower scores of anger and higher scores of aggressiveness than younger ones. This result is partially in line with previous findings suggesting that anger decreases with age [88], thus exasperating the anger-reduction effects of martial arts in adults [35]. Instead, aggression levels were seen to increase with age, with the older categories (4 and 5) scoring higher than the youngest athletes (i.e., category 1). This was somewhat surprising, as previous works showed an age-related decline in aggression, especially physical aggression, after peaking at about 20–30 years of age [89]. Nonetheless, our sample only displayed a significant difference in aggressiveness between adults (over 30 years) and young (under 18 years) athletes, thus suggesting that, at a young age, competitors may not experience as much competitive aggressiveness as their older counterparts. It is worth emphasizing that we recorded anger and aggressiveness in a competitive domain, and not a general one. Traditional martial arts emphasize aspects of philosophy, meditation, and kata, all of which have been associated with a reduction of aggressiveness [90]. Conversely, taekwondo, as an Olympic discipline, adds a series of competitive elements, potentially modulating the aggression-reduction effects of traditional martial arts training. Indeed, data from a recent study showed that athletes who engaged in combat sports reported higher levels of hostility and general aggressiveness than martial arts practitioners, highlighting the influence of competition when discussing martial arts and aggressiveness [91]. These findings emphasize the importance of taking into account the competitive aspects of a given sport when investigating its dynamics within a sport context, as a distinction between the competitive and the general dimension is warranted [74].

Gender analyses revealed that males scored higher than females in the domain of competitive aggressiveness and perceived physical abilities, in line with existing literature [[92], [93], [94], [95]], but see also [96] attributing these differences mostly to genetic components [97] and environmental factors [98]. Although our study does not address the underlying reasons for self-efficacy, other researchers have suggested that the amount of mastery opportunities and external support (e.g., verbal) contributes to the quality of female experience and, consequently, their self-efficacy beliefs [95,99]. Therefore, females’ lower scores in self-efficacy might stem from fewer mastery experiences and less support in sport and exercise. Nevertheless, these differences in physical ability SE seem to disappear when SE is task-focused, suggesting that task-focused SE might not be affected by the same factors as global SE.

Interestingly, males reported higher scores in negative emotion regulation SE than females. Previous works have reported females using more emotional regulation, being more internally focused, and engaging in more rumination [100,101]. Males might be motivated to choose emotion regulation strategies, which attempt to control or change the situation that may be giving rise to the emotion they wish to regulate, such as problem-solving and reappraisal [101]. Yet, Nolen-Hoeksema and Aldao [102] found that, while females reported using emotion regulation strategies more than males, these differences were lost when emotion suppression was accounted for, indicating a moderating effect of strategy selection.

Study 2 also investigated the relationship between anger, SE in regulating negative emotions, and task-focused SE. The results showed that SE in regulating negative emotions partially mediates the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE. In other words, athletes' beliefs about their ability to regulate their negative emotions might drive them to view unpleasant emotions as functional to their performance and athletic goals. Consistent with the individual zones of optimal functioning (IZOF) model, emotional components of performance processes may be experienced with differential hedonic valence (i.e., pleasant and unpleasant), and may support functional or dysfunctional effects on performance processes and outcomes [103,104]. As emotions may shape one's thoughts and actions, people may be driven to experience certain emotions to optimize performance [105]. Consequently, cognitive and behavioral performance motives might prompt athletes to gravitate towards emotion regulation strategies that support instrumental (i.e., functional) interpretations of unpleasant emotions [24,105]. Although our study did not directly address this, one might make the argument that instrumental motives influence athletes' tendencies to prefer regulation strategies which support functional interpretation of performance-related emotions.

Furthermore, the results argue in favor of previous findings supporting the utility of unpleasant emotions being functional to individual performance and athletic goals [106,107]. As discussed above, both positive and negative emotions can act as affordances to support individually functional athletic behaviors [107]. Moreover, in the context of meta-experiences (i.e., the beliefs that one may have in regards to their past experiences [5,108]), coping may be an inherent part of athletic competition, whether of short or long duration. Hence, the effectiveness of athletes' strategies and their ability to regulate emotions might be influenced by previous experiences and by how these influence the selection and implementations of specific emotion regulation strategies [24,108]. Additionally, research on highly skilled karate athletes showed that emotions were associated with functional and successful performances, regardless of the pleasant or unpleasant emotions' hedonic tone [109]. In agreement with this perspective, our data suggests an instrumental motive in support to functional emotion-regulation, emphasizing an idiosyncratic balance between the hedonic tone of an emotion and its plausible functionality to performance [26]. Accordingly, athletes’ past experiences might reinforce their beliefs on their ability to use emotion-regulation strategies in order to better handle anger in a way that optimizes its effects on task-focused SE.

Taken together, current findings may inform practitioners that general and task-focused SE may affect performance differently. Hence, precise tools are advised to assess their levels in order to correctly understand competitive performance. To this note, the RKSES-IT provides a means to monitor task-focused SE in taekwondo athletes. Moreover, the mediating effect of emotion regulation SE on the relationship between competitive anger and task-focused SE indicates that athletes should aim to optimize emotion regulation strategies to be instrumentally functional to their athletic goals and performance.

Limitations and future directions

Although the present sample emphasizes the implications for highly experienced taekwondo athletes (i.e., >87 % of them held black belts in taekwondo), its specificity may simultaneously also limit the generalizability of the findings. Indeed, as SE levels have been suggested to relate to experience [110], we advise against generalizing the present outcomes to less experienced athletes, and suggest that future studies may address this limitation by expanding their samples to include a more experientially diverse population (i.e., lower belts). Moreover, the cross-sectional approach implemented in the study does not permit to infer causality from our data, and proper methodological approaches should be applied in future investigations to infer causality amongst variables. Additionally, the specific typology of sport see Ref. [111] for an example of classification should be taken into consideration, as embedded sport characteristics might be implicated in athletes’ levels of antisocial tendencies in competitive sport settings [75].

Conclusion

Here, current findings suggest that one's beliefs in their ability to regulate negative emotions can mediate the effects of competitive anger on task-focused SE. Thus, they emphasize the importance of instrumental approaches to emotion regulation in assisting sport-specific SE and, consequently, athletic performance. Moreover, as one's confidence on sport-specific skills may influence performance [10,112,113], it emphasizes the need for assessment tools. To this purpose, the RKSES-IT stands as a quick yet practical tool for athletes, researchers, and practitioners that might be interested in assessing the taekwondo task-focused SE.
