Biliary Stent Placement by Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography: Quality Indicators and Technical Applications
Authors: Yoon Suk Lee, Tae Joo Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has become a pivotal therapeutic option for the management of various biliary disorders. ERCP is an intrinsically complex procedure associated with risks of adverse events, including pancreatitis, bleeding, and perforation; thus, experienced endoscopists must maintain adequate procedural competence. Therefore, endoscopic societies have established quality indicators to ensure procedural safety and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, achieving optimal technical outcomes in biliary stent placement requires a thorough understanding of the appropriate selection and the characteristics of different biliary stents for each clinical situation. Although the most common indication for ERCP is common bile duct stone removal in the setting of acute cholangitis, benign and malignant biliary strictures remain a major focus of clinical research. In this context, this review summarizes the current quality benchmarks proposed by major endoscopic societies and provides an overview of mechanical properties of plastic stents and self-expandable metal stents and their technical considerations in pancreatobiliary disorders that are frequently encountered in clinical practice.Keywords: Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography, Bile ducts, Stents, Biliary tract procedure, Quality indicators

INTRODUCTION

Biliary stent placement via endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has become the standard of care for managing various biliary disorders, including acute calculous cholangitis and obstructive jaundice.1,2 Successful biliary stent placement requires a certain level of procedural competence.3,4 Furthermore, appropriate selection of biliary stents, based on their characteristics, is critical and considerably influences clinical outcomes of stent migration or stent-related complications, such as acute cholecystitis or recurrent cholangitis.5,6 Recent advances in stent design and materials of plastic stents (PS) and self-expandable metal stents (SEMS) have diversified therapeutic options, each with unique mechanical and clinical properties.7,8

ERCP is a technically demanding procedure associated with potentially serious adverse events, including pancreatitis, bleeding, and perforation. International endoscopic societies have established quality indicators to enhance procedural safety and standardization. In South Korea, quality indicators for ERCP have been recently published by the Korean Pancreatobiliary Association (KPBA), aligning with previous benchmarks from the American Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) and European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE).9-11 The established quality indicators from major endoscopic societies are summarized to highlight current standards for procedural success and safety.

While ERCP is most frequently performed for common bile duct (CBD) stone removal in patients with acute cholangitis, the management of benign and malignant biliary strictures continues to represent a major indication.12 Therefore, this review outlines current quality indicators proposed by leading endoscopic societies and discusses the mechanical characteristics of PS and SEMS, with an emphasis on essential technical considerations for biliary stent placement in commonly encountered clinical scenarios. Furthermore, this review, prepared by the Policy-Quality Management Committee of the KPBA, aims to provide a standard educational framework for ERCP endoscopists to implement core principles safely and effectively in their daily practice.

QUALITY INDICATORS FOR BILIARY STENT PLACEMENT

Over the past decade, endoscopic societies have emphasized quality indicators for endoscopic procedures, including upper endoscopy, colonoscopy, and ERCP. In 2015, the ASGE introduced comprehensive guidelines on quality indicators for ERCP, establishing key benchmarks for procedural success, including biliary stent placement rates and complication thresholds.9 Subsequently, the ESGE published performance measures on ERCP in 2018, expanding the concept of quality assessment to include both technical success and patient-centered clinical outcomes, such as symptom resolution and avoidance of repeat procedures.10 Most recently, in 2024, the KPBA proposed quality indicators tailored to the Korean medical environment for medical practice of ERCP while aligning with international standards.11 Collectively, these indicators reflect a global consensus on the need for measurable, evidence-based metrics to evaluate procedural proficiency and safety of ERCP performance (Table 1).

1. ASGE quality indicators (2015)

In 2015, the ASGE defined specific intraprocedural quality indicators for ERCP, emphasizing documentation and procedural success. Among these, biliary stent placement for obstruction in patients with normal anatomy whose obstruction is below the bifurcation was designated as a prior indicator, with a target success rate of ≥90%.9 This benchmark reflects the expectation that competent endoscopists should achieve high rates of successful biliary drainage in standard anatomical conditions. Other indicators include successful deep cannulation, stone extraction, and fluoroscopy time documentation, all of which contribute to assessing the technical quality of ERCP.

2. ESGE performance measures (2018)

In 2018, the ESGE established a structured set of performance measures for ERCP. The measures encompass several domains: (1) pre-procedure, (2) completeness of procedure, (3) identification of pathology, (4) management of pathology, (5) complications, (6) patient experience, and (7) post-procedure.10 Among principal indicators proposed by the ESGE, appropriate stent placement for biliary obstruction serves as a key benchmark. For lesions located below the hepatic hilum, the recommended target success rate is ≥95%.10 This indicator underscores the necessity of precise stent positioning in appropriate locations to ensure adequate biliary drainage.

3. KPBA quality indicators (2024)

In 2024, the KPBA introduced quality indicators for ERCP to harmonize domestic clinical practice with international standards reflecting the unique procedural environment in Korea. The quality indicators of KPBA maintained the benchmark of ≥90% successful biliary stent placement in patients with normal anatomy and obstruction below the bile duct bifurcation,11 consistent with the ASGE recommendations.10 However, the Korean guidelines placed additional emphasis on structured reporting, assessing procedural difficulty, and competency-based credentialing to enhance the safety of ERCP performance.11

TECHNICAL ASPECTS OF BILIARY STENTING

1. Equipment and technique

Biliary stent placement during ERCP requires a combination of precise endoscopic technique, appropriate biliary stent selection, and anatomical understanding of each patient.1,13 Furthermore, to minimize procedure-related complications, optimal stent sizing and proper stent material fitted to the target lesion are crucial, and these factors influence clinical outcomes. The procedure for biliary drainage typically begins with deep biliary cannulation using a standard catheter or papillotome preloaded with a guidewire. A radiopaque guidewire is first advanced beyond the stricture to secure access, and the stent delivery system is advanced over the wire, which generally consists of a plastic guiding catheter and a pushing catheter of identical diameter, which can be preassembled or used separately. Inserting thin PS 7 Fr directly over a guidewire is an alternative approach. The use of radiopaque contrast during cholangiography facilitates delineation of stricture length and position. However, in cases of high risk of cholangitis after biliary stenting, such as hilar or intrahepatic biliary stricture, air cholangiography may be a useful alternative for preventing post-ERCP cholangitis.14-16

2. Lesion length measures and stent length

Accurate measurement of the length of target lesion is essential to ensure biliary decompression and avoid mechanical injury or other complications from stent migration.17 In general, the stent should extend 1 to 2 cm above the proximal margin of the stricture and approximately 1 cm into the duodenal lumen to prevent migration and ensure drainage.13,18 Excessive intraduodenal protrusion should be avoided, as it can increase the risk of mucosal injury, ulceration, or perforation when spontaneous migration occurs into the duodenum. On the other hand, short placement may result in premature dysfunction or dislodgement.19

Regarding the technical aspect, the length of target lesion is usually measured under fluoroscopic guidance. The distance from the proximal end of the obstruction to the duodenal papilla can be measured using a catheter by withdrawing the catheter from the targeted location to the papilla.20 Additionally, the diameter of an endoscope shaft can be used as a reference for comparing it with the target lesion. For distal CBD strictures, stents ranging from 7 to 9 cm are usually sufficient to cover the biliary stricture. Furthermore, there is a technical consideration when deploying SEMS, i.e., the phenomenon of stent shortening. SEMS are loaded in a constrained, elongated state within the delivery catheter. Upon deployment, as the stent expands to its functional diameter, it naturally shortens. The reported shortening rate typically ranges from 20% to 30%. This substantial shortening means that the deployed length is shorter than the constrained length visible within the catheter. However, the laser-cut stent (Zilver Biliary Stent; Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) does not have shortening phenomenon. Consequently, the endoscopist must accurately estimate this shortening effect and position the delivery system precisely, especially the proximal end of the stent, to ensure the expanded stent appropriately covers the entire length of the stricture without being deployed too far proximally or distally.

STENT TYPE: PLASTIC VS METAL STENTS

Biliary stents are broadly classified into PS and SEMS. PS and SEMS differ significantly in their specifications, patency, and cost (Table 2).21 The size of PS is standardized using the Fr catheter scale, where 1 Fr is equivalent to approximately 0.33 mm in diameter. Commonly used PSs for ERCP typically range from 7 to 11.5 Fr. Unlike PS, the deployed diameter of a SEMS is significantly larger, commonly reaching up to 10 mm, which corresponds to 30 Fr. This major difference in diameter translates into a substantial increase in the cross-sectional area and thus, greater luminal patency. The internal cross-sectional area of a 10 mm diameter SEMS is approximately nine times larger than that of a standard 10 Fr PS and 18 times larger than a 7 Fr PS. This dramatically larger lumen size is the primary reason SEMS offer a significantly longer patency period compared to PS. In general, SEMS offer an expected duration of 6 to 9 months, whereas PS typically lasts for 3 to 4 months.22,23 Securing the appropriate diameter is essential for clinicians to select the appropriate stent size to match the diameter of the bile duct and the size constraints of the endoscope’s working channel. PS primarily malfunction due to clogging and migration; SEMS are prone to tumor ingrowth/overgrowth, and migration. Particularly, regarding stent migration, covered SEMS (cSEMS) is known to be more frequent than uncovered SEMS.24 Tumor ingrowth refers to the scenario where cancer tissue grows inward through the mesh of the uncovered stent, obstructing the lumen from within the stent itself (Fig. 1A), and tumor overgrowth occurs when the cancer grows at the proximal to distal ends of the stent, typically proliferating over the edge of the stent and obstructing the bile duct lumen from outside the stent (Fig. 1B). Therefore, cSEMS are designed to mitigate these issues, offering a barrier to prevent tumor ingrowth, though they may still be susceptible to tumor overgrowth at both ends. From a resource perspective, PS are characterized by a low cost and are easily exchangeable, whereas SEMS entail a higher cost and are generally more difficult to remove, except for cSEMS which allow for possible exchange. Regarding fully cSEMS, stent removal is usually successful when indwelled for up to 6 months; however, the removal success rate decreases to approximately 85% when the indwelling duration extends to 10–12 months.25,26 The inability to remove a full cSEMS is related to tissue overgrowth from mucosal hyperplasia or proximal migration, which can lead to serious potential implications. In case of tumor overgrowth or ingrowth, stent-in-stent method as a rescue technique can be helpful to treat hyperplastic tissue hindering the stent removal.25,27 Therefore, according to the American College of Gastroenterology guidelines, while full cSEMS placement for benign strictures is considered relatively safe for up to 6 months, a scheduled exchange every 6 months is recommended if a prolonged duration of stenting is required.28

The decision to use PS versus SEMS depends on the underlying pathology, anticipated duration of stent patency, patient’s expected survival, and cost-effectiveness. PS is generally preferred for short-term drainage in benign conditions or bridge to surgery. It has several stent configurations, including straight, half pigtail or pigtail design, usually ranging from 7 to 10 Fr in diameter and 5 to 12 cm in length. SEMS, on the other hand, is preferred for malignant biliary obstruction due to its larger luminal diameter (8 to 10 mm) and longer stent patency, and is categorized as uncovered, partially covered, or fully covered, depending on whether the mesh is encased in a polymer coating that prevents tumor ingrowth. Recently, lumen-apposing metal stents have been designed for transluminal drainage.

1. Mechanical properties of SEMS: AF vs RF

The performance and safety profile of SEMS in clinical practice are highly dependent on their distinct mechanical properties, necessitating careful selection based on the patient's anatomy and pathology. The mechanical properties of SEMS depend on their structure, wire thickness, and stent material.29 Two crucial properties are the axial force (AF) and the radial force (RF).30,31 AF is the straightening force on the central axis of the stent, and RF is the expanding force that induces radial deformation of the stent (Fig. 2). High AF may be associated with adverse events like biliary kinking, cholecystitis, and pancreatitis.32,33 The AF of a SEMS impacts its ability to conform to the natural curvature of the bile duct. When a SEMS with high AF is deployed in the bile duct with sharp angulation or tortuous anatomy, it can lead to loss of biliary conformability and potentially cause complications like kinking due to the strong straightening force. Conversely, a SEMS with low AF is more flexible, allowing it to maintain the native, curved path of the bile duct (Fig. 3). In cases where the bile duct exhibits severe angulation, selecting a SEMS with lower AF is preferred, thereby reducing the risk of complications such as cholecystitis or pancreatitis. Conversely, a weak RF could lead to mal-expansion or stent dislodgement.34 Accordingly, endoscopists must choose a specific SEMS type with a balanced AF and RF profile to optimize for the specific anatomical location and disease type to achieve effective drainage while minimizing stent-related complications.

CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF BILIARY STENT PLACEMENT

The strategies of biliary stent placement are determined by the etiology and anatomical location of the obstruction. The anatomical classification of biliary tract is categorized into intrahepatic, perihilar and distal extrahepatic bile duct, and the etiology falls into two major groups: benign and malignant.28,35 While malignant stricture includes cholangiocarcinoma (intrahepatic, hilar, or distal) to pancreatic head cancer and gallbladder cancer, benign conditions commonly include primary sclerosing cholangitis, chronic pancreatitis (CP), and post-liver transplantation strictures (Table 3).28,36 Furthermore, appropriate biliary stent placement is also critical for managing complications, including post-cholecystectomy bile leakage or stricture, and facilitating the eventual clearance of incomplete CBD stone removal,1,13 while biliary stent placement is frequently employed in the palliative management of malignant obstruction.

1. Incomplete clearance of CBD stones

ERCP has become the primary treatment for CBD stone removal. However, in a subset of stones referred to as difficult biliary stones, complete clearance is challenging using only basic ERCP techniques, thereby necessitating alternative or supplementary measures.12 Difficult biliary stones account for approximately 10% to 15% of all CBD stones.38 When complete stone extraction fails at the first session of ERCP, temporary biliary stenting serves as an effective bridge therapy. The difficulty in stone clearance is related to the stone itself or the patient’s anatomy.38 Stone-related factors include a large diameter (>15 mm), multiplicity, location within intrahepatic or cystic duct, or unusual barrel-shape. Anatomical factors are also related to the difficulty, including acute angulation of the bile duct, the presence of pre-stenotic biliary stricture, or surgically altered anatomy due to previous hepatobiliary surgery.

The ESGE recommends temporary biliary PS placement when CBD stones could not be completely removed.1 After a period of stent indwelling, the PS should be removed at the subsequent ERCP session once CBD stone extraction has been achieved. In cases where definitive stone removal is deferred, scheduled stent exchange is advised within 3 to 6 months.1 This timely exchange or removal of inserted PS is underscored by long-term outcome data, showing that biliary stenting group showed 20.9% of the enrolled patients developed cholangitis over a median duration of 16 weeks, while stone clearance group only developed 13.9%.39 This finding supports the principle that prolonged PS placement leads to a higher rate of infectious complications. Furthermore, in a multicenter study, the use of 7 Fr stents was identified as a strong independent predictive factor for achieving complete clearance at the second ERCP, alongside significant stone size reduction when compared to a 10 Fr stent.40 This suggests that utilizing a 7 Fr PS (often double-pigtail type) is a more effective strategy for pre-treatment of difficult biliary stones (Fig. 4).

2. Post-cholecystectomy and iatrogenic biliary stricture

Post-cholecystectomy bile duct injuries, while uncommon, represent serious complications requiring precise management. The reported incidence ranges from 0.18% to 0.6% after cholecystectomies.41 They are often caused by inappropriate applications of metal clips or thermal damage during the cholecystectomies, leading to bile leakage or bile duct stricture. For the treatment of benign biliary strictures complicating cholecystectomy, the temporary insertion of multiple PSs (MPS) was recommended. The objective of MPS is to provide a cumulative radial dilating force to remodel the strictured area over an extended period,42 typically involving stent exchange every 3-month interval for 12 or more months up to 24 months.43 Recently, a fully covered SEMS (fcSEMS) can be considered as an alternative for strictures located >2 cm from the main hepatic confluence.1,25

For bile leakage that is not due to complete transection of the CBD or hepatic duct, endoscopic drainage is highly effective.44 The duration of PS placement is recommended by ESGE for 4 to 8 weeks. A comparative study did not show the clinical difference between 7 Fr versus 10 Fr PSs in the management of bile leakage. Furthermore, SEMS may be a valuable alternative in the case of refractory bile leakage.

3. CP-related stricture

Biliary stricture is a frequent complication of advanced CP, affecting approximately 10% to 30% of CP patients.25,45 CP-related biliary stricture is usually associated with the parenchymal fibrotic changes of the pancreas. Given the fibrotic features of CP-related biliary stricture, long-term stenting with multiple PS is required. Furthermore, careful surveillance for malignant transformation is needed. In a multicenter trial focusing on CP-related symptomatic benign strictures, 11 out of 164 enrolled patients (6.7%) were subsequently diagnosed with pancreatic cancer during the follow-up period.26 This finding highlights the necessity of distinguishing between benign and malignant stricture, and the need for continuous surveillance in the CP-related biliary stricture.46 Recently, endoscopic therapy with a single fcSEMS has been reported as an alternative treatment option for MPS. In 2021, a randomized trial by Ramchandani et al.26 demonstrated that fcSEMS achieved similar stricture resolution rates (77% in multiple PS vs 75.8% in fcSEMS) and comparable safety profiles. A procedural advantage of fcSEMS is that it requires fewer ERCP procedures over the treatment course compared to MPS (3.9 procedures in MPS vs 2.6 in fcSEMS).26 Therefore, the ESGE suggests the temporary insertion of MPS or fcSEMS for treating benign biliary strictures related to CP.1

4. Suprapapillary versus across the papilla stenting for hilar biliary stricture

Recently, interest has grown in suprapapillary stent placement, in which the distal end of the stent is positioned above the papilla rather than across it.47 This configuration prevents exposure of the stent lumen to duodenal contents, thereby reducing reflux of intestinal fluid and bacterial contamination. This strategy is designed to achieve longer patency than conventional PSs placed across the papilla. A recent single-center retrospective study by Uemura et al.,48 evaluated 106 patients with unresectable malignant hilar biliary obstruction who underwent ERCP-guided drainage using either suprapapillary PS (SPPS) or SEMS. This study revealed that while uncovered SEMS achieved a longer time to recurrent biliary obstruction (SEMS 211 days vs SPPS 149 days), SPPS resulted in a significantly better lifelong biliary drainage success rate and a longer time to lifelong biliary drainage failure (95.0% vs 54.2% at 500 days). Furthermore, there were no cases in which the SPPS could not be removed. The authors concluded that SPPS may be preferable as an initial drainage strategy for unresectable malignant hilar biliary obstruction, especially in patients expected to undergo repeated reinterventions or receive systemic therapy. The clinical advantage of SPPS is primarily attributed to its removability, which preserves further therapeutic options such as conversion surgery or subsequent advanced interventions, especially considering the rising cases of conversion surgery with use of advanced chemotherapeutic agents and immune checkpoints inhibitors for biliary tract cancer.

CONCLUSIONS

ERCP-guided biliary stenting remains a high-value, continuously evolving procedure in managing various pancreatobiliary diseases. Therefore, endoscopists performing ERCP should be familiar with established quality indicators and continuously cultivate a high level of procedural competence to meet the current standards. Furthermore, successful biliary stent placement necessitates appropriate stent type to the specific pathology and location. Moreover, endoscopic biliary stenting demands careful consideration of stent material characteristics and optimization of SEMS AF and RF balance for individual anatomical features. Thus, endoscopists must integrate technical skills and material stent properties with meticulous evidence-based strategies individualized to each patient.
