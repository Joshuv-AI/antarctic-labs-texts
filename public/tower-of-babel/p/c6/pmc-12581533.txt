IGF1-mediated mesenchymal-endothelial transition as a potential regulatory target in calcific aortic valve disease
Authors: Binglin Li, Mi Zhou, Jianghong Guo, Tai Kuang, Tao Lyu, Dan Zhu, Xiaoyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic valve interstitial cells (VICs) are considered a highly plastic heterogeneous mesenchymal cell population. Although previous studies have demonstrated their potential to differentiate into myofibroblasts, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes, their ability to differentiate into endothelial cells has not been confirmed. Notably, normal aortic valve tissue is avascular, but in the diseased valves of patients with calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD), we often find cells with an endothelial phenotype, but the origin of these cells is unclear.MethodsWe performed paraffin section immunofluorescence analysis on calcified aortic valves from 16 patients with CAVD. Primary VICs and valve endothelial cells (VECs) were isolated from normal porcine aortic valves. Endothelial cell-related functional assays, including in vivo Matrigel plug assay and in vitro tube formation assay, demonstrated the acquisition of endothelial functions by VICs. Transcriptome sequencing, western blot, qPCR, and immunofluorescence were used to identify the molecular mechanisms underlying mesenchymal-endothelial transition (MEndT). We constructed a CAVD mouse model by wire injury using Col1a2-CreERT: R26R-tdTomato mice. Finally, our results were further validated using publicly available single-cell sequencing data related to CAVD.ResultsWe identified a rarely discussed population of CD31-positive and α-SMA-negative non-vascular structural cells in the calcified aortic valves. Following osteogenic stimulation, we demonstrated through in vitro and in vivo experiments that VICs showed increased expression of endothelial-associated markers and acquired endothelial cell-related functions. We demonstrated that the IGF1-PI3K-AKT-HIF pathway mediates MEndT. Furthermore, in the CAVD mouse model, we confirmed that intraperitoneal injection of recombinant mouse IGF1 protein significantly increased the proportion of endothelial cells of mesenchymal origin in the aortic valve and alleviate disease progression; conversely, intraperitoneal administration of the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 reversed this effect. Analysis of single-cell sequencing data from two different CAVD datasets revealed that as valve interstitial cells differentiate toward an osteogenic lineage, they also enrich biological processes related to endothelial cell function.ConclusionsWe provide the first evidence that VICs have the capability to undergo endothelial transition under calcific conditions and that MEndT occurs in aortic valve of the CAVD mouse model. These findings reveal the presence of MEndT in CAVD, offering a potential therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04433-z.Keywords: Calcific aortic valve disease, Mesenchymal-endothelial transition, IGF1

Background

Aortic valve calcific disease (CAVD) is a common cardiovascular valve disorder characterized by aortic valve stenosis, thickening, and leaflet calcification [1, 2]. This disease is closely associated with widespread inflammation and cell differentiation occurring in a mechanically active environment [3]. Currently, the pathological mechanisms are not fully understood, and there is a lack of effective drug treatments, with surgery being the only viable therapeutic option.

The normal aortic valve is avascular tissue, consisting mainly of valve endothelial cells on the valve surface and valve interstitial cells in the interstitial layer [4]. Numerous studies have identified a large number of endothelial phenotype-positive cells in calcified aortic valves from CAVD patients, and these cells have been found to play an important role in disease progression. Several studies have found the occurrence of neovascularization in aortic valve leaflets derived from CAVD patients [5, 6], and neovessels in the diseased valve may affect disease progression of CAVD through intraleaflet hemorrhage, infiltration of inflammatory cells and metabolites of red blood cells [7, 8]. Furthermore, many studies have successfully induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) of valve endothelial cells through in vitro stimulation with inflammatory factors [9–11] and alterations in cyclic strain [12] and found that endothelial cells undergoing EndMT can participate in extracellular matrix remodeling. Thus, it is speculated that cells positive for both endothelial (CD31) and mesenchymal (α-SMA) markers found beneath the calcified valve endothelium represent endothelial cells undergoing EndMT. However, there is a lack of studies systematically and comprehensively analyzing the phenotypic characteristic and cellular origin of endothelial phenotype-positive cells in the entire calcified aortic valve derived from CAVD patients.

Aortic valve interstitial cells (VICs) are considered a heterogeneous population of mesenchymal cells, including fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells, which are involved in maintenance of the extracellular matrix and have a significant impact on the occurrence and development of CAVD [1]. VICs exhibit significant plasticity, with at least five distinct phenotypes: progenitor mesenchymal/endothelial cells, VIC progenitors, activated (myofibroblast) VICs, quiescent VICs, and osteoblastic VICs [13]. Studies have shown that VICs have the potential to differentiate into myofibroblasts, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes [14, 15]. However, whether VICs possess the ability to differentiate into endothelial cells has not yet been discovered. Additionally, several literatures indicate that both mesenchymal stem cells [16, 17] and fibroblasts [18–20] can be reprogrammed into endothelial cells in vitro, and subsequently, the role of mesenchymal-to-endothelial transition (MEndT) has been confirmed in various pathological processes. For example, in a mouse myocardial infarction model, resident fibroblasts can promote heart neovascularization [21] and myocardial repair [22] through mesenchymal-to-endothelial transition. Meng et al. also provided evidence of mesenchymal-endothelial transition through lineage tracing in a mouse hindlimb ischemia model [23]. However, whether VICs, particularly in the context of CAVD, possess the ability to transdifferentiate into endothelial cells remain unclear.

Therefore, the aims of this study are (1) to comprehensively analyze the phenotypic characteristic and distribution of endothelial phenotype-positive cells in the complete aortic valve leaflets derived from patients with CAVD and (2) to investigate whether VICs, in a calcified environment, may have the potential to differentiate into an endothelial phenotype and explore the relevant molecular mechanisms.

Methods

Isolation and culture of primary aortic valve cells (porcine and human)

Isolation

Porcine primary aortic valve interstitial cells (pVICs) and valve endothelial cells (pVECs) were extracted with slight modifications from previously described methods [24]. In brief, the aortic valves were harvested after euthanizing healthy 6-month-old pigs by intravenous injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) and immediately placed into a 35-mm culture dish containing 3 ml of collagenase solution (600 U/mL Collagenase Type II, 10% FBS), then incubated at 37 °C for 7 min. The valve endothelial layer (pVECs) was gently removed by rotating sterile cotton swabs on the valve surface. The collagenase II solution containing the endothelial cells was collected and centrifuged. After discarding the supernatant, the cells were resuspended in 3 ml of complete medium, centrifuged again to discard the supernatant, and finally resuspended in 5 ml of complete medium before being seeded into a collagen-coated T25 flask. The valve was then transferred to a new 35-mm culture dish containing pre-chilled Collagenase Type II solution, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and any remaining endothelial cells were wiped off the valve with sterile cotton swabs. After washing the remaining valve tissue with PBS, it was immediately placed in a 15-ml centrifuge tube containing 10 ml of collagenase solution, incubated at 37 °C for 15 h. The cells were collected and seeded into T75 flasks to obtain valve interstitial cells (pVICs). To ensure the absence of residual endothelial cells within pVICs, anti-CD31 flow cytometric sorting was performed once the cells reached confluency, selecting for CD31-negative cells for further culture to ensure the purity of pVICs. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Chest Hospital (No. KS(Y)22200), and in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used for experimental purposes. In parallel, primary human aortic valve interstitial cells (hVICs) and endothelial cells (hVECs) were obtained from macroscopically normal valve tissues collected during heart transplantation. The extraction procedure followed similar principles as described above. All experiments were conducted with informed consent and approved by the Human Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Chest Hospital (No. KS(Y)22200).

Culture

Both pVICs/pVECs and hVICs/hVECs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, Fetal Bovine Serum, Premium Plus, Australian origin, A56697010) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (complete culture medium, GM), used between passages 2 and 6. Osteogenic differentiation of pVICs/hVICs was induced using an osteogenic differentiation medium (OM, Sciencell, 7531). For the pathway inhibition assays, pVICs were pre-treated for 48 h with complete medium containing PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (20 µm), Akt inhibitor Perifosine (10 µm), HIF-1α inhibitor KC7F2 (10 µm), and DMSO (20 µm) prior to induction with OM. In drug stimulation experiments, IGF1 protein (100 ng/ml) and IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 (1 µm) were added to GM and OM, respectively, to assess the cellular response.

Flow cytometry cell sorting

Flow cytometry cell sorting was utilized to isolate CD31-negative cells from primary porcine valvular interstitial cells, ensuring the absence of residual CD31-positive endothelial cells within the valvular interstitial cell population. In brief, harvested primary porcine valvular interstitial cells were resuspended in DPBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum and incubated with APC-conjugated anti-CD31 antibody (Invitrogen, MA5-16769) at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by three DPBS washes before sorting with a BD FACS AriaIII system.

Immunofluorescence staining

Multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemical staining

This study collected valvular samples from 16 patients who underwent aortic valve replacement surgery due to calcific aortic valve disease. Exclusion criteria included rheumatic aortic valvulopathy, congenital valve disease, and infective endocarditis. All the studies involving humans complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Chest Hospital (No. KS(Y)22200). All patients have signed informed consent. The collected aortic valve calcified leaflets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with 10% EDTA solution, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned continuously (4 µm). The staining procedure was conducted according to the instructions of the kit (abs50012, absin, China). In brief, paraffin sections were baked in a 65 °C oven for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in a graded series of ethanol, subjected to antigen retrieval in a low heat solution for 20 min, blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed 3 × with PBS, incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, washed 3 × with PBS, incubated with TSA dye at room temperature for 30 min, and then the antigen retrieval step was repeated for subsequent antibodies, with the final step being mounting with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI. The primary antibodies included the following: anti-CD31 (novus, NB100-2284, 1:100), anti-a-SMA (CST, 19245S, 1:100), anti-COL1A2 (proteintech, 14,695–1-AP, 1:100), and anti-IGF1 (proteintech, 28,530–1-AP, 1:50). All samples were scanned with the Vectra multispectral imaging system (Vectra 3) for full-slide multispectral analysis, with the number of CD31-positive cells and vessels within the valve manually quantified. CD31-positive cells were quantified only when a corresponding DAPI-positive nucleus was present within the same cell boundary. High-magnification imaging was performed using a KEYENCE microscope (BZ-X800) and a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710). To construct three-dimensional images, 20-µm sections were prepared and scanned along the Z-axis using a confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells

Cells were seeded onto coverslips, fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h, mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium containing DAPI, and imaged using a confocal microscope. The primary antibodies included as follows: anti-CD31 (novus, NB100-65336, 1:100), anti-CDH5 (abcam, ab33168, 1:100), anti-α-SMA (abcam, ab240654, 1:100), and anti-Vimentin (abcam, ab92547, 1:100). The percentage of CD31⁺ and CDH5⁺ cells was calculated as the number of positively stained cells relative to total DAPI⁺ nuclei. For each sample, five randomly selected fields at × 100 magnification were analyzed, and the average value was used. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ.

Alizarin red stain

After dewaxing and rehydrating the paraffin sections, 2% Alizarin Red S Stain Solution (Sciencell, 0223) was applied onto the valve tissue, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 min, then washed three times with deionized water, and after drying, the sections were coverslipped with a mounting medium. After staining, all sections were scanned in their entirety using a digital pathology scanner (OCUS). And the area of calcification in the human aortic valve tissue was analyzed and calculated using ImageJ software.

qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell samples using the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R701). Following RNA extraction, reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR kit (Vazyme, R223). Subsequent PCR reactions were conducted on the PCR instrument (ABI Q.DX) with the reverse-transcribed products, using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔct method. The primer sequences used in the PCR reactions are shown in Additional file 1: Tables S1.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted by lysing cells with RIPA buffer (Sigma), followed by ultrasonication. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0009). Forty microgram of protein samples from each group was separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes, followed by blocking with 5% milk at room temperature for 1 h and incubation with the primary antibody overnight, washed three times with TBST, and then incubated with DyLight-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h prior to imaging. ImageJ software is used to quantitative analysis.

Scratch assay

Cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and allowed to grow to confluence. Then, a sterile pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the cell monolayer. Afterward, the wells were washed three times with DMEM and filled with the appropriate medium. Cells were incubated at 37 °C and photographed at the same location at 0, 12, and 24 h. The migration area at each time point was analyzed using ImageJ software to determine the percentage of the scratch area covered by the cells.

Tube formation assay

For the two-dimensional (2D) assay, each well of a 48-well culture plate was coated with 80 μL of Matrigel (Corning, 356234) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gelation. Treated cells were then seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 8 h at 37 °C. Tube formation was assessed by capturing images of three randomly selected fields per well under a phase-contrast microscope (× 10 objective). The total tube length was quantified using the Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin in ImageJ software.

For the three-dimensional (3D) assay, 5 × 104 treated cells were resuspended in 80 μL of Matrigel and embedded into a 3D cell scaffold (Engineering For Life, EFL-SCR-3D-48–1–12). The constructs were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify, and then transferred into a 48-well plate containing complete medium for continued culture over 3 days. After fixation, the gel blocks were subjected to immunofluorescence staining for CD31, followed by three-dimensional imaging using a confocal microscope.

Dil-ac-LDL uptake assay

To assess the endothelial function of the cells, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 h in complete medium containing 50 µg/mL Dil-Ac-LDL, followed by three washes with DMEM, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, incubated with DAPI stain at room temperature for 10 min, and after three PBS washes, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. Quantification was performed by calculating the average fluorescence intensity of Dil-ac-LDL using ImageJ software.

In vivo Matrigel plug assay

To test the ability of the cells to form blood vessels in vivo, an in vivo Matrigel plug assay was performed. Five-week-old NOD-SCID mice were used to avoid immune rejection. A total of 1× 10⁶ cells (e.g., VICs, VECs, or cells after induction) were suspended in 200 μL of cold, growth factor-reduced Matrigel Matrix (Corning, 356234), and injected subcutaneously into the abdominal region. A blank control group received Matrigel mixed with an equal volume of PBS. After 1 week, Matrigel plugs were excised, fixed in 10% formalin overnight, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Five sections from each plug were randomly selected for histological analysis. Vessel density was quantified per section per plug. Each data point represents 1 plug from 1 mouse.

Live cell tracking

To assess the homogeneity between OM-induced pVICs and pVECs, we co-cultured the two types of cells. To distinguish between the two different cell types within the same system, we employed live-cell tracing dyes to label the cells with distinct fluorescence. We labeled OM-induced pVICs with CellTracker™ Red CMTPX dye (ThermoFisher, C34552) and pVECs with CellTracker™ Green CMFDA (ThermoFisher, C2925). Following the instructions, the dye was prepared into a stock solution (10 mM), then diluted to the working concentration (25 µm) with serum-free DMEM, pre-warmed in the cell culture incubator for 30 min, and subsequently added to the cell culture dish to incubate at 37° for 45 min before removing the supernatant. The cells were then used for subsequent immunofluorescence staining and in vitro 3D tube formation assays.

siRNA transfection

Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates 1 day prior to transfection. For siRNA transfection, the transfection reagent INTERFERin (Polyplus) was mixed with either IGF1-specific siRNA or negative control siRNA (si-NC), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being added to the cells in complete medium to achieve a final siRNA concentration of 50 nM. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 4 days, after which knockdown efficiency was assessed by qPCR prior to subsequent experiments. The siRNA sequences used are listed in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Chest Hospital (No. KS(Y)22200), and in accordance with the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU for the protection of animals used for experimental purposes. All mice were of a C57BL/6 background, and the Col1a2-CreERT: R26RtdTomato mice was reported previously [21, 22]. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment, and treatment/assessment order was randomized to minimize potential confounders. Tamoxifen (1 mg, Sigma-Aldrich) was administrated daily via intraperitoneal injection for 10 consecutive days to induce Cre-mediated recombination in Col1a2-CreERT mice. Five days after the cessation of tamoxifen, the animals were subjected to aortic valve wire injury. A total of 41 mice were used in this study (n = 8 per group). The experimental unit was a single animal. The sample size was based on our preliminary experiments and previous studies using similar models, which indicated that this number was sufficient to detect significant differences in aortic valve hemodynamics. Mice that died during or immediately after surgery were excluded from analysis. No animals were excluded for other reasons. All exclusion criteria were pre-established. Mice were randomly selected and allocated into treatment groups. Investigators performing echocardiographic measurements and histological analysis were blinded to group allocation. The ARRIVE guidelines were followed (Additional file 2).

Aortic valve wire injury mouse model and echocardiography

Male Col1a2-CreERT : R26R-tdTomato mice aged 8 weeks were used. The aortic valve wire injury (AVWI) mouse model was conducted according to previously described [25–27] procedures with minor modifications. Briefly, the tip of the wire was first bent at a 15° angle. Under 2.5% isoflurane anesthesia, the common carotid artery was exposed, and the wire was inserted through it to reach the aortic root. Under ultrasound guidance, the wire was passed through the aortic valve, and then it was moved back and forth 300 times and rotated 300 times at the aortic valve orifice. Subsequently, the wire was removed, the carotid artery was ligated, the skin was closed, and the wound was disinfected. Sham-surgery was performed in the same way but with no wire insertion into the left ventricle. To investigate the effects of IGF1 and the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 on aortic valve injury, mice that underwent aortic valve wire injury surgery were randomly divided into three groups. Each group received intraperitoneal injections every 2 days with either saline (100 μl), recombinant mouse IGF1 protein (1 μM, 100 μl; MCE, HY-P7070), or BMS-536924 (1 mg; MCE, HY-10262). 8 weeks post-surgery, the mice were assessed by transthoracic echocardiography to evaluate the blood flow velocity and diameter of the aortic valve orifice under 2.5% isoflurane anesthesia. More specifically, the blood flow velocity at the aortic valve orifice was measured through the suprasternal notch view, and the diameter of the aortic valve orifice was assessed using the aortic arch view. Finally, the hearts of the mice were collected for paraffin embedding after euthanizing mice by cervical dislocation. For immunostaining, 5-μm paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. tdTomato and CD31 were visualized using immunofluorescence staining with anti-tdTomato (CST, 20,163, 1:100) and anti-CD31 antibodies (novus, NB100-2284, 1:100), respectively. All remaining steps were performed as described in “ Immunofluorescence staining”. After staining, an autofluorescence quenching reagent (Serviebio, G1221) was applied to the sections to minimize background signals. The percentage of tdTomato-labeled cells expressing CD31 was calculated based on the double-positive area within the aortic valve leaflet region using ImageJ software. The thickness of the aortic valve leaflet was quantified by measuring the vertical distance across the leaflet at five evenly distributed positions along its length [28]. The values were averaged to obtain the overall leaflet thickness for each sample. Measurements were performed using ImageJ software.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

The RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, R223) was used to extract total RNA from the target samples. The obtained RNA underwent quality assessment through Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent, G2991A) electrophoresis and quantification using Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33216)/NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, NDONE). VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, NR602) was employed for library construction, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed mRNA libraries were quality-checked using Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33216) and Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent, G2991A). Qualified libraries underwent Illumina sequencing with a PE150 strategy. The obtained data were successively processed using trim_galore (version 0.6.4) to filter out low-quality and short reads, bowtie2 (version 2.4.2) to remove rRNA, and hisat2 (version 2.2.0) for alignment against the pig reference genome. RNA quantification was performed using htseq-count (version 0.13.5). Subsequently, the expression counts and FPKM matrix were analyzed for expression differences and significance based on grouping information using the R package edgeR. The differentially expressed genes (Q0.05FC2) were subjected to GO and KEGG analysis using the clusterProfiler package, employing Fisher’s exact test.

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis

We separately obtained previously published single-cell transcriptomic data from aortic valves with normal anatomical structures (tricuspid aortic valves) [29] and those with bicuspid valve deformities [30], sourced from patients with calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD). We mapped the sequencing libraries to the human genome (version GRCH38) using the cellranger toolkit (v7.2.0). Subsequently, each sample was filtered based on mitochondrial gene expression and gene counts, and doubletFinder was employed to remove doublets. To mitigate the impact of batch effects, we utilized SCTransform in conjunction with IntegrateLayers for data integration, as determined by the R package SeuratV5. Basic analysis, following the official tutorials, involved performing UMAP dimensionality reduction and cell clustering using the top 30 principal components (PCs). The resolution was set to 0.6 based on the clustering results from clustertree. Cell types were classified based on significant gene expression differences between each cell cluster and other clusters, considering known marker genes and conservatively expressed genes. Further clustering of valvular interstitial cells utilized the same parameters. Single-cell trajectory analysis was conducted using Monocle2 (version 2.30.0). Enrichment analysis was performed using the R package ClusterProfiler (version 4.10.0).

Statistical analysis

Most statistical data and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) of the experiments. ANOVA non-parametric Kruskall–Wallis multiple comparison test with post hoc Dunn’s test was used for comparison of multiple groups or a two-sided Student’s t test with Welch’s correction. The association between two variables was evaluated using two-tailed Pearson correlation analysis. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The symbols in the figures represent: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Identification of CD31-positive cells in calcified aortic valve tissues

CD31 is one of the most classical markers of endothelial cells, while α-SMA is one of the markers for myofibroblasts and endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) [31]. To comprehensively analyze the phenotypic characteristic and distribution of endothelial phenotype-positive cells in the complete aortic valve leaflets from patients with CAVD, we performed multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemical staining for CD31 and α-SMA on calcified aortic valve leaflets from 16 patients undergoing aortic valve replacement surgery. Additionally, we conducted whole-slide scanning analysis and adjacent section staining with Alizarin Red to quantify the area fraction of calcific lesions in different valve tissue sections to assess the degree of calcification (Fig. 1A,B; Additional File 3: Figure S1-S2).

The complete fluorescence images of multiple immunofluorescence staining reveal two forms of CD31-positive cells in the calcified valve leaflets: tubular structures (resembling blood vessel structures) (Fig. 1D*) and non-tubular structures (Fig. 1C–F). To elucidate the relationship between CD31-positive cells within the valve and the degree of valve calcification, we quantified the proportion of non-tubular CD31-positive cells to the total cell count within all calcified aortic valve leaflets, as well as the number of tubular structures formed by CD31-positive cells. Correlation analysis was then performed between these measures and the area percentage of calcified lesions in corresponding adjacent sections, revealing a positive correlation between both measures and the degree of valve calcification (Fig. 1G–I). It is noteworthy that although similar to several studies [9–12], we also observed the presence of CD31 and α-SMA co-positive cells in the sub-endothelial region (Fig. 1E; indicated by yellow arrows), we found that the majority of CD31-positive cells remained in a CD31-positive, α-SMA-negative form (Fig. 1C–F; indicated by white arrows). Furthermore, non-tubular CD31-positive α-SMA-negative cells were not only present in the subendothelial region but also widely distributed in the central area of calcified valves, particularly around neovessels (Fig. 1D, F). Considering α-SMA is also the markers for EndMT, the cell origin of CD31 and α-SMA co-positive cells might originate from endothelial cells undergoing EndMT, as mentioned in multiple studies [9–12]. However, CD31-positive and α-SMA-negative non-tubular cells in calcified valves are rarely discussed. We further examined the expression of mesenchymal marker COL1A2 and unexpectedly found that this group of cells also expressed COL1A2 (Fig. 1J–M). To exclude the possibility that COL1A2 expression is a general feature of valve endothelial cells, we additionally stained healthy human aortic valve tissues. In these samples, endothelial cells on the valve surface were CD31-positive but negative for both α-SMA and COL1A2 (Fig. 1R; indicated by white arrows), and no CD31-positive/α-SMA-negative/COL1A2-positive cells were observed within the valvular interstitial layer (Fig. 1P–R). Therefore, we hypothesize that the CD31-positive and α-SMA-negative non-tubular cells in calcified valves may potentially originate from VICs.

The application of OM can induce mesenchymal-endothelial transition in pVICs

Initially, we rigorously isolated primary VICs (pVICs) and VECs (pVECs) from porcine aortic valve tissue under strict digestion and separation conditions (see “ Methods”). Following successful seeding of pVICs into culture dishes, we further performed flow cytometry to select for CD31-negative cells to ensure the absence of residual endothelial cells (Additional File 3: Figure S3a), and additional comparative experiments were conducted in parallel using pVECs to control for potential effects of contaminating cells. Microscopic observation revealed a spindle-shaped arrangement of pVICs and a cobblestone-like growth pattern of pVECs (Additional File 3: Figure S3b). Immunofluorescence stain demonstrated that the cultured pVICs expressed mesenchymal markers α-SMA and Vimentin, but not endothelial markers CD31 and CDH5; in contrast, pVECs expressed endothelial markers CD31 and CDH5 and were negative for mesenchymal markers α-SMA and Vimentin (Additional File 3: Figure S3c).

Subsequently, pVICs and pVECs were cultured in osteogenic differentiation medium (OM) for 1–14 days. Total mRNA was extracted from cells at each time point to detect the expression of endothelial progenitor cell marker CD34, endothelial markers CD31 and CDH5, and mesenchymal markers α-SMA and COL1A1. The results revealed that OM could promote the expression of endothelial progenitor cell marker CD34 and endothelial markers CD31 and CDH5 in pVICs, with peak expression of CD34 observed on day 4, and peak expression of CD31 and CDH5 on day 8. Concurrently, the expression of mesenchymal markers α-SMA and COL1A1 gradually decreased after day 6, particularly with a significant reduction in α-SMA expression compared to the control group cultured in growth medium (GM). Additionally, while OM significantly increased the expression of α-SMA in pVECs, it also maintained high expression levels of endothelial-related markers (Fig. 2A). To validate the osteogenic effect of OM, pVICs were cultured in OM for 21 days followed by Alizarin Red staining, revealing the formation of calcified nodules (Additional File 3: Figure S3d). The expression of calcification marker Runx2 was detected in pVICs cultured in OM for 1–14 days, demonstrating that OM could promote an increase in Runx2 expression (Additional File 3: Figure S3e). The above results indicate that in the early stages of OM-induced osteogenic differentiation of pVICs, there is an increase in the expression of endothelial-related markers in pVICs, reaching its peak at day 8. Although OM can promote an increase in α-SMA expression in pVECs, it also maintains a high expression state of endothelial-related markers. Therefore, we selected day 8 as the time point to evaluate the changes in endothelial and mesenchymal phenotypes of pVICs and pVECs in response to OM.

We first examined the morphological changes of pVICs and pVECs induced by OM for 8 days. We found that after 8 days of OM induction, the morphology of pVICs changed from the initial spindle-shaped to an endothelial-like cobblestone shape. In contrast, pVECs cultured in GM for 8 days lost their normal endothelial morphology, while after 8 days of OM culture, some cells began to transition to a spindle shape, while others maintained a normal endothelial-like morphology (Additional File 3: Figure S4a). This suggests that pVICs may undergo mesenchymal-endothelial transition (MEndT) in OM, while pVECs can maintain partial endothelial cell status in OM. Subsequently, we further used qPCR to detect that after 8 days of OM induction, the mRNA expression of endothelial markers TIE2, KDR, and vWF in VICs was upregulated (Additional File 3: Figure S4b). Similarly, Western blotting (WB) confirmed the upregulation of CD31 and CDH5 protein expression in VICs after 8 days of OM induction, while the protein expression of the mesenchymal marker α-SMA decreased (p < 0.0001; Fig. 2B; Additional File 3: Figure S5a-c). WB results further demonstrated that OM culture for 8 days could maintain the high expression of endothelial markers CDH5 and CD31 in pVECs, while also promoting α-SMA expression (Fig. 2C; Additional File 3: Figure S5d-f). Immunofluorescence also showed that after 8 days of OM culture, CD31 and CDH5 positive cells appeared in pVICs, and some cells seemed to form tubular structures (Fig. 2D; Additional File 3: Figure S3f). Additionally, multiplex immunofluorescence results indicated that CD31-positive cells in OM-cultured pVICs were COL1A2-positive and α-SMA-negative (Fig. 2E). To further explore the effect of OM on the endothelial cell function of pVECs, we examined the ability of pVECs cultured with OM for 8 days to uptake Dil-ac-LDL, and found that OM could maintain the ability of pVECs to uptake Dil-ac-LDL (Additional File 3: Figure S4c-d). Meanwhile, scratch assays indicated that OM significantly enhanced the migration ability of pVECs (Additional File 3: Figure S4e-f), which may be related to the promotion of α-SMA expression in pVECs by OM. Therefore, these results demonstrate that OM can promote MEndT in pVICs and maintain the endothelial function of pVECs. Based on the above results, we consider that a subpopulation of pVICs cultured in OM for 8 days acquires endothelial-like characteristics. These OM-induced pVICs were used for subsequent phenotypic and functional analyses.

Evidence of functional endothelial properties in OM-induced pVICs

We evaluated the endothelial-related functions of OM-induced pVICs through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. We first performed Dil-ac-LDL uptake and tube formation assays in vitro to assess endothelial function. The experimental results demonstrated that OM-induced pVICs are capable of uptaking Dil-ac-LDL (Fig. 3A). To ensure that functional differences were not due to cytotoxic effects, we first performed a CCK-8 assay to assess cell viability following OM treatment. The results showed no significant differences compared to control cells (Additional File 3: Figure S7c), indicating that the observed functional changes were not attributable to differences in cell viability. Simultaneously, OM-induced pVICs can form tubular structures on Matrigel, similar to pVECs (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3B, Additional File 3: Figure S4g). To further validate the autonomous vasculogenic capacity of OM-induced pVICs, we molded Matrigel containing OM-induced pVICs onto cell scaffolds and, after 5 days of culture in GM, performed immunofluorescence staining for CD31. The three-dimensional images revealed tubular structures within the Matrigel composed of CD31-positive cells (Fig. 3C). Lastly, to assess the homogeneity between OM-induced pVICs and pVECs, we labeled OM-induced pVICs (red) and pVECs (green) with cell tracker dyes and co-cultured them at a 1:1 ratio. Upon confluence, cells were subjected to immunofluorescent staining, revealing the formation of CD31-positive cellular junctions between the two cell types (Fig. 3D). When both cell types were re-suspended in Matrigel and cultured on cell scaffolds in GM, it was found that the OM-induced pVICs could also directly connect with pVECs to form tubular structures (Fig. 3E). Then, we subcutaneously injected Matrigel containing PBS, pVICs, OM-induced pVICs, or pVECs into the abdomen of NOD-SCID mice. One week later, the Matrigel plugs were harvested, and the results showed that both OM-induced pVICs and pVECs implantation induced vessel formation, while pVICs and PBS groups did not induce angiogenesis (p = 0.0064, p < 0.0001; Fig. 3F,G). To further determine the species origin of the endothelial cells forming new blood vessels in the Matrigel plug, we performed immunofluorescence staining using a pig-specific CD31 antibody to detect porcine endothelial cells and TER-119, a marker specific for mouse erythrocytes, to identify red blood cells. Before performing the Matrigel plug assay, we first verified the species specificity of the antibodies used. The pan-species CD31 antibody successfully labeled endothelial cells in both mouse and pig tissues, whereas the pig-specific CD31 antibody produced clear staining only in pig valve endothelium but showed no detectable reactivity in mouse tissues (Additional File 3: Figure S7a). To further validate erythrocyte markers, we performed immunostaining of spleen tissues from both species. Glycophorin A, a pan-erythrocyte marker, labeled red blood cells in both mouse and pig tissues, while TER119 showed reactivity exclusively in mouse erythrocytes but not in pig erythrocytes (Additional File 3: Figure S7b). These results confirm that the pig-CD31 antibody specifically labels porcine endothelial cells without cross-reactivity to murine endothelium and that TER119 is strictly specific for mouse red blood cells. In the Matrigel plug assay, newly formed vessels were positive for pig-specific CD31, supporting the conclusion that these endothelial-like cells originated from OM-induced pVICs undergoing MEndT (Fig. 3H). The above results demonstrate that endothelial cells derived from pVICs undergoing MEndT possess independent vascular formation capability and can coalesce with mature endothelial cells to form tubular structures.

VICs undergo mesenchymal-endothelial transition through the PI3K-Akt pathway and HIF-1 pathway

To explore the pathways through which OM promotes MEndT in pVICs, we extracted RNA from pVICs, OM-induced pVICs, and pVECs for transcriptome sequencing analysis, with four biological replicates per group. Initially, differential expression gene analysis between pVICs vs. OM-induced pVICs and pVICs vs. pVECs revealed 524 and 1874 significantly differentially expressed genes, respectively and nearly half of the significant differentially expressed genes in the pVICs vs. OM-induced pVICs group overlapped with those in the pVICs vs. pVECs group (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses on the differentially expressed genes from the pVICs vs. OM-induced pVICs group and the aforementioned intersecting genes. The results indicated that the two sets of differentially expressed genes were highly consistent in the biological process terms enriched in the GO analysis and were related to endothelial cell function or angiogenesis regulation (Fig. 4B, C). This observation strongly suggests that the intersecting genes may play a key role in the changes related to endothelial cell function or angiogenic development in OM-induced pVICs, meaning they are important in the MEndT process in pVICs. To further illustrate the endothelial characteristics of OM-induced pVICs at the transcriptomic level, we generated a heatmap of representative endothelial-related genes, which showed increased expression in the OM-induced pVICs group (Additional File 3: Figure S7g). Furthermore, to delve deeper into the signaling pathways mediating MEndT, we conducted KEGG analysis on the two sets of differentially expressed genes, which revealed that both gene sets were enriched in the PI3K-Akt and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig. 4D, E). Moreover, these two pathways are closely associated with cell growth, development, and differentiation.

Next, we investigated whether these two pathways were involved in mediating the OM-induced MEndT in pVICs. Initially, we cultured pVICs for 8 days in GM and OM and used Western blotting to detect the expression of PI3K, Akt, P-Akt, and HIF-1α, revealing notably increased expression in the OM group (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p = 0.0001; Fig. 4F, Additional File 3: Figure S5g-i). Concurrently, WB and qPCR results showed that pre-treatment with PI3K phosphorylation inhibitor LY294002, Akt phosphorylation inhibitor Periforsine, and HIF-1 pathway inhibitor KC7F2 significantly inhibited the MEndT process, thus preventing the upregulation of the endothelial markers CDH5 and CD31 induced by OM and promoting the expression of the mesenchymal marker α-SMA (Fig. 4G,H, Additional File 3: Figure S6a-c). Additionally, tube formation assays and Dil-ac-LDL uptake assays indicated that the three pathway inhibitors could prevent the acquisition of endothelial functionality in OM-induced pVICs (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001; Fig. 4I–L). CCK-8 assays showed no significant difference in cell viability after the three pathway inhibitors treatment (Additional File 3: Figure S7d), excluding cytotoxicity as a confounding factor.

IGF1 regulates MEndT through the mediation of the PI3K-Akt and HIF-1 pathway

To identify key molecules in the MEndT process of pVICs promoted by OM, we conducted a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis on the intersection genes mentioned above, ranking them by betweenness centrality, with the top five being SOD2, CCL2, CCN2, IGF1, and COL3A1 (Fig. 5A). We further analyzed the expression of these five protein genes in the OM-induced pVICs group, revealing that SOD2, CCL2, and CCN2 were downregulated, while IGF1 and COL3A1 were significantly upregulated in OM-induced pVICs (Fig. 5B). And IGF1 primarily exerts cellular functions by promoting the phosphorylation activation of the IGF1 receptor (IGF1R), which is associated with cell proliferation [32] and differentiation [33].Therefore, we hypothesize that the IGF1 may be a key factor mediating the MEndT of pVICs. Consequently, we first examined the protein expression of IGF1, IGF1R, and P-IGF1R in OM-induced pVICs via Western blotting, finding increased expression of IGF1 and IGF1R in OM-induced pVICs, as well as enhanced phosphorylation of IGF1-R (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001; Fig. 5C, Additional File 3: Figure S5j-l). The qPCR results also indicated elevated mRNA expression of IGF1 and IGF1R in OM-induced pVICs (Fig. 5D). Thus, it is evident that the IGF1 pathway is activated in OM-induced pVICs.

To further confirm the relationship between IGF1 and MEndT, we cultured pVICs for 8 days in GM and OM supplemented with IGF1 (100 ng/ml) and the IGF1R phosphorylation inhibitor BMS-536924 (1um), respectively. Western blotting for CDH5, CD31, α-SMA, PI3K, Akt, P-Akt, and HIF-1α showed that IGF1 could further promote the expression of endothelial markers (CDH5 and CD31; p = 0.0109, p = 0.0011) and the activation of the PI3K-Akt and HIF1 pathways. In contrast, BMS-536924 (1um) significantly (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001) inhibited this process (Fig. 5G; Additional File 3: Figure S6d-i). The qPCR results were consistent (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, we further validated the effects of IGF1 and BMS-536924 on endothelial function in OM through tube formation assays, which demonstrated that IGF1 could further enhance the tube-forming ability of the cells, while BMS-536924 significantly inhibited the tube formation induced by OM (p < 0.0001, p < 0.0001; Fig. 5K, E). CCK-8 assays confirmed that treatment with IGF1 or BMS-536924 did not significantly affect cell viability (Additional File 3: Figure S7e), indicating that the observed functional differences were not due to cytotoxic effects. Concurrently, we knocked down IGF1 expression in pVICs using siRNA (Additional File 3: Figure S8a) and found that the downregulation of IGF1 could also prevent the OM-induced increase in CD31 and CDH5 expression (Fig. 5I,J; Additional File 3: Figure S6j-l), as well as inhibit their tube-forming ability (Fig. 5L, F). Similarly, CCK-8 results showed that IGF1 knockdown did not reduce cell viability, supporting a specific effect on endothelial function rather than general cell health (Additional File 3: Figure S7f). Likewise, Dil-ac-LDL uptake assay results indicated that both the knockdown of IGF1 and the inhibitor BMS-536924 could suppress the endothelial uptake function in OM-induced pVICs (Additional File 3: Figure S8b-c). The biological function of the PI3K-Akt pathway acting through the HIF1 pathway has been validated in multiple studies [34, 35]. Therefore, we can conclude that OM can promote the MEndT in pVICs via the IGF1-PI3K-AKT-HIF1α pathway.

To investigate the relationship between IGF1 expression and CD31 positive cells within the valve in CAVD lesions, we conducted multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemical staining on paraffin-embedded valve sections, simultaneously marking CD31, α-SMA, and IGF1 (Additional File 3: Figure S8d). The results revealed that CD31 positive and α-SMA-negative cells within the valve also highly express IGF1 (Yellow arrow), and a large number of IGF1-high-expressing cells are also clustered around this group of cells (White arrow).

Human VICs exhibit similar MEndT potential and IGF1-mediated regulation

To support the translational relevance of our findings, we conducted complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments using primary human valvular interstitial cells (hVICs) isolated from normal donor aortic valve tissues. First, we successfully isolated both hVICs and human valvular endothelial cells (hVECs) using the same method applied to porcine cells (Additional File 3: Figure S9a–c). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that hVICs expressed mesenchymal markers (α-SMA and Vimentin), but not endothelial markers (CD31 and CDH5), while the hVECs displayed the opposite profile (Additional File 3: Figure S9d). After 8 days of OM treatment, hVICs exhibited increased expression of CD31 and CDH5 by immunofluorescence staining (Additional File 3: Figure S9e), while Western blot analysis showed a corresponding upregulation of CDH5 (p < 0.0001) and CD31 (p = 0.0012) and a downregulation of α-SMA (p = 0.0078) (Additional File 3: Figure S9f), suggesting that hVICs underwent MEndT in response to OM. Functional assays further demonstrated that OM-treated hVICs acquired the ability to uptake Dil-ac-LDL and form tube structures in vitro (Additional File 3: Figure S10c–f). Additionally, in vivo Matrigel plug assays showed that OM-treated hVICs could participate in the formation of vascular-like structures in immunodeficient mice (Additional File 3: Figure S9g).

To investigate whether the IGF1 signaling pathway plays a similar regulatory role in hVICs as in porcine cells, we first measured IGF1 pathway activation by Western blot. The results showed that OM stimulation led to increased levels of IGF1, IGF1R, and phosphorylated IGF1R in hVICs (p = 0.0429, p = 0.0028, p = 0.0011; Additional File 3: Figure S10a), suggesting pathway activation. Next, we treated OM-induced hVICs with either recombinant IGF1 protein (100 ng/mL) or the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 (1 μM). Western blot and functional assays, including tube formation and Dil-ac-LDL uptake, showed that IGF1 further promoted MEndT, while IGF1R inhibition BMS-536924 suppressed it (Additional File 3: Figure S10b–f). Importantly, CCK-8 assays confirmed that none of these treatments significantly affected cell viability, ruling out cytotoxicity as a confounding factor (Additional File 3: Figure S10g). These results collectively demonstrate that OM can induce MEndT in human VICs and that IGF1 signaling plays a conserved and functional role in regulating this process across species.

The presence of mesenchymal-derived CD31-positive cells in the aortic valve leaflets of AVWI mouse model

To verify the occurrence of MEndT during CAVD disease progression, we generated Col1a2-CreERT:R26R-tdTomato mice to label cells of mesenchymal origin in the aortic valve. Using these mice, we established a mouse AVWI model of CAVD by inducing aortic valve injury through wire injury (Fig. 6A). And to investigate the role of the IGF1 pathway in this process, we administered intraperitoneal injections of saline, recombinant mouse IGF1 protein, and the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 in the AVWI mouse model. We first assessed the Transaortic peak velocity and Aortic valve annular diameter in each group of mice using echocardiography. The results showed that, compared to saline, intraperitoneal injection of recombinant mouse IGF1 protein in AVWI mice increased the Aortic valve annular diameter (p = 0.0239) and reduced the Transaortic peak velocity (p = 0.0003). In contrast, the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 produced the opposite effect (p = 0.0291, p = 0.0009; Fig. 6B–D). Additionally, HE staining of mouse aortic valve sections showed that intraperitoneal injection of recombinant protein IGF1 significantly reduced (p = 0.0009) the aortic valve thickness in AVWI mice, while BMS-536924 significantly increased (p = 0.0013) the aortic valve thickness (Fig. 6E,F). Next, we used multiplex immunofluorescence staining for endothelial marker CD31 and tdTomato to detect the presence of endothelial cells of mesenchymal origin in the aortic valve of the AVWI mousse. In the area near the endothelium of the aortic valve in the AVWI mice, we have observed tdTomato-labeled cells expressing CD31, with their proportion significantly increased (p < 0.0001) in the IGF1 group and significantly decreased (p = 0.0157) in the BMS-536924 group (Fig. 6E, G). These findings indicate that the IGF1 pathway may alleviate disease progression in the AVWI mouse model by mediating MEndT.

Enrichment of endothelial cell-related biological processes: cellular fate analysis of VIC in CAVD

To further investigate the potential transdifferentiation of VICs to endothelial cells (ECs) during CAVD, we obtained CAVD single-cell data from both normal anatomical structure (tricuspid aortic valve, TAV) [29] and bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) [30]. We performed trajectory analysis on the VICs in these datasets. After standard data processing and quality control (Additional File 3: Figure S11a-h), we obtained single-cell transcriptomes for 45,280 cells (TAV) and 32,962 cells (BAV). Subsequently, we identified 13 and 8 distinct cell subpopulations through UMAP analysis (Additional File 3: Figure S12a-d). Through analysis of the unique expression patterns of marker genes within these subpopulations, we categorized them into three main cell types (VIC, VEC, Immunocyte) (Fig. 7A,G) and identified genes with significantly differential expression within each type (Additional File 3: Figure S12e-f). Based on the UMAP projection, we further divided the VIC into 10 (TAV) and 9 (BAV) cell subgroups, and selected specific cell marker genes for each subgroup (Fig. 7B, H; Additional File 3: Figure S13a-d). Subsequently, we used Cytotrace to identify and predict the developmental potential of cells, determining G0S2 + VIC (TBA) and PRG4 + VIC (BAV) as the least differentiated subgroups in their respective datasets (Fig. 7C, I; Additional File 3: Figure S14a, b). Therefore, we chose the two VIC subgroups as starting points and used Monocle2 to construct cell fate trajectories for VIC populations in the two datasets separately (Fig. 7D, J; Additional File 3: Figure S15a-f), classifying all cells into three distinct states: one pre-branch and two cell fates. Next, cluster heatmaps were utilized to analyze differential gene expression patterns in the various branch cell fates (Fig. 7E, K). To precisely annotate the biological functions of differentially expressed genes associated with distinct cell fates, we conducted GO enrichment analysis on these genes. Interestingly, we found that in both tricuspid and bicuspid aortic valve-derived CAVD single-cell data, there is an enrichment of cell fate trajectories in the VIC toward both osteogenic-related biological processes as well as endothelial cell-related biological processes (Fig. 7F, L). This suggests that during the process of calcific aortic valve disease, VICs undergo a transition toward endothelial cell direction while differentiating toward osteogenic direction.

Discussion

In this study, we have discovered the presence of abundant CD31-positive α-SMA-negative non-tubular cells in the calcified aortic valve leaflets from patients with CAVD, existing as individual cells without forming distinct vascular structures. CD31 is one of the most classical markers of endothelial cells, while α-SMA is one of the markers for myofibroblasts and EndMT. Previous studies on endothelial phenotype-positive cells in calcified valves have mainly focused on CD31-positive cells forming tubular structures and cells co-expressing CD31 and α-SMA [9, 10, 12, 15] with little attention paid to CD31-positive α-SMA-negative non-tubular cells present in calcified valves. We further identified that this group of cells co-expressed the mesenchymal marker COL1A2. Considering that VICs exhibit considerable plasticity, we speculated whether these cells in valve could originate from a transformation of VICs. Our findings revealed that pVICs, when subjected to osteogenic differentiation induction, exhibited increased expression of endothelial-related markers. Functional assays, both in vitro and in vivo, demonstrated that a subset of pVICs undergoing osteogenic differentiation acquired endothelial cell functionality, indicating the occurrence of MEndT. The RNA sequencing further validated that endothelial-related biological processes were enriched in VICs undergoing osteogenic differentiation. Additionally, the IGF1-PI3K-AKT-HIF pathway was identified as a key mediator of MEndT. Moreover, lineage-tracing experiments in a CAVD mouse model confirmed the presence of endothelial cells derived from mesenchymal cells in the aortic valves. Single-cell trajectory analyses of CAVD datasets from both tricuspid and bicuspid aortic valves showed enrichment of endothelial-related processes during the osteogenic differentiation of VICs. Collectively, data from in vitro studies, animal models, and clinical specimens consistently validated the occurrence of MEndT during CAVD progression.

Notably, CAVD was once considered a passive degenerative disease; however, growing evidence now suggests that it is an active disease process associated with multiple factors, including inflammatory responses, mechanical stress, and lipid deposition, which induce transdifferentiation of resident valvular cells [1]. And the integrity of the valvular endothelium is crucial for maintaining the normal architecture and function of the valve. Abnormalities in valvular endothelial cells have been identified as a major contributor to the pathogenesis of CAVD [4, 31]. However, most studies on CAVD have focused on VICs, with only a few dedicated to the role of valvular endothelium. In this study, we used the AVWI mouse model that mechanically injured the endothelium of the aortic valve via guidewire to simulate the progression of CAVD. We also employed lineage tracing techniques to label mesenchymal-derived cells in the valve. Fluorescence staining of the aortic valve in these mice clearly demonstrated that cells undergoing MEndT were involved in the formation of the valvular endothelium following the surgery. And intraperitoneal injection of recombinant IGF1 protein significantly promoted the occurrence of MEndT in the aortic valve and alleviated disease progression, whereas the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924 produced completely opposite effects. Notably, we observed a tendency toward better-preserved valve endothelial integrity in the IGF1-treated group compared to other groups, although this was not quantitatively assessed. Although our analysis of calcified valves from CAVD patients found a positive correlation between the proportion of CD31-positive and α-SMA-negative non-vascular cells and the degree of calcification, the phenomenon of these cells accumulating in the sub-endothelial region was more commonly observed in valves with a lower degree of calcification. This trend is not contradictory to the findings in the animal model. Therefore, we propose that VICs may contribute to valvular endothelial repair through MEndT, thereby alleviating disease progression.

MEndT or EndMT? Undoubtedly, EndMT is a widely validated cellular transformation process that plays a significant role in many diseases and tissue development processes, including CAVD [31]. The majority of valve interstitial cells in the aortic valve are derived from embryonic endocardial cells via EndMT [4]. Xu et al. [29] and Lyu et al. [30] identified the presence of EndMT through pseudotime analysis of single-cell transcriptomic sequencing of calcified aortic valves from CAVD patients. However, these observations do not negate the existence of MEndT during CAVD. Moreover, Chow et al. [36] discovered the occurrence of MEndT processes in the heart valves during the developmental stages in a zebrafish model. This, at least, validates the capability of VICs to undergo endothelial transformation in the early developmental process. Therefore, we posit that both MEndT and EndMT processes are likely to coexist in the aortic valve tissue of CAVD, contributing to the disease progression in their respective ways. Moreover, several studies increasingly indicate that MEndT plays a significant role in the pathology of various diseases [17, 21–23, 37, 38]. Interestingly, in 2021, Chen et al. [39] discovered that vitamin D3 mediates mesenchymal-to-endothelial transition in mesenchymal stem cells via the IGF1 signaling pathway, further substantiating the importance of the IGF1 pathway in MEndT.

Vascular formation is one of the most classic and common functions of endothelial cells. Our analysis revealed that CD31 + non-tubular cells within calcified valves from CAVD patients were predominantly located around blood vessels and sub-endothelial region, and the proportion of CD31 + non-tubular cells in the valve was positively correlated with the number of blood vessels in the valve. Furthermore, we conducted extensive in vivo Matrigel angiogenesis assays and in vitro tube formation assays to verify that cells undergoing MEndT possess angiogenic capabilities. However, we did not observe vascular formation in the aortic valves of CAVD mouse models. Therefore, whether MEndT in CAVD disease progression influences vascularization in the valve to affect disease progression requires further investigation.

Of course, this study also has some limitations. In the animal experiments, we validated the impact of the IGF1 pathway through intraperitoneal injection of recombinant IGF1 protein and the IGF1R inhibitor BMS-536924, but we did not knock down the expression of IGF1 in VICs in mice at the gene or protein level for further validation. While our study integrates murine, porcine, and human data, species differences remain a key limitation. The AVWI mouse model reflects acute endothelial injury but cannot fully mimic the chronic and multifactorial progression of human CAVD. Porcine VICs, though widely used, differ from human cells in gene expression and signaling. Although we successfully isolated primary human VICs from normal donor valves and validated MEndT under osteogenic conditions in vitro, this setting does not fully replicate the inflammatory and calcific microenvironment of diseased valves. Additionally, our human tissue data represent static endpoints, limiting dynamic interpretation. These findings should be further validated in models that more closely simulate native disease progression. Nevertheless, the discovery of MEndT during CAVD provides a novel perspective for reinterpreting the pathophysiological processes underlying calcific aortic valve disease.

Conclusions

In this study, we provide evidence that aortic valve interstitial cells are capable of undergoing MEndT under calcifying conditions and that this phenomenon also occurs in the aortic valves of a murine CAVD model. We demonstrate that IGF1 signaling contributes to this process and that modulation of this pathway alters the extent of endothelial transition. These findings reveal the presence of MEndT in CAVD and suggest that regulating IGF1-mediated MEndT could represent a novel therapeutic strategy.

Nevertheless, limitations exist, including the need for further lineage-tracing studies in human valve tissue and functional validation in larger animal models. Taken together, our work provides mechanistic insights into the cellular plasticity of VICs during valve calcification and highlights MEndT as a potential regulatory target in the progression of CAVD.
