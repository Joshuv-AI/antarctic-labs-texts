Validity and reliability evidence for the bronchiectasis health questionnaire in a sample of Turkish adults with bronchiectasis
Authors: Şaban Melih Şimşek, Selda Günaydın, Deniz Kızılırmak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Bronchiectasis Health Questionnaire (BHQ), a 10-question instrument designed to assess quality of life and disease-specific issues, was developed in 11 languages, including English. The present study aims to translate the original version of the BHQ into Turkish using a standardised methodology and to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the BHQ.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at Giresun Training and Research Hospital between August 2024 and January 2025, with ethical approval (decision number 03.07.2024/03) and informed consent from all participants. A total of 132 clinically stable participants with bronchiectasis were enrolled. The study was performed in two stages: (1) cross-cultural adaptation of the original Bronchiectasis Health Questionnaire (BHQ) into Turkish (T-BHQ) according to established guidelines, and (2) psychometric validation of the T-BHQ. Alongside the T-BHQ, data on socio-demographics, clinical characteristics, mMRC dyspnea score, pulmonary function tests, Bronchiectasis Severity Index (BSI), SF-12 quality of life questionnaire, and exacerbation history were collected. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and test–retest analysis, while construct validity was evaluated through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, convergent validity with clinical measures, and Bland–Altman analysis.ResultsThe validity and reliability of the T-BHQ were evaluated in present study involving a total of 134 participants with bronchiectasis, and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.729 was obtained. In the exploratory factorial analysis, factorial loadings of > 0.40 were determined for all items and were evaluated as appropriate. Utilising the Bland-Altman analysis to assess the test’s repeatability, (mean difference: 0,059, β = 0.03, p:0.840). The convergent validity of the T-BHQ with mMRC was strong (r = − 0.648, p:< 0.001), with BSI were moderate (r: -0,437, p:<0,001), while concurrent validity with SF-12 subtypes mainly were at a moderate level (Physical role functioning: r = − 0.661 p:<0.001, other subtypes at a moderate level).ConclusionsThe T-BHQ has been demonstrated to possess both reliability and validity in evaluating the disease-related quality of life and health status of participants with bronchiectasis in Turkey.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03981-7.

Background

Bronchiectasis is defined as a chronic disease of the airways characterised by enlargement and thickening of the walls of one or more bronchi [1]. While the incidence of bronchiectasis is decreasing in developed countries, it is relatively common in developing countries [2]. Although the prevalence of bronchiectasis is reported to be 52–139/10,000 in developing countries, its absolute prevalence is not known [2, 3].

Symptoms of bronchiectasis include shortness of breath, cough, expectoration, and hemoptysis. Scales such as the Bronchiectasis Severity Index (BSI) and the FACED score are used to determine the severity of bronchiectasis [4, 5]. The assessment of the impact of bronchiectasis on health and quality of life can be facilitated by the utilisation of validated questionnaires. In this regard, the Quality of Life-Bronchiectasis Questionnaire has been developed to evaluate the quality of life in individuals affected by bronchiectasis [6]. A Turkish validation of this questionnaire, comprising 37 questions, has been conducted, providing a substantial amount of data pertaining to the disease [7]. Its integration into clinical practice, however, may be hindered by its length and the absence of a comprehensive scoring system. Non-bronchiectasis-specific respiratory questionnaires, such as the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) and mMRC, are used, however these questionnaires do not adequately reflect disease-specific symptoms such as sputum and hemoptysis [8, 9].

The reliability and validation of the Bronchiectasis Health Questionnaire (BHQ), developed by Spinou et al., was performed in 11 languages [10]. These include nine Indo-European languages (Dutch for Belgium and the Netherlands, English for the USA, French for Belgium and France, German, Italian, and Spanish for both Spain and the USA), as well as Japanese (Japonic) and Mandarin (Sino-Tibetan). The questionnaire is short, reproducible and allows the patient to self-assess [10]. The original English version of the BHQ is provided in the supplementary material Sect [8]. In addition to these languages, adaptations have been made for Danish, Korean, and Brazilian Portuguese [11–13]. A Turkish reliability and validity study of the questionnaire was planned in order to ensure that patients with bronchiectasis can be evaluated by themselves and by physicians in daily practice. Permission was obtained from Dr. Surinder S. Birring, who prepared the original version of the questionnaire.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted by the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Giresun Training and Research Hospital’s Scientific Research Ethics Committee has authorised the study (decision number 03.07.2024/03). Patients who met the inclusion criteria provided written informed consent and were then enrolled as participants.

This study was conducted in two stages. In the first stage, the original BHQ was cross-culturally adapted for use in Turkey, following previously established guidelines [14]. In the second stage, the measurement properties of the Turkish version of the BHQ (hereafter referred to as T-BHQ) were tested. The reporting of this study was prepared by the recommendations of the ‘The Test Adaptation Reporting standards (TARES) Checklist’ guideline [15].

Our sample of patients diagnosed with bronchiectasis was calculated to be 132 people with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. It was seen that the sample should be at least 100 with a recommendation of 10 participants per item for questionnaire validation studies in the literature [16]. Participants were recruited from patients with an established diagnosis of bronchiectasis, who presented to the chest diseases outpatient clinics of our institutions between August 2024 and January 2025. Bronchiectasis diagnoses were confirmed by clinical evaluation and computed tomography scans found in the patients’ medical records.

Stage 1: cross-cultural adaptation

The cross-cultural adaptation process followed a modified version of the guidelines proposed by Beaton et al. (2000) [17]. The initial translation into Turkish was carried out by the research coordinator. Cognitive debriefing was conducted with two participants, during which one item was reworded to improve comprehensibility. Subsequently, a second researcher, blinded to the original translation, performed a back-translation into English. The final version was reviewed by the research team and a certified translator to ensure semantic equivalence between the English and Turkish versions. Although the process did not fully adhere to all recommended steps (e.g., the number of translators and sample size for pre-testing), it ensured linguistic and cultural equivalence, and the original developers of the BHQ also confirmed this method. It was considered adequate for validation purposes (Fig. 1).

Stage 2: evaluation of measurement properties

In addition to the socio-demographic form and T-BHQ, basic clinical information, including modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) score, pulmonary function test, bronchiectasis severity index (BSI), Medical Outcomes Study (MOS) 12-item short form health questionnaire (SF-12), and history of exacerbations, was collected. The accuracy of exacerbation history and antibiotic use was determined by a chest disease physician based on patient history and medical documents. The T-BHQ was repeated 2 weeks after the first test in 30 participants who volunteered in our study group.

Testing procedures

Turkish version of bronchiectasis health questionare (T-BHQ)

It is a 10-item 7-point Likert-type questionnaire developed initially by Spinou et al. It provides scoring between 0 and 100 with bronchiectasis-related questions [10]. The T-BHQ, adapted from the original BHQ as described in the methodology, is presented in the Supplementary Material.

Modified medical resource council (mMRC) dyspnea scale

mMRC is a 5-item scale created by Bestall et al. that allows the severity of dyspnea to be measured [9].

Bronchiectasis severity index (BSI)

It is an index consisting of 9 items by Chalmers et al. It is used to determine the risk of mortality, hospitalization, and exacerbation due to bronchiectasis. It allows bronchiectasis to be defined as mild, moderate, and severe disease [5].

Turkish version of SF-12 health survey

It is a quality of life scale consisting of 12 items and 8 subtypes. Turkish validation was performed by Soylu et al. [18]. The SF-12 is a widely used, well-validated generic health-related quality of life questionnaire that has been extensively applied in chronic respiratory diseases, including bronchiectasis.

Spirometry

Pulmonary function tests were performed using standard spirometry in accordance with international guidelines, and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁) and forced vital capacity (FVC) values were recorded.

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was assessed by examining the correlation between the T-BHQ and BSI, mMRC, and the SF-12 quality of life scores, as both instruments measure related constructs of health status. Additionally, a spirometry test was performed.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained in the study were statistically evaluated using the “Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Statistics for Windows®, version 21.0 and SPSS Amos 21.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).” Frequency, percentage values, median (interquartile range), mean, and standard deviation values were determined as descriptive statistics. For comparisons, the Student t-test was used for numerical variables that fit the normal distribution, and the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for numerical variables that did not fit the normal distribution. Comparisons between categorical variables were made with the chi-square test. Comparative correlation analyses (Pearson, Spearman) were performed to determine the variables affecting mortality, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in the statistical analyses.

In reliability analysis, Cronbach’s alpha was used for internal consistency analysis. Values between 0.70 and 0.90 were considered appropriate [19]. In line with recommended validation procedures, the total sample was randomly divided into two independent subgroups. The first subgroup was used for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to identify the underlying factor structure, while the second subgroup was used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the fit of the derived model. This approach was applied to prevent overfitting and inflated model fit indices that may occur when the same sample is used for both EFA and CFA [20].

Exploratory factorial validity (EFA), the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) criterion, and Bartlett’s sphericity test were applied to analyze whether the data matrix could be submitted to factorization. The KMO indices were categorized as follows: unacceptable (< 0.5), mediocre (between 0.5 and 0.7), good (0.7 to 0.8), very good (>0.8), and excellent (0.9) [14, 21]. For data extraction, we utilized principal component analysis (PCA) with varimax orthogonal rotation. Factor analysis aims to reduce the number of variables to a smaller set of factors, and this analysis is conducted for this purpose. We retained only those factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1. The factorability of the correlation matrix was assessed with the following interpretations: minimal (≥ 0.30), important (≥ 0.40), and practically significant (≥ 0.50) [22]. To determine the level of fit between the model and the data, multiple model fit indices were examined. In this respect, χ2/sd (Chi-square), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) were examined [23–30].

Bland-Altman analysis were applied to evaluate the reproducibility of the T-BHQ. In addition, the correlation between BSA and SF-12, mMRC, and BSI were analyzed using Pearson’s method for validity.

Exploratory factor analysis revealed a three-factor solution. The first factor, labeled “Functional limitation burden”, included dyspnea, activity limitation, cough, and sleep disturbance. The second factor, labeled “Infective burden”, comprised hemoptysis, antibiotic use, and sputum production. The third factor, labeled “Psycho-emotional burden”, consisted of items related to fatigue, anxiety, and embarrassment. Each factor contained at least three items, with no cross-loadings, and demonstrated adequate loadings, supporting the structural validity of the instrument. In the T-BHQ, exploratory factor analysis revealed three factors that were not defined in the original questionnaire. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for these subscales were 0.765, 0.680, and 0.693, respectively, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Together, these factors explained 60.01% of the total variance. However, consistent with the original BHQ, which is scored as a single total score rather than separate subscales, the primary analyses were conducted using the total BHQ score. Items Q2 and Q6 demonstrated cross-loadings, with Q2 loading on both Factor 1 (0.418) and Factor 3 (0.682), and Q6 loading on both Factor 1 (0.704) and Factor 2 (0.423). In both cases, the items showed a stronger association with one factor, and were therefore retained in line with the theoretical structure of the original BHQ.

Bland-Altman plots were used for the validation of the T-BHQ, administered initially and after two weeks. A mean difference of 0.059 was seen in the Bland-Altman plot with upper and lower limits of agreement of −3.058 and 3.176, respectively; and β = 0.03 (p = 0.840) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, the Turkish version of the BHQ (T-BHQ) was adapted, and its validity, reliability, and compatibility with similar questionnaires were demonstrated. The T-BHQ, translated from the original version, is a questionnaire with proven validity and reliability that can be used in daily practice in participants with bronchiectasis in Türkiye. This 10-question questionnaire can be used to assess disease-related quality of life. In contrast to scales like the BSI or FACED, which healthcare professionals complete, the BHQ was designed as a patient-reported questionnaire that can be completed directly by participants. This study provides similarly high Cronbach’s alpha values to the original questionnaire, Danish, and Brazilian Portuguese versions in the literature (0.85, 0.739, and 0.92, respectively). When the test was re-evaluated 15 days later on the same subjects, it showed similar reproducibility coefficients to other studies in the literature (0.89, 0.799, and 0.86, respectively) [10–13].

Although three factors emerged in the Turkish version that were not reported in the original BHQ, these likely reflect cultural or linguistic differences in item grouping. Interestingly, a similar three-factor structure was also observed in the Brazilian Portuguese adaptation, suggesting that the multidimensional structure may not be unique to the Turkish sample. In contrast, the Korean and Danish versions of the BHQ were adapted linguistically, but psychometric properties such as factor structure were not evaluated, making direct comparisons across languages difficult [11–13]. As the original BHQ was designed and validated to be used as a single total score, we retained this approach in our analyses. We reported the subscale structure only as supplementary findings.

In our study, the T-BHQ demonstrated significant negative correlations with the mMRC dyspnoea scale and the BSI, which is consistent with previous validation studies of the BHQ in other languages [12, 13]. When subjective, patient-reported measures were considered, the T-BHQ was moderately correlated with the SF-12 subscales, indicating that it captures important aspects of disease-related quality of life. In contrast, its correlations with objective measures such as spirometric parameters were weaker, suggesting that the T-BHQ evaluates dimensions of health status that are not fully reflected by physiological indices alone. These findings align with earlier studies reporting stronger associations of the T-BHQ with patient-reported outcomes compared to clinical measures, thereby supporting its role as a concise, patient-centered instrument for assessing health status in bronchiectasis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that survey length has a direct impact on response and completion rates. Specifically, completion rates have been reported to decrease to around 37% for long questionnaires, whereas shorter instruments achieve rates of up to 63% [31, 32]. The relatively small number of questions in the BHQ contributes to its practicality and supports its use in daily life; this characteristic has been preserved in the Turkish adaptation. From this perspective, it is presented as an alternative to the 37-question Quality of Life-Bronchiectasis Questionnaire. The literature indicates that a low number of questions may lead to a reduction in scope and loss of detail [31]. Previous validation studies of the BHQ in different languages, including Mandarin and Brazilian Portuguese, have demonstrated strong correlations with longer respiratory-specific HRQoL questionnaires such as the SGRQ and the QoL-B [10, 13, 33]. Importantly, the BHQ has a considerably shorter completion time (mean 3.8 min) compared with the SGRQ (approximately 12 min) and the QoL-B (approximately 10 min), highlighting its practicality for routine use without compromising validity [13]. In this regard, it should be borne in mind that the Quality of Life-Bronchiectasis Questionnaire may provide more detailed data.

Linguistic validation has an essential place in the translation stages for questionnaire validity and reliability studies. Because each language and its associated words have their cultural infrastructure. When the literature is examined, the original BHQ has been translated into 14 different languages and validated [10–13, 34]. Unlike Turkish, which belongs to the Altaic language family, most of these languages belong to the Indo-European language family [35, 36]. One study noted that it is difficult to translate precisely the same meaning even in interlingual translations belonging to the same language family [36]. In preparing the T-BHQ, it was necessary to add parenthetical explanations, particularly in item 4, for these reasons. Korean, which belongs to the same language family as Turkish, there is a need for a similar provision in the validation of the BHQ [12, 35].

The grouping of items under the three extracted factors may reflect distinct dimensions of health-related quality of life in Turkish adults with bronchiectasis, including physical functioning, symptom burden, and psychosocial impact. Cultural factors and healthcare practices in Turkey, such as the high emphasis on symptom control and the strong role of family support in daily care, may also influence how patients perceive and report their quality of life. Comparisons with validation studies in English and other languages suggest both commonalities in the core domains assessed by the BHQ and subtle cultural differences in item interpretation [10, 13, 33].

Our study has several limitations. First, the participating centres were not located in the major cities of our country; the study was conducted only in tertiary hospitals, and therefore, the sample may not fully represent different population regions. Second, feedback during the translation phase was obtained from only two adults with bronchiectasis; obtaining input from a larger number of peoples would have provided more robust insights into linguistic and cultural adaptation. Third, we did not include a bronchiectasis-specific or even a respiratory-specific quality of life instrument for comparison. Rather, we relied on the SF-12, a generic health-related quality of life questionnaire, to assess convergent validity. Future studies should aim to replicate this research in different regions of our country and directly compare the T-BHQ with disease-specific quality of life measures in Turkish populations.

This study has certain limitations regarding the cross-cultural adaptation process. According to Beaton et al. [17], multiple independent forward translations and larger pre-testing samples are generally recommended. In our study, only one forward translation was performed, and cognitive debriefing was conducted with two participants, which may not fully reflect the diversity of patient perspectives. Nevertheless, the essential steps—including forward translation, back-translation, patient feedback with item revision, and final review by the research team and a certified translator—were completed, and the original BHQ developers approved the process. Future studies should incorporate multiple translators and larger pre-testing samples to enhance linguistic and cultural equivalence further. The presence of cross-loading in items Q2 and Q6 should be acknowledged as a methodological limitation. However, the higher loadings on Factor 3 for Q2 and on Factor 1 for Q6 support their retention, and such findings are commonly reported in psychometric validation studies without substantially compromising construct validity.

Despite these limitations, the methodological rigor of our study is comparable to that of other published BHQ validation studies, which supports the reliability and cross-cultural relevance of our findings.

As a result, the original BHQ was adapted into Turkish, and its validity and reliability were evaluated. The T-BHQ has been demonstrated to be reliable and valid in assessing disease-related quality of life and health status in participants with bronchiectasis in Turkey. The correlation of the questionnaire with BSI, SF-12 quality of life tests, and especially mMRC, which can be used in bronchiectasis, has been shown. However, the practicality of the BHQ and its disease specificity may support its use in daily clinical practice.
