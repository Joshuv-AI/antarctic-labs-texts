Postoperative hyperinflammation and recovery challenges in necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Christopher John Luschen, Catherine J Hunter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) remains one of the most devastating gastrointestinal emergencies in neonates, particularly affecting premature infants with immature intestinal, immune and microvascular systems. While surgical intervention is often lifesaving in severe NEC, the postoperative period introduces distinct challenges that critically influence survival and long-term outcomes. This review examines postoperative hyperinflammation as a central driver of morbidity following surgical NEC. Despite resection of necrotic bowel, many infants experience persistent systemic inflammation characterized by dysregulated innate immune activation, excessive cytokine release and sustained gut barrier dysfunction.Key mediators including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β and IL-8 contribute to endothelial injury, capillary leak, hemodynamic instability and multiorgan dysfunction. Ongoing intestinal permeability and microbial translocation perpetuate inflammatory signaling, delaying mucosal healing and predisposing infants to recurrent NEC, sepsis and short bowel syndrome. Clinically, postoperative hyperinflammation manifests as systemic inflammatory response syndrome, coagulopathy, feeding intolerance, impaired wound healing and neurodevelopmental injury.Recovery is frequently prolonged by nutritional compromise, dependence on parenteral nutrition and recurrent infections, which further amplify inflammatory stress. Current management remains largely supportive, focusing on optimization of nutrition, infection prevention and multidisciplinary care. Emerging immunomodulatory and microbiome-based therapies remain investigational. Postoperative hyperinflammation remains a difficult and complex sequelae of NEC and future investigation is needed to improve long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a serious gastrointestinal emergency of neonates, with significant morbidity and mortality despite advances in neonatal intensive care and surgical techniques.1 This condition predominantly affects premature infants, whose intestinal barrier, immune responses and microvascular regulation are not yet fully developed.1 The multifactorial pathogenesis of NEC involves an interplay of intestinal ischemia, dysbiosis, immature mucosal immunity and an exaggerated inflammatory cascade, culminating in bowel necrosis and perforation in severe cases.

When medical management fails, or when intestinal perforation and gangrene develop, surgical intervention involving an exploratory laparotomy with resection of necrotic bowel or peritoneal drainage is the de facto treatment.1 However, postoperative care introduces a new set of challenges distinct from the initial inflammatory insult. Infants recovering from NEC often experience persistent systemic inflammation, hemodynamic instability, delayed gut motility and difficulty reaching goal enteral feeds.2 These complications are compounded by the unique physiology of the premature infant, whose inflammatory mediators are dysregulated, and immunologic homeostasis is fragile.2

Postoperative hyperinflammation is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of recovery outcomes. It reflects the ongoing activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukins (IL) (IL-6, IL-8) and platelet-activating factors, along with the disruption of intestinal epithelial integrity and microbial translocation.3 This exaggerated immune response can cause multiorgan dysfunction, including respiratory decompensation, renal impairment and cardiovascular instability.3 Furthermore, persistent inflammation delays intestinal healing, promotes adhesions and may predispose survivors to short bowel syndrome (SBS) or intestinal failure.3

The postoperative course is also marked by nutritional and metabolic challenges. The interruption of enteral feeding, dependence on parenteral nutrition (PN) and loss of absorptive surface area leads to growth failure and increased vulnerability to infection.4 Additionally, the neurodevelopmental outcomes of NEC survivors are often compromised due to systemic inflammatory effects on the developing brain and the prolonged critical illness that follows surgery.4 5

This review aims to explore the basis of postoperative hyperinflammation following surgical NEC, emphasizing the interplay between innate immune dysregulation, microbial translocation and tissue injury. It will further examine the clinical manifestations, biomarkers and therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating inflammatory injury and optimizing recovery. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for improving survival and long-term outcomes in this fragile patient population.

Pathophysiology of postoperative hyperinflammation

Postoperative hyperinflammation reflects the convergence of immune immaturity, cytokine amplification and persistent intestinal barrier dysfunction. Central to this process is exaggerated activation of innate immune pathways, particularly toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, which is introduced here as the primary mechanistic driver and referenced throughout subsequent sections.5

Immune immaturity in preterm infants results in impaired regulation of inflammatory responses, with reduced anti-inflammatory signaling and exaggerated cytokine production.5 Surgical stress, ischemia-reperfusion injury and microbial exposure further amplify this response, producing a sustained inflammatory state.5

Simultaneously, the intestinal barrier remains compromised. Impaired epithelial restitution and tight junction dysfunction allow continued microbial translocation, perpetuating immune activation.6 This creates a self-reinforcing cycle of inflammation, tissue injury and delayed healing.6

Emerging evidence suggests that postoperative hyperinflammation in NEC is not solely a continuation of the initial disease process but may represent a distinct immunologic state characterized by immune-metabolic dysregulation, impaired epithelial regeneration and persistent host–microbiome disequilibrium.7–9 These evolving concepts highlight the need to move beyond static models of inflammation towards dynamic, systems-based approaches that better capture the complexity of recovery in premature infants as shown in figure 1.

Immature immune system activation

Preterm neonates exhibit profound developmental limitations in both innate and adaptive immunity. The neonatal immune system is characterized by reduced neutrophil storage pools, diminished chemotaxis, impaired phagocytic activity and limited capacity for antigen presentation.7 Additionally, premature infants have lower levels of anti-inflammatory mediators such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), which are critical for regulating inflammatory responses.7

During surgical management of NEC, exposure to bacterial endotoxins, necrotic tissue and ischemia-reperfusion injury triggers a massive activation of pattern recognition receptors particularly TLR4 on enterocytes and macrophages.10 11 The engagement of TLR4 by lipopolysaccharide initiates downstream NF-κB signaling, leading to the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.3 11 In the immature intestine, this pathway is poorly regulated, resulting in disproportionate cytokine release and overwhelming systemic inflammation.11

Consequently, neonates develop a heightened vulnerability to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), characterized by hemodynamic instability, capillary leak and metabolic derangements.9 This hyperactivation can persist long after surgical correction, as the neonatal immune system struggles to restore homeostasis.9

Cytokine storm and organ dysfunction

The postoperative inflammatory environment in NEC is marked by elevated circulating levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-8 as well as downstream mediators such as nitric oxide and platelet-activating factors.3 These cytokines propagate a cytokine storm, amplifying systemic vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction.3 IL-6 and TNF-α contribute to increased vascular permeability, leading to capillary leak syndrome and hypotension, which may necessitate aggressive fluid and vasopressor support.10

At the organ level, these mediators induce pulmonary dysfunction through alveolar-capillary barrier disruption and surfactant inhibition, predisposing infants to respiratory failure and prolonged ventilator dependence.12 Hepatic and renal perfusions are compromised by inflammatory microthrombosis and vasoplegia, contributing to multiorgan dysfunction syndrome.12 Furthermore, systemic inflammation interferes with myocardial contractility and oxygen utilization, worsening tissue hypoxia and continuing a cycle of inflammatory injury.12

Persistent gut barrier dysfunction

Despite surgical removal of necrotic bowel, the remaining intestine often exhibits ongoing mucosal injury and increased permeability.13 The intestinal epithelium in preterm infants has limited capacity for restitution and tight junction repair, leaving it susceptible to continued translocation of luminal bacteria and endotoxins.10 13 This persistent microbial translocation maintains immune activation through continuous stimulation of macrophages and dendritic cells within the lamina propria.13

Moreover, postoperative factors such as enteral feeding interruption, antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and ischemia from fluctuating perfusion exacerbate the disruption of the intestinal barrier.1 The sustained exposure to microbial products promotes a vicious inflammatory cycle, wherein cytokine-mediated epithelial apoptosis further weakens mucosal integrity, amplifying systemic inflammation.1

Persistent gut barrier dysfunction also has long-term implications, including the risk of SBS, cholestasis secondary to prolonged PN and delayed attainment of enteral autonomy.4 Collectively, these factors underscore the complex interplay between local intestinal injury and systemic immune dysregulation in postoperative NEC.

Systemic inflammatory response syndrome

Neonates recovering from surgical NEC frequently exhibit clinical and biochemical features consistent with SIRS.15 This constellation of signs includes temperature instability (both hypothermia and hyperthermia), tachycardia, leukocytosis or leukopenia and respiratory compromise.8 15 The physiologic immaturity of neonates often blunts classical febrile responses, making subtle changes in perfusion or respiratory effort critical early markers of systemic inflammation.16 Persistent SIRS in this context may evolve into sepsis-like physiology, even in the absence of confirmed infection, underscoring the difficulty of distinguishing sterile inflammation from true bacteremia in postoperative NEC.16

Hemodynamic instability

Excessive cytokine release and endothelial activation drive capillary leakage, leading to intravascular volume depletion, generalized edema and hypotension.3 These infants often require aggressive fluid resuscitation, albumin supplementation and inotropic or vasopressor support to maintain perfusion. Despite intervention, myocardial dysfunction and vasoplegia may persist due to ongoing exposure to inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β and nitric oxide.3 This instability contributes to tissue hypoperfusion and increases the risk of secondary ischemic injury, particularly in the kidneys, liver and remaining bowel.10

Coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia

In the setting of severe inflammation, the coagulation cascade becomes pathologically activated, predisposing infants to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and consumptive coagulopathy.17 18 Thrombocytopenia is common and may result from both increased platelet consumption and impaired marrow production secondary to sepsis or cytokine toxicity.17 DIC not only increases the risk of intracranial and gastrointestinal hemorrhage but also contributes to microvascular thrombosis, worsening organ dysfunction.17 18 Management often necessitates careful transfusion support and correction of underlying inflammatory drivers.

Recovery challenges

Recovery from surgical NEC is shaped by persistent inflammation and its downstream effects rather than isolated organ dysfunction.10 These processes are interdependent and reinforce one another within the broader framework of postoperative hyperinflammation.

Delayed gastrointestinal recovery

Prolonged gastrointestinal dysmotility is a hallmark of postoperative NEC recovery. Infants often exhibit ileus, vomiting, abdominal distension, and high gastric residuals, reflecting ongoing bowel wall edema, neural injury, and inflammatory mediator effects on intestinal smooth muscle.19 Inflammation-driven bowel wall edema increases the diffusion of oxygen and nutrients and mechanically impairs coordinated smooth muscle contraction.3 Concurrently, enteric neural circuits are damaged by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β and reactive oxygen species disrupting enteric neuron excitability as well as loss of interstitial cells of Cajal which propagate peristalsis.20

Experimental models of NEC demonstrate smooth intestinal muscle impairment and downregulation of key contractile proteins.21 In NEC, nitric oxide synthase isoforms become dysregulated in the inflamed tissue, resulting in excessive production.21 TLR4 activation directly inhibits enteric neuronal migration and disrupts neuronal-glial communication, compounding postoperative dysmotility.11 21 These molecular derangements are accompanied by microvascular changes, endothelial injury and impaired autoregulation reducing perfusion to the muscularis propria by limiting ATP availability.21 These changes impair contractility and perpetuate ileus.

As a result, advancement of enteral feeds is often delayed, prolonging dependence on PN and increasing the risk of PN-associated liver disease.22 Multiple mechanisms cause PN-associated liver disease including phytosterol-mediated cholestasis, impaired bile acid signaling, gut–liver axis inflammation and altered microbiome-derived metabolites.22 Therefore, postoperative dysmotility in NEC is not only a clinical barrier to feeding but also a manifestation of ongoing molecular injury with systemic metabolic consequences.

Risk of recurrent NEC

Even after surgical resection, recurrent NEC remains a significant postoperative threat, reflecting the fact that the underlying pathophysiology of NEC is not fully resolved by removal of necrotic bowel alone.9 The recurrence often presents with similar clinical findings, feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, pneumatosis and may require further surgical intervention. Persistent microvascular dysfunction, such as endothelial swelling, impaired nitric oxide signaling and compromised autoregulation, leads to an inadequate perfusion of remaining tissue.9 21 This localized hypoxia and reactive oxygen species generation creates epithelial injury and perpetuates ferroptosis and apoptosis-related cell death pathways.23 Additionally, incomplete resolution of the inflammatory cascade driven by TLR4 activation, neutrophil recruitment and pro-inflammatory cytokines leaves the mucosa vulnerable to renewed injury.21

At the cellular level, compromised tight junction integrity due to the downregulation of claudins and occludins as well as the loss of Paneth cell antimicrobial function creates a permissive environment for pathogenic bacterial expansion.24 Immature enteric immune regulation further contributes to recurrence with macrophages and dendritic cells displaying an exaggerated inflammatory response with limited capacity for resolution.11

The prevention of recurrence hinges on cautious refeeding protocols, optimization of intestinal perfusion and modulation of the gut microbiome through careful antibiotic stewardship and probiotic support where appropriate.3 Optimization of intestinal perfusion by maintaining hemodynamic stability, adequate oxygen delivery and avoidance of vasoconstrictive medications supports recovery and enhances barrier regeneration.25 Modulation of the gut microbiome with antibiotic stewardship prevents further disruption of microbial diversity.26 Additionally, interventions such as targeted probiotic supplementation may enhance short-chain fatty acid production, competitive exclusion of pathogens and mucosal immune maturation.26 Prevention of recurrent NEC required not only surgical correction but also coordinated restoration of epithelial integrity, vascular health and microbiome balance.

Short bowel syndrome

Extensive bowel resection in NEC can lead to SBS, a condition marked by insufficient functional small intestine to support normal digestion, absorption and growth. The loss of mucosal surface area results in impaired uptake of macronutrients, bile acids, fat-soluble vitamins and trace elements driving chronic diarrhea, malnutrition and dependence of PN.27 Resected segments often include regions with specialized absorptive functions such as the ileum, which plays a central role in bile acid recycling and vitamin B12 absorption.27 The degree of long-term morbidity depends on the length and function of the residual small intestine and an intact ileocecal valve, which provides slower transit time, reduces bacterial overgrowth and improves nutrient salvage.

Infants with SBS face challenges including growth failure, metabolic derangements and recurrent sepsis.3 SBS triggers a complex intestinal adaptation response driven by enterocyte proliferation, villus lengthening and crypt hyperplasia mediated by growth factors such as glucagon-like peptide-2, epidermal growth factor (EGF) and insulin-like growth factor-1.28 These adaptive processes are often insufficient in neonates recovering from NEC where ongoing inflammation, fibrosis and dysbiosis impair epithelial regeneration.28 Bacterial overgrowth in the dilated dysmotile intestine can exacerbate mucosal injury, increase lactic acidosis and increase the risk of recurrent sepsis through translocation across a compromised epithelial barrier.28

Long-term management involves a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach integrating parenteral and enteral nutrition, intestinal rehabilitation, pharmacologic agents and, in some cases, bowel-lengthening procedures or transplantation.29 Intestinal rehabilitation programs emphasize motility management, prevention of bacterial overgrowth, micronutrient supplementation and meticulous catheter care.29 In severe cases with refractory PN, surgical bowel-lengthening procedures such as the STEP or Bianchi or intestinal transplantation may become necessary.30 SBS secondary to NEC represents a complex interplay between anatomic loss, impaired mucosal biology, dysregulated microbiota and systemic metabolic vulnerability, requiring sustained coordination across surgical, nutritional and medical disciplines.

Sepsis and recurrent infections

Persistent inflammation, impaired mucosal immunity and the need for invasive devices such as central venous catheters create a predisposition for late-onset sepsis and catheter-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI).31 At the tissue level, NEC survivors exhibit epithelial barrier dysfunction characterized by disrupted tight junctions, altered mucin production and diminished Paneth cell antimicrobial defense.32 This compromised environment facilitates bacterial translocation from the lumen to the systemic circulation. The immature immune system of premature infants is marked by reduced neutrophil chemotaxis, limited complement activity and blunted pathogen recognition, leading to impaired microbial clearance.32

Central venous catheter, although essential for prolonged nutrition and medication administration, is a main adjunct for biofilm formation.33 Pathogens such as coagulase-negative staphylococci, Staphylococcus aureus and Gram-negative rods adhere to catheter surfaces producing extracellular polymeric matrices that shield them from host immune response and antibiotic penetration.33 This biofilm makes CLABSI difficult to eradicate and prone to recurrence.33 Additionally, systemic inflammation from NEC induces endothelial activation and leukocyte dysfunction, creating a pro-infectious environment, in which even low-grade bacteremia can escalate to clinically significant sepsis.21

Recurrent infections further amplify systemic inflammation through sustained activation of NF-kB pathways, release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8 and TNF-α as well as generate reactive oxygen species.3 This persistent inflammatory state delays intestinal healing, delays enteral feeding tolerance and exacerbates dysmotility, and increases mortality risk.9 This proinflammatory state also hinders nutritional progress by increasing catabolic demands, reducing nutrient absorption and prolonging dependence on PN, increasing exposure to catheter-related risks.

The cyclical relationship between inflammation and infection represents a major challenge in achieving stable recovery. Each episode of sepsis perpetuates mucosal injury and further destabilizes the microbiome, further leading to infectious susceptibility.34 Ultimately, breaking this cycle requires coordinated approaches aimed at reducing catheter exposure, enhancing mucosal healing, optimizing antimicrobial stewardship and supporting immune maturation through adequate nutrition and controlled inflammatory modulation.

Impaired wound healing

Hyperinflammation, tissue edema and nutritional deficiencies collectively compromise postoperative wound healing in infants recovering from NEC.35 Systemic inflammation driven by persistent activation of innate immune pathways, elevated cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, and oxidative stress interferes with the normal progression of wound healing phases.16 35 Excessive inflammatory signaling prolongs the early inflammatory phase, impairs macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory to reparative phenotypes, and disrupts fibroblast recruitment and extracellular matrix deposition.35 Tissue edema, common in postoperative NEC due to capillary leak and hypoalbuminemia, increases interstitial pressure and reduces perfusion for the healing wound bed thus limiting delivery of oxygen, nutrients and immune cells.16

Nutritional deficiencies are often seen in NEC, particularly inadequate protein, zinc, vitamin A and essential fatty acids, which further hinder fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis and collagen synthesis.36 Protein-energy malnutrition is frequent in NEC survivors because of delayed enteral feeding and dependence on PN reduces substrate availability for ECM production and slows granulation tissue formation.22 36 37 Additionally, metabolic stress associated with sepsis or chronic inflammation increases catabolic demand, thereby diverting amino acids away from tissue repair processes.37

Surgical sites in these physiologically stressed infants are prone to dehiscence, infection and subsequently incisional hernia formation.38 Infections exacerbate local inflammation and degrade collagen via protease release, further destabilizing wound integrity.38 The immature neonatal dermis adds another layer of vulnerability: neonatal skin contains fewer fibroblasts, less organized collagen bundles and reduced tensile strength compared with older children.39 Premature infants also display reduced keratinocyte migration and diminished growth factor signaling such as TGF-β, platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), slowing re-epithelialization and prolonging the window of susceptibility to infection.39 40

Optimizing protein and micronutrient intake is therefore essential to support fibroblast activity and collagen deposition.41 Strategies to minimize edema such as improving hemodynamic stability, correcting hypoalbuminemia and reducing systemic inflammation help restore adequate tissue perfusion and oxygenation.37 Meticulous aseptic wound care, including sterile dressing changes, gentle handling and early detection of infection, is critical to prevent bacterial invasion and support orderly progression of healing wound. Ultimately, successful postoperative wound healing in NEC survivors hinges on the coordinated restoration of nutritional adequacy, vascular integrity and immune balance.

Neurodevelopmental sequelae

Beyond gastrointestinal and systemic complications, survivors of surgical NEC face a substantial risk of lifelong neurodevelopmental impairment.42 Increasing evidence shows that NEC is not solely an intestinal disease but part of a multisystem inflammatory syndrome in premature infants.43 The intense systemic inflammatory response triggered by intestinal necrosis is marked by elevated circulating cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β, and damage-associated molecular patterns, which cross the immature blood-brain barrier, activating microglia and astrocytes in the developing brain.42 Microglial overactivation drives excessive release of pro-oxidant molecules and proteolytic enzymes, disrupting oligodendrocyte maturation and myelination.42 This inflammatory neurotoxicity contributes directly to diffuse white matter injury and later cognitive deficits.

Recurrent sepsis further amplifies this inflammatory burden. Each septic episode triggers endothelial dysfunction, increases blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability and promotes systemic hypoperfusion, compounding susceptibility to ischemic brain injury.44 Additionally, premature infants with NEC often experience periods of hemodynamic instability driven by hypotension, anemia and impaired cerebral autoregulation that diminishes oxygen and nutrient delivery to vulnerable periventricular regions.44 These children have higher rates of cerebral palsy, cognitive delay, attention deficits and sensory impairments compared with preterm peers without NEC.43 44

Longitudinal follow-up with developmental specialists is critical to ensure early identification of neurocognitive delays.43 Early intervention programs with occupational, physical, and speech therapy support synaptic plasticity and promote functional recovery during critical neurodevelopmental windows.43 Nutritional optimization plays a key role in neurodevelopment. Adequate protein, essential fatty acids, iron and micronutrients support myelination, neurotransmitter synthesis, and overall brain growth.45 Together coordinated neurodevelopmental surveillance and early therapeutic engagement are critical for post-NEC care aimed at mitigating long-term cognitive and motor sequelae.

Management strategies

The management of postoperative hyperinflammation following surgical NEC remains primarily supportive. Current clinical practice emphasizes:

Optimizing nutrition

Nutritional support is a cornerstone of recovery after NEC. Human milk, either maternal or donor, is consistently associated with improved intestinal repair, reduced inflammation and lower recurrence risk.46 Its bioactive components, including anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-7, IL-10, IL-18), EGF, oligosaccharides, immunoglobulin A (IgA), lactoferrin and anti-inflammatory fatty acids (omega-3 fatty acids), promote mucosal healing, modulate immune responses and attenuate the postoperative hyperinflammatory response.46 47 Human milk actively shapes the neonatal immune environment by downregulating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and promoting regulatory immune responses, therefore attenuating the cycle of postoperative inflammation.47

Additionally, careful advancement of enteral feeds following a ‘trophic feeding’ strategy allows for gradual adaptation of the remaining bowel while minimizing risk of intolerance or recurrence. PN should be used judiciously, balancing adequate caloric intake against the risks of cholestasis and infection.22 The addition of probiotics remains under investigation but may support intestinal microbial homeostasis and reduce inflammatory signaling when administered appropriately.48

Infection control

Given the high incidence of sepsis in postoperative NEC, infection control measures are critical.34 This includes evidence-based antibiotic stewardship to minimize microbial resistance and dysbiosis, meticulous central line care to prevent CLABSI and vigilant clinical surveillance for early signs of sepsis.31 Laboratory markers such as C-reactive protein, procalcitonin and serial blood cultures can guide antibiotic initiation and de-escalation.49 Effective control of infection not only reduces immediate morbidity but also helps attenuate systemic inflammatory activation and limit postoperative hyperinflammation. Infection control efforts must be coordinated across all disciplines to mitigate one of the most preventable causes of morbidity and mortality in this group.

Surgical considerations

Surgical strategy in NEC aims not only to remove necrotic tissue but also to preserve bowel length and promote long-term intestinal function.50 Second-look laparotomies can help identify evolving ischemic segments, preventing further necrosis while avoiding unnecessary resection.51 The creation of stomas such as an ileostomy or colostomy allows for decompression and control of contamination in unstable patients, with delayed reanastomosis once inflammation subsides.50 These staged approaches can minimize ongoing inflammatory insult and promote better nutritional recovery. In select cases, minimally invasive techniques may reduce systemic stress and postoperative adhesions, though their application in extremely low-birth-weight infants remains technically challenging.52

Multidisciplinary support

Successful recovery from surgical NEC requires coordinated multidisciplinary care, integrating neonatologists, pediatric surgeons, nutritionists, developmental specialists, infectious disease consultants and social workers. This team approach ensures comprehensive management encompassing medical stabilization, nutritional rehabilitation, neurodevelopmental monitoring and family support. Early involvement of occupational and physical therapists can promote feeding skill development and enhance neurodevelopmental outcomes.53 Moreover, psychosocial support for families coping with the prolonged and emotionally taxing course of NEC is essential to holistic care.53

Prognosis and long-term outcomes

Postoperative NEC remains associated with high mortality and significant long-term morbidity, reflecting the cumulative impact of systemic inflammation, intestinal loss and critical illness.1 Mortality rates among surgical NEC cases range from 30% to 50%, with outcomes strongly influenced by gestational age, residual bowel length and the severity of postoperative inflammatory complications.1

Long-term follow-up in multidisciplinary clinics is critical for surveillance of growth, neurodevelopment and intestinal adaptation. Early intervention services, tailored nutritional plans and proactive management of feeding difficulties can significantly improve functional outcomes and quality of life.53 Emerging evidence also suggests that structured intestinal rehabilitation programs may reduce dependence on PN and improve enteral tolerance over time.53

Conclusion

Postoperative hyperinflammation in NEC represents a crucial determinant of both short-term survival and long-term developmental outcomes. The pathophysiologic cascade of immune immaturity, cytokine overactivation and persistent gut barrier dysfunction extends beyond the immediate postoperative phase, shaping the trajectory of recovery.

Effective management requires integrated, multidisciplinary strategies that target inflammation, maintain nutritional integrity and prevent infectious and metabolic complications. Ultimately, optimizing recovery after NEC depends not only on surgical expertise but also on comprehensive, collaborative care that extends from the neonatal intensive care unit to long-term developmental follow-up.

Research gaps and future directions

Key areas for future investigation include the development of biomarker-guided immunomodulation, precision nutrition strategies and targeted microbiome-based therapies. Biomarker-driven approaches will be critical to identifying distinct inflammatory endotypes and guiding individualized therapeutic interventions, including the selective use of anti-inflammatory or immune-modulating agents in infants with persistent or dysregulated immune activation.54 Advances in cytokine profiling, transcriptomics and metabolomics may enable early risk stratification and real-time monitoring of therapeutic response.54

Precision nutrition strategies represent another promising avenue, particularly in optimizing intestinal adaptation while minimizing inflammatory burden. Tailored approaches to macronutrient composition, human milk utilization and bioactive supplementation (such as growth factors, immunoglobulins and short-chain fatty acid precursors) may help support epithelial repair, modulate immune responses and improve gut barrier function in a developmentally appropriate manner.55

Targeted microbiome-based therapies including probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics and potentially postbiotics offer the potential to restore microbial balance and disrupt pathogenic host–microbe interactions that perpetuate inflammation.56 Future work will need to focus on identifying specific microbial signatures associated with recovery versus disease progression as well as ensuring safety and efficacy in this highly vulnerable population.

Improved understanding of intestinal repair mechanisms and neurodevelopmental vulnerability will be essential to the development of NEC treatment. Advancing care in postoperative NEC will ultimately depend on translational research with targeted interventions that can interrupt the cycle of hyperinflammation and promote intestinal recovery.
