SGLT2 inhibitors reduce the salt sensitivity of blood pressure in type 2 diabetes via enhanced postprandial natriuresis
Authors: Martina Chiriacò, Domenico Tricò, Luca Sacchetta, Lorenzo Nesti, Noemi Cimbalo, Lorenza Santoni, Simone Gallo, Simona Baldi, Tiziana Scozzaro, Alberto Giannoni, Andrea Natali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) lower blood pressure (BP) and provide cardiovascular protection. In animal models, SGLT2i blunt sodium-induced BP elevation, but this effect remains unexplored in humans. We investigated whether SGLT2i reduce the salt sensitivity of BP in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and explored the underlying mechanisms.MethodsIn an open-label, non-randomized study, 26 patients with T2D (14 on treatment with SGLT2i, 12 controls) completed two sequential 7-day dietary phases consisting of a very-low-sodium background diet with the addition of either high sodium (HS, + 4,800 mg/day) or low sodium (LS, + 1,200 mg/day). Changes in twenty-four hour ambulatory BP monitoring (24-hour ABPM), postprandial natriuresis and diuresis, plasma renin and aldosterone concentrations, glycosuria, and hydration were assessed.ResultsThe median change in systolic BP between HS and LS diets was greater in controls (median [IQR] Δ24-h SBP: +5.6 [2.0, 20.3] mmHg, p = 0.005), than in the SGLT2i group (Δ24-h SBP: -1.2 [-3.6, 4.2] mmHg, p = 0.594; p = 0.007 between groups). Diastolic BP changes showed similar patterns (Δ24-h DBP: +4.6 [1.3, 9.8] mmHg vs. -1.2 [-3.1, 1.4] mmHg; p = 0.002 between groups). SGLT2i-treated participants showed enhanced postprandial natriuresis during both HS (median [IQR]: 55.0 [28.3, 126.6] µEq/min vs. -0.7 [-148.6, 109.0] µEq/min; p = 0.049) and the LS diet (18.4 [1.3, 42.2] µEq/min vs. -14.5 [-30.2, 15.7] µEq/min; p = 0.046), and postprandial diuresis during the HS diet (median [IQR]: 0.52 [0.10, 1.18] ml/min vs. -0.21 [-0.58, 0.45] ml/min p = 0.041). In response to the LS diet, renin and aldosterone levels increased markedly in controls but not in SGLT2i-treated patients. Hydration and glycosuria were unchanged and unrelated to natriuretic responses.ConclusionsIn T2D, treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors is associated with a reduced BP response to high sodium intake, accompanied by enhanced postprandial sodium and water excretion, along with attenuated neurohormonal activation. These findings identify physiological associations that may contribute to the BP-lowering and cardiovascular protective effects of SGLT2 inhibition.Trial registrationNCT06007157 (registered on 18/08/2023).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03095-2.Keywords: Blood pressure, Sodium, SGLT2 inhibitors, Salt sensitivity

Introduction

Salt sensitivity of blood pressure (BP) is a clinically relevant but highly heterogeneous trait, influenced by renal sodium handling, neurohormonal activation, vascular function, and metabolic status [1].

Dietary sodium restriction is a key non-pharmacological intervention for hypertension, with the DASH-Sodium trial showing that lowering sodium intake by ~ 1,800 mg/day reduces systolic BP by roughly 7 mmHg [2]. However, sustained sodium restriction is challenging to achieve in practice, and the BP response to reduced sodium intake varies markedly across individuals. The greatest benefit is seen in salt sensitive individuals, who exhibit considerable BP changes in response to dietary sodium modifications. Salt sensitivity affects about one-third of healthy individuals and more than half of those with hypertension, with even higher prevalence in people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1], and is independently associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, regardless of BP levels, sodium intake, or other risk factors [1, 3–5].

Reducing salt sensitivity could allow for more flexible dietary sodium recommendations, which remain difficult to maintain even under structured dietary protocols and nutritional counselling [6, 7]. Yet, effective pharmacological approaches to mitigate salt sensitivity are still lacking and largely exploratory [8].

Recent animal studies demonstrate that sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) blunt the hypertensive response to salt loading [9–12], but this effect has not been demonstrated in humans and the underlying physiological mechanisms remain unclear.

In general, SGLT2i consistently lower systolic BP (SBP) by 2–4 mmHg in clinical trials [13, 14], an effect traditionally attributed to osmotic diuresis and natriuresis [15, 16]. However, these renal effects are most prominent in the acute phase and tend to diminish with chronic therapy, raising questions about their contribution to long-term BP regulation and, specifically, to salt sensitivity [17–19]. Notably, Ferrannini et al. reported enhanced post-meal natriuresis shortly after a single pre-prandial dose of empagliflozin in individuals with and without T2D [20]. Although not explored in depth, this finding suggests that SGLT2i may increase post-prandial natriuresis, potentially mitigating the hemodynamic impact of dietary sodium intake and thereby reducing salt sensitivity. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted an exploratory, hypothesis-generating physiological study in individuals with T2D, examining BP responses to controlled variations in dietary sodium intake and assessing potential physiological correlates, including natriuresis, diuresis, neurohormonal activation, and changes in hydration status.

Methods

We conducted an exploratory open-label, non-randomized, dietary intervention study in individuals with T2D, comparing those treated with SGLT2i to matched controls receiving other glucose-lowering therapies.

Study population

Patients were prospectively enrolled from the Section of Dietology and Clinical Nutrition of the Santa Chiara University Hospital (Pisa, Italy). The study was conducted at the Laboratory of Metabolism, Nutrition and Atherosclerosis, University of Pisa, between March 2023 and September 2024. The overall study design is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Participants were not allocated to treatment by the study protocol; rather, group assignment reflected pre-existing chronic therapy. Individuals in the SGLT2i group were already receiving stable treatment for at least 12 weeks before enrollment, while control participants had not been treated with SGLT2i during the last 12 weeks. Groups were matched at baseline on key clinical characteristics relevant to BP regulation and sodium handling, including age, sex, BMI, baseline BP, renal function and background antihypertensive therapy. At the screening visit, demographic information, medical history, and current medication use were recorded, and a comprehensive physical examination was conducted. Inclusion criteria were: age 40–85 years; diagnosis of T2D; stable body weight and glucose-lowering therapy, for at least 12 weeks before enrollment. Main exclusion criteria included: insulin treatment; eGFR < 45 ml/min/1.73m2; CV events within the previous 6 months; history of cerebrovascular events; heart failure (HF) or treatment with loop diuretics; uncontrolled hypertension (BP > 160/100 mmHg); evidence of hypotension (SBP < 90 mmHg and/or DBP < 60 mmHg) or orthostatic hypotension (drop in SBP ≥ 20 mmHg or diastolic BP [DBP] ≥ 10 mmHg within 3 min of standing); BMI > 40 kg/m2; chronic inflammatory disease; active cancer; significant alteration of liver function (cirrhosis); uncontrolled tireopathy (TSH > 6.0 or < 0.1 µUI/ml); women with childbearing potential; life expectancy-changing systemic disease. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined according to the KDIGO guidelines, taking into account eGFR and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) categories [21].

Dietary intervention

According to current AHA guidelines for the gold-standard measurement of salt sensitivity [22], all patients underwent a controlled dietary intervention consisting of two consecutive dietary phases: a high-sodium (HS) phase followed by a low-sodium (LS) phase. Beginning with a HS intake suppresses the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and confers more uniformity to the subsequent BP response to LS intake, during which the physiological RAAS activation may vary across individuals.

Throughout the study, participants followed a very low sodium (VLS) background diet, designed by a registered dietitian and based on the Mediterranean diet, providing approximately 570 mg of sodium per day for men and 450 mg per day for women (Supplementary Material). During days 1–6 of each dietary phase, participants followed a “free VLS diet”, with the possibility to choose daily among a predefined range of low-sodium meal options. Although food choices could vary, all meal combinations were designed to provide comparable caloric content and sodium intake, approximating the target VLS sodium levels. Participants were instructed to prepare meals using unseasoned, low-sodium foods and to avoid high-sodium food throughout both dietary phases.

On the final day of each phase (day 7 for the HS phase and day 14 for the LS phase), participants consumed a standardized VLS diet, identical in food composition and quantity across all individuals, to minimize interindividual variability during physiological assessments. The standardized diet provided 1,845 kcal and 569 mg of sodium for men, and 1,452 kcal and 451 mg of sodium for women. Breakfast consisted of 200 mL of a low-sodium oral nutritional supplement (Hyperdrink Juicy; Nutrisens Italia, Collegno, Italy; 300 kcal, 12 mg sodium). As the majority of dietary sodium from standardized meals was consumed at dinner (Supplementary Table 1), dinner was selected as the index meal for postprandial physiological measurements.

Dietary sodium intake was modulated by the daily addition of sodium chloride vials to cooked food. During the HS phase, participants received 12 g of NaCl per day (equivalent to 4,800 mg of sodium), whereas during the LS phase they received 3 g of NaCl per day (equivalent to 1,200 mg of sodium). Sodium vials were consumed daily throughout each intervention week, including the final study day, and were added to meals in the same manner during both free and standardized VLS diet days. Adherence to the dietary intervention was monitored through weekly telephone contacts and objectively assessed by measurement of 24-hour urinary sodium excretion at the end of each dietary phase.

Clinical and biochemical measurements

Clinical and biochemical assessments were performed in the morning at 08:00 AM, following an overnight fast, on day 1 (baseline), day 8 (after HS phase), and day 15 (after LS phase), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Venous blood samples were obtained from a peripheral vein under fasting conditions. Anthropometric measurements, cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., family history, smoking habits), comorbidities, diabetic complications (micro- and macrovascular), and medications were recorded. Laboratory tests included blood cell count, fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin, blood lipids, creatinine, plasma and urine electrolytes, liver function, C-reactive protein (CRP), urinalysis, and uACR. Neurohormonal profiling was performed by measuring renin, aldosterone and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels. Plasma renin was measured by immunochemiluminometric assay and aldosterone by chemiluminescence immunoassay. Urine electrolytes were assayed by spectrophotometric enzymatic methods on a Beckman UniCel DXC600 Synchron Analyzer (Fullerton, CA). Within-assay and between-assay coefficients of variation ranged between < 1% and < 5% for all. Urinary glucose concentrations were determined by an enzymatic colorimetric glucose oxidase–peroxidase (GLUC-PAP) method using an RX DAYTONA PLUS automated analyzer (Randox Laboratories Ltd, UK).

Fractional urine collection

Urine collection was performed over the entire 24-hour period of the final day of each dietary phase (day 7 for HS and day 14 for LS), from 08:00 AM to 07:59 AM the following morning, using a fractionated collection protocol to assess pre- and post-meal natriuresis and diuresis (Supplementary Fig. 2). Urine was collected at defined intervals before and after meals, with total volume and timing recorded. The protocol began with the disposal of the first morning void, followed by urine collection until lunchtime. Immediately before lunch, a urine sample was obtained to assess preprandial natriuresis. The container was then emptied, and postprandial urine was collected over the subsequent 180 min after meal initiation, a time window corresponding to the expected postprandial insulin peak. For each collection period, total urine volume and exact time interval duration were recorded.

This procedure was repeated for dinner, with additional collections performed before sleep and upon waking the next morning. In total, five urine samples were collected for sodium quantification, enabling calculation of total natriuresis and sodium excretion rates. Participants received illustrated instructions (Supplementary Fig. 2) and were advised to thoroughly mix the urine in the graduated container before transferring the appropriate volume into numbered vials.

Ambulatory blood pressure measurements

To assess changes in BP in response to sodium intake and to determine salt sensitivity, we conducted 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure measurements (ABPM).

The ABPM recordings were performed during the final day each dietary phase (day 7 for HS and day 14 for LS), while patients continued their usual medical treatment, using an oscillometric BP monitor (TM 2440 - A&D Company, Tokyo, Japan) with adequate cuff size. BP readings were taken every 15 min during the daytime hours from 07:00 to 22:00 and every 30 min during the night-time hours from 22:00 to 07:00. The ABPM reading was analyzed with the Aries software (Intermed S.r.l., Milan, Italy) and considered acceptable if at least 70% of the BP recordings were deemed as valid. The ABPM readings were analyzed by an independent physician who was unaware of the background therapy of the participants. The BP endpoints were derived from the average values of SBP and DBP in the 24-hour, diurnal and nocturnal periods.

Bioimpedance analysis

Bioelectrical impedance analysis was performed under fasting conditions at 08:00 AM on days 1, 8, and 15, immediately after blood sampling. Measurements were obtained using a Tanita MC780 device (Tokyo, Japan), which measures total body water (TBW) and extracellular water (ECW). The ECW/TBW ratio was used as a marker of interstitial fluid content. Measurements of reactance (Xc) and resistance (R) enabled bioelectrical impedance vector analysis (BIVA), which evaluates an individual’s hydration status relative to a sex-specific healthy reference population [23]. Higher standard deviation (SD) values indicate overhydration, while lower values suggest dehydration. NT-proBNP levels were also measured to assess volume status.

Endpoints

The pre-specified primary endpoint was the change in 24-hour SBP in response to dietary sodium intake modification. Secondary endpoints included changes in 24-hour DBP, postprandial natriuresis, glucose excretion, and diuresis, as well as variations in hydration status and neuro-hormonal activation.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, and as median with interquartile range (Q1, Q3) for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables. Within-group differences in repeated measures were tested with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Correlation was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation test. Multivariable linear regression analyses reporting standardized regression coefficients (std β), tested the association between SGLT2i treatment and BP change while controlling for potential confounders. Statistical analysis was performed using a complete case analysis. The sample size was determined based on available data from the GenSalt and DASH studies [2, 24], we calculated that 30 participants (15 per group) would provide 80% power to detect a 6 mmHg difference in 24-hour SBP in response to sodium intake modifications. We successfully enrolled 30 patients, with 26 included in the final analysis after exclusion for non-compliance. Given the exploratory design of the study and the absence of randomization and blinding, all statistical analyses are mainly descriptive.

Analyses were performed using JMP Pro software version 18.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Graphical representations were realized with GraphPad Prism 8.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). All tests were conducted at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

Results

Population characteristics

Of the 30 enrolled patients, 4 (3 in the control group and 1 in the SGLT2i group) were excluded due to non-adherence to the dietary protocol. Thus, 26 patients (12 controls and 14 SGLT2i-treated individuals) were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The two groups showed comparable baseline characteristics, including age, sex distribution, adiposity indices, baseline BP, antihypertensive and antidiabetic medications, metabolic parameters, inflammatory markers (CRP), hydration status, renin and aldosterone concentrations, NT-proBNP levels, and renal function. In the SGLT2i group there was a higher, but non-significant, prevalence of CKD (p = 0.070) (Table 1). A high proportion of participants (70–80%) had controlled hypertension, with similar prevalence across groups. A total of 8 patients (57%) in the SGLT2i group were treated with empagliflozin and 6 (43%) with dapagliflozin, and both treatments were prescribed at their maximal approved doses (25 mg and 10 mg once daily, respectively).

Specifically, BP changes were significantly smaller in the SGLT2i group compared to controls across all time intervals: 24-hour, diurnal and nocturnal systolic and diastolic BP (Fig. 1; Table 2). In a multivariable analysis including relevant factors such as baseline eGFR, age, sex, BMI, and baseline SBP, SGLT2i treatment was the only independent variable associated with Δ24-BP responses to sodium manipulation (Δ24-h SBP: std β = 0.74, p = 0.001; Δ24-h DBP: std β = 0.63, p = 0.002), with the exception of BMI for Δ24-h SBP (Supplementary Table 2). Natriuresis and diuresis

In the fasting state, both groups showed a higher fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa, %) at the end of the HS diet compared to the LS diet (Table 2).

The median difference in 24-hour sodium excretion between the HS and LS diets was 2850 [2340, 4273] mg in the control group (p < 0.001) and 2455 [1752, 2701] mg in the SGLT2i group (p < 0.001), with no difference between the two study groups (p = 0.057), (Table 2). This was accompanied by an increase in 24-hour diuresis with the HS diet (median [IQR]: 413 [-170, 741] ml, p = 0.004) in the control group, while the numerical increase was not statistically significant in the SGLT2i group (median [IQR]: 170 [-145, 540], p = 0.087), with no differences in 24-hour diuresis change between groups (p = 0.163) (Table 2).

In both groups, the postprandial changes in natriuresis were more pronounced after dinner, consistently with the higher sodium content of the standard dinner compared to lunch (Table 3, Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, the SGLT2i group exhibited greater post-dinner natriuresis compared to controls during both the HS diet (median [IQR]: 55.0 [28.3, 126.6] µEq/min vs. -0.7 [-148.6, 109.0] µEq/min; p = 0.049) and the LS diet (median [IQR]: 18.4 [1.3, 42.2] µEq/min vs. -14.5 [-30.2, 15.7] µEq/min; p = 0.046), with lower absolute values during the LS diet (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the SGLT2i group demonstrated a higher post-dinner diuresis compared to controls during the HS diet (median [IQR]: 0.52 [0.10, 1.18] ml/min vs. -0.21 [-0.58, 0.45] ml/min p = 0.041) (Fig. 2B).

We additionally examined the between-diet difference (HS-LS) in postprandial responses (Table 3). When expressed as HS-LS differences, post-dinner natriuresis was significantly greater in the SGLT2i group compared with controls (median [IQR]: 52.1 [10.9, 103.0] vs. -12.4 [-102.2, 82.9] µEq/min; p = 0.031), indicating an enhanced postprandial sodium excretion during HS intake relative to LS intake in SGLT2i-treated patients. A similar pattern was observed for post-dinner diuresis, with a significantly larger HS-LS difference in the SGLT2i group than in controls (0.22 [-0.21, 1.20] vs. -0.17 [-0.54, 0.10] mL/min; p = 0.032). No significant between-group differences were observed for post-lunch HS–LS changes in natriuresis or diuresis.

Additionally, an increase in nocturnal natriuresis with the HS diet compared to the LS diet was observed in both groups, and it was larger in the control group than in the SGLT2i group (median [IQR]: 41.9 (11.2, 74.7) µEq/min vs. 27.0 (15.2, 34.9) µEq/min; p = 0.044), (Table 2).

Correlation between natriuresis changes and blood pressure changes

To further explore the relationship between postprandial natriuresis in the BP response to sodium intake, we performed a correlation analysis between the change in 24-hour BP (HS-LS diet) and the change in postprandial natriuresis (HS-LS diet at dinner) in the whole study population.

We found that post-dinner natriuresis inversely correlated with 24-hour BP changes in response to sodium intake, for both SBP (ρ=-0.56; p = 0.006) and DBP (ρ=-0.42; p = 0.045) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Glycosuria

Glucose excretion was analyzed only in the SGLT2i group, as glycosuria in the control group was negligible (approximately 0.04 g/day during both weeks). Total daily glucose excretion was similar between the HS and LS diets (61 [42–72] vs. 55 [41–74] g/day, p = 0.910), and independent of sodium excretion (ρ=-0.003; p = 0.213) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistently, no significant correlations were observed between absolute sodium and glucose excretion rates, before or after meals, under either the HS or LS diet conditions. Likewise, there were no significant associations between the relative postprandial changes in sodium and glucose excretion (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Similarly, no association between glucose excretion and diuresis was evident before or after meals, nor there was a correlation between postprandial changes with changes in diuresis (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Neurohormonal activation and hydration

Neurohormonal activation was evaluated by measuring plasma renin and aldosterone levels at the end of each week. In the control group, renin levels increased significantly during the LS diet compared to the HS diet, consistent with neurohormonal activation (median [IQR]: 30.1 [12.8, 542.2] vs. 7.1 [3.7, 20.5] mUI/l; p = 0.044), with marked interindividual variability, whereas in the SGLT2i group the increase did not reach statistical significance (24.9 [10.9, 160.2] vs. 9.5 [5.2, 17.4] mU/L; p = 0.065). Similarly, aldosterone levels increased with the low-sodium diet in controls (11.9 [7.9, 17.6] vs. 7.1 [5.3, 12.4] ng/dL; p = 0.0002), while no significant change was observed in the SGLT2i group (13.3 [10.9, 20.3] vs. 10.5 [5.9, 17.4] ng/dL; p = 0.174) (Fig. 3). The median change in NT-proBNP levels between HS and LS diets was higher in the control group than in the SGLT2i group (Table 2). Changes in hydration status assessed by BIVA, along with alterations in extracellular water distribution and total body water, were small and similar in both groups (Table 2).

Renal function

At the end of the HS diet, eGFR was higher in both controls and SGLT2i-treated patients than at the end of the LS diet (Table 2). The change in eGFR, although modest, was greater in the control group than in SGLT2i (2.9 [2.1, 10.8] vs. 1.4 [-0.3, 4.7] mL/min/1.73 m², p = 0.041) and linearly correlated with the change in 24-hour SBP in both the SGLT2i group (ρ = 0.35, p = 0.024) and controls (ρ = 0.39, p = 0.042), reflecting the physiological BP-natriuresis response (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Metabolic and inflammatory markers

We evaluated the effects of dietary sodium manipulation on metabolic parameters, including total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and fasting plasma glucose, along with inflammatory markers (CRP). None of these variables showed significant changes with the dietary intervention in either group (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Discussion

In this exploratory physiological study, we show that individuals with T2D receiving chronic SGLT2 inhibition exhibit a markedly attenuated BP response to controlled variations in dietary sodium intake, indicating reduced salt sensitivity. This blunted BP reactivity was accompanied by an enhanced postprandial natriuretic and diuretic response and a diminished neurohormonal activation during a low sodium intake, consistent with coordinated modulation of sodium handling and RAAS activity.

To date, evidence regarding the impact of SGLT2 inhibition on salt sensitivity has been limited to preclinical studies. Specifically, Takeshige et al. reported that obese rats treated with empagliflozin displayed reduced BP responses to sodium loading along with increased urinary sodium excretion [9]. Similar results were observed by Kim et al. in uninephrectomized rats, and by Wan et al. in models of CKD treated with luseogliflozin [10, 11]. Likewise, in a nondiabetic model of salt sensitive hypertension, Kravtsova et al. demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibition mitigated BP elevation without major compensatory changes in other sodium transporters or hormonal regulators [12]. Whether these effects translated to humans, however, had remained unknown until the present study.

Our novel findings show that chronic SGLT2 inhibition is associated with reduced salt sensitivity in individuals with T2D. Importantly, these findings were observed under conditions of prolonged SGLT2 inhibitor treatment, when acute osmotic diuretic effects are known to be attenuated [17, 18]. Indeed, consistent with previous studies, the participants on chronic SGLT2 inhibition showed comparable total 24-hour natriuresis with controls, under either HS or LS intake conditions, However, the attenuated BP response to sodium loading was accompanied by an increase in postprandial natriuresis and diuresis, consistent with a redistribution of sodium and water excretion toward the postprandial period [25]. Supporting this shift, the SGLT2i group showed reduced nocturnal sodium excretion in response to high sodium intake than controls, suggesting that more sodium was cleared after meals and less overnight in SGLT2i-treated patients. This complementary pattern (greater postprandial but lower nocturnal sodium excretion) may help account for why overall 24-hour natriuresis remained similar despite different post-feeding sodium handling.

From a physiological perspective, our findings are compatible with the hypothesis that SGLT2i may exert their strongest effects during feeding, when sodium entry increases and insulin promotes renal sodium retention [26]. Indeed, insulin has a well-established sodium-retentive action on the distal nephron, a physiological mechanism that helps restore sodium balance after meals, compensating for fasting-induced renal sodium losses [27]. In addition, the inhibitory effect of SGLT2i may be more pronounced after eating, when SGLT2 transporter activity is likely increased due to higher glucose availability, resulting in greater glucose filtration and, consequently, increased SGLT2-mediated glucose and sodium reabsorption [28].

The feeding-related timing of this effect is consistent with the observations of Ferrannini et al., who reported that empagliflozin increases postprandial natriuresis both with acute and chronic administration, while leaving fasting natriuresis unchanged [18]. In our study, the control group exhibited reductions in natriuresis and diuresis after dinner (the meal with the highest sodium content), reflecting the physiological insulin-mediated antinatriuretic and antidiuretic response. In contrast, patients treated with SGLT2i showed increases in both parameters, indicating enhanced sodium and water excretion following sodium intake (Table 3). These findings are consistent with and extend the observations of Ferrannini et al. under free-living conditions, demonstrating a concurrent increase in postprandial diuresis and, associating this effect to a reduced BP response to high sodium intake. Indeed, the potential physiological relevance of enhanced postprandial natriuresis is further supported by the inverse correlation observed: greater postprandial natriuresis was associated with smaller increases in BP in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Importantly, the observed changes in natriuresis and diuresis do not appear to be driven by osmotic mechanisms related to glycosuria. In our cohort, variations in sodium or water excretion were not correlated with changes in urinary glucose, indicating a dissociation between glucose and sodium handling both in the fasting state and after meals. This aligns with previous studies showing that glycosuria-driven osmotic diuresis is most prominent at treatment initiation and stabilizes during chronic SGLT2i use [29, 30].

In contrast, the selective postprandial increase in natriuresis and diuresis observed in our study occurred in the context of chronic treatment, suggesting a sustained, meal-specific difference in renal sodium handling, compatible with long-term adaptations in renal sodium handling rather than transient drug effects. To this regard, ~ 30% of participants in both groups were treated with thiazide diuretics, which can acutely increase diuresis and natriuresis. However, these effects diminish with chronic use due to renal adaptive mechanisms (including nephron transporter upregulation and RAAS modulation) [31, 32]. Thus, diuretic treatment is unlikely to have influenced our findings, consistent with the multivariable analysis showing that SGLT2i treatment remained independently associated with BP change after adjustment for diuretic use (Supplementary Table 3).

We also found that the increase in eGFR typically associated with sodium loading and BP elevation [33] was attenuated in SGLT2i-treated patients. This effect is likely attributable to the smaller BP changes observed in this group, as the relationship between BP reduction and eGFR decline was similar between groups (Supplementary Fig. 7). Of note, a larger proportion of SGLT2i-treated participants had early CKD, which could theoretically influence SGLT2i-related natriuresis as the osmotic diuretic effect declines with reduced kidney function (particularly at eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m²) [34]. However, we excluded advanced CKD, and available evidence indicates that BP lowering with SGLT2i is largely independent of baseline eGFR [35]. Therefore, early CKD is unlikely to have materially influenced BP responses in our study, consistent with multivariable analyses showing no association between baseline eGFR and BP changes, whereas SGLT2i treatment remained strongly independently associated (Supplementary Table 2).

A notable and unexpected finding was the blunted neurohormonal response to LS intake in SGLT2i-treated patients. While this effect was evident at the group level, the renin response showed substantial interindividual heterogeneity, consistent with known variability in RAAS activation. The known physiological response to sodium depletion is the increase in renin and aldosterone secretion caused by the reduced sodium chloride delivery to the macula densa, which activates antinatriuretic pathways [36–38]. In contrast, SGLT2i-treated individuals in our study showed a markedly attenuated rise in renin and aldosterone during sodium restriction. Whether this reflects a long-term adaptation, increased macula densa sodium delivery, or an acute pharmacodynamic effect cannot be determined from our study design. However, chronic SGLT2 inhibition is known to increase sodium delivery to the macula densa, reducing the stimulus for renin release, and the evidence from our findings of persistence of enhanced postprandial natriuresis during low sodium intake in our study, is consistent with this interpretation.

Importantly, while increased postprandial natriuresis may help blunt daytime BP responses, it does not fully explain the nocturnal BP differences observed in the SGLT2i group. Nocturnal BP regulation is strongly influenced by RAAS activity [39], and the blunted renin and aldosterone responses suggest that SGLT2 inhibition may modulate neurohormonal activity in a way that stabilizes BP across the 24-hour cycle.

In this regard, the observed attenuation of neurohormonal activation in SGLT2i-treated participants may also be relevant in conditions marked by RAAS dysregulation, such as heart failure (HF), where SGLT2i reduce hospitalizations [16, 40]. Notably, low sodium intake in HF has been associated with poorer outcomes [41–43], likely due to RAAS activation triggered by sodium depletion [44]. If SGLT2i are associated with a blunted response, as suggested by our findings, this may contribute to their established benefits in HF. Notably, most participants were hypertensive and treated with ACE/ARBs inhibitors, which could limit the interpretability of our findings regarding RAAS changes. However, the RAAS activation observed in response to the LS diet reflects the physiological response to sodium restriction, even more so in the presence of background RAAS blockade. Additionally, we excluded individuals with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension, but given the prominent roles of salt sensitivity and neurohormonal activation in these conditions [45], it is plausible that the effects of SGLT2i could be at least as pronounced, or even greater, in these phenotypes.

Although SGLT2i appear to facilitate the handling of excess dietary sodium, the mechanisms by which they prevent BP elevation may extend beyond natriuresis alone. In our study, we did not observe meaningful changes in hydration status attributable to sodium intake, and differences in ECW and TBW between groups were minimal. Notably, NT-proBNP, which correlates with volume status [46], rose significantly in the control group during HS intake but remained stable in SGLT2i-treated patients, suggesting a reduced dependence of circulating volume on dietary sodium. However, the magnitude of the NT-proBNP change was modest and remained below established clinical cutoffs, supporting a physiological signal rather than a clinically relevant interpretation.

Nevertheless, direct measurements of effective circulating volume were not performed. Moreover, sodium-induced changes in BP are not solely mediated by volume expansion: hormonal, vascular, and endothelial pathways also play key roles and were not assessed in this study [47]. In particular, SGLT2i may influence non-osmotic sodium storage in tissues such as skin, muscle, or the endothelial glycocalyx, a compartment increasingly recognized in salt-sensitive hypertension, but not captured by 24-hour urinary sodium excretion [48].

Our study has some limitations. The final analyzed sample was smaller than originally planned due to participant non-compliance, rendering the study underpowered relative to the a priori sample size calculation. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Within this scope, the between-group differences were consistent and directionally concordant, supporting the main findings, although confirmatory studies in larger cohorts are warranted. Second, the study was non-randomized and open-label, and participants were not allocated to treatment by the protocol but were enrolled based on pre-existing clinical therapy. Although groups were matched at baseline, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Additionally, diet order was not randomized, which represents a limitation. However, recent evidence from a large crossover trial suggests that the BP response to sodium manipulation is largely independent of intervention sequence [49].

Third, dietary sodium intake was not controlled through direct supervision or provision of pre-packaged meals. Despite this, the sodium excretion achieved a median of 5,376 mg during the HS diet and 2,566 mg during the LS diet, which were reasonably close to the targeted intakes of 4,800 mg and 1,200 mg, after accounting for the contribution of the baseline sodium content of the VLS diet (~ 450–570 mg/day). Finally, ad libitum fluid intake was not recorded during the dietary intervention period, which may limit the ability to fully account for individual variations in volume status.

Conclusions

This study highlights a previously unrecognized physiological association in which SGLT2 inhibition may mitigate the hemodynamic consequences of dietary sodium excess. We provide the first evidence that chronic SGLT2 inhibition is associated with reduced salt sensitivity in humans, a key determinant of hypertension and cardiovascular risk. This effect was accompanied by a targeted enhancement of postprandial natriuresis and diuresis, together with blunted neurohormonal activation. These findings support further investigation of SGLT2 inhibition as a potential approach to attenuate the vascular and neurohormonal consequences of sodium excess, with particular relevance for individuals with T2D, who commonly display increase salt sensitivity.
