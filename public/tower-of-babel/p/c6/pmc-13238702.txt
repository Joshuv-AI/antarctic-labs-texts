Laparoscopic Repair of a Strangulated Right Diaphragmatic Hernia Following Blunt Trauma
Authors: Rany Aoun, Rhea Akel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRight‐sided traumatic diaphragmatic hernias are rare and easily missed due to liver shielding. Persistent or delayed respiratory or abdominal symptoms after blunt trauma warrant CT evaluation. Early laparoscopic repair enables safe reduction, tension‐free closure, and rapid recovery, preventing life‐threatening complications like strangulation or bowel ischemia.Keywords: blunt trauma, colic obstruction, diaphragmatic hernia, laparoscopic surgery

Case Presentation

A 67‐year‐old woman presented to the emergency department with acute dyspnea, diffuse abdominal pain, and nausea. Three days earlier, she had sustained a right‐sided blunt trauma from a ground‐level fall, without any head injury or loss of consciousness.

Physical examination showed a hemodynamically stable patient, with a distended abdomen but no signs of peritonitis. No external signs of trauma or chest wall deformities were observed. Laboratory investigations were within normal limits.

CT scan revealed a right diaphragmatic defect measuring approximately 23 mm, through which the hepatic flexure of the colon had herniated into the thoracic cavity (Figure 1). The herniated colon appeared distended, and the findings were consistent with a strangulated right diaphragmatic hernia causing mechanical intestinal obstruction (Figure 2). There was no evidence of pneumothorax or free intraperitoneal fluid.

Patient underwent urgent laparoscopic intervention. The colon was gently reduced into the abdominal cavity and inspected for viability, which was preserved. The diaphragmatic defect was closed with interrupted non‐resorbable sutures.

The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on day four in good general condition. She was discharged on day four in good general condition. Follow up at 1 month showed a healthy woman completely recovered without any complaints.

Traumatic diaphragmatic hernias are rare, occurring in approximately 0.8%–5% of blunt thoracoabdominal injuries [1]. The diagnosis is frequently missed during initial evaluation, especially in right‐sided cases where the liver obscures the defect. Clinical presentation can vary from asymptomatic to acute respiratory distress or intestinal obstruction, depending on the herniated contents and timing of presentation [2]. The delayed onset of symptoms highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in post‐traumatic abdominal or respiratory complaints.

Computed tomography remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Early diagnosis and surgical repair are crucial to prevent complications like ischemia, necrosis, or perforation of the herniated bowel.

Laparoscopic repair has gained acceptance in the therapeutic approach to diaphragmatic injuries [3]. It allows for direct visualization of the defect, gentle reduction of herniated organs, and tension‐free closure. Compared to open repair, laparoscopic treatment offers the advantages of reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery. However, it requires careful patient selection and experienced surgical technique, particularly in cases of strangulation or compromised bowel viability.

Primary suture closure with non‐resorbable material remains the standard technique for small defects, like in our case. Mesh reinforcement is reserved for larger or tension‐prone defects.
