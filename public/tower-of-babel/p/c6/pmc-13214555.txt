Female sex, vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge, sleep quality, and excess weight are associated with intention to lose weight among university students: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Jacksaint Saintila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntention to lose weight (ILW) is common among university students and may be influenced by sociodemographic, nutritional, behavioral, and anthropometric factors. However, evidence integrating these factors in university populations remains limited.ObjectiveTo analyze sociodemographic, lifestyle, vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge (NK), sleep quality, and body mass index (BMI) factors associated with ILW among university students.MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among 301 university students. Sociodemographic variables, sleep quality, physical activity, vegetarian status, NK, and BMI were assessed. Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (PR/aPR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).ResultsThe prevalence of ILW during the previous 12 months was 56.8% (n = 171; 95% CI: 51.1–62.4). Female sex (aPR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.03–1.61; p = 0.028), vegetarian status (aPR = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.08–1.52; p = 0.004), poor sleep quality according to the PSQI (aPR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.13–1.32; p < 0.001), and excess weight (aPR = 1.94; 95% CI: 1.55–2.42; p < 0.001) were associated with a higher prevalence of ILW. Compared with high NK, low NK (aPR = 0.62; 95% CI: 0.44–0.87; p = 0.006) and medium NK (aPR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.63–0.99; p = 0.041) were associated with a lower prevalence of ILW.ConclusionILW was frequent among university students and was independently associated with female sex, vegetarian status, higher NK, poor sleep quality, and excess weight. These findings may inform university-based strategies for nutrition education, sleep health, and supervised weight-management counseling.

Introduction

Overweight and obesity are growing public health problems in university populations. During this stage, students experience changes in lifestyle, dietary autonomy, irregular academic schedules, and physical activity patterns, all of which may contribute to weight gain [1,2]. In this context, intention to lose weight, understood as a behavioral approach to weight control [3], is common among university students, particularly among those with overweight or obesity [4]. This intention may reflect greater awareness of health and the risks associated with excess weight; however, it may also be influenced by body dissatisfaction, appearance-related pressure, and subjective weight perception [4,5].

Therefore, studying intention to lose weight is relevant because it represents a prior psychological component that may guide behaviors such as dietary modification, increased physical activity, and regulation of food intake. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior, intention to lose weight, together with perceived behavioral control, has been shown to predict weight loss during follow-up [3]. However, strong intentions do not always translate into sustained behavioral changes, a phenomenon described as the intention-behavior gap in weight management [6,7]. Among university students, this intention has been linked to dieting and exercise practices, as well as to higher physical activity and healthier eating patterns [5,[8], [9], [10]]. Thus, intention to lose weight can be considered a relevant indicator within the weight-management process, although it does not necessarily guarantee the adoption of healthy strategies.

Previous studies show that intention to lose weight is associated with multiple factors. Female sex and excess weight are among the most consistent determinants. For example, women more frequently report perceived excess weight and attempts to lose weight, particularly as BMI increases [11]. In addition, a systematic review found that perceiving oneself as overweight is strongly associated with intention or attempts to lose weight, regardless of actual weight status [12]. Vegetarianism may also be related to this intention, although the association is more complex. Although vegetarian diets may be adopted for ethical, environmental, or health-related reasons, weight loss or weight control has also been described as a frequent motivation for adopting plant-based dietary patterns [13]. In university populations, some studies have reported that certain vegetarian or semi-vegetarian subgroups show greater dietary restraint or disordered eating behaviors, suggesting that, in some cases, these patterns may be part of broader weight-control strategies [14,15].

Other behavioral and cognitive factors may also be relevant. Higher nutritional knowledge may help individuals identify healthier weight-control strategies, such as improving food choices, reducing energy intake, and increasing physical activity [16,17]. However, available evidence has focused mainly on actual weight-loss behaviors rather than declared intention. Similarly, poor sleep quality has been associated with less successful weight-loss interventions, lower adherence to diet and physical activity recommendations, and greater difficulty maintaining lost weight [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Although these findings do not prove that poor sleep directly increases intention to lose weight, they suggest that sleep may be part of the behavioral and metabolic context related to weight management.

Although the frequency of weight-loss attempts and their relationship with sex, BMI, body perception, and some lifestyle behaviors have been documented, evidence among Peruvian university students remains limited. Moreover, few studies have simultaneously integrated sociodemographic, nutritional, behavioral, and anthropometric variables to explain intention to lose weight. In particular, little is known about the combined role of vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge, sleep quality, and excess weight in this population. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze sociodemographic, lifestyle, vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge, sleep quality, and body mass index factors associated with intention to lose weight among university students. We hypothesized that female sex, vegetarian status, higher nutritional knowledge, poor sleep quality, and excess weight would be associated with a higher prevalence of intention to lose weight.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted among university students between March and April 2026. The sample was selected using non-probability convenience sampling. Recruitment was carried out through a face-to-face intercept approach at the two main gates of a private university in East Lima, Peru, during periods of higher student circulation, mainly in mid-morning after the first academic hour. Students were invited to participate when leaving the campus after receiving an explanation of the study objectives and confirming their voluntary participation. Although recruitment was conducted during periods of high student circulation, this approach may have underrepresented students attending evening classes or those spending less time in the main campus areas. Data collection included a structured survey and field-based anthropometric measurements. The survey collected: (a) sociodemographic information; (b) lifestyle variables, such as physical activity frequency and sleep quality; (c) a specific question to assess vegetarian status; (d) a questionnaire to assess nutritional knowledge; and (e) intention to lose weight, which was considered the dependent variable.

Direct anthropometric measurements were also taken in the university exit areas. Body weight and height were measured using a scale with an integrated stadiometer. Students of both sexes who were enrolled during the 2026-I academic term and agreed to participate voluntarily were included. Records with incomplete information in the main study variables were excluded. After database cleaning, the final analytical sample consisted of 301 university students.

Sample size and selection criteria

The sample size was estimated using Free Statistics Calculators version 4.0, considering an effect size of 0.15, statistical power of 0.80, 12 predictor variables, and a significance level of 0.05 [23]. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was 127 participants. However, to compensate for possible losses and incomplete records, the initial plan was to recruit 350 students. During data cleaning, 49 records were excluded because of incomplete information in the main variables; therefore, the final sample consisted of 301 university students, exceeding the minimum estimated sample size.

Data collection instruments and variables

Sociodemographic variables. Information was collected on age, sex, geographic origin, residence, academic year, faculty, and religion. These variables were included in the descriptive analyses and considered as covariates in the adjusted model according to their conceptual relevance and availability in the database.

Sleep quality. Sleep quality was assessed using the Spanish version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [24]. This questionnaire includes 19 items organized into seven dimensions: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction [24]. Each dimension is scored from 0 to 3, with lower values reflecting better sleep quality and higher values indicating greater sleep-related difficulties. The sum of the seven dimensions generates a global score ranging from 0 to 21 points. According to the criteria proposed for the instrument, a global score of ≤ 5 indicates good sleep quality, whereas values > 5 indicate poor sleep quality [24]. Regarding its psychometric properties, the PSQI has shown adequate reliability, with a reported Cronbach's alpha of 0.81 [25].

Physical activity. Physical activity was assessed using the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF) [26]. This instrument includes seven items: six collect information on the frequency (days) and duration (minutes per day) of vigorous-intensity, moderate-intensity, and walking activities during the previous seven days [27]. The remaining item explores time spent sitting as an indicator of sedentary behavior during the previous week. Previous studies have reported acceptable psychometric properties for the IPAQ-SF, with test-retest reliability coefficients reaching 0.79 [28]. Moderate criterion validity has also been documented when compared with accelerometry (Spearman coefficient = 0.30; 95% CI: 0.23–0.36) [29]. According to standardized IPAQ criteria, participants were classified into three levels: low, when they did not meet the criteria for the other categories; moderate, when they achieved at least 600 MET-min/week through a combination of walking and moderate or vigorous physical activity; and high, when they accumulated at least 1500 MET-min/week of vigorous activity or 3000 MET-min/week of total physical activity.

Vegetarian status. Dietary pattern was determined by self-report using the question: “What type of diet do you follow?” Based on the participant's response, two categories were established. The non-vegetarian category included students who reported consuming red meat, poultry, fish, and plant-based foods. The vegetarian category included those who reported following a diet based on plant foods and who could also consume milk, eggs, and their derivatives. This classification differentiated an omnivorous dietary pattern from a lacto-ovo-vegetarian pattern, using an operational definition centered on the exclusion of meat and fish from habitual intake.

Nutritional knowledge. Nutritional knowledge was assessed using a 16-item structured questionnaire on healthy eating, originally developed based on a literature review and the WHO nutritional pyramid [30]. The original instrument showed acceptable reliability using the Kuder-Richardson coefficient (KR = 0.807) [30]. In the present study, each correct answer received 2 points, while incorrect or unanswered items received 0 points, generating a total possible score ranging from 0 to 32 points. The score was categorized into three levels: low (<17 points), medium (17–25 points), and high (>25 points) [30]. In the current sample, reliability was assessed using the Kuder-Richardson coefficient, yielding a KR-20 value of 0.73, which suggests acceptable internal consistency according to commonly used psychometric criteria for coefficients ≥0.70 [31].

Body mass index. Anthropometric data were collected following standardized procedures for measuring adult weight and height, using a calibrated SECA 813 digital scale (SECA®, Hamburg, Germany) with a maximum capacity of 200 kg and a SECA 213 portable stadiometer with a measurement range of 20–205 cm. BMI was calculated using Quetelet's index by dividing weight in kilograms by height in meters squared (kg/m2). For this study, BMI was classified into two categories: normal weight and excess weight. This classification was based on adult cutoff points, where excess weight includes BMI values of 25.0 kg/m2 or higher [32].

Intention to lose weight. Intention to lose weight was assessed using the following direct question: “During the past 12 months, have you tried to lose weight?” Responses were recorded dichotomously (yes/no) [[33], [34], [35]]. This variable was considered the main study outcome and allowed identification of students who, during a recent period, had reported behavior oriented toward body weight control or reduction. This measure represents a self-reported approximation of intention or attempt to lose weight and is widely used in epidemiological studies on weight control and body perception [[33], [34], [35]].

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was performed for sociodemographic, lifestyle, vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge, and body mass index variables. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and age was summarized using mean and standard deviation. Pearson's chi-square test was used to compare participant characteristics according to intention to lose weight. Crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs), with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), were then estimated using Poisson regression with robust variance. The adjusted model included age, sex, residence, academic year, faculty, vegetarian status, nutritional knowledge level, sleep quality, and body mass index. Physical activity was analyzed descriptively and in bivariate models; however, it was not included in the final adjusted model because of the low frequency of students in the high physical activity category, which could generate unstable estimates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, more than half of the students reported having tried to lose weight during the previous 12 months. This intention was more prevalent among women, vegetarian students, participants with higher nutritional knowledge, poor sleep quality, and excess weight. These findings suggest that intention to lose weight among university students is not determined only by weight status, but also by cognitive, behavioral, and lifestyle factors.

The prevalence observed in the present sample was higher than that reported in previous studies of university populations. Whereas 56.8% of students in this study reported intention to lose weight, the proportion was 47.9% among students in Madrid [36,37].8% in another university study [38], and 30% among university students in Beirut [37]. This difference may be explained, at least in part, by the high proportion of students with excess weight in the current sample. In this context, intention to lose weight may represent an opportunity to promote healthy habits, although it may also reflect body-related concern or pressure to modify physical appearance.

Students with excess weight had an almost twofold higher prevalence of intention to lose weight than those with normal BMI; moreover, among those reporting this intention, 65.5% had excess weight. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that weight-loss attempts increase progressively as BMI rises [11], as well as with evidence of more frequent attempts among young people who perceive themselves as having overweight or obesity [39]. Similarly, Ojala et al. [40] observed that adolescents with overweight or obesity were more likely to try to lose weight. However, Radwan et al. [4] warn that those trying to lose weight are not always those who most need it clinically, and that some people with excess weight may not attempt to lose weight if they perceive their weight as appropriate.

Women had a 29% higher prevalence of intention to lose weight than men. This finding is consistent with studies reporting a higher frequency of weight-loss attempts among university women [11,38], adult women [41], and adolescent girls [42]. This difference may be related to greater sociocultural pressure on female body image and a greater likelihood of perceiving oneself as overweight even within normal BMI ranges. However, because this study did not assess weight perception, body satisfaction, social media influence, or specific motivations for losing weight, it is not possible to determine whether intention to lose weight was mainly driven by health reasons, appearance concerns, or body dissatisfaction [43].

Vegetarian students also showed a higher prevalence of intention to lose weight. This result is consistent with studies describing a complex relationship between vegetarianism, weight control, and eating behaviors. For example, vegetarians have been reported to consume more fruits and vegetables, have lower fat intake, and be less likely to have excess weight; however, they may also show more frequent loss-of-control eating episodes or extreme weight-control behaviors, especially among former vegetarians [44]. Some students also adopt vegetarianism for weight-related reasons and may show greater dietary restraint, overvaluation of weight/shape, and body dissatisfaction [45]. Even so, vegetarianism should not necessarily be interpreted as a restrictive behavior, since it may respond to ethical, religious, environmental, cultural, or health-related motivations [46], and when well planned, it may be associated with better diet quality and lower BMI [47]. Therefore, it is not possible to establish whether intention to lose weight preceded the adoption of vegetarianism or whether both phenomena share underlying factors such as greater nutritional awareness, health concern, or body self-perception.

Students with low and medium nutritional knowledge showed a lower prevalence of intention to lose weight than those with high knowledge. This association persisted after adjustment for sociodemographic variables, vegetarian status, sleep quality, and BMI, suggesting that nutritional knowledge may be related to greater awareness of eating behaviors and weight control. Laz et al. [16] reported that higher nutritional knowledge was associated with healthy weight-loss practices, such as eating less, choosing lower-calorie products, increasing physical activity, consuming more fruits and vegetables, and reducing sugars. Similarly, Belogianni et al. [48] found that nutritional knowledge was related to healthier dietary behaviors among university students in the United Kingdom. However, the evidence is not uniform. For example, Navia et al. [36] found no important differences in dietary habits or nutritional knowledge between university students with and without desire to lose weight, while a study among Peruvian university students reported that knowledge of healthy eating does not necessarily translate into better eating habits or attitudes [30]. Therefore, this association should be interpreted with caution, as the cross-sectional design does not allow determination of whether knowledge promotes intention to lose weight or whether students who wish to lose weight seek more nutritional information.

Finally, poor sleep quality was associated with a higher prevalence of intention to lose weight. Students with PSQI >5 had a 22% higher prevalence of this intention, which may reflect a relationship between poor sleep, weight concern, and weight-control behaviors. Vargas et al. [49] found that poor sleep quality among university students was associated with a higher likelihood of excess weight, a condition that may motivate intention to lose weight. Hawkins et al. [20] also observed that better sleep health was inversely related to BMI and self-reported weight change among adults who had attempted to lose weight. From a biological and behavioral perspective, poor sleep may promote higher energy intake, consumption of energy-dense foods, irregular eating patterns, and alterations in leptin and ghrelin, mechanisms that could hinder weight control [50]. However, Claydon et al. [51] found no association between dieting or exercising to lose weight and subjective sleep quality among university students. Consequently, the relationship observed in the present study may depend on the interaction between poor sleep quality, excess weight, academic stress, fatigue, eating habits, and body image.

These results indicate that intention to lose weight among university students is a multifactorial phenomenon associated not only with BMI, but also with sex, dietary pattern, nutritional knowledge, and sleep quality. This approach may help guide university-based interventions that integrate nutrition education, sleep health, physical activity, weight-management counseling, and early identification of restrictive or unsupervised practices.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, because of its cross-sectional design, it is not possible to establish causality or determine the temporal direction of the associations analyzed. Therefore, although excess weight, poor sleep quality, vegetarianism, and higher nutritional knowledge were associated with intention to lose weight, it cannot be concluded that these factors preceded or caused such intention.

Second, the sample was selected through non-probability convenience sampling at a private university in East Lima, which limits the generalizability of the findings to students from other universities, regions, or sociocultural contexts. In addition, data were collected over a relatively short period, from March to April 2026, and the sample size was moderate. Face-to-face recruitment may also have favored the participation of students with greater availability or interest in health topics, generating possible selection bias. Furthermore, the recruitment strategy based on circulation through the main campus gates may have underrepresented students attending evening classes or those spending less time in central campus areas. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger studies with longer recruitment periods and more diverse university populations.

Third, several variables were assessed by self-report, including intention to lose weight, sleep quality, physical activity, vegetarian status, and nutritional knowledge, which may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias, or classification errors. In addition, intention to lose weight was measured using a single question; although this approach has been used in previous research, it may not fully capture the intensity, duration, motivations, or methods used to lose weight. Another limitation is that relevant psychological and perceptual variables were not assessed, including weight perception, body satisfaction, self-esteem, anxiety, depressive symptoms, academic stress, social comparison, and social media influence, which could partially explain intention to lose weight in university populations.

Vegetarian status was also assessed broadly, without distinguishing vegetarian subtypes, duration of adherence, motivations, or nutritional quality of the diet. Similarly, nutritional knowledge was measured using a brief questionnaire and may not capture broader dimensions of food literacy, practical skills, or real-life application of knowledge. Regarding sleep, although the PSQI was used, objective measures such as actigraphy or sleep diaries were not included, nor were variables such as chronotype, screen use, caffeine intake, night work, or academic workload. With respect to anthropometric status, BMI was classified into only two categories, without differentiating overweight from obesity or assessing other indicators such as waist circumference or body fat percentage.

In addition, physical activity was analyzed descriptively and in bivariate models but was not included in the final adjusted model because of the low frequency of students in the high physical activity category, which could have generated unstable estimates. Therefore, the applicability of these findings to university populations with higher physical activity levels may be limited. Finally, although the model included several covariates, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out, including socioeconomic level, previous nutritional counseling, habitual dietary intake, history of dieting, eating disorders, use of weight-loss products, or metabolic diseases. Future studies should use longitudinal designs, probabilistic samples, and broader measurements of psychological, dietary, and behavioral factors to better understand the determinants of intention to lose weight among university students.

Study implications

The findings of this study have implications for health promotion in university settings. The high prevalence of intention to lose weight suggests that weight control is a frequent concern and should be addressed through comprehensive strategies that are not focused exclusively on body weight reduction. Universities can implement nutrition education programs, healthy lifestyle counseling, screening for poor sleep quality, and prevention of inappropriate weight-control practices. In particular, interventions should prioritize students with excess weight, women, vegetarians, and those with poor sleep quality, while promoting sustainable changes in diet quality, physical activity, sleep, and the relationship with food and body image.

Conclusion

In conclusion, intention to lose weight was frequent among university students. Female sex, vegetarian status, poor sleep quality, and excess weight were associated with a higher prevalence of this intention, with excess weight showing the strongest association. In contrast, low and medium levels of nutritional knowledge were associated with a lower prevalence of intention to lose weight compared with high nutritional knowledge. These results suggest that intention to lose weight should not be interpreted solely as a response to excess weight, but rather as a multifactorial phenomenon involving sociodemographic, nutritional, behavioral, anthropometric, and possibly perceptual factors.
