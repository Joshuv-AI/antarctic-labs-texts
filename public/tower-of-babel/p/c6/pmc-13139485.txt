Environmental food allergen levels in the homes of 3–4-month-old infants: findings from the second phase Chiba study of mother and child health (2nd C-MACH)
Authors: Norimichi Suzuki, Keiichi Shimatani, Kohki Takaguchi, Kayo Yoda-Tsumura, Yoshitake Nakayama, Akifumi Eguchi, Fumitoshi Ogino, Masayuki Mikuriya, Hiroko Nakaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric food allergy is a growing health concern, with hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and wheat being the most common allergens in Japan. The dual allergen exposure hypothesis proposes that early oral intake induces immune tolerance but that cutaneous exposure may promote sensitization. However, little is known about environmental exposure to food allergens before the introduction of complementary foods. This study investigated the presence and concentration of food allergens in the home environments of 3- to 4-month-old infants who had not yet started complementary feeding. Dust samples were collected from 26 households participating in the C-MACH birth cohort study. Allergen concentrations for hen’s egg, cow’s milk, wheat, peanut, walnut, and house dust mites (Der f 1, Der p 1) were measured using ELISA. Statistical analyses were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis and Steel–Dwass tests. Hen’s egg, cow’s milk, wheat, and Der f 1 were detected in all samples. Peanut, Der p 1, and walnut occurred in 88%, 81%, and 35% of samples, respectively. Hen’s egg, cow’s milk, wheat, and peanut allergens were present at significantly higher concentrations than house dust mite allergens. These findings suggest that environmental exposure to food allergens begins prior to oral intake and may contribute to sensitization via the skin. This underscores the importance of considering both dietary and environmental sources in strategies for food allergy prevention. The study provides preliminary evidence of allergen exposure in early infancy and highlights the need for longitudinal research to examine its role in the development of allergic diseases.

Introduction

Pediatric food allergy has received considerable attention because of its potential impact on the health-related quality of life and psychosocial well-being of children1. In a Japanese national birth cohort study, the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), the prevalence of food allergy at ages 1, 2, and 3 years was reported to be 7.6%, 6.7%, and 4.9%, respectively, based on caregiver-reported data2. According to the study, proteins from hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and wheat were the three most common food allergens. Additionally, the Japanese Guidelines for Food Allergy 2020 indicated that peanuts and tree nuts are common triggers of new-onset food allergies in Japan3. The dual allergen exposure hypothesis has been proposed to facilitate understanding of the causes and prevention of food allergies in children4. This hypothesis suggests that exposure to food allergens through the skin may lead to allergic sensitization by stimulating the immune system, whereas early oral intake may promote immune tolerance4,5. Oral tolerance refers to an active process by which the gut immune system induces local and systemic immune unresponsiveness to harmless dietary antigens such as food proteins. This tolerogenic state is thought to help prevent pathological immune reactions, including food allergy6,7.

It has been reported that the early introduction of peanuts and eggs significantly reduces the risk of developing allergies later in life8. Moreover, randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that this effect extends to infants, even those at risk of atopic dermatitis, egg allergy, or peanut allergy9,10. These findings suggest that oral tolerance can be induced by introducing food allergens at an early stage, around six months of age.

Shoda et al. have suggested that dermal exposure to food allergens during infancy may contribute to allergic sensitization11. However, little is known about the actual levels of environmental food allergens to which infants are exposed before the introduction of complementary foods. The JECS pilot study, which commenced before the main study, collected and analyzed dust samples from the beddings of the participants when they were 3 years old. Egg protein, with levels exceeding those of house dust mites, was detected in all samples12. However, to date, few studies have investigated the effects of infants’ exposure to environmental food allergens before the introduction of complementary foods on the subsequent development of food allergies. In particular, investigating such exposure levels may provide insights into how environmental factors could potentially interact with established risk conditions, such as atopic dermatitis, in the development of food allergies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the types and concentrations of food allergens present in the home environment of infants prior to the introduction of complementary foods.

Materials and methods

This study was based on samples collected from homes of the children participating in the Chiba Study of Mother and Child Health (C-MACH). Additional details of this birth cohort study, launched in 2016, along with its methodology have been described elsewhere13. A second version of C-MACH involving a larger number of participants and an optional assessment of their indoor environments was initiated in 202114. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, Chiba University (approval number: M10032), in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects, which are based on the Declaration of Helsinki and other international ethical principles. The study was conducted with written informed consent obtained from all caregivers.

We requested that caregivers participate in the environmental survey and collect dust samples from the surroundings of infants aged 3 to 4 months who had not yet been introduced to complementary foods. This study reports on 26 dust samples for which allergen analyses have been completed. Each household contributed one dust sample. Regarding housing characteristics, the building age was < 3 years in 7 (26.9%), 3–10 years in 10 (38.5%), and ≥ 10 years in 8 (30.8%) (unknown, 1 [3.8%]). The housing structure was wood in 15 (57.7%) and steel/other non-wood in 9 (34.6%) (unknown, 2 [7.7%]). The housing type was detached house in 15 (57.7%) and apartment/condominium in 11 (42.3%).

The dust samples were evaluated for several allergens, including hen’s egg, cow’s milk, wheat, peanuts, walnuts, Dermatophagoides farinae (D. farinae) (Der f1), and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (D. pteronyssinus) (Der p1). Participants collected dust samples using handheld vacuum cleaners (Anker Eufy HomeVac H11) provided to them during a four-week period scheduled between July 2022 and March 2023. To describe the meteorological conditions around the estimated sampling date, we obtained the monthly mean outdoor air temperature and relative humidity for the corresponding month from the Japan Meteorological Agency for Chiba City15. The monthly mean outdoor air temperature ranged from 6.3 to 27.7 °C, and the monthly mean relative humidity ranged from 54.4 to 75.4% during the study period.

The samples were collected from areas where the infants frequently spent time. Subsequently, the filters from the vacuum cleaners were retrieved and stored at − 80 °C until measurements were made. For the measurement, the dust was removed from the filter and passed through a 300 μm sieve. Food allergens except for walnut were measured using the FASTKIT ELISA Ver. III series (NH Foods Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Walnut allergen was analyzed using the Allergeneye ELISA II series (Prima Meat Packers, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). For Der f 1 and Der p 1, house dust samples were analyzed using the ITEA Der f 1 and Der p 1 ELISA Kit (ITEA Inc., Tokyo, Japan).

The lower detection limits were set at 1.56 µg g–1 for food allergens and 0.094 µg g–1 for house dust mite allergens. The allergen concentrations in the house dust samples were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test. Additionally, multiple comparisons were performed using the Steel–Dwass test as a post hoc analysis to assess differences in allergen levels between food and house dust mite proteins. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2.), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the results of the study. Proteins from D. farinae, hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and wheat were detected in all samples. Proteins from peanuts, D. pteronyssinus, and walnuts were detected in 88%, 81%, and 35% of samples, respectively. The median concentrations of Der f1 and Der p1 were 3.5 µg g− 1 dust and 0.62 µg g− 1 dust, respectively. The median levels of hen’s egg, cow’s milk, wheat, and peanut proteins were 73 µg g− 1 dust, 330 µg g− 1 dust, 110 µg g− 1 dust, and 11 µg g− 1 dust, respectively. The walnut protein level was below the detection limit. Significant differences were observed in the concentrations of each protein. The concentrations of proteins from hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and wheat were significantly higher than those from Der p1 and Der f1. The concentration of peanut allergens was also significantly higher than the concentration of Der p1. Figure 1 illustrates the variability in protein concentrations across samples, highlighting substantial differences in distribution patterns among allergen types. Allergens such as Der p1 and walnut showed highly skewed distributions, with a large proportion of values near or below the detection limit, whereas hen’s egg, cow’s milk, and wheat allergens exhibited broader distributions with higher concentrations.

Discussion

In recent years, food antigens have been reported in household dust, and exposure to environmental antigens through the skin (transdermal sensitization) has been recognized as a risk factor for allergic sensitization, particularly in infants and young children with atopic dermatitis4,16. In this study and the JECS pilot study12, egg antigens were detected at higher concentrations than mite antigens. Furthermore, in this study, the concentrations of milk, wheat, and peanut proteins were higher than those of Der f1 and Der p1, implying a potential impact of the household antigen environment on allergic sensitization in early childhood.

Trendelenburg et al. reported that the cooking and consumption of eggs rapidly increased the concentration of egg antigens in household dust within 48 h17, demonstrating that food-related behaviors in the home are directly linked to environmental antigen levels. Similarly, Brough et al. found a positive correlation between household peanut consumption and peanut antigen concentrations in house dust18, indicating that the spread of food antigens into the domestic environment is a phenomenon common to various allergens. These findings suggest that cooking and eating behaviors in the household influence indoor allergen concentrations and that food antigens may not remain solely within the individual who consumes them but may physically accumulate in the environment and serve as sources of exposure. Collectively, the rapid responsiveness of environmental antigen levels to household food intake behaviors, along with the presence of such antigens in infants’ surroundings prior to ingestion, strongly suggests that infants may be exposed to multiple food allergens before oral introduction.

In particular, infants, who have immature skin barrier function and spend prolonged periods of time in direct contact with floors and bedding, may be at particular risk of transdermal sensitization19,20. In the present study, food allergens were detected in house dust of infants’ bedding at higher levels than mite allergens. Thus, these antigens may have been encountered at least in part via skin exposure before oral intake. Furthermore, data from the JECS study at age 3 also showed high detection frequencies and concentrations of egg antigens12, supporting the idea that environmental antigen exposure is not merely transient but may persist continuously and cumulatively. Whether skin exposure to food antigens before oral intake contributes to the development of food allergy could be clarified through prospective longitudinal studies that jointly assess (i) the pre-introduction exposure environment, (ii) subsequent oral intake, and (iii) the incidence of food allergy as the outcome.

This study, one of the first to perform a quantitative assessment of multiple allergens in the environment of infants aged 3–4 months, prior to the introduction of complementary foods, demonstrates that infants are already exposed to a diverse array of environmental antigens at this early stage. These findings reaffirm the importance of managing not only dietary intake but also environmental allergen exposure in future strategies for allergy prevention. Further prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate causal links between environmental allergen burden and sensitization or clinical onset of food allergy.

Conclusion

In this study, house dust mites and several food allergens were detected in household dust samples collected from the surroundings of 3- to 4-month-old infants. The findings suggest that infants may be exposed to environmental food allergens before oral introduction, potentially contributing to future allergic sensitization. Further epidemiological studies will be conducted to investigate IgE sensitization and infant eczema, as well as the relationship between early environmental exposure and the development of food allergies.
