Machine learning–assisted evolutionary optimization of hole quality and surface integrity in abrasive waterjet drilling of polycarbonate
Authors: J. Bharani Chandar, C. Rathinasuriyan, N. Lenin, M. Sivakumar, Robert Čep, Sachin Salunkhe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abrasive Waterjet Machining (AWJM) is a reliable non-traditional technology for machining polymer-based engineered materials with low thermal degradation, dimensional inaccuracy, and surface damage. This study examines polycarbonate hole drilling performance using 125 full-factorial experimental trials using process parameters water pressure, standoff distance, and traverse rate. With a coordinate measuring machine and stylus-based profilometer, kerf angle, entry and exit circularity, and surface roughness were measured in a detailed metrological research. Four ML models were created to develop accurate predicting capabilities, with the Random Forest (RF) model performing best in all responses. RF predicted processes accurately with high coefficient-of-determination R2 values (0.92, 0.82, 0.82, and 0.92) and low error coefficients (RMSE 0.196, 0.045, 0.042, and 0.452). The best drilling settings were found using evolutionary algorithms like Biogeography-Based Optimization (BBO), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Salp Swarm Optimization (SSO), and Tug-of-War Optimization (TWO). Deng’s similarity metric ranked SSO as the best optimizer based on many responses. The SSO produced the optimal settings (Wp = 250 MPa, Sd = 1.5, and Tr = 300 mm/min) for reduced kerf deviation, circularity and surface roughness. Under optimal circumstances, anticipated responses matched experimental results, proving the integrated ML-optimization framework’s strength.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42482-3.

Introduction

The polycarbonate has found extensive usage in optical, biomedical, aerospace and defense applications due to its great impact strength, dimensional stability and optical transparency. Its machinability has been investigated in various conventional and non-conventional processes with each offering specific difficulties and optimization demands1. In abrasive jet micromachining, Getu et al.2 demonstrated that erosion rate in PMMA, acrylic, and PC is highly sensitive to traverse direction at oblique angles, with forward scanning enhancing material removal. They developed an analytical model that accurately predicted micro-channel and micro-hole profiles. A broader review by Madhu et al.3 identified Water Pressure (Wp), Standoff Distance (Sd), Traverse Rate (Tr), particle size, and impact angle as the dominant parameters influencing Kerf Angle (Ka) and Surface Roughness (Sr) in composite laminates, reinforcing the importance of parameter control for dimensional accuracy. In mechanical micro-milling, Jahan et al.4 showed that ductile mode cutting of PC glass can be achieved with optimal combinations of depth of cut and feed rate, reducing cutting forces and improving Sr; finite element modeling effectively predicted these forces for process optimization. Adeniji et al.5 further reported that burr formation and Sr are governed more by cutting-edge radius, feed rate, and tool edge condition than by depth of cut. Tool wear investigations by Jahan et al. revealed that TiAlN-coated tools significantly reduce adhesion wear compared to uncoated carbide tools, although delamination wear may arise under higher loads; medium cutting parameters produced the best compromise between tool life and surface quality. Hybrid and assisted machining approaches have been applied to enhance process performance6. Singh et al.7 optimized chemical-assisted ultrasonic machining of bullet-proof PC glass using Taguchi design, grey relational analysis and response surface methodology, finding that 30% slurry concentration, appropriate grit size, and Nimonic-80 A tooling maximized Material Removal Rate (MRR) while minimizing tool wear rate and Sr. In micro-electric discharge machining, Vishwakarma et al.8 demonstrated that graphene nanoplatelet reinforcement improves conductivity and machinability of PC composites, and that rotating electrodes enhance flushing efficiency, resulting in higher MRR and improved Sr. Laser-based machining of PC has also been studied for micro-feature fabrication. Moradi et al.9 optimized continuous CO2 laser micro-drilling, showing that properly selected laser power and cutting speed minimized heat affected zone and taper, with theoretical predictions matching experiments. Similarly, Moradi et al.10 concluded that reducing focal plane position and laser power, combined with higher cutting speeds, improved Ka quality in low-power CO2 laser cutting of injection molded PC. In high-precision optical manufacturing, Mishra et al.11 achieved nanometric Sr (Ra = 18.1 nm) and sub-micron form accuracies (Pv = 0.116 μm) in ultra-precision diamond turning of PC optics through optimal parameter selection, temperature control, and vacuum clamping. Multi-objective optimization via GRA provided an effective balance between surface finish and profile accuracy.

Recent studies on PC processing clearly demonstrate a growing reliance on systematic optimization techniques to enhance surface integrity, mechanical performance, and functional properties. Abdulwahab et al.12 optimized CO2 laser micro-drilling of PC using central composite design and response surface methodology, identifying laser power, exposure time, and focal plane position as dominant parameters, with optimized conditions minimizing heat-affected zone and improving hole geometry. Singh et al.13 applied Taguchi L27 design coupled with grey relational analysis to chemical-assisted ultrasonic machining of PC bullet-proof glass, optimizing abrasive size, slurry concentration, power rating, and HF acid content to achieve over 100% improvement in MRR with controlled tool wear and Sr. In additive manufacturing contexts, Meena et al.14 optimized the Sr of FDM-printed PC spur gears using Taguchi-based abrasive flow finishing, showing that controlled post-processing parameters can drastically reduce Sr. Likewise, Faroze et al.15 combined dimensional analysis with machine learning to predict and optimize the mechanical properties of FFF-printed polycarbonate components, and supported the fact that data-driven models are superior to purely analytical modeling of complex, nonlinear process-property relationships. Along with structural elements, the optimization strategies have also been applied to functional PC systems. Zahmatkesh et al.16 used artificial neural networks with multi-objective evolutionary optimization (NSGA-II) in the context of customizing the thermophysical behavior of polycarbonate ultrafiltration membranes, and Xiao et al.17 showed machine-learning-aided material design of fire-safe polycarbonate by effectively sampling large compositional design space. Altogether these studies point to the obvious shift in the traditional single-factor experimentation to combined DOE-ML-evolutionary optimization models that can accommodate several goals and conflicting performance demands.

While optimization methods have been extensively utilized in polycarbonate processing, previous research has predominantly concentrated on additive manufacturing, membrane systems, and material composition. AWJM of polycarbonate received comparatively little focus within integrated machine learning-assisted multi-objective optimization frameworks. Although machine learning–evolutionary optimization techniques have been investigated in the AWJM of metals and composites, their implementation in precision polymer drilling, with simultaneous assessment of various geometric integrity requirements, is still underdeveloped.

Most current AWJM research on polymers depends on regression-based or single-response optimization models, which may inadequately represent the nonlinear interactions between the jet and polymer that influence dimensional accuracy and surface integrity. This study introduces a comprehensive experimental-computational framework that integrates full-factorial experimentation, comparative machine learning modeling, multi-algorithm evolutionary optimization, and experimental validation to simultaneously predict and optimize Kerf Angle (Ka), Hole Entry Circularity (Cen), Hole Exit circularity (Cex), and Surface Roughness (Sr) in polycarbonate drilling.

Materials and methods

The current research incorporates a systematic experimental-computational approach and explores the nature of AWJM in the process of drilling holes on polycarbonate sheets. The general workflow of the research incorporates controlled experimentation, precision metrology, prediction with the use of machine learning, and multi-objective evolutionary optimization. This combination can be used to reach a quantitative comprehension of the machining mechanism as well as perform a systematic discovery of the process conditions that reduce quality degradation during polymer drilling. Figure 1 presents the systematic experimental-computational framework.

Polycarbonate was chosen as the working material because it finds a broad spectrum of uses in industries as lightweight structural parts, protective covers and optical housings where the dimensions and the integrity of the surface are very vital18–20. A polycarbonate sheet measuring 100 × 100 × 6 mm was used in all the trials to provide uniformity. The specimen was washed with isopropyl alcohol to remove machining residue and surface contaminants which could affect jet interaction. The 6-mm thickness selection follows typical commercial uses and enables consistent assessment of top and bottom geometry during AWJM drilling.

Experimental-computational framework

The parameters of drilling Polycarbonate were selected using the deformation properties of the material used and the underlying erosion mechanisms due to the jet. One of the dominant factors was chosen to be the water pressure (250 MPa-350 MPa) due to the fact that it directly influences the kinetic energy of the jet and its impact force which directly affect the efficacy of material removal in polymeric substrates. Standoff distance (1.5–2.5 mm) was varied in the control of jet stability and the attenuation of the energy before interacting with the surface, which had a large impact on the kerf formation and surface quality as the jet dispersion grew. The rate of the traverse (300 mm/min-500 mm/min) was adjusted to manage the time of interaction of the jet and the workpiece so that an optimal balance was established between machining efficiency and surface finish. In order to eliminate the influence of hydraulic parameters, the mass flow rate of abrasive (300 g/min) was constant and this ensured the abrasive action was similar and further reduced the variability of the experiment21–23.

To find out the effect of three important process parameters, a full factorial design of experiment was adopted, Wp, Sd, and Tr. Each of the parameters was assessed on five levels, which created 125 combinations of experiments. This design is adequate to capture the effects of interaction and maintain statistical efficiency. The selection of a 5³ full factorial design was motivated by the objective of constructing data-driven predictive models rather than fitting predefined polynomial response surfaces. Although RSM and CCD offer experimental efficiency with fewer runs, they are primarily optimized for estimating second-order regression models under assumed quadratic behavior. In contrast, nonparametric machine learning algorithms benefit from dense and uniformly distributed sampling across the design space, which enhances their ability to capture higher-order nonlinear interactions and localized response regimes. The full factorial structure ensures balanced representation of all parameter combinations, reduces extrapolation risk during prediction, and improves model stability when integrated within evolutionary optimization loops. Therefore, the 125-run design was selected to support robust learning of complex jet–polymer interaction behavior rather than to minimize experimental count.

Pilot tests were used to determine the choice of parameters to use, including those that generate consistent jet shapes and allow quantitative variation of the kerf geometry without leading to thermal distortion and excessive tearing of the polymer substrate. The drilling experiments were performed on a CNC-controlled AWJM (model: G3020) as illustrated in Fig. 2 and set up in such a way as to preserve a constant jet quality over the course of the experimental campaign. The system employed a high pressure intensifier pump (60HP), carbide orifice with a garnet (80 mesh) abrasive feed system as is seen in the state-of-art machining centres. To reduce the deformation caused by vibration workpieces were firmly clamped on a sacrificial backing plate, to ensure that the jet remained stable as the breakthrough took place.

Each of the drilled holes was measured according to four main response characteristics namely; Ka, Cen, Cex, and Sr. The Coordinate Measuring Machine (CMM) with a 0.5 mm ruby stylus was used to carry out the geometric measurements, with traceable ring gauges used to calibrate it. The circularity of each hole was determined by obtaining several boundary points in the hole entry and exit profiles, and then circle-fitting by least-squares. The method provides strong and reproducible estimation of non-circular geometry deviation. The level of surface roughness was measured on the inside of the hole wall by a contact-type stylus gauge using a Gaussian filter (ISO 4287 − 1997) and normal evaluation length (1 mm). The measurements were again taken around the circumference and an average was taken to be analyzed later. Figure 3 (a and b) respectively show the response measurement procedure of circularity and surface roughness respectively. The difference between the entry and exit diameters and the sheet thickness was used to compute the kerf angle, an indicator of jet divergence within the material. Integrated machine learning and algorithmic approach

In order to estimate the correlation between process parameters and machining responses, various machine learning algorithms were designed such as Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), AdaBoost Regressor (ABR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Multi Linear Regression Model (MLRM). Such models have been selected because they are able to model nonlinear interactions and perform very well on structured experimental data.

Tree-based and ensemble methods such as RF and XGB are particularly effective in handling complex feature interactions, reducing overfitting through aggregation and regularization mechanisms, and providing stable generalization performance without requiring extensive data preprocessing. The chosen algorithms provide practical advantages over alternative models like Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) for the current investigation. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models generally necessitate extensive datasets for consistent convergence and exhibit sensitivity to network architecture and weight initialization. The efficiency of SVR is heavily influenced by kernel selection and hyperparameter optimization, potentially escalating computing complexity in multi-response modeling. Gaussian Process Regression, while effective for uncertainty quantification, becomes computationally demanding during iterative evaluations in evolutionary optimization processes. Conversely, ensemble tree-based approaches offer computational efficiency, scalability, less susceptibility to hyperparameter initialization, and consistent performance for nonlinear regression problems using structured experimental data. As a result, these models were deemed better suited for integration with multi-objective evolutionary optimization in the AWJM framework.

The entire dataset was split into training (80%) and testing (20%) parts. All algorithms were written in custom Python instead of an existing library, which enabled complete control over the learning process and report clear models behaviour. The appropriateness of each of the models was evaluated based on the measures of coefficient of determination, mean absolute error, and root-mean-square error, and the predictive accuracy is the same regardless of the responses.

The fourth stage of the study was minimization, which used four Evolutionary Algorithms (EA) BBO, PSO, SSO and TWO to identify process conditions that optimize the reduction of Ka, Cen, Cex, and Sr at the same time. These algorithms were chosen due to their effectiveness in the multi-modal optimization problems that are complex and yet complimentary exploration strategies. Both algorithms worked with the prediction models based on machine-learned models instead of actual experimental data, allowing the rapid and computationally efficient estimation of the optimal combinations of parameters. Results and discussion

Statistical inferences

In this section, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is used to analyze the experimental data to determine the statistically significant process parameters that affect the effectiveness of abrasive water jet machining in terms of kerf angle, circularity, and surface roughness. The ANOVA findings serve as a measure of the relative contribution of main effects, intersection terms and quadratic items obtained by the responsiveness models and also measure the suitability of the models through the estimation of errors. The discussion gives a clear idea of the roles of Wp, Sd, and Tr to the observed responses by correlating statistical significance to physical machining behaviour, which provides a reliable foundation to interpretation and optimization of the process. Minitab software was used to perform ANOVA and the results are presented in Supplementary Tables S2-S5.

Figure 4 indicates that specific governing mechanisms apply to each AWJM response despite the fact that the adequacy of the developed models is confirmed by the percentage contribution analysis. Water pressure dominates the variation in the Kerf angle with the quadratic and linear terms of the pressure fluctuations making an average of 34.7 and 22.7%, respectively, with a total of more than 57% showing a great dependence of the kerf formation on the jet energy and nonlinear amplification, the Wp X Tr interaction (6.4%) and Tr2 (4.8%) are secondary with the error of the experiment being minimal (6.3%). The pattern of contribution of entry circularity shows the quadratic effect of traverse rate (15.2%), the linear effect of traverse rate (14.4%), and the SdxTr interaction (13.2%) are dominant, and terms involving water-pressure influence also play significant roles with moderate and the error is limited to 6.3%.

Exit circularity once again illustrates pressure-controlled behavior with the contribution of water pressure and quadratic term being about 34.8% and 18.5% respectively which is higher than 53% and the traverse rate (14.1%) and the Wp x Tr interaction (13.5%) further modify the exit profile with the error contribution being low (6.4%). The single-factor domination is greatest with surface roughness, followed by standoff distance and its square term which collectively contribute some 23.4% and 38.8% respectively to the variation due to jet divergence and energy dispersal at higher nozzle heights; water pressure and the interaction terms used have secondary influence and the error term of 7.2% confirms the high level of reality consistency of the experiment. All this evidence shows that penetration-related responses (Ka and Cex), the rate of traverse and standoff controls Cen and Sd is the major determinant of Sr.

In addition, the suitability of the regression models was also assessed by normal probability plots of the residuals of all response variables that can be found in Supplementary Figures S1–S4. The Pareto charts and the residual analyses, together, prove that there are specific dominant mechanisms controlling each AWJM response and proves the statistical validity of the elaborated regression models. In the case of Ka and Cex, the pressure of water and the squares of this pressure become the most significant factors due to the key role of the jet energy and the nonlinear intensification of it in the determination of the kerf geometry and exit-profile formation. Entry circularity, on the contrary, shows a more spread out influence pattern with traverse rate standoff interaction and Tr2 significant influencing factor, which means that inlet geometry is not influenced by one dominant factor. The most dominant parameter is the surface roughness where the standoff distance and Sd2 represent majority of the effect, which is in line with surface deterioration caused by defocusing of the jet and the low concentration of abrasives at greater distances. In all answers, it is confirmed that the model assumptions are met: the residuals follow a normal distribution, they do not involve patterns, and they do not contain heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation. A combination of these diagnostics helps to conclude that the fitted models are statistically valid, physically interpretable and can be used to effectively represent the parameter response relationships driving performance at AWJM.

Influence of AWJM process parameters on responses through 3D surface plots and 2D contour plots

The combined response surfaces in Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8 reveal that abrasive waterjet drilling performance in polycarbonate is governed by a delicate balance between jet coherence, energy distribution, and interaction time, which collectively dictate geometric accuracy and surface integrity. Across all responses, Sd consistently emerges as the most influential parameter, followed by Wp and Tr, though their effects vary depending on the quality metric considered.

In addition, to improve interpretation and the comprehension of interactions, relevant 2D contour plots are provided for each response (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8 (d–f)). In these plots, the color gradient signifies the magnitude of the response variable, with cooler colors denoting lower values and warmer colors denoting higher values. This depiction facilitates a clear view of interaction areas and ideal parameter ranges.

Figure 5(a) indicates that Ka is dominated by Sd which rises by 26% as Sd increases between 1.5 and 2.5 mm by progressive jet divergence and lower energy density at the exit. Water pressure is nonlinearly affecting the jet coherence, where mild Wp (300–325 MPa) stabilizes the jet coherence and minimizes the taper and the pressure high above (350 MPa) enhances Ka up to 2–3 times at larger Sd due to jet instability and particle fragmentation.

As illustrated in Fig. 5(b), Tr further amplifies taper, with Ka increasing by 15% as Tr rises from 350 to 500 mm/min owing to shortened jet–material interaction time. At low Tr, excessive Wp sharply deteriorates kerf quality (Ka = 2.1°), whereas moderate Wp (300 MPa) suppresses taper even at higher Tr, yielding minimum Ka values (0.71°) and forming a distinct valley region where jet momentum and exposure time are optimally balanced. The combined influence of jet dispersion and reduced dwell time is evident in Fig. 5(c), where increasing Sd at Tr = 300 mm/min raises Ka by 37%, confirming Sd as the dominant taper driver, while the maximum taper (1.5°) occurs under the synergistic action of high Tr and large Sd. Overall, Figs. 5(a–c) identify low Sd, moderate Wp, and moderate Tr as the optimal parameter window for minimizing kerf taper.

In general, Fig. 6(a–c) determine moderate Wp with higher Sd and restrained Tr as the good conditions to reduce entry circularity.

Machine learning approach

In order to overcome the high nonlinearity of AWJM drilling, machine learning approach based on data was applied to determine credible input to output correlations. Structured data splitting was initially used to condition experimental results obtained after systematically designed drilling trials to facilitate a stable learning. Wp, Sd and Tr have been taken as model input and Ka, Cen and Cex circularity, and Sr have been taken as response variables. In a manner of independent training, ABR, XGB, DT, and RF models were trained in order to capture the underlying jet-material interaction mechanisms. The coefficient of determination, and error-based measures were used to compare the model performance to determine the predictor that was most accurate in comparison to MLRM. The choice of the ML model was followed by the combination with the evolutionary optimization algorithms, and the optimal drilling parameters were tested experimentally to assure the predictive accuracy and consistency of the processes.

Hyperparameter tuning and cross validation

To achieve systematic optimization, specific hyperparameter ranges were developed for each machine learning approach (DT, RF, ABR, and XGB) prior to training. The ranges were established with algorithmic stability in mind, as well as generally reported effective intervals in regression-based modeling of structured experimental datasets. Discrete hyperparameters (for example, tree depth, minimum sample split) were drawn from integer uniform distributions, whereas continuous hyperparameters (for example, learning rate, subsample ratio) were drawn from uniform distributions. Establishing constrained yet adequately vast search domains mitigates underfitting from overly restrictive parameters and overfitting resulting from excessively intricate model architectures. Hyperparameter optimization was conducted by randomized search with 50 iterations for each model. In each iteration, a randomly chosen group of hyperparameters was selected from the established search space and assessed using 5-fold cross-validation. Considering a dataset of 125 experimental observations, 5-fold cross-validation was deemed a statistically suitable balance between bias and variance. The dataset was randomly divided into five equal subgroups, each containing 25 samples. In each iteration, four folds (100 samples) were utilized for model training, while the remaining fold (25 samples) served for validation. The method was executed five times, with each fold utilized once as the validation set. Employing cross-validation in hyperparameter tuning mitigates bias from a singular train-test partition and enhances resilience to overfitting.

The model’s predictive performance was assessed by calculating the mean and standard deviation of performance measures across five cross-validation stages. The coefficient of determination (R2) was employed to assess the predictive ability. The R2 values for the five iterations ranged from 92% to 96%. The average R2 obtained after 5-fold cross-validation was 9% with standard deviation of 0.02, considered a more dependable measure of model generalization than a single train-test split, as outlined in Table 5.

Polycarbonate removal under abrasive waterjet drilling is governed by ductile deformation, localized plastic flow, and stochastic abrasive impact distribution, leading to strong nonlinear and interaction-driven behavior among water pressure, standoff distance, and traverse rate. The ANOVA results indicate significant quadratic and interaction effects, particularly for kerf angle and circularity responses.

Random Forest is well suited to capture such piecewise nonlinear relationships because it partitions the parameter space into multiple locally adaptive regions and aggregates their predictions through ensemble averaging. Evolutionary optimization algorithmic approach

The four evolutionary algorithms applied in the current work were BBO, PSO, SSO, and TWO which were used to find the optimal AWJM parameters using the multi-indicator evaluation model presented in Table 8. Every algorithm was repeated 21 times individually and the best solution of each run was selected on the overall Objective Value (OV) in Supplementary Tables S5–S8. The optimization process also involved several performance metrics such as Convergence Iteration (CI), Convergence Value (CV), Inter-Generated Distance (IGD), Diversity (Div), Spacing (SP), and Hypervolume (HV) and all the indicators had equal weight.

To assess the comprehensive efficacy of each optimization procedure across various machining responses Ka, Cen, Cex, and Sr a single composite objective value was calculated utilizing Deng’s similarity-based aggregation method. Given that all responses denote minimization-type objectives, each response value was normalized as follows:

Most responses (Ka, Cen, Cex and Sr) stabilized the fastest with SSO, most frequently in 1–5 iterations. This implies that it could exploit the situations promptly and did not swing very widely. BBO and PSO demonstrated characteristic features of moderate convergence, which typically applied at 10 to 90 iterations, according to the response variable. More iterations were required to stabilize TWO, with Cex and Sr frequently requiring over 85–100 iterations. This indicates that it was more exploratory in search. Of the four methods, SSO always yields a smaller and evenly spaced set of non-dominated solutions that are closer to the ideal zone, whereby kerf angle and circularity deviations at entry and exit are reduced to the minimum as well as the surface roughness. The elegant and small Pareto fronts produced with the help of SSO show a steady convergence and effective handling of contrary goals.

Conclusion

This research developed a combined experimental and data-based platform of abrasive waterjet drilling of polycarbonate. The adequacy of the models was statistically proven, and quadratic models have described more than 92–94% of the total variability of the kerf angle, entry, and exit circularity, and surface roughness. The most influential factor on penetration-related responses was determined to be water pressure which contributed over 50% of the variation in kerf angle and exit circularity with standoff distance being the controlling factor of surface roughness with a contribution over 60% due to jet divergence effects. Scope for future study

Future studies may extend the present framework by investigating a wider range of AWJM process parameters to improve model generalization beyond the current experimental domain.
