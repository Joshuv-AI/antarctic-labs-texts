Effect of epidural dexmedetomidine on post-operative nausea & vomiting in adult patients undergoing abdominopelvic surgery: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Piyawan Koopredechat, Wanida Chongarunngamsang, Manita Kaweewong, Nattamon Thitipanichayankul, Salinee Poonjan, Thanyalak Chanakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) has been reported to reach as high as 33.5% in patients undergoing abdominal surgeries. Intravenous dexmedetomidine has been shown to be effective in reducing the incidence of PONV, possibly through the inhibition of catecholamine release and decreased opioid requirements. This study aims to investigate the potential efficacy of epidural dexmedetomidine in preventing PONV in patients undergoing abdominal and pelvic surgery under combined general-epidural anesthesia.MethodsThe study enrolled patients aged 18–65 years with an ASA physical status I–III who were scheduled for elective open abdominal or pelvic surgery. Patients were randomized to receive either a single bolus dose of dexmedetomidine during peritoneal closure or placebo. No additional PONV prophylaxis was provided. Patients in the dexmedetomidine group received 2 mg of morphine combined with dexmedetomidine 1 mcg kg−1 via the epidural catheter, whereas those in the placebo group received only morphine diluted to an equivalent volume.ResultsOf the 108 patients randomized between August 2024 to April 2025, 99 were included in the final analysis. The incidence of PONV within 1 and 6 h postoperatively was significantly lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared to placebo (3.8% vs. 30.4%; RR 0.12, 95% CI 0.03–0.52, p < 0.001 and 13.2% vs. 32.6%, RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.18–0.91, p = 0.021), respectively. The severity of postoperative nausea was not different between the groups (p = 0.557). The incidence of postoperative shivering was significantly lower in the dexmedetomidine group (0% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.003).ConclusionsA single administration of epidural dexmedetomidine at 1 mcg kg−1 significantly reduced both the incidence of early PONV at 1 and 6 h postoperatively and postoperative shivering without increasing hemodynamic complications.Clinical trial registrationRetrospectively registered with Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20250331001) on 31 March 2025.Keywords: Dexmedetomidine, Epidural anesthesia, PONV

Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) remain one of the most common complications after anesthesia despite routine pharmacological prevention. A recent meta-analysis found the prevalence of PONV across all surgical procedures to be 27.7%, with a subgroup analysis identifying an even higher prevalence at 33.5% in abdominal surgeries [1]. This problem not only leads to patient discomfort and dissatisfaction, but also increased nursing workload and length of stay in the post anesthesia care unit (PACU). A variety of agents have been used as part of a multimodal prophylactic regimen to prevent PONV. This may include agents such as serotonin antagonists, antimuscarinics, histamine antagonists, dopamine antagonists, and neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists [2].

Dexmedetomidine is a selective alpha-2 adrenoreceptor agonist widely recognized for its use as an intravenous sedative agent. Recently its role as an antiemetic has been examined. Many studies found that intravenous dexmedetomidine can lower the incidence of PONV by as much as 51% compared to placebo [3–5]. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, it is hypothesized to involve inhibition of catecholamine release and decreased opioid requirements [3]. Non-intravenous routes, including epidural administration, have also been explored and shown to be safe and effective. It provided sedation and acted synergistically with regional anesthesia to provide analgesia, prolonging both the duration of sensory and motor block, as well as reducing postoperative opioid consumption [6, 7]. However, despite its growing use in regional anesthesia, no studies have specifically evaluated whether epidural dexmedetomidine confers the same antiemetic benefit observed with the intravenous route. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the potential of epidural dexmedetomidine in preventing early PONV at 1 and 6 h post operation in patients undergoing abdominal and pelvic surgery under combined general-epidural anesthesia.

Materials & methods

Study design

This was a single center, randomized, prospective trial conducted at Her Royal Highness Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn Medical Center, Nakhon Nayok, Thailand. The trial was approved by the human research ethics committee of Srinakharinwirot University on July 11, 2024 (SWUEC-671030) and retrospectively registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20250331001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18–65 years, ASA physical status I–III, scheduled for elective open abdominal or pelvic surgery. Exclusion criteria included known allergy to study drugs; comorbidities known to increase PONV risk (e.g., impaired gastric motility, vestibular disorders); obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²); uncontrolled hypertension; hepatic or renal dysfunction (Child-Pugh class C liver disease or end-stage renal disease on dialysis); history of alcohol or substance abuse; use of antiemetics, psychotropics, or glucocorticoids within 24 h preoperatively; cardiac conduction abnormalities of second- or third-degree AV block; neurological or brain injuries; pregnancy or breastfeeding; chronic opioid use; and contraindications to epidural anesthesia (e.g., coagulopathy, local infection, or patient refusal).

Randomization, allocation concealment, blinding

Patients were randomly assigned to receive either dexmedetomidine (HYDEX, Intas Pharmaceuticals, Matoda, India) or placebo using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence. Allocation concealment was maintained using the SNOSE method. Patients, recruiters, attending anesthesiologists, and outcome assessors were unaware of group allocation. The research team opened the sealed envelope and administered the assigned intervention during peritoneal closure.

Anesthesia procedure

An epidural catheter was inserted prior to anesthesia induction in all participants using a 16G Tuohy needle. General anesthesia was induced with propofol 1–2 mg kg−1. Neuromuscular blockade was achieved with cisatracurium 0.15 mg kg−1 or, in cases with aspiration risk, succinylcholine 1.5 mg kg−1. Following successful tracheal intubation, a loading dose of 0.25% bupivacaine 5 ml was administered via the epidural catheter in both groups, followed by a continuous epidural infusion of 0.25% bupivacaine at a rate of 3–8 ml hr−1, titrated according to the attending anesthesiologist’s judgment. General anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in a mixture of air and oxygen at 0.8–1.2 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC).

Intraoperatively, bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats min−1) was treated with atropine 0.6 mg IV, and hypotension (mean arterial pressure < 20% of baseline) was treated with either ephedrine 6 mg IV or norepinephrine 5 mcg IV. No prophylactic antiemetic drugs were given during surgery. During peritoneal closure, patients received the assigned drug via the epidural catheter, prepared by one of the investigators. The dexmedetomidine group received 2 mg of morphine with dexmedetomidine 1 mcg kg−1 in 5 ml normal saline; the placebo group received 2 mg of morphine in 5 ml normal saline. Administration at this stage corresponded with the 15 min onset of dexmedetomidine [4]. During subcutaneous tissue closure, the epidural infusion was discontinued. And at the conclusion of surgery, sevoflurane was discontinued, neuromuscular blockade was reversed, and patients were extubated.

After surgery, patients were transferred to the PACU. PONV was assessed within the first hour following surgery by a blinded assessor. Patients who experienced at least one episode of vomiting, had persistent nausea lasting more than 15 min, reported severe nausea (rated on a 4-point scale) [8, 9], or requested antiemetic treatment were administered 8 mg of ondansetron intravenously. If symptoms persisted after 15 min, 10 mg of metoclopramide was administered, followed by 50 mg of dimenhydrinate if symptoms remained after an additional 15 min.

Postoperative analgesia via the epidural catheter was provided to both groups using 0.0625% bupivacaine combined with morphine 0.02 mg ml−1. The infusion rate was determined at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist. If the anesthesiologist judged that the sensory block level was insufficient to manage the patient’s pain, additional epidural boluses of 1% xylocaine with adrenaline (1:400,000) were administered until an adequate level of analgesia was achieved. If pain control remained inadequate, tramadol 50 mg IV was administered, followed by parecoxib 40 mg IV or paracetamol 1 g IV if pain persisted. For postoperative shivering, 20 mg of pethidine IV was administered. A follow-up visit was conducted 6 h postoperatively by an anesthesia resident or nurse anesthetist to assess the presence of PONV.

Outcomes

Baseline patient characteristics, including age, weight, height, ASA physical status, Apfel score for PONV, and type of surgery were recorded. Intraoperative data collected included pre-induction vital signs, epidural placement level, total fluid intake and output, anesthesia and surgery duration, and any adverse events. Postoperative data included nausea severity assessed using a 4-point scale (0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = severe) [8, 9], incidence of vomiting, shivering, requirement for rescue analgesia, and patient satisfaction measured on a 5-point scale (0 = least satisfied to 5 = most satisfied) [10].

The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV within the first postoperative hour. Participants who vomited or received antiemetic treatment within this period were classified as experiencing PONV. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of PONV at 6 h postoperatively, hemodynamic complications such as bradycardia and hypotension, incidence of shivering, need for rescue analgesia within 24 h, and overall patient satisfaction.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated from a study led by Massad who investigated the effects of intravenous dexmedetomidine in patients undergoing laparoscopic gynecologic surgery [5]. The study reported a PONV incidence of 31% in the dexmedetomidine group compared to 59% in the placebo group. Based on these proportions, the sample size was calculated using STATA software (version 14), with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90%. Allowing for a 10% dropout rate, 54 participants were required per group, resulting in a total sample size of 108 participants.

Data were analyzed using two-sided tests. Continuous data were presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range) and analyzed using independent student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as number (percentage) and analyzed using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Demographic and baseline characteristics were presented for all randomized participants. Subsequent analyses included only participants who completed the study.

Results

108 patients were randomly assigned in the study between August 2024 to April 2025. The final analysis included 53 patients from the dexmedetomidine group and 46 patients from the placebo group, following dropouts due to protocol violation, as shown in Fig. 1. Baseline demographic characteristics and perioperative data were comparable among all participants (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of Apfel scores, intraoperative fluid balance, or the duration of anesthesia and surgery.

The incidence of PONV within 1 h postoperatively was significantly lower in the dexmedetomidine group compared to placebo (3.8% vs. 30.4%; relative risk [RR] 0.12 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.03–0.52), p < 0.001). The incidence of PONV at 6 h postoperatively was also significantly lower in the dexmedetomidine group (13.2% vs. 32.6%, p = 0.021) (Fig. 2a). However, there was no significant difference in the incidence and severity of postoperative nausea (Fig. 2b).

Intraoperative hemodynamic complications were comparable between the groups. Bradycardia occurred in 2 (3.8%) patients receiving dexmedetomidine and 3 (6.5%) in the placebo group. Hypotension was observed in 44 (83%) patients in the dexmedetomidine group and 37 (80.4%) in the placebo group. Median ephedrine and norepinephrine doses were 6 mg and 5 mcg, respectively, in the dexmedetomidine group, compared to 12 mg and 10 mcg in the placebo group. Notably, no patients in the dexmedetomidine group experienced postoperative shivering, compared to 15.2% in the placebo group (95%CI 0.00-0.37, p = 0.003). Significantly more patients in the dexmedetomidine group required rescue analgesia during the first 24 postoperative hours (11 [20.8%] vs. 2 [4.4%], p = 0.037). However, the average doses of tramadol administered for pain management were similar between the two groups (54 mg vs. 50 mg, p = 0.538). Regardless, most patients in both groups reported feeling most satisfied with the anesthesia service (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that epidural dexmedetomidine was able to reduce the incidence of PONV at 1 and 6 h postoperatively. Primary triggers for PONV include inhalational anesthetics, opioids, and vagal-stimulating surgeries such as abdominal and gynecological procedures. The pathophysiology involves coordination between the chemoreceptor trigger zone, nucleus tractus solitarius, and vestibular system, which integrate emetogenic signals from the bloodstream and gastrointestinal tract. This complex response is modulated by various neurotransmitters including serotonin, dopamine, histamine, acetylcholine, and substance P [11]. The exact mechanism underlying the antiemetic effect of dexmedetomidine remains uncertain, but it is thought to involve a reduction in sympathetic outflow, suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, and modulation of neurotransmitter pathways, including serotonin and dopamine [3, 8].

The overall incidence of PONV in this study was higher than that reported in previous studies in which patients received prophylactic antiemetic therapy [12, 13]. In this study, PONV was defined as either the occurrence of vomiting or a patient request for antiemetic medication. This component of the definition may have introduced variability due to differences in symptom perception and provider judgment. Nonetheless, our result demonstrated that dexmedetomidine administration was associated with an 87.6% relative risk reduction in PONV during the first postoperative hour and a 59.5% reduction within six hours. This is consistent with the studies examining the antiemetic effect of dexmedetomidine when administered intravenously. Li and colleagues reported a significant reduction in PONV using a single intravenous bolus dose of 0.4 mcg kg−1 administered 30 min before the end of surgery [8]. Similarly, Geng and colleagues demonstrated a comparable effect with 0.5 mcg kg−1 loading dose before induction, followed by a continuous infusion at 0.1 mcg kg−1 hr−1 [9]. However, in both studies, the antiemetic effect appeared to diminish after two hours, likely due to the drug’s relatively short terminal half-life. The results of our study showed that the relative risk reduction in PONV remained significant, though less pronounced, at 6 h postoperatively. This sustained effect may be due to the prolonged action of epidurally administered dexmedetomidine, potentially explained by its limited diffusion across the blood-brain barrier, which may delay systemic clearance [6]. When specifically examining the incidence and severity of postoperative nausea, this study found no significant difference between the groups. This may be explained by the high proportion of patients in both cohorts reporting no nausea, which attenuated the effective sample size and limited the statistical power to detect differences in nausea severity. Furthermore, differences in how patients interpreted this subjective scale may have limited its discriminatory value.

Intravenous administration of dexmedetomidine typically induces a biphasic hemodynamic response. Following a bolus dose, an initial increase in systemic vascular resistance leads to transient hypertension. As the plasma concentration subsequently declines, alpha-2 receptor agonism promotes vasodilation and inhibits catecholamine release, resulting in hypotension. Additionally, the reduction in sympathetic outflow and the modulation of the baroreceptor reflex contribute to the observed bradycardia [14]. With epidural administration, the transiently high plasma concentrations associated with intravenous bolus injection were avoided, and hypertension was not observed. The incidence of hypotension and bradycardia was comparable to that of the placebo group, and the need for vasopressor therapy and atropine was not significantly different.

We also observed that epidural administration of dexmedetomidine significantly reduced the incidence of postoperative shivering. This finding is in agreement with a study led by Hanoura, who evaluated the effects of adding dexmedetomidine to epidural bupivacaine and fentanyl in patients undergoing cesarean section. His team reported that, in addition to improving intraoperative conditions and postoperative analgesia, dexmedetomidine also significantly lowered the incidence of shivering [15]. Talke explained that by activating alpha-2 adrenoceptor in the central nervous system, dexmedetomidine lowers the threshold for vasoconstriction and shivering, thereby widening the range of body temperature that do not activate thermoregulatory responses [16].

Although epidural dexmedetomidine reduced the incidence of PONV and shivering, we were unable to demonstrate its effect on reducing the need for rescue analgesia. This may be due to variations in dosing and administration methods. Hetta and team investigated the analgesic effects of continuous epidural dexmedetomidine infusion, using a bolus dose of 0.5 µg kg−1 in combination with bupivacaine, followed by a continuous infusion at 6 ml hr−1 during major upper abdominal cancer surgery. The study found that, compared to bupivacaine alone, the dexmedetomidine group had significantly lower cumulative morphine consumption within the first 48 h postoperatively [6]. Eskandr and Ebeid also examined the effects of a 0.2 µg kg−1 infusion of dexmedetomidine in combination with bupivacaine at a rate of 2.5 ml hr−1 in patients following total knee arthroplasty. The study found that the addition of dexmedetomidine reduced postoperative pain, both at rest and during movement, as well as nalbuphine consumption [17]. Additionally, the inconsistent analgesic effects observed may be due to inter-individual variation in epidural spread and absorption of both bupivacaine and dexmedetomidine. Four patients from the dexmedetomidine group and two patients from the placebo group required adjustment of their epidural catheters on the next postoperative day to achieve satisfactory analgesia. In our study, although a greater number of patients in the dexmedetomidine group required rescue analgesia, the average amount of tramadol administered did not differ significantly between the groups. We also did not observe a significant difference in patient satisfaction scores. However, this result may be subject to response bias, as patients were asked to rate their satisfaction directly to a healthcare professional.

To our knowledge, this was the first study to explore the antiemetic effect of dexmedetomidine when administered epidurally. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size calculation was based on data from intravenous dexmedetomidine, and generalizing these findings to the epidural route may limit the accuracy of our assumptions. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 6 h postoperatively, and thus, the impact on delayed PONV could not be assessed. Third, postoperative sedation was not formally assessed, which may have confounded our subjective definition of PONV. Lastly, we used the requirement for rescue analgesia as a surrogate measure of its analgesic property, which may not adequately reflect its true effect. Future studies that involve a standard PONV prophylaxis protocol and directly evaluate pain outcomes could offer a more comprehensive understanding of its antiemetic benefit and analgesic efficacy.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a single administration of epidural dexmedetomidine at 1 mcg kg−1 significantly reduced the incidence of PONV at both 1 and 6 h postoperatively without increasing hemodynamic complications. Additionally, the incidence of postoperative shivering was significantly reduced. However, no reduction in the need for rescue analgesia was observed in this study. These results highlight the potential of dexmedetomidine for reducing early PONV and shivering, though further research is needed to obtain a more precise understanding regarding its effect on delayed PONV and analgesia.
