Enhancing durability of automotive fuel cells via selective electrical conductivity induced by tungsten oxide layer coated directly on membrane electrode assembly
Authors: Sang-Hoon You, Sang-Mun Jung, Jinheon Park, Jaerim Kim, Jong Kyu Kim, Junwoo Son, Yong-Tae Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The poor durability, attributed to catalyst corrosion during start-up/shutdown (SU/SD), is a major obstacle to the commercialization of fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs). We recently achieved durability enhancement under SU/SD conditions by implementing a metal-insulator transition (MIT) using proton intercalation/deintercalation in WO3. However, such oxide-supported catalysts were unsuitable for direct application to the mass production stage of membrane electrode assembly (MEA) process due to their physical and chemical properties. Here, we report a unique approach that achieves the same durability enhancement in SU/SD situations while being directly applicable to the conventional MEA fabrication process. We coated WO3 on the bipolar plate, gas diffusion layer, and MEA to investigate whether the MIT phenomenon was realized. The WO3-coated MEA demonstrated 94% performance retention during SU/SD, the highest level to our knowledge. It can directly contribute to enhancing the durability of commercial FCEVs and be immediately applied to the MEA mass production process.

INTRODUCTION

Since the Paris Agreement in 2015, considerable efforts have been directed toward global carbon emission reduction. Hydrogen-energy systems are emerging as a promising alternative to fossil fuel energy systems. The research on fuel cells, which generate electricity in a hydrogen economy, has recorded remarkable progress thus far. However, several drawbacks are hindering the commercialization of fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs), including the high cost and low durability of fuel cells. To reduce the cost, extensive studies have been conducted on noble-metal reduction technologies (1–4) and nonprecious metal-catalyst development (5–9). Large-scale production of fuel cells has the potential to notably reduce the cost (10, 11). However, because the severity of the durability limitation was only recently highlighted (12–15), studies on the durability degradation mechanisms and countermeasures are absolutely lacking. As is well known, the catalyst corrosion phenomenon occurring during the start-up/shutdown (SU/SD) situation is the major cause of the durability degradation (16–18). Under the SU/SD condition, an unwanted oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) occurs at the anode, attributed to locally existing oxygen, leading to a substantial increase in the cathode potential to ~1.5 V. At the cathode, carbon oxidation reaction (COR) occurs on the catalyst layer (CL), which supplies the anode with the protons and electrons required for the ORR, causing corrosion of the carbon support and the subsequent durability deterioration (Fig. 1) (18–20).

The mechanism of anodic and cathodic electrochemical processes occurring during SU/SD of PEMFCs.

During normal operation, a hydrogen atmosphere is formed in the anode. Accordingly, the Open Circuit Voltage sets the potential at the cathode to be around 1.0 V. Because of the oxygen locally introduced into the anodic flow field, unnecessary ORR occurs at the anode, which causes a high potential of 1.4 V or more to be applied at the cathode. As a result, a problem occurs in which the carbon support of the catalyst layer (CL) is corroded in the cathode, resulting in performance degradation (Φ, electrolyte potential; Va, anode potential; c, cathode potential).

Various methods have been proposed to solve this problem, and they can be broadly classified into two categories. The first involves tailoring the SU/SD operation from a systemic viewpoint. For example, an inert gas can be injected into the anode to remove oxygen and air during the SU/SD process (21), a solenoid valve can be additionally installed to block the inflow of air (22), or an external load can be applied to the cathode to prevent the increase in potential (21, 23, 24). Unfortunately, this systematic approach results in overly complicated structures and an increase in the device size/cost. Another strategy for overcoming the corrosion issue is by applying newly developed catalyst or material (25–32). Notably, an approach that suppresses the ORR at the anode, which is considered the ultimate cause of catalyst corrosion, and allows only the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) to occur is drawing attention. Initially, the HOR selectivity was realized by loading organic materials, such as calix[4]arene (25, 26) or dodecanethiol (27), onto the catalyst surface using the self-assembled molecule technique, but the organic matter was unstable under thermal and electrochemical conditions. Recently, our group developed a Pt/HXWO3 catalyst based on the metal-insulator transition (MIT) phenomenon with excellent HOR selectivity while overcoming the problem of organic materials (33). However, subsequent research revealed that tungsten oxide, such as HXWO3, used as a catalyst support, is unsuitable in nanoparticle form for commercialization due to the following fatal drawbacks. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller surface area of tungsten oxide is typically lower than that of carbon black in used commercial Pt/C (34). In addition, HXWO3 particles with a particle size distribution of 150 to 200 nm tends to be too large (33), considering that the particle size of carbon black is around 50 nm (35). These properties can increase the viscosity of the catalyst ink and lead to poor uniformity in the CL, ultimately limiting catalyst performance. Moreover, when more tungsten oxide is exposed by the aggregation and migration of Pt during the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) decal transfer process (36), the interfacial resistance increases due to the low intrinsic conductivity of tungsten oxide, which can also lower the ionic conductivity. Another factor to consider is that tungsten oxide is hydrophilic unlike carbon black, which prevents the optimal dispersion of the ionomer in the catalyst ink, leading to difficulties in coating a uniform and stable CL on the membrane during MEA fabrication (37–39). These challenges highlight the need for further research on more appropriate methods for applying tungsten oxide to MEA.

Here, we propose a unique strategy for achieving HOR selectivity without changing the conventional MEA manufacturing process with Pt/C, by using a coating layer instead of a nanoparticle catalyst that reveals various limitations. Previous studies were limited to controlling only the catalyst system in the CL, whereas our proposed method achieves HOR selectivity by controlling the electronic conduction path in the entire cell system of the fuel cell. The electronic conduction path in the polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cell(PEMFC) comprises a pathway from the CL through the gas diffusion layer (GDL) and the bipolar plate (BP) to the current collector. We coated WO3 on the surfaces of CL, GDL, and BP, respectively, which belong to the anodic conduction path by physical vapor deposition (PVD) or decal transfer technique, and applied them individually to the cell. As a result, it was confirmed that the HOR selectivity by the MIT phenomenon was implemented the same as that of the Pt/HXWO3 catalyst, suggesting that blocking electronic conduction path by coating any one of the anodic components with WO3 can limit the charge transfer for ORR and ultimately enhance the durability of the PEMFC under SU/SD conditions. Among them, the MEA including the anodic CL coated with WO3 showed a performance retention rate of 94% under SU/SD conditions, which was superior to previous studies. To the best of our knowledge, this result is the highest level among similar studies reported. On the basis of the idea, we are convinced that adding the WO3 coating process to the conventional MEA fabrication is the simplest and the most excellent solution to overcome the abovementioned drawbacks of the nanoparticle Pt/HXWO3 catalyst.

RESULTS

WO3 deposited on BP, GDL, and MEA by PVD

To find out which anodic components should be coated with WO3 for the most effective durability enhancement under SU/SD conditions, WO3 was deposited by PVD technology on the surfaces of BP, GDL, and CL, respectively, which belong to the anodic conduction path. Because CL belongs to MEA, it will be referred to as MEA for convenience hereinafter. Figure 2 presents the position of WO3 layer formed on each component by magnetron sputtering. The WO3 layer formed on each component is confirmed by element-mapped cross-sectional images obtained by scanning electron microscope (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) (Fig. 3 and table S1). The SEM images in Fig. 3 (A, B, E, and F) show that distinct heterogeneous layers are formed on the surface of WO3-deposited BP and MEA compared to bare BP and MEA. In addition, EDS elemental mapping confirms that the WO3 layer was deposited on the surface of both components due to the high concentrations of W and O in the heterogeneous layer. On the other hand, the SEM image of WO3-deposited GDL shows that the heterogeneous layer formed on the surface of the GDL could not be distinguished. Still, in the EDS elemental mapping, it was identified that W and O elements were distributed close to the surface of the GDL (Fig. 3, C and D), suggesting that WO3 was infiltrated and deposited inside the porous structure of the GDL, unlike the rigid BP. Similarly, it can be confirmed even in the WO3-deposited MEA that some of W was infiltrated inside the MEA due to the flexible nature of the membrane. Pt mapping appears only in bare and WO3-deposited MEA due to Pt/C contained in the CL in MEA, and C mapping is also clearly visible as a layer on the surface of MEA for the same reason.

We compared the operational durability of unit cells applied with WO3-deposited BP, GDL, and MEA, respectively, in an environment simulating the SU/SD events. After shutdown of the normally operating PEMFC, air is flooded into the anode where the internal pressure is lowered by stopping the hydrogen supply. The air is diluted in concentration as it exits the anode flow field by resupplying hydrogen during start-up. As such, SU/SD conditions are characterized by the instantaneous coexistence of hydrogen and air at the anode. On the basis of this transient behavior, the SU/SD protocol tests (Fig. 4) were constructed by injecting hydrogen under normal operating conditions and air during SU/SD events into the anode. Figure 5 shows the comparison results of performance, represented by power density, and durability, represented by retention rate of power density, of the unit cells to which WO3-deposited anodic components were applied before and after the SU/SD protocol test. By adding the evaluation results for pristine MEA, we set it as a standard sample for comparison. Considering the performance (Fig. 5, E to H) first, the pristine MEA retained ~36% of the initial peak power density from ~0.86 to ~0.31 Wcm−2 after 100 simulated SU/SD cycles. The WO3-deposited BP retained ~58% of the peak power density (~0.33 to ~0.19 Wcm−2), but the initial power density was quite low as observed. The power density retention rate of WO3-deposited GDL is only ~53% (~0.87 to ~0.46 Wcm−2). On the other hand, WO3-deposited MEA shows a markedly superior power density retention rate of 81% (~0.85 to ~0.69 Wcm−2). This means that ORR at the anode, which is the cause of performance degradation, can be effectively suppressed as in previous studies using nanoparticles just by depositing WO3 on conventional MEA. It is even a 10% improvement over the state-of-the-art durability enhancement record that we reported (33). In summary, depositing WO3 on the MEA rather than BP and GDL showed the best performance and durability among all the components belonging to the anodic conduction path. It was also confirmed that the WO3-deposited MEA provides greater stability during SU/SD cycles when compared to the pristine MEA. However, it is crucial to verify that the introduction of an additional layer to the existing system does not compromise performance or stability. Therefore, we have included the PEMFC polarization curves in fig. S1, which directly compare the performance and power density of the MEA with and without the WO3 layer. Moreover, apart from the SU/SD test, an accelerated durability test was conducted to validate that the addition of the WO3 layer did not adversely affect long-term stability and was similarly stable to pristine MEA (fig. From the cyclic voltammograms (CVs) for the anode in single cells (Fig. 5, I to L), it is possible to infer the cause of the difference in durability of each anodic electrode under SU/SD conditions. In general, the CVs for the Pt catalyst electrode displays the characteristic hydrogen adsorption/desorption redox peaks in the potential range below 0.4 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) and the oxygen species adsorption/desorption redox peaks in the potential range above 0.5 V versus RHE, as shown in Fig. 5I. The hydrogen adsorption/desorption peaks related to HOR appear clearly in all CVs, but the oxygen species adsorption/desorption peaks related to ORR do not appear only in the CVs of WO3-deposited MEA, implying that the HOR selectivity is realized only in the WO3-deposited MEA. In previous studies, we have revealed that the HOR selectivity is realized due to the MIT phenomenon in tungsten oxide (33). WO3 deposited on the MEA becomes HXWO3 as protons are intercalated under HOR conditions and returns to WO3 as protons are deintercalated under ORR conditions. As a result, WO3 with low conductivity disables ORR, and HXWO3 with high conductivity enables HOR. It is known that the formation of HXWO3 through repetitive proton intercalation/deintercalation is accompanied by an increase in the electrochemical capacitance (40, 41). In the CVs for WO3-deposited MEA, symmetrical redox current appeared in the low potential region below 0.4 V versus RHE, and the Faradaic current became higher after enough cell operation as seen in fig. S3. The reason why only WO3 deposited on MEA could be converted to HXWO3 was ultimately related to the distribution of ionic conduction path, which also explains the excellent initial performance and high durability of the WO3-deposited MEA. The WO3 layer on the MEA had no difficulty in forming HXWO3 because the ionic conduction path was directly connected to the CL containing Nafion ionomer, which continuously supplied protons during cell operation. However, the WO3 layer on BP could not receive protons due to the ionic conduction pathway blocked by GDL, which has no ionic conductor, and had difficulty in converting to HXWO3, so it could not serve as a conductor required for HOR as well as ORR. The WO3 layer on GDL also had problems in converting to HXWO3 due to poor connection with the ionic conduction path, but above all, it was fatal that it could not completely block the electronic conduction path. It was not possible to form a perfect WO3 layer on the surface of GDL due to its porous structure, and even that was easily collapsed because of the compression during fastening the cell. This results in the GDL, rather than the deposited WO3 layer, locally directly contacting the CL and an electron conduction pathway bypassing the WO3 layer is formed, leading to the situation in which the WO3 layer cannot control charge transfer at all. For these reasons, although WO3 was deposited identically, the WO3-deposited BP had low electronic conductivity, and the WO3-deposited GDL could not completely suppress the ORR at the anode. Only the WO3-deposited MEA could realize HOR selectivity, thereby achieving enhanced durability. These phenomena could also be confirmed through electrochromism known to accompany the conversion of WO3 to HXWO3 by proton intercalation/deintercalation (42–45). Figure S4 shows surface images of each anodic component in initial state, after magnetron sputtering WO3, and after SU/SD protocol test. The surface color of the MEA loaded with Pt/C, which is originally black, becomes violet after being overlaid with the deposited WO3 layer. The surface color of the WO3-deposited MEA changes to dark blue due to the conductivity generated in HXWO3 formed on MEA through repeated proton intercalation during cell operation. After the SU/SD protocol test, the WO3-deposited BP without an ionic conduction path has a yellow surface by complete proton deintercalation, and the WO3-deposited GDL shows that the collapsed surface was partially due to its porous structure and cell clamping pressure, as previously described.

This improvement in durability during SU/SD can also be observed as the suppression of ORR at the anode and subsequent limitation of the potential jump experienced by the cathodic CL. To directly verify the suppression of the potential jump at the cathode during SU/SD, the potential variation of cathode, anode, and cell voltages during H2/air mixing in the anode were measured by a three-electrode MEA configuration using the dynamic hydrogen electrode (DHE), as shown in Fig. 5 (M to P). The suppression of the ORR at the WO3-deposited MEA is demonstrated by the lower peak potential experienced by the cathode CL following introduction of air into the anode compared to the other samples. The WO3-deposited MEA exhibited excellent HOR selectivity as the potential did not reach 1.2 V, whereas that the cathode potential for the pristine MEA surged to 1.5 V and the WO3-deposited GDL also allowed a substantial potential increase without completely suppressing ORR. Exceptionally, WO3-deposited BP also induced only a slight increase in potential, but it was purely due to its low electronic conductivity, so it is unreasonable that the ORR suppression ability was improved.

Next, the changes in the cross-sectional thickness of cathodic CL in the MEA before and after the 100 simulated SU/SD cycles were compared in the SEM results shown in Fig. 5 (Q to T). The WO3-deposited MEA retained the thickest cathodic CL among all samples, showing its excellent ORR suppression results. WO3-deposited MEA allowed only ~9% (8.46 to 7.67 μm) reduction in the thickness of the cathodic catalyst later, whereas the thickness reduction of ~10% (9.01 to 8.09 μm) for the WO3-deposited BP, ~23% (8.52 to 6.53 μm) for the WO3-deposited GDL, and even ~41% (8.75 to 5.15 μm) for the pristine MEA was observed. The degree of this change in the thickness of the cathodic CL due to carbon corrosion is consistent with the order in which the potential rise was radical during the SU/SD event in the potential profiling results. The excellent durability of the WO3-deposited MEA can be confirmed even in change of the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) in the cathode after SU/SD test (fig. S5 and table S2). The WO3-deposited MEA showed an ECSA reduction of ~26% (58.2 to 33.8 cm2) before and after the SU/SD protocol test, while the WO3-deposited BP was ~49% (56.1 to 21.0 cm2), the WO3-deposited GDL was ~42% (51.6 to 26.5 cm2), and the pristine MEA even allowed an ECSA reduction of ~63% (56.4 to 41.6 cm2). As an extra to cathodic CL, SEM-EDS analysis was also conducted on the anodic CL, further confirming that the WO3 layer was maintained after the MEA performance evaluation as well as after the SU/SD cycles (fig. S6).

Together, the above results confirmed that the WO3 layer deposited on the MEA notably improved the durability of the cathodic CL under the SU/SD condition through efficient ORR blocking ability and was the most suitable for application to the HOR selective system among all samples. Accordingly, we subsequently conducted further investigations focusing on the HOR selectivity for WO3-deposited MEA.

To begin with, we compared the performance and durability of WO3 layers deposited with 100, 500, and 1000 nm on the MEA to determine the optimal thickness of the WO3 layer. The results are shown in fig. S7, and the WO3 layer deposited with 500 nm on MEA demonstrated the best performance and durability. For the 100-nm deposition, ORR blocking was not perfect, resulting in worse durability than that of 500 nm. On the other hand, the 1000-nm deposition resulted in considerably reduced performance due to the high ohmic resistance caused by the insulator characteristic of WO3. Therefore, we selected 500 nm as the optimal deposition thickness and applied WO3-deposited MEA with a thickness of 500 nm for all experiments. WO3-deposited MEA with a thickness of 500 nm was verified for reproducibility by coplotting fuel cell degradation data from experiments under the same conditions for samples fabricated three times separately (fig. S8).

Figure S9 and table S3 display the results of the investigating the gas emitted from the cathode under normal and SU/SD conditions using gas chromatography (GC) and estimating the corrosion degree of the cathodic CL in WO3-deposited MEA and pristine MEA (20, 46). In the gases emitted from the cathode, O2, N2, CO, and CO2 were detected by the GC. Because O2 and N2 originated from air, while CO and CO2 originated from COR in the carbon support caused by an increase in cathode potential under the SU/SD event, we estimated the degree of corrosion with the signals of CO and CO2. For the WO3-deposited MEA, it was evident that the measured CO and CO2 intensity did not considerably increase under SU/SD conditions as well as under normal conditions. Conversely, for the pristine MEA, it was found that the intensity of CO and CO2, which were hardly measured under normal conditions, increased rapidly under SU/SD conditions. Therefore, it was clearly confirmed once again that the carbon corrosion at the cathode under SU/SD conditions can be alleviated in the unit cell where the WO3-deposited MEA was applied to the anode.

The selective conductivity via proton intercalation/deintercalation in the WO3-deposited MEA

To elucidate the durability enhancement mechanism of the WO3-deposited MEA under SU/SD conditions, the change in the electrode internal resistance was scrutinized through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis (fig. S10). The anode onto which the WO3-deposited MEA was applied was measured in the N2-saturated atmosphere. The resistance measurement was performed under two conditions: 0.05 and 1.0 V versus RHE. A value of 0.05 V is the potential for the proton adsorption/desorption on the catalyst surface, which implements an environment similar to the normal operating condition at anode. A value of 1.0 V is the potential for oxygen species adsorption/desorption on the catalyst surface, which implements the ORR condition similar to SU/SD events at the anode. The red semicircle was formed for the normal operating condition case (0.05 V versus RHE) and a blue semicircle was drawn for the SU/SD event case (1.0 V versus RHE). These experimental Nyquist spectra simulated by the circuit are indicated with red and blue boxes, respectively, at the top of fig. S10 (47–52). The circuits consist of one resistance element and two parallel-connected resistance and capacitance elements connected in series. Considering the internal structure of the electrode at the anode, the single resistor located at the front implements the solution resistance between the membrane and CL. The subsequent parallel-connected resistors and capacitors represent resistive and capacitive responses between the CL and WO3 layers. The last item is related to the interface between the WO3 layer and the GDL. On the basis of the circuit values, we closely examined the internal resistance configuration of the WO3-deposited MEA, and only the resistance values ​​are shown as a bar graph in Fig. 6A. The resistance between the membrane and CL is 593 milliohms under the normal operating condition and 617 milliohms under the SU/SD condition; these values are similar with a small difference of 24 milliohms. The resistance values between the WO3 layer and the GDL are 27.9 and 2.41 milliohms, respectively, and not considerably different also. Noteworthy is the resistance between the CL and WO3 layers, indicated by the red bar graph in Fig. 6A. This charge-transport resistance was as high as that of an insulator at 6930 milliohms under the SU/SD condition, but it decreased notably to 518 milliohms under the normal operating condition. From this result, it is evident that the conductivity varies between the CL and the WO3 layer depending on the operating conditions. Because the possibility of a notable difference in conductivity in the CL, which is mainly composed of Pt and C, depending on the operating conditions is low, it can be interpreted that the selective conductivity is conferred on the WO3 layer through the MIT phenomenon. Moreover, under the normal operating condition, the capacitance value between the CL and WO3 layers in the EIS simulation data increased by more than 2.5 times (434 to 1090 mF) compared to that under the SU/SD condition. This is because the capacitance was increased by forming hydrogen tungsten bronze (HXWO3) through proton intercalation in WO3. As a result, it can be seen that this conversion from WO3 to HXWO3 increases the number of conductive carriers and improves the electrical conductivity and cell performance during the normal operation (53, 54). Conversely, reversion from HXWO3 to WO3 markedly reduces electrical conductivity, resulting in ORR suppression under SU/SD conditions, thereby enhancing cell durability. Furthermore, we evaluated the resistance change in MEAs with and without a WO3 layer, considering the potential role of the WO3 layer as a resistive component during fuel cell operation (fig. S11). The pristine MEA and the WO3-deposited MEA exhibited similar resistance values and characteristics except for the difference in the anode-side circuit configuration due to the existence of the WO3 layer. This suggests that the additional conductivity loss due to the WO3 layer is not an issue under operating conditions.

Measurement of resistance change depending on the hydrogen concentration in the atmosphere.

(A) Resistance configuration measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and (B) in situ resistance modulation measurement.

On the basis of the results so far, it was found that the behavior of the WO3 layer being converted to HXWO3 and vice versa is a major factor in implementing the selective electrical conductivity. We noted that this change in resistance was different between normal operation condition and SU/SD conditions and speculated that it was a phenomenon dependent on the hydrogen content in the atmosphere, which varies with operating conditions. To further investigate this hypothesis, the WO3-deposited MEA was placed in a chamber maintained at 80°C, and its resistance according to the change of H2 concentration in the atmosphere was directly measured in situ while sequentially injecting air and H2, as depicted in Fig. 6B. After the injection of H2, the resistance rapidly decreased by three orders of magnitude, indicating an insulator-to-metal transition due to the formation of HXWO3. This is because, during the transition to HXWO3, H2 molecules are adsorbed on the surface of the WO3 layer and converted into protons, which then intercalated into the WO3 lattice to transfer electrons, thereby increasing the number of conductive carriers. Upon subsequent reexposure of the hydrogenated HXWO3 film to ambient air, proton deintercalation occurs in the WO3 lattice, which converts HXWO3 back to WO3 and increases resistance by up to four orders of magnitude. Hence, the in situ resistance measurement result of the WO3-deposited MEA can be a direct evidence that the electrical resistance of the WO3 layer varies depending on the hydrogen content in the atmosphere. In addition, this suggests that the selective electrical conductivity is attributed to the MIT phenomenon caused by the intercalation/deintercalation of protons in the WO3 lattice.

The MIT phenomena that enable this selectivity of electrical conductivity are also clearly identified in x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis. XPS examined in detail the proton intercalation/deintercalation mechanism of the WO3-deposited MEA in terms of electronic structural changes of the pristine MEA and the WO3-deposited MEA before and after activation (fig. S12). The activation refers to the process of sufficiently changing the initial state of WO3 into HXWO3 before performance evaluation. The ex situ XPS results include the W 4f, O 1s, and Pt 4f core-level photoemission spectra and distribution. For W 4f spectra of the WO3-deposited MEA, relative concentrations of stoichiometric W6+ and off-stoichiometric W5+ were obtained (40, 55), as shown in Fig. 7A. Thereafter, peak deconvolution was performed for O 1s, after which the spectral weight of the peaks was obtained and analyzed through a mixed Gaussian-Lorentzian function (Fig. 7B and table S4). After activation, the relative intensity of W5+ increased considerably compared to that in the initial state, and the W 4f7/2 peak red-shifted by 0.1 eV from 35.00 eV of the initial data to 34.90 eV. These behaviors are attributed to the reduction of WO3 to HXWO3 as the protons intercalate. Similarly, for O 1s, the relative concentration of the oxygen vacancy or surface hydroxyl increased after activation as many impurities and defects that serve as a conduction path to transfer electrons were generated inside the WO3 lattice (56). This phenomenon that occurs in WO3 layer upon activation indicates a change in stoichiometry with HXWO3 formation through proton intercalation. In addition, this correlation between stoichiometry and electrical conductivity further clarifies the process by which HOR selectivity is imparted to the WO3 layer. For Pt 4f, while a clear peak exists for the pristine MEA, no Pt peak appears for the WO3-deposited MEA (fig. S12A). This is because the 500-nm thickness of WO3 is overly high for the XPS measurement; thus, the CL located below WO3 layer cannot be measured.

In summary, under normal operating conditions, H2 atoms dissociated by Pt and adsorbed at the interface between the CL and the WO3 layer are intercalated in the form of protons inside the WO3 lattice. As protons continue to be intercalated, the WO3 layer changes into HXWO3, with numerous defects and oxygen vacancies. HXWO3 layer provides the electrical conduction path through which electrons generated via HOR can move to the current collector during PEMFC operation, based on abundant defects. In addition, repeated proton intercalation/deintercalation results in high capacitance and sufficient proton retention, preventing such as fuel starvation, which may occur during unexpected transient conditions, and maintaining a stable hydrogen supply to the anodic electrode. However, upon the introduction of air or oxygen as in SU/SD event, the protons in the WO3 lattice are deintercalated again, resulting in a loss in conductivity. In such cases, the WO3 layer as a common oxide insulator merely inhibits the transfer of Faradaic charge. As a result, the selective electrical conductivity in WO3-deposited MEA, which is enabled by the MIT phenomenon, notably enhances the PEMFC durability by limiting the degradation of the cathode catalyst under the SU/SD conditions.

WO3-coated MEA by decal transfer technique instead of PVD process

Thus far, it has been demonstrated that the WO3 layer located on the CL of MEA improves the durability of fuel cells under SU/SD conditions by promoting the HOR selectively through proton intercalation/deintercalation. However, the PVD technique may not be perfectly suitable for commercialization, because it requires a high vacuum condition and the WO3 layer must be selectively deposited on the CL region. Therefore, we tried to show that the MEA coated with WO3 can be produced even with the decal transfer technique commonly used for MEA production. Figure 8 presents the performance and durability before and after the SU/SD protocol tests of a cell in which MEA coated with a WO3 layer is applied to the anode side. WO3-deposited MEA made using the PVD technique retained ~81% (~0.85 to ~0.69 Wcm−2) of its initial peak power density, which was already an improvement compared to previous studies, in Fig. 5H. Furthermore, WO3-coated MEA made using the decal transfer technique retained ~94% (~0.82 to ~0.77 Wcm−2), achieving once more innovative durability improvement. In the PVD technique, ion and electron conduction pathways are formed inside the WO3 lattice only through proton intercalation. However, the decal transfer technique accompanying the mixing of WO3 and Nafion ionomer is further advantageous in improving the ionic conductivity because the Nafion and the WO3 layer coexist in the first place. Consequently, both performance and durability of WO3-coated MEA fabricated by the decal transfer technique were superior, and the activation loss, which can be observed in the low–current density region in the current-voltage (I-V) curve, was also notably improved in decal transfer technique compared to the PVD method. Compared with the results shown in Fig. 5P, where the potential exceeds 1.1 V under the SU/SD condition, the WO3-coated MEA fabricated by the decal transfer technique exhibits a gentle potential peak that does not exceed 1.1 V in Fig. 8B. Comparing the cathodic CL thickness before and after the SU/SD protocol tests, a decrease of ~8% (8.31 to 7.62 μm) was observed in Fig. 8C for the decal-obtained WO3-coated MEA, which surpasses the ~9% (8.46 to 7.67 μm) of the WO3-deposited MEA obtained by PVD in Fig. 5T. Therefore, the WO3-coated MEA fabricated by the decal method shows notable potential for commercialization, due to its simplicity, excellent performance, and durability.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have demonstrated that selective conductivity by MIT phenomena in the WO3 layer via proton intercalation/deintercalation is a promising approach to mitigate cathode degradation and enhance durability during SU/SD events of FCEVs. Instead of using a nanoparticle catalyst, which faces several challenges for commercialization, we have developed a facile method that can be applied to the established manufacturing process. The WO3 layer formed on the anodic CL in the MEA is obtained either by deposition with PVD process or by coating with a decal transfer technique. Selectivity of electrical conduction is achieved by enabling electron and ion conduction paths through the conversion from WO3 to HXWO3 with proton intercalation in a high-concentration H2 atmosphere, while insulator characteristics become prominent with proton deintercalation again in a low-concentration H2 atmosphere. Excellent durability is confirmed by HOR selectivity that sufficiently inhibits ORR in unit cell onto which the WO3-deposited MEA by PVD was applied. Furthermore, WO3-coated MEA by decal transfer technique that can overcome the limitations of the PVD method is revealed to be more suitable for commercialization and successfully achieved state-of-the-art durability under the SU/SD condition of FCEV. Together, simply coating the MEA with the WO3 layer enhances the durability of PEMFC under the SU/SD condition, strongly suggesting that it is the most effective way to overcome the problem of PEMFC operational longevity that is considered a substantial obstacle to the commercialization of FCEVs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fabrication of MEA and formation of the WO3 layer on the anodic components

The MEA was prepared using the Pt/C [Tanaka Kikinzoku Kogyo (TKK), TEC10E50E, 46.9 wt % Pt on Ketjen Black] electrocatalyst at 0.1 mgPt cm−2 for the anode and 0.4 mgPt cm−2 for the cathode. The slurry in which the Pt/C powder was mixed with a 5% Nafion ionomer solution and isopropyl alcohol was stirred for 24 hours. A sufficiently stirred catalyst ink was printed on an imide film coated with polytetrafluoroethylene using a 150-μm doctor blade. After 14 hours at room temperature, the sufficiently dried catalyst film was laminated on both sides with a Nafion 211 membrane (Dupont, USA) between them, followed by thermocompression at 150°C.

For WO3 deposited on the anodic components by PVD process, the WO3 layer was deposited on the BP, GDL, and MEA by radiofrequency magnetron sputter deposition in a sputter deposition chamber (Korea Vacuum Tech) with a pressure of 40 mtorr and power of 40 W.

For WO3-coated MEA by decal transfer technique, after the imide film coated with the preprepared mixed slurry of WO3 and Nafion solution using a doctor blade was sufficiently dried, CL for anode is overcoated on it. After drying in the air for 14 hours, the imide film on which the WO3 layers and CL were stacked was cut to fit the active area of ​​5 cm2. Thereafter, on the basis of the membrane, CL (0.1 mgPt cm−2)/the WO3 layer/imide film for the anode was laminated on one side, while the CL (0.4 mgPt cm−2)/imide film for cathode was laminated on the other side and transferred by hot pressing.

Material characterization

X-ray diffraction (Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany) analysis was performed to confirm the formation of the WO3 layer, and the purpose of the analysis was not achieved because the signal for other substances was stronger than that of WO3. Alternatively, the cross section of each component on which the WO3 layer was deposited was investigated by field-emission SEM (FE-SEM) and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy [high-resolution FE-SEM-II (JSM 7800F PRIME with dual EDS)] at the National Institute for Nanomaterials Technology. Afterward, to investigate the electronic state of WO3 according to the experimental conditions, high-resolution photoemission spectroscopy (HR-PES) analysis was performed at the 8A1 (HR-PES I) undulator beamline in the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory.

In situ resistance modulation measurement

Ambient-controlled in situ electrical characterization was performed in a chamber probe station equipped with a temperature control system and a precisely controllable gas flow system. The hydrogenation of the WO3-deposited MEA was performed while introducing a 5% hydrogen-forming gas into the chamber at 80°C at a flow rate of 1000 standard cubic centimeter per minute. The dehydrogenation process was performed by exposing the chamber to ambient air.

Evaluation of performance and durability for the unit cell

The I-V curves were measured using a fuel cell station (CNL Energy) to evaluate the performance of the unit cell. H2 was supplied to the anode, while N2 containing 21% oxygen was supplied to the cathode. During the evaluation, the relative humidity was 100%, and the cell temperature was 70°C. In the SU/SD protocol test, a gas switching device (CNL Energy) that replaces the gas supplied to the anode from H2 to air was used. The gases emitted from the cathode of the unit cell were directly injected into an online GC (YL6500) equipped with flame ionization and thermal conductivity detectors to determine the changes in the CO and CO2 contents under the SU/SD condition.

Electrochemical measurements

All the electrochemical tests were performed using a potentiostat (Autolab PGSTAT204, Metrohm). The CVs shows the electrochemical characteristics of the anode before and after 100 cycles of the SU/SD protocol test in the unit cell. Cyclic voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 50 mVs−1 between 0.05 and 1.0 V versus RHE. For EIS analysis, the anode was saturated with N2, while the cathode was purged with H2, and the potentiostat was galvanostatically connected to the unit cell. EIS was performed in the frequency range from 1000 kHz to 20 mHz, applying 0.05 and 1.0 V versus RHE to the anode, sequentially. Notably, 0.05 V versus RHE was the potential region where hydrogen was adsorbed, and the intended normal condition under which hydrogen was supplied to the anode was realized. The potential region where oxygen was adsorbed is 1.0 V versus RHE, and the SU/SD condition was realized under which oxygen was supplied to the anode. The EIS analysis on MEAs with and without tungsten oxide layer was performed under ex situ operating conditions with anode/cathode saturated with H2/air and after cutting off at 0.6-V voltage sweeps started from 0.9 V. Metrohm’s software, NOVA 2.1, was used for the EIS to determine the electrode internal resistance configuration. In addition, we could numerically analyze the resistance and capacitance values ​​of each identified resistor by conducting fittings and simulations using NOVA 2.1.
