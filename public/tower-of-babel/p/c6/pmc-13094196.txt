Addressing knowledge–practice disparities through community-based health education: integrating a one health framework to mitigate wastewater-associated infectious diseases and antimicrobial resistance in Gombe, Nigeria
Authors: Zeenatuddeen Muhammad, Muhammed Tukur Adamu, Lawal Garba, Umar Abdullahi Tawfiq, Abubakar Muhammad Abdulmumini, Usman Abdullahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor wastewater management in sub-Saharan Africa contributes significantly to infectious disease transmission and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) development, representing a critical One Health challenge at the human–environment interface. While technical solutions exist, sustainable behavior change requires understanding local knowledge systems and community dynamics. Effective AMR prevention cannot be achieved without robust community-centered health education that addresses environmental pathways of resistance development. This study examined how culturally tailored social science approaches can improve household wastewater management practices in Nigerian communities to combat AMR through a One Health lens.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) study across seven districts in Gombe Metropolis, Nigeria (n = 320), followed by a quasi-experimental (non-controlled) 12-week community-centered health education incorporating One Health principles in the lowest-performing district. No biological samples were collected, all outcomes were based on self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and practices assessments. The intervention addressed AMR awareness gaps, environmental transmission pathways, and integrated human-environmental health considerations using local knowledge systems, community leadership structures, and culturally appropriate communication strategies.ResultsBaseline assessment revealed critical knowledge-practice gaps despite moderate correlations among domains (r = 0.51–0.57), with knowledge explaining only 26–32% of variance in attitudes and practices: while 39.7% demonstrated high knowledge levels, only 20% had heard of AMR, and 81% disposed of expired medications improperly, practices directly contributing to environmental AMR development. Gender differences favored males across all domains (p < 0.05), and income strongly influenced performance (p < 0.001). Post-intervention assessment revealed substantial changes in addressing these One Health challenges: knowledge scores increased by 80% (2.5 to 4.5, p < 0.001), AMR awareness rose substantially, and self-reported practice adoption showed 60% improvement (2.5 to 4.0, p < 0.001), including better medication disposal practices. Attitude changes remained moderate, reflecting persistent structural constraints including inadequate waste management infrastructure, limited municipal sanitation systems beyond household control, and socioeconomic barriers requiring longer-term One Health interventions.ConclusionsWhile the quasi-experimental design limits definitive causal attribution, these findings suggest potential benefits of culturally adapted health education approaches. Community-centered health education interventions respecting local knowledge while introducing evidence-based One Health practices offer promising pathways for bridging knowledge-practice gaps in AMR prevention. The persistence of moderate attitudes despite improved knowledge and practices highlights that effective AMR control requires sustained, multi-faceted community engagement addressing both individual behaviors and systemic infrastructural barriers, as attitude formation depends not only on education but also on enabling environmental conditions that facilitate safer practices. These findings have important policy implications for urban environmental health planning in Nigeria, suggesting that effective AMR prevention strategies should integrate community health education with improvements in municipal waste management infrastructure, establishment of medication take-back programs, and cross-sectoral coordination among health, environmental, and urban planning agencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26922-3.

Introduction

Sub-Saharan Africa faces severe public health challenges from inadequate wastewater management [1, 2], which drives infectious disease burden and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) development through complex environmental pathways [3]. Environmental contamination facilitates AMR pathogen emergence and spread, demonstrating the interconnected human-animal-environment health dynamics central to One Health frameworks [4, 5]. Nigeria's rapid urbanization and infrastructure deficits intensify these challenges, with contaminated wastewater systems creating reservoirs and transmission pathways that enable resistant organism proliferation [6].

Without effective intervention, AMR could result in approximately 10 million deaths annually by 2050, positioning resistance as a critical global health threat [7]. Environmental contamination from improper wastewater management constitutes an underrecognized yet significant driver of AMR development and dissemination. Antibiotics entering environmental systems through human waste, agricultural runoff, and pharmaceutical disposal exert selective pressure favoring resistant bacterial survival and proliferation in environmental reservoirs [8, 9].

Resource-limited settings such as Nigeria face particularly acute environmental AMR challenges. Limited sewage treatment infrastructure allows human waste laden with antibiotic residues and resistant bacteria to contaminate water bodies directly, establishing extensive environmental resistance gene reservoirs with cross-species transfer potential [10, 11]. This environmental AMR development occurs independently of clinical antibiotic use, making it a critical component that must be addressed through comprehensive One Health strategies.

One Health frameworks emphasize the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health domains, necessitating coordinated cross-sectoral interventions for effective solutions [12]. Despite increased One Health recognition among policymakers and researchers, community-level implementation remains difficult, especially for addressing environmental AMR pathways [13].

Robust community-centered health education provides the essential foundation for successful One Health AMR prevention approaches. Unlike clinical AMR manifesting as observable treatment failure, environmental AMR develops through complex, invisible processes, rendering awareness and education essential for behavioral change [14, 15]. Individual household wastewater disposal and medication management practices collectively drive community-wide AMR development, necessitating widespread behavioral changes achievable only through effective health education [16, 17]. Current AMR education predominantly addresses clinical antibiotic use while neglecting environmental pathways potentially equivalent or superior in driving community-level resistance development [18]. Consequently, communities may fail to perceive environmental AMR risks as relevant or actionable without culturally appropriate education building upon local knowledge systems.

Traditional health education approaches frequently fail to achieve AMR prevention goals by neglecting local knowledge systems, cultural beliefs, and socioeconomic contexts shaping environmental health behaviors [18, 19]. Community-centered health education offers a more effective alternative through participatory approaches leveraging existing community strengths while addressing AMR awareness and prevention gaps [20, 21].

Environmental pathways introduce complexity to knowledge-attitude-practice relationships in AMR prevention. Communities often grasp basic hygiene principles yet remain unaware of how wastewater management and medication disposal practices contribute to AMR development. This knowledge-practice gap poses significant challenges since environmental AMR prevention demands consistent, sustained behavioral modifications across entire communities [21]. Such changes require voluntary adoption through education and awareness rather than mandates.

Gombe State and other Nigerian regions encounter specific One Health implementation challenges for AMR prevention, stemming from rapid population growth, infrastructure investment limitations, socioeconomic disparities, and limited environmental health awareness [22]. As a rapidly expanding urban center, Gombe Metropolis offers an optimal context for investigating community-centered approaches to these multifaceted One Health AMR challenges.

While the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) framework effectively informs health behavior understanding and intervention design [23, 24], limited research explores these domain relationships within environmental AMR prevention and One Health implementation in Nigerian communities. Additionally, research remains scarce on culturally tailored interventions addressing knowledge-practice disparities specific to environmental AMR pathways.

We address these critical gaps through: (1) comprehensive KAP assessment regarding wastewater management and environmental AMR prevention across multiple Gombe Metropolis districts; (2) examination of sociodemographic influences on KAP performance within One Health contexts; (3) implementation and evaluation of a community-centered health education intervention explicitly integrating One Health principles and AMR awareness; and (4) development of evidence-based recommendations for scaling effective One Health AMR prevention approaches in resource-limited urban settings.

The central hypothesis of this research is that effective One Health implementation for AMR prevention is fundamentally dependent on robust community-centered health education that addresses environmental pathways of resistance development. Technical and policy interventions alone prove insufficient for addressing community-level AMR crises without this educational foundation.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We implemented a mixed-methods design integrating cross-sectional survey research with quasi-experimental intervention methodology to address One Health dimensions of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) prevention. The research was conducted in two phases in Gombe Metropolis, the capital city of Gombe State in northeastern Nigeria, between December 2024 and April 2025. Gombe Metropolis contains eleven administrative districts encompassing 46,112 households across various districts [25]. This medium-sized Nigerian urban center exhibits mixed formal and informal settlements, variable infrastructure development levels, and diverse socioeconomic conditions creating heterogeneous environments for AMR development and transmission.

This setting offers particular relevance for One Health AMR research given the close proximity among human settlements, animal husbandry activities, and environmental contamination sources. Livestock maintenance by numerous households creates cross-species resistant organism transmission potential, while inadequate waste management establishes environmental resistant gene reservoirs.

We applied multi-stage random sampling to select communities and households, ensuring diverse socioeconomic and geographic representation. Seven districts were randomly selected from the state's administrative divisions, representing both urban and rural settings. Proportional sampling within each district ensured population-proportionate district representation. We randomly selected two wards per district through balloting, then applied simple random sampling for household selection within wards to minimize selection bias and ensure geographical representativeness.

Contextual observations of community characteristics

While our primary data collection employed structured questionnaires, field teams documented contextual observations of community organizational characteristics through enumerator debriefing sessions and informal reports. Enumerators noted differences in community structure, leadership engagement, and collective organization that affected data collection processes (e.g., ease of community entry, presence of coordinating community structures). Community members also made unsolicited references to local health activities and organizational structures during household visits. These qualitative observations, while not systematically measured with structured observational tools, provided valuable contextual understanding of district-level variations. We acknowledge that these observations represent field-based impressions rather than rigorously measured variables, but their consistency across multiple enumerators and community interactions suggested meaningful differences in community organizational capacity that warranted discussion as potential contextual factors influencing KAP performance.

Phase 2: one health-centered health education intervention

Intervention site selection

Phase 1 results guided selection of Pantami district for intervention based on its consistently lowest KAP domain performance, especially in AMR awareness and environmental health practices. This selection offered optimal conditions for demonstrating intervention effectiveness while targeting greatest One Health education needs.

Intervention description following TIDieR Framework

Why: We developed the intervention responding to baseline findings revealing critical AMR awareness gaps (only 20% had heard of AMR), poor medication disposal practices (81% disposing expired medications in household trash), and inadequate environmental health behaviors. Our theoretical framework integrated Social Cognitive Theory, Community-Based Participatory Research principles, and One Health approaches addressing interconnected human, animal, and environmental health dimensions in AMR prevention.

What (Materials): Educational materials comprised illustrated English and Hausa flipcharts depicting AMR transmission pathways, wastewater management technique posters, handwashing and medication disposal demonstration kits, One Health principle summary sheets for home use, and community discussion guides for group sessions.

What (Procedures): Households received an initial 45-min structured education session addressing AMR concepts, environmental transmission pathways, and preventive behaviors, with three subsequent visits at weeks 4, 8, and 12 for reinforcement and problem-solving. Practical demonstrations covered proper handwashing techniques, safe medication disposal methods, and improved wastewater management practices. Bi-weekly community-wide sessions at central locations reinforced messages and facilitated peer learning.

Who Provided: Four trained community health educators (two male, two female, all fluent in English and Hausa) and twelve Gombe State University AMR Club volunteers delivered the intervention. All implementers completed comprehensive three-day training addressing One Health principles, AMR concepts, participatory education methods, and culturally sensitive communication strategies.

How: Delivery occurred through face-to-face household visits (primary method), small group community sessions (15–20 participants), and integration with existing structures including religious gatherings, women's groups, and youth organizations. Our approach prioritized participatory dialogue over lectures, encouraging experience-sharing and collective solution development for AMR prevention challenges.

Where: All intervention activities occurred within Pantami district at participant households and community gathering spaces.

When and How Much: The intervention ran for 12 weeks from mid-January to mid-April 2025. Households received four direct contacts (baseline visit plus three follow-ups) lasting 30–45 min each, totaling approximately 2–3 h of direct contact. Six bi-weekly community-wide sessions of 90 min each occurred throughout the intervention period.

Tailoring: We adapted content according to household literacy levels (increased visual aids for limited literacy), primary language preference (English or Hausa), household composition (age-appropriate messaging for children or elderly presence), and baseline assessment concerns.

Modifications: Minor protocol modifications included flexible household visit scheduling to accommodate work schedules and brief reinforcement messages (5 min) during Friday prayers with religious leader permission, reaching an estimated 200 additional community members beyond target households.

Implementation process

A team of trained community health educators and Gombe State University AMR Club volunteers, all trained in One Health principles and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) prevention, implemented the intervention. During the 12-week period, Pantami district households received at least four direct engagements complemented by community-wide outreach activities. Household sessions, lasting 30–45 min, followed a structured curriculum while allowing flexibility to address specific concerns related to environmental AMR prevention.

Educator training and intervention

Fidelity Educator Selection and Training: Selection criteria for community health educators included: minimum diploma-level health-related education, English and Hausa fluency, prior community health outreach experience, and Gombe Metropolis residence. All educators and volunteers completed comprehensive three-day training (December 17–19, 2024) addressing One Health principles and AMR concepts (Day 1), participatory education techniques and cultural sensitivity (Day 2), and intervention protocol (Day 3). Training materials comprised video demonstrations of household visit techniques and case studies of common implementation challenges. We assessed competency through practical demonstrations before field deployment.

Intervention Fidelity Monitoring: We employed multiple fidelity strategies to ensure consistent implementation quality. Structured visit logs completed after each household contact monitored process fidelity, documenting delivered content, duration, materials used, and encountered barriers. Bi-weekly team meetings reviewed logs, discussed challenges, and ensured standardized approaches. The research coordinator directly observed 20% of household visits using standardized observation checklists. We assessed content fidelity using a 12-element core content checklist for household visits, with spot checks during community sessions verifying key message delivery. Dose fidelity tracking documented contact number and duration per household plus community session attendance records.

Fidelity Results: Monitoring data analysis demonstrated high implementation fidelity: 96% of household visits included all 12 core content elements, 94% of households (301 of 320) received all four planned visits (6% received three visits due to temporary absence), 89% met the 30-min minimum duration (mean 37 min, SD = 8.5), and 83% of households had at least one family member attend community sessions (mean attendance 2.1 sessions per household). Direct observations showed 92% protocol adherence with feedback provided for improvement areas.

Protocol Deviations: Protocol deviations included: reducing monthly community-wide "AMR awareness days" to two events (weeks 6 and 12) due to logistical constraints and community preference for smaller gatherings; adding brief reinforcement messages (5 min) during Friday prayers with religious leader approval; and developing simplified visual materials for low-literacy households (approximately 15% of participants). We extended the intervention timeline from 12 to 14 weeks to accommodate Ramadan observance scheduling. All deviations received real-time documentation, research team approval, and did not compromise participant safety or study integrity.

Data analysis

We performed data analysis using R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2024) within RStudio version 2023.12.1. Our analytic workflow employed several R packages: tidyverse (v2.0.0) for data manipulation and visualization, psych (v2.3.12) for descriptive and reliability statistics, car (v3.1–2) for diagnostic tests and ANOVA procedures, ggplot2 (v3.4.4) for graphical outputs, corrplot (v0.92) for correlation matrices, effsize (v0.8.1) for effect size computation, and rstatix (v0.7.2) for statistical tests. We exported data from KoboToolbox as CSV files and imported into R. Quality control included verifying data completeness, checking numeric ranges, and assessing logical consistency (e.g., ensuring education levels matched respondent age). All 320 baseline surveys demonstrated complete responses for the primary KAP variables. We retained "Prefer not to say" responses for socio-demographic items, particularly household income (n = 44), as a separate analytic category rather than treating as missing data. No imputation procedures were applied. We generated KAP scores using standardized scoring procedures with deliberate AMR-related knowledge weighting. Knowledge scoring (0–6 scale) reflected correct responses to factual questions on wastewater, hygiene, disease transmission, and AMR, emphasizing environmental transmission pathway awareness. Attitude scores (0–3 scale) derived from Likert-scale responses reflecting AMR risk perceptions and environmental prevention beliefs. Practice scores (0–6 scale) reflected reported wastewater management, hygiene, and medication disposal behaviors. Composite scores were calculated as the mean of item-level responses within each domain, allowing for fractional values (e.g., a median of 2.5 on the 0–3 attitude scale represents the midpoint between response categories). All statistical comparisons used these continuous composite scores rather than categorical responses. Cronbach's alpha assessment indicated acceptable internal consistency reliability for knowledge (α = 0.78) and attitude (α = 0.71) scales, and good reliability for the practice scale (α = 0.82). We categorized participants into high, moderate, or low KAP levels based on score percentiles: high (≥ 75th percentile), moderate (25th–74th percentile), and low (< 25th percentile).

We evaluated normality assumptions for continuous variables using Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q-Q plot inspection, applying parametric tests when assumptions were met and non-parametric alternatives otherwise. Two-group comparisons used independent samples t-tests (normal distributions) or Mann–Whitney U tests (non-normal distributions). Multi-group comparisons (≥ 3 groups) employed one-way ANOVA (normal distributions) or Kruskal–Wallis tests (non-normal data). Intervention group pre-post comparisons used paired samples t-tests (normal outcomes) or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (non-normal outcomes), with effect sizes reported as Cohen's d or r respectively. Categorical variable analysis employed chi-square independence tests (expected cell counts ≥ 5) or Fisher's exact tests (expected cell counts < 5). Correlation analysis used Pearson coefficients (normal continuous variables) or Spearman coefficients (non-normal/ordinal variables).

We applied Bonferroni correction for multiple testing in seven-district comparisons, yielding an adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.007. Exploratory socio-demographic analyses (age, gender, education, occupation, income) received no correction as they were hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05 (two-tailed) for all analyses except where Bonferroni adjustments were applied. We interpreted effect sizes using Cohen's conventional thresholds: small (0.2 or r = 0.1), medium (0.5 or r = 0.3), and large (0.8 or r = 0.5). All p-values are reported to three decimal places, with values < 0.001 denoted accordingly.

One health-centered health education intervention results

The 12-week One Health-centered intervention produced significant improvements. Knowledge scores (0–6 scale) increased by 80% (median 2.5 to 4.5, p < 0.001), AMR awareness rose from 20 to 75%, and understanding of environmental AMR pathways improved from 7.5% to 60%. Recognition of medication disposal risks as contributors to AMR development increased from 27 to 80% of participants.

Practice scores (0–6 scale) improved from a median of 2.5 to 4.0 (p < 0.001), representing a 60% improvement based on self-reported behaviors. Proper medication disposal practices improved from 19 to 65%, while use of improved wastewater management practices increased from 33 to 70%. The use of septic tanks or proper treatment facilities increased from 7 to 25%. However, attitude scores (0–3 scale) showed a slight decrease from 2.5 to 2.0.

Environmental protection behaviors increased from 15 to 55% of participants, while households implementing multiple AMR prevention practices increased from 5 to 40%. Community discussions about environmental health increased substantially. Discussion

Our findings reveal critical knowledge-practice gaps and limited antimicrobial resistance (AMR) awareness in Gombe Metropolis, highlighting community-centered health education's role within One Health frameworks. Despite most respondents having at least secondary education (Table 1), AMR awareness remained low (Table 3), consistent with evidence that formal education alone does not ensure antimicrobial resistance awareness [29].

Sanitation and waste management practices indicated widespread AMR risk exposure. Household reliance on unsafe sanitation systems and poor wastewater handling practices (Tables 2 and 3) likely contribute to the circulation of resistant pathogens in the environment. Expired medicine disposal patterns (Table 5) suggest an underrecognized environmental AMR development pathway. These findings align with evidence from other resource-limited settings where environmental AMR drivers remain underrecognized yet potentially significant [30, 31].

Baseline assessment revealed an apparent paradox: relatively high general health knowledge coexisted with limited AMR awareness and weak preventive practices (Tables 3 and 5). Medicine disposal patterns particularly highlight an underrecognized yet potentially important environmental resistance pathway. In settings with inadequate waste infrastructure, discarded antibiotics can contaminate soil and water systems, sustaining environmental reservoirs of resistance [32]. The intervention suggests communities can adopt safer practices when informed of these risks, as reflected in improved disposal behavior (Fig. 9).

Attitudinal barriers represent a major challenge to AMR prevention [33, 34]. Although respondents demonstrated general hygiene knowledge, they showed limited understanding of connections between WASH practices, medication disposal, and resistance development (Table 4). The moderate but incomplete knowledge-attitude correlation (r = 0.51, explaining only 26% of variance) observed in our baseline analysis (Fig. 3) demonstrates that knowledge alone is insufficient to shape risk perceptions and beliefs. This substantial unexplained variance (74%) in attitudes highlights the role of factors beyond knowledge, including lived experiences with infrastructure, cultural beliefs, and socioeconomic realities. This may partly explain why knowledge and practices improved post-intervention while attitudes showed minimal change (Fig. 9). Attitude formation requires not only education but also enabling structural conditions and longer-term exposure to change environmental realities.

Our analysis revealed significant geographic variations in KAP performance across the seven districts (Fig. 2, p < 0.001), with important implications for understanding contextual determinants of environmental AMR prevention capacity. District-level performance patterns reflected complex interactions among infrastructure, socioeconomic, and community factors. Jeka da Fari and Kumbiya Kumbiya districts demonstrated highest overall KAP scores, while Pantami consistently showed lowest performance across all three domains. These variations cannot be attributed to geographic proximity alone, as districts with similar locations showed divergent performance. Several contextual factors likely shaped these district-level differences:

Infrastructure and Environmental Conditions: Districts with better-established sewage systems and regular waste collection services (Jeka da Fari, Kumbiya Kumbiya) showed higher practice scores, suggesting that infrastructure availability enables safer behaviors. Pantami's lower scores corresponded with documented infrastructure deficits including irregular waste collection, frequent wastewater overflow in public spaces, and limited access to improved sanitation facilities. This pattern reinforces that knowledge and attitudes cannot easily translate to practices without enabling infrastructure.

Socioeconomic Composition: District-level income distributions correlated with KAP performance. Higher-performing districts had greater proportions of middle-income households with resources to invest in improved waste management practices (e.g., septic tank maintenance, purchasing cleaning products). Pantami's higher proportion of low-income households may constrain adoption of resource-intensive preventive behaviors, even when knowledge is present.

Community Organization and Social Capital: During data collection and community engagement processes, field enumerators consistently noted differences in community organizational capacity across districts. Higher-performing districts (Jeka da Fari, Kumbiya Kumbiya) demonstrated more active community health committees and stronger neighborhood associations. Enumerators reported that community entry was facilitated by organized community structures in these districts, including community leaders who coordinated household visits and health committees that provided local context. Community members in these districts made spontaneous references to ongoing health activities and collective environmental maintenance efforts. In contrast, Pantami and lower-performing districts required individual household-by-household engagement without centralized community coordination, and community members less frequently mentioned organized health activities. While not captured through structured observational instruments, these consistent patterns across field teams and districts provided contextual insight into how community social capital may influence environmental health behaviors and receptivity to health messaging. Previous Exposure to Health Programming: Districts with recent health education programming (regardless of topic) showed somewhat higher baseline knowledge scores, suggesting possible spillover effects from general health literacy development. This highlights the value of sustained community engagement across health topics.

Settlement Patterns and Population Density: Pantami's rapid population growth and informal settlement expansion may outpace infrastructure development and community organization, creating conditions where environmental health risks accumulate faster than community capacity to address them.

These contextual variations suggests that One Health AMR prevention interventions must be tailored to specific community circumstances rather than uniformly applied. The success of our intervention in Pantami — the lowest-performing district, demonstrates that targeted, intensive community engagement can overcome initial disadvantages, but sustained improvements likely require addressing underlying infrastructural and socioeconomic constraints.

Future research should systematically examine how infrastructure availability, socioeconomic conditions, community social capital, and settlement characteristics interact to shape environmental AMR prevention capacity. Such understanding would inform more precise targeting of interventions and appropriate combinations of education, infrastructure investment, and community mobilization strategies for different urban contexts.

The intervention suggests potential benefits of culturally tailored One Health-centered education. Post-intervention data showed substantial gains in AMR awareness and improvements in preventive practices (Fig. 9). However, attitudes showed minimal change, which we interpret as reflecting structural and contextual constraints rather than intervention ineffectiveness. Specifically, attitude formation toward environmental AMR prevention is constrained by: (1) inadequate municipal waste management infrastructure beyond household control, limiting residents' ability to properly dispose of wastewater and medications regardless of knowledge; (2) absence of accessible medication take-back programs or hazardous waste collection systems; (3) socioeconomic barriers including poverty that constrains adoption of resource-intensive safer practices; and (4) the long-term nature of attitude change, which may require sustained interventions beyond our 12-week timeframe [35]. These findings underscore that One Health AMR prevention cannot be achieved through individual behavior change alone but requires coordinated improvements in environmental infrastructure, waste management systems, and enabling policy environments.

Observed sociodemographic inequities have important implications for AMR prevention. Gender gaps across all KAP domains (Fig. 5) suggest potential systemic barriers to women's environmental health information access, despite their central role in household waste management. Poverty showed strong association with poor preventive practices (Fig. 6), suggesting structural inequities may constrain safer behavior adoption even when knowledge exists.

The intervention's effectiveness may have been enhanced by integrating One Health concepts with local knowledge and cultural practices. Traditional ecological understanding of environmental health offered a foundation for explaining AMR in locally meaningful and scientifically sound ways. This cultural adaptation may have contributed to rapid knowledge and practice improvement uptake [36, 37]. Nonetheless, environmental AMR risks' invisibility, unlike clinical treatment failure's immediacy, may present a barrier to attitude change.

Scaling up One Health-centered education likely requires broader structural support. Educational interventions should likely be complemented by health system strengthening, rational antibiotic use, and improved diagnostic capacity [38]. Infrastructure for safe disposal and sanitation appears necessary, as communities may struggle to sustain behavior change without enabling [39, 40]. Effective One Health implementation likely requires cross-sectoral collaboration spanning human health, veterinary services, environmental management, and education sectors [41].

This study addressed four critical gaps in environmental AMR prevention research that we identified in our conceptualization. First, our comprehensive KAP assessment across seven districts in Gombe Metropolis (Gap 1) revealed substantial baseline knowledge deficits regarding environmental AMR pathways, with only 20% having heard of AMR and 81% disposing medications improperly. This multi-district assessment demonstrated that environmental AMR awareness gaps are widespread across diverse urban contexts, not isolated to specific communities. The geographic variation we documented (Fig. 2) underscores the need for context-specific interventions rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.

Second, our examination of sociodemographic influences on KAP performance (Gap 2) revealed systematic disparities with important One Health implications. Gender differences across all domains (Fig. 5), despite women's primary responsibility for household waste management, suggest structural barriers to women's environmental health information access. Income-related disparities (Fig. 6) indicate that poverty constrains AMR-preventive behavior adoption even when knowledge exists, highlighting that One Health AMR prevention cannot succeed without addressing socioeconomic inequities. These findings demonstrate that effective AMR prevention must account for social determinants of health and environmental behaviors.

Third, our community-centered intervention explicitly integrating One Health principles and AMR awareness (Gap 3) demonstrated that culturally adapted education can rapidly improve knowledge and practices related to environmental AMR prevention. The substantial increases in AMR awareness (20% to 75%) and improved medication disposal practices within 12 weeks suggest that when One Health concepts are presented through culturally appropriate frameworks and local knowledge systems, communities can quickly grasp connections between human behaviors, environmental contamination, and AMR development. However, the moderate attitude change despite knowledge and practice improvements confirmed that education alone is insufficient without structural support.

Fourth, our findings inform evidence-based recommendations for scaling One Health AMR prevention in resource-limited settings (Gap 4). Effective scaling requires: (1) culturally adapted education integrating local knowledge systems rather than top-down technical messaging; (2) complementary infrastructure investments in waste management and medication disposal systems, as education cannot overcome structural barriers; (3) gender-responsive approaches addressing women's information access and decision-making roles; (4) socioeconomically sensitive strategies that do not place undue burden on economically disadvantaged households; and (5) sustained engagement beyond short-term interventions, particularly for attitude change and behavioral maintenance. These recommendations emphasize that One Health AMR prevention requires multi-level, multi-sectoral approaches addressing individual, community, and structural determinants.

By addressing these four gaps, our study contributes a comprehensive understanding of how community-centered One Health approaches can advance environmental AMR prevention in sub-Saharan African urban contexts, while highlighting the persistent structural and contextual barriers that education alone cannot overcome.

These findings may have broader implications beyond our study context. Environmental AMR pathways, though often underrecognized, may represent important drivers of resistance. Community education may rapidly improve prevention behaviors, though sustained change likely depends on cultural adaptation, infrastructure support, and long-term engagement. The persistence of limited attitude change in this study suggests deeper community engagement may be needed to develop lasting beliefs around environmental AMR risks.

Study limitations and future research

This study has several important limitations that affect interpretation of findings. First, the quasi-experimental design without a concurrent control group limits causal inference; observed changes may reflect factors beyond the intervention itself, including temporal trends, secular changes, or measurement effects. Second, the 12-week intervention period provides insufficient time to assess long-term sustainability of behavior changes or to determine whether improved practices translate into measurable environmental AMR reduction. Third, the urban setting and relatively high educational attainment (88% with at least secondary education) may limit generalizability to rural communities or populations with lower educational levels, where different strategies might be needed. Fourth, the selection of the lowest-performing district for intervention, while justified for demonstrating need, may introduce selection bias affecting outcome interpretation. Fifth, our reliance on self-reported practices rather than objective observations may introduce social desirability bias, potentially overestimating actual behavior change. Sixth, the lack of microbiological data measuring actual environmental AMR levels means we cannot confirm that reported practice improvements translate into reduced environmental resistance. Seventh, the intervention's resource intensity (four household visits plus community sessions) may limit scalability in resource-constrained settings. Finally, the study's focus on a single metropolis within one Nigerian state limits generalizability to other contexts with different cultural, infrastructural, or socioeconomic conditions. Future research should address these limitations through: (1) controlled trials with concurrent comparison groups to strengthen causal inference; (2) longitudinal follow-up extending beyond 12 weeks to assess sustainability; (3) objective behavioral observations complementing self-reports; (4) environmental microbiological monitoring (e.g., resistance gene quantification in wastewater) to verify that practice changes reduce environmental AMR; (5) testing in diverse contexts including rural, low-literacy settings; and (6) cost-effectiveness analyses to inform scalability decisions.

Conclusions

This study examined how culturally tailored, community-centered health education can transform household wastewater management practices to combat antimicrobial resistance through a One Health lens in Gombe, Nigeria. Our findings demonstrate that such interventions can rapidly improve knowledge and practices related to environmental AMR prevention pathways, though the quasi-experimental design limits definitive causal conclusions.

The baseline KAP assessment revealed critical gaps that the intervention successfully addressed: AMR awareness increased from 20 to 75%, knowledge scores improved by 80% (from 2.5 to 4.5, p < 0.001), and self-reported wastewater management and medication disposal practices showed 60% improvement (from 2.5 to 4.0, p < 0.001) within 12 weeks. These substantial changes in knowledge and practices suggest that community-centered education integrating One Health principles with local knowledge systems offers a promising approach to addressing environmental AMR pathways in resource-limited urban settings.

However, moderate attitude change despite knowledge and practice improvements highlights a key challenge: One Health AMR prevention may require not only knowledge and skill acquisition, but also improvements in structural conditions that enable safer behaviors. The limited attitude change likely reflects infrastructural constraints beyond household control, including inadequate municipal waste management systems, absence of medication disposal facilities, and socioeconomic barriers, rather than educational intervention failure. This finding emphasizes that sustainable AMR prevention through One Health approaches requires coordinated action across multiple levels: individual education, community engagement, infrastructure development, and supportive policies. Future interventions should integrate longer-term community engagement with systemic improvements in waste management infrastructure and enabling environments for behavior change, though this hypothesis requires testing through extended follow-up studies and multi-sectoral interventions.

For policymakers and public health practitioners in Nigeria and similar sub-Saharan African contexts, these findings suggest that: (1) culturally adapted community-centered education can rapidly improve AMR-related knowledge and practices; (2) interventions addressing environmental AMR pathways should be prioritized given the critical baseline knowledge gaps identified; (3) sustainable behavior change requires complementary investments in waste management infrastructure and medication disposal systems; and (4) attitude formation and sustained practice adoption likely require longer-term interventions beyond typical 12-week programs.

Future research should test these approaches through controlled trials with longer follow-up periods, incorporate environmental microbiological monitoring to verify that practice improvements reduce actual AMR levels, and evaluate cost-effectiveness for scalability. The integration of community health education with infrastructure development and enabling policy environments represents a critical frontier for One Health-based AMR prevention in urban sub-Saharan Africa.
