Unveiling prognostic factors and predictive modeling in lung adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation
Authors: Wei Li, Yuanming Pan, Siyu Cai, Shenglong Xie, Bin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSome lung cancer patients are pathologically confirmed to have lung adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation (LUAD-ND). However, research on this subtype remains limited. This study aimed to systematically investigate the metastatic patterns and prognosis-related factors of LUAD-ND, and construct neural network-based prediction models for survival outcomes.MethodsBy analyzing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, we employed the Cox proportional hazards model to investigate prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in patients with LUAD-ND. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and detailed the median survival time and specific time survival probabilities for different features in the LUAD-ND population. Finally, using a neural network algorithm, we developed a predictive model for forecasting LUAD-ND’s OS and CSS, evaluating its performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsMost patients with LUAD-ND were diagnosed at an advanced stage. The OS time of patients with LUAD-ND was 12 (95% CI: 10–14) months, and the CSS time was 14 (95% CI: 12–16) months. The most common distant metastatic sites were bone, followed by liver, brain, and lung. Surgery (HR =0.51; 95% CI: 0.31–0.82; P=0.006) and chemotherapy (HR =0.33; 95% CI: 0.21–0.50; P<0.001) were associated with improved OS. Similarly, surgery (HR =0.49; 95% CI: 0.28–0.84; P=0.01) and chemotherapy (HR =0.31; 95% CI: 0.19–0.49; P<0.001) were linked to better CSS. The neural network-based tool can effectively predict the prognosis of LUAD-ND, achieving an AUC of 0.852–0.864 for 6-month OS and 0.835–0.883 for 6-month CSS.ConclusionsPatients with LUAD-ND face a dismal prognosis, yet chemotherapy and surgical interventions can ameliorate their outcomes. The neural network tool developed in this study yields precise prognostic estimations.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), neuroendocrine differentiation (ND), metastasis, prognosis, prediction model

• Neural network model enables personalized prognosis assessment.

Introduction

Lung cancer is currently the second most common malignancy in both genders and also has the highest mortality rate among cancers (1,2). Adenocarcinoma is the most prevalent histological subtype of lung cancer (3-5). Following the classification criteria established by the World Health Organization (WHO), neuroendocrine carcinoma, another subtype found in lung cancer, is characterized by tumor tissue that exhibits positive neuroendocrine markers, and the minimum threshold for positivity should be more than 50% of cells (6,7). Meanwhile, some lung adenocarcinoma tissues can exhibit positive neuroendocrine markers, typically in less than 50% of cases. This subtype is classified as lung adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation (LUAD-ND) (6,7). Immunohistochemistry has confirmed that approximately 10–30% of non-small cell lung cancer (like lung adenocarcinoma) patients exhibit neuroendocrine differentiation (7-11). Unfortunately, it has been observed that LUAD-ND is associated with poorer prognosis than those without neuroendocrine differentiation (7,12). And due to the rarity of LUAD-ND, relevant research is limited, and our understanding of its clinical significance remains constrained. Detailed reports on LUAD-ND’s metastatic patterns and survival probabilities area also lacking.

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, one of the largest tumor databases in the United States, is particularly suitable for researching rare diseases such as LUAD-ND. In this study, we comprehensively investigated the metastatic patterns of LUAD-ND, analyzed the prognosis related factors, reported median survival time and specific time survival probabilities, and constructed a neural network-based prediction model by using the SEER database. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-12/rc).

Methods

Study design and data source

This study was conducted in the form of a retrospective case-control study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. We selected cases from the SEER sub-dataset Incidence-SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2023 Sub (2000–2021). The inclusion criteria for cases were as follow: patients were diagnosed with malignant lung tumors [by setting Site and Morphology Behavior code International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd edition (ICD-O-3) as Malignant, and Site and Morphology Site recode ICD-O-3/WHO 2008 as Lung and Bronchus], and the pathological examination confirmed as LUAD-ND (by setting Site and Morphology ICD-O-3 Hist/behav, malignant as 8574/3: adenocarcinoma with neuroendocrine differentiation) (Figure 1).

The flowchart for this study. ICD-O-3, International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd edition; SEER, Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results; WHO, World Health Organization.

The primary study outcome for this research was overall survival (OS), followed by cancer-specific survival (CSS). Since this study exclusively utilized publicly available SEER database data for analysis, and SEER has obtained ethical approval prior to collecting data, institutional review board approval or written consent from patients was not required for this study.

We collected the following clinical features: age, race, sex, primary site, laterality of tumor, history of malignancy, American Joint Committee on Cancer tumor node metastasis stage (TNM stage), tumor size, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, therapy (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy and systemic therapy) and survival status. The study data were submitted by the SEER team in November 2023, with the last follow-up date being December 31, 2021. Due to limitations in SEER records, patients aged over 90 years were still recorded as 90 years, and survival time less than 1 month was still recorded as 1 month.

Survival analysis

In survival analysis, the Cox proportional hazards model was applied for univariate analysis by incorporating age, race, sex, history of malignancy, TNM stage, tumor size, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and systemic therapy. Factors with P values less than 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate regression. We used backward stepwise selection to derive the final regression model. And we focused on OS and CSS endpoints respectively to identify factors associated with LUAD-ND prognosis. We partially merged the staging categories, grouping them into broader classes. For example, T1a was consolidated into T1, and IIa and IIb were collectively referred to as stage II. Based on the results of survival analysis, we calculated the hazard ratio (HR), median survival time and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for both the overall LUAD-ND patient population and specific subgroups. Additionally, we estimated their survival probabilities at 6, 12, 24, 36, and 60 months, along with their corresponding 95% CIs.

Feature engineering and prognostic model construction

We randomly divided all patients into two cohorts: the discovery cohort (comprising 80% of the total population) and the validation cohort (comprising 20% of the total population). We used the discovery cohort for constructing the prognostic model and further evaluated model performance in the validation cohort. Prior to modeling, missing values were imputed using chained equations in the R mice package. Based on clinical experience, age, race, sex, primary site, laterality, history of malignancy, T, N, M, stage, tumor size, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are potential predictive variables. For variable selection, we employed least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Specifically, using the glmnet R package with the discovery cohort data, we built LASSO regression models to apply separately for OS and CSS endpoints. The optimal lambda values in above models were determined by using 5-fold cross-validation. Finally, we constructed LASSO regression models for OS and CSS using the minimum lambda value, observing the coefficients of each clinical feature. Clinical features with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO regression were considered predictive variables for the model in either OS or CSS.

The prognostic models were developed in Python 3.9. First, categorical clinical data were converted into numerical encodings (Table S1). For numeric clinical features in the dataset, mean and standard deviation were computed (Table S2). Subsequently, these numeric features were standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation (referred to as z-score transformation or data normalization). The validation cohort underwent the same preprocessing steps.

Next, we extracted the predictive variables identified by LASSO and the two survival outcomes. Using pandas, we read the file and converted it into a Numpy numerical matrix. Following the DeepSurv theory, we employed Pycox and PyTorch to build neural network models for predicting patients’ OS and CSS outcomes (13). During training, we enabled batch normalization layers for automatic data standardization. Batch training was performed with a batch size of 32 samples. Additionally, we included dropout layers (10–20%) and used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate set to 0.01. Early stopping function was implemented to monitor training: if the model’s performance did not significantly improve after 20 epochs, training would automatically terminate. The final model output was a matrix (excluding bias terms), which, after sigmoid transformation, represented the survival probabilities (OS or CSS) for different patients in subsequent months.

Model evaluation and compression

We assessed the predictive performance of the models by truncating its predictions at 6, 12, 36, and 60 months. The primary evaluation metric was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), followed by specificity, sensitivity, accuracy, negative predictive value (NPV), and positive predictive value (PPV). Finally, the model was designed using PySide6 as a 64-bit Windows software and packaged into an executable file using PyInstaller.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4. For continuous data, the Wilcoxon rank sum test was employed, while categorical data were analyzed using either Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s Chi-squared test. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

This study encompassed a total of 643 patients, with a nearly even distribution between females (n=309) and males (n=334). The median age was approximately 68 years, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 61 to 76 years. A majority of the patients were White (80%, n=513), followed by Black (14%, n=88), Asian or Pacific Islander (5%, n=34), and a small percentage of American Indian/Alaska Native (less than 1%, n=3). The primary site of cancer was predominantly the upper lobe of the lung (52%, n=335), followed by the lower lobe (24%, n=156). The right side was the origin of the primary cancer in 53% (n=338) of cases. A history of malignancy was present in 26% (n=167) of the patient population. T4, N2 and M0 classifications were prevalent. Half of the patients were at stage IV (50%, n=198) at diagnosis. The median tumor size was 35.0 (IQR, 24.0–59.0) mm (Table 1).

Prognostic relevant clinical features

Clinical features were incorporated into Cox regression models. For univariate analysis, variables with P values less than 0.05 were included in the multivariate Cox regression using backward stepwise selection. The final model revealed associations between these factors and worse OS: T4 (HR =2.17; 95% CI: 1.14–4.13; P=0.02), N2 (HR =1.78; 95% CI: 1.10–2.90; P=0.02), N3 (HR =1.91; 95% CI: 1.05–3.47; P=0.03), stage II (HR =2.18; 95% CI: 1.08–4.41; P=0.03), stage IV (HR =7.53; 95% CI: 3.31–17.15; P<0.001), and bone metastasis (HR =2.15; 95% CI: 1.33–3.45; P=0.002). Conversely, surgery (HR =0.51; 95% CI: 0.31–0.82; P=0.006) and chemotherapy (HR =0.33; 95% CI: 0.21–0.50; P<0.001) were associated with better OS. Similar associations were observed for CSS: T4 (HR =2.37; 95% CI: 1.16–4.84; P=0.02), N2 (HR =1.81; 95% CI: 1.08–3.03; P=0.02), N3 (HR =1.91; 95% CI: 1.02–3.61; P=0.045), stage II (HR =2.53; 95% CI: 1.13–5.69; P=0.02), stage IV (HR =10.21; 95% CI: 4.11–25.37; P<0.001), and bone metastasis (HR =2.12; 95% CI: 1.29–3.48; P=0.003). Conversely, surgery (HR =0.49; 95% CI: 0.28–0.84; P=0.01) and chemotherapy (HR =0.31; 95% CI: 0.19–0.49; P<0.001) were associated with better CSS. Interestingly, lung metastasis (HR =0.46; 95% CI: 0.25–0.85; P=0.01) also showed an association (Table 2).

Prognostic model

After imputing missing values (Figure S1A), all patients were randomly divided into a discovery cohort (80% of the total sample) and a validation cohort (remaining 20%). There were no significant differences in clinical characteristics between the two cohorts (Table S5). The discovery cohort was used for variable selection, and combining LASSO regression, we found that the following clinical features had non-zero regression coefficients when OS or CSS was the endpoint: age, race, sex, primary site, laterality, history of malignancy, T, N, M, stage, tumor size, bone metastasis, brain metastasis, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and systemic therapy (Table S6 and Figure S1B). Therefore, they were selected as predictive variables for the models. Using neural network techniques, we separately built models to predict OS and CSS time for patients with LUAD-ND. The OS model training ended at 37 epochs using an early stopping function (Figure 3A), while the CSS model training ended at 31 epochs (Figure 3B).

The model performance summary was provided in the Table 3. When predicting OS, the discovery cohort performed best at 6 months with an AUC of 0.864 (95% CI: 0.831–0.897). At 60 months, the performance remained reasonable with an AUC of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.735–0.837). The validation cohort also exceled at 6 months, achieving an AUC of 0.852 (95% CI: 0.786–0.917) while at 60 months, the AUC decreased to 0.714 (95% CI: 0.614–0.814). For CSS, the discovery cohort performed well at 6 months with an AUC of 0.835 (95% CI: 0.798–0.871). At 60 months, the AUC decreased to 0.717 (95% CI: 0.667–0.767). Similarly, the validation cohort exceled at 6 months with an AUC of 0.883 (95% CI: 0.826–0.940) while at 60 months, the AUC decreased to 0.717 (95% CI: 0.625–0.809). Overall, the models demonstrated favorable predictive values for OS and CSS in patients with LUAD-ND (Table 3).

Discussion

Despite some remaining controversies, the latest research has indicated that the prognosis of LUAD-ND significantly differs from that of adenocarcinoma, with a worse prognosis for LUAD-ND (7,8,12,14,15). For example, the study conducted by Tai et al. have pointed out that the prognosis of LUAD-ND is worse than that of lung neuroendocrine carcinoma or lung adenocarcinoma by using SEER database (7). Therefore, it is necessary to conduct a detailed study specifically focused on LUAD-ND. However, due to the rarity of LUAD-ND, research on it remains limited. There are currently no reports on its metastatic patterns, detailed survival probabilities, or time-related outcomes. Additionally, effective prognostic tools are lacking. In this study, we found that most patients with LUAD-ND were diagnosed at an advanced stage. The OS time of patients with LUAD-ND was 12 (95% CI: 10–14) months, and the CSS time was 14 (95% CI: 12–16) months. The most common distant metastatic sites were bone, followed by liver, brain, and lung. Surgery (HR =0.51; 95% CI: 0.31–0.82; P=0.006) and chemotherapy (HR =0.33; 95% CI: 0.21–0.50; P<0.001) were associated with improved OS. Similarly, surgery (HR =0.49; 95% CI: 0.28–0.84; P=0.01) and chemotherapy (HR =0.31; 95% CI: 0.19–0.49; P<0.001) were linked to better CSS. The neural network-based tool can effectively predict the prognosis of LUAD-ND, achieving an AUC of 0.852–0.864 for 6-month OS and 0.835–0.883 for 6-month CSS.

The causes and mechanisms underlying LUAD-ND remain unclear. Some scholars have noted that the LUAD-ND phenomenon may be associated with genetic mutations (such as RET), and targeted drugs (like osimertinib, a kind of EGFR inhibitors) have therapeutic effects on it (16-18). RET can even be used to further identify subtypes of LUAD-ND (19). Literature also reports that some patients initially diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma may undergo pathological subtype transformation during subsequent treatment, exhibiting neuroendocrine differentiation. This process may be associated with the upregulation (such as CD133, BCL-2, and SOX2) or downregulation of certain genes, including CRACD, related to plasticity of tumor environment (19-26). LUAD-ND has also been associated with multidrug resistance in some patients (27). The detailed mechanisms, genetic mutation landscape, and selection of targeted drugs for LUAD-ND still lack systematic research.

Our study found that patients with LUAD-ND were predominantly at stage IV and median survival time was about 12 months, consistent with the findings reported by Li et al. (28). However, their research did not demonstrate therapeutic significance for chemotherapy, and this potential difference cannot be ruled out due to sample size variations (they included only 47 patients). We found that surgical treatment was associated with better prognosis in LUAD-ND patients, consistent with the research by Tai et al. (7). However, their competitive risk analysis for CSS in patients with LUAD-ND did not endorse the therapeutic value of chemotherapy. The differences in the applied models may contribute to this discrepancy. However, Petrović et al.’s research indicated that LUAD-ND responded favorably to paclitaxel-cisplatin treatment (29). They also observed that cells with neuroendocrine differentiation exceeding 30% exhibited good chemotherapy response (29). Multifactorial analysis suggested that the percentage of neuroendocrine differentiation-positive tumor cells correlated with better prognosis (29). Therefore, the proportion of positive cells may be an important factor influencing chemotherapy response and could potentially explain the discrepancy between our study and the aforementioned research. Besides, Liu et al.’s research also indicated that gene-based subtype stratification can identify immunotherapy-sensitive populations for patients with LUAD-ND (30). However, given that the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a common indication for the use of immunotherapy, is infrequent in LUAD-ND, the utilization of immunotherapy for LUAD-ND remains an area requiring further investigation (17).

There are limitations in this study. The SEER database was employed in this study. To mitigate potential selection bias, it is imperative to conduct further validation of the study’s conclusions. This can be achieved through the utilization of additional independent validation datasets or data sourced from Asian populations, which would contribute to a more comprehensive and robust assessment of the findings. As a retrospective study, the presence of missing values in certain clinical information, particularly in tumor staging (e.g., T and N categories), may introduce information bias.

Conclusions

Patients with LUAD-ND face a dismal prognosis, yet chemotherapy and surgical interventions can ameliorate their outcomes. The neural network tool developed in this study yields precise prognostic estimations.
