LLM‐based keyword augmentation for title‐driven evidence selection: A practical approach
Authors: Sanghyun Yoo, Doowon Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKeyword‐based search is widely used in digital forensic investigations, yet its effectiveness depends strongly on investigator experience, leading to inconsistent results and missed evidence. While previous studies have explored machine learning and large language models (LLMs) to address this, practical deployment is often constrained by confidentiality requirements and the infrastructure costs of maintaining high‐performance models locally. We propose a practical LLM‐based keyword augmentation method that expands investigator‐supplied seed keywords, whether single words or multiple words, while restricting inputs to non‐sensitive case context. This enables rapid evidence triage using file names without transmitting primary evidence content to external services. We validate the approach in three stages: (i) using 426 documents, we confirm that file names correlate with document bodies through semantic similarity and keyword coverage analyses, showing clear separation from randomized file name–content pairings; (ii) in a benchmark of 1500 file names comprising 500 relevant cases and 1000 controls, prompt‐only keyword generation using ChatGPT models demonstrates effective retrieval performance, with ChatGPT‐4.1 achieving the best overall balance; and (iii) in a usability study involving 20 digital forensic investigators, augmented keywords improve evidence detection, with junior investigators showing statistically significant gains as assessed by the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Overall, the method supports efficient triage in geographically distributed and large‐scale investigations by applying LLM‐augmented keywords, thereby reducing experience‐related performance gaps.Keywords: file title‐based selection, keyword augmentation, keyword‐based search, large language model, remote triage

INTRODUCTION

Keyword‐based search, where investigators craft queries to locate evidence, is widely used in digital forensics due to its simplicity and reproducibility [1, 2]. However, the rapid proliferation of smartphones and cloud services has led to exponential growth in the volume of data requiring analysis. Combined with constraints in resources and personnel, this surge poses significant challenges for timely investigations [3, 4].

To address these challenges, investigators often perform triage and selection based on file names, assuming that the authors generally assign meaningful titles that reflect core content [5]. This approach has proven effective in narrowing down large datasets under resource constraints, but it introduces risks. The choice and combination of search keywords, whether single words or multiple words, depend heavily on investigator expertise and intuition, resulting in variability and the potential to miss critical evidence [6].

Such inconsistencies are particularly problematic in contexts where skilled investigators cannot be deployed to every case. Furthermore, while research suggests that title‐based selection is effective for large‐scale filtering, similar to web search, systematic validation of its quantitative reliability and validity remains insufficient. This gap may undermine the admissibility of such results in court [7, 8].

In other forensic domains, such as image and video analysis, automated machine learning methods yield strong performance and are already adopted by some law enforcement. By contrast, classification of textual strings—especially in multilingual contexts—remains limited. Short textual metadata, such as document file names, constrain performance when analyzed using traditional approaches [9, 10, 11]. Meanwhile, large language models (LLMs) designed for language tasks show significant potential to enhance investigation performance. However, transmitting sensitive evidence to external LLM services raises confidentiality and procedural concerns. Local LLMs, while mitigating these issues, demand high deployment and maintenance costs and struggle to match the pace of commercial model improvements [4].

To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a methodology that utilizes commercial LLMs (e.g., Chat GPT) to augment search keywords without transmitting sensitive primary evidence to external servers. The method leverages LLMs to semantically expand investigator‐provided keywords, producing augmented sets that enable rapid title‐based screening of large document collections. The augmented keyword list is returned as recommendations, either for immediate use in searches or as a foundation for further refinement.

We evaluate the proposed methodology through three experiments, each aligned with a specific research question (RQ). RQ1 asks whether file names reliably represent the content of their bodies; to test this, we analyzed the correlation between titles and full texts across 426 public documents. RQ2 examines whether meaningful evidence selection can be achieved using only LLM‐provided keywords; to address this, we performed benchmark tests with 1500 synthetic file names measuring precision and recall of LLM‐generated recommendations. RQ3 investigates whether LLM‐based keyword augmentation supports practical forensic work; to evaluate this, we conducted a usability study with 20 digital forensic investigators at varying levels of experience.

This study makes three key contributions to the digital forensic community:

Validation of title‐based selection: We empirically demonstrate that file names correlate strongly with their full content, providing scientific support for forensic use.

Reduction of skill disparities: The LLM‐based augmentation approach enhances overall search performance and particularly benefits novice investigators, helping to close experience gaps.

RELATED WORK AND BACKGROUND

Keyword‐based search in digital forensics

Keyword‐based search remains one of the most common methods for identifying candidate evidence during forensic analysis. Most digital forensic tools (e.g., EnCase Magnet AXIOM) support keyword search and, for advanced users, regular expressions. These tools can extend searches beyond file names and titles into unallocated and slack space. Because search inputs and outputs are explicit and reproducible keyword search offers strong evidentiary admissibility in court [2, 6].

However, its effectiveness depends heavily on the investigator's experience, subjectivity, and level of fatigue. Files containing slang, obfuscations, or intentional misspellings may evade detection, resulting in missed evidence [3]. In addition, the exponential growth of digital data has raised practical error rates when relying solely on manual keyword search workflows, highlighting the need for complementary automated methods such as triage and title‐based selection.

Digital forensics and machine learning

Machine learning has been increasingly applied to digital forensics. Serhal et al. [11] proposed automated item classification using smartphone file metadata, evaluating algorithms such as naive Bayes, k‐nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machines (SVM), classification and regression trees (CART), multi‐layer perceptron (MLP), and random forest (RF) on approximately two million records from Android smartphones seized in a terrorist case. SVM achieved the highest F1‐score (0.9735), while the lowest‐performing model (naive Bayes) still reached 0.8795. Li et al. [10] explored content classification using only file names. By vectorizing titles with TF‐IDF and applying multiple algorithms—including SVM, naive Bayes, RF and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—alongside Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT), they found that RF achieved the highest accuracy. Their findings suggest that file names alone can predict content with relatively high accuracy.

These studies confirm the potential of machine learning for forensic classification tasks. However, most approaches remain limited to metadata or title‐level classification and face challenges in multilingual settings, particularly in selection (triage) problems where recall is critical under vocabulary mismatches.

Digital forensics and large language models

More recently, LLMs have been studied for forensic applications. Kim et al. [12] demonstrated the utility of ChatGPT‐4o in analyzing messenger data from a narcotics case. However, transmitting case data to external services risks unauthorized disclosure and may compromise chain‐of‐custody integrity. Hargreaves et al. [4], surveying forensic examiners worldwide, reported caution about adopting LLMs due to concerns about accuracy, explainability, and security.

To address these concerns, local or on‐premises LLMs have been developed. Sharma et al. [13] introduced ForensicLLM, trained on Digital Forensics and Incident Response (DFIR) research articles and specialized materials, as a domain‐specific local model. Henseler and Meconi [14] presented Hansken Copilot, an LLM integrated with the Netherlands Forensic Institute (NFI)'s Hansken platform, offering summarization, translation, entity detection, and query generation within a secure private network.

Despite these advances, local deployments incur significant costs and require continual updates to remain competitive with commercial LLMs. Cloud‐based models quickly incorporate state‐of‐the‐art improvements, whereas local solutions often demand re‐engineering, fine‐tuning, and validation. Hallucinations—factually incorrect but plausible outputs—remain another challenge [13]. This trade‐off motivates our work: a pragmatic approach leveraging commercial LLMs for keyword augmentation without exposing sensitive primary evidence, thereby addressing the limitations of traditional keyword search while maintaining investigative security.

EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION OF TITLE‐BASED SELECTION

This section addresses RQ1, which explicitly asks: Do file names reliably represent the core content of their bodies? To answer this question, we quantitatively validate the reliability of title‐based document selection for forensic investigations. The primary goal is to determine whether file names accurately capture their corresponding content, thereby providing a scientific foundation for the use of title‐driven search in digital forensics.

Data collection

A total of 426 documents in Portable Document Format (PDF), Hangul Word Processor (HWP), and Microsoft Word Document Open XML (DOCX) formats were collected from the Korean government's open data platform [15]. The dataset covered a wide range of topics, including research reports, operational manuals, policy papers, recipes, and public health guidelines, ensuring diversity and avoiding concentration on a single subject domain.

Title–content correlation modeling

When analyzing the correlation between file names and their content, treating the entire body as a single analysis unit has inherent limitations. General lexical items within the body that are not directly related to the title's key terms act as noise, thereby distorting the measured association. To mitigate this, the body text is partitioned into semantically coherent chunks, and the chunk exhibiting the highest similarity to the title is designated as the optimal chunk.

For instance, in a document from our dataset titled “Cultivating Creativity in Korean Elementary School Class‐rooms (II): The Development of Measuring Indicators and Assessment Tools,” the selected optimal chunk corresponds to the abstract section beginning with “The purpose of this study is to develop and validate indicators and surveying tools….” In this case, our model showed a title–chunk cosine similarity of 0.85 For each optimal chunk, two complementary measurements are applied: (i) cosine similarity between the title and the optimal chunk to capture semantic similarity; and (ii) a coverage‐based metric that quantified the proportion of keywords extracted from the title and appearing in the optimal chunk, verifying lexical correspondence.

Statistical testing and evaluation

To evaluate the modeling procedure described in Section 3.2.1, original title–content pairs are compared against randomized pairs generated through derangements, in which each title is reassigned to a different document without replacement. This randomization process is repeated 100 times to obtain a stable baseline for comparison.

For the keyword‐coverage analysis, coverage is computed per document as the fraction of its top‐k title keywords that appear in the document's optimal chunk. Mean coverage across all I=426 documents is calculated for each k (k=1,…,10). The same derangement procedure is applied to obtain random baselines, and results are visualized with error bars.

To assess the statistical reliability of the results, the following measures are employed:

The following section discusses the proposed LLM‐based keyword augmentation methodology in detail.

LLM‐BASED KEYWORD AUGMENTATION

This section addresses RQ2, which explicitly asks: Can meaningful document selection be achieved using only LLM‐provided keywords? It also addresses RQ3, which explicitly asks: Does the proposed keyword‐augmentation method assist practical forensic investigations? To answer these questions, we design and evaluate an augmentation method that leverages LLMs to generate additional candidate keywords while preserving forensic admissibility and efficiency.

To systematically evaluate these assumptions, we design a two‐stage assessment: (i) a quantitative benchmark test using a constructed dataset to answer RQ2, and (ii) an expert usability evaluation with professional investigators to answer RQ3.

Construction of experimental dataset

To enhance the realism of the experiment, we developed a hypothetical case scenario in which an unauthorized actor infiltrated the Korean Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport (MOLIT) and exfiltrated 500 real‐estate policy documents. Based on this scenario, we constructed a ground‐truth dataset consisting of 500 file names. Among these, 200 titles were taken from publicly available Korean government documents, while the remaining 300 were newly authored by the researchers to simulate sensitive internal documents—such as draft reports and confidential briefings—that are not publicly available but are critical targets in exfiltration scenarios. These documents were drafted with reference to actual policy‐making procedures and statutes [18]. The plausibility and validity of the generated titles were verified by a current government officer with more than 10 years of central ministry experience, including extensive practice in reporting to policy decision‐makers.

To reflect the noise present in real‐world forensic environments, we also generated a control group of 1000 additional titles. We established a 2:1 ratio of irrelevant to relevant files as the minimum requirement for experimental reliability. These titles were designed to follow common office naming conventions, incorporating elements such as numerical prefixes and approval terminology. The control set was intentionally constructed to exhibit superficial lexical similarities to the ground‐truth dataset, while remaining semantically irrelevant, thereby simulating realistic investigative conditions.

Stage 1: Benchmark evaluation

Evaluation methods

The benchmark test directly addresses RQ2. The evaluation proceeded as follows. A standardized prompt was used to assign each model the role of a digital forensic investigator. Under identical conditions—defined by a domain context related to MOLIT, including real estate and transportation topics with 30 seed keywords—five OpenAI models (GPT‐5, GPT‐4.1, GPT‐4o‐mini, GPT‐o3, and GPT‐o4‐mini) were tested. Each evaluation was repeated 10 times to improve reliability.

Each model generated a list of 30 keywords in JSON format. These keywords were combined into a single search query using OR operators. The retrieval results were evaluated using an exact string matching procedure, yielding counts of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN). Although this exact‐matching criterion is conservative and may underestimate recall, it was uniformly applied across all models to enable consistent relative comparisons.

The F1‐score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, provided a comprehensive measure of overall retrieval performance.

Stage 2: Expert usability evaluation

Evaluation methods

To address RQ3, we conducted a usability study with 20 digital forensic investigators. Participants were grouped by experience: senior (≥5 years, n = 5), intermediate (3–5 years, n = 6), and junior (<3 years, n = 9). Each investigator performed two investigative tasks under time constraints: a Baseline condition, where only investigator‐provided keywords were used, and a “Proposed condition,” where these keywords were augmented with LLM‐generated recommendations. Each task was limited to 10 min.

A custom web‐based tool was developed to support the usability evaluation (see Figure 3). The tool continuously recorded all participant actions in real time and was designed with three functional modules:

Interface of the web‐based tool used in the expert usability evaluation (keyword input and augmentation then search and selection).

Keyword input and augmentation: When participants entered their initial keywords the LLM suggested up to N additional candidate terms (maximum of 30). Participants could then decide to include or exclude each recommendation before proceeding.

Search and selection: The entered and augmented keywords were used to search file names. From the resulting list, participants identified and marked items they judged to be evidence.

Control and logging: The system included a timer and automatically logged user actions, including keyword additions or deletions, search execution timestamps, and evidence selection or saving events.

For the LLM component, ChatGPT‐4.1 was selected because it produced the highest keyword quality in the benchmark evaluation. It was configured with parameters of temperature = 0.3 and presence_penalty = 0.8 to enable direct comparison with benchmark results.

Quantitative effect of LLM assistance

As shown in parts A and B of Table 5, the average number of input keywords increased from 17.35 to 31.16, a gain of approximately 79%. Keyword quality, as measured by the F1‐score, improved from 0.56 to 0.68, representing a 21% increase. Consequently, the number of retrieved documents increased from 204.75 to 232.63, confirming that the keyword augmentation function of the LLM effectively broadened the search scope. Evidence detection performance also improved: the number of documents selected as evidence (cumulative selection) increased by 116.05 on average, of which 93.7 were additional true positives. Because cumulative selection represents the evidence actually collected within the 10‐min time frame, it serves as a strong indicator of practical investigative output.

Performance by experience level

As shown in part C of Table 5, all groups exhibited performance improvements, and the effect of reducing gaps across experience levels was evident. In the Baseline condition, recall differed clearly by experience group: seniors achieved 0.54, intermediates 0.43, and juniors 0.35. However, under the augmented condition, recall for both the senior and intermediate groups converged to 0.66, eliminating group boundaries, while the junior group improved to 0.54. The improvement for the junior group was statistically significant, as confirmed by the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test (W=1.0000, p=0.0078). This result demonstrates that LLM‐based keyword augmentation provides substantial assistance to less experienced investigators and, as a consequence, helps equalize performance levels across groups.

DISCUSSION

Practical implications

Target identification in distributed environments

The shift toward remote work and Bring‐Your‐Own‐Device (BYOD) policies has dispersed digital evidence across geographically distributed endpoints, making traditional “seize‐everything” approaches inefficient. To address these challenges, this study proposes a practical workflow that combines a file list–based rapid triage strategy with the proposed LLM‐augmented keyword approach (Figure 4). Instead of traveling to every potential location, investigators can remotely collect and aggregate lightweight file‐system metadata—such as Windows Master File Table (MFT) entries (or file lists on Linux and macOS)—and integrate filenames and paths into a centralized index on the investigator's workstation. Investigators then apply LLM‐augmented keywords to this index to quickly assess the potential relevance of each endpoint, filter out low‐priority devices, and focus resources on locations where high‐value evidence is most likely concentrated. This workflow reduces delays and operational burden while supporting timely preservation of critical evidence in geographically distributed investigations.

Workflow for remote triage and targeted identification using LLM‐augmented keywords.

Evidence investigation in large‐scale environments

The practical utility of our approach extends to forensic laboratory environments, where in‐depth analysis of acquired data is conducted. Identifying relevant evidence within tens of thousands of files extracted from computers or mobile devices remains a significant operational challenge, particularly when investigators lack comprehensive background knowledge of the specific case. In this context, semantically relevant keywords generated by LLMs serve not merely as simple filters but as powerful navigational tools for exploration. Specifically, these keywords facilitate the identification of naming patterns and the mapping of potential evidence distribution across massive datasets. This approach enables investigators to rapidly pinpoint suspects or devices where relevant documents are concentrated and to recognize decisive keywords used in actual filenames, thereby significantly reducing the time required to uncover critical evidence.

Limitations

This section discusses the limitations of the present study and outlines directions for future research to enhance its practical applicability.

Validation in real‐world investigation

Although the dataset was designed to emulate a plausible government data‐exfiltration scenario, the study was conducted in a controlled setting and has not yet been tested using real case corpora from real‐life investigations handled end‐to‐end by examiners. Document distributions, naming practices, and operational constraints in real cases (e.g., mixed personal/corporate artifacts, incomplete context, time pressure, and evidentiary workflows) may differ from our experimental setup. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as a controlled evaluation of LLM‐assisted keyword generation rather than a definitive claim of effectiveness in all real‐life investigations. Future work should validate the approach with real examiners using case‐representative datasets and field‐like procedures.

Language scope

The dataset, prompts, and evaluation procedures were developed and administered exclusively in Korean for Korean forensic investigators. As a result, the performance of keyword‐based retrieval in multilingual or cross‐lingual contexts (e.g., Korean–English, Korean–Japanese, Korean–Chinese) was not directly assessed. Cross‐linguistic differences in morphology, spacing conventions, and orthographic variation (e.g., loanwords, Romanization, transliteration, abbreviations, and code‐switching) may significantly influence both the recall–precision balance under literal string matching and the keyword distributions proposed by LLMs. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within a monolingual Korean context, and further validation is necessary before generalization to multilingual settings.

Measurement protocol

We observed decreased recall for certain reasoning‐oriented models (e.g., GPT‐5). This outcome is largely attributable to the evaluation protocol, which prioritized efficiency and relied on strict exact string matching without stemming or fuzzy matching. As a result, abstract expressions, synonyms, and compound terms generated by the models contributed to an increase in false negatives. Prior work has also reported that reasoning‐oriented models may underperform on comparatively simple tasks [19]. Thus, the observed degradation should be understood as an interaction between evaluation criteria and model characteristics, rather than as an inherent model limitation. Future research should complement exact matching with lemmatization, fuzzy matching, and embedding‐based retrieval to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Benchmark–field discrepancy

For GPT‐4.1, performance declined from the benchmark evaluation (F1‐score 0.80) to the expert usability study (F1‐score 0.68). Uncontrolled factors in operational settings—such as investigator fatigue, heterogeneous search strategies, and time constraints—likely contributed to this gap. Additionally, participants were not provided with formal training on the custom‐built web application used in the experiment. This lack of familiarization may have skewed results, particularly among senior examiners who are used to established more traditional tools (e.g., Magnet AXIOM or EnCase). The cognitive load required to adapt to a novel interface likely influenced search efficiency, independent of the keyword quality itself. These findings highlight the need for user interface enhancements and for prompt templates specifically tuned to practitioner workflows.

Trade‐offs between general‐purpose and domain‐specialized models

The adoption of general‐purpose LLMs involves trade‐offs. While fine‐tuning can improve detection within specific domains, it introduces sensitivity to training data and the risk of catastrophic forgetting. In many investigative scenarios, the broader knowledge base of a general‐purpose LLM may provide greater practical value, even if task‐specific performance is somewhat lower. For low‐level analyses (e.g., ambiguous file system forensics or recovery of CCTV data requiring codec‐level interpretation), domain‐specialized, fine‐tuned LLMs are likely to be more effective. In this regard, hybrid approaches—such as lightweight adapters or retrieval‐augmented generation using domain‐specific corpora—represent a promising direction for balancing adaptability with forensic precision.

CONCLUSION

This study proposed a methodology that leverages the knowledge of LLMs to expand investigator‐provided keywords and enable rapid, title‐based document triage. To validate the approach, 426 public‐sector documents were analyzed to confirm both semantic and lexical associations between titles and content. Building on this finding, a hypothetical “MOLIT real‐estate policy document exfiltration” scenario was constructed, and a benchmark dataset of 1500 documents (500 ground‐truth, 1000 control) was assembled to evaluate the keyword‐generation performance of multiple LLMs.

For experimental evaluation, an LLM‐based keyword‐augmentation tool was developed using OpenAI GPT‐4.1, the best‐performing model in the benchmark. A usability study involving 20 digital forensic investigators, stratified by experience level, was conducted with the benchmark dataset. The results showed that LLM‐augmented keywords substantially expanded initial search results, enabled the identification of more evidentiary documents, and provided particularly meaningful improvements for less‐experienced investigators.

As practical applications, the study proposed a lightweight triage workflow that analyzes minimal metadata, such as the Windows MFT or file lists, to rapidly identify locations warranting on‐site investigation in distributed environments. Furthermore, we demonstrated that this approach extends to forensic laboratory settings, where generated keywords serve as navigational tools to identify naming patterns within massive datasets. These applications allow investigative resources to be concentrated where they are most needed and support efficient evidence discovery in both field and lab contexts.

Future work will focus on implementing an integrated framework to ensure legal admissibility. Although the correlation analyses provided scientific validation, they do not by themselves guarantee procedural legitimacy. To address this gap, we plan to develop an end‐to‐end framework in which investigators can select an LLM appropriate to the situation, and all elements of the process—prompts, models, and parameters—are automatically logged and exported in report form. This approach is expected to improve both courtroom admissibility and reproducibility.

Another priority is the development of crime‐specific, tailored prompts to enhance retrieval performance. Through in‐depth interviews with specialized investigators, prompts optimized for distinct crime types will be designed. For domains such as narcotics investigations, where slang usage is pervasive, “slang prompts” informed by investigative expertise will be constructed to uncover emergent terms associated with known slang, thereby broadening both the scope and accuracy of retrieval.
