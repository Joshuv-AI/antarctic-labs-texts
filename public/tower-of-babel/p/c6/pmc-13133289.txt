Causal effects of drug exposure on gastric cancer risk: a Mendelian randomization study involving 23 commonly used medications in the European population
Authors: Ganlu Zhang, Jindan Xia, Yi Zhou, Jin Qian, Dashan Yin, Wangxia Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have indicated potential associations between some drugs and cancer risk, but the causal relationship with gastric cancer remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the causality between 23 medications and gastric cancer using Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MethodsA two-sample MR analysis was conducted to assess the causal effects of medications on gastric cancer risk. Primary causal estimates were derived using the inverse variance weighted method, complemented by weighted median, MR-Egger, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. Sensitivity analyses (MR-PRESSO, MR-Egger regression, Cochran’s Q test, leave-one-out analysis, Steiger filtering) validated the robustness of significant findings. Furthermore, enrichment analysis was used to analyze the biological functions of medication-related genes.ResultsAfter Bonferroni correction, anti-migraine formulations (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.70–0.98]; p = 0.0253), drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.73–0.94]; p = 0.0035), and adrenergic drugs/inhalants (OR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.76–0.98]; p = 0.0212) were found to be suggestively associated with a reduced risk of gastric cancer in European populations. Sensitivity analyses revealed no significant heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy (all p > 0.05), and the Steiger filtering excluded reverse causality-related bias, collectively supporting the robustness of the primary findings. No significant causal relationship was found between the remaining 20 medications and gastric cancer risk. The causal association between 23 medications and gastric cancer differed between East Asian and European populations, indicating that the applicability of the causalities to non-European populations remains limited.ConclusionThis study revealed negative genetically proxied associations related to drug targets of anti-migraine formulations, drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system, and adrenergic drugs/inhalants with gastric cancer risk. These hypothesis-generating findings may help inform future mechanistic and clinical investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04672-3.

Introduction

Gastric cancer ranks among the leading malignancies globally and poses a significant public health burden due to its high morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Clinically, Helicobacter pylori eradication, dietary regulation, and endoscopic screening have reduced gastric cancer risk to a certain extent [3–5]. However, the intricate etiology of gastric cancer still poses challenges for its prevention and treatment. Particularly, the unavoidable use of medications for comorbid conditions may inadvertently modify cancer risk [6–8]. Epidemiological studies showed that some clinically used drugs, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acid suppressants, statins, and antidiabetic drugs, are associated with the risk of gastric cancer [9–12]. Concurrently, mechanistic studies have provided biological plausibility. For example, Huang et al. found that famotidine (an acid suppressant) triggered pyroptosis in gastric cancer cells, releasing inflammatory factors that exacerbated local inflammation and promoted gastric cancer progression [13]. Metformin, an antidiabetic drug, reduced the proliferation of gastric cancer cells by regulating the cell cycle and inhibiting the expression of cell proliferation-related proteins [14]. Although these observations provide potential avenues for drug repurposing, conventional observational studies are inherently limited by confounders and reverse causality [15]. Specific manifestations include the confounding by indication, where the disease being treated is a cancer risk factor, thereby confusing the drug’s effect [16]. Reverse causality may arise when undiagnosed early-stage tumors affect the patient’s medication behavior, leading to a misjudgment of causality [17]. This underscores the need for more robust causal inference approaches to accurately delineate drug effects on gastric cancer risk.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis, a causal inference method using genetic variation as instrumental variables (IVs), demonstrates unique advantages in exploring the relationship between factors and diseases [18]. Genetic variation is randomly assigned at conception and remains stable throughout life, making it largely unaffected by environmental influences and disease. Thus, MR analysis effectively avoids reverse causality and reduces confounders, thereby providing more reliable causal evidence [18, 19]. In recent years, MR analysis has been extensively applied in exploring the causal relationship between drugs and cancer risk [20–23]. Yun et al. revealed that drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system could reduce the risk of prostate cancer [20]. Min et al. explored the role of lipid-lowering drugs in cancer prevention, identifying that certain statins were associated with reduced risks of colorectal cancer and cholangiocarcinoma [21]. In addition, studies have shown the causal relationship between antihypertensive/antithrombotic drugs and the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [22, 23]. Although these MR Studies have identified the risk-reducing effects of certain drugs in multiple cancer types, systematic MR Evidence for gastric cancer remains limited. Given the preliminary associations between certain drugs and gastric cancer risk suggested by observational studies, there is an urgent need to employ MR methods for rigorous causal inference to obtain more reliable genetic evidence.

In this study, we conducted a two-sample MR analysis to investigate the relationship between 23 medications and gastric cancer risk. These drugs were involved in a variety of diseases, including cardiovascular, digestive, inflammatory, metabolic, neurological, and ocular conditions, as well as skeletal disorders and pain management. Among them, 19 medications have been explored by Yun et al. for causal associations with the risk of four common cancers (breast, lung, colorectal, and prostate) [20]. This study aimed to identify drug types linked to increased or decreased gastric cancer risk by quantifying genetically predicted drug exposure effects, providing genetic evidence to guide clinical studies. This also highlights drug classes with potential long-term safety signals relevant to cancer risk, thereby further enhancing pharmacovigilance.

Materials and methods

Study design

A crucial prerequisite for this MR analysis was the fulfillment of three assumptions: (1) relevance: IVs are strongly associated with the exposure; (2) independence: IVs are independent of any confounders and affect the outcome only through the exposure; (3) exclusion restriction: IVs are not directly related to the outcome but can only influence the outcome through exposure [24].

Data source

The exposure variables were derived from the genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data of 23 medications reported by Wu et al. (including 1,752 individual medicines) [25]. This GWAS analyzed the self-reported drug use of approximately 320,000 participants from the UK Biobank database. All medications were defined according to the World Health Organization Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system. These GWAS data can be downloaded from the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). Furthermore, GWAS data on gastric cancer from FinnGen_R12 (https://Finngen.gitbook.io/documentation/) were used as the outcome variables, including 2,296 cases and 378,749 controls. These summary-level data were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry, which helps to reduce biases from population stratification. Furthermore, there was no known sample overlap. Additionally, we obtained the GWAS data on 23 medications and gastric cancer in East Asian populations to assess the generalizability of the MR results. These datasets, including GWAS IDs and other relevant information, were shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Selection of IVs

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with exposure variables were screened as IVs based on the GWAS data, with p < 5.0 × 10− 8 as the threshold. To ensure obtaining sufficient SNPs as IVs for MR estimation, we relaxed the p-value threshold to p < 5 × 10− 6 for opioids and antidepressants with fewer than 5 IVs, consistent with IV screening thresholds in a previous MR study [26]. SNPs with linkage disequilibrium (LD) were excluded to ensure the independence of IVs, with a correlation coefficient R2 <0.001 and a genetic distance = 10,000 kb. Data harmonisation between exposure and outcome summary statistics was performed using the harmonise_data function to align effect alleles. Non-palindromic SNPs were aligned by flipping the effect allele and beta sign when required. For palindromic SNPs (A/T or C/G), we used effect allele frequency (EAF) to resolve strand ambiguity. Palindromic SNPs with ambiguous frequencies (EAF between 0.42 and 0.58) were excluded, whereas those with clearly distinguishable EAF (< 0.42 or > 0.58) were aligned by inferring strand orientation using allele frequency information from the 1000 Genomes reference panel. SNPs that remained inconsistent or were unavailable in the outcome dataset were removed. To satisfy the independence assumption, we queried all candidate SNPs in the LDlink database and excluded variants that showed significant associations with Helicobacter pylori. The F-statistic was calculated for each IV to assess the relevance assumption and to ensure that “weak instruments” with F < 10 were excluded [27, 28]. The formula is R2 = 2×MAF×(1-MAF) ×β2, F = R2(N − 2)/(1 − R2), where R2 is the degree of exposure explained by each IV, MAF is the minor allele frequency, β is the allele effect sizes, and N is the sample size [27].

Statistical analysis

This study used various methods to infer the causal relationship between the 23 medications and gastric cancer, involving inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median, MR-Egger, simple model, and weighted model. Among them, IVW served as the primary method for MR analysis. IVW method combined Wald ratio estimates for different SNPs to assess the effect of 23 medications on gastric cancer risk and showed the most reliable results in the absence of horizontal pleiotropy [29, 30]. The weighted median method provided consistent estimates when at least half of the SNPs were valid IVs [31], and demonstrated higher accuracy in the results compared to MR-Egger [32]. Simple mode and weighted mode were performed as supplementary analyses [33].

In addition, sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the exclusion restriction assumption using various methods. MR-Egger regression was utilized to assess whether the IVs exhibited horizontal pleiotropy, with p > 0.05 and the intercept close to zero indicating no pleiotropy [34]. MR-pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) test identified and corrected for horizontal pleiotropy by excluding outliers [35]. Cochran’s Q test for IVW and MR-Egger was used to evaluate the heterogeneity of the effects, with p > 0.05 indicating no significant heterogeneity [36]. The leave-one-out method was performed to eliminate individual SNPs one by one, thereby assessing their impact and robustness on overall results [37]. The Steiger filtering was performed to ensure that the selected SNPs had a stronger correlation with the exposure than with the outcome (R2 exposure > R2 outcome, p < 0.05). When this condition was met, the SNP’s effect direction was categorized as “TRUE”, indicating a lower likelihood of reverse causality in our MR analysis [38].

Bonferroni correction was used to identify false positives arising from multiple testing. P < 0.002 [0.05/ (23 exposures × 1 outcomes)] was considered statistically significant, while 0.002 < p < 0.05 was regarded as suggestive evidence of a potential association. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.0).

Enrichment analysis

To comprehensively explore the potential impact of medications on the occurrence of gastric cancer, we conducted enrichment analysis based on the SNPs of the selected three medications. The GWAS4D website (http://www.mulinlab.org/varnote/index.html) was used to map the medications’ SNPs to neighbouring genes [39]. The web server could integrate tissue-specific epigenomic annotations, chromatin interaction (Hi-C) data, transcription factor binding motifs, and non-coding regulatory annotations to identify the most likely target genes for each index SNP. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the DIVAD database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [40, 41]. Enrichment P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Results

Selection of IVs

Two-sample MR analysis was performed to investigate the potential causal association between 23 medications and gastric cancer. During IV selection, LD clumping removed a median of 98.4% of candidate SNPs (range: 90.5%-99.9%). For 21 exposures with genome-wide significant instruments (p < 1 × 10− 8), we identified a median of 20 SNPs per exposure (range: 5-139). The mean F-statistic across all selected instruments was 808.1 (standard error, 363.6), indicating strong instrument strength. For opioids and antidepressants (p < 1 × 10− 6), 37 SNPs (mean F-statistic 104.84) and 24 SNPs (mean F-statistic 412.62) were obtained, respectively (Supplemental Table 2).

The causal effect of 23 commonly used medications on gastric cancer risk

The results of the MR analysis supported a causal relationship between three medications and the risk of gastric cancer (Fig. 1A-B, Supplemental Table 2). In detail, IVW showed that anti-migraine formulations (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.70–0.98]; p = 0.0253), drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.73–0.94]; p = 0.0035), and adrenergic drugs/inhalants (OR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.76–0.98]; p = 0.0212) significantly reduced the risk of gastric cancer. Bonferroni showed that these causal relationships were suggestive. The causal estimates for the renin-angiotensin system drugs (OR range: 0.83–0.94) and adrenergic drugs/inhalants (OR range: 0.79–0.93) showed consistent direction and magnitude with the IVW method across all four complementary MR methods (Supplemental Table 2). For anti-migraine formulations, three MR methods showed results consistent with the IVW estimate (OR range: 0.90–0.91), except for MR-Egger (OR = 2.21, 95% CI: 0.82–5.94, p > 0.05), which yielded a non-significant estimate in the opposite direction. However, the IVW method provided more accurate causal estimates than MR-Egger [42]. Furthermore, no causality was observed between the other 20 medications and gastric cancer.

Sensitivity analysis

The MR-Egger intercept test showed that there was no horizontal pleiotropy in the IVs of drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system (intercept = -0.001, 95% CI: -0.017 to 0.015, p = 0.868), and adrenergic drugs/inhalants (intercept = 0.008, 95% CI: -0.022 to 0.038, p = 0.588) (Supplemental Table 3). For anti-migraine formulations, the intercept was slightly negative and its confidence interval narrowly excluded zero (intercept = -0.145, 95% CI: -0.289 to -0.0006), suggesting a potential tendency toward downward bias; however, the p-value was non-significant (p = 0.097), indicating that any such bias is unlikely to materially influence the causal estimate. MR-PRESSO analysis detected no significant outliers (global test p > 0.05 for all 23 exposures), further revealing no significant evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Supplemental Table 3). Cochran’s Q tests generally indicated no evidence of heterogeneity (p > 0.05). However, some exposures (e.g., calcium channel blockers, Q_p = 0.073) showed borderline heterogeneity, suggesting the possibility of residual variability (Supplemental Table 3). The Steiger filtering results for the three identified medications indicated that the direction of all SNPs was “TRUE” (R2 exposure/R2 outcome ratio, range: 96.20-132.29), and the causal relationship was not disturbed by reverse causal effect (p < 0.05) (Supplemental Table 4). In addition, the leave-one-out analysis showed no significant change in the causal effect estimates after excluding individual IVs, indicating robust results (Fig. 2).

Enrichment analysis

An exploratory GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted on 23 medications to identify potential biological patterns (Supplemental Table 5). The results revealed both shared and distinct enrichment profiles among the medications. For instance, opioid analgesics exhibited unique enrichment in neurodevelopmental and synaptic pathways. Lipid and cholesterol metabolism processes were enriched in antithrombotic agents and HMG CoA reductase inhibitors. Immune- and inflammation-related pathways were commonly observed in thyroid preparations, immunosuppressants, glucocorticoids, and adrenergic drugs/inhalants. These shared pathways may influence drug–disease interactions.

Subsequently, we focused on three medications associated with genetic susceptibility to gastric cancer. The results revealed that the genes related to anti-migraine formulations were enriched in the cellular component (CC) of the “Golgi apparatus” and the biological process (BP) of “cellular response to amyloid-beta”, with no enrichment observed in molecular function (MF) or KEGG pathways. For drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system, the main CCs involved cytoplasm and the nucleus, the main BPs involved metabolism, substance transport, and cell physiology and angiogenesis-related processes, and the main MFs involved ion channel functions, various molecular binding characteristics, and enzyme activities. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in several classical signaling pathways and tumor-related pathways, such as “MAPK, Calcium, cGMP-PKG, Rap1, and Apelin signaling pathways”, as well as “Pathways in cancer” (Fig. 3A). The genes related to adrenergic drugs/inhalants were mainly enriched in the CC of “plasma membrane”, and participated in inflammatory immune-related BPs (such as “immune response” and “cytokine-mediated signaling pathway”) and transcription regulation-related MFs. The main KEGG pathways were related to immunity and signal transduction, such as “Th17 cell differentiation”, “PI3K − Akt signaling pathway”, “JAK−STAT signaling pathway”, and “Pathways in cancer” (Fig. 3B). GWAS traits associated with the SNPs based on LDlink analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 6.

External validation based on East Asian populations

In the East Asian population, we did not observe significant casual associations between drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system, adrenergic drugs/inhalants, and anti-migraine formulations and the risk of gastric cancer (Fig. 4). In contrast, antithrombotic agents (suggestive, p = 0.006), drugs used in diabetes (significant, p < 0.001), and calcium channel blockers (significant, p < 0.001) were associated with a reduced risk of gastric cancer. No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected (Supplemental Table 7). Although heterogeneity was present, MR-PRESSO indicated that the removal of outliers did not alter these causal estimates. In addition, thyroid preparations showed a suggestive association with an increased risk of gastric cancer (p = 0.004). Leave-one-out analyses further confirmed that these findings were not driven by any single SNP (Supplemental Fig. 1). Overall, these results suggested that the genetic associations between the 23 medications and gastric cancer susceptibility may differ across populations.

Discussion

At present, the potential impact of clinical medication on tumor risk has been extensively studied [9, 17]. Exploring the causal relationship between drugs and tumor risk through MR analysis provides robust evidence and novel insights for assessing drug safety, promoting drug repositioning, and guiding precision prevention [20, 43, 44]. Our study explored the causality between 23 commonly used medications and gastric cancer risk using MR analysis. Among them, anti-migraine formulations, drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system, and adrenergic drugs/inhalants were suggestively associated with gastric cancer risk reduction. There was no significant association between the other 20 medications and gastric cancer. These results generate hypotheses for clinical studies and provide a theoretical basis for understanding the potential biological links between these medications and gastric cancer.

Anti-migraine formulations, such as triptans, exert their effects by activating serotonin receptors [45, 46]. These receptors were also distributed in the stomach [47] and might reduce the risk of gastric cancer by regulating the neurological function [48] and mucosal integrity [49] of the stomach. Ergot alkaloids, including ergotamine and dihydroergotamine, were another commonly used migraine treatment drug and had been reported to have antitumor effects [50–52]. A study found that dihydroergotamine exerted anti-tumor proliferation effects by inhibiting STAT3 activation in liver cancer [51]. Ergotamine targeted and inhibited KIF5A to promote cancer cell anoikis in lung adenocarcinoma [52]. Although there was no relevant report on the application of ergot alkaloids in gastric cancer, some studies found that they could activate the D-tryptamine receptors in the stomach [53] and promote gastric secretion [54], suggesting their potential application in gastric cancer treatment. In the present study, we found a possible causal relationship between anti-migraine formulations and gastric cancer risk reduction, providing new evidence for their potential protective effects. However, these potential mechanistic pathways remain exploratory hypotheses and should be further investigated.

Drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system demonstrated potential in reducing the risk of gastric cancer in our study. This finding might be related to the regulatory effect of these drugs on angiotensin II (Ang II), a key factor in both blood pressure regulation and tumorigenesis [55]. This link is particularly relevant in gastric cancer, as H. pylori infection upregulates angiotensin receptor expression in the gastric mucosa [56], potentially creating a niche for Ang II-driven oncogenesis [57]. Indeed, studies have confirmed that angiotensin II directly participates in tumor growth and metastasis by promoting gastric cancer cell proliferation, migration, and tumor angiogenesis [58–60]. Consistently, our medication-related gene enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in angiogenesis-related biological processes and tumor-related pathways. This is further consistent with the potential role of targeting the renin-angiotensin system in the prevention of gastric cancer from a genetic perspective. However, although these enrichment findings provide a supportive biological context, they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence of specific biological mechanisms. In addition, the renin-angiotensin system plays an essential role in the immune regulation of the tumor microenvironment [61]. Some inhibitors and antagonists significantly inhibited tumor growth by inducing neutrophil polarization towards an anti-tumor phenotype [62], offering another plausible pathway.

Adrenergic drugs/inhalants, commonly used in treating respiratory diseases, also showed a trend of reducing gastric cancer risk in this study. Adrenergic receptors play critical roles in various physiological and pathological processes, including stress response, inflammation regulation, and cell proliferation [63, 64]. For example, β2-adrenergic activation promoted tumor autophagy to accelerate gastric cancer progression [63] and upregulated MUC4 expression to mediate gastric cancer resistance [65]. Interestingly, our enrichment analysis revealed that medication-related genes were significantly enriched in inflammatory immune-related biological processes and LDlink revealed associations with immune traits. These observations may not solely reflect pleiotropy but, more importantly, indicate crosstalk between drug targets and immune pathways, potentially contributing to a protective tumor microenvironment. Moreover, the potential role of adrenergic antagonists in other cancers, such as metastatic renal cell carcinoma [66], hepatocellular carcinoma [67], and oral cancer [68], has been preliminarily confirmed. This suggests that they may have broad anti-tumor effects. Future experimental studies are essential to validate these proposed mechanisms.

The borderline heterogeneity observed for calcium channel blockers should be interpreted with caution. While such residual heterogeneity may arise from multiple sources, including the existence of subtypes within the exposure phenotype, differences across medication user populations, or variation in specific drug targets and pharmacological mechanisms within a broader drug class, a borderline Cochran’s Q result alone does not invalidate the primary MR estimate. Rather, it suggested that the association should be interpreted in the context of overall consistency across MR methods, sensitivity analyses, and biological plausibility.

Although MR allows causal inference by using genetic variants as proxies for drug targets, this approach cannot capture key exposure characteristics such as prescribed dosage, treatment duration, or drug-drug interactions. Clinical trials and observational studies have consistently demonstrated that many drugs exhibit dose-dependent effects and responses tied to treatment duration [69, 70]. These factors may also influence the risk of adverse events [71]. Furthermore, the enrichment of shared biological pathways across certain medications in our study suggests the potential for drug-drug interactions, which may introduce additional complexities that are not captured by MR-based genetic proxies. Therefore, our MR findings should be interpreted as target-based rather than dosage-specific effects, and evidence from RCTs and observational studies remains essential to understand how treatment intensity modifies drug-disease associations.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the sample size and coverage of genetic variations may limit statistical efficacy, resulting in some drugs not being significantly associated with gastric cancer risk. Secondly, although the relaxed IV threshold (p < 5 × 10− 6) for opioids and antidepressants may theoretically increase false-positive risk, both exposures showed no evidence of causality with gastric cancer, so the impact is minimal. Future larger GWAS may enable stricter thresholds for validation. Thirdly, because no publicly available GWAS data exist for gastric cancer subtypes or stages, subtype-specific MR analyses could not be performed. Nonetheless, our study provides evidence for overall gastric cancer risk, and future work will incorporate subtype- or stage-specific data once available. Fourthly, MR analysis using medications as exposure variables may obscure the individual drug’s true causal effects due to the diversity of drug types and differences in their effect sizes. Fifth, our study may be susceptible to collider bias. Medication use is partly determined by underlying disease severity and comorbidities, which may be influenced by the same genetic variants used as IVs. Furthermore, the failure to replicate our findings in East Asian populations suggests this result may be restricted to European populations. This may be related to differences in genetic background, clinical medication patterns, and epidemiological characteristics of gastric cancer among different races. Future work will involve larger and more detailed MR analyses or cross-ethnic meta-analyses to validate these findings and explore potential pharmacological interventions for gastric cancer risk reduction via clinical trials.

Conclusion

In summary, this study conducted an MR analysis of 23 commonly used medications and gastric cancer risk, revealing suggestive associations between genetically predicted drug target effects of anti-migraine formulations, drugs acting on the renin-angiotensin system, and adrenergic drugs/inhalants and a reduced risk of gastric cancer. These findings represent hypothesis-generating genetic evidence related to drug targets and may provide insights for future mechanistic, experimental, and clinical investigations, rather than direct evidence supporting medication use in gastric cancer prevention.
