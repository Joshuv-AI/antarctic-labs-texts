The World Health Organization’s package of essential noncommunicable diseases in the West Bank: Health education, disease control, and bio-physical outcomes
Authors: Masarra M. Khasib, Mahmoud T. Al-Kalaldeh, Imad N. Thiltheen, Wesam T. Almagharbeh, Nashi Alreshidi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are prevalent worldwide and contribute to public health challenges. Health education, driven by World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) Package of Essential Non-Communicable Diseases (PEN), is a mainstay in managing these NCDs in primary healthcare settings in Palestine. This study evaluated the effect of the WHO’s PEN’s health education protocol on NCD patients’ bio-physical outcomes in the West Bank, Palestine.MATERIALS AND METHODS:Patients with NCDs receiving within PEN protocol at 49 primary healthcare clinics in the Ramallah district of the West Bank were identified and were selected into study consecutively at the time of their visit to the clinic. Patients’ perspectives towards management of their NCDs were retrieved from the WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS). Bio-physical measurements were taken at the time of the visit to clinic and compared to previous records. Data analysis performed using SPSS; mean and standard deviations computed for continuous variables, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Paired sample t-test used to test for statistical significance for changes between initial and last bio-physical or bio-chemical measurements.RESULTS:A total of 377 patients were included in the study. Self-evaluation showed positive feedback towards assessing, monitoring, and managing all assessed NCDs. Majority of participants reported rarely or never receiving health education on quitting smoking (85.6%), nutrition (95.5%), physical exercise (91.5%), and medication-related issues (84.9%). Biophysical parameters showed reductions in weight by 1.5 kg/m2, waist circumference by 2.2 cm, and systolic and diastolic BP by 6.5 and 4 mmHg, respectively. Biochemical value reductions were observed for Hemoglobin A1c by 0.6%, total cholesterol by 25mg/dl, triglyceride by 11.5mg/dl, and low-density lipoprotein by 25mg/dl over a year.CONCLUSION:Despite its significant bio-physical outcomes, the PEN health education protocol needs systematic planning and evaluation to attain the best outcomes.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, cancer, and respiratory conditions, pose a significant public health challenge worldwide and account for a substantial portion of morbidity and mortality rates. In Palestine, NCDs are the predominant cause of death, with hypertension and diabetes mellitus identified as the most common conditions.[1] The World Health Organization’s (WHO) Package of Essential Non-communicable (PEN) Disease Interventions offers a systematic framework for primary healthcare systems, particularly within low-resource environments, to mitigate the escalating burden of NCDs through prevention, early detection, and management strategies.[2] The PEN framework comprises two key components: Protocol 1, which emphasizes preventive measures, and Protocol 2, which aims at delivering comprehensive health education to foster behavioral modifications.[3]

Health education is integral to the management of chronic illnesses and equips individuals with the essential knowledge and competencies required to adopt healthier lifestyle choices. Research from various regions, including South Asia and Kyrgyzstan, has illustrated that the incorporation of health education into primary healthcare enhances adherence to lifestyle alterations, such as improved dietary practices and increased physical activity, both of which are vital for the management of NCDs.[45] Nevertheless, despite these encouraging findings, numerous challenges persist in the effective dissemination of health education. A recent comprehensive review on community-based health education revealed that notwithstanding the observed positive outcomes, consistent tailored delivery of educational interventions is frequently impeded by such systemic barriers as overcrowded healthcare environments, insufficient specialized personnel, and resource constraints.[6] Additionally, obstacles related to staff competency, heavy workload, and poor communication were recognized as significant hindrances to the efficacy of health education initiatives.[7]

This study sought to evaluate the effect of health education protocols, driven by PEN, within primary healthcare services in the West Bank. In this study, care delivery in participating primary healthcare clinics was guided by PEN, which standardizes risk assessment, lifestyle counseling, pharmacotherapy, and follow-up for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular risk management in resource-limited primary care settings. In parallel, outcome evaluation used instruments adapted from the WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk-factor surveillance (STEPS), a standardized platform for assessing behavioral, physical, and biochemical risk factors.[8] Accordingly, PEN defined the content and processes of care (the care model/exposure), whereas STEPS provided the structured measurement framework (the evaluation tool) for patients’ perspectives and bio-physiological indicators.

Focusing on patients with NCDs, the study assessed the effect of PEN on patients’ bio-physiological measurements, including changes in Body Mass Index (BMI) and waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting blood sugar (FBS), a hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C) test, and lipid profile over the last year.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional pre-post analysis study of outpatient clinics of different specialties was carried out between January 2024 and June 2024. The cross-sectional quantitative study was carried out in all 49 governmental primary healthcare clinics in Ramallah city, located in the central West Bank and the administrative capital of the State of Palestine. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board vide Letter No. R-2023/A/2/N dated 28/12/2023 and written informed consent was taken from all the participants in the study.

The study population consisted of patients diagnosed with chronic NCDs receiving care within the PEN protocol. Patients attending these clinics who were diagnosed with one or more of the NCDs, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic respiratory diseases, and chronic renal failure, were eligible to participate in the study. We used a convenience (non-probability) sampling approach. During the data-collection period, the research team worked with clinic registries and head nurses at the 49 participating primary health-care clinics to identify all patients who met the eligibility criteria. Eligible patients were then approached consecutively during routine clinic visits, when their eligibility was verified against medical records, and those who provided written informed consent were enrolled. Recruitment continued every clinic day throughout the study window until the target sample size was reached.

Based on the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics’ estimate of N = 20,000 NCD patients in the Ramallah governorate, we calculated the minimum required sample size using Thompson’s equation and Raosoft Sample Size Calculator, assuming a two-sided 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05), and a conservative response distribution of P = 0.50.[910] The final sample included 377 patients who completed the survey.

A patient was eligible for participation if he/she was diagnosed with one of the previous NCDs and had regular follow-ups for at least one year at one of the included clinics that implement the WHO’s PEN protocol. Patients were excluded if they were at a terminal stage of cancer (confirmed by the physician), psychiatric and mentally ill patients, physically handicapped patients, sensory deficit patients, dementia or cognitive deficit patients (identified by family and physicians). No limitations were applied to gender, age, or socio-economic status.

Patient’s assessment data is recorded in a clinical sheet at every follow-up visit. Patients with NCD are supposed to visit the clinic every three months. Register forms for NCDs are sent monthly from each primary health clinic to the Directorate a quarterly report to be prepared by the central NCDs health department of the Ministry.

The study data consisted of three key sections aimed at gathering quantitative data on patients’ perspectives and bio-physiological measurements associated with their NCDs. Demographic information such as age, gender, education level, marital status, residency, and employment was collected. The second section assessed participants’ perspectives toward measurement, diagnosis and management of blood pressure, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease. This part was retrieved from the WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS). The third section was based on bio-physiological measurements, obtained at the time of data collection and then compared with the previous patient’s records for at least one year prior. These included two types of measurements. The first was Blood Pressure (BP) measurements, and anthropometric measurements, including waist circumference, weight, and height, BMI. The second was biochemical analysis, which included cholesterol levels, triglycerides, Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL), High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL), Glycated Haemoglobin (HbA1c), FBS. These tests were performed using equipment and kits provided by the Al-balo’ Health Directorate Laboratories, to ensure consistency and standardization in data collection.

For each participant, we extracted two prespecified time points for analysis: Initial and Last. Initial was defined as the first recorded clinic measurement within ≤3 months before (or up to 2 weeks after) the index PEN education encounter during the study window. Last was defined as the most recent measurement within 12 ± 2 months of the initial value. When multiple readings existed within either window, we used the value closest to the window midpoint (blood pressure = average of two seated readings from the same visit when available).

Data collection was commenced after obtaining ethical approval from the IRB committee based in the Arab American University in Palestine and the Ministry of Health. Each prospective patient who agreed to participate in the study signed the designated informed consent. Eligible patients were found in each involved clinic with the assistance of the registry and head nurses, who facilitated identification and meeting with eligible patients at a convenient place on the clinic premises. Each potential patient was provided with a written invitation to participate in the study. This included information on the purpose of the study its objectives, their rights including confidentiality, anonymity and the right to refuse. Patients who signed the informed consent were requested to respond to the questionnaire with/without the assistance of the research team. Family members were allowed (if possible) to facilitate the patient’s responses.

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software, version 25.0 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY: USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and health-related characteristics of the study population. For continuous variables, the mean, standard deviation, and range were calculated, while for categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were reported. Inferential statistics were employed to determine the statistical significance of changes between initial and bio-physical or bio-chemical measurements using a paired sample t-test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 377 patients with various NCDs participated in the study. It was found that 59.4% of participants were female, 77.7%, were aged above 50 years, 81.4% were married (81.4%), and 76.6% had secondary education and above. Regarding the residency and employment status, nearly three-quarters lived in rural areas, and 53.6% were unemployed.

Three major categories of NCDs were noted overtly: 131 (34.7%) had Hypertension (HTN); 82 (21.8%) had Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (DM2); and 115 (30.5%) had both Hypertension and Diabetes mellitus (HTN + DM2). Regarding the time since NCD diagnosis, approximately three-quarters of the patients (74.8%) had been diagnosed more than 5 years previously [Table 1].

It was noted that 85.7% of patients claimed positive feedback regarding measuring BP by medical staff, being informed of elevated BP (81.4%), receiving information within the past 12 months (80.4%), and being treated with antihypertensive medication in the past few weeks (78.8%). However, only 10.3% of the participants had consulted traditional healers to manage BP, and only 8.2% used herbal or traditional medicines to manage BP.

Of the 377 participants, 54.4% claimed having their blood sugar measured by a medical professional. Almost half of the participants (51.2%) reported having been told of elevated blood sugar by a physician or other healthcare provider, and similarly, 52.5% had recent information about DM in the past 12 months. Regarding DM management, 47.2% of the participants claimed they had been given a medical prescription of diabetic medication by a physician in the previous few weeks, and only 17.5% of them used insulin as instructed by a healthcare provider or physician. Very few of the participants admitted using traditional healers for their diabetes (2.1%) or being treated with herbal or traditional medicines (2.7%) [Table 2].

Eighty-two percent of the participants said their cholesterol levels had been checked by the order of a physician or other health professional. Nearly half of the participants (47.2%) reported having elevated cholesterol according to a physician or other healthcare provider; of these 46.2% had received a diagnosis during the previous 12 months, suggesting that they had recently been made aware of the condition. Regarding dyslipidemia management, 41.6% of the participants had been treated using oral medication prescribed by a physician or other health professional. However, only 3.4% of the participants relied on traditional healers or used herbal or traditional medicines to manage elevated cholesterol (3.2%) [Table 2].

A total of 21.0% of the participants stated that they had suffered a stroke, angina, or a heart attack. Nevertheless, 81.2% of all participants reported using aspirin on a regular daily basis. Additionally, 50.1% of the participants declared they were taking statins daily [Table 2].

Despite previous positive surveillance and control towards the major NCDs and comorbidities, health advice concerning dietary, smoking, physical activity, and medication behaviors was not much emphasized by healthcare professionals as shown in Figure 1. The largest proportion of participants reported the lowest frequency of health education on quitting smoking, counseling on nutrition and physical exercise, and medication-related education (85.6%, 95.5%, 91.5%, and 84.9%, respectively). However, about 15.0% of the participants claimed they had medication-related counseling when needed.

The HgbA1c level and fasting blood glucose dropped between the first and last measurements (7.8% to 7.14%, 172 mg/dL to 131 mg/dL, respectively, P < 0.001). For elevated blood glucose, nearly half (48.0%) said they were using insulin or another prescription drug. Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of the PEN’s health education protocol for managing NCDs in the West Bank. After undergoing structured health education, patients reported positive changes that translated into meaningful biometric improvements. BMI and waist circumference reductions align with recent meta-analyses showing that health education has positive effects on metabolic risk factors, including Hba1c.[111213] Notably, studies from low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) also observed similar outcomes, with community-based interventions yielding reductions in body weight (approximately-2.3 kg), significant glucose lowering,[13] and a significant reduction in both systolic and diastolic BP.[1114]

Similarly, in high-income countries, structured lifestyle programs, such as the U. S. Diabetes Prevention Program, have successfully reduced the progression to diabetes by 58% through intensive lifestyle counseling.[15] Nevertheless, contradictions exist in resource-limited settings where some rigorous trials have found no benefit from brief education alone.[16171819] Indeed, our results recognized that while behaviors improved, many patients still did not receive consistent counseling related to diet, smoking, and physical activity. Likewise, in Gaza, only about 55% of patients reported getting advice on exercise, and <50% on diet.[20] These gaps in implementation limit the impact of health education, a trend that mirrors findings from other LMICs. In contrast, high-income countries often support patient education with robust infrastructure, such as multidisciplinary teams, electronic registries, and patient–provider communication tools, that yield greater effect.[21]

The positive outcomes of this study suggest several practical steps to enhance NCD management in low-resource settings like the West Bank. First, formalization of patient counseling as a routine component of primary care should be prioritized. Training and incentivizing primary care staff to consistently deliver lifestyle education is essential. Given that adherence to the WHO-PEN counseling protocol was only approximately 70%, further investment in provider training, both initial and ongoing, would likely improve this rate.[21]

Second, integrating community health workers (CHWs) and peer-support models could significantly extend the reach of health education programs. Our findings are consistent with other studies that have shown that CHW programs in refugee camps in the West Bank have improved diabetes and hypertension control.[22] Policies could be introduced to fund CHW training and link these workers to primary healthcare clinics, thereby leveraging their ability to provide culturally tailored education and follow-up.[2324]

Third, the use of standardized protocols and tools could ensure consistent delivery of counseling. Systematic reviews emphasize that standardized NCD management guidelines and patient registries are critical for improving health outcomes.[25] Adapting WHO-PEN materials and utilizing digital reminders may also help reinforce health education messages.[26]

Although the study provided inferences about PEN’s health education protocol and its contribution to effective NCDs management and control, the generalizability of the study findings could be affected by a relatively limited sample size. In addition, patients’ self-reported information would be more validated had it been verified by other sources of information, such as nursing reports or family observation.

This study leveraged a relatively large sample across 49 primary health-care clinics and used standardized instruments (PEN-guided care processes with STEPS-aligned measurements) to describe bio-physical changes in patients with non-communicable diseases. However, several limitations warrant caution. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the observed changes. Second, several outcomes relied on self-report and were not validated against objective clinical records, introducing potential recall and social-desirability bias. Third, we did not adjust for key confounders (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities, medication regimens, or socioeconomic status), which may influence both exposure to counseling and outcomes. Fourth, measurement time points and data elicitation were not fully synchronized across clinics, which could have created some inconsistencies between baseline and follow-up values. These constraints may bias effect estimates toward or away from the null. Future work employing prospective designs with standardized follow-up schedules, linkage to electronic clinical records, and multivariable adjustment (or propensity methods) is needed to estimate the effects of PEN-aligned health education more robustly in this setting.

Conclusion

This study highlighted the potential of health education to improve patient behaviors and health outcomes in low-resource settings like the West Bank. PEN’s health education protocol contributes to the effect of NCD management and control. The results suggested that structured health education could play a crucial role in NCD management by improving dietary habits, physical activity, and key biomarkers. However, the success of such programs depended on local adaptation, sufficient resources, and a supportive healthcare system. This requires consistent counseling on diet, smoking, and physical activity, which is still deficient. Ongoing monitoring and continuous adaptation were essential to ensure that health education remained effective and sustainable in the context of West Bank’s primary healthcare system.

The study recommended adopting a structured health education protocol that demonstrates a positive impact on patients’ bio-physical improvements. On policy and practice, health education should be efficiently integrated into the health system to include standardized protocols and tools for measuring outcomes. Bio-physical and bio-chemical parameters should be amplified into predesigned health education interventions with clear scheduling, materials, and follow-ups.
