Analysis of antimicrobial resistance and genetic diversity of Acinetobacter baumannii in a tertiary care hospital in Haikou City
Authors: Hai-Li Zhang, Mir Muhammad Nizamani, Yanjing Wang, Xiaoli Cui, Hao Xiu, Muhammad Qayyum, Qinghui Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the distribution and antimicrobial resistance of Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) in a medical facility in Haikou City, aiming to provide essential insights for enhancing in-hospital treatment and prevention strategies. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 513 A. baumannii isolates collected from a tertiary care hospital in Haikou between January 2018 and December 2020, focusing on their antimicrobial resistance patterns. Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD) analysis was performed on 48 randomly selected A. baumannii strains. Using Gel-pro4.0 and NTSYSspc2.10 software, we constructed dendrograms to assess the genetic diversity of these strains. Our results indicate that males between 60 and 70 years old are particularly vulnerable to A. baumannii infections, which are most frequently detected in sputum samples, with a detection rate exceeding 70%. Alarmingly, over 50% of the isolates were identified as multi-drug resistant. The RAPD-PCR fingerprinting cluster analysis demonstrated substantial genetic diversity among the strains. Using primer OPA-02 at a 45% similarity coefficient, the strains were categorized into four groups (A-D), with group A being predominant (39 strains). high-prevalence areas like the Neurosurgery and Intensive Care Medicine Wards require enhanced surveillance and targeted interventions to manage Group C infections effectively. Additionally, the varied presence of other groups necessitates customized strategies to address the specific risks in each ward. Similarly, primer 270 at a 52% similarity coefficient classified the strains into five groups (E-I), with group E being most common (36 strains). The study highlights a concerning prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, particularly multi-drug resistance, among A. baumannii strains in the Haikou hospital. The significant genetic diversity, especially within groups A and E, underscores the need for tailored hospital treatment protocols and prevention measures. Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii are strictly aerobic, non-motile, Gram-negative bacteria, known for exhibiting catalase-positive and oxidase-negative reactions1. These conditionally pathogenic bacteria are frequently encountered in clinical settings2, posing heightened risks particularly to immunocompromised patients, including the elderly, infants and children, post-operative ICU patients, and those requiring respiratory endoscopy or long-term ventilator use due to respiratory diseases3,4. A. baumannii is capable of causing a wide range of infections such as ventilator-associated pneumonia, urinary tract infections, wound infections, bacteremia, peritonitis, and meningitis1. Despite extensive studies on its resistance mechanisms, significant gaps remain in understanding the distribution patterns and resistance profiles of A. baumannii across different hospital settings. Tertiary care hospitals, which handle complex and critical cases, are particularly important venues for such studies due to their high usage of broad-spectrum antibiotics and their diverse patient population. Additionally, addressing localized variations in resistance mechanisms and infection control challenges is crucial. This study aims to fill this gap by offering detailed insights into the distribution and antimicrobial resistance patterns of A. baumannii in Haikou City, thus providing valuable data to enhance local infection control and treatment strategies.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) in bacteria is characterized by resistance to three or more antimicrobial agents5. Extensively drug-resistant (XDR) pathogens exhibit resistance to all but colistin and tigecycline, while pan-drug-resistant bacteria (PDR) are resistant to all antibiotics, including colistin and tigecycline6. The overuse of antibiotics has contributed to the increase in MDR, XDR, and PDR A. baumannii isolates. A study in northeastern Iran reported resistance rates to tobramycin (97%), ceftazidime (96.6%), ciprofloxacin (97.4%), and imipenem (97%), with the majority of the strains being MDR and XDR7. The lack of effective drugs has resulted in over 8,500 hospitalizations and approximately 700 deaths annually due to A. baumannii infections8. Recognizing the severity of this issue, the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have classified these infections as an urgent threat9.

Current research indicates that A. baumannii possess complex drug resistance mechanisms, including target mutations, diminished outer membrane permeability, and multidrug efflux pumps such as the RND export systems: AdeABC, AdeIJK, and AdeFGH10,11. Enzymatic drug degradation, including actions by β-lactamases and aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, also contributes to resistance12. The acquisition of drug-resistant genes through plasmids significantly contributes to the emergence of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) and pan-drug-resistant (PDR) strains13. Additionally, A. baumannii can harbor plasmids that contain clusters of drug resistance genes. These plasmids have the capability to suppress the type VI secretion system (T6SS), potentially diminishing bacterial competition and enhancing survival in the host environment14. A. baumannii forms a virulent biofilm that enhances its ability to colonize and evade the host’s immune system15. Consequently, the drug resistance situation in A. baumannii has become increasingly alarming, leading to substantial clinical challenges in prevention and treatment.

A study that investigated Acinetobacter baumannii in intensive care units (ICUs) across different provinces in China found that carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) strains were prevalent in 71.4% of the ICUs surveyed. The study highlighted that the resistance was predominantly due to carbapenem-hydrolyzing enzymes like blaOXA-23 and that colistin and tigecycline remained effective against most isolates. This pattern of resistance is crucial for understanding the challenges faced in Haikou City16. Another study from Ahvaz, Iran, focused on clinical isolates of A. baumannii, reporting high resistance to key antibiotics like rifampin, imipenem, and meropenem. The genetic analysis revealed a high prevalence of resistance genes, such as tetA, tetB, and qnrA, which are associated with multidrug resistance. Such findings highlight the importance of genetic diversity in influencing resistance profiles17. A study examining A. baumannii in various regions, including Greece, Italy, and Israel, found significant clonal spread of resistant strains, particularly those harboring the blaOXA-23 gene. The study emphasized that horizontal gene transfer and clonal dissemination were key drivers of resistance, which could be relevant in understanding the spread in Haikou City18. Research conducted in Brazil found a high prevalence of plasmids carrying resistance genes, particularly the blaOXA-23 gene, which is widespread in A. baumannii isolates. This regional dissemination of resistant plasmids may parallel the situation in Haikou City, where similar patterns might be observed19.

In this study, the fundamental information on clinical distribution characteristics and drug resistance of 513 A. baumannii strains isolated from a hospital was analyzed, and genotyping was conducted using RAPD technology and cluster analysis. The primary objective of this study is to provide a comprehensive understanding of the prevalence, resistance patterns, and genetic diversity of A. baumannii in the hospital, aiming to support the development of effective, localized prevention and treatment measures.

Materials and methods

Materials

This study analyzed 513 isolates of A. baumannii infections collected from January 2018 to September 2020 at a medical facility in Haikou City. Emesis samples refer to samples of vomit previous used by Kirby et al., (2011)20. These were analyzed to detect the presence of A. baumannii in cases where the pathogen might be present in the gastrointestinal contents, possibly due to systemic infection or ingestion. Isolates were stored in glycerol-based media at -30 °C and revived for experimentation. The strains originated from 44 departments, including emergency care, pulmonary medicine, radiation oncology (II), and gastrointestinal surgery. A subset of 48 isolates was randomly selected for genotypic analysis.

Methods

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The antimicrobial susceptibility of isolates was assessed using the VITEK-32 microbiological analyzer (bioMérieux, France), following CLSI M100-2B guidelines. Quality control involved using Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853).

Random amplified DNA polymorphism genotyping (RAPD-PCR)

RAPD-PCR was performed as per established protocols. Primers OPA-02 (5’-TGCCGAGCTG-3’) and 270 (5’-TGCGCGCGGG-3’) were used (Hsueh et al., 2002; Mohammadi et al., 2021; Valencia-Ledezma et al., 2022). The PCR protocol included initial denaturation (94 °C, 5 min), followed by 4 cycles of annealing (36 °C, 5 min) and extension (72 °C, 5 min), then 35 cycles of denaturation (94 °C, 1 min), annealing (36 °C, 1 min), and extension (72 °C, 2 min), concluding with a final extension (72 °C, 10 min). PCR products underwent electrophoresis on 1.2% agarose gels.

Statistical analysis

Distinct and reproducible DNA bands identified by RAPD were recorded as loci using Gel-pro4.0 software. The number of polymorphic bands and total bands were counted to calculate the percentage of polymorphism, using descriptive statistics to summarize the data. Band presence was denoted as ‘1’ and absence as ‘0’. Data were tabulated in Excel. RAPD results were then analyzed using NTSYSpc2.10 software, which facilitated the calculation of genetic distances using Euclidean distance metrics. Subsequently, cluster analysis was performed using the UPGMA (Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean) method. This statistical approach allowed for the creation of a dendrogram that illustrates the clustering relationships among the samples based on their genetic distances.

Results

Source of specimens, age distribution, and distribution of specimens

Our study analyzed the distribution of A. baumannii isolates from 44 departments in a Haikou City hospital between January 2018 and September 2020. Specimens were primarily found in Critical Care Medicine (28.1%), Respiratory Medicine (7.5%), Respiratory Endoscopy (6.1%), and Integrative Medicine (6.1%). Departments like General Internal Medicine, General Oncology and Surgery each contributed 0.2% of the specimens. The specimens included sputum (74.85%), urine (8.97%), secretions (7.60%), and other types such as puncture fluid, whole blood, and thoracic and abdominal fluid, totaling 10 categories. The majority were obtained from patients aged 60–70 years (160 samples), followed by the 70–80 year age group (135 samples). Among the 513 specimens, 348 were from male patients, predominantly in the 60–70 age group.

Antimicrobial susceptibility results

Resistance to 24 clinical antibiotics was assessed for the 513 A. baumannii isolates. Detailed results are shown in Table 1. Excluding tests with limited specimens, notable resistance rates included gentamicin (66.47%) and ticarcillin/clavulanate (60.82%). Resistance rates for ciprofloxacin, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefepime, amikacin, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and levofloxacin were over 55%. Resistance to meropenem, piperacillin, and piperacillin/tazobactam exceeded 50%. Conversely, strains showed high sensitivity to polymyxin B (88.45%) and minocycline (94.92%). Sensitivity rates for cotrimoxazole, gabapentin, and tobramycin were above 55%.

Distribution of genetic types

Using primer 270 at a 52% similarity coefficient, the isolates were classified into five types (E-I). Notably, 36 isolates (75%) fell into type E, suggesting a high prevalence of this genetic profile. The distribution of other types included five isolates in type F, three in type G, three in type H, and one in type I. Further analysis revealed a significant homology among A. baumannii isolates from different hospital departments (Table 3). Specifically, in departments such as respiratory, emergency ICU, neurosurgery, and intensive care medicine, types C (primer OPA-02) and G (primer 270) were predominant.

Department-specific isolate grouping

The isolates were classified into nine distinct groups (A - I) based on their genetic profiles using Primer OPA-02 and Primer 270. Group A comprised a single isolate from the Neurosurgery Ward. Group B included four isolates from Urology, the Neurosurgery Ward, Intensive Care Medicine Ward, and Comprehensive Internal Medicine. Group C had the majority of isolates (33), spanning various sections such as the Joint Trauma Ward, Department of Respiratory Medicine, Emergency ICU, Spinal Orthopedic Surgery Ward, Interventional Medicine, Urology, Neurology II, Neurosurgery Ward, Gastrointestinal Surgery, and Intensive Care Medicine Ward. Group D contained one isolate from the Emergency ICU, while Group E had three isolates from the Emergency ICU, Intensive Care Medicine Ward, and Neurosurgery Ward. Groups F and G each had one isolate from the Neurosurgery Ward and Intensive Care Medicine Ward, respectively. Group I included four isolates from the Emergency ICU, Neurosurgery Ward, and Comprehensive Internal Medicine. Group J comprised two isolates from the Neurosurgery Ward, and Group K included four isolates from the Neurosurgery Ward and Intensive Care Medicine Ward.

Epidemiological and clinical correlations

The epidemiological and clinical correlation analysis revealed notable trends. In the Neurosurgery Ward, Group C was predominant, with 17 out of 27 isolates, indicating a potential trend related to patient profiles or treatment protocols. The Emergency ICU displayed a diverse presence of Groups C, D, E, and I, suggesting varied patient conditions and infection sources. The Intensive Care Medicine Ward also primarily consisted of Group C isolates, with additional isolates from Groups B, E, G, and K, indicating consistency in patient profiles or clinical conditions treated in this section. Group C’s widespread presence across high-risk areas like the Emergency ICU and Intensive Care Medicine Ward may point to common infection control practices or similar patient demographics. The limited presence of Groups D, E, G, and K in specific sections suggests targeted epidemiological patterns related to particular patient conditions or ward-specific practices (Table 3).

Implications for infection control

This genetic diversity across diverse departments underscores the potential for cross-departmental transmission of A. baumannii in the hospital setting, highlighting the importance of stringent infection control measures. The predominance of certain genetic types in specific wards may also offer insights into targeted strategies for managing A. baumannii infections in these areas.

Discussion

In-hospital drug resistance of A. Baumannii

The distribution of A. baumannii in hospital settings, as observed in our study, aligns with patterns seen in another research that A. baumannii infections were more prevalent in male patients, particularly those aged over 60 years, and were most commonly found in intensive care units21,22. Studies have shown that A. baumannii is commonly found in critical care and respiratory-related departments, which is consistent with our findings of high prevalence in Critical Care Medicine and Respiratory Medicine. Multiple studies have found that A. baumannii is predominantly isolated from intensive care units (ICUs) and respiratory departments. The highest detection rates of A. baumannii were in respiratory medicine and ICUs, particularly from respiratory tract specimens23. Another study revealed that sputum samples were the most common source of A. baumannii isolates, particularly in ICUs and respiratory departments24. The prevalence of multidrug-resistant A. baumannii (MDRAB) in these departments, particularly in ICU settings, has been emphasized, with a significant proportion of these strains being resistant to carbapenems, a last-resort antibiotic25. The persistence of A. baumannii on surfaces, even after disinfection processes, contributes to its recurring outbreaks in hospital settings26. Acinetobacter baumannii is known for its ability to survive for long periods in hospital environments, particularly on inanimate surfaces, which act as reservoirs for cross-colonization and infection outbreaks. The age and gender distribution of the specimens in our study, with a predominance in older male patients, also reflects common trends observed in hospital settings. A. baumannii infections tend to be more common in older patients and those with compromised immune systems, often associated with healthcare environments27.

Recent data underscore the formidable challenges posed by the augmented multiplication, proliferation capacity, and drug resistance of Acinetobacter baumannii, which significantly complicate the prevention and treatment of nosocomial infections worldwide. For instance, surveillance of nosocomial infections in the United States and drug resistance testing in China have identified A. baumannii as a major non-fermenting bacterium responsible for hospital-acquired infections, ranking fourth after Pseudomonas aeruginosa18. This aligns with findings from other studies, which also report high prevalence rates of A. baumannii in clinical specimens such as blood, respiratory, urinary, and cerebrospinal fluids, especially in patients in intensive care units or those dependent on long-term ventilation19,20.

The robust antibiotic resistance displayed by A. baumannii is particularly concerning. It develops resistance to multiple antibiotic classes including penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, quinolones, and macrolides through mechanisms such as mutations in outer membrane porins, beta-lactamase production, and the presence of drug efflux pumps21. Additionally, horizontal gene transfer plays a crucial role in acquiring exogenous resistance genes, exacerbating the multi-drug resistance scenario22. Comparatively, similar studies have documented the rapid evolution of resistance mechanisms in A. baumannii, underscoring a global health concern over the diminishing efficacy of conventional antibiotics like doxycycline, minocycline, colistin, and tigecycline, which are often limited by toxicity and uncertain therapeutic outcomes. These comparisons highlight the critical need for novel therapeutic strategies and underline the importance of continuous surveillance to monitor the evolving resistance patterns of A. baumannii. The global health community must prioritize research into new antibiotics and alternative treatment modalities to effectively combat the high resistance levels of this pathogen.

The drug sensitivity results from this study demonstrated that A. baumannii isolates in a Haikou, Hainan Province hospital exhibited high resistance rates to specific drugs and multidrug resistance, warranting serious attention. The highest resistance rate to gentamicin was 66.47%, followed by more than 55% resistance rates to ticarcillin/clavulanate, amikacin, ampicillin/sulbactam, ciprofloxacin, and cefepime, complicating clinical antibiotic treatment. Fortunately, A. baumannii demonstrated sensitivity to minocycline and polymyxin B, with sensitivity rates of 94.92% and 88.45% respectively, and increased sensitivity to cotrimoxazole and tobramycin, with sensitivity rates exceeding 55%, providing a reliable basis for rational clinical use. However, even polymyxin, considered the “last resort,” has reported emerging drug resistance in recent years28, necessitating caution in clinical use. In this study, 268 multidrug-resistant A. baumannii strains were identified among 513 A. baumannii strains, accounting for 52.24%, more than half of the total strains, underscoring the severity of the drug resistance situation.

According to this study, multidrug-resistant A. baumannii was primarily detected in respiratory system diseases (59.33%) and digestive system diseases (20.52%). This outcome may be attributed to patients with respiratory system diseases often undergoing various therapeutic measures, such as tracheal intubation or incision and mechanical ventilation29. These procedures typically result in some respiratory mucosa damage in patients, reduced cilia clearance ability, increased mucus viscosity, and an environment conducive to A. baumannii growth and multiplication. Meanwhile, medical devices such as ventilators, fiberoptic bronchoscopes, and aspirators can easily cause A. baumannii infections and epidemic outbreaks if not strictly disinfected. For patients with digestive diseases, such as stress ulcers, long-term acidulant use can lead to intestinal bacterial overgrowth in the stomach. If the patient experiences gastroesophageal reflux or gastric tube retention, it can easily result in gastro-lower respiratory tract reverse endogenous infection development30.

Drug sensitivity results

The resistance patterns of A. baumannii to various antibiotics, as observed in our study, align with global trends that have been documented in recent research. These trends are a significant concern in healthcare settings due to the increasing difficulty in treating infections caused by this pathogen. A study conducted in a tertiary teaching hospital in Lebanon provides a detailed assessment of A. baumannii’s susceptibility patterns. This study used the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method for antimicrobial susceptibility testing, adhering to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. It included a comprehensive data collection process, covering patients’ socio-demographic information, potential risk factors for mortality-associated infections, primary site and onset of infection, and the empirical antibiotic therapy administered. The study’s robust methodology offers valuable insights into the resistance patterns of A. baumannii and contributes to our understanding of this pathogen’s behavior in different healthcare settings31​​.

Additionally, a study from Pakistan indicates that A. baumannii has become multidrug resistant due to the use of a wide range of drugs. The study identifies colistin, polymyxin B, and vancomycin as preferable drugs for treating A. baumannii infections, highlighting the limited treatment options available for this pathogen32. A comprehensive review article also discusses the evolving and cunning nature of A. baumannii, underscoring the pathogen’s high resistance rates, particularly to tigecycline and polymyxin - the last-resort drugs for extensively drug-resistant strains. The review emphasizes the critical need to understand the resistance mechanisms of A. baumannii, which is crucial for the rational use of antibiotics in clinical settings and for the development of new antibiotics33​.

​ Our study’s findings are consistent with these global observations and underscore the importance of ongoing surveillance and research to adapt treatment strategies and combat the spread of resistant A. baumannii strains. The high sensitivity rates to polymyxin B and minocycline observed in our study are particularly valuable, as they represent some of the few remaining effective treatments against resistant A. baumannii strains. The global variation in resistance patterns, influenced by factors such as local antibiotic use and hospital infection control practices, further highlights the need for continual monitoring and adaptation of treatment strategies in response to changing resistance patterns.

RAPD-PCR amplification and clustering analysis

Our analysis of Acinetobacter baumannii isolates using Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD)-PCR offers significant methodological advantages when compared to other genomic typing techniques like Pulse Field Gel Electrophoresis (PFGE) or Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST). RAPD-PCR is particularly suited for rapid, preliminary investigations of genetic diversity due to its simplicity, speed, and cost-effectiveness. This method does not require prior knowledge of the DNA sequence, making it ideal for pathogens with less characterized genomes. In the context of hospital infection control, the ability of RAPD-PCR to quickly identify genetically similar outbreaks of A. baumannii is crucial. For instance, our study aligns with recent findings where RAPD-PCR was used to analyze A. baumannii strains34,35. These studies underscored that antibiotic-resistant strains showed more homogeneous gene content, suggesting a selection advantage due to gene consistency, while non-resistant strains exhibited more genetic diversity. Moreover, the correlation between antibiotic resistance patterns and phage susceptibility suggested by our data provides potential insights into alternative treatments like phage therapy, which could be pivotal in combating drug-resistant infections.

The specific findings from our study revealed significant homology among isolates from different hospital departments, with the predominance of specific genetic types in certain wards like Neurosurgery, where invasive procedures and prolonged stays increase susceptibility to infections. This emphasizes the importance of genetic profiling for developing targeted infection control strategies and understanding transmission dynamics within hospital settings36​. The distribution patterns revealed by RAPD polymorphism across different hospital wards also provide critical epidemiological insights. For example, the varied conditions and treatment protocols in the Emergency ICU and Intensive Care Medicine Ward reflected a broader diversity of A. baumannii isolates, aligning with findings from other studies in high-risk hospital environments37. This ability to map the distribution of pathogenic clusters across hospital wards showcases RAPD-PCR’s utility in real-time surveillance and outbreak management. Thus, the use of RAPD-PCR in our study not only supported the existing literature but also contributed unique epidemiological data crucial for hospital infection control measures, particularly in identifying and managing outbreaks of A. baumannii.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively analyzed the distribution and resistance patterns of Acinetobacter baumannii within a hospital in Haikou City, revealing a notable concentration in high-risk departments such as Critical Care and Respiratory Medicine. The demographic analysis indicated that older male patients, particularly in the 60–70 age range, are disproportionately affected, underscoring the necessity for demographic-specific surveillance and preventive measures. The genetic diversity identified through RAPD-PCR highlights the risk of cross-departmental transmission, reinforcing the need for stringent and tailored infection control protocols. Significantly, despite widespread resistance to many antibiotics, the isolates showed sensitivity to polymyxin B and minocycline, offering viable alternatives for treatment. These findings necessitate the implementation of targeted surveillance and enhanced infection control strategies, especially in departments where prevalence is high. Such measures should include the development of customized protocols that consider the unique resistance patterns and genetic diversity of the pathogen, thereby preventing its spread. Moreover, the integration of sensitivity data into antibiotic prescribing practices can promote effective antibiotic stewardship. Ultimately, this study not only advances our understanding of A. baumannii in tertiary care settings but also emphasizes the critical need for ongoing research and adaptive strategies to manage and mitigate the impact of this pathogen in the face of rising antibiotic resistance.
