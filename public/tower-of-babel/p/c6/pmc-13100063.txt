Machine learning-based method for determining regional parameters of the HSS model: a case study of Qingdao, China
Authors: Changfeng Yuan, Qiming Zhang, Hao Feng, Ke Wang, Shunzhe Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The hardening soil small-strain (HSS) model is widely recognized for its ability to simulate complex soil stress paths, leading to improved prediction accuracy in geotechnical engineering. However, its practical application is often hindered by the large number of required parameters and their significant regional variability, making determination costly and time-consuming. To address this, we propose a machine learning-based framework for efficient determination of locality-specific HSS parameters. The method begins with a statistical analysis of parameter ratios relative to the compression modulus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{\mathrm{s}}}^{{1 {-} 2}}$$\end{document} to identify sensitive and non-sensitive parameters. For sensitive parameters with high regional variability, a parameter sampling design is implemented within established value ranges. A BP neural network is then trained to establish a nonlinear mapping between parameter combinations and foundation pit displacements, enabling intelligent inverse analysis. The proposed method is applied to typical strata in Qingdao, China—plain fill, silty clay, and medium-fine sand—where regional HSS parameters were previously unavailable. Through validation with two independent cases, numerical simulations using inversion parameters showed a high consistency with on-site monitoring data: the root mean square error (RMSE) for both the lateral displacement of the retaining structure and ground settlement was below 0.51 mm, and the mean absolute error was below 0.4 mm. Introduction

Finite element method (FEM) has become an indispensable tool in geotechnical engineering for predicting soil–structure interaction and deformation, particularly for complex projects such as deep excavations in urban environments. FEM offers significant advantages over simplified analytical methods by accommodating complex geometries, nonlinear material behavior, and staged construction processes1–3. Its accuracy, however, critically depends on the use of appropriate constitutive models and their parameters. In recent years, the construction of deep and large-scale foundation pits often occurs in complex surroundings such as high-rise buildings, underground utility networks, and subway tunnels. This has raised higher demands for accurately controlling the safety of deep excavations and adjacent structures. Numerical simulation with appropriate parameters and constitutive models has become one of the primary methods for predicting the stress and deformation of deep foundation pits in complex environments. Compared to the Mohr–Coulomb (M–C) and hardening soil (HS) models, the hardening soil small-strain (HSS) model can more comprehensively simulate complex soil stress paths, leading to predictions that better align with engineering practice4.

However, the HSS model involves numerous parameters, some of which exhibit strong regional specificity, limiting its widespread application. Several researchers5–10 have studied HSS parameter values for soft soils in the Shanghai area, obtaining local HSS parameters through extensive testing and identifying the initial shear modulus \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} as the most sensitive parameter. Wang et al.11 determined stiffness parameters such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{{\mathrm{oed}}}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{{50}}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{{\mathrm{ur}}}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} for marine soil in Dafeng, Jiangsu through experiments. Other scholars12–15 have provided HSS parameter values for regions including Zhengzhou, Kunming, Zhuhai, and Hebei based on experimental studies. Meng et al.16 investigated the sensitivity of parameters of Hangzhou silty clay and concluded that the small-strain parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} significantly affects excavation deformation, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma_{0.7}$$\end{document} has minimal influence; they also provided values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} for local silty clay. Li et al.17 and Shen et al.18 studied the ratio relationships and regional variations between HSS parameters and the compression modulus in Jinan and Ningbo, respectively. These studies indicate that some HSS parameters exhibit notable regional variations, and certain parameters are highly sensitive to deformation predictions. However, such traditional methods usually rely on extensive indoor trials, which are costly and time-consuming.

To enhance efficiency, scholars have proposed various improved schemes. Kong et al.19 introduced a parameter determination method for the HSS model based on the “reference in-situ overburden pressure” and “reference in-situ void ratio”. This approach systematically estimates parameters using conventional geotechnical test data, and its engineering practicality was validated through a deep excavation project in Kunming, significantly reducing testing costs. On the other hand, intelligent optimization algorithms have been introduced to replace complex laboratory tests. Xiao et al.20 utilized the particle swarm optimization algorithm to automatically inversely determine five key parameters of the hardening soil model based on triaxial test data. This ensured that the error between the numerical curve and the experimental curve did not exceed 8.2%. The optimized parameters were then applied to excavation analysis, resulting in a deviation of only 6.12% between the calculated settlement and actual monitoring data, demonstrating efficient and accurate optimization capabilities.

In summary, existing research has made progress in revealing the regional characteristics of parameters, developing economical and practical estimation methods, and exploring intelligent inversion techniques. To further achieve efficient and accurate parameter acquisition, the introduction of intelligent methods such as machine learning to invert region-specific key parameters represents a significant future direction. Therefore, this study takes Qingdao as an example and introduces a machine learning method to determine regional HSS parameters. The marine strata in Qingdao are characterized by widespread soft soil layers and flowing sand layers, yet no research on HSS parameter values in this region has been conducted. By compiling HSS parameters of relevant soil layers from various regions in China, the values of parameters with low regional variability and sensitivity are determined, while value ranges are established for those with high regional variability and sensitivity. Parameter sampling are designed for the latter category, and machine learning is employed to establish a nonlinear mapping between soil response and parameters—intelligently substituting parts of complex testing. Finally, forward analysis is conducted to compare computational results with field monitoring data, verifying the reliability of the HSS parameters obtained through this machine learning inversion method.

Determination of the HSS model parameters

First, relevant literature on HSS model parameters is reviewed to determine recommended values for parameters with low regional variations and sensitivity, as well as value ranges for parameters exhibiting significant regional differences and high sensitivity. Next, for parameters with both high regional specificity and sensitivity, parameter sampling are designed based on their value ranges, and a neural network inversion method is introduced to establish a nonlinear mapping relationship between soil response and parameters, thereby replacing some complex experiments to determine HSS model parameters suitable for the Qingdao region. Finally, the calculated results are compared with field monitoring data to verify the reliability of the parameters obtained through the machine learning inversion approach. Parameter sampling design

In this study, we conduct orthogonal tests and numerical computations based on a specific project in Qingdao to ascertain the values of the parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{{\mathrm{ur}}}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} within the HSS model, which characterizes the typical formations in Qingdao.

The investigated foundation pit project, located in Qingdao, spans 126 m in length and 65 m in width, with an excavation depth ranging from 7.2 to 8.3 m. The eastern side of the pit has a site elevation of 17.1 m, a pit-bottom elevation of 8.8 m, and an excavation depth of 8.3 m, while the western side features a site elevation of 16 m, a uniform pit-bottom elevation of 8.8 m, and a shallow excavation depth of 7.2 m, as depicted in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Elevation diagram of the foundation pit.

Model construction

Figure 4 presents a schematic illustration of the support system for the deep foundation pit, which is segmented into four distinct support units. Unit C-1 employs a combination of peripheral piles and counterpressure soil for support, while units C-2, C-3, and C-4 are reinforced with pile and anchor systems. In the depicted Fig. 4, the designations D1–D18 correspond to the monitoring points for surface settlement, and W1–W14 denote the monitoring points for horizontal displacement at the tops of the encircling piles.Fig. 4Schematic diagram of the support structure.

Schematic diagram of the support structure.

Utilizing engineering survey data and the structural design documents of the pit enclosure, a three-dimensional numerical model was developed using Plaxis 3D software. To account for the influence of the boundaries, the model’s horizontal extent was set to three times the width of the excavation, while the vertical depth reaches 3H (where H is the depth of excavation). Consequently, the dimensions of the model were defined as 350 m in length, 300 m in width, and 30 m in depth. Related scholars25 pointed out that it can be proved that the results of designing the enclosing piles according to the equivalent diaphragm wall are in favor of safety and reasonableness. Following the equivalent stiffness conversion method described in Hu26, the peripheral piles were simulated as underground diaphragm walls using slab elements, with the equivalent formula provided in Eqs. (3) and (4)3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{{12}}\left( {{\mathrm{D}} + {\mathrm{t}}} \right)^{3} {\mathrm{h}}^{3} = \frac{1}{{64}}\pi {\mathrm{D}}^{4}$$\end{document}4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{h}} = 0.838{\mathrm{D}}^{3} \sqrt {\frac{1}{{1 + \frac{{\mathrm{t}}}{{\mathrm{D}}}}}}$$\end{document}where D is the diameter of the enclosing piles, t is the clear distance between the enclosing piles, and h is the thickness of the equivalent underground diaphragm wall.

Crown beams were modeled with beam elements, while retaining walls and shotcrete facings on slopes were represented with slab elements. An interface element was introduced to effectively capture the interaction between the underground diaphragm wall and the soil layer. Additionally, point-to-point anchor cables and embedded piles were employed to simulate the free and anchored segments of the anchor cables, respectively. The modulus of elasticity for concrete was set at 30 GPa, with a Poisson’s ratio of 0.2. The finite element mesh was discretized using 10-node tetrahedral elements. For the retaining structure, anchors, and soil within the excavation zone, a mesh refinement factor of 0.5 was applied, while the remaining soil domains were assigned a factor of 1. The resulting three-dimensional model comprised 256,207 elements and 387,185 nodes, as illustrated in Fig. 5. To verify mesh independence, the mesh refinement factor was progressively reduced: the factor for the retaining structure, anchors, and excavated soil was successively set to 0.3536 and 0.25, while the corresponding factor for the remaining soil was adjusted to 0.7071 and 0.5. Simulation results before and after mesh refinement showed discrepancies within 3%, confirming the mesh independence of the numerical model.Fig. 5Finite element model.

Inversion of parameters using BP neural network

The BP neural network structure comprises an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer27. The number of neurons in the hidden layer is determined in accordance with the empirical Eq. (5)28. The hidden layer utilizes the hyperbolic tangent sigmoid (‘tansig’) activation function, while the output layer employs a linear (‘purelin’) activation function. Training was conducted using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. An early stopping mechanism was implemented based on validation set performance to prevent overfitting, with the maximum number of training epochs set to 1000.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{n}}_{{2}} = \sqrt {{\mathrm{n}}_{{1}} + {\mathrm{m}} + {1}} + {\mathrm{a}}$$\end{document}where n2 is the number of neurons in the hidden layer; n1 is the number of neurons in the input layer; m is the number of neurons in the output layer; a is a conditioning constant between 1 and 10.

The data sets derived from Tables 4 and 5 were allocated into training (75%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets, subsequently imported into MATLAB for neural network training. With the parameters n = 4 and m = 6 established, the number of neurons in the hidden layer was iteratively adjusted from a = 1–10 for the training phase, culminating in an optimal hidden layer configuration of a = 6. whereas the ratios of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{G}}_{{0}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{{\mathrm{ur}}}}^{{{\mathrm{ref}}}}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{E}}_{{\mathrm{s}}}^{{1 {-} 2}}$$\end{document} exhibit greater variations, indicating high sensitivity.After designing experiments based on statistical ranges and acquiring training samples, the trained model achieved a mean square error (MSE) of 0.1924 MPa, a mean absolute error (MAE) of 0.2352 MPa, and a correlation coefficient R of 0.98336, indicating good training performance.
