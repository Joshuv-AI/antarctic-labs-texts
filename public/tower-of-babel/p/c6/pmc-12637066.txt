Preparation and evaluation of an injectable HAp/Col I composite for promoting tendon-to-bone healing in ACL reconstruction☆
Authors: Zhanhong Liu, Qingsong Jiang, Liren Wang, Zhiru Chen, Yang Xu, Xiangdong Zhu, Jinzhong Zhao, Yong Wang, Hai Lin, Xingdong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tendon-to-bone healing is considered as a critical determinant influencing the quality of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. The establishment of a microenvironment conducive to tendon-bone healing remains an urgent challenge for rapidly restoring the structural integrity and functional capacity of damaged tissue. In this study, an injectable hydroxyapatite/collagen type I (HAp/Col I) composite was developed and optimized. Its efficacy in ACL reconstruction was verified in a beagle dog model. During the early postoperative period, this composite successfully established an optimal regenerative microenvironment, thereby promoting cell proliferation, adhesion, and extracellular matrix secretion. Micro CT analysis revealed that the HAp/Col I composite significantly accelerated the mineralization process of local tissue and facilitated the contraction of the artificial bone tunnel. Tensile pull-out tests and nanoindentation experiments demonstrated that HAp/Col I composite enhanced both the macroscopic tensile pull-out strength of the ligament and the elastic modulus at the microscopic level. Furthermore, Raman spectroscopy and histological evaluations indicated that the tendon-to-bone interface exhibited a composition and structure closely resembling native tissue in terms of inorganic components and fiber alignment. Additionally, the injectable HAp/Col I composite consisted of safe, non-toxic materials, featured a convenient injection process, and was compatible with arthroscopic procedures, suggesting promising clinical application prospects.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears represent a prevalent condition in sports medicine, comprising for over 50 % of knee joint injuries [1]. Annually, approximately 60,000 to 150,000 individuals in the United States undergo ACL reconstruction surgery [2]. Despite ongoing advancements in surgical techniques and rehabilitation protocols, failure rates for this procedure have been reported as high as 13.3 % [3], which remains clinically unacceptable. Current ligament repair methods rely predominantly on mechanical fixation, which fails to effectively promote the well-organized native transition between soft tissue and bone [4]. Furthermore, the inherent limitations of these fixation techniques contribute to a pronounced “windshield wiper effect” within the bone tunnel, leading to delayed healing and potentially increasing bone tunnel enlargement [5]. Consequently, addressing the critical need for enhanced healing following ACL reconstruction, while restoring structural integrity and functionality, is of paramount importance.

The tendon-to-bone interface (TBI), referred to as the enthesis, is a complex tissue with variations at both compositional and structural levels. Compositionally, the enthesis exhibits a gradient distribution, with hydroxyapatite (HAp) and collagen (Col) gradually transitioning over a distance of approximately 500 μm [6]. Structurally, Sharpey's fibers near the enthesis are vertically embedded into the hard tissue interface [6]. This unique combination of compositional and structural features plays a critical role in the functionality of the tendon-to-bone interface, particularly regarding stiffness and elasticity, which facilitate the dissipation of forces during passive conduction. The healing process can generally be divided into three sequential stages: inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [7]. An insufficient healing environment may result in an overabundance of inflammatory exudates or fibroblasts, leading to uneven healing between interfaces and hindering the restoration of structural and functional integrity [8,9]. Therefore, it is essential to prevent scar tissue formation and establish a conducive regenerative microenvironment.

Creating an effective microenvironment for tendon-to-bone interface regeneration represents a critical focus for tissue engineering and biomaterial scientists. This area of research encompasses extensive investigations into decellularized scaffolds, growth factors, and stem cell therapies. Decellularized scaffolds exhibit outstanding biocompatibility and minimal immunogenicity, with their structure and composition closely resembling those of natural tissues. These properties make them highly effective for tendon-to-bone healing [10]. Kang et al. utilized a human umbilical cord (UC)-derived scaffold retaining ECM components such as collagen and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) to enhance tendon-to-bone healing. In a canine rotator cuff model, the scaffold promoted cartilage-like interface regeneration and improved mechanical strength. Preliminary clinical results further showed reduced inflammation and a more mature TBI structure [11]. Lun et al. developed a MnO2-functionalized decellularized tendon scaffold and demonstrated that, when applied to tendon repair, it promoted collagen alignment, enhanced mechanical strength, and significantly improved tendon-to-bone integration by alleviating oxidative stress and preserving tenogenic activity [12]. Growth factors are potent biologically active substances that effectively promote tendon-to-bone interface regeneration. Extensive prior research has underscored their significance in this content [13,14]. Du et al. innovatively developed a controlled-release system for growth factors (GFs) by integrating cerasomes with 3D-printed PCL scaffolds, which effectively promoted enthesis healing. This system not only enhanced the recruitment and differentiation of BMSCs, but also facilitated fibrocartilage and bone regeneration at the tendon–bone interface, thereby restoring biomechanical function. These findings underscored the crucial role of sustained GF delivery in tendon–bone healing [15]. Zhang et al. further advanced this field by developing a MOF-based hydrogel incorporating BMP-2 as the key growth factor to promote tendon-bone healing. The controlled release of BMP-2 enhanced osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and immune modulation, leading to improved collagen fiber regeneration, biomechanical strength, and tissue integration in a rat rotator cuff model [16]. Cells also play a pivotal role in the regeneration, compositional variation, and in vivo equilibrium of stratified interface tissues [17]. Wang et al. investigated the effects of co-culturing fibroblasts and osteoblasts on the responses of ACL fibroblasts or BMSCs, revealing that paracrine factors and physical cell interactions influenced BMSC differentiation [18]. Chen et al. enhanced the formation of collagen fibers and fibrocartilage in rabbit rotator cuffs by seeding BMP-12-overexpressing BMSCs onto PLGA scaffolds [19]. Collectively, these strategies have established a conducive microenvironment for tissue regeneration, achieving promising initial outcomes. However, the majority of existing research has predominantly focused on material design or cell-level experiments, with limited comprehensive investigation into broader aspects of tendon-to-bone healing [20,21]. Additionally, the materials proposed in these studies may raise concerns regarding potential toxicity or economic feasibility, while the experimental protocols are often labor-intensive and complex, thereby hindering their clinical translation. The optimized design of materials for tendon-to-bone healing thus remains an unresolved challenge.

Collagen is a natural polymer that serves as a primary component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in tendons, comprising approximately 90 % of the ECM's main constituents [22]. It is characterized by its low immunogenicity and ability to promote cell adhesion [23,24], which enables it to play a crucial role in tendon repair and tendon-to-bone healing [25]. Due to its structural and compositional resemblance to natural tissues, collagen-based biomaterial is suitable for application in tendon-bone healing. Hydroxyapatite, on the other hand, exhibits superior osteoinductive properties and has been demonstrated in animal studies to enhance tendon-bone integration [26,27]. Additionally, hydroxyapatite can enhance the osseointegration of artificial ligaments through coating or mixing techniques [22,28,29]. The combination of collagen and hydroxyapatite creates a favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration, promoting repair without the need for exogenous growth factors or stem cells. An injectable composite primarily composed of collagen and hydroxyapatite has been developed and applied to ACL reconstruction in beagle dogs, where it achieved favorable outcomes during the mid- and late stages of healing [30]. However, the role of this material in reconstructing the critical early microenvironment was not adequately addressed, and a comprehensive and in-depth investigation into the interfacial composition and structure at the reconstructed enthesis remains lacking.

To address this gap, we developed a novel strategy based on a specific design philosophy. Our approach to "biomimetic" design in this study focuses on two aspects: compositional and functional biomimicry. Rather than attempting to perfectly replicate the complex hierarchical structure of the native enthesis from the outset, we aim to use native biological components (collagen and hydroxyapatite) to create a microenvironment that induces the endogenous regeneration of a native-like interface. Accordingly, to translate this philosophy into a solution compatible with minimally invasive arthroscopic surgery, this study was designed to first systematically optimize such an injectable HAp/Col I composite, and subsequently to validate its long-term efficacy in a canine ACL reconstruction model. The evaluation focused specifically on the material's role across all stages of tendon-to-bone healing and the critical interplay between the regenerated interface's composition, structure, and function.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of injectable HAp/Col I composite

Preparation of injectable HAp/Col I composite

The Hydroxyapatite particles (HAp) and Type I collagen (Col I) were provided by the National Engineering Research Center for Biomaterials (Sichuan, China). The Type I collagen used in our study was derived from fetal bovine skin and prepared via a patented technology. The molecular weight of the collagen is approximate 300 kDa as measured by SDS-PAGE (data were not shown). The successful application in tissue engineering and the biocompatibility of this collagen as implantable scaffold have been validated in previous publications [31,32]. To prepare HAp/Col I composite, 20 mg/mL Col I solution was firstly placed in a beaker, and the pH was adjusted to 7.2–7.4 using acetic acid and NaOH (both from Kelong, China). Following the final concentration of Col I solution was diluted to 10 mg/mL at 4 °C, HAp particles which were separated into five groups based on particle size: <25 μm, 25–40 μm, 40–71 μm, 71–100 μm, and 100–200 μm were uniformly dispersed into the prepared Col I solution.

The concentration of HAp in the composite was optimized to balance osteogenic potential with clinical deliverability. Composites with 30 %, 40 %, and 50 % (w/w) HAp contents were prepared and evaluated. Formulation with 30 % HAp showed excellent injectability but the low mineral content was not the most conducive to rapid bone formation. Conversely, the 50 % HAp formulation was unstable, with particle aggregation leading to frequent clogging of an 18G needle during extrusion tests. The 40 % HAp composite, however, represented the optimal balance, demonstrating good homogeneity while being injected smoothly and reliably. Therefore, this formulation was selected for all subsequent studies. The compounding process was conducted under ice bath conditions to maintain thermal stability [30].

To eliminate entrapped air bubbles, the attained composite was centrifuged at 1500 r/min for 30s using a low-temperature centrifuge (Heraeus ryofuge8000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). Finally, the composites were transferred into 1 ml syringes, sealed, and stored at 4 °C until further use.

Characterization of injectable HAp/Col I composite

The particle sizes of HAp and the morphological characteristics of freeze-dried HAp/Col I composite were observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Japan), and the particle size distribution was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. Subsequently, samples from different HAp/Col I composite groups were embedded, frozen, and sectioned at a thickness of 20 μm using a cryostat. The sections were stained with Alizarin Red S for detailed microscopic observation. Additionally, HAp/Col I composite samples from each group were loaded into 5 ml glass syringes, fitted with custom-made hollow molds, and equipped with 18G needles at the syringe tips. The extrusion force required to dispense the composite was then measured using a universal testing machine.

In vitro cell culture

Cell viability assay

The HAp/Col I composite extract was prepared using DMEM medium (Solarbio, China) supplemented with 10 % fresh fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). Subsequently, the extract was diluted to various concentrations (100 %, 50 %, 25 %, and 12.5 %) using DMEM containing 10 % FBS for further study.

L929 mouse fibroblasts were procured from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured and passaged to the P3 generation, followed by enzymatically digested and resuspended in culture medium to achieve a final cell density of 1 × 105 cells/mL. Next, 100 μL of cell suspension was seeded into each well of a 96-well plate, with 100 μL of medium added to designed wells as a blank control. The plate was incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 for 24 h to allow the formation of a semi-confluent monolayer. Subsequently, the culture medium was replaced with 150 μL of the aforementioned extract dilutions. A 20 % DMSO solution served as the positive control, while DMEM containing 10 % FBS acted as the negative control. All groups were incubated under identical conditions for an additional 24 h, with 5 replicate wells established for each group.

After 24 h of incubation, the supernatant was carefully removed, and 150 μL of CCK-8 (Dojindo, Japan) solution was added to each well. The plate was then placed in a light-protected incubator at 37 °C with a 5 % CO2 atmosphere for 4 h. Following this incubation period, 100 μL of supernatant from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated according to the methodology outlined in ISO 10993-5.

Cell adhesion and proliferation evaluation

The osteogenic capacity of the samples was evaluated using MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. A suspension of MC3T3-E1 cells was prepared and inoculated into HAp/Col l composite blocks with dimensions of 8 mm in diameter and 2.5 mm in height, at a cell density of 5 × 106 cells/mL. The culture was conducted in α-MEM medium (Solarbio, China), supplemented with 10 % standard FBS. The samples were incubated under standardized conditions of 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 21 days, with 3 replicates per group. For comparison, collagen I at a concentration of 10 mg/mL and calcium phosphate ceramic sheets (PC) were selected as controls, following an identical experimental protocol. The calcium phosphate ceramic sheets (PC) were used as the positive control group for osteogenic gene expression.

Following culture of the samples for 1, 3, 7, and 14 days, they were retrieved from the culture medium, washed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS), and fixed in a 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 48 h. The samples were then washed again with PBS and subjected to a gradient dehydration process by sequential immersion in ethanol solutions ranging from 30 % to 100 % (v/v) for 30 min at each concentration. Subsequently, the dehydrated samples were dried using a critical point dryer (Leica EM CPD300, Germany), and SEM observation was conducted.

At 3 and 14 days, the samples were stained with fluorescein diacetate (FDA, Sigma, USA) and propidium iodide (PI, Sigma, USA) for 5 min. The stained cells were then washed with PBS and cell activity was analyzed using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, ZEISS LSM 880, Germany). In addition, another set of samples was washed with PBS, fixed with paraformaldehyde, and incubated with rhodamine-phalloidin solution (Sigma, USA) for 60 min. Subsequently, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, USA), and the actin cytoskeleton was visualized using CLSM. Cell viability and proliferation were quantitatively evaluated using a CCK-8 assay kit.

Gene expressions determined by RT-qPCR

The gene expression levels of ALP, RUNX2, BMP2, OCN, and VEGF were quantitatively analyzed at various time points during a culture period of 21 days using RT-qPCR. Total RNA was extracted using the chloroform (Kelong, China), and cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA samples using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis kit (Bio-Rad). Real-time PCR amplifications were performed on a CFX96t real-time thermocycler (Bio-Rad) using the SsoFast™ EvaGreen® Supermix kit (Bio-Rad). The resulting data were analyzed with CFX Manager software, with each group comprising 3 technical replicates. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative ΔΔCt method, normalized to the internal reference gene GAPDH. In vivo evaluation

Surgical planning and procedures

The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University (Approval No. KS2022996). For in vivo ACL reconstruction, 56 mature male beagles (weight: 10 ± 1 kg) were randomly allocated into experimental (n = 28) and control (n = 28) groups. The surgical procedure was conducted in accordance with a previous published methodology [30]. In brief, the peroneus longus tendon of each beagle was harvested and braided to form the graft. Following exposure of the joint cavity and excision of the ACL, an artificial bone tunnel was created from the lateral tibia to the medial femur using a Kirschner needle (diameter = 1.5 mm) and a hollow drill (diameter = 4.5 mm). In the experimental group, the composite was injected into the bone tunnel via syringe, whereas no biomaterial was administered in the control group. Subsequently, the graft was positioned and secured. After thorough irrigation of the joint cavity, the surgical incision was closed with sutures. It is important to note that ACL reconstruction was performed exclusively on the right hind limb of each animal.

General observation and harvest of tissue samples

Seven beagles from each group were sacrificed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery for sample collection. Firstly, drawer tests were performed to evaluate joint stability. Subsequently, the joint cavity was surgically exposed to enable macroscopic evaluation of the internal grafts, articular cartilage, and meniscus. The distal femur, middle soft tissue, and proximal tibial were harvested for subsequent experiments. In addition, normal joint tissues obtained from each beagle served as the control group.

Within each of the three groups, a subset of the graft/ACL samples (n = 3) was excised from the joint cavity and fixed in a 4 % PFA solution for 24 h. Following gradient alcohol dehydration, the tissues subjected to critical point drying and subsequently analyzed by SEM for morphology assessment.

Micro CT analysis

The samples were fixed in 4 % PFA for 24 h after the removal of surface soft tissues. Subsequently, grayscale images were obtained using a micro-CT scanning system (SCANCO medical AG μCT100, Switzerland) with an isotropic voxel size of 30 μm. The acquired image data were reconstructed, calibrated, and subjected to 3-dimensional analysis using Evaluation V6.5 software. A region of interest (ROI) was delineated through threshold segmentation and Boolean operations, which facilitated the analysis of the morphological characteristics of the tendon-to-bone interface as well as the calculation of new bone volume, bone volume to tissue volume ratio (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD). Finally, the rendering was visualized according to BMD values using Avizo 2020.1.

Histological analysis

After undergoing micro-CT scanning, the samples were sequentially subjected to gradient dehydration using alcohol and were cleared with xylene. Thereafter, they were embedded in PMMA (Technovit 9100, Germany), and sectioned along the longitudinal axis of the artificial bone tunnel using a saw microtome (2500E, Leica, Germany). The sections were further ground and polished to a thickness of 5 μm, and then were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Masson's trichrome stain. High-resolution imaging was performed using a research slide scanner (VS200, Olympus, Japan) equipped with the Extended Focal Imaging module.

Micro-Raman spectroscopy analysis

Micro-Raman spectroscopy was performed using a Raman confocal microscope (Renishaw inVia Qontor, United Kingdom) equipped with a 785 nm laser. Prior to analysis, the surface of the tendon-to-bone resin-embedded block was polished and cleaned with 7000-grit sandpaper. The sample was then mounted on the carrier stage. The laser was focused onto the sample surface through a 100× objective lens and scanned across an array with dimensions of 150× 50 μm. The lateral resolution achieved was ≤0.4 μm, while the longitudinal resolution along the optical axis was ≤1.5 μm. Subsequently, baseline correction and cosmic ray reduction were conducted using Renishaw WiRE 5.5 software. Variations in phosphate content across different regions of the tendon-to-bone interface were primarily reflected at the Raman shift position of 960 cm−1 [33]. Based on the peak area of this band, mapping was generated and the width of the phosphate gradient variation was quantified.

Nanoindentation test

Nanoindentation tests were performed on a high-throughput nano-indenter (Hysitron TI-950, United Kingdom) equipped with a Berkovich diamond indenter. Prior to testing, the surfaces of resin-embedded samples were polished using alumina powder with an average particle size of 1 μm and subsequently cleaned via ultrasonic treatment. To ensure complete rehydration, PBS solution was carefully applied dropwise to the sample surface. Nanoindentation tests were conducted on randomly selected 50 × 50 μm areas at the tendon-to-bone interface, with 5 measurement points per area. The maximum loading force of 10 mN was applied at a loading/unloading rate of 20 mN/min, followed by a holding period of 10s at peak load [34,35]. The elastic modulus and hardness were calculated using the Oliver-Pharr method based on the unloading segment of the load-penetration curve. Assuming isotropic behavior and a Poisson's ratio is 0.3 for the tissue samples, the measurement locations were positioned immediately adjacent to the tendon-to-bone interface. Five locations were measured for each sample, and the resulting elastic modulus (Eb) and hardness (Hb) values were averaged to represent the mechanical properties of the interface.

Macro-biomechanics test

The macro-biomechanics properties of the grafts were tested using an electromechanical universal testing system (Instron 5985, United States). Surrounding soft tissues and fixation wires were carefully removed, leaving only the tibial-ACL-femoral structure intact. The tibia and femur were fixed to each arm of the instrument (n = 4), and the mechanical arm was stretched along the longitudinal axis of the graft at a rate of 10 mm/min, referring to the international standard ISO 527-2.

Statistical analysis

All data were collected from at least 3 independent experiments unless otherwise stated, analyzed using GraphPad Prism v9.0, and presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with the following notations: ns, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results

Physic-chemical properties of the injectable HAp/Col composite

Fig. 1A illustrated the primary factors to be considered during the design and preparation of Col l and HAp composite, including pH, temperature, collagen concentration, component ratio, HAp morphology, and HAp particle size. Based on previous studies, the influence of HAp particle size on the extrusion properties of injectable composite was further explored. The mixtures were pre-filled into a syringe, as shown in Fig. 1B, forming a homogenous milky-white colloid without noticeable air bubbles. Five groups of HAp particles with distinct size ranges were prepared and screened, specifically: 0–25 μm, 25–40 μm, 40–70 μm, 70–100 μm, and 100–200 μm. SEM images of these HAp particles were shown in Fig. 1C. The HAp particle sizes exhibited a roughly normal distribution, with average particle sizes of 13.8 μm, 31.6 μm, 54.9 μm, 84.4 μm and 140.2 μm, respectively. Fig. 1D displayed the surface morphology of the lyophilized composites containing HAp particles of varying sizes. Spherical HAp particles were uniformly dispersed within the reticulated Col l matrix. The surface of lyophilized composites exhibited abundant interconnected pores, with smaller HAp particle sizes resulting in more pronounced pore interconnectivity. Fig. 1E showed alizarin red S-stained images of HAp/Col l composite with different particle sizes, where calcium ions chelated with the staining agent, resulting in a pink coloration. Groups with smaller HAp particles demonstrated relatively inhomogeneous dispersion and a tendency to form vacuoles. Fig. 1F presented the push-out force-displacement curves for each composite group when extruded from a 1 mL syringe, while Fig. 1G provided statistical analysis of the maximum loading force at stabilization. The results indicated that the curves for the 0–25 μm, 25–40 μm, and 40–70 μm groups exhibited smooth profiles with similar trends. During the initial period, the loading force increased rapidly to their respective maximum values, all below 5 N, followed by stabilization until the end of the experiment. In contrast, the 70–100 μm and 100–200 μm groups displayed a sawtooth-like sharp increases in push-out force, reaching tens of Newtons as displacement increased. These curves lacked a stable phase, and the extrusion force during the middle and late stages exceeded 20 N. Fig. 1H illustrated the rheo-mechanical curves of the composites, reflecting viscosity values at continuously varying shear rates. It was found that the five composites exhibited distinct initial viscosities, with smaller particle sizes correlating to higher initial viscosities. As the shear rate increased, viscosity progressively decreased, demonstrating typical shear-thinning behavior characteristic of non-Newtonian fluids. The influence on cell behavior and phenotype

The cytotoxicity and biocompatibility of HAp/Col I composite was systematically evaluated by co-culturing with cells, with calcium phosphate ceramics and collagen type I hydrogel serving as controls. As depicted in Fig. 2C, extracts of HAp/Col l composite at concentrations of 100 %, 50 %, 25 %, and 12.5 % demonstrated cell survival rates exceeding 70 % after 24h of co-culture with L929 cells, which was classified as non-cytotoxic according to ISO 10993-5 standard. During the co-culture period, MC3T3-E1 cells exhibited robust spreading on Col l, PC, and HAp/Col l surfaces (Fig. 2A). From day 3 to day 14, continuous cell proliferation was observed across all groups, as confirmed by the CCK-8 assay results presented in Fig. 2D. The cellular spreading on the material surfaces was observed via SEM imaging as shown in Fig. 2B, and the individual cell spreading areas were quantified and presented in Fig. 2E. These results revealed a gradual increase in cell number and spreading area over the 14-day period. However, no significant differences were observed among the groups. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis results of five key genes associated with bone regeneration, including ALP, BMP2, RUNX2, OCN, and VEGF were provided in Fig. 2F–G. The expression levels of all observed genes showed an increasing trend with extended culture time, although the onset of significant up-regulation varied among genes. Notably, ALP expression was significantly up-regulated earlier than other genes. At day 14, the expression levels of ALP, RUNX2 and BMP2 in the HAp/Col I and PC groups were significantly higher than those in the Col I group, while no significant differences observed between the HAp/Col I and PC groups. Postoperative quality assessment of joints and intra-articular ligaments

The in vivo experimental procedures were shown in Fig. 3A–C. Following the sacrifice of the beagles, the reconstructed joints were evaluated using the anterior drawer test (Fig. 3D). A success rate of 100 % was observed in both treated and control groups, indicating a high efficacy of the reconstructive surgery. The general view of the joint cavity following the dissection was presented in Fig. 3F. In the native group, the ligament surface appeared a smooth and rounded morphology, whereas the grafts in treated and control groups exhibited a more elongated structure, with some hyperplastic-like tissue evident on their surfaces. After further desiccation of the ligaments or grafts within the joint cavity, the surface morphology was examined and presented in Fig. 3G. Both native ligaments and retrieved grafts displayed characteristic aligned fiber bundles. Specifically, in the native group, these fiber bundles were regular and uniformly arranged, and primarily consisting of nanoscale fibers that aggregated into secondary fiber bundles approximately 20 μm in diameter, which subsequently formed larger tertiary fiber bundles. The grafts in the treated group showed structural resemblance to the native ligament, exhibiting a multi-scale fiber architecture at 1 M post-implantation. However, the nanofiber organization was less mature compared to that of the native group. At 3M, the diameter of the fiber bundles in the treated group decreased, resulting in a relatively sparse arrangement. By 6M, the grafts transitioned back into secondary fiber bundles comparable to those observed at 1M. At 12M, clearly defined secondary fiber bundles were observed in the grafts, with diameters ranging from 10 to 20 μm, indicating advanced matrix remodeling and maturation. In comparison, the fiber structures of grafts in the control group consistently exhibited laxity throughout the entire observation period, reflecting a low level of tissue maturity. At 1M post-procedure, ligament bundles in the control group measured approximately 10 μm in diameter and were significantly sparser than those observed in the native group. The biomechanical properties of reconstructed ligaments

The efficacy of reconstructive surgery was assessed macroscopically by evaluating the resistance of the reconstructed ligament during pull-off experiments under non-restrained conditions. Following the biomechanical testing, common sites of ligament rupture were identified and were illustrated in Fig. 4A: the mid-portion of the ligament within the intra-articular segment and the enthesis adjacent to the tibial plateau. Effect of the new bone strength in the tunnel at the microstructural level

To evaluate the degree of mineralization of newly formed bone within the bone tunnel, longitudinal tissue sections along the axis of the bone tunnel were prepared for evaluation of the nanomechanical properties using nanoindentation (Fig. 4G–S). The shape of the nanoindentation curve and the slope of the force unloading segment corresponded to the hardness and Young's modulus at the measurement site, thereby reflecting the degree of mineralization or the distribution of other components in the newly formed bone. Fig. 4G illustrated the sample preparation, testing sites selection, and test procedures. Fig. 4P and Q showed the representative force-displacement curves from the control and experimental groups at various time points. Further analysis revealed that, in terms of Young's modulus, the treated group exhibited significantly higher values at 3M, 6M, and 12M compared to the control group (Fig. 4R). At 1M and 3M, no significant difference in hardness was observed between the experimental and control groups; however, statistically significant differences emerged at 6M and 12M, with the treated group demonstrating greater stiffness (Fig. 4S). These findings suggested that the material exerted a more pronounced effect on bone development during later stages of the healing process (after 3M or 6M), leading to a substantial increase in the hardness and elastic modulus of the new bone at the interface.

Furthermore, we systematically compiled and analyzed the progression of mechanical properties of newly formed bone at distances of 20 μm, 40 μm, and 60 μm from the interface. Distance was found to significantly influence Young's modulus in both the treated and control groups, with the highest Young's modulus observed for new bone located 60 μm from the interface. Additionally, compared to the control group, the treated group exhibited higher Young's modulus in tissues situated further from the interface (Fig. 4H–K). Regarding stiffness, although significant differences were observed among different distances within each group, no overall significant difference was observed between the treated and control groups (Fig. 4L–O). These findings suggested that the Young's modulus and hardness of newly formed bone were more closely correlated with location relative to the interface. Furthermore, material addition appeared to have a greater impact on the Young's modulus than on hardness, potentially due to structural characteristics at the interface.

Discussion

The regeneration of the tendon-to-bone interface is crucial for successful ACL reconstruction. The native, uninjured tendon-to-bone interface exhibits significant structural and compositional heterogeneity [4,6]. This gradient structure facilitates efficient force transmission and dissipation [36]. However, in reconstructed bone tunnels, the connection between the graft and host tissue is frequently mediated by fibrosis scar tissue, which compromises postoperative outcomes [37,38]. Therefore, enhancing the local microenvironment during the early healing phase and restoring both the functional and structural integrity of the tendon-to-bone interface are of paramount importance.

Collagen type I is a biodegradable and biocompatible natural polymer that provides numerous adhesion sites and microenvironments, which facilitate cell differentiation and functional expression. As a result, it is widely utilized in tissue engineering. Due to its favorable biological properties, Col I serves as an ideal material for scaffold fabrication and has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for diverse clinical applications. Hydroxyapatite, the primary mineral component of human bone, possesses excellent bioactivity, degradability, and osteoconductivity. These characteristics support the regeneration of both bone and cartilage, making HAp particularly important in tendon-to-bone healing processes. Moreover, HAp does not induce local or systemic toxicity, inflammation, or foreign body reactions, thereby demonstrating high biocompatibility and suitability for bone repair applications [39]. Research has confirmed that the organized arrangement of collagen plays a critical role in tissue regeneration [40], and HAp has been shown to activate fibroblasts, thereby promoting collagen formation [[41], [42], [43]]. Therefore, composites incorporating HAp and Col l hold great promise for enhancing tendon-to-bone healing.

In this study, we prepared an injectable HAp/Col l composite. The compositional structure, stability and injectability of the HAp/Col l composite were systematically screened and characterized. SEM imaging revealed no obvious morphological alternations in the spherical HAp particles before and after composite formation. Each group of HAp particles was uniformly dispersed within the collagen network, ensuring homogeneity and stability of the system. Alizarin red S staining revealed that hydrogels containing smaller HAp particles exhibited a higher number of vacuoles. This phenomenon may be attributed to the larger specific surface area associated with smaller HAp particles, which necessitates greater liquid infiltration and complicates mixing with the carrier matrix. Consequently, this leads to reduced fluidity and increased bubble entrapment, both of which hinder system stabilization. Injection force testing and rheological analysis are critical parameters for assessing the injectability and manipulability of the composites. It was found that the extrusion force of injectables containing HAp particles within the size range of 70–200 μm increased sharply in a fluctuating pattern with displacement, reaching tens of mN. This behavior is primarily caused by the accumulation and blockage of larger HAp particles within the needle lumen, resulting in unstable push-out force fluctuations and eventual injection failure. Therefore, injectable hydrogels incorporating HAp particles within the size range of 40–70 μm demonstrate superior potential for practical application.

Col I, hydroxyapatite ceramic sheets and HAp/Col I composites were respectively co-cultured with MC3T3-E1 cells. As expected, HAp/Col I composite demonstrated excellent cytocompatibility in vitro, exhibiting bone-enhancing properties comparable to those of HAp ceramic sheets and cell-adhesive capacities similar to those of natural collagen. Furthermore, the HAp/Col I composite functioned as a microenvironmental scaffold, effectively promoting the proliferation and mineralization of host osteoblasts and mesenchymal stem cells.

The ACL reconstruction model in beagle dogs was successfully established, as confirmed by the drawer test [44]. Throughout the experimental period, the reconstructed ligaments remained structurally intact without signs of rupture or resorption. However, they did not fully replicate the structural integrity and natural luster observed in native ligaments. In the treated group, the reconstructed ligaments exhibited well-aligned tissue organization, which may be attributed to the enhanced mass transport between the graft and surrounding bone tissue. In contrast, the control group displayed a more disorganized structure, along with indications of partial degradation and resorption.

The resistance of the reconstructed graft to pull-off serves as a critical indicator for evaluating the repair of the tendon-to-bone interface and the structure integrity of the graft itself [45,46]. According to the results of in vivo study, the Young's modulus of the anterior cruciate ligament exhibited a gradual decrease within the first 3M post-operation. Following this initial period, the treated group demonstrated a progressive increase in Young's modulus, while no significant trend was observed in the control group. These findings suggested that the application of HAp/Col l composites enhances mass transport at the tendon-to-bone interface, promoting the transition of the graft from degradation to regeneration. Consequently, the overall quality of the grafts in the treated group showed significant improvements compared to the control group after 6M.

The repair rate of the artificial bone tunnel is a critical determinant in the success of ACL reconstruction [47]. Bone tunnels typically exhibit delayed healing, and the “wiper effect” is frequently observed following ACL reconstruction. This phenomenon arises when the graft repeatedly rubs against the bone tunnel over time, potentially leading to graft loosening, rupture, and ultimately surgical failure. Therefore, the rate of bone tunnel healing and the extent of new bone formation within the tunnel are pivotal factors influencing surgical outcomes [48,49]. In this study, micro-CT was employed to characterize the internal condition of the bone tunnel. A significant increase in new bone volume was detected at 1M post-treatment in the experimental group compared to the control group. However, due to the potential interference from undegraded HAp in the HAp/Col l composite during the early stages, no conclusive findings could be made at this time point. When combined with the histological analysis of hard tissue sections, as presented in Fig. 7A, it was observed that HAp particles within the material were almost completely degraded by 3M. Therefore, between 3M and 12M post-treatment, the new bone volume in the bone tunnels of the experimental group was significantly greater than that of the control group. Moreover, the rate of bone tunnel contraction was markedly greater in the experimental group. These findings suggested that HAp/Col l composite played a substantial role in promoting bone tunnel maturation and increasing new bone formation. Additionally, it was noted that the a-end of the bone tunnel in the control group exhibited larger dimensions and irregular morphology, whereas the experimental group demonstrated a more uniform and structured appearance. This suggested that the HAp/Col l composite provided early graft fixation, mitigated the detrimental effects of the “wiper effect”, and facilitated rapid interface reconstruction and bone tunnel healing.

Further, we investigated the newly formed bone mass, calcium distribution and histological composition of the tendon-to-bone interface from three perspectives: biomechanical, imaging and histological. Specifically, nanoindentation was employed to evaluate the qualities of newly formed bone tissue. This technique allows for the assessment of microscopic elastic modulus and stiffness under high-resolution displacement and loading conditions [48]. The elastic modulus of the matrix correlates with the collagen content, whereas hardness reflects the degree of mineralization [4]. From a temporal perspective, at 1M, there was minimal difference in the Young's modulus of the new bone between groups; however, within the range of 3M–12M, the treated group generally exhibited a higher Young's modulus compared to the control group. In terms of hardness, the treated group demonstrated superior values over the control group between 6M and 12M. These findings indicated that HAp/Col l promoted both collagen and calcium enrichment during tendon-to-bone interface repair, with collagen accumulation preceding mineralization. This temporal discrepancy may subsequently contribute to the development of the fibrocartilage layer or non-calcified bone at the interface.

The distribution of calcium at the tendon-to-bone interface was further analyzed using Raman confocal spectroscopy. This technique enables the visualization of chemical composition and spatial distribution on tissue surface at both micro- and nano-scale levels. A topographical analysis was conducted to evaluate the distribution of phosphate groups across the reconstructed interface. At 1M postoperatively, the phosphate distribution was relatively narrow, albeit influenced by the implanted HAp/Col l composite. From 3M to 12M postoperatively, the distribution gradually broadened, showing a significantly wider spread in the treated group compared to the control group. The expanded phosphate distribution is beneficial for the reconstruction of the tendon-to-bone interface, as it facilitates compositional and structural integration, effectively mitigates stress concentration during mechanical loading, and indicates superior bone-graft integration [50,51].

Histological staining remains the gold standard for evaluating the osteogenic efficacy of bone implants. Histological examination of undecalcified bone sections showed that HAp/Col I composite penetrated approximately 200 μm into the cancellous bone following implantation. Spherical HA particles were actively engaged in the remodeling process at the interface, while immature Sharpey's fibers were simultaneously observed anchoring toward the newly formed bone. These findings indicated that HAp/Col l not only fulfilled a structural role in bridging the gap between host bone and the graft during tissue interface remodeling, but also contributed to the reconstruction of the local microenvironment, thereby promoting bone remodeling, enhancing tendon cell recruitment, and promoting the formation of Sharpey's fibers. By 24 weeks post-implantation, the tendon-to-bone interface appeared a distinct four-layered architecture closely resembling that of native bone [6].

A key finding of this study is the success of our "functional biomimicry" strategy. As stated in the introduction, the injectable composite does not possess a pre-formed gradient structure upon implantation. However, long-term in vivo results provide compelling evidence that it successfully orchestrates a complex, biomimetic healing response. The regeneration of critical histological features, such as Sharpey's fibers and a native-like four-layer architecture, coupled with the development of a gradual mineral gradient confirmed by Raman spectroscopy, validates the central hypothesis: a compositionally biomimetic material can functionally induce the formation of a structurally and compositionally sophisticated tissue in vivo.

Conclusion

In summary, by optimizing the ratio of collagen and hydroxyapatite, as well as the particle size of hydroxyapatite, a uniformly stable and easy-to-use injectable composite was successfully developed. This material actively contributed to reconstructing the regenerative microenvironment during the early stages of tendon-to-bone healing, thereby promoting the regeneration of Sharpey's fibers and bone tissue. It promptly facilitated the timely contraction of artificial bone tunnels, reducing the potential risk associated with the windshield-wiper effect. Moreover, it modulated the distribution of mineral components at the interface, achieving an expanded gradient distribution area, which enhanced the tensile detachment resistance of the reconstructed ligament. This injectable composite not only effectively promoted the reconstruction of the regenerative microenvironment but also provided critical support for tendon-to-bone healing. Its safety, stability, and convenience of use made it highly promising for broad clinical applications.
