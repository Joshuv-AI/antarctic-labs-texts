Late-Onset Upper Eyelid Edema Following Temporal Hollow Correction With Hyaluronic Acid
Authors: Charbel El Feghaly, Pierre Tawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Hyaluronic acid (HA) fillers are widely used for facial rejuvenation, offering favorable safety outcomes. Although early-onset complications such as lower eyelid edema and tear trough Tyndall effect are well documented, late-onset manifestations—particularly upper eyelid edema occurring more than 6 months postinjection—remain poorly understood and often misdiagnosed. We report the case of a 51-year-old woman who presented with isolated, persistent, bilateral upper eyelid swelling. Imaging and laboratory workup, including thyroid function tests and orbital magnetic resonance imaging, were unremarkable. Given the inconclusive findings, upper blepharoplasty was performed for both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes. Histopathologic analysis revealed dermal basophilic deposits consistent with HA, with no inflammatory reaction. Only after surgery did the patient recall undergoing temporal filler injections 2 years earlier. This case illustrated the resilience and migration potential of HA fillers—especially when injected into avascular gliding planes such as the temporal region—and the capacity for remote complications. Unlike other documented cases involving direct periorbital injection and successful hyaluronidase resolution, our case required surgical intervention due to delayed diagnosis. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for HA-related complications, even when symptoms manifest far from the typical injection site and long after the procedure. Thorough aesthetic history-taking is essential to avoid misdiagnoses such as thyroid orbitopathy or Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of chronic upper eyelid edema secondary to temporal HA filler injection, and we advocate for greater awareness of this presentation within the aesthetic and ophthalmologic communities.

Hyaluronic acid (HA) fillers have become the cornerstone of nonsurgical facial rejuvenation due to their relative safety, reversibility, and biocompatibility.1 Although early adverse effects are well recognized and typically short-lived, there is growing awareness of late-onset complications that may occur months or even years after injection.2 Among these, chronic periorbital edema is rare, particularly when the site of swelling is anatomically distant from the injection point.

Despite manufacturer claims suggesting that HA fillers degrade within 9–12 months, recent reports challenge this timeline and reveal that HA can persist much longer.3,4 Furthermore, HA can migrate from its original site of placement, complicating both diagnosis and treatment.

This case report presents a rare instance of isolated, bilateral, chronic upper eyelid edema following temporal hollowing correction with HA filler—without any direct periorbital injection. The case illustrates the potential for HA migration, its resilience against degradation, and the diagnostic challenges it posed.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 51-year-old woman, with no significant medical history, presented to the plastic surgery department of our institution with a 1-year history of persistent, gradually worsening bilateral swelling of the upper eyelids. The edema, fusiform in shape and localized to the upper lids, had initially led to a presumed diagnosis of dermatochalasis (Fig. 1). The patient was referred for surgical evaluation.

Preoperative photograph of a 51-year-old patient presenting with persistent eyelid edema.

Clinical examination was unremarkable, aside from the bilateral upper eyelid swelling. No systemic symptoms or signs of thyroid disease or inflammatory pathology were observed. Given the atypical presentation, the patient underwent ophthalmologic assessment. Thyroid function tests and orbital magnetic resonance imaging were performed to investigate differential diagnoses, including thyroid eye disease and Morbihan syndrome. Imaging revealed superficial eyelid edema without evidence of deep orbital pathology, and laboratory results were within normal limits.

Although the absence of orbital involvement confirmed that surgery could be performed safely, the negative magnetic resonance imaging findings did not contribute to establishing a definitive diagnosis. This lack of diagnostic clarification ultimately prompted the decision to proceed with surgical excision, both for therapeutic purposes and to obtain tissue for histopathologic analysis, which was essential for reaching the final diagnosis.

No gross abnormalities were observed during the blepharoplasty. However, histopathologic analysis of the excised tissue revealed dermal basophilic deposits dissociating collagen fibers, without inflammation—findings consistent with residual HA filler. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays Alcian blue [A] and hematoxylin–eosin–saffron [B] colorations showing clear signs of HA deposits, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E760.)

When asked again postoperatively, the patient reaffirmed that she had never received periorbital filler injections but had routinely undergone botulinum toxin treatments. Upon more detailed inquiry, she recalled receiving HA filler injections for temporal hollowing 2 years prior, using a fanning technique. Her aesthetic practitioner confirmed that the only HA filler administered to her face was in the temples, using the fanning technique with Juvederm Ultra 4 (24 mg/mL cross-linked HA with 0.3% lidocaine; Allergan, an AbbVie company, Pringy, France; G′: ~ 102.21 ± 11.46 Pa and G″: ~ 29.81 ± 3.59 Pa at 0.1 Hz and 37°C).5

At 6 months postsurgery, the patient was pleased with the aesthetic results. Although minor edema persisted on the left upper eyelid, her symptoms had significantly improved, and no recurrence was observed (Fig. 2). The patient gave her full informed consent for the use of her medical history and clinical photographs for the purpose of this publication.

DISCUSSION

Late-onset upper eyelid edema following HA filler treatment is a rarely documented phenomenon, particularly when the swelling is bilateral and remote from the injection site. The time lag between injection and symptom onset often misleads clinicians to consider alternative diagnoses such as thyroid eye disease, autoimmune conditions, or idiopathic lymphedema syndromes, including Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome or Morbihan disease.6 As in this case, the diagnostic process may become unnecessarily prolonged or invasive if the link to prior filler treatment is overlooked.

In 2020, the study titled “Late-onset upper eyelid and brow edema as a long-term complication of hyaluronic acid filler injection” presented the largest series to date of chronic upper eyelid edema linked to HA fillers, documenting 17 such cases.2 A consistent pattern across all patients was the delayed onset of swelling, with all injections administered in the periorbital region—including the upper eyelids, brow, and glabella. These cases were typically associated with supraperiosteal filler placement and resolved rapidly with hyaluronidase injections.

HA fillers are typically believed to degrade within 1 year. However, many products, such as those in the Juvederm family, are cross-linked to increase resistance to enzymatic degradation and often exhibit a relatively high elastic modulus (G′ = 164 Pa for Juvederm Ultra 4), features that can extend in-tissue persistence beyond the nominal duration stated by manufacturers.7 Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the filler can persist for years when placed in avascular planes—such as the interfascial temporal space—where reduced vascularity and lymphatic clearance limit enzymatic degradation.8

HA migration patterns within tissue seem to be governed by cohesion, viscosity, and fragmentation. Indeed, partially degraded or less cohesive fractions may seep along native connective tissue planes under the influence of gravity, muscle action, and local tissue laxity, whereas more cohesive or stiff gels may be displaced “en bloc” along planes of least resistance.9

Two previous reports document similar chronic periorbital edema linked to prior HA filler use. In 1 case, a 42-year-old woman experienced unilateral left upper eyelid swelling 7 years after receiving HA in the superior sulcus.3 In another, a 54-year-old woman developed bilateral upper eyelid edema 6 years after receiving HA in the brow.4 Both cases resolved rapidly following targeted hyaluronidase injections. In contrast, our case required surgical intervention to obtain a diagnosis, as neither patient history nor imaging initially suggested a filler-related complication (Table 1).

Uniquely, our patient had no history of periorbital injection, and the edema occurred at a site distant from the filler placement. The presence of HA in the upper eyelid—confirmed histologically—demonstrates the product’s ability to migrate through facial planes and remain undetected for extended periods. This migration phenomenon should raise awareness among practitioners to include prior filler injections in the patient’s aesthetic or medical history, regardless of the time elapsed or injection site.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlighted the rare but significant complication of delayed upper eyelid edema following HA filler injection for temporal hollowing. It revealed the surprising ability of HA to migrate from remote injection sites to periorbital tissues and to persist for years without degradation. This can be directly linked to the rheological and physicochemical properties of cross-linked HA fillers. Failure to recognize this possibility can lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment, such as unnecessary surgery or immunosuppressive therapy.

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of upper eyelid edema secondary to HA migration following a temporal fanning injection, confirmed histologically after blepharoplasty. We urge the medical and scientific community to recognize this underreported complication and include filler history in the differential diagnosis of chronic eyelid edema. Prior HA injection must be systematically ruled out through careful anamnesis before diagnosing potentially impactful conditions such as thyroid orbitopathy or Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome, or before proceeding with surgical treatment.
