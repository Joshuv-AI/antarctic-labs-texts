Mussel-inspired adhesive hydrogel reprograms osteoimmune niche for robust bone regeneration in complex defects
Authors: Zhaoquan Liang, Qili Sun, Haotian Gao, Da Song, Huicheng Cao, Zihang Liu, Wanyan Hu, Jingle Chen, Yucong Li, Bingsheng Yang, Chao Xie, Bin Tang, Lijun Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplex bone defects with irregular geometry, interfacial micro-motion and compromised immune microenvironment pose a formidable clinical challenge. Ideal bone repair materials should simultaneously achieve robust interfacial integration and actively regulate the osteoimmunological milieu. Herein, we developed an injectable and photo-curable double-network hydrogel, termed DPG@MA, based on mussel-inspired chemistry and a nanocomposite strategy. The hydrogel features a dynamically cross-linked network formed by a 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA)-pectin conjugate (DP) and gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), enabling stable and conformal adhesion to irregular bone defects. Furthermore, the incorporation of alendronate-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MA) confers the hydrogel with the capacity for sustained co-release of silicon ions and alendronate, effectively steering macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward the pro-healing M2 phenotype. This actively reshapes the immune microenvironment into one conducive to bone regeneration. In rat calvarial and tibial defect models, DPG@MA significantly enhanced bone regeneration and osseointegration while mitigating complications, outperforming the clinical standard Novabone®. This study proposes a novel strategy for designing bone repair materials with integrated “interfacial adhesion-immunomodulation-osteogenesis” functionality, offering a promising therapeutic avenue for complex bone defects with challenging healing barriers.Keywords: Bone defect repair, Multifunctional integrated hydrogel, Bone adhesion, Immunomodulation, Osteogenesis

Introduction

The repair of bone defects, frequently resulting from trauma, tumor resection, infection and congenital deformities, remains a pivotal challenge in orthopedics. This is particularly true for complex defects, where irregular anatomy, interfacial instability and the potential coexistence of chronic inflammation collectively pose a significant challenge. According to a 2017 specialized report, over 20 million patients worldwide annually suffer from bone tissue loss due to congenital conditions, trauma, or disease, with this number progressively increasing owing to factors such as an aging population and increased high-energy trauma [1]. The success of repair directly determines limb function and long-term prognosis, where inadequate outcomes often lead to chronic pain, joint dysfunction, or even disability [2]. Pathologically, local mechanical instability after bone defect formation impedes cell migration and growth factor enrichment, while trauma-induced chronic inflammation disrupts the bone resorption-formation equilibrium, failing natural repair processes [[3], [4], [5]]. Although current clinical approaches-such as bone grafts and synthetic filler materials-can partially restore bone structure, they face notable limitations: the susceptibility of materials to displacement and loosening in complex anatomical environments directly compromises the mechanical stability of the repair interface; the inability to actively regulate the inflammatory microenvironment hinders the immune balance essential for bone healing; and the potential to induce heterotopic ossification introduces new complications. Collectively, these factors render existing solutions inadequate in meeting the comprehensive clinical requirements for minimal invasiveness, high therapeutic efficacy, and low complication rates in complex bone defect repair [6]. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel bone repair materials that are compatible with complex defects and integrate both stability and biofunctional performance.

To address the clinical challenges in bone defect repair, researchers have conducted extensive studies on bone repair materials and achieved notable progress [7,8]. In terms of material types, the field has evolved from traditional autografts and allografts to synthetic inorganic non-metallic materials (e.g., hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate), polymers (e.g., polylactic acid, chitosan) and composites [9,10]. More importantly, in terms of functional design concepts, the research focus has long gone beyond simple defect filling and is increasingly integrating multi-disciplinary cross-strategies, dedicated to developing comprehensive solutions with bone induction, bone conduction and biological activity, such as smart materials integrating biological factor controlled-release systems [11,12]. However, significant limitations remain. Most bone repair materials lack adequate tissue adhesion, resulting in poor integration with the host bone, compromised regeneration efficiency and risks of local tissue irritation due to material displacement [13,14]. Moreover, existing materials show limited ability to modulate the osteoimmune microenvironment, failing to balance local inflammatory responses. Excessive inflammation drives macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, releasing cytokines that suppress osteogenesis [3,15]. Furthermore, clinically used bone fillers such as Novabone®, despite offering certain osteoinductive and osseointegrative properties, often lack bone-adhesive capability, leading to migration in complex defect sites, inflammation, adjacent tissue damage, or even heterotopic ossification, which considerably restricts their clinical utility [16,17]. These shortcomings highlight the need for materials that simultaneously overcome the bottlenecks of adhesion, immunomodulation and complication control to meet clinical demands.

Adhesive hydrogels have emerged as ideal candidates for complex bone defect repair owing to their wet adhesion, injectability and biocompatibility [18]. Various adhesive components can be utilized to construct such hydrogels, among which mussel-inspired strategies have gained considerable attention in bioengineering for providing universal and robust wet adhesion [[19], [20], [21]]. The core of this approach involves the incorporation of catechol groups, such as DOPA, dopamine and their structural analogs. The catechol moieties enable strong binding to moist biological interfaces by forming covalent and non-covalent interactions with diverse functional groups on tissue surfaces [22].

Pectin, a natural polysaccharide, exhibits dual osteogenic and anti-inflammatory activities. Studies have demonstrated that it promotes osteoblast differentiation and mineralization by modulating the Ca2+/CaMKII and BMP-SMAD1/5 signaling pathways [[23], [24], [25]], while also mitigating inflammatory responses via blockade of the TLR2-TLR1 pathway in macrophages [26,27]. Furthermore, the abundant carboxyl groups along its galacturonic acid backbone facilitate functionalization, making pectin an ideal substrate for grafting DOPA moieties to confer mussel-inspired adhesive properties [28]. In this study, we synthesized a DOPA-pectin conjugate (DP) by grafting DOPA onto the pectin chain, establishing a foundation for constructing an adhesive hydrogel network. However, the cohesive strength of DP, a single-network material, is often insufficient to withstand the complex physiological environment and mechanical loads in vivo [29,30].

To address the mechanical limitations of a single soft network, we adopted a double-network strategy by incorporating gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) as a rigid network capable of rapid ultraviolet (UV)-initiated crosslinking [31,32]. GelMA effectively enhances the overall cohesive strength and structural stability of the hydrogel [33]. Through component ratio optimization, the resulting DPG double-network hydrogel, which is composed of DP and GelMA, retains the interfacial adhesion and bioactivity of the soft network while acquiring the reinforced mechanical properties and photo-curing advantages of the rigid network.

To synergistically enhance both the mechanical properties and biological functionality of the hydrogel, this study introduces alendronate (ALN)-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MA). Beyond serving as passive fillers, the strategic incorporation of functional nanoparticles has emerged as a frontier in engineering advanced polymer networks, enabling synergistic enhancement of both structural and biological properties [34,35]. The incorporation of nanoparticles reinforces the network structure by serving as dynamic cross-linking sites through multiple physical interactions-such as hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces-with polymer chains [36,37]. Under mechanical stress, these nanoparticles act as "sacrificial bonds" that dissipate energy through preferential breakage, thereby suppressing crack propagation and improving both toughness and adhesive stability [38]. Beyond the benefits of plain nanoparticles, MA offers additional functional advantages: its mesoporous architecture enables the controlled and sustained release of ALN, which holds greater potential for clinical translation than protein-based factors such as bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) due to its intrinsic chemical stability and ease of storage [39]. Studies have reported that the combined action of ALN and silica effectively promotes osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), suppresses osteoclast activity and modulates macrophage polarization [[40], [41], [42]]. Therefore, incorporating MA into the DPG hydrogel system is expected to yield synergistic improvements in mechanical reinforcement and functional regulation.

Herein, we successfully developed an injectable and photo-curable bone-adhesive hydrogel (DPG@MA) for the repair of complex bone defects (Fig. 1). This integrated hydrogel comprises a mussel-inspired adhesive network, a photo-crosslinkable rigid network and functional nanocomponents, synergistically endowing it with wet tissue adhesion and multiple bioactivities. The double-network design based on DP and GelMA ensures robust in situ stability of the hydrogel at the defect site, while the incorporation of MA nanoparticles further enhances the toughness and adhesive durability of the network. More importantly, owing to the sustained co-delivery of ALN and silicon ions from the MA nanoparticles, the hydrogel system enables sustained regulation of the local microenvironment toward an "osteogenic-anti-inflammatory" state: it directly promotes osteogenesis and suppresses osteoclast activity via ALN and silicon ions, while simultaneously steering macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype to reshape a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment. The high efficacy of DPG@MA in promoting bone defect repair was systematically validated in a rat calvarial defect model and its therapeutic performance was further compared with Novabone in a rat tibial defect model. This study provides a novel integrated adhesive hydrogel strategy to address the critical issues of "material displacement, uncontrolled inflammation and inadequate osteogenesis" that often lead to delayed healing or failure in complex bone defects.

Results

Fabrication and Characterization of MA

Synthesis and Structural Characterization of MA

This section focused on the preparation of MA nanoparticles for the delivery of ALN. The pristine MSN were first surface-modified with polydopamine (PDA) to obtain the intermediate MSN-PDA. The PDA layer acts as a bridge between the MSN and ALN, facilitating more stable incorporation of ALN into the MSN framework. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the typical stellate mesoporous structure of the synthesized MA (Fig. 2B). Dynamic light scattering analysis (Fig. 2C) revealed an average particle size of 105.7 nm for MA, which is larger than the 80 nm observed for plain MSN. Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 2D) indicated an increase from -28.0 ± 1.37 mV for MSN to -23.7 ± 1.05 mV for MSN-PDA. After ALN loading, the Zeta potential of MA decreased to -35.5 ± 0.53 mV. This evolution in Zeta potential reflects changes in surface charge characteristics and suggests improved dispersibility of MA in aqueous medium, which is crucial for its subsequent incorporation into hydrogels.

Analysis of the chemical composition of MA by Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy showed no distinct spectral differences among MSN, MSN-PDA and MA (Fig. S1). To accurately verify the chemical composition, particularly the elemental valence states, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed. The XPS survey spectrum confirmed the presence of C, O, N, Si and Na elements (Fig. 2E). Comparison of the high-resolution XPS spectra of MA and MSN-PDA (Figs. 2F-G) revealed a characteristic P2p peak in the binding energy range of 130-134 eV exclusively for MA, indicating successful ALN loading into the MSN. For the C 1s spectrum (Fig. 2H), deconvolution yielded four sub-peaks at 283.6 eV (C–C), 285.4 eV (C–O/C=N), 288.1 eV (C=O) and 291.4 eV (π→π∗ shake-up satellite peak). The π→π∗ shake-up satellite peak corresponded to the aromatic conjugated skeleton, while the presence of C=O and C–O bonds indicated the existence of quinone-phenol tautomers on the material surface [43]. These surface functional groups could form multiple interactions (i.e., coordination bonds, hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions) with the –NH2/–PO32- groups of ALN [44], thereby providing active sites for efficient drug loading. Additionally, the binding energy of N 1s (Fig. 2I) exhibited a negative shift from 399.48 eV (in MSN-PDA) to 398.98 eV (in MA). This shift is characteristic of the Schiff base reaction, which corresponds to the conversion of primary amines (-NH2) to imines (-C=N-) [45]. Collectively, these characterization results conclusively confirm the successful preparation of MA.

Thermal Stability and Drug Loading Analysis of MA

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) revealed distinct thermal degradation profiles (Fig. S2). Based on the residual mass difference, the ALN loading was estimated to be approximately 1.48%.

To more accurately determine the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of MA, a ferric ion complex-based spectrophotometric method was employed. The Fe (III)-ALN complex exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at 343 nm, and a linear relationship (R2 = 0.9905) was established between absorbance and ALN concentration in the range of 50-800 μg/mL (Fig. S3). Analysis of the MA supernatant after centrifugation yielded an encapsulation efficiency of 16.7% and a drug loading capacity of 1.67%, values consistent with the TGA estimation, thereby confirming the reliability of the data.

Preparation and Properties of the DPG@MA Composite Hydrogel

Synthesis and chemical characterization of DP

The DP conjugate was synthesized by grafting DOPA onto pectin via an amidation reaction. FTIR analysis (Fig. 3B) provided the first evidence of successful conjugation: a new amide II band emerged at 1523 cm-1 (N–H bending/C–N stretching), the amide I band intensified at 1608 cm-1 and the carboxyl signal at 1710 cm-1 concurrently weakened, indicating consumption of pectin carboxyl groups due to the amidation reaction and the concomitant formation of the amide I band (C=O stretching vibration) [46]. High-resolution 1H NMR (Fig. S4Fig. S4) corroborates these findings: the dopamine α-CH proton shifts 0.5–0.7 ppm downfield from δ 3.5–3.8 ppm to δ ∼4.2–4.5 ppm upon conjugation, directly evidencing the transformation of –CH(NH2)– into –CH(NHCO)– and confirming covalent DOPA grafting onto the pectin backbone.

Construction and Optimization of the DPG Double Network

Preparation and Stability Assessment of DPG

To meet the clinical need for UV-controllable curing, GelMA was introduced and mixed with DP at 50 °C to form a DPG double network. In a wet environment, pure DP dissolved completely within 2 hours, whereas DPG retained nearly constant residual mass (Figs. 3C-D). This stability is attributed to the significantly enhanced cohesion imparted by the UV-crosslinked GelMA network, which prevented rapid swelling and loss of the DP component. FTIR analysis (Fig. 3F) indicated that the spectrum of DPG represented a simple superposition of the characteristic peaks of DP and GelMA, with no new peaks or peak shifts. Nevertheless, a broad and intensified absorption band appeared in the 3500-3200 cm-1 region, corresponding to O-H/N-H stretching vibrations that were red-shifted and broadened due to hydrogen bonding. Concurrently, the intensity of the C-O bond peak at 1101 cm-1 decreased as a result of altered vibrational environment upon participation in hydrogen bonding, collectively evidencing strong hydrogen-bond interactions between the molecular chains of DP and GelMA.

Rationale for Optimal DPG Formulation Selection

The optimal formulation was determined by evaluating the cancellous bone adhesion performance of DPG hydrogels with varying mass ratios (DP: GelMA = 2:1, 1:1, 1:2) (Fig. 3E). Pure DP exhibited an adhesion strength of up to 80 kPa. As the proportion of GelMA increased, the adhesion strength gradually decreased, which can be attributed to the dilution of catechol adhesive groups from DP by the rigid GelMA network, despite its contribution to improved cohesion. The DPG (2:1) formulation maintained an adhesion strength exceeding 40 kPa while demonstrating excellent cohesion, showing no significant structural disintegration in wet conditions. This formulation thus achieves an optimal balance between interfacial adhesion and bulk cohesion and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments.

Fabrication and Characterization of the DPG@MA Composite Hydrogel

Preparation and Structural Verification of DPG@MA

DPG@MA composite hydrogels were prepared by blending MA with the optimized DPG (2:1) formulation at varying mass ratios. FTIR spectra (Fig. 3F) showed no new peaks in DPG@MA other than the characteristic signals of DP and GelMA. The distinct peak at 1070 cm-1, corresponding to the Si-O-Si framework vibration of MA, was clearly observable, confirming the homogeneous distribution of MA within the DPG network.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis further corroborated these findings (Fig. 3G). DOPA exhibited sharp diffraction peaks, indicative of a typical crystalline structure. DP displayed a broad amorphous halo superimposed with weak crystalline peaks, suggesting partial retention of DOPA's ordered structure. GelMA showed a broad amorphous halo centered at ∼20°, consistent with its non-crystalline nature. The DPG double network exhibited an intense, broad diffuse peak at ∼20°, characteristic of an amorphous structure, with the disappearance of the weak crystalline peaks originally present in DP. This indicates that hydrogen bonding and chain entanglement between DP and GelMA disrupted the weakly ordered structure. MA presented a broad, diffuse "hump" in the 20-30° range, attributed to its amorphous silica framework. The DPG@MA composite similarly showed an intense, broad diffuse peak at ∼20°, while the mesoporous shoulder peak of MA disappeared, suggesting that the MA nanoparticles were effectively encapsulated by the DPG polymer chains. The absence of any new crystalline peaks in all composites confirms excellent physical compatibility among the components without crystallization or phase separation.

Physicochemical Characteristics of DPG@MA

The DPG@MA hydrogel exhibited a series of performance characteristics highly relevant to the clinical application of bone defect repair.

Rheological profiling (Fig. S5AFig. S5A) corroborated that, immediately after UV curing, DPG@MA was a mechanically robust hydrogel. Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA, Fig. S5BFig. S5B) further elucidated the gelation kinetics and the time-dependent evolution of its viscoelastic response, thereby furnishing a comprehensive mechanical signature of the network.

Regarding swelling behavior, a certain degree of swelling is essential for a hydrogel to adapt to irregular defect contours and ensure intimate contact with the host tissue. Swelling experiments (Fig. 3H) revealed that after 48 hours of immersion in simulated body fluid (SBF), the DPG@MA hydrogel reached swelling saturation, with its weight increasing by 91.8% and its volume expanding by 60.0% relative to the initial state. These results demonstrate that DPG@MA possesses suitable swelling properties to meet the filling requirements of irregular bone defects.

The degradation profiles of the hydrogels, as shown in Fig. 3I, differed significantly between pure DPG and DPG@MA. The pure DPG hydrogel degraded relatively rapidly, approaching complete degradation by day 34, which is likely attributable to its looser network structure and inherent degradation mechanism. In contrast, the incorporation of MA nanoparticles into the DPG@MA hydrogel substantially reinforced the internal structure, leading to a markedly slower degradation rate. This observation further indicates that MA nanoparticles effectively enhance the cohesion of the polymer network and improve the structural stability of the hydrogel.

Furthermore, the release profiles of bioactive ions and drugs were characterized. Silicon ion release, detected by ICP-MS (Fig. 3J), showed an initial burst release within the first 14 days, followed by a sustained release plateau. ALN release, measured by ELISA (Fig. 3K), demonstrated a rapid release phase within the first 3 days, transitioning to a slower release rate from days 3 to 21, and a further stabilized release thereafter. These controlled release profiles are crucial for providing sustained osteogenic and anti-inflammatory stimuli during the bone regeneration process.

Furthermore, the DPG@MA hydrogel demonstrates excellent UV-curability (enabling rapid solidification upon irradiation for easy handling and shaping), injectability (adapting to defects of various shapes and locations for convenient delivery) and moldability (conforming to the defect site after implantation) (Fig. 3L), thereby meeting the clinical procedural demands of bone defect repair.

Adhesive Properties of DPG@MA

The adhesive properties of DPG@MA, crucial for its application in bone defect repair, were thoroughly investigated through multiple experiments. Rheological tests (Figs. 4A-B) showed that the viscosity of DPG@MA before UV curing initially increased and then decreased with rising MA content. The initial viscosity enhancement can be attributed to nanoparticles acting as nanoscale "physical cross-linking sites" within the hydrogel network. Under external stress, the non-covalent bonds (hydrogen bonding, electrostatic and van der Waals interactions) between nanoparticles and polymer chains undergo repeated breaking and reformation, effectively dissipating energy and thereby significantly improving cohesive energy [38]. This enhanced cohesion helps prevent internal fracture of the adhesive under mechanical load. However, when the MA content exceeded 5%, nanoparticle aggregation likely occurred beyond the network's accommodation limit, disrupting stress transfer pathways and consequently impairing cohesion [47].

Consistent with the rheological trends, lap shear tests on cancellous bone (Figs. 4C-D) revealed maximum adhesive strength (55.41 ± 2.02 kPa) at 5% MA content. This improvement is not only due to increased viscosity but may also result from nanoparticles forming "Pickering bridges" at the gel-substrate interface, transforming weak point contacts into extensive multi-point interactions that significantly increase the effective contact area [48,49]. Balancing cohesion and interfacial adhesion, DPG@MA with 5% MA was selected for subsequent in vitro and in vivo experiments.

To systematically evaluate interfacial stability under dynamic physiological conditions (Figs. 4E-F), three materials-GelMA, DPG@MA and Novabone-were filled into bovine cancellous bone defects and subjected to continuous shaking in SBF at 37 °C to simulate in vivo motion and fluid flow. After 4 hours, Novabone showed significant detachment, while GelMA and DPG@MA were still fully occupied by the defects. By 24 hours, GelMA began loosening and Novabone had completely detached; only DPG@MA remained intact. After 48 hours, all materials except DPG@MA had fully dislodged. These results indicate that DPG@MA maintains stable integration with the bone defect wall even under sustained fluid shear stress. Furthermore, shear lap tests (Fig. 4G) confirmed that the adhesive strength of DPG@MA significantly exceeded that of both GelMA and Novabone. When compared to commercial gelatin adhesive (Figs. 4H-I), DPG@MA demonstrated a 2.80-fold greater adhesion strength (19.80 ± 1.73 kPa versus 55.41 ± 2.02 kPa). The robust anchoring of DPG@MA under continuous fluid shear originates from its synergistic "cohesion-interface" design (Fig. 4J): UV light rapidly cross-links GelMA with pectin-DOPA into a dual network combining rigidity and flexibility, while mesoporous silica nanoparticles further reinforce the overall network through hydrogen bonds between surface silanol hydroxyl groups and DOPA catechol groups, along with physical entanglement of polymer chains within the pores. Concurrently, in situ curing and moderate swelling enable the hydrogel to fully envelop the micro-rough bone surface, creating mechanical interlocking. This chemical-mechanical dual coupling provides reliable adhesive filling for complex bone defects.

Cytocompatibility and Bioactivity Evaluation of DPG@MA

Cytocompatibility of DPG@MA

To investigate the effect of DPG@MA on BMSC proliferation, cells were treated with concentration gradients (0-400 μg mL-1) for 3 days and assessed via CCK-8 assay (Fig. 5A). No statistically significant difference was observed between the ≤50 μg mL-1 groups and the negative control (NC). However, at concentrations ≥100 μg mL-1, cell proliferation decreased in a concentration- and time-dependent manner, primarily attributed to the inhibitory effect of ALN on BMSC viability at high doses [50,51]. Consequently, a concentration of 50 μg mL-1 was selected for all subsequent functional evaluations to ensure safety.

Cytocompatibility was further evaluated by live/dead staining (Fig. 5B). For BMSCs cultured with extracts of DPG, MA, or DPG@MA for 1 and 3 days, no dead cells were detected, and live cell densities were comparable to the NC group, indicating no cytotoxicity, which is consistent with the results of the CCK8 assay (Fig. S6). In RAW264.7 macrophages, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in cell viability (Fig. 5C) were observed among the DPG, MA and DPG@MA groups compared to NC after 1 and 3 days. Although the average viability in the MA group was slightly lower than NC (95.91% vs. 99.63% at day 1; 99.36% vs. 99.76% at day 3)-likely due to rapid ALN release from direct MA extraction-it remained well within the non-cytotoxic range (viability > 70%) specified by ISO 10993-5:2009 [52]. These results confirm the good cytocompatibility of DPG@MA and its precursors, supporting their safe use in subsequent functional studies.

Hemocompatibility is a crucial prerequisite for bone repair materials to avoid acute hemolysis, thrombosis and inflammatory responses during early implantation. Hemolysis assays (Figs. 5D-E) revealed that DPG@MA hydrogel exhibited hemolysis rates of 2.18 ± 1.67% at 12 h and 2.94 ± 2.12% at 24 h, significantly lower than those of the Novabone group (9.67 ± 0.75% and 34.98 ± 1.22%, respectively; P < 0.0001). Both values remained below the 5% safety threshold stipulated in ISO 10993-4 [52], indicating no acute hemolytic risk. Therefore, DPG@MA demonstrates favorable hemocompatibility, providing an experimental basis for its safe application in the hemorrhagic microenvironment of bone defects.

Anti-inflammatory Activity of DPG@MA

This study investigated the anti-inflammatory activity of the DPG@MA hydrogel using the RAW264.7 murine macrophage line as an in vitro model. Macrophages were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to induce polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, mimicking a pathological inflammatory state. Changes in the expression of inflammatory mediators were quantitatively analyzed using reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) (Figs. 6A-D) and immunofluorescence (Figs. 5E-H).

LPS stimulation successfully triggered M1 polarization, as evidenced by significantly elevated mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory markers inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), along with a marked decrease in the anti-inflammatory marker mannose receptor (CD206). However, co-treatment with extracts from DPG@MA, DPG, or MA substantially reversed these changes, significantly attenuating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and restoring CD206 expression. These findings indicate that all three components effectively suppress M1-associated inflammation and promote a shift toward the M2 phenotype.

Immunofluorescence analysis (Figs. 5E-H) corroborated the RT-PCR results. Cells treated with DPG@MA or its precursors exhibited significantly reduced iNOS protein expression and concomitant upregulation of CD206. Notably, DPG@MA demonstrated superior efficacy in modulating macrophage polarization compared to individual DPG or MA components, underscoring the synergistic anti-inflammatory effect between pectin and silicon ions. These results confirm the ability of DPG@MA to regulate inflammatory factor expression, providing a solid theoretical and experimental foundation for its application in treating complex bone defects compromised by chronic inflammation.

Osteogenic Activity of DPG@MA

To investigate the osteogenic activity of DPG@MA hydrogel at the genetic level, we analyzed the expression of osteogenesis-related genes in BMSCs after 5 days of treatment. Results (Figs. 7A-C) showed that MA, DPG and DPG@MA groups all upregulated osteogenic gene expression-including osteocalcin (OCN), ALP and osteoprotegerin (OPG)-compared to the NC group. Importantly, DPG@MA treatment resulted in significantly higher expression levels of these genes compared to either the MA or DPG groups alone. Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and osteopontin (OPN) protein expression. As shown in Figs. 7D-E, BMSCs treated with DPG@MA exhibited markedly higher levels of both RUNX2 and OPN compared to control groups. Statistical analysis of the fluorescence intensity (Figs. 7H-I) confirmed these observations. These results indicate that DPG@MA effectively enhances the expression of key transcription factors and bone matrix proteins in BMSCs, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation.

To further validate the osteoinductive capacity of DPG@MA, ALP and ARS staining were conducted. After 14 days of induction, the DPG@MA group exhibited substantially darker ALP staining than other groups (Figs. 7F and J), indicating higher ALP activity. Additionally, ARS staining at day 21 (Figs. 7G and K) revealed significantly more reddish-brown mineralized nodules in the DPG, MA and DPG@MA groups compared to the NC group. The results from both ALP and ARS staining further corroborate the osteogenic potential of DPG@MA: intense ALP staining reflects enhanced early osteogenic differentiation, while abundant mineralized nodules indicate advanced matrix mineralization, a late hallmark of osteoblast differentiation and bone formation [53].

In Vivo Evaluation of DPG@MA for Bone Defect Repair

Calvarial Defect Model Experiment

To evaluate the in vivo bone healing efficacy of DPG@MA hydrogel and its components (DPG and MA), a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model was established (Fig. 8A). Animals were randomly divided into five groups: (1) Control (Ctrl) group, implanted with an equal volume of PBS to establish a baseline for natural healing; (2) MA group, receiving MA dispersed in PBS at the same mass/volume ratio as in the DPG@MA group; (3) DPG group, implanted with DPG dissolved in PBS at an equivalent concentration to the DPG@MA group; (4) DPG@MA group, the core experimental group, implanted with DPG@MA dissolved in PBS to assess its comprehensive repair effect.

(C-F) Quantitative micro-CT analysis: BV/TV (C), BS/TV (D), Tb.N (E) and BMD (F) (n = 6). (G-I) H&E (G), Masson's trichrome (H) and Goldner's trichrome (I) of defect regions. After 6 weeks, bone regeneration was analyzed using micro-CT. 3D reconstructed images (Fig. 8B) revealed minimal mineralized tissue formation in the Ctrl group, confirming the inability of critical-sized defects to heal spontaneously. Both the DPG and MA groups promoted some peripheral bone ingrowth, but most of the defect remained unhealed, indicating limited efficacy of the individual components. In contrast, the DPG@MA group exhibited substantial new bone formation. Quantitative analysis of bone morphometric parameters (Figs. 8C-F) further supported these observations, showing that the DPG@MA group had significantly higher bone volume fraction (BV/TV), bone surface density (BS/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N) and bone mineral density (BMD) than all other groups.

Histological staining provided further insight into new bone formation capacity. H&E staining (Fig. 8G) showed sparse new bone in the Ctrl group, whereas the DPG, MA and DPG@MA groups all induced varying degrees of osteogenesis, with the most pronounced effect observed in the DPG@MA group. Masson's Trichrome (Fig. 8H) and Goldner's Trichrome staining (Fig. 8I) corroborated these findings, demonstrating that the DPG@MA group had the largest area of new bone within the defect, containing the most abundant collagen fibers and mineralized matrix compared to other groups. Immunohistochemistry (Figs. 8J–L) showed that DPG@MA produced the highest Arginase-1 (Arg-1)-positive area (%) and the lowest iNOS-positive area (%) within the marginal connective tissue, confirming an M2-dominated, pro-regenerative immune profile in vivo.

Finally, H&E staining of major organs (Fig. S7) showed normal morphological structures without apparent pathological features such as cell necrosis or inflammatory infiltration. These results collectively indicate that DPG@MA effectively treats critical-sized bone defects without inducing adverse side effects, providing important experimental evidence for its clinical safety.

Tibial Defect Model Experiment

The tibial defect model served as a critical supplement to evaluate the performance of the DPG@MA hydrogel in bone repair, specifically aiming to validate its efficacy under dynamic loading conditions and assess its clinical translational potential [54,55]. We established a rat tibial defect model (Fig. 9A), which better recapitulates the physiological stress environment experienced by bones during activity compared to calvarial defects. This model is particularly crucial for assessing material performance in complex defects susceptible to interfacial micro-motion. The study included a positive control group treated with Novabone and a blank control (Ctrl) group. Systematic comparison of bone regeneration quality and complication incidence was performed via micro-CT analysis (Figs. 9B-F) and quantitative assessment (Figs. 9G-N) at 4 weeks post-operation.

(B-E) Representative micro-CT images: 3D reconstruction (B), coronal (C), sagittal (D) and transverse (E) views of tibial defects. Red boxes indicate defect regions; orange areas denote ectopic mineralization observed in the Novabone group. Scale bars: 2 mm. (F) 3D reconstruction of newly formed bone in the defect area (cortical and trabecular bone). Scale bars: 2 mm. Quantitative results demonstrated that compared to the Ctrl group, the DPG@MA group showed significant improvements (p < 0.05) in key bone regeneration parameters within the tibial defect area. Cortical bone thickness (Ct.Th) (Fig. 9I) increased by 55.447% (Ctrl: 94.470 ± 8.331 μm; DPG@MA: 146.850 ± 19.320 μm), while the cortical bone area to total area ratio (Ct.Ar/Tt.Ar) (Fig. 9J) increased by 43.573% (Ctrl: 34.823 ± 6.175; DPG@MA: 49.996 ± 6.982). Concurrently, trabecular bone repair also showed advantages, with BV/TV (Fig. 9K) increasing by 30.419% (Ctrl: 0.043 ± 0.009%; DPG@MA: 0.056 ± 0.005%), Tb.N (Fig. 9M) increasing by 96.141% (Ctrl: 0.347 ± 0.178 mm-1; DPG@MA: 0.681 ± 0.145 mm-1) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. 9N) decreasing by 33.339% (Ctrl: 0.472 ± 0.135 mm; DPG@MA: 0.630 ± 0.147 mm). Three-dimensional reconstruction further confirmed that the DPG@MA group achieved effective defect filling with regenerated bone, exhibiting a trabecular-cortical bone structure closely resembling that of the sham group.

In contrast, the Novabone group displayed distinctly different repair characteristics. Micro-CT (Figs. 9B-F) revealed significant mineralization of the filler material, forming a dense, cortical-like structure with a BV/TV of 0.511 ± 0.110, significantly higher than other groups (p < 0.05). However, two major issues were observed. First, the formation of cortical-like mineralization within the trabecular bone region suggested a potential stress-shielding effect, leading to resorption of the surrounding host trabecular bone. Second, obvious heterotopic bone hyperplasia was detected around the bone surfaces adjacent to the defect in the Novabone group. 3D reconstruction (Figs. 9B and D) showed irregular mineralized nodules (orange areas), with quantitative analysis indicating a heterotopic bone volume of 4.192 ± 0.587 mm3, accounting for 36.964 ± 16.038% of the total defect volume. No significant heterotopic ossification was detected in the other groups. This ectopic bone formation is likely attributable to material loosening, migration and subsequent induction of non-physiological bone formation in the surrounding soft tissues.

The results demonstrate that DPG@MA promotes superior regeneration of both cortical and trabecular bone in the defect area, while effectively avoiding complications such as heterotopic ossification caused by interfacial micro-motion and material displacement. These findings further underscore the advantages and potential clinical value of the DPG@MA hydrogel for bone defect repair.

Discussion

This study successfully developed an injectable, photo-curable DPG@MA hydrogel integrating wet adhesion, osteogenic induction and immunomodulatory functions. The results demonstrate that the hydrogel achieves stable in-situ anchoring within irregular bone defects through its robust interfacial adhesion and cohesive properties. It synergistically promotes efficient bone repair via the sustained co-release of ALN and silicon ions, coupled with active modulation of the local immune microenvironment. Its overall performance surpasses that of the Novabone, offering a new solution to the intertwined clinical challenges of material displacement, uncontrolled inflammation and inadequate osteogenesis.

Notably, DPG@MA lost approximately 80 % of its mass within 30 days and exhibited a lower modulus than highly cross-linked GelMA; nevertheless, this rapid resorption was intentionally engineered to align with the natural chronobiology of bone healing. During the first 2–4 weeks, the photocross-linked network and nanoparticles provided sufficient adhesion and resistance to interfacial micromotion. Thereafter, the hydrogel underwent progressive hydrolysis, gradually surrendering space for ingrowth and mineralization of nascent bone matrix. This timely resorption minimized chronic foreign-body responses and ultimately promoted complete osseointegration.

The favorable performance of DPG@MA originates from its rational structure-property design, realized through a hierarchical architecture combining a double-network with functional nanoparticles, with advantages highlighted by comparisons to relevant counterparts. Firstly, the double-network strategy achieves adhesion-mechanics synergy. Unlike single-network adhesive hydrogels, which often sacrifice mechanical strength for adhesion [29,30], this work integrates a mussel-inspired adhesive network with a photo-crosslinkable rigid network. This ensures strong adhesion to wet bone surfaces via DP while enhancing cohesive strength and structural stability via GelMA. The interfacial adhesion is primarily driven by the catechol groups of DOPA in DP, which form multifaceted interactions-including covalent cross-links (Michael addition/Schiff base reaction with collagen amines), metal coordination (with Ca2+), hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking-with bone components [22]. The bulk cohesive strength derives from a triple synergistic mechanism: the UV-triggered covalent cross-links of GelMA form a permanent mechanical skeleton [33] ; hydrogen bonds and π-π interactions within the DP network act as reversible sacrificial bonds that dissipate energy upon deformation; and the incorporated MA nanoparticles further reinforce the network through surface hydrogen bonds between silanol groups and DOPA catechols, and by acting as physical cross-links via chain pinning and entanglement [48]. Together, they significantly improve the hydrogel's toughness, anti-swelling capacity and structural integrity, preventing cohesive failure and ensuring the translation of strong interfacial chemistry into macroscopically stable adhesion. Secondly, the MA nanoparticles confer dual reinforcement-drug delivery functionality. The ALN-loaded MA nanoparticles serve a "triple role"-acting as nanoreinforcers to consolidate the network structure while functioning as carriers for the controlled co-delivery of ALN and silicon ions, laying the foundation for bioactivity. Adhesion tests confirmed that this design yields a significant improvement in adhesive strength (∼55 kPa) for DPG@MA, substantially outperforming traditional fibrin glue (∼10 kPa). This integrated design of a "rigid network-flexible network-functional nanoreinforcer" is a key reason for DPG@MA's ability to balance strong adhesion with mechanical stability.

Regarding in vitro biological performance, DPG@MA demonstrates promising osteogenic and anti-inflammatory activities. While many current studies endow materials with osteogenic activity by loading growth factors like BMP-2 [[56], [57], [58]] , they face challenges such as high cost, poor stability and potential risks of ectopic ossification [39]. This study utilizes the synergistic action of pectin, silicon ions and ALN to achieve potent osteogenic effects with more stable and safer components. Specifically, the pectin in DPG contains abundant groups that bind to cell receptors, promoting osteoblast adhesion. It can also sequester environmental calcium ions and induce mineralization by modulating the Ca2+/CaMKII and BMP-SMAD1/5 signaling pathways in osteoblasts [25,59]. The MA provide sustained release of ALN, a bisphosphonate that directly upregulates ALP activity and the expression of osteogenic genes and proteins in BMSCs [60]. Concurrently, the silicon ions released from MA degradation may activate the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promoting osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells [61]. The synergistic osteogenic effect observed when DPG is combined with MA can be explained by the following rationale: the ECM-mimetic properties of pectin provide a stable matrix for the sustained release of ALN and silicon ions from MA, preventing burst release, while the mesoporous structure of MA enhances the mechanical stability of the pectin-based matrix. ALN, silicon ions and pectin degradation products collectively activate osteogenic signaling pathways, ultimately achieving highly efficient osteogenesis through dual regulation of biochemical signals and physical structure. In terms of immunomodulation, DPG@MA, without requiring exogenous cytokines, utilizes its intrinsic components to effectively steer macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 towards the pro-healing M2 phenotype. Specifically, studies indicate that pectin in DPG can block the NF-κB pathway via Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), modulating the M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance and reducing excessive inflammation [62]. Silicon ions can suppress inflammatory MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways and promote caspase-dependent apoptosis in macrophages, thereby modulating the inflammatory response in the osteoimmune microenvironment [63]. The synergistic immunomodulatory effect observed after combining DPG and MA may be attributed to their complementary regulatory networks on target pathways, collectively promoting a stable shift of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype.

Next-generation bone repair materials have transcended the traditional role of being passive bone defect fillers. The core of the "active regeneration" concept lies not only in guiding bone regeneration through intrinsic bioactivity but, more critically, in simultaneously fulfilling the synergistic regulation of "stable material anchoring, balanced inflammatory microenvironment and efficient osteogenesis." These three dimensions constitute the core evaluation criteria for active regeneration and are prerequisites for adapting to complex clinical bone defect scenarios [64,65]. Currently commercialized products like Novabone represent only a preliminary implementation of the active regeneration concept. While they can achieve basic osseointegration via osteoinductive components and provide mechanical support, their applicability is largely confined to bone defects with a relatively static healing environment and absence of chronic inflammation interference (e.g., localized defects in non-articular areas) [17]. In such scenarios, where materials are not subjected to interfacial micro-motion and the local microenvironment is free from inflammatory disturbance, their basic properties might suffice for simple repair needs. However, clinical bone defect conditions often exceed this applicable boundary, exposing Novabone's limitations and its fundamental misalignment with the "stability-anti-inflammation-osteogenesis" synergy required for active regeneration. Concerning stable material anchoring, its lack of interfacial adhesion becomes a critical flaw in irregular defects prone to micro-motion (e.g., peri-articular defects like tibial plateau edges). It fails to conform tightly to the defect cavity, allowing postoperative joint movement to induce material-bone interface micro-motion or even particle migration, which disrupts the osteogenic microenvironment and can trigger heterotopic ossification [13,66]. Regarding balanced inflammatory microenvironment, its bioactivity focuses solely on osteoinduction, completely lacking osteoimmunomodulatory capacity. Faced with defects complicated by chronic inflammation (e.g., post-traumatic persistent inflammation) or foreign body reaction, it cannot suppress excessive M1 macrophage polarization to balance inflammation, ultimately leading to delayed union or non-union [67,68]. The inadequacy in efficient osteogenesis is essentially a cascading effect of the first two shortcomings - material displacement and uncontrolled inflammation jointly disrupt the stable microenvironment essential for osteogenesis, preventing its osteoinductive potential from being fully realized, resulting in a passive outcome of inadequate osteogenesis [69].

Consistent with the principle of staged validation, the tibial defect was used to compare the complete DPG@MA system against the Novabone under load-bearing conditions, rather than to repeat the component-wise comparison that had already been completed in the calvarial model. This design minimized animal use while directly addressing translational performance under micromotion. The in vivo animal results of DPG@MA successfully address the limitations of Novabone across three critical dimensions-material displacement, uncontrolled inflammation and inadequate osteogenesis-enabling superior implementation of the active regeneration concept for next-generation bone repair materials, with each breakthrough substantiated by clear experimental evidence. To address Novabone's limitation-namely material displacement under dynamic conditions and induced ectopic ossification,the rat tibial defect model simulating clinical "dynamic weight-bearing scenario around joints" demonstrated that DPG@MA exhibits favorable wet adhesion, enabling tight conformation and firm attachment to defect cavities. This fundamentally prevents material loosening and interfacial micro-motion, directly circumventing the displacement issue inherent to Novabone's lack of adhesion. Consequently, no ectopic ossification was observed, resolving the "material stability" challenge. Regarding the limitation of uncontrolled inflammation disrupting the osteogenic microenvironment, previously validated in vitro experiments showed that DPG@MA, through the synergistic action of its components, guides macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the pro-healing M2 phenotype, upregulating M2 markers (CD206) and downregulating M1 markers (iNOS, TNF-α). This creates a stable microenvironment foundation for bone regeneration, avoiding healing delays caused by chronic inflammation. Concerning the issue of "inadequate osteogenesis resulting from the cascade of the first two limitations", the osteogenic efficacy of DPG@MA was directly confirmed in the rat calvarial defect model. This result correlates with the in vitro conclusion of "DPG and MA synergistically promoting osteogenesis", indicating that its osteogenic activity is not isolated but is an efficient expression built upon the foundation of material stability and inflammatory balance, thereby solving the "inadequate osteogenesis" problem.

Naturally, this study still has some limitations. First, although the rat bone defect model is a classic choice, its regeneration mechanism and mechanical environment still differ from those of human weight-bearing bones. Second, the 4-6 weeks observation period has not yet covered the evaluation of long-term repair effects and bone remodeling processes. Third, although DPG@MA has been shown to modulate macrophage polarization, the precise molecular mechanisms governing M2 polarization and the downstream molecular network through which it influences osteoblasts remain to be elucidated by means of gene-knockout models or transcriptomic sequencing. Based on the above limitations, future research will be carried out in a targeted manner: First, the safety and repair efficacy of DPG@MA will be verified in weight-bearing bone defect models of large animals (e.g., sheep and pigs) to bridge the gap between small animal models and human clinical scenarios. Second, the observation period will be extended to systematically evaluate the long-term degradation behavior and bone integration effects of the hydrogel, so as to supplement its long-term performance data. Third, multi-omics techniques will be used to deeply reveal the molecular mechanism by which the hydrogel regulates the immune-osteogenesis cross-cellular crosstalk, providing more precise theoretical support for its functional optimization.

Conclusion

In summary, through ingenious material design, this study has successfully developed an injectable, photo-curable DPG@MA hydrogel that integrates wet adhesion, osteogenic induction and immunomodulatory capacity. It addresses the physical limitations of poor adhesion and easy displacement associated with traditional materials, while achieving synergistic coordination between osteogenesis and anti-inflammation at the biological level. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments have demonstrated its high efficacy in promoting bone regeneration and overcoming the application limitations of Novabone. This integrated adhesion-immunity-osteogenesis approach offers a versatile platform technology for challenging bone defects. This work provides a promising biomaterial and a novel therapeutic strategy for the repair of complex bone defects.
