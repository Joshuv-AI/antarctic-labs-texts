International water conventions, the exploitation of trans-boundary rivers and the Nile water dispute among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt
Authors: Abren Gelaw, Mulugeta Debele
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEthiopia, Sudan and Egypt entered into dispute since Ethiopia began the construction of the Renaissance Dam along the Blue Nile in 2011. This article is aimed at assessing the core facts about water conventions, the use of trans-boundary rivers and the main argument points of Egypt and Sudan against Ethiopia's use right of the Nile. Data were obtained from conventions and laws, study reports and television interviews. Percent and ratios were used for quantifying data. The 1997 UN Water Convention, having seven criterions for equitable water use by riparian nations, is a better platform for the negotiation on the Nile water. Ethiopia contributes 86.52 billion m3 to the Nile water although Egypt's contribution is negligible. Despite the built-up of 3 huge dams by Egypt and over 10 by Sudan, these countries aggressively contest the Renaissance Dam of Ethiopia. Egypt diverts about 46 billion m3 water for irrigation. Turk, an upstream nation (alike Ethiopia in the Nile basin), built 11 dams along the Tigris and Euphrates Rivers with rare contest from the downstream nations. The existence of ‘adequate rainfall’ and ‘other rivers’ in Ethiopia, the colonial treaties, the Nile as the only resource, and the impact of the Renaissance Dam are among the main points of argument of Egypt and Sudan against Ethiopia's use right of the Nile. The Nile water use by Ethiopia is inevitable as 73.7 % of the rivers' flow, 65.3 % of the hydropower and 50.6 % of the irrigation potential of all Ethiopian rivers is in the Nile basin; besides, significant share of the population has no electric service (>60), no pure water access (42.7 %) and is below poverty line (29.6 %). Thus, the three countries need to be flexible for the ‘give and take’ principle-based negotiation and ensuring the equitable use of the Nile water.Keywords: Transboundary watercourse, Water convention, Equitable water use, Nile, Ethiopia, Egypt

Introduction

The world consisted of about 263 trans-boundary rivers, which accounts three-fifth (60 %) of the freshwater of the global landmass; and, trans-boundary rivers drain across the territories of 145 nations, which are inhabited by almost two-fifth (40 %) of the global population [50]. Trans-boundary watercourses refer to water resources (e.g. rivers, lakes, subsurface water) which drain (or exist) across the international boundaries of two or more nations, known as riparian nations [52]. Abay (Blue Nile) River, being a trans-boundary, is a common resource of Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt [41]. The Niger River basin belongs to Mali (25 %), Niger (25 %), Nigeria (26 %), Algeria (9 %), Guinea (4 %), Burkina Faso (3 %), Benin (2 %), Cote d'lvoire (1 %), Cameroon (4 %) and Chad (1 %) in the Western Africa [12]. Similarly, the Euphrates River is a common resource of Turk, Iraq, Iran, Saudi Arabia and Jordan in the Middle East region [3].

Theoretically, riparian nations have to exploit transboundary watercourses equitably and reasonably on account of their biophysical conditions, socioeconomic needs, share (%) of their population reliant on the common water resource, the effects of the water use by one riparian nation over the other/s, the existing and potential benefit of the transboundary water, its sustainability, and the existing options having comparative value with the watercourse [39]. Riparian countries are supposed to cooperate within the framework of international water conventions and laws for the conservation, protection, development and sustainable use of transboundary watercourses. In addition, control over transboundary water could not be achieved through water wars rather than using a suite of power-related tactics and strategies [55]. Globally, 60 % (158) of the 263 trans-boundary watercourses (rivers) have no specific bilateral and/or multilateral agreements; and, 66.7 % (70) of the 105 trans-boundary watercourses having specific bilateral and/or multilateral agreements failed to include one or more of the riparian nations [41]. Because of this and their communal nature, the use right of trans-boundary watercourses is thought to be one of the major sources of conflicts among riparian nations of the contemporary world. The potential conflicts among countries of trans-boundary water are believed to be minimal where the riparian nations cooperate under the framework of international water conventions and laws [41, 50].

The Nile Basin Initiative (NBI) for cooperation of countries within the Nile River basin is one of the specific multilateral development cooperation among trans-boundary watercourse nations [35] although implementation of its projects was speculated (soon after it was instituted) to take over two decades due to political, economic and climatic challenges [28]. A Cooperation Framework Agreement (CFA) was also drafted (before a decade ago) for the cooperation to the development and sustainable management of the Nile basin by its riparian nations [11]. However, likely reflecting the ‘game theory’ (and economic behavior) scenario, Egypt and Sudan declined to be part of the CFA for the sustainable management of the Nile. The ‘game theory’ quantifies the cooperation and conflict among players of a game in a mathematical model; the ‘game theory’ states that actors are supposed to design strategies (to players of a game) for predicting the costs and profits of the decisions by opponent players, and to determine the ideal (perfect) reaction based on their prediction [30]. According to this theory, a transboundary water is assumed as a non-cooperative game field where opponent players design counter strategies for conflict and confrontation rather than cooperation [45]. In fact, the CFA is not practical as most of the riparian nations have not signed on it yet; that is, for the CFA to be practical and useful, it should be signed by at least six (over 50 %) of the eleven riparian nations [11]. Hence, the NBI and the CFA are far from enhancing cooperation and resolving the disputes among the riparian nations of the Nile. But Lesotho and South Africa are enjoying mutual benefits from their trans-boundary water via a bilateral cooperation [42]. The same is true of the riparian countries of the Zambezi and Niger Rivers in Africa [12].

Disputes have been observed about the use right of the Nile among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt since Ethiopia has launched the construction of the Renaissance Dam along the Abay/Blue Nile River in 2011 [17, 24]. The Dam negotiation, having been an agendum twice at the UN Security Council in 2021, indicates that the Nile water use by Ethiopia is considered by Egypt as a threat on its “peace and security”. Conversely, the Dam Project and the use-right of the Nile are considered by Ethiopia a matter of ensuring its legitimate water use right, its sovereignty and national pride. The difference in the platform to be used for the negotiation on the Renaissance Dam and the Nile water use is also said to have been one of the main causes of the dispute among the three countries. Unlike Ethiopia which sticks solely on international water conventions and laws (e.g. the 1997 UNWC) for the negotiation, Egypt and Sudan showed the tendency to relay on the 1902, 1929 and 1959 colonial treaties, and also on the 1997 UNWC for contending the Dam project and sustaining the ‘natural and historical right’ of using the Nile river [40]. Egypt's contest against the Renaissance Dam of Ethiopia was overtly supported by the US government especially during the Donald Trump administration period (2016–2020); and, this is one of the indicators of bias about the Nile water use since the US support to Egypt's and Sudan's contest on the Dam Project of Ethiopia can't be justified with legitimate reasons related to the equitable use of the Nile water [17]. Anyway, confusions (on the Nile water use right) seemed to have been observed among citizens of Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt, independent observers and other bodies worldwide.

Some studies have been made on the Renaissance Dam and the Nile water in the recent past. The studies were conducted about the ‘Benefit of Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam [46], ‘equitable and reasonable utilization: reconstructing the Nile … water allocation dialogue’ [40], ‘quantifying the UN watercourse convention indicators … for equitable water sharing’ [21], ‘Ethiopia's renewable energy potentials and current state’ [23], the ‘filling and operation plans and negotiations’ [1], ‘the political deadlock on the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam’ [7], ‘hydro-conflicts … analysis of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam project’ [17], and ‘Ethiopia's hydropower development and the Nile Basin hydro politics' [24]. The studies tried to address the treaties and conventions about transboundary watercourses [21, 40], the profiles of the riparian nations of the Nile [35, 36, 40], the water resource and renewable energy potentials of Ethiopia [8, 19, 23], the hydropower development and actual energy service difference between Ethiopia and Egypt [1, 24], the estimation of equitable share of the Nile water [21], the hydro-conflicts among the riparian nations of the Nile [7, 17], and the benefits of the Renaissance Dam to Sudan and Egypt [17 ,46]. However, none of these studies assessed the experiences on the exploitation of transboundary rivers (i.e. Euphrates, Tigris, Indus, Niger and Zambezi) as a mirror of the transboundary water use realities to the disputing riparian nations of the Nile and other bodies, identified and analyzed the main argument points of Egypt and Sudan in the 2019–2021 negotiations on the Renaissance Dam and the specific bases of the points from the perspective of water conventions and laws, and conducted a comparative analysis of the actual vs., potential hydropower generation between the ‘Nile basin’ and the ‘off-Nile basin’ of Ethiopia [1, 7, 8, 17, 19, 21, 23, 24, 40]; here, the last point clearly shows how much Ethiopia was shay in exploiting the tributaries of the Nile River. Besides, although Egypt's desire for preserving its supremacy over the exploitation of the Nile water seemed to have been built since the country had begun to send its Patriarchies from the Coptic Church of Alexandria to the Ethiopian Orthodox Church centuries ago, studies that have assessed the impact of such a religious relationship on the mindset up of the Ethiopians and the Nile water use are rare. Assessing the gaps (summarized above) of the studies enables scholars, observers and other bodies to have a clear (better) picture about the status of equitable share (benefit) of the Nile water among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt, and to reduce the observed biases about the use right of the Nile River [41].

The themes of this study have rooted largely from the points of argument of Egypt and Sudan during the 2019–2021 negotiation on the Renaissance Dam of Ethiopia. Thus, this article is aimed to: (1) examine the main facts regarding water conventions and the exploitation of trans-boundary rivers; (2) document the main points of dispute of Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt in the 2019–2021 negotiations; (3) assess the main points of negotiation of Egypt and Sudan regarding the Renaissance Dam and the right of using the Nile, and (4)) analyze how much the main points of arguments of these countries regarding the use right of the Nile water are convincing and acceptable.

The Nile Basin and methods of the study

The Nile River basin/catchment

The Great Nile River course is about 6695 km long, and its basin area (i.e. the green shaded portion of Fig. 1), being 3.18 million km2, is about one-tenth (10 %) of the Africa continent [40]. The White Nile originating from Victoria lake of Tanzania-Kenya-Uganda and the Blue Nile stemming from Tana lake of Ethiopia are the two largest tributaries of the Great Nile River. The Nile River Basin covers about eleven (11) countries such as Ethiopia (11.5 %), Eritrea (0.8 %), Kenya (1.6 %), Uganda (7.6 %), Rwanda (0.6 %), Burundi (0.4 %), Tanzania (3.7 %), Democratic Republic Congo (0.7 %), South-Sudan (19.5 %), Sudan (44 %) and Egypt (9.5 %) [35].

Although the mean annual flow of the Nile River was often cited at 84 billion m3 by different studies [6,36], the river flow (including the evaporation-led water loss) was also shown to be 94.5 billion meter cube [35]. However, the main tributaries of Ethiopian origin alone such as Abay/Blue Nile (54.4 billion m3), Tekeze/Atbara River (8.2 billion m3), Baro-Akobo/Sobat (23.2 billion m3) and the upper tributaries (0.72 billion m3) of Mereb River in Wollo and Tigray contribute 86.52 billion m3 to the Nile River – which constitutes 85–90 % of the Nile water [35]. In fact, the 86.52 billion m3 did not account the evaporation-led water loss [8]. In addition to the use of the river for irrigation and home purpose, an increasing through flow and infiltration as one moves from the upstream to the downstream (the lower course) could be among the causes of the likely unconvincing difference between the upstream (86.5 billion m3) and the downstream (94.5 billion m3) measurements of the annual water flow of the Nile River. Rainfall in Ethiopia, Uganda, South Sudan and other riparian nations of East Africa is the main source of water for the Nile River [40].

Methods

Evidences used for this study were obtained largely from secondary sources. Documents about treaties, international water conventions and laws, statistical reports, articles/journals, research reports and other internet sources have been used for the study. Data were obtained from trusted sources. Conventions such as the ‘Convention on the Law of the Non-navigational Uses of International Watercourses’ [47, 49], ‘Agreement on the Nile River Basin Cooperative Framework’ [11], ‘The Politics of the Nile Basin’ [6]-a PhD dissertation about the 1902, 1929 and 1959 treaties on the Nile River, ‘Summary Notes of the International Water Law [50]’, the ‘UNWC: User's Guide First Edition’ [41], the ‘International Law Facilitating Transboundary Water Cooperation’ [52], a ‘AQUASTAT Country Profile – Ethiopia’ [19], and others. Data were also gathered from different articles published by different reputable journals [1, 3, 4, 8, 21, 23,24, 29, 40, 43], and others.

Concrete evidences have also been acquired from interviews of the Ethiopian negotiating members of the Renaissance Dam, and the Water, Irrigation and Energy Ministry of Ethiopia through the Ethiopian Television (ETV), the Amhara Television (TV) and the Aljazeera TV. An attempt was carried out to validate or cross check the evidences obtained from the TV interviews with the support of empirical evidences of studies conducted about related issues. The study has tried to integrate (or bridge) evidences of social science and natural science disciplines. As this study is purely qualitative, tools like percentages and ratios were used to quantify evidences. The presentation involved assessments and comparisons of evidences, linking, interpreting and deriving meaning, analyzing and triangulating evidences obtained from the different sources, and reporting of the results.

Analyses and discussions

Treaties and conventions on water

The 1902, 1929 and 1959 treaties on the Nile water

Three Britain or United Kingdom (UK)-led treaties were signed about the Nile water in 1902, 1929 and 1959 as Sudan and Egypt were the then colonies of UK. Great Britain and Ethiopia (Emperor Menelik II) signed a treaty on May 15, 1902 in Addis Ababa. Even if the 1902 treaty was signed solely on Ethio-Sudan border, the treaty also incorporated an article (Article III) about the Nile water use. This article states that ‘His Majesty Emperor Menelik II … engages himself to the Britannic Majesty … not to construct or allow to be constructed any work on the Blue Nile, Lake Tana or the Sobat, which would arrest the flow of their waters except in agreement with the Britannic Majesty's government and the government of Sudan’ [6]. The treaty stresses that, Ethiopia should consult the downstream nations (Egypt and Sudan) if the country wants to exploit the Nile water. The 1929 agreement was signed between Egypt and Great Britain representing its East African colonies (e.g. Kenya, Uganda and Tanzania), which guarantees Egypt the ‘natural and historic right’ of using the Nile [40]. This treaty allocated 83.3 % (70 billion m3) of the Nile water for Egypt and 4.8 % (4 billion m3) for Sudan. The 1959 treaty was signed between Egypt and Sudan; and, this treaty allocated 66.1 % (55.5 billion m3) of the Nile water for Egypt and 22 % (18.5 billion m3) for Sudan, assuming that 11.9 % (10 billion m3) of the water is evaporated and percolated [17].

The 1929 and 1959 agreements marginalized Ethiopia's use right of the Nile River [6] as both treaties gave guarantee of the use right of 100 % of the Nile water for Egypt and Sudan. Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt also signed a ‘Declaration of Principles’ in Khartum (Sudan) in 2015 [21], and Article ‘V’ of this agreement confirms that the construction and filling of the Renaissance Dam would be done in parallel (Engineer Gedion Asfaw via ETV on July 11, 2021). That is why Ethiopia has made the first, second, third and the fourth round filling in July–August 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023, respectively (before the dam construction is completed).

International water conventions

International water conventions about trans-boundary watercourses were signed in 1966, 1996 (Helsinki), 1997 (UNWC), 2000 and 2004 (Berlin) [41]. These conventions offer the legal ground about the need for equitable distribution of trans-boundary watercourses among riparian nations [18]. Article ‘V’ (1) of the 1997 UNWC (whose base is the 1966 convention) states that trans-boundary watercourse nations are entitled for ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of their common water resource [10, 52]. Accordingly, Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt have the right for ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of the Blue Nile River. Here, the phrase ‘equitable use’ indicates the right of the nations to exploit the common water resource fairly; but this does not mean equal share of the water as there are criterions to be accounted for sharing the trans-boundary water among riparian nations [49]. Whereas, the phrase ‘reasonable use’ indicates that the use of a trans-boundary water by one nation should be justified with the need to tackle real socioeconomic problems of the nation by considering that the water use does not result in significant adverse impacts on the other riparian nation/s [47].

Article ‘VI’(1) of the 1997 UNWC consists of seven factors or criterions to be accounted for the ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of trans-boundary water such as: (1) the ‘geographical, hydrography, hydrological, climatic and ecological’ conditions of the riparian nations, (2) the socioeconomic needs of the nations, (3) population of the riparian nations reliant on the trans-boundary water, (4) the impact of the water use by one nation over the other/s, (5) the existing and potential uses of the water, (6) the conservation, protection, development and economy of use of the river's water and the cost of measures taken to that effect, and (7) the available alternatives of comparative value i.e. water and/or other broader options yielding similar benefit to a particular planned or existing use of the water [21]. But the authors of this article believe that these criteria are not sufficient as the UNWC should have incorporated criterions like ‘the water contributions of riparian nations’.

On the other hand, the CFA on the development of the Nile basin added two more factors (# 8 and # 9) for equitable use of the Nile water [11, 40] such as: (8) the water contribution of each riparian nation to the Nile River, and (9) the proportion of the Nile basin in each riparian nation. It is surprising to see criteria #8 and #9 unincorporated into the UNWC so far, which indicates, perhaps, the need for improvement of Article VI (1) of the convention (UNWC). This is so because, a failure or decline for incorporation of the “water contribution of riparian nations” as one criterion for the equitable use of trans-boundary water cannot be justified with legitimate and moral reasons other than bias. Most riparian nations should sign on the CFA of the Nile basin for factor # 8 and # 9 to be practical. The factors, whether seven or nine, lack measurability for equitable water distribution among the riparian nations [21, 43]. Meaning, how much is the practical weight of each of the seven factors in the ‘equitable’ share of water (e.g. Nile River) among the riparian nations is ambiguous. Thus, the equitable water share may require formulating a basin-specific hydrological model.

Experiences about the exploitation of trans-boundary rivers

Rivers such as Euphrates, Tigris, Indus, Niger, Zambezi and Nile River are among the main trans-boundary watercourses about which the exploitation experiences are briefly presented under this sub-title. The Euphrates and Tigris Rivers are being exploited by Turk for different purposes without any critical threat from the downstream nations such as Iraq, Syria and Iran where the average annual discharge of the Euphrates River was 90.6 billion m3 and that of Tigris River was about 2.2 billion m3 (meter cube) [3]. Turk and Iraq have built five (5) dams each along the Euphrates River; they also built six (6) and eight (8) dams along the Tigris River, respectively. While Syria built four (4) dams along the Euphrates River, Iran developed the same number (4) of dams along the Tigris River. The upstream nation, Turk, built eleven (11) dams along the Euphrates (5 dams) and the Tigris (6 dams) Rivers for hydropower (HP), irrigation and flood control with rare serious objection from the downstream nations such as Iraq, Syria and Iran [3].

The Indus River is the other trans-boundary river that crosses India, Pakistan, Afghanistan, China, Tibet and Nepal in the Asia continent [34]. But the largest proportion of the river water is used by Pakistan and India as 86 % of its basin area (52.5 % Pakistan and 33.5 % India) and 88 % of its annual flow (69 % India and 19 % Pakistan) originates from these two nations [33]. India and Pakistan, by signing the Indus Water Treaty (IWT) in 1960, agreed to use the river water fairly; meaning, the treaty allocated 3 eastern tributaries (i.e. Ravi, Beas and Sutlej) for India and 3 western tributaries (i.e. Chenab, Jhelum and Indus) for Pakistan. The Indus River consisted of over 144 large and small dams, most which were built by India and Pakistan. Both India (the upstream) and Pakistan (the downstream) use the Indus River for hydropower and irrigation, where 74 % of the total irrigated land of the whole river basin was found in Pakistan [29]. Afghanistan also uses Kabul River (a tributary of the Indus River) for energy and irrigation. India and Pakistan entered in to conflict since the mid-1990s when India began to launch over 3 dam projects on Chenab River, which enables to generate a hydropower of 1931.2 MW; and, the conflict has persisted as India has continued to propose other hydropower projects along the Chenab and Jhelum Rivers [33]. The India-Pakistan water-based conflict is different from the Ethio-Sudan-Egypt dispute in that the conflict of the Asian nations was induced due to violation of the 1960 IWT by the upstream nation, India [33, 34].

In Western Africa, the Niger River drains across 10 riparian nations (i.e. its basin area in bracket) such as Mali (25 %), Niger (25 %), Nigeria (26 %), Algeria (9 %), Guinea (4 %), Burkina Faso (3 %), Benin (2 %), Cote d'lvoire (1 %), Cameroon (4 %) and Chad (1 %), where it is used for hydropower and irrigation [12]. Mali (an upstream nation) has built 3 (Selingue, Markala and Talo) dams, Nigeria (a downstream nation) has 2 (Kainji and Jebba) dams and Cameroon built 1 (Legdo) dam along the Niger River and its tributaries for hydropower [12]. Guinea, Mali, Benin, Niger and Nigeria have had under construction and planned dams; that is, Mali (Djenne and Touassa), Niger (Kandadji and Gambou) and Nigeria (Mambila and Zungeru) have 2 dams (each) while Guinea (Fomi) and Benin (Kandadji) have 1 dam each [26, 53]. The Niger River and its tributaries are also widely used for irrigation by Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Guinea, Benin, Burkina Faso, Cote d'lvoire, Cameroon and Chad [12].

The Zambezi River, in the Southern Africa region, is used for hydropower and irrigation by 8 riparian nations (i.e. the basin area of each nation in bracket) such as Zambia (41.6 %), Zimbabwe (15.6 %), Angola (18.5 %), Mozambique (11.8 %), Malawi (8 %), Tanzania (2 %), Botswana (1.4 %) and Namibia (1.2 %) [44, 51]. Zambia has 9 functioning, under construction and/or proposed dams (i.e. Kariba North Bank, Itezhi-Tezhi, Victoria-Fall, Bubi-Lupane, Batoka North, Kafue Gorge Upper, Kafue Gorge Lower, Mupata Gorge and the Devil's Gorge Dams), Mozambique 7 dams (i.e. Cahora-Bassa, Tedzani, Kapichira-I, Kapichira-II, Mphanda-Nkuwa, Nkula-Fall and Boroma Dams), Angola 6 dams (i.e. Cambambe-I, Cambambe-II, Capanda, Lauca, Nyanga and Caculo-Cabaca Dams), Malawi 6 dams (i.e. Kholombidzo, Mpatamanga, Fufu, and Songwe-I, II and III), Zimbabwe 4 dams (i.e. Batoka South, Kariba South, Gwayi-Shangani and Tokwe-Mukosi) and Tanzania more than 3 dams (i.e. Songwe-I, II and III) along Zambezi River and its tributaries; Botswana has 1 (Chobe-Zambezi water transfer) scheme [26]. These nations often enter in to dispute (each other) due to the quest for equitable water use for irrigation [44].

The Nile water use is very controversial. The contribution of Egypt to the water flow of the Nile is negligible; whereas, Ethiopia contributes about 86.52 billion m3 water to the annual flow of the River (Table 1). Egypt built huge dams, namely, the Old Aswan Dam, the (improved) Aswan High Dam and the Naga-Hammadi Dam; Sudan built over ten (10) dams (i.e. Jebel-Aulia, Khashm-el-Girba, Merowe, Roseires, Sennar, Atbara-Setit, Rumela, Shereyk, Kajbar and Dal Dam) for hydropower and irrigation along the Nile and its tributaries; Egypt diverts about 46 billion m3 water for irrigation from the Aswan High Dam alone per/year [54], which is 54.8 % of the annual useable water of the Nile. It also enjoys over 13.7 billion KWH per year [16], where the per capita energy service exceeds 1800 KW. Ethiopia is generating only 1078.4 MW power from the Nile tributaries such as Tekeze (300 MW), Fincha (134 MW) and Amerti-Neshi (97 MW) along the Finch River, the Tana lake-based power station of Tana-Beles (460 MW), Tis-Abay-I (14.4 MW) and Tis-Abay-II (73 MW) [15, 24]. The per capita electric service of Ethiopia is about 73 KW [23]. However, Egypt and Sudan contest the Renaissance Dam, which is built along the principal Blue Nile River by Ethiopia. If these are the existing realities about the three nations, where is the ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of the Nile water [47]?

As it is revealed in the CFA of the Nile, unavailability of criteria # 8 (i.e. the water contribution of each riparian nation to its transboundary water) in the criterions [of Article VI (1) of the 1997 UNWC] for equitable use of a transboundary water would unfairly and adversely affect the riparian nations with greater contributions to the discharge of the transboundary water. In contrast, riparian nations having larger proportion of their population dependent on the transboundary water are believed to earn a better benefit from the common water resource. For instance, 93.8 % (over 92 million) of the population of Egypt lives in the Nile basin so that the livelihood of this population, directly or indirectly, is linked to the Nile water; whereas, the Nile basin in Ethiopia was inhabited only by 37.6 million of the country's 2015 total population [36], which becomes 46.02 million in 2023 when it is projected based on the 2007 census data about the national population growth rate (2.8 %) of Ethiopia [13]. Because of this, Egypt becomes a better beneficiary of the Nile water than Ethiopia even if its contribution to this transboundary river is negligible [40].

Although 85 % of the Nile water is from Ethiopia [54], the water has been used by Egypt and Sudan since the time immemorial. Whereas, the share of water used, except the small (1078.4 MW) hydropower from Nile [15], by Ethiopia so far is insignificant. Lesotho enjoys significant economic (electric and revenue) benefit following the built-up of three reservoir dams over its highland (separating the Vaal and Orange River Basins) by the South Africa government [42]; and, where the Johannesburg-Pretoria metropolitans of the downstream nation, South Africa, are enjoying water supply benefit from the water originating from Lesotho. That is, by signing a bilateral economic cooperation under the Lesotho Highland Water Project in 1986, Lesotho and South Africa are earning mutual benefits from their trans-boundary water [42].

Contrary to the reality of Lesotho and South Africa, experiences in the past [28] reveal that Egypt has been speculated to “cooperate” with Ethiopia only if the “cooperation” results in the loosened national solidarity, the economically weakened people and government, and the politically and territorially disintegrated State of Ethiopia. One indicator of this fact is that the Orthodox Christian farmers living in the Nile Basin of the Amhara (i.e. Wollo, Gondar, Gojam and North Showa) and Tigray regions of Ethiopia abstain farm activities during the holidays of the Orthodox Christian Church every month (the principal author's observation as a family of an Orthodox Christian farmer in Gondar). The Orthodox Christian farmers of these regions believe strongly that doing farm activities (i.e. plowing, weeding, cutting and trashing) during the holidays of every month devoted to the holiness of the Orthodox Saints (by the Church) is vice and sin. As the Egyptian Patriarchies (from Alexandria) had led the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church until 1959 EC/1966 [22], such a strong and deep-rooted belief of the farmers, probably, is an outcome of an intentional and institutional-based religious discourse (preach) from the basis by the Egyptian Patriarchs who seemed to have used the Old Testament for sustaining Egypt's supremacy on the use right of the Nile.

For instance, the Orthodox Christian farmers in one of the rural localities (i.e. Recha) of Estie Woreda in Gondar abstain farm activities for 14 days such as (i.e. names of the religious holidays in bracket) the 3rd (Bahta), 5th (Abuye), 6th (Arsema/Qusquam), 7th (Silassoch) 11th (Awuneharra) 12th (Michael) 16th (Kidanemihiret), 19th (Gebrel), 21st (Mariam), 23rd (Georgis), 24th (Tekle-Haimanot), 25th (Merkoriwos), 27th (Medhanialem) and 29th (Bahle-Egiziabher) dates, and the 8 days of the four week-end dates (4 Saturday and 4 Sunday) per/month or for 216 days (14 + 8 = 22, 22-4 = 18 and 18∗12 = 216) per/year (the principal author's experience as a family of an Orthodox Christian farmer in Recha, Gondar). The authors said only 18 days/month, on average, considering that 50 % (4) of the 8 days of the four week-ends often overlap with the non-week-end holidays of each month. It should be noted that the dates of work abstention reveal slight variations from locality to locality depending on the difference in the kind of Orthodox Christian Church available in each locality. Most Orthodox Christian farmers of the areas (pointed in the preceding paragraph) abstain farm activities for 216 days annually implies that they waste about 59.2 % of the total 365 days per/year; and, this in turn adversely affects the crop yield, production and livelihoods of the farmers, and the economic development of Ethiopia.

The authors of this article claim that Egypt seems to have used the Old Testament for maintaining its supremacy on the use right of the Nile, because: (i) Abstaining farm activities during the monthly holidays of the Orthodox Tewahedo Christian Church is an imprint (or deep rooted) belief nowhere the religion prevails worldwide other than in northern and northwestern Ethiopia, where Tana Lake, Tekeze River and most tributaries of the Blue Nile River, constituting 66.2 % (62.6 billion m3) of the total annual flow of the Great Nile River (Table 1), are located. (ii) The Egyptians believe strongly that any economic progress in Ethiopia would threaten Egypt's ‘natural and historical use right’ of the Nile; meaning, they believe that Ethiopia's capacity and desire for the exploitation of the Nile tributaries would increase as its economy booms [28]. (iii) The Egyptians also fear that Ethiopia would become the economic power of the Nile basin region if the country exploits its abundant land and water resources.

Egypt and Sudan have refused to be part of the 2009 CFA on the Nile basin [1,35]. In the recent past, these nations have not reached at consensus in the negotiation about the Renaissance dam with Ethiopia. It is indicated that Egypt has been speculated in discouraging financial donations and loans from the developed nations and the international organizations to the upstream nations (Ethiopia and others) of the Nile [28]. Prof Yakob Arsano (a member of the Ethiopian-side negotiators) through ETV in March 2021 said that the ultimate goal of Egypt's and Sudan's on and off in the Renaissance Dam negotiation could be to interrupt the Dam work or to pullback its pace of construction and/or to pressurize Ethiopia for incorporation of binding rules (restricting further dam development along the Blue Nile) into the final agreement document of the negotiation about the Dam.

The unsuccessful 2019–2021 negotiations among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt

Ethiopia is characterized by low crop yield, deficient food supply and high food insecurity, deep-rooted poverty, low socioeconomic service provision, low transport and communication services, high educated and uneducated unemployment, low Gross Domestic Product (GDP), rapidly growing population and so on [32]. In response to these and other problems, the government of Ethiopia has launched different development projects since the beginning of the 2010s. One of the main development projects is the Grand Renaissance Dam which is built along the Abay (Blue Nile) River where 100 % of the budget of the Dam Project was planned to be covered by the financial contributions of the citizens and government of Ethiopia.

Frequent news have been heard through the digital and electronics media about the dialogue and dispute over the Blue Nile water among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt since Ethiopia had begun the construction of the Renaissance Dam in 2011 [17]. The construction of the dam is primarily aimed at generating hydropower for satisfying some share (%) of the demand of over three-fifth of the population without electric service and for tackling the huge energy supply problem of the manufacturing and service sectors [20,32]. The current dispute over the Blue Nile water among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt is said to have come from Egypt's egocentric and unlawful stand against the construction of the dam by Ethiopia. Principle and technical issues-based negotiations were carried out among Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt since 2012 [7]. However, the three countries have not reached at a consensus especially in the 2019–2020 negotiations, which were done under the “observers” (“mediators?”) of the US and World Bank, and the 2020–2021 negotiations under the observers of the Africa Union [1,24].

The main departure points of the disagreement between Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt in the 2019–2020 negotiation, according to Dr. Silesh Bekele (the former Minister of Water, Irrigation and Energy Ministry of Ethiopia via ETV in July 2020), the Ethiopian side negotiators and [1], were: (i) Egypt's desire on the duration of filling the Renaissance Dam to be over one and half to two-decades instead of the five to seven-years’ duration, which (according to Engineer Gedeon Asfaw, one of the Ethiopian negotiators, to the Aljazeera TV on June 15, 2021) was scientifically and technically designed by the joint experts of Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt; meaning, it was designed by fifteen experts (i.e. five from each) of the three countries; (ii) Egypt's interest to make Ethiopia release water of the Dam anytime drought incidence would be observed in the future; however, remember that Egypt is dominated by arid climate, and the where of the drought incidence, Egypt was talking about, by itself is questionable; and (iii) Egypt's desire to control the water management of the Renaissance Dam, which is unacceptable for Ethiopia [1]. Moreover, Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt also failed to agree in the 2020–2021 negotiation conducted under the observers of the African Union [i.e. the president of South Africa, Cyril Ramaphosa (earlier) and that of Democratic Republic of Congo, Felik Tshisekedi (later)] due to the desire of Egypt and Sudan for incorporation of rules which restrict (deprive) Ethiopia's effort on the construction of more dams along the Abay (Blue Nile) River in the future (Professor Yakob Arsano, one of the negotiating members of Ethiopia, through ETV in March 2021).

Points of argument of Egypt and Sudan on the Nile water use-right of Ethiopia

Egypt and Sudan raise different arguments for contesting the construction of the Renaissance Dam and Ethiopia's use-right of the Nile. Hereafter, evidences-based analyses and reactions are made on five (5) argument points of Egypt and Sudan, focusing on the bases of the arguments and the reasons why the arguments are less convincing, misleading and/or unacceptable from the viewpoint of the existing realities about Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt, and also from the perspective of international water conventions and laws.

The ‘colonial treaties/agreements’

Since Egypt and Sudan were the colonies of Great Britain (UK), British-led treaties were signed about the use of the Nile water in 1902, 1929 and 1959. However, these treaties are not acceptable for five reasons: first, the intentionally misleading meaning of article III of the 1902 treaty has been used by Egypt and Sudan for their contest on Ethiopia's use right of the Nile water. That is, Article III states that the then government of Ethiopia (Menelik II) should not construct or allow to construct any project on the Blue Nile and Baro Rivers or Lake Tana “except in (without) agreement” with the British and Sudanese governments as it “would arrest” the water flow of the Nile [6]. The English version of Article III of the 1902 treaty has two key phrases: (i) “would arrest” – it may mean that the development of any water project along the Nile tributaries by Ethiopia would stop the whole water flow to the downstream nations; (ii) “except in agreement” – this indicates that Ethiopia needs to agree (negotiate) with the British and Sudanese governments if it wants to use the Nile. The phrase “would arrest” looks like an inappropriate, controversial and/or intentionally used phrase of Article III (perhaps, by Britain). It seem that the phrase is misinterpreted by Egypt and Sudan, roughly, as ‘Ethiopia should not touch the Nile water at all as any exploitation of the Abay and other Nile tributaries would seize the whole water flow from Ethiopia’. Thus, Egypt and Sudan seemed to use this likely misleading meaning (of ‘would arrest’) to contest the Renaissance Dam, which is unacceptable (according Silesh Bekele, former Minister of Water, Energy and Irrigation Ministry of Ethiopia in July 2020). The phrase ‘would arrest’ is misleading as evidences of dam projects that had completely stopped the natural flow of huge rivers such as the Nile are rare so far. Second, the 1902 and 1929 treaties are unacceptable as Britain (one of the parties) is not a riparian nation of the Nile.

Third, the 1929 and 1959 agreements were not inclusive as both marginalized Ethiopia and the other upstream nations of the Nile basin. Hence, while the 1929 treaty guarantees over four-fifth of the Nile water to Egypt, the 1959 treaty gave the exclusive use-right of the river water for Egypt and Sudan [6]. Fourth, the 1902, 1929 and 1959 treaties were pure agreements of a colonial power, and the global community and even the colonizers know that colonialism had gone because it was an era of oppression, exploitation, brutality and injustice. That is why Britain had advised (before independence of its East African colonies) the need to revise the 1929 and 1959 treaties in a state that incorporates all the riparian nations of the Nile [40]. Fifth, the 1902, 1929 and 1959 treaties contradict with regulations of the latest international or universal conventions (i.e. 1966, 1996, 1997, 2000 and 2004) about ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of trans-boundary watercourses [52]. Not only this, Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt have also signed a ‘Declaration of Principles’ on the equitable use of the Blue Nile water in 2015. Thus, it seems universal that older agreements (e.g. the 1902, 1929 and 1959 treaties on Nile) about one issue (e.g. water use) become obsolete since the signing or endorsement of new/recent ones (e.g. the 1966, 1996, 1997, 2000, 2004 and 2015) about the same issue. In other words, the dialogues of Egypt and Sudan on the use-right of the Nile water based on provisions of obsolete treaties are unacceptable.

Ethiopia receives “adequate rainfall”: Egypt and Sudan

This argument of Egypt and Sudan seems to have stemmed from factor # 1 (e.g. geo-hydrography, hydrology, climate, …) and factor # 7 (e.g. available alternatives …) of the criteria of the 1997 UNWC accounted in the ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of trans-boundary rivers by riparian nations [41]; i.e., the ‘reasonable use’ of the Nile water by Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt should depend, among others, on the climate condition (e.g. rainfall) of the three countries [47]. Egypt's argument that ‘Ethiopia enjoys adequate rainfall’ is crude. Whether this argument is convincing requires assessing evidences about the challenges of topography, rainfall amount and distribution, arable land supply, farm size, and crop production and productivity of land in Ethiopia [19, 20, 25, 31, 32, 38]. Judging whether this argument of Egypt and Sudan is a strong/weak, relevant/irrelevant, acceptable or unacceptable requires a clear understanding of the four key points presented below.

First, Ethiopia gets better total annual rainfall (900–1500 mm) in the highlands constituting only 45 % of the area of the country where the altitude is over 1000 m [19, 25, 37]. But the greatest share of the Ethiopian highlands is uncultivable due to landscape steepness and ruggedness; besides, it is known by dense settlement, small average farm-size (<0.50 ha/HH) [31], severe degradation and poor land productivity due to severe soil erosion and acidity, over-cultivation and variable rainfall [2, 5, 9, 14, 20,21, 32]. That is why only 44.8 % (16.259 million ha) of the total arable-land of Ethiopia is cultivated under rain-fed and traditional irrigation [19], which is largely part of the highlands where rainfall is better. Second, the adequate rainfall, Egypt is talking about, is erratic and variable in its spatial, seasonal and inter-annual distribution in the highland region itself [4, 19], where the inter-annual rainfall coefficient of variation in the largest area proportion of the country is 31 %–50 %, making crop farming risky [2, 5, 38]. That is why Ethiopia had faced drought in 1974, 1984, 1988, 1991, 1994, 1998, 2004, 2009, 2015/16 [19], 2018 and 2021/2022 (in Oromia and Somale regions). For instance, the country lost 50–100 % of the total annual crop harvest in the 1984 drought [37]. Moreover, rainfall-based crop farming in the highland region of Ethiopia is often impacted by other extreme whether events such as hail, frost, unseasonal rainfall [4], pests and diseases (e.g. wag, fungi) and air pollution-induced acid rain.

Third, the total annual rainfall, ranging within 0–800 mm [25, 38], is scarce in lowlands (having elevation below 1000 m), which constitutes about 55 % of the whole Ethiopia, and where 55.2 % (20 million ha potentially cultivable uncultivated land) of the total arable area of the country is found [19]. This huge uncultivated land requires water for irrigation. Again, over 50.6 % of the irrigation potential of Ethiopia is found in Tekeze (Atbara), Abay (Blue Nile) and Baro-Akobo (Sobat) basins of the Nile (Table 1). Fourth, due to the facts briefed under the ‘First,’ ‘Second’ and ‘Third’ points above, Ethiopia is featured by low crop yield and high food insecurity [5], inadequate access to pure water [36] (i.e. 51.4 % of the rural and 42.7 % of the total population in 2015 had no pure water access) [19], deep-rooted poverty (i.e. 29.6 % of the population was below the poverty line when the Renaissance Dam Project was launched) [32] and acute shortage of energy (i.e. over 60 % of the population being without electric service) (Prof Yakob Arsano via the Amhara TV on June 11, 2021). These socioeconomic needs, which are well recognized by criteria # 2, Article VI (1), of the 1997 UNWC and the ‘climate resilient green economic development policy’ of the government [20], therefore, necessitate Ethiopia to exploit its rivers for hydropower, pure water supply and irrigation services.

The existence of different rivers other than the Nile in Ethiopia

Egypt and Sudan raise the existence of different rivers (other than the Blue Nile) in Ethiopia as one justification for their objection against the construction of the Renaissance Dam along the Abay (Blue Nile) River. This argument, again, seems to have been raised based on factor # 7 of the seven criterions of Article VI (1) of the 1997 UNWC accounted in for the ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of trans-boundary water among riparian nations. That is, the distribution of ‘equitable and reasonable (water) use’ among (say) Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt should be done on account of criteria # 7 (‘available alternatives … ’) and other factors [47].

However, to which of the rivers [having comparative value with the value of Abay/Blue Nile] do Egypt and Sudan want Ethiopia to take hydropower projects such as the Renaissance Dam? Should it have been built along the Gumara River (Gondor), Megech River, Ribb River, Meterai River, Wanqa-Chena River, Gumara River (Gojam), Chemoga River, Gilgel-Beles River, Bikolo-Abay River, Dabus River, Dedessa River, Muger River, Fincha River, Guder River, Jemma River, Beshilo River, Baro River, Akobo River, Tekeze River or the Upper tributaries of Mereb River (in Wollo and Tigray)? Or which other river of Ethiopia was appropriate for such a hydropower project? These rivers of Ethiopia have a sum annual flow of 86.52 billion m3 (Table 1), which is 69.8 % (including the Rift-valley lakes) or 73.7 % of the annual flow of all rivers, and 65.3 % of the hydropower and 50.6 % of the irrigation potentials (Table 1). The first 16 rivers listed above are tributaries of the Abay (Blue Nile) River. Baro and Akobo Rivers are tributaries of Sobat River, and Tekeze and the upper streams of Mereb are tributaries of the Atbara River. Moreover, all the 20 rivers of Ethiopia (listed above) are tributaries of the Great Nile River - the use-right of which is claimed by Egypt and Sudan.

In Table 1, the total hydropower generation potential of Ethiopia is about 158,700 GW. The hydropower generation potential in the ‘Nile basin’ of Ethiopia is about 103,700 GW, which includes Abay (78,800 GW), Baro-Akobo (18,900 GW) and Tekeze River (6000 GW). And, the hydropower potential of the country from the ‘Off-Nile basin’ (i.e. other than Abay, Baro-Akobo and Tekeze) is about 55,000 GW (158,700–103,700 GW (see Table 1). Ethiopia has better exploited those rivers, which are found outside the Nile (Off-Nile) basin (see Fig. 2). Even if the annual flow of Wabishebele (3.4 billion m3) and Genale-Dawa (6.0 billion m3) rivers is relatively small (Table 1), these two rivers are being used for hydropower and irrigation. That is, Ethiopia built the Melka-Wakena dam with an electric generation capacity of 153 MW along the Wabishebele; and, Yadot (0.4 MW) and Genale-Dawa-III (254 MW) are built along Genale-Dawa [15, 23].

The Omo-Gibe (16.6 billion m3) and Awash (4.9 billion m3) Rivers, having a total annual discharge of 21.5 billion m3, are being intensively exploited for hydropower and irrigation; i.e. Ethiopia has built three dams with an electric generation capacity of 2474 MW along the Omo-Gibe River such as Gibe-I (184 MW), Gibe-II (420 MW) and Gibe-III (1870 MW) even if the country is unable to narrow the huge gap between the supply and the raising demand of energy [15]. Hydropower stations such as Koka (43.2 MW), Awash-II (32 MW) and Awash III (32 MW) are built along the Awash River, and the Aba-Samuel Station (6.6 MW) is built in the Afar Triangle [15]. Generally, about 73.5 % (2995.2 MW) of the total hydropower (4073.6 MW) of Ethiopia (before 2020) was generated from the ‘Off-Nile basin’ where the hydropower generation potential of Ethiopia is 34.7 % only; in other words, only 26.5 % (1078.4 MW) of the hydropower was generated from the water resource in the Nile basin (Fig. 2), where 65.3 % of the total hydropower potential is located (Fig. 2; Table 1). This is another reasonable indicator of the appropriateness of the Renaissance Dam development within the Nile basin.

Based on criteria # 7, Article VI (1), of the UNWC [21], Egypt argues that Ethiopia should have shifted the Renaissance Dam to other rivers (than the Blue Nile). But which other alternative river has a ‘comparative value’ for building the Renaissance Dam [49]? The answer is no; this is so because, the cost of constructing two, three or more dams (outside the Nile basin) that enable to generate a hydropower equivalent to the designed energy amount of the Renaissance Dam (5550 GW) would be (by far) more expensive than that of the Grand Dam – especially for a country which (directly or indirectly, overtly or covertly) is banned to receive loans for its development projects from international lenders [28]. Ethiopia has the largest hydropower potential (65.3 %) on the major tributaries of the Nile River (Table 1).

Egypt seems to have effectively used criteria #1, #2 and #7 of Article VI of the 1997 UNWC for its argument during the Renaissance Dam negotiation. Criteria #1 (biophysical conditions) # 5 (the existing and potential water use …), #6 (conservation, protection, …) and #7 (available alternatives …) of the UNWC could also benefit Ethiopia. For instance, criteria # 5 requires considering the current share (%) of the Nile water-use by Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt, and the potential use of the river [47]; this criteria may favor Ethiopia as the share of the Nile water used by the country so far is limited. Besides, the Egyptians and Sudanese played no role whenever Victoria and Tana lakes have been suffering from invasive (water hyacinth) plants. However, Ethiopia has spent huge financial, material and labor resources for liberating Tana lake from an invasive (in Amharic known as) ‘emboch’ plant in 2019–2021. Riparian nations should use, conserve and protect their transboundary water according to Article V (2) and Article VI (1) of the 1997 UNWC. Article V (2) states not only the right of the riparian nations for equitable and reasonable water use but it also stresses on the need to participate in the ‘development and protection’ of their trans-boundary watercourse e.g. the Nile River [47,52].

The Nile basin of Ethiopia, based on own projection of the 2015 population of the basin using the 2007 national growth rate (2.8 %) of Ethiopia [13], was inhabited by 46.02 million in 2023. This Ethiopian population of the Nile basin has the right to get electric power and pure water supply services from the water resources of the basin. Besides, over 60 % of the total Ethiopian population being without electric service [24], the frequently interrupted power provision, and the high and growing energy demand of the manufacturing and the service sectors [32] necessitated Ethiopia to exploit its rivers for hydropower generation. These (socioeconomic needs) facts of Ethiopia are well supported by criteria # 2 (socioeconomic needs) of Article VI (1) of the 1997 UNWC about the ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of trans-boundary water such as the Nile River [21]. Thus, the built-up of the Renaissance Dam along the Blue Nile River looks to have stemmed (not from Ethiopia's intention to threaten the interests of the Egyptians and Sudanese but) based on legitimate, verifiable and convincing facts about the ongoing dam construction.

Egypt looks assuming as if the Nile River is its only resource

It is known that the Egyptians are entertaining hydroelectric power (13.7 billion KWH per year in 2014) [16] and irrigation-based surplus food supply due to the limitless exploitation of the Nile River to which 85 % of the water stems from Ethiopia [35]. Contending the construction of the Renaissance Dam along the Blue Nile by Ethiopia, Egypt seems assuming as if the Nile is its only resource. The Foreign Affairs Minister of Egypt (Sameh Hassan Shokry Selim at the UN Security Council Forum, July 8, 2021) said that the Renaissance Dam ‘has casted a long and dark shadow on the future fate’ of 100 million Egyptians [48]. This is not a reality if it is assessed in view of criteria # 1 (geography, hydrography, hydrology …) and # 7 (available alternatives having comparative value …) of the 1997 UNWC. The argument becomes less convincing as Egypt has alternatives such as abundant ground water and surface (Mediterranean and Red Sea) water supply, high solar and wind energy potentials, and rich petroleum (oil) and natural gas supply [16]. Based on criteria #1 of the 1997 UNWC, the low-lying, sandy and plain topography favored Egypt to be rich in ground water supply. The resources listed above, based on factor # 7 of the 1997 UNWC, are alternatives having values comparative with the value of the Nile water; in other words, these resources can be surrogates of the expected gaps (if any) in crop production, power generation and the import-export economic role of the Nile, which could perhaps be observed due to the equitable use of the River's water by the upstream nations such as Ethiopia and others. Egypt is among the five top producers of crude oil and natural gas in Africa such as Nigeria, Egypt (itself), Angola, Libya and Algeria [16,27]. Moreover, Egypt has abundant untapped wind energy potential at the coasts and on the surfaces of the Mediterranean and Red Seas, and also untapped oil and natural gas potentials under the floors of the Seas [16]. Despite these facts, Egypt seems pretending to the international community as if the Nile water is the only and ultimate resource of the country.

It is, again, known that an estimated volume of 10–16 billion m3 water is lost from the Aswan Dam and Nassir lake every year due to high evaporation (Dr. Silesh Bekele, former Minister of Water, Irrigation and Energy Ministry of Ethiopia in July 2020) [24]. This water volume (10–16 billion m3) is thought to fill the Renaissance Dam of Ethiopia in 5–7 years. Significant volume of the Nile water is also lost in Egypt because of unwise (lack of conserved) use of water for irrigation, and due to the cultivation of high water-consumptive crops (e.g. rice, sugar cane) in the country. The availability of alternatives such as the huge ground water supply, petroleum, natural gas, solar and wind energy, and the significant loss of the Nile water due to mismanagement indicate that the objection of Egypt on Ethiopia's use right of the Nile looks like rooted neither from the quest for ‘equitable and reasonable use’ of the river water nor from the legitimacy about it.

“The Renaissance Dam significantly impacts the downstream nations”: Egypt and Sudan

Egypt and Sudan argue that the Renaissance Dam would have significant socioeconomic impacts on the downstream nations (themselves). This argument seemed to have originated from factor # 2 (i.e. socioeconomic needs), # 3 (i.e. population reliant on the trans-boundary water) and # 4 (i.e. the impact of the water use by one riparian nation over the other) of the criteria under Article VI (1) and Article VII (i.e. ‘obligation not to cause significant harm’) of the UNWC [47]. In fact, criteria # 2 also favors Ethiopia since the Renaissance Dam Project has been justified based on the country's ‘socioeconomic needs’ such as alleviating the energy supply problem of over three-fifth of the population without electric service, lessening the frequent power cuts, satisfying the high and increasing power demand of the manufacturing and the service sectors, reducing the food insecurity and poverty problems, improving income and others [32].

Initially (before September 2020), Sudan was convinced that the Renaissance Dam would be useful in many ways for its citizens. That is, enhancing the power supply (to Sudan), flood control, conserving and regulating water flow to the dams, reducing sediment load into the dams and minimizing the cost of clearing sediment out of the dams are among the main positive impacts (benefits) of the Renaissance Dam to the downstream nations such as Sudan and Egypt [46]. Above all, this Dam would have no significant impact on the water shares of Sudan and Egypt as: (i) it was primarily planned to generate a hydropower; (ii) its filling in the last four years (2020–2023) was done during the rainy (July–August) season when the discharge of the Blue Nile River becomes so high to the extent that it induces flood hazard in the downstream nations (Sudan and Egypt). But changing its stand on the benefits of the Dam, the transitional government of Sudan has preferred to stress on the possible “adverse impacts” of the Dam since Sept 2020. According to Ambassador Ibrahim Edris (one of the Ethiopian-side negotiators via ETV on January 4, 2021), Egypt and Sudan argue that huge shares (10 %–20 %) of their population would be displaced to abroad due to the impact of the Dam Project of Ethiopia. Egypt's Minister of Foreign Affairs (Sameh Hassan Shokry Selim) also argued (at the UN Security Council meeting, July 8, 2021) that the huge Renaissance Dam, being built along the ‘artery’ (Blue Nile) of the Egyptians, ‘has casted a long and dark shadow on the future fate’ of more than 100 million people [48]. However, Egypt and Sudan have no tangible evidences about their hypotheses.

A scholar wrote that the impact of the Renaissance Dam (together with the dam projects of South-Sudan and Sudan) on the water budget of Egypt becomes 90 % and the per capita water of Egypt would decline to 350 m3 below the water poverty line (i.e. 1000 m3) by 2050 if Ethiopia constructs further dams on the Nile tributaries [54]. These crude figures indicate that this scholar was unable to show the specific tangible adverse effect of the Renaissance Dam on the water share of Egypt. However, one thing the same scholar [54] has underlined is that Ethiopia (by exploiting its abundant water and land) would monopolize the electric supply power in the Horn of Africa and the Nile Basin region. This long-run expectation is the likely key reason behind the unconvincing dialogues of Egypt and Sudan in the negotiation on the equitable use of the Blue Nile River.

Conclusion and suggestions

The 1997 UNWC is a better platform for the negotiation on the use of trans-boundary rivers such as the Nile although the seven criteria of Article VI (1) of the convention are not sufficient. The 2019–2021 negotiations on the Renaissance Dam ended up without consensus due to Egypt's quest for lengthy duration (15–21 years) of filling the dam, its desire for the release of the dam water during drought and controlling the dam water management, and for incorporation of rules that restrict the construction of more dams along the Nile River by Ethiopia. The use of the Euphrates, Tigris, Niger and Zambezi Rivers for hydropower and irrigation by riparian nations of the respective rivers, and the mutual benefit of Lesotho and South Africa (from water) are good experiences of the equitable use of trans-boundary water. Egypt and Sudan are intensively utilizing the Nile water for hydropower and irrigation; whereas, Ethiopia's utilization of the Nile water is insignificant.

Egypt's and Sudan's arguments on the Renaissance Dam negotiation and the use right of the Nile were based on criteria #1, #3, #4 and #7 of Article VI (1) of the 1997 UNWC. The colonial treaties, the “adequate rainfall” and availability of other rivers of Ethiopia, assuming the Nile as the only resource and the impact of the Dam were among the specific argument points of Egypt and Sudan in the negotiation about the Dam. Most arguments of Egypt and Sudan against Ethiopia's use right of the Nile seem unacceptable: (1) as nearly 3/4th of the water flow, 2/3rd of the hydropower and over half of the irrigation potential of all Ethiopian rivers is confined to the Nile basin; (2) as over 3/5th (60 %), above 2/5th (42.7 %) and less than 1/3rd (29.6 %) of the population has no electric service, no pure water access and below poverty line (in 2011), respectively; and (3) as their objection seems originating from: (i) their desire to see Ethiopia within the vicious circle of poverty forever so that their ‘natural and historic use-right of the Nile’ is sustained, and (ii) their anxiety of the Renaissance Dam and other dams-based economic boom and the subsequent increase in the diplomatic and the strategic geopolitical importance of Ethiopia in the Nile Basin, the Horn of Africa, the Middle East Region and the whole Africa.

The three countries need to be flexible for the ‘give and take’ principle-based negotiation to ensure the equitable use of the Nile water. The Egyptians and Sudanese should begin believing that the exploitation of the Nile tributaries for hydropower (and other uses by Ethiopia) is inherent, legal and inevitable. Egypt should focus on improving the inefficient water use for irrigation. Ethiopia should: (i) work for the endorsement of the two more criterions added in the CFA of the Nile basin so as to enhance its negotiating capacity, and (ii) use its conservation efforts of the Nile basin, supported by Article V of the UNWC, as one of the points of argument for the Nile water negotiations in the future. Further study is needed about the impacts of the Renaissance Dam on Egypt and Sudan, and the available alternative resources of Egypt as our study lacked quantified data on these issues. It is ambiguous to quantify the effect of the Dam on the water share of the downstream nations so far as the Dam construction is not completed yet and as it is not fully functioning yet. Thus, it is difficult to show the accurate adverse effect of the Dam on the water share of Egypt and Sudan. Again, it was not possible to access quantified data about the ground water, petroleum and solar energy potentials of Egypt for displaying the country's available alternatives (based on criteria #7, Article VI of the UNWC).
