Type 2 diabetes and risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and multiple myeloma: a pooled analysis
Authors: Andres V Ardisson Korat, Emily L Deubler, Kimberly A Bertrand, Lauren R Teras, James V Lacey, Alpa V Patel, Bernard A Rosner, Yu-Hsiang Shu, Charlie Zhong, Sophia S Wang, Brenda M Birmann, Chun R Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior studies suggest positive associations of type 2 diabetes (T2D) with risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and multiple myeloma (MM), but few studies had sufficient statistical power to evaluate associations for specific histological subtypes.MethodsWe pooled data from the Cancer Prevention Study-II Nutrition Cohort, California Teachers’ Study, Health Professionals Follow-up Study, Nurses’ Health Study (NHS), and NHSII cohorts and a sample of Kaiser Permanente Southern California members (585,114 total study participants). We confirmed incident diagnoses of NHL and MM using medical records or cancer registries. T2D history was assessed by self-report or clinical diagnosis. We estimated the associations of T2D history (yes/no) and T2D duration with risk of overall NHL, NHL subtypes, or MM using multivariable Cox regression models adjusted for age, sex, cohort, follow-up year, race, education, smoking, and body mass index.ResultsWe confirmed 11,478 NHL and 2,783 MM diagnoses over a median follow-up of 20 years. T2D history was not associated with overall NHL risk but was positively associated with risk of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL; hazard ratio [HR] = 1.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.04 to 1.28) and MM (HR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.07 to 1.35) and inversely associated with risk of lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma/Waldenström macroglobulinemia (HR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.27 to 0.75), T-cell NHL (HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.62 to 0.97), and mycosis fungoides/Sézary syndrome (HR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.46 to 0.98). T2D duration was positively associated with risk of DLBCL and MM.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a role for T2D in DLBCL and MM; thus, T2D prevention may be important in reducing their incidence. Some unexpected inverse associations require further investigation.

Introduction

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) comprises a heterogeneous group of lymphoid malignancies classified into ≥40 histological subtypes.1 In the United States, 104,040 new NHL cases and 23,850 deaths were expected in 2025.2 Severe immune compromise is the most consistent NHL risk factor, but it explains a small proportion of cases.3 Other risk factors include family history of lymphoid malignancy, history of autoimmune disease, some oncogenic infections, height, obesity, and occupational exposure to pesticides and solvents.3,4 Several of these associations exhibit significant heterogeneity by NHL subtype.3,5 Many of these factors possess immune-modulating properties, suggesting that other factors that influence immune function are plausible candidates for exploration in NHL etiology. Multiple myeloma (MM) is a plasma cell neoplasm (ie, like B-cell NHL subtypes, a mature B-lymphoid malignancy) and the second most common hematological malignancy.1 MM was projected to account for 36,110 new cases and 12,030 deaths in the United States in 2025.2 Most established risk factors are not modifiable and include older age, male sex, African ancestry, and family history of hematological malignancy.6-8 To date, young adult and “usual” adult obesity (cumulatively averaged obesity during adulthood) are the only known modifiable risk factors for MM.7

A history of type 2 diabetes (T2D) is an established risk factor for several solid tumors.9 Emerging evidence suggests an etiological role of hyperglycemia and T2D in lymphomagenesis.10,11 Previous meta-analyses reported significant positive associations between T2D history and risk of overall NHL (pooled risk ratios = 1.15 to 1.28).10-13 However, these meta-analyses included both case-control and prospective cohort studies, inconsistent adjustment for covariates, and a relatively small number of NHL diagnoses, especially for NHL subtypes, which introduced heterogeneity in their pooled estimates.11 T2D is commonly diagnosed in patients presenting with MM14,15 and is suspected to influence myelomagenesis through mechanisms related to hyperglycemia and insulin resistance,16,17 consistent with evidence from observational studies.11

The limited information on NHL subtype-specific associations with T2D status warrants a detailed examination in a pooled prospective analysis with sufficient statistical power for subtype-specific analyses and harmonized exposure and covariate data. Further, few studies have reported associations of T2D duration with risk of NHL or MM. Therefore, this pooled study aims to evaluate the associations of T2D history and duration with risk of NHL—including several individual histologic subtypes—and MM. We hypothesized that T2D history and longer T2D duration would be associated with higher risk of NHL and MM.

Methods

Study populations

We included data from 5 prospective cohorts: the California Teachers Study (CTS),18 the Cancer Prevention Study-II (CPS-II) Nutrition Cohort,19 the Health Professionals Follow-up Study, (HPFS),20 the Nurses’ Health Study (NHS), and the NHSII.21 We also included data from a nested case-control sample of 41,634 individuals from the Kaiser Permanente Southern California (KPSC) member cohort (NHLs and MMs diagnosed between 2007 and 2014 and 5 controls per case selected using incidence density sampling), with estimated control sampling probabilities to facilitate the pooled prospective analysis (see “Statistical Analyses” below).22 Cohort participants provided information on their medical history, medications, lifestyle, and other disease risk factors via baseline and follow-up questionnaires (Table S1). Data for the KPSC case-control sample were obtained from electronic medical records. We excluded participants with cancer diagnoses (except nonmelanoma skin cancer) before study enrollment, those whose diabetes diagnosis occurred before age 30 (for CPS-II and CTS only; to exclude likely type 1 diabetes diagnoses since those cohorts did not query diabetes type), those with missing or unknown baseline information on diabetes diagnosis (for CPS-II and CTS only, since these 2 cohorts did not collect T2D diagnosis date), and those with missing height values.

This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of City of Hope, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, Emory University, KPSC, Tufts University, and those of participating cancer registries as required. Informed consent was implied by the return of the respective cohort’s baseline questionnaire. The need for informed consent was waived by the KPSC IRB.

Case ascertainment

Incident (first primary) NHL diagnoses (ICD-8 or -9 codes 200, 202, and 204.1; ICD-O-3 9590, 9591, 9596, 9597, 9671, 9675, 9678-9680, 9684, 9690, 9691, 9695, 9698-9670, 9673, 9678-9680, 9684, 9687-9689, 9699, 9700-9702, 9708, 9709, 9712, 9714, 9716, 9717, 9724-9728, 9735, 9737, 9760, 9761, 9764, 9823, 9826, 9832, 9833, 9835, 9836, and 9940) and incident plasma cell neoplasm diagnoses (ICD-8 or -9 code 203; ICD-O-2 code 9732; ICD-O-3 code 9731, 9732, and 9734) were self-reported and verified via medical records or linkage with state cancer registries, or they were identified through linkage to a SEER-affiliated cancer registry (CTS and KPSC). All NHL cases were further classified by histological subtype according to the World Health Organization classification scheme.23 There were 11,478 NHL diagnoses over 12,268,525 person-years and 2,783 MM diagnoses over 12,115,736 person-years of follow-up. We analyzed the following NHL subtypes (see Table S2 for the endpoint-specific ICD codes): Burkitt lymphoma (BL, n = 95), chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL, n = 2,977), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL, n = 2776), follicular lymphoma (FL, n = 1,833), mantle cell leukemia (MCL, n = 307), mycosis fungoides/Sézary syndrome (MF/SS, n = 265), marginal zone lymphoma (MZL, n = 1,005), peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL, n = 310), Waldenström macroglobulinemia/lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL/WM, n = 224), and other NHL (n = 1,024). We analyzed all the plasma cell neoplasms in aggregate due to the inability to disentangle the subtypes in 3 cohorts (HPFS, NHS, NHSII), and we refer to those cases hereafter as “MM” for brevity. Plasmacytomas were rare overall, comprising an estimated <5% of the MM endpoint.

Exposure assessment

For the 5 cohort studies, we defined our primary exposure, history of T2D, based on self-reports of physician-diagnosed diabetes or insulin use (CPS-II and CTS)24,25 or self-reported T2D diagnosis confirmed by a supplementary questionnaire (HPFS, NHS, NHSII) (Table S3).26 For CPS-II and CTS, participants who self-reported diabetes diagnoses were considered to have T2D only if aged ≥30 years at diagnosis. For KPSC, T2D diagnoses were captured from electronic medical records (Table S3).27 For all cohorts except CTS, time-varying T2D duration was defined as the difference between the applicable questionnaire return date (or follow-up cutoff date for KPSC) minus the T2D diagnosis date, in years.

Statistical analyses

To harmonize the time intervals between questionnaires across cohorts, we subdivided follow-up time into 1-year intervals and classified person-time according to the most recent cohort-specific questionnaire. We calculated person-time for each participant from the return date of the baseline questionnaire (1992 CPS-II; 1995-1996, CTS; 1986, HPFS; 1976, NHS; 1989, NHSII) or study baseline (KPSC; 2007 or first plan enrollment after 2007) to NHL or MM diagnosis, death, other cancer diagnoses (except for nonmelanoma skin cancer), or end of follow-up (HPFS: 2012; KPSC and NHS: 2014; CTS & CPS-II: 2015; NHS-II: 2019), whichever came first.

We used extended Cox proportional hazards regression models, fit to the pooled sample and stratified by sex, cohort, age (continuous), and calendar year of follow-up, to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the associations between T2D history and risk of NHL and MM separately. Sampling weights (inverse of the sampling probability) were applied to the controls in KPSC to analyze the KPSC study sample as a cohort design using the Cox models.28,29 We observed little evidence of heterogeneity by cohort using Cochran’s Q test in a random-effects meta-analysis;30 thus we directly pooled the data across all studies to maximize sample size. In multivariable models, we adjusted for educational attainment (< high school, high school graduate, college graduate, missing), race (White, non-White), smoking status (never, past, current, missing), height (continuous, cm), and cumulatively averaged (“usual”) adult body mass index (BMI, continuous, kg/m2).4 We excluded HRs and CIs for any stratum with <10 endpoint diagnoses. Missing continuous covariate values (1.6% of records) were imputed using age-, sex-, cohort-, and calendar year-specific medians. All covariates were modeled as time-varying, except for sex, race, height, and educational attainment. We used the time on study (follow-up time) as the timescale. T2D history was modeled as a binary variable (yes/no). The duration of T2D was modeled in 3 categories: <5 years, 5-9.9 years, and ≥10 years. CTS was excluded from the analysis of T2D duration.

To assess potential effect measure modification, we performed stratified analyses according to sex (male, female), self-reported race (White, Black/African American, Native American/Pacific Islander/Asian American; other), usual adult BMI (<25 kg/m2, 25-29.9 kg/m2, ≥30 kg/m2), and young adult BMI (<25 kg/m2, ≥25 kg/m2) using models adjusted for sex, cohort, age, and calendar year. Product terms between T2D status and the stratum-defining variable were included in those models. We calculated the P value for interaction with a Wald test. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P values <.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

At baseline, participants were 50.5 years old on average, 77% were women, and 90% identified as White (Table 1, Table S4). T2D history was not associated with risk of overall NHL (multivariable HR = 0.99, 95% CI = 0.94 to 1.05) but was positively associated with DLBCL risk (HR = 1.15, 95% CI = 1.04 to 1.28) and inversely associated with risk of LPL/WM (HR= 0.45, 95% CI = 0.27 to 0.75), T-cell NHL (T-NHL; HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.62 to 0.97), and MF/SS (HR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.46 to 0.98) (Figure 1, Table S5). No significant associations were observed between T2D history and other NHL subtypes. T2D history was positively associated with MM risk (HR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.07 to 1.35) (Figure 1, Table S5).

We did not observe significant effect measure modification by sex for the associations of T2D history with overall NHL risk, most histologic subtypes of NHL, or MM (Pinteraction > .05), except for MCL (Pinteraction = .03), for which the HRs were significantly lower for women (Table S7). Additionally, we did not observe statistically significant effect measure modification by race (Table S8), except for the associations of T2D history with DLBCL and CLL/SLL (Pinteraction = .04 and .05, respectively), which were weaker for White than for non-White participants. For the associations between T2D history and MM risk, we observed stronger positive associations for non-white than for white participants (Pinteraction = .009) (Table S8). We did not observe evidence of effect measure modification by adult BMI, except for the associations with T-NHL risk (Pinteraction = .045), which were stronger in the 25-29.9 kg/m2 BMI stratum (Table S9). Similarly, we observed no evidence of effect measure modification by young adult BMI except for DLBCL risk (Pinteraction = .05); those associations were stronger for participants with young adult BMI <25 kg/m2 (Table S10).

Discussion

In this large, pooled prospective study, T2D history was not associated with risk of overall NHL. However, we found associations of T2D history with 15% higher risk of DLBCL and with 20% higher MM risk compared with participants without T2D. Conversely, we observed associations of T2D history with 55%, 22%, and 33% lower risk of LPL/WM, T-NHL, and MF/SS. These inverse associations were unexpected and require confirmation from other prospective studies. In analyses of T2D duration, compared with participants without T2D, risks of overall NHL and DLBCL were elevated for those who had T2D for more than 5 years, and risk did not increase further with longer duration. Conversely, the inverse associations for overall T-NHL were more pronounced ≥10 years after T2D diagnosis. For MM, risk was most elevated within the first 10 years of T2D diagnosis. In stratified analyses, we observed little evidence of effect measure modification by sex, race, or young adult or usual adult BMI. However, our results for DLBCL and MM were stronger among Black/African American participants.

The lack of association for overall NHL risk differs from previous meta-analyses, which reported associations of T2D history with 15% to 28% increased overall NHL risk.10-13 Few studies have reported associations for individual histological subtypes, and in those studies findings differed by study design, highlighting the importance of relevant etiological windows. For example, 2 case-control studies found nonsignificant positive associations with T-NHL risk,31,32 whereas 1 prospective study observed nonsignificant inverse associations with risk of T-NHL.14 Furthermore, prior meta-analyses did not harmonize covariate adjustment. Our findings for MM were consistent with results from a previous meta-analysis of 10 studies (7 cohort, 3 case-control) reporting a borderline significant increased MM risk (HR = 1.22, 95% CI = 0.98 to 1.53) comparing participants with vs without a history of T2D.11

The mechanisms linking T2D with lymphomagenesis are not fully understood. T2D has been associated with several factors relevant to the etiology of NHL, including chronic inflammation,33 immunosuppression,34 and B-cell and T-cell dysfunction.35 Additionally, hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia are hypothesized to be associated with tumor formation and growth for many types of cancer.36 Insulin may promote cell proliferation and tumor growth directly and through the upregulation of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1).16 Further, variants in the IGF1 gene related to T2D may influence the risk of MM.17 Previous findings from the HPFS and NHS cohorts support a link between endogenous insulin production and risk of several cancers.36 For instance, risks of several cancers increased in the first 8 years after T2D diagnosis, consistent with a rise in endogenous insulin production during this 8-year period, as evidenced by increased levels of C-peptide.36 Both C-peptide levels and cancer risk subsequently decreased 8-10 years after T2D diagnosis, suggesting that declining endogenous insulin production might explain the lack of increase in cancer risk after 8-10 years’ T2D duration.36 In the present pooled analysis, we observed elevated DLBCL risk 5-9.9 years after T2D diagnosis that remained constant ≥10 years after diagnosis, suggesting that T2D-related mechanisms other than hyperinsulinemia may influence longer-term risk of DLBCL. In contrast, MM risk was highest within 10 years after T2D diagnosis and returned to baseline ≥10 years after T2D diagnosis, supporting hyperinsulinemia as a relevant mechanism in MM etiology.

Excess adiposity is a common risk factor for T2D, MM,37 and NHL, in particular for DLBCL.4 Although our observed associations were independent of BMI, excess adiposity not captured by BMI may introduce residual confounding.38 Additionally, therapies to treat T2D may be of importance for NHL incidence. For instance, epidemiological evidence supports an inverse association of metformin use with risk of overall NHL39-41 but with limited evidence for specific NHL subtypes.41 In contrast, positive associations of insulin,40,42 alpha-glucosidase inhibitors, and sulfonylurea use with NHL risk have been reported.40 These therapies may influence lymphomagenesis or myelomagenesis by controlling hyperglycemia or downregulating IGF-1, or by supplementing exogenous insulin, which may exert immunogenic or tumorigenic effects.13,36 Furthermore, T2D treatment type is related to severity, which may also be associated with T2D duration. We were not able to adjust for treatment in these analyses because not all studies collected these data; furthermore, medication use was a component of the T2D definition for some participating cohorts.

However, these mechanisms are unlikely to explain the inverse associations with risk of T-NHL, MF/SS, and LPL/WM. We are not aware of other epidemiological studies evaluating the role of T2D in the development of LPL/WM. Of relevance, our findings mirror inverse associations observed for usual or young adult BMI with LPL/WM risk;4,43 those findings were hypothesized to implicate an influence on the physiological milieu of IgM-producing B-cells in fat-associated lymphoid clusters surrounding adipose tissue.43,44 However, any underlying biological mechanisms linking hyperglycemia or hyperinsulinemia with MF/SS or T-cell malignancies are unknown. Lastly, we cannot rule out the possibility of chance in these findings.

This study has some limitations, including relatively small numbers for rare NHL subtypes, which may have resulted in imprecise effect estimates, warranting caution in their interpretation. We cannot rule out residual confounding by excess adiposity not captured by BMI. We also lacked detailed information on certain NHL risk factors, such as history of oncogenic infections or occupational exposure to pesticides and solvents; however, these exposures are expected to be rare in the included cohorts. Further, we lacked clinical measures of hyperglycemia to confirm T2D diagnoses, their severity, or the level of T2D control. Participants in the 5 cohort studies provided self-reported T2D diagnosis, which was confirmed by supplementary questionnaires that increased their validity. We expect misclassification in KPSC (eg, due to erroneous codes in electronic medical records) to be fairly small. In our main analyses, we evaluated the associations of T2D with several NHL subtypes and did not adjust for multiple comparisons, given that each endpoint was tested separately to support assessment of subtype-specific heterogeneity. Similarly, in effect modification analyses, we evaluated T2D associations across several variable strata and did not perform multiple-comparison adjustment; thus, we acknowledge the possibility of false positive findings. In 3 cohorts, we cannot disentangle diagnoses of plasmacytomas from full-blown MM; however, we estimated that plasmacytomas represented <5% of the MM endpoint. Finally, we could not control for or directly assess potential effects of T2D medications on risk of NHL and MM because details of T2D treatment were unavailable for most participants.39,42,45

Our pooled study has many strengths, including the prospective design, more than 10,000 NHL cases and nearly 3,000 MM cases, characterization of NHL histological subtypes, and long duration of follow-up, allowing us to examine associations by T2D duration. Additionally, we had detailed information on important suspected and established risk factors to adjust for potential confounding and to conduct stratified analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, and young and usual adult BMI.

In summary, we observed that T2D history is associated with elevated risk of DLBCL and MM. These findings suggest that T2D prevention, independent of BMI, may be important in strategies to reduce MM and DLBCL incidence. The unexpected inverse associations for T-NHL, MF/SS, and LPL/WM should be interpreted with caution, given the small numbers of “exposed” cases and the lack of plausible biological mechanisms to explain them. These observations warrant confirmation in other epidemiological studies and further work to elucidate potential mechanisms.
