Association between statin therapy as primary prevention and mortality in adults 50 years and older with chronic kidney disease without other indications
Authors: Julia Malmquist, Jason Davidge, Karl Bjurström, Anders Halling, Björn Agvall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) increases cardiovascular and mortality risk. Guidelines recommend statins for primary prevention in individuals aged ≥50 years with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, but implementation and outcomes remain unclear. This study examined statin use and its association with all-cause mortality in individuals with CKD and no other indication for statin therapy.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using healthcare data derived from Region Halland, Sweden. Adults aged 50–89 years with ≥2 eGFR measurements <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 during 2018–2021 were included, excluding those with prior cardiovascular disease, diabetes, primary hyperlipidemia, dialysis, or kidney transplantation. Follow-up from January 2021 to December 2023 included prevalence of statin use and all-cause mortality. Cox regression estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HR) for mortality, accounting for age, sex, CKD stage, albuminuria, hypertension, and use of statins, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAASi) inhibitors, and sodium–glucose-cotransporter-2 (SGLT2i) inhibitors.ResultsAmong 7,177 individuals (50% women), 38% received statins. Statin use was associated with lower three-year mortality (14% vs. 19%, p < 0.001). Crude mortality rates were 42 vs. 57 deaths per 1,000 person-years. Statin use was associated with reduced mortality risk (HR 0.82, 95% CI:0.72–0.92). Higher age, advanced CKD stage, and hypertension were independently associated with increased mortality. RAASi use was protective (HR 0.31, 95% CI:0.27–0.35).ConclusionStatin therapy was associated with lower all-cause mortality but was underutilized in adults ≥50 years with CKD. These findings have supported broader implementation of guideline-recommended statin therapy.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects nearly one in ten adults worldwide and is associated with increased cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, even in early stages [1–3]. In older adults, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events, often exceeding the risk of progression to end-stage renal failure [4,5]. Most patients with CKD are treated in primary care, often as part of multimorbidity, without referral to nephrology services [6,7]. General practitioners therefore play a central role in risk assessment and preventive interventions, including lipid-lowering therapy. Previous studies have shown that CKD management in primary care often falls short of guideline recommendations, with gaps in monitoring and preventive interventions such as lipid-lowering therapy [8].

Statins are recommended for primary prevention in adults aged ≥50 years with CKD stages 3–5, regardless of baseline low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and Swedish guidelines [9,10]. Evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses has shown that statins reduce overall and cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKD [3,11,12]. The Cholesterol Treatment Trialists’ Collaboration reported a 21% reduction in major vascular events per 1 mmol/L LDL reduction, independent of baseline lipid levels [13]. Despite this, statin therapy remains underutilized in primary care, as shown in Swedish and international studies [6,7,14]. Barriers include therapeutic inertia, polypharmacy, uncertainty about benefits in very elderly patients, and deprescribing trends in frail populations [6–8].

CKD is often detected in primary care through routine blood tests or hypertension follow-up. Management decisions are complicated by multimorbidity and competing priorities, which may lead to under-prescribing of preventive therapies. Observational studies suggest that guideline adherence to statin initiation guidelines in CKD is inconsistent, particularly among patients without established cardiovascular disease [6,7,14]. Few studies have examined outcomes of statin use in a population within primary care settings.

In accordance with current guidelines, other pharmacological strategies recommended for reducing cardiovascular risk in CKD include renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASi), sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists for patients with type 2 diabetes [10,15]. The mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist finerenone is also advised, but its use is restricted to patients with CKD and type 2 diabetes who have albuminuria, normal potassium levels, and an eGFR above 25 mL/min/1.73 m2 [16]. Other MRAs, such as spironolactone and eplerenone, have not been evaluated for cardiovascular risk reduction in CKD [10]. Despite these recommendations, uptake of these therapies in primary care remains limited [6,7]. Since most patients with CKD are treated in primary care, observational studies provide valuable insight into statin use and outcomes in a setting relevant to clinical practice. This study aims to examine the prevalence of statin use for primary prevention and its association with all-cause mortality in adults aged ≥50 years with CKD stages 3–5 and no other indication for statin therapy.

Methods

Study design

This study used a retrospective observational design using healthcare registry data. The primary outcomes were overall all-cause mortality and the prevalence of statin use among individuals with CKD.

Data sources

Data were obtained from the Regional Healthcare Information Platform (RHIP), which is a data registry provided and managed by Region Halland that covers a population of approximately 340,000 residents. It integrates electronic health record data from primary care, specialist outpatient care, and inpatient services. RHIP has been validated for research purposes and described in a published data resource profile [17]. It provides comprehensive clinical and demographic data and has been used in previous CKD studies [6,7].

Region Halland comprises three hospitals, two emergency departments, and 46 primary care centers, of which 23 are privately operated. The study utilized data from the RHIP, which compiles clinical and demographic information from primary care, specialist outpatient care, and inpatient services in Region Halland, Sweden, dating back to 2011. Clinical and demographic data relevant to CKD and statin therapy were extracted for the purposes of this study. Mortality data, including dates of death, were also recorded in RHIP. Medication data were retrieved from the Swedish National Prescribed Drug Register [18].

Study population

CKD was defined according to KDIGO criteria as an eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, confirmed by at least two measurements separated by a minimum of 90 days [10]. The study included individuals aged 50–89 years who met this criterion during the period 2018–2021 (Supplementary Table 1). The follow-up period was from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023. International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes, as listed in Supplementary Table 2, were used to support classification and subgroup analyses but were not used as inclusion criteria. Individuals with comorbidities such as primary hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, or previous cardiovascular disease were excluded from the study population, as there was an alternative treatment indication for statin therapy. Patients undergoing dialysis or with a history of kidney transplantation were not included. Only individuals with CKD stage 3–5 during the year 2021 were included in the study, since the indication for statin therapy in primary prevention is based on the presence of kidney dysfunction from stage 3 onwards in individuals aged 50 years or older.

Data collection

Collected variables included age, sex, CKD stage based on eGFR, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), presence of hypertension (ICD-10 code I10), LDL cholesterol levels, and use of pharmacotherapies, including statins, RAASi (RASi and MRA), and SGLT2i. Statin use, RAASi use, and SGLT2i use were defined as having picked up prescriptions for the respective medication during at least two out of the three years of the study period. For individuals who lived only one year, at least one prescription pickup during that year was required. For those who lived for two years, at least one year with prescription pickup was required. All-cause mortality data were also collected to enable survival analyses. Annual values for eGFR and LDL cholesterol were recorded to assess trends over time. Details on pharmacotherapy variables are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations and analyzed using Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was applied.

Age was categorized into two groups: 50–69 years and 70–89 years. Lipid levels were categorized by LDL-cholesterol as <2.5 mmol/L or ≥2.5 mmol/L. Kidney function was categorized according to CKD stages based on eGFR values into CKD stages 1–2 ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, CKD stage 3 (30–59 mL/min/1.73 m2), and stage 4–5 (<30 mL/min/1.73 m2), as detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Inclusion was limited to individuals with CKD stage 3–5 in 2021, as the indication for statin therapy applies from stage 3 onwards in adults aged 50 years or older. Kidney function was reassessed annually during follow-up, and those participants who improved were categorized as stage 1–2 in 2022 or 2023. All living individuals continued to be followed each year regardless of changes in kidney function.

Albuminuria was classified using UACR in three categories, A1 (<3 mg/mmol), A2 (3–30 mg/mmol) and A3 (>30 mg/mmol) [10]. Patients without UACR measurements were screened using a regular urine dipstick, which did not lead to further analysis with UACR tests. Changes in pharmacotherapy use between 2021 and 2023 were assessed using McNemar’s test for paired samples.

Changes in mean eGFR across the three follow-up years within each group were assessed using the Friedman test for repeated measures. This non-parametric test was chosen because eGFR values across years were not assumed to follow a normal distribution.

All-cause mortality over the three-year study period (2021–2023) was compared between statin-treated and non-treated individuals using descriptive statistics and Cox proportional hazards regression. Mortality rates were calculated for each group. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using Cox regression. Cox regression analyses included two models: Model 1, unadjusted, and Model 2, adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, CKD stage, and medication use (statins, RAAS inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors). Hypertension and medication use were entered as categorical variables (presence vs. absence). Reference categories were no hypertension and no use of the respective medication class. Age was categorized as <70 years versus ≥70 years, and CKD stage as stage 3 versus stages 4–5. No imputation was performed due to minimal missing data. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 7,177 individuals aged 50–89 years, who had two eGFR measurements <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 during 2018–2021, were included in the study population. Among these individuals, 4,416 (62%) were individuals with no statin therapy, and 2761 (38%) had statin therapy. Baseline albuminuria testing was limited, and 67% had no UACR measurement, and <1% had UACR >30 mg/mmol in the total cohort.

During the three-year follow-up period (2021–2023), the cohort contributed approximately 21,500 person-years. The overall mortality rate was 17% (95% CI: 16–18). Crude mortality rates were 57 deaths per 1,000 person-years in the non-statin group and 42 per 1,000 person-years in the statin group (p < 0.001). Mortality was higher among individuals not receiving statins compared with those treated with statins (19% vs. 14%, p < 0.001). Mortality data are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

This study offers insights relevant to primary care, where most individuals with CKD are treated. Statin therapy for primary prevention was associated with reduced all-cause mortality in adults aged ≥50 with CKD stages 3–5, even in the absence of established cardiovascular disease. Despite guideline recommendations, statin use remains suboptimal in this group. The observed association between statin therapy and reduced all-cause mortality is consistent with prior studies demonstrating cardiovascular and survival benefits in CKD populations. While sex was not significantly associated with mortality, hypertension showed a clear association with increased risk, underscoring the importance of considering both traditional cardiovascular and renal-metabolic markers in risk assessment.

The observed 18% reduction in all-cause mortality among statin-treated individuals is aligned with previous findings [2,4]. Meta-analyses have confirmed the efficacy of statins in CKD stages 3–5, showing reductions in mortality, myocardial infarction, and stroke [3,11,12]. Previous studies have suggested that statins may exert pleiotropic effects beyond lipid lowering, including improved endothelial function, reduced vascular inflammation, and plaque stabilization [19,20]. It was notable that mean eGFR changed during follow-up, with a slight decline in the no statin group and a slight improvement in the statin use group. These changes were statistically significant and indicate that statin use also has benefits regarding kidney function, which has been discussed in previous studies [3,11,13]. However, this should be interpreted with caution since the present study was not designed to evaluate kidney function trajectories. Possible explanations include baseline differences, survivor effects, regression to the mean, and the limited three-year follow-up, which may be insufficient to assess long-term kidney function trends.

Despite strong recommendations from KDIGO and Swedish national guidelines, statin therapy continues to be underused among patients with CKD who do not have established cardiovascular disease [9,10]. This is particularly evident in primary care, where most CKD patients are treated [6,7]. Contributing factors may include clinical inertia, complexity of multimorbidity, and uncertainty regarding statin benefits in very elderly individuals. These challenges are common in general practice and may influence prescribing decisions [6,7]. However, only 38% of eligible individuals in this study received statins. This gap may reflect limited awareness of CKD-specific cardiovascular risk or hesitancy to initiate treatment in adults 50 years and older with limited life expectancy. Although reluctance to prescribe statins in the elderly persists, evidence shows similar reductions in all-cause mortality across age groups, including very old adults [20]. Individuals aged 90 years and older were excluded from this study to minimize confounding by age-related treatment decisions. Nevertheless, actual usage remains below recommended levels [21]. Broader implementation of statin therapy could reduce cardiovascular events, improve survival rates, and lower healthcare costs [22].

CKD stages were strong independent predictors of all-cause mortality, while sex and hypertension were not significantly associated in adjusted models. These findings highlight the importance of CKD stage as a prognostic marker for mortality and support its integration into routine risk assessment in primary care. Albuminuria has been consistently associated with both renal and cardiovascular disease progression [5]. In the present study, albuminuria was incompletely assessed in this cohort and was not included in the adjusted model, which limits our interpretation of its prognostic value. Although not a standalone indication for statin therapy, albuminuria is included in risk stratification frameworks in both KDIGO and Swedish guidelines [9,19,23]. Recent analyses, including data from the UK Biobank, have shown that combining eGFR with albuminuria improves cardiovascular risk prediction compared to traditional models [24]. These findings support the integration of renal function and albuminuria into routine cardiovascular risk assessments in primary care [5,9,24].

Pharmacotherapy patterns in CKD stage 3–5 showed modest changes over the three-year period. Although the primary objective was to assess statin use and mortality, other pharmacotherapies were presented to provide a broader perspective on CKD management and to ensure transparency regarding potential confounders. Statin and RAASi use remained relatively stable, while SGLT2i use increased from 2% to 10% (p < 0.001). This trend reflects growing awareness of the renal and cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2i, which can slow CKD progression and reduce cardiovascular events regardless of diabetes status [25]. Despite these advances, uptake of evidence-based therapies remains suboptimal. Although combination therapy with statins, RAAS inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors may offer additive benefits, prescribing decisions should account for patient-specific factors such as frailty, comorbidities, potential adverse effects, and treatment costs. These considerations are particularly relevant in older adults with CKD, where polypharmacy is common. Most patients with CKD are treated in primary care, where integrated care models supported by decision tools and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential to improve guideline-concordant prescribing and long-term adherence [6,8].

Strengths and limitations

This study used a large, population-level dataset with comprehensive coverage of both primary and secondary care, enabling robust identification of individuals with CKD and detailed tracking of pharmacotherapy and outcomes. Exclusion of individuals with established cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or primary hyperlipidemia allowed for focused evaluation of statin therapy in a true primary prevention setting.

Limitations include the retrospective observational design, which precludes causal inference and may be subject to residual confounding. Statin exposure was based on prescription data, which may not fully reflect adherence. Data on frailty, cognitive status, and socioeconomic factors were unavailable, although the upper age limit of 89 years was chosen to reduce confounding from age-related treatment decisions.

Statin dosage data were unavailable due to incomplete and inconsistent recording in the source dataset, which limited reliability for analysis. This does not affect the main objective, as current guidelines for adults aged 50 years and older with CKD stages 3–5 prioritize initiation of statin therapy rather than titration based on lipid levels. Although median doses in prior studies approximate 20 mg/day simvastatin, the absence of dose-specific information limits interpretation of treatment intensity and its association with outcomes [3,9, 12,14,20]. A limitation is that changes in MRA use were reported for descriptive purposes, even though these agents are primarily indicated for patients with diabetes and albuminuria. MRAs may also be prescribed for treating resistant hypertension and heart failure in individuals without diabetes, but their inclusion does not directly relate to the primary study objective.

Conclusion

Statin therapy was significantly associated with reduced all-cause mortality in individuals aged ≥50 years with CKD stages 3–5 and no established cardiovascular disease. Despite strong recommendations from international and national guidelines, statin use remains underutilized in this high-risk population, which commonly presents in primary care. These findings support prioritizing statin therapy as a cornerstone of primary prevention in CKD, particularly for patients with impaired renal function and elevated albuminuria. Broader implementation in primary care could improve outcomes and reduce premature mortality.
