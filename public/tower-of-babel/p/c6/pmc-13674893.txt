Autologous blood-assisted closure of an unanticipated posterior scleral perforation: A novel rescue technique
Authors: Günhal Şatırtav, Hürkan Kerimoğlu, Mehmet Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The surgical challenge of intraocular foreign bodies (IOFBs) in the posterior pole is compounded by the fact that posterior scleral perforations are often not anticipated preoperatively. We describe a novel, simplified rescue technique using an autologous blood clot to seal a traumatic posterior scleral defect. A 34-year-old male presented with a gunshot injury and a radiopaque IOFB embedded in the posterior sclera. Following pars plana vitrectomy and IOFB removal, a posterior scleral defect was identified. An autologous blood clot was placed over the defect intraoperatively under air to act as a biological seal. Endolaser was applied on and around the clot, and perflouropropane (C3F8) gas was used as a tamponade. Postoperatively, ocular integrity was successfully restored with noted visual improvement. This technique offers a readily available alternative for managing small, unanticipated posterior perforations without the need for complicated suturing of the sclera or donor tissues.

Intraocular foreign bodies (IOFBs) are a common complication of penetrating ocular injuries, and they can enhance ocular morbidity.[12] IOFBs embedded in the retina tend to result in a worse final visual acuity compared to IOFBs retained in the anterior chamber or vitreous.[345] In 27% to 52% of patients, IOFBs become lodged in the retina or sclera, making removal difficult for the surgeon.[15] Encountering a posterior scleral dehiscence after removal of a deeply embedded foreign body poses a challenge in management. This can lead to escape of intravitreal fluid/air into the orbit and can increase the risk of air embolism. ‘Ab interno’ intravitreal suturing of a large traumatic scleral perforation at the posterior pole has been performed to close a scleral defect caused by an intrascleral foreign body.[6] Also, other surgical techniques including use of an amniotic membrane, fibrin glue, temporal fascia graft, and donor scleral tissue to seal the posterior scleral defect site have been described.[7891011] However, a posterior perforation may not always be anticipated preoperatively, leaving the surgeon without specialized materials like donor tissue. In this report, we describe a simplified, novel surgical technique using autologous blood for the closure of a small posterior pole defect caused by an intrascleral foreign body.

Surgical Technique

A 34-year-old male presented with a gunshot injury and a radiopaque IOFB embedded in the posterior sclera. Following primary corneal repair and a 7-day waiting period, a three-port 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy was performed. After core vitrectomy and posterior hyaloid detachment, the IOFB was carefully removed from its intrascleral bed. During the fluid–air exchange, massive subconjunctival and sub-tenon air was noticed, which was presumed to be leaking from the defect in the scleral bed where the IOFB was removed. Approximately 0.2–0.3 ml of autologous venous blood was drawn from the patient’s antecubital vein. Autologous blood was gently applied over the posterior scleral defect using a fine-gauge cannula under air. Around 0.1 ml of blood clotted on the surface of the defect. The remaining clotted blood that had pooled over the optic disk due to gravity was moved over the defect. The fluid around the clot complex was aspirated. The blood then acted as a biological glue, forming a stable clot that seals the perforation. Laser photocoagulation was applied to the blood clot covering the defect to stabilize the pieces of clots. Laser was also applied to the retina around the defect. Perfluoropropane gas was injected as a tamponade to maintain pressure against the clot during the initial healing phase.

This technique offers a significant advantage by reducing surgical complexity in cases where a posterior perforation is encountered unexpectedly. Unlike the amniotic membrane or donor sclera, autologous blood is readily available, cost-effective, and biocompatible and eliminates the risk of graft-related complications. This rescue approach allows for an effective seal of small posterior defects without the need for technically demanding ‘ab interno’ suturing.
