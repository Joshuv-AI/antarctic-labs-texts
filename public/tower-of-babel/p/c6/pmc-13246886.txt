SQLE as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for LIHC: a multi-cohort and spatial study
Authors: Jin Xie, Gao-Chun Xiao, Xin-Yang Qu, Rui-Min Wu, Yu-Long Zou, Hao-Shen Yu, Xue Hu, Xu-Sheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) remains a highly aggressive malignancy, necessitating novel biomarkers for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention. This study investigates the role of Squalene epoxidase (SQLE), a key enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, in LIHC. Integrating multi-cohort analyses of nine GEO datasets and TCGA revealed significant upregulation of SQLE in LIHC tissues. These findings were corroborated at the proteomic level via multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) on a tissue microarray of 48 paired clinical samples, where spatial analysis revealed a strong correlation between SQLE and the epithelial marker Pan Cytokeratin. Clinically, SQLE demonstrated potential diagnostic value and served as an independent prognostic factor for poor overall survival. Functional assays using shRNA knockdown in LIHC cell lines confirmed that SQLE inhibition decreased cellular proliferation, colony formation, and lateral migration. Mechanistically, elevated SQLE expression was associated with lipid metabolism modulation, m6A RNA modification, DNA damage responses, and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Notably, mIHC analysis of clinical TMAs revealed that high SQLE protein expression in tumor cells was significantly and negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8 + T cells. Collectively, these results highlight SQLE as a promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarker that is associated with LIHC progression, potentially through metabolic and immunomodulatory pathways.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) represents a prevalent and highly aggressive malignancy, posing a major global health challenge1,2. It is characterized by a high mortality rate, largely attributable to delayed diagnosis and the scarcity of effective therapeutic interventions in advanced stages3–6. The increasing incidence of LIHC has raised concerns about effective management and early detection, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic tools and biomarkers. Current diagnostic methods, including imaging and serum biomarkers like AFP, have limited specificity and sensitivity, leading to missed diagnoses; thus, identifying novel biomarkers is crucial for improving LIHC diagnosis accuracy and patient outcomes7–12.

Within the realm of cell metabolism studies, squalene epoxidase (SQLE) serves as a critical rate-limiting enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway. Its irregular expression across multiple cancers has attracted considerable interest, especially concerning its involvement in tumorigenesis and progression13,14. Recent studies have identified SQLE as a key driver of progression and immune evasion in multiple malignancies15–20. Particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma, while preliminary studies have revealed abnormal SQLE expression21, its precise molecular regulatory network, complex interactions with the tumor microenvironment, and potential associations with novel epigenetic modifications and cellular stress pathways have not yet been comprehensively elucidated. An in-depth investigation into the multifaceted biological roles of SQLE in LIHC, particularly its possible associations with N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification, DNA damage response (DDR), and the tumor immune microenvironment, is crucial for revealing previously unidentified connections in the pathogenesis of LIHC. This research may also offer innovative molecular targets for disease diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic strategies.

The aim of this study is to comprehensively evaluate the differential expression, diagnostic value, and prognostic relevance of SQLE in LIHC. This is accomplished by integrating extensive public transcriptome databases, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), through multi-cohort bioinformatics analyses that utilize stringent statistical methodologies. To validate these transcriptomic findings at the translational level, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was performed on clinical tissue microarrays to confirm SQLE protein upregulation and elucidate its spatial distribution within the tumor microenvironment. Complementing these clinical insights, functional validation in HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cell lines further substantiates the critical role of SQLE in driving tumor cell proliferation, colony formation and migration. Moreover, this investigation examines the potential correlations between SQLE and m6A RNA modification, the DDR pathway, as well as immune cell infiltration within the context of LIHC. By systematically clarifying the function of SQLE and its intricate interactions with essential molecular processes, this research offers novel insights into the molecular pathology associated with hepatocellular carcinoma. It also establishes a robust basis for the creation of SQLE-based early diagnostic instruments, precise risk assessment models, and personalized treatment approaches. Results

Multi-cohort differential analysis reveals consistent overexpression of SQLE in LIHC

We conducted a comprehensive limma-based differential expression analysis across nine GEO liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) datasets (GSE6764, GSE36411, GSE50579, GSE62232, GSE84402, GSE102079, GSE112790, GSE113996, GSE121248) (Fig. 2A). Applying the thresholds of |LogFC | > 1.0 and P < 0.05, we identified 1556, 772, 3134, 1354, 2005, 1433, 1494, 18, and 876 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), respectively, highlighting a certain degree of inter-cohort heterogeneity in expression patterns. Petal plot analysis of genes meeting the predefined screening criteria (LogFC > 1.0 and P < 0.05) identified SQLE as the only consistently upregulated gene across all nine GEO datasets (Fig. 2B), suggesting a reproducible pattern of aberrant SQLE expression in LIHC. The SQLE differential expression results in 9 GEO datasets are shown in Table 1. See Supplementary Table 1 for the complete DEG outputs. Subsequent analyses utilizing violin plots verified that the expression levels of SQLE were markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues across all examined cohorts (Fig. 2C, P < 0.05). To assess its diagnostic capabilities, ROC curves were constructed for each dataset and depicted in a forest plot (Fig. 2D). The AUC values (95% confidence intervals) were as follows: GSE6764, 0.849 (0.708–0.989); GSE36411, 0.891 (0.814–0.968); GSE50579, 0.837 (0.708–0.966); GSE62232, 0.865 (0.783–0.948); GSE84402, 0.903 (0.789–1.000); GSE102079, 0.922 (0.857–0.988); GSE112790, 0.921 (0.859–0.984); GSE113996, 0.723 (0.563–0.882); and GSE121248, 0.854 (0.773–0.935). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SQLE exhibits stable overexpression in LIHC across multiple cohorts and possesses notable diagnostic discriminative potential.

TCGA LIHC analysis supports SQLE as a potential biomarker

To further validate the expression profile and potential diagnostic significance of SQLE in LIHC, we performed a comprehensive differential expression analysis using the TCGA LIHC transcriptomic dataset with the DESeq2 algorithm. To reduce the false-positive rate, a more stringent threshold of |LogFC | > 1.5 was applied. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 3A), SQLE was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (LogFC = 1.614, P = 1.90E-17), indicating statistical significance. The analysis conducted using the violin plot (Fig. 3B) revealed a significantly elevated expression of SQLE in the tumor cohort compared to the normal cohort (P < 0.001). Furthermore, paired sample assessments consistently corroborated this upregulation associated with tumors (P < 0.001). Additionally, the evaluation through the ROC curve indicated that SQLE demonstrated robust diagnostic capabilities within the TCGA LIHC cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.884 (95% CI: 0.838–0.930) (Fig. 3C), which emphasizes its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker.

TCGA LIHC analysis supports SQLE as a potential biomarker.

A TCGA LIHC cohort DESeq2 volcano plot (|LogFC | > 1.5; SQLE LogFC = 1.614, P = 1.90E-17). B Non-paired and paired analyses showing elevated SQLE expression in tumor tissues (P < 0.05). C ROC curve (AUC = 0.884, 95% CI: 0.838–0.930) (***P < 0.001).

Expression differences of SQLE across clinical subgroups and stratified diagnostic performance

To initiate our study, we systematically investigated the potential correlations linking SQLE mRNA expression levels with a spectrum of fundamental clinicopathological features available in the TCGA-LIHC dataset. The outcomes of this analysis revealed that variations in SQLE expression did not exhibit a statistically significant relationship with patient demographics such as age and gender, nor were they associated with tumor status (With Tumor vs. Tumor-free) (Fig. 4A, B, F; P > 0.05). These findings imply that these specific baseline clinical parameters likely exert minimal regulatory influence over the transcriptional activity of SQLE. However, SQLE exhibited significant expression differences across subgroups stratified by serum AFP level, pathological stage, and histologic grade (Fig. 4C–E, P < 0.05). In particular, the expression of SQLE was found to be significantly greater in the high-AFP cohort (AFP levels exceeding 400 ng/mL) in comparison to the low-AFP group (AFP levels of 400 ng/mL or less). Furthermore, this expression increased with the progression to advanced pathological stages (Stages III and IV) when contrasted with the earlier stages (Stages I and II). Additionally, SQLE levels were notably heightened in poorly differentiated tumors (grades G3 and G4) as opposed to well-differentiated tumors (grades G1 and G2).

To further assess its stratified diagnostic performance, ROC curves and AUC values were calculated (Fig. 4G–I). SQLE expression yielded an AUC of 0.585 (95% CI: 0.518–0.652) for distinguishing early- versus late-stage LIHC (Stage I/II vs. III/IV), 0.592 (95% CI: 0.532–0.652) for differentiating tumor grades (G1/G2 vs. G3/G4), and 0.560 (95% CI: 0.500–0.620) for discriminating tumor status (With tumor vs. Tumor free). Although the stratified AUC values indicate limited discriminatory power, the expression trend suggests potential relevance to disease progression and histologic differentiation.

High SQLE expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in LIHC patients

To rigorously determine the prognostic significance of SQLE expression levels, in conjunction with a range of standard clinicopathological parameters within the context of liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), we performed a comprehensive survival analysis. Specifically, both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed utilizing clinical data derived from a total of 373 patients enrolled in the TCGA-LIHC cohort. As visually depicted in Fig. 5A, the results obtained from the initial univariate analysis demonstrated that both the pathological stage of the tumor and the expression abundance of SQLE were statistically significantly associated with overall survival (OS) outcomes. Specifically, when compared to patients in Stage I, the hazard ratios (HR) for those in Stage III and Stage IV were 2.734 (95% CI: 1.792–4.172, P < 0.001) and 5.597 (95% CI: 1.726–18.148, P = 0.004), respectively. Additionally, elevated SQLE expression was linked to an increased risk of mortality (HR = 1.510, 95% CI: 1.065–2.140, P = 0.021), while factors such as age (P = 0.295), gender (P = 0.200), and AFP levels (P = 0.772) did not demonstrate statistical significance.

Prognostic evaluation of SQLE and its time-dependent predictive efficacy within the TCGA-LIHC cohort.

A The univariate Cox regression analysis indicates that both the pathological stage (Stage III/IV compared to Stage I) and elevated SQLE expression are significantly linked to overall survival (OS), whereas factors such as age, gender, and AFP levels do not show a statistically significant association. B The multivariate Cox regression analysis further substantiates that pathological stage and elevated SQLE expression act as independent prognostic risk factors for patients with LIHC. C–E High SQLE associated with worse OS, DSS, and PFI (P < 0.05). F–H The calculated Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics for predicting OS were 0.678 at 1 year, 0.571 at 3 years, and 0.553 at 5 years. Similarly, regarding DSS, the AUC values were recorded as 0.677, 0.574, and 0.548, respectively; while for PFI prediction, the model yielded AUCs of 0.574, 0.569, and 0.615 for the 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals, respectively.

The multivariate analysis corroborated that pathological stage and SQLE expression function as independent prognostic indicators. Patients classified as Stage III and Stage IV exhibited a notably heightened risk of mortality compared to those in Stage I (HR = 2.620, 95% CI: 1.713–4.006, P < 0.001; HR = 6.587, 95% CI: 2.010–21.589, P = 0.002). Furthermore, high SQLE expression was determined to independently forecast poorer OS (HR = 1.527, 95% CI: 1.044–2.234, P = 0.029) as depicted in Fig. 5B.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis further illustrated that elevated SQLE expression is significantly linked to adverse prognostic outcomes. Patients exhibiting high SQLE expression experienced shorter OS (P = 0.020, Fig. 5C), poorer disease-specific survival (DSS) (P = 0.021, Fig. 5D), and a heightened risk of disease progression or recurrence in progression-free interval (PFI) (P = 0.013, Fig. 5E).

Time-dependent ROC analysis indicated that SQLE possesses moderate predictive capability for short-term outcomes. The AUC values for OS prediction were recorded at 0.678, 0.571, and 0.553 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 5F). A similar pattern was noted for DSS (AUCs: 0.677, 0.574, 0.548; Fig. 5G). For PFI, the AUCs were determined to be 0.574, 0.569, and 0.615 at the 1, 3, and 5-year marks (Fig. 5H). Collectively, these findings suggest that high SQLE expression serves as an independent prognostic biomarker for LIHC, demonstrating enhanced predictive value for short-term survival outcomes.

Validation of SQLE protein expression via multiplex immunohistochemistry

To validate the protein expression patterns of SQLE in LIHC, mIHC was performed on a TMA containing 48 paired tumor and paracancerous tissues (Fig. 6). Representative mIHC images of cancer and paracancerous tissues are shown in Fig. 6A, displaying the spatial distribution of SQLE (red, 690 nm) and the epithelial marker Pan CK (green, 520 nm).

Subsequent quantitative assessment derived from the Tissue Microarray (TMA) data provided compelling evidence of a marked upregulation of SQLE protein levels within tumor specimens when juxtaposed against their corresponding matched control tissues. More specifically, the analytical results indicated that the proportion of cells staining positive for SQLE (designated as % SQLE+ Positive Cells) was statistically significantly elevated in the cancer cohort relative to the adjacent paracancerous tissue group (P < 0.001, Fig. 6B). Furthermore, to explore the potential cellular associations within the tumor microenvironment, we deeply scrutinized the relationship between SQLE expression patterns and Pan CK, a canonical marker for epithelial tumor cells. Notably, Spearman correlation analysis revealed a robust and significant positive linear correlation between the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Pan CK (Pan CK Cell Avg Intensity) and that of SQLE (SQLE Cell Avg Intensity) (R = 0.595, P < 0.001, Fig. 6C). To substantiate these correlational findings with greater rigor, the patient population was systematically stratified into distinct Pan CK-Low and Pan CK-High subgroups, utilizing the median Pan CK intensity value as the discriminatory threshold. As visually demonstrated in Fig. 6D, the quantitative data confirmed that the mean intensity of SQLE was significantly augmented in the Pan CK-High subgroup compared to the levels observed in the Pan CK-Low subgroup (P < 0.001). To provide visual validation, representative multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) images that vividly depict the differential expression profiles of SQLE across these groups are displayed in Fig. 6E.

Effect of SQLE knockdown on cell proliferation

To assess the knockdown efficiency of SQLE, qPCR analysis indicated that sh-SQLE#1 and sh-SQLE#2 produced the most pronounced reduction of SQLE expression in HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells, and these two constructs were selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 7A, P < 0.05). Cell proliferation was then evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, revealing that both sh-SQLE#1 and sh-SQLE#2 significantly reduced proliferation rates in both cell lines compared to the sh-NC control group, indicating that SQLE downregulation suppresses cell growth (Fig. 7B, C, P < 0.05). Consistently, colony formation assays demonstrated that HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells treated with sh-SQLE#1 or sh-SQLE#2 formed fewer colonies than control cells, corroborating the CCK-8 results and further confirming that SQLE inhibition impairs proliferative capacity (Fig. 7D–G, P < 0.05).

SQLE knockdown and its effect on proliferation in HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells.

A qPCR analysis showing that sh-SQLE#1 and sh-SQLE#2 significantly reduced SQLE expression and were selected for subsequent experiments. B, C CCK-8 assays demonstrating that both sh-SQLE#1 and sh-SQLE#2 markedly decreased cell proliferation compared to sh-NC in both cell lines. D–G Colony formation assays further indicate that cells treated with sh-SQLE#1 or sh-SQLE#2 formed fewer colonies than controls, corroborating the CCK-8 results (**P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

SQLE knockdown impairs LIHC cell migration

To further explore the oncogenic role of SQLE, we assessed cell motility via wound healing assays. In both SK-HEP-1 (Fig. 8A, B) and HuH-6 (Fig. 8C, D) cell lines, the migration capacity was significantly impaired following SQLE knockdown. Compared to the sh-NC group, cells transfected with sh-SQLE#1 and sh-SQLE#2 exhibited a reduced wound closure rate at multiple time points (P < 0.05), indicating that SQLE is essential for the migration of LIHC cells.

Interaction networks highlight the central role of SQLE in lipid metabolism and LIHC

In order to understand the molecular role of SQLE in LIHC, we initiated a PPI analysis utilizing the STRING database (Fig. 9A). The findings identified ten key proteins that exhibited high-confidence interactions with SQLE, indicated by a combined score exceeding 0.97. Among these, FDFT1 (0.999), LSS (0.999), and CYP51A1 (0.998) exhibited the highest interaction scores, suggesting their close functional association with SQLE. Additional interactors included MSMO1 (0.992), HMGCS1 (0.986), DHCR7 (0.983), NSDHL (0.982), SIGMAR1 (0.974), HMGCR (0.973), and CH25H (0.971). Most of these proteins are key enzymes in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, such as farnesyl-diphosphate farnesyltransferase (FDFT1), lanosterol synthase (LSS), and lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51A1), reinforcing the role of SQLE as a rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis and implicating its involvement in LIHC progression through metabolic regulation.

Protein and gene interaction networks of SQLE.

A PPI network of SQLE constructed using the STRING database. The network highlights ten core proteins with high-confidence interactions (combined score > 0.97), with FDFT1, LSS, and CYP51A1 showing the highest interaction scores. To further delineate its functional network, we conducted a gene–gene interaction (GGI) analysis using GeneMANIA (Fig. 9B). SQLE was embedded in a complex interaction network involving co-expression, co-localization, physical interactions, predicted interactions, and shared protein domains. In co-expression analysis, SQLE was highly co-expressed with multiple cholesterol biosynthesis genes, including FDFT1, HMGCS1, MSMO1, NSDHL, ACAT2, HMGCR, DHCR7, LSS, as well as transcription factor SREBF2 and metabolic enzymes ADSL and KMO. Co-localization analysis indicated that SQLE and FDFT1, HMGCS1, ACAT2, and HMGCR may occupy similar subcellular compartments. Physical interactions were also predicted between SQLE and FDFT1, LSS, NFYA/B/C, SREBF2, SMIM22, and MARCHF6, which may regulate its transcriptional activity or functional complex formation. In addition, several genes such as LBR, NSDHL, FAHD1, and ACAT1 were predicted to interact functionally with SQLE. Shared protein domain analysis revealed structural similarities between SQLE and KMO/COQ6, suggesting potential similarities in molecular function or catalytic mechanism.

Significant association of high SQLE expression with m6A modification and DNA damage response pathways

To systematically elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms and signaling cascades that contribute to the aberrant upregulation of SQLE expression within the context of LIHC, a comprehensive Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was executed leveraging data sourced from the TCGA-LIHC cohort. For the purpose of this analysis, the patient population was dichotomized into two distinct subgroups based on their relative gene expression levels. By strictly adhering to significant filtering criteria, we successfully discerned a substantial number of enriched gene sets. Specifically, our screening revealed a total of 218 pathways that were significantly enriched within the c2 curated canonical pathway reference collection, in addition to 608 pathways identified within the c5 Gene Ontology (GO) reference set. Of particular significance to our study, a subset of 12 among these enriched pathways was found to be intimately associated with critical biological processes, specifically N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification and the DNA damage response mechanisms.

In the c2 reference set (Fig. 10A), high SQLE expression was significantly enriched in DNA repair-related pathways, including REACTOME_DISEASES_OF_DNA_REPAIR, REACTOME_DNA_DOUBLE_STRAND_BREAK_REPAIR, REACTOME_HOMOLOGY_DIRECTED_REPAIR, and REACTOME_DNA_REPAIR, as well as DNA damage signaling modification (REACTOME_SUMOYLATION_OF_DNA_DAMAGE_RESPONSE_AND_REPAIR_PROTEINS) and the integrated cancer signaling pathway (WP_INTEGRATED_CANCER_PATHWAY).

GSEA indicates noteworthy enrichment of pathways associated with m6A modifications and DNA damage response in the group exhibiting elevated expression levels of SQLE.

In the c5 reference set (Fig. 10B), enriched pathways were associated with m6A-related molecular functions and biological processes, such as GOMF_N6_METHYLADENOSINE_CONTAINING_RNA_BINDING, and processes related to DNA damage signaling (GOBP_SIGNAL_TRANSDUCTION_IN_RESPONSE_TO_DNA_DAMAGE), RNA localization (GOBP_RNA_LOCALIZATION), RNA-mediated gene silencing (GOBP_GENE_SILENCING_BY_RNA), mRNA transport (GOBP_MRNA_TRANSPORT), and CRD-mediated mRNA stabilization (GOBP_CRD_MEDIATED_MRNA_STABILIZATION).

Positive correlation between SQLE and m6A-related genes and their potential synergistic role in LIHC

To explore the functional relationship between SQLE and m6A methylation modifications in LIHC, we initially utilized the GEPIA online analysis tool to evaluate the association between SQLE and a composite signature comprising 20 m6A-related genes. The findings revealed a notable positive correlation (R = 0.28, P = 2.9E-8) (Fig. 11A), indicating a potential link between SQLE expression and the overall activity of m6A modifications.

Subsequently, Pearson correlation analyses were performed between SQLE and the aforementioned 20 m6A regulators within the TCGA-LIHC cohort and across nine independent GEO datasets. Notably, VIRMA consistently exhibited the most robust and significant positive correlation with SQLE across nearly all datasets. In the TCGA cohort, the correlation between SQLE and VIRMA was the strongest among all m6A regulators (cor = 0.552, P < 0.001). This prominent association was consistently validated in all nine GEO cohorts: GSE6764 (cor = 0.641, P < 0.001), GSE36411 (cor = 0.515, P < 0.001), GSE50579 (cor = 0.495, P < 0.001), GSE62232 (cor = 0.471, P < 0.001), GSE84402 (cor = 0.663, P < 0.01), GSE102079 (cor = 0.378, P < 0.001), GSE112790 (cor = 0.405, P < 0.001), GSE113996 (cor = 0.446, P < 0.05), and GSE121248 (cor = 0.685, P < 0.001). Beyond VIRMA, other regulators such as IGF2BP1 and IGF2BP2 also showed significant correlations with SQLE in multiple cohorts, but their associations were generally less consistent or strong compared to VIRMA (Fig. 11B). The pervasive and strong co-expression between SQLE and VIRMA suggests a particularly close functional link or potential co-regulatory relationship in the context of LIHC.

Remarkably, VIRMA, IGF2BP2, and METTL3 consistently demonstrated positive correlations with SQLE across multiple cohorts. Among them, VIRMA stood out as the most prominent interactor, not only showing a significant positive association in all analyzed datasets (TCGA and all nine GEO cohorts) but also exhibiting the strongest correlation coefficients in the majority of them. To further validate this robust co-expression relationship, we performed comparative analyses based on SQLE expression stratification. Utilizing scatter plot visualizations complemented by rigorous statistical testing, we confirmed a distinct upregulation in the expression levels of VIRMA. Specifically, the abundance of VIRMA was found to be significantly higher within the high-SQLE subgroup relative to the low-SQLE subgroup. This robust trend proved to be consistent across all examined datasets (demonstrating statistical significance with P < 0.05), as graphically represented in Fig. 11C–L. This consistent pattern at both the correlation and differential expression levels strongly underscores a specific and close functional link between SQLE and VIRMA in LIHC.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis within the TCGA LIHC cohort further substantiated the adverse prognostic impact of elevated VIRMA expression. Patients with high VIRMA expression exhibited significantly poorer Overall Survival (OS, P = 0.021) and a notably shorter Progress Free Interval (PFI, P = 0.028) compared to those with low expression (Fig. 11M, 11N). These consistent findings across both OS and PFI endpoints strongly underscore the role of VIRMA as a robust and detrimental prognostic biomarker in LIHC.

SQLE expression and immune cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment of LIHC

With the primary objective of elucidating the potential regulatory influence of SQLE on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), we systematically evaluated the association between SQLE gene expression and the relative infiltration abundance of twenty-four distinct immune cell subpopulations within the TCGA-LIHC patient cohort. As graphically illustrated through heatmap visualization, the comprehensive immune landscapes exhibited discernible discrepancies when comparing the cohorts stratified by high versus low SQLE expression levels (Fig. 12A). Furthermore, detailed correlation analyses uncovered that the expression of SQLE maintained a statistically significant and positive correlation with specific immune subsets. Notably, this included T helper cells (r = 0.142, P < 0.05) as well as Th2 cells (r = 0.201, P < 0.001). Conversely, SQLE expression showed significant negative correlations with multiple anti-tumor and innate immune cell populations, including B cells (r = −0.132, P < 0.05), CD8 T cells (r = −0.298, P < 0.001), cytotoxic cells (r = −0.261, P < 0.001), dendritic cells (DC) (r = −0.238, P < 0.001), eosinophils (r = −0.107, P < 0.05), mast cells (r = −0.172, P < 0.001), neutrophils (r = −0.238, P < 0.001), NK CD56dim cells (r = −0.193, P < 0.001), natural killer (NK) cells (r = −0.235, P < 0.001), plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC) (r = −0.216, P < 0.001), T gamma delta (Tgd) cells (r = −0.106, P < 0.05), Th1 cells (r = −0.135, P < 0.05), and Th17 cells (r = −0.145, P < 0.05) (Fig. 12B).

Resonating with the results from the correlation analysis, the high-SQLE expression group was characterized by a broad and significant suppression in the infiltration of numerous immune cell populations. Discussion

LIHC is recognized as one of the most prevalent forms of liver cancer globally and is a significant cause of cancer-related mortality1,2. The increasing incidence of LIHC is alarming, often attributed to underlying conditions such as chronic liver disease, viral hepatitis, and metabolic syndromes, which contribute to the disease’s progression and poor prognosis4,22. Moreover, LIHC imposes considerable economic burden due to late-stage diagnosis and complex treatment regimens, underscoring the need for novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches3,23–29.

In this study, we investigated the role of SQLE, a pivotal enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis30,31, in the context of LIHC. By integrating multi-cohort gene expression data, clinicopathological information, multi-omics analysis, and in vitro cell experiments, we systematically revealed the crucial role of SQLE in the development and progression of LIHC, highlighting its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.

Over the past few years, a growing body of contemporary research has elucidated that SQLE exhibits aberrant expression profiles across a diverse spectrum of human malignancies. Furthermore, substantial evidence suggests that this dysregulation is intimately linked to the fundamental biological processes driving both tumor tumorigenesis and subsequent neoplastic progression. For instance, Zhang et al. demonstrated that SQLE transcription is regulated by KDM4A, and its inhibition disrupts redox balance, suppressing bladder cancer growth15. Similarly, Zhao et al. identified SQLE as a prognostic biomarker in cervical cancer, linking its overexpression to enhanced cholesterol synthesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune infiltration16. Liu et al. revealed that SQLE amplification in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma generates the oncometabolite 2,3-oxidosqualene, which suppresses the Hippo pathway, thereby promoting metastasis17. Additionally, Pan et al.19 and Liu et al.20 implicated SQLE in immune evasion, showing that its upregulation in pancreatic and gastric cancers fosters an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Despite these preliminary observations, the precise molecular intricacies underlying the mechanism of action of SQLE specifically within the context of LIHC remain largely obscure. Furthermore, its potential clinical utility, serving as a reliable biomarker for both diagnosis and prognostic stratification, has yet to be comprehensively elucidated.

To bridge this knowledge gap in the current investigation, we employed a robust multi-cohort differential expression analysis approach encompassing nine independent datasets from the GEO. This rigorous screening process successfully identified SQLE as the sole gene exhibiting consistent upregulation across every examined dataset, thereby effectively overcoming the inherent biases and statistical limitations frequently associated with single-cohort studies. Subsequent cross-validation performed within the large-scale TCGA-LIHC cohort further corroborated these initial findings, confirming that SQLE expression levels are significantly elevated in tumor tissues when quantitatively compared to adjacent normal liver tissues. While the ROC analysis demonstrates a robust discriminative performance of SQLE (AUC = 0.884–0.922) in distinguishing tumor from normal tissues, this potential biomarker value does not yet establish superior clinical utility relative to existing standards. Specifically, its diagnostic efficacy has not been compared head-to-head with current clinical benchmarks, such as serum AFP levels or established clinicopathologic staging models. Therefore, SQLE should currently be considered a potential adjunct biomarker, and its practical integration into clinical diagnostic workflows requires further investigation to determine its incremental value over existing models. To corroborate these transcriptomic findings at the translational level, we performed multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) on a tissue microarray containing paired clinical samples. This analysis provided compelling proteomic evidence that SQLE is significantly overexpressed in LIHC tissues compared to matched paracancerous tissues. Furthermore, the spatial profiling revealed a strong positive correlation between SQLE and Pan Cytokeratin (Pan CK) intensity. Given that Pan CK is a hallmark of epithelial malignancies, this colocalization strongly suggests that SQLE upregulation is intrinsically driven by the tumor epithelial compartment rather than the stromal microenvironment.

Mechanistically, our functional assays demonstrated that SQLE knockdown significantly inhibited proliferation, colony formation and cell migration in both HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells. Specifically, the consistent phenotypic effects observed in SK-HEP-1, a cell line with endothelial characteristics, and HuH-6, a hepatoblastoma-derived line, suggest that the pro-tumorigenic role of SQLE may extend beyond classical hepatocyte-derived HCC. However, we acknowledge that the use of these specific cell models presents a lineage caveat, and further validation in a broader range of primary hepatocellular carcinoma models is warranted to fully generalize these functional findings. Notably, our findings are consistent with and extend those of previous reports. Shen et al. identified SQLE as one of the commonly upregulated genes in both nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)21. In contrast, our study provides multi-center, large-sample evidence for its consistent overexpression and exceptional diagnostic performance, highlighting its value not only in prognosis but also in early disease detection. A deeper insight lies in the fact that SQLE, as a rate-limiting enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, represents more than just a simple change in gene expression—it is a striking hallmark of cancer cell metabolic reprogramming. Persistent overexpression of SQLE may enhance de novo cholesterol synthesis, thereby providing tumor cells with a crucial metabolic advantage that promotes their growth and survival13,14,18. This metabolic dependency offers a novel molecular window for the diagnosis of LIHC and also suggests the potential of therapeutic strategies targeting the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway in LIHC treatment.

This study further revealed a significant association between SQLE expression levels and the clinicopathological features of patients with LIHC. We observed that SQLE expression was markedly higher in tumors with elevated serum AFP levels, more advanced pathological stages, and poorer histological grades. Although its stratified diagnostic performance (AUC value) was relatively limited, this trend of association with disease progression and increasing malignancy provides important clues for considering SQLE as a potential marker of tumor progression. More importantly, both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses consistently demonstrated that high SQLE expression was an independent adverse prognostic factor for overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in LIHC patients, a finding that remained significant even after adjusting for confounding factors such as pathological stage. Kaplan–Meier survival curves clearly illustrated shorter survival durations in patients with high SQLE expression. Although time-dependent ROC curve analysis indicated a stronger predictive value of SQLE for short-term survival, its predictive efficiency declined over longer follow-up periods, suggesting that SQLE may exert greater prognostic utility in the early or intermediate stages of LIHC, whereas its prognostic impact in advanced disease may be masked by more complex molecular events. Collectively, these findings indicate that SQLE is not merely a marker of LIHC presence but may also act as a key driver of tumor malignant progression, with sustained overexpression predicting more aggressive tumor behavior.

To investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms driving the high expression of SQLE, we performed PPI, GGI network analyses, and GSEA. Our network analyses (PPI and GGI) confirm that SQLE acts as a central node in cholesterol biosynthesis, interacting closely with enzymes like FDFT1 and CYP51A1. Cai et al. reported that FDFT1 overexpression in HCC correlates with poor prognosis and promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis32. Similarly, CYP51A1, another direct interactor of SQLE, has been implicated in coordinating sterol metabolism with multiple forms of regulated cell death33. GeneMANIA analysis constructed a broader interaction network, revealing complex associations between SQLE and the transcription factor SREBF2 (sterol regulatory element-binding protein 2), as well as other metabolic enzymes and potential regulatory factors. Mok et al. demonstrated that SREBP2 activation, triggered by caspase-3–mediated cleavage, promotes cholesterol biosynthesis and supports the expansion of cancer stem cell populations, thereby contributing to acquired resistance to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in HCC34. SREBF2 is a critical transcriptional regulator of cholesterol biosynthesis, and its co-expression and physical interaction with SQLE suggest that the SREBP signaling pathway may directly regulate SQLE expression, thereby forming a positive feedback loop that drives excessive cholesterol synthesis in LIHC.

One of the most innovative findings of this study is that GSEA revealed a strong enrichment of m6A modification and DDR pathways in the high SQLE expression group. m6A is the most prevalent internal modification in eukaryotic mRNA and is extensively involved in various aspects of RNA metabolism—including splicing, stability, transport, and translation—thereby exerting fine-tuned regulation of gene expression35,36. The DDR pathway is essential for maintaining genomic stability, and its dysregulation is closely linked to tumorigenesis, cancer progression, and drug resistance37. In the SQLE-high group, we observed enrichment of multiple DNA repair-related pathways as well as m6A-associated molecular functions and biological processes.

This association suggests a novel molecular axis in which SQLE-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming may influence m6A modification and DDR through yet-unknown mechanisms. For instance, SQLE-derived metabolites may modulate the activity of the m6A writer complex, or SQLE mRNA itself may undergo specific m6A modifications that enhance its stability in tumor cells. Our correlation analysis further supports this hypothesis, revealing significant positive correlations between SQLE and several m6A-related genes—particularly VIRMA, METTL3, and IGF2BP2. Notably, VIRMA exhibited a strong positive correlation with SQLE across all ten datasets examined, and high VIRMA expression was also associated with poor prognosis. As a key component of the m6A writer complex, VIRMA has been reported to play an important role in LIHC progression36,38. In our study, the synchronous upregulation of VIRMA and SQLE may cooperatively promote aberrant m6A modification patterns in tumors, thereby affecting the stability or translational efficiency of specific oncogene or tumor suppressor gene mRNAs, ultimately driving cancer progression. In addition, lipid metabolic imbalance may induce oxidative stress or endoplasmic reticulum stress, which in turn could activate DDR pathways, leading to increased genomic instability and providing a foundation for tumor cell evolution. The proposed putative regulatory network identified through integrated enrichment and correlation analyses offers a fresh perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of LIHC, expanding the conventional view of SQLE beyond its role in lipid metabolism. While reciprocal knockdown experiments and MeRIP-seq are required to definitively determine whether cholesterol metabolism drives m6A changes or if m6A modification regulates SQLE expression, our current correlation and clinical data provide a strong rationale for this interplay. We have noted this as a critical area for future investigation when more resources are available.

The investigation into the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) revealed that high SQLE expression in LIHC is closely associated with a profound shift from an anti-tumor immune profile toward an immunosuppressive state. A critical finding of our study was the significant positive correlation between SQLE expression and the infiltration of Th2 cells, alongside a concurrent reduction in CD8 T cells and NK cells. This reciprocal pattern suggests that SQLE may contribute to immune evasion by modulating the balance of adaptive and innate immune effector cells. The observed elevation of Th2 cells in SQLE-high tumors is indicative of a “Th1/Th2 imbalance,” a well-documented mechanism of immune subversion in the liver. While Th1 cells promote cellular immunity and anti-tumor responses, Th2 cells typically secrete cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-10, which antagonize Th1 functions and facilitate an environment conducive to tumor progression39,40. The positive correlation between SQLE and Th2 cell infiltration suggests that SQLE-driven metabolic changes might promote this pro-tumorigenic polar shift.

Concomitantly, our results showed that high SQLE expression was significantly associated with decreased levels of CD8 T cells and NK cells, both of which are primary effectors of the anti-tumor immune response. CD8 T cells are essential for the direct recognition and lysis of malignant cells via the release of granzymes and perforin; however, their exhaustion or exclusion is a hallmark of advanced LIHC41. Similarly, NK cells serve as the first line of innate defense, and their reduced infiltration or impaired activity has been linked to increased tumor recurrence and reduced overall survival in LIHC patients42. The observed negative correlation between SQLE and CD8 + T cell infiltration in the TCGA-LIHC cohort and our clinical TMA implies that SQLE overexpression may hinder the recruitment or survival of these cytotoxic populations, contributing to an “immune-cold” phenotype. This metabolic barrier poses a significant challenge for endogenous anti-tumor immunity and cellular therapies. For virus-associated malignancies like LIHC, targeting tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or oncogenic viral antigens via engineered T cell immunotherapy has shown potential, yet these approaches often face obstacles such as T-cell exhaustion and a restrictive tumor microenvironment43. Therefore, strategies combining SQLE-targeted metabolic inhibition with T-cell based immunotherapies or immune checkpoint inhibitors may be necessary to overcome the immunosuppressive niche and enhance the efficacy of precision treatments in LIHC.

In conclusion, this study comprehensively demonstrates that SQLE is ubiquitously overexpressed in LIHC at both the transcriptional and proteomic levels, holding robust value for diagnostic and prognostic assessment. Crucially, our multiplex immunohistochemistry analysis validated the spatial enrichment of SQLE specifically within Pan CK-positive tumor cells in clinical tissues, confirming its tumor-intrinsic expression pattern. Complementing these clinical findings, functional validation in HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells revealed that SQLE knockdown significantly inhibits cell proliferation, colony formation and migration, providing direct evidence for its role in tumor progression. Mechanistically, SQLE may drive malignant behavior in LIHC through modulation of lipid metabolism, which subsequently influences m6A RNA modification, DNA damage response pathways, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Collectively, these findings not only offer novel molecular insights into LIHC pathogenesis but also position SQLE as a multifunctional biomarker and a potential therapeutic target, laying the groundwork for future precision diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Data collection and datasets

In order to examine the expression levels of SQLE in LIHC and to evaluate its potential diagnostic and prognostic significance, we employed a variety of publicly accessible datasets. The primary data were obtained from the GEO database44 and TCGA45. The GEO datasets included for our analysis were GSE6764, GSE36411, GSE50579, GSE62232, GSE84402, GSE102079, GSE112790, GSE113996, and GSE121248, which, together with the TCGA-LIHC cohort, yielded ten cohorts in total for the purpose of differential expression analysis.

Data pre-processing and normalization

Upon loading the expression data from GEO, the numerical range was evaluated to determine the necessity of log-transformation. Raw expression counts were log2-transformed using the formula log_2(x). To generate a gene-centric expression matrix, probe IDs were mapped to their corresponding gene symbols using the platform annotation files. For genes represented by multiple probes, we applied a maximization strategy: the average expression value of each probe across all samples was calculated, and the probe with the highest mean expression was selected to represent the gene. Missing values were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Subsequently, to minimize inter-sample heterogeneity and technical batch effects, the data underwent quantile normalization using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma R package.

Differential expression analysis

To perform differential expression analysis, the limma package46 was applied to analyze the datasets obtained from the GEO database. The identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor samples and adjacent normal tissues was based on the thresholds of |LogFC | > 1.0 and P < 0.05. Concurrently, the DESeq247 tool was applied to the transcriptomic dataset from TCGA focusing on LIHC, implementing a more rigorous threshold of |LogFC | > 1.5 to mitigate the incidence of false-positive findings. For visual representation of the results, volcano plots and violin plots were created, adhering to the criterion of statistical significance (P < 0.05) for the observed differences in gene expression. The analysis results of multiple volcano plots were generated using the CNSknowall platform (https://cnsknowall.com).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis

The diagnostic performance of SQLE was assessed through ROC curve analysis. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) was computed for each dataset to measure the diagnostic effectiveness of SQLE expression in differentiating tumor tissues from normal ones. This assessment was conducted for each dataset utilizing R statistical software, with AUC values interpreted to examine the reliability of SQLE as a prospective biomarker for LIHC.

Clinical subgroup analysis

The relationship between SQLE expression and diverse clinical attributes was examined utilizing the TCGA LIHC dataset. Patients were categorized into clinical subgroups according to age, gender, tumor status (with tumor vs. tumor free), serum AFP levels (high vs. low), pathological stages (I/II vs. III/IV), and histological grades (G1/G2 vs. G3/G4). Subsequently, the variations in SQLE expression across these subgroups were analyzed, and the effectiveness of SQLE in differentiating between the subgroups was evaluated through the construction of ROC curves and the computation of AUC values.

Prognostic analysis

The prognostic relevance of SQLE expression was assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. These analyses were conducted on data from the TCGA LIHC cohort, which included 373 patients. Univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to identify significant prognostic indicators, integrating SQLE expression with multiple clinical variables. Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to determine independent prognostic factors, while adjusting for potential confounding variables including pathological stage and additional clinical characteristics. Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was utilized to evaluate and contrast overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) between cohorts exhibiting high and low SQLE expression levels stratified by the median SQLE expression. Log-rank tests were applied to determine the statistical significance of these differences. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to assess the predictive accuracy of SQLE for 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS, DSS, and PFI outcomes.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) and gene-gene interaction (GGI) network analysis

To investigate the interactions between SQLE and other proteins involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database48. From this analysis, the ten proteins exhibiting the highest interaction scores were determined. Furthermore, gene-gene interaction (GGI) analysis was conducted through GeneMANIA49 to delve deeper into the functional network associated with SQLE. Both co-expression and co-localization analyses were executed to elucidate the possible molecular mechanisms that regulate SQLE function in LIHC.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

In order to explore the molecular mechanisms responsible for the increased expression of SQLE in LIHC, we conducted GSEA utilizing the TCGA-LIHC dataset. The analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1), employing the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4)50 to discern pathways that are significantly enriched between groups with high and low SQLE expression levels. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25, alongside an adjusted P < 0.05, was established to determine statistical significance. We incorporated the c2 canonical pathway and c5 Gene Ontology reference sets to identify pathways linked to DDR and m6A RNA modification, which may play a mechanistic role in the upregulation of SQLE in LIHC.

Correlation with m6A-related genes

The association between SQLE and a composite signature encompassing 20 m6A-related genes was initially evaluated through the GEPIA online analysis platform51. Following this, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the association between SQLE and the aforementioned 20 m6A-related genes within the TCGA LIHC dataset, as well as across nine independent GEO datasets (GSE6764, GSE36411, GSE50579, GSE62232, GSE84402, GSE102079, GSE112790, GSE113996, and GSE121248). The selection criteria for the m6A-related genes were derived from prior investigations52–55. Additionally, we performed a systematic comparison regarding the expression profiles of statistically significant genes associated with m6A modification. This was accomplished by stratifying samples into cohorts defined by either elevated or diminished SQLE expression levels and utilizing two-group comparison plots for visualization. Subsequently, to rigorously assess the potential prognostic value of VIRMA within the clinical context, we implemented Kaplan–Meier survival analysis based on data sourced from the TCGA-LIHC cohort.

Immune cell infiltration analysis

For the purpose of conducting a thorough investigation into the complex immunological microenvironment characteristic of liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) samples, our study utilized the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) method, which is implemented via the “GSVA” R package (version 1.46.0)56. Specifically, the relative infiltration abundance of 24 distinct immune cell populations was calculated by interrogating specific gene signatures derived from previously validated literature57. This method was selected for its ability to provide a comprehensive and independent enrichment score for a wide array of immune subtypes within each individual sample. To ensure the reliability of these transcriptomic predictions, the core findings regarding cell infiltration were further validated through multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) in clinical samples. Initially, samples retrieved from the TCGA-LIHC cohort were systematically dichotomized into high-SQLE and low-SQLE expression subgroups, utilizing the median expression value as the defining cutoff. To rigorously elucidate the potential association between SQLE expression levels and the estimated infiltration abundance of various immune cell types, we conducted Spearman’s rank correlation analyses. Subsequently, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to quantitatively compare the discrepancies in immune cell infiltration patterns distinguishing the high-SQLE group from the low-SQLE group. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance, and the comprehensive results were visually displayed using heatmaps, lollipop charts, and boxplots.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

To evaluate the protein expression and spatial distribution of SQLE and Pan Cytokeratin (Pan CK) in LIHC, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) staining was performed on a tissue microarray (TMA). The TMA (Cat. No. ZL-LivHcc963; Shanghai Zhuoli Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) contained 96 tissue cores, comprising 48 pairs of cancer and matched paracancerous tissues.

The mIHC assays were performed employing a sequential and cyclic staining methodology. In brief, tissue sections underwent standard deparaffinization and rehydration processes, followed by heat-induced epitope retrieval utilizing AR6 buffer maintained at 95°C for a duration of 25 min. Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched, and non-specific binding sites were effectively blocked to minimize background noise. The slides were then incubated with specific primary antibodies, succeeded by the application of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated polymeric secondary antibodies. Visualization of the resulting antigen-antibody complexes was achieved via Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) fluorophores. Following the signal detection phase, antibody elution was performed, and the entire sequence—encompassing antigen retrieval, blocking, primary and secondary antibody incubation, and the TSA reaction—was iteratively repeated for each subsequent target.

The specific panel of antibodies utilized in this study comprised the following: anti-SQLE (diluted at 1:200; Cat. No. 12544-1-AP, sourced from Proteintech; Rabbit IgG), which was detected within the 690 nm spectral channel, and anti-Pan CK (diluted at 1:2000; Cat. No. BX50143, obtained from biolynx; Rabbit IgG), visualized in the 520 nm channel, and anti-CD8 (diluted at 1:200; Cat. No. BX50036-C3, biolynx; Rabbit IgG), detected within the 620 nm spectral channel. For nuclear visualization, counterstaining was performed using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), after which the slides were mounted to prepare them for imaging.

High-resolution whole-slide scanning was executed utilizing the Vectra Polaris Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (manufactured by Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Subsequent quantitative image analysis was carried out via the APTime software platform (Version 2.0.0, 3Dmedcare). Protein expression levels were rigorously determined by calculating both the percentage of positively stained cells and the mean fluorescence intensity corresponding to the target proteins.

Cell culture and shRNA transfection procedures

All reagents utilized for cell culture were sourced from Gibco, including fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10099141 C), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 12634010), Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, 11095080), penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, 15140-122), trypsin-EDTA (C25200056), and PBS (C10010500BT). To functionally validate the oncogenic role of squalene epoxidase (SQLE) in hepatic malignancies, two human liver cancer–associated cell lines with distinct biological characteristics were employed: HuH-6 (iCell-h239), a well-characterized epithelial-like hepatoblastoma cell line, and SK-HEP-1 (iCell-h190), a widely used endothelial-like hepatocellular carcinoma cell line representing a different subtype of liver cancer. Both cell lines were obtained from iCell. HuH-6 cells were cultured in DMEM, whereas SK-HEP-1 cells were maintained in MEM, each supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For transient transfection assays, Lipofectamine 2000 (Biosharp, BL623B) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

In preparation for cell culture, frozen cells were quickly thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 1 to 2 min, subsequently transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube, and mixed with 5 to 10 mL of pre-warmed complete medium. Following centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in fresh medium. The cells were then seeded into T25 flasks, gently agitated to ensure even distribution, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were washed with PBS and treated with 1–2 mL of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 1–2 min. The trypsinization process was halted by the addition of complete medium, after which the cells were gently resuspended and subcultured at a ratio of 1:3. Transfection was carried out according to the instructions provided in the Lipofectamine 2000 protocol, with cells cultured for 48 h prior to subsequent analyses. For the knockdown of SQLE, specific shRNA sequences were synthesized and cloned into the pLVX-shRNA2-Puro plasmid vector (Shanghai Haijihaoge Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., China) by Nanjing Zhongke Shikang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., with the insert ligated between the BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites. The expression of the shRNA was driven by the U6 promoter. The vector also encoded ZsGreen1 for fluorescent tracking and a puromycin resistance gene (PuroR) for selection. The standardized construct architecture consisted of a 5’ BamHI-compatible linker (GATCC), the sense sequence, a short hairpin loop (CTCGAG), the antisense sequence, and a poly-T termination signal (TTTTTG). Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

The execution of quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays was primarily carried out using the Gentier 96 detection system manufactured by Tianlong. Ancillary instrumentation essential for sample processing and precise nucleic acid quantification included the Velocity 18 R Pro centrifuge (Dynamica) and the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. Regarding the specific biochemical reagents, the experimental protocol incorporated RNAiso Easy for isolation, the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit for reverse transcription, and TB Green Premix Ex Taq for the amplification reaction, all of which were procured from Takara. The primers for SQLE were designed as follows: GCCTGCCTTTCATTGGCTTC for the forward primer and TTCCTTTTCTGCGCCTCCTG for the reverse primer. For GAPDH, the forward primer was AGGCCGGATGTGTTCGC, while the reverse primer was CAAATCCGTTGACTCCGACC. Total RNA extraction was carried out using RNAiso Easy, followed by the addition of Solution H (at two-fifths volume), centrifugation at 12,000 × g, isopropanol precipitation, ethanol washing, and subsequent dissolution in RNase-free water. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000. Reverse transcription was performed in a 20 μL reaction mixture that included the RNA template, PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix, RT Primer Mix, and buffer, incubated at 37 °C for 15 min and subsequently heated to 82 °C. The qPCR reactions (20 μL) comprised 10 μL of TB Green Premix Ex Taq, 0.4 μL of each forward and reverse primer (at a concentration of 10 μM), 1 μL of cDNA, and sterile water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, concluding with a melting curve analysis ranging from 65 °C to 95 °C. The relative expression of genes was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCT method, normalized to GAPDH.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) cell proliferation assay

To quantitatively monitor the proliferative capacity of the cells, the CCK-8 assay was employed following standard protocols. Specifically, cells were harvested and resuspended to a density allowing for the inoculation of approximately 5000 cells (5 × 103) per well into 96-well culture plates, with a final volume of 100 μL of complete medium. These plates were subsequently maintained in a humidified incubator set to standard conditions of 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere for distinct incubation intervals of 24, 48, and 72 h. Upon the conclusion of each specified time point, the culture medium was supplemented with 10 μL of the CCK-8 reagent (KGA317; Nanjing KeyGen BioTECH Co., Ltd., China). Following gentle agitation, the plates were incubated for an additional 4-h reaction period to facilitate color development. Finally, the optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm to assess cell viability.

Colony formation assay

Following the completion of transfection procedures, the cells were enzymatically dissociated utilizing trypsin digestion to obtain a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, a standardized aliquot comprising approximately 800 cells was evenly seeded into each individual well of 12-well culture plates supplemented with the requisite complete growth medium. These cultures were maintained in a controlled environment within a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 tension. During the proliferation phase, cellular growth status was continuously observed, and the culture medium was replenished intermittently to preserve optimal physiological conditions for colony development. The experimental endpoint was determined when macroscopic colonies (approximately 50 colonies per well) became visible; at this juncture, the supernatant was aspirated, and the adherent cells underwent two consecutive washing steps using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Fixation was achieved by immersing the samples in 4% paraformaldehyde for a distinct period of 20 min. Immediately thereafter, the fixed colonies were subjected to staining with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for another 20-min interval, after which excess dye was rinsed off and the plates were air-dried. Finally, colony morphology was documented via an inverted microscope, and quantitative counting was executed using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

To evaluate the effect of SQLE knockdown on the migratory capacity of LIHC cells, a wound healing assay was performed. HuH-6 and SK-HEP-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well. Upon reaching confluence, three horizontal reference lines were marked on the bottom of the plate, and a 200 μL pipette tip was used to create vertical scratches perpendicular to these lines. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Scratch closure was monitored and photographed at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h using an inverted microscope. The wound area was quantified using ImageJ software to calculate the migration rate.

Statistical methods

In this study, the entire suite of statistical computations and data analyses was executed within the R software environment (version 4.2.1). Regarding the comparison of continuous variables, non-parametric approaches were selected based on the number of cohorts: the Mann–Whitney U test was designated for pairwise comparisons between two distinct groups, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to evaluate variance across three or more independent groups. Furthermore, the distribution discrepancies and associations among categorical variables were rigorously examined utilizing the Chi-square test. To assess prognostic outcomes, survival probabilities were estimated via the Kaplan–Meier method, and the significance of differences between survival curves was determined using the log-rank test. For all hypothesis testing, a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, and all confidence intervals (CIs) were reported at the 95% level.
