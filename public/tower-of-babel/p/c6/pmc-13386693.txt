Use of external breast prostheses, associated factors and relationship with selected quality of life aspects among post-mastectomy women in Sri Lanka: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Chamod Danosha Perera, Dinil Niketh Perera, Udeshika Dhananjani Pitigala, Carukshi Arambepola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite advancements, over 60% of women undergo mastectomy as primary treatment following breast cancer, which could impact their quality of life (QOL). In this regard, external breast prostheses (EBP) have shown potential to improve post-mastectomy QOL globally, but the evidence is limited in South Asia. This study assessed the use of EBP, factors associated with its current use and relationship with selected aspects of QOL among post-mastectomy women in Sri Lanka.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 158 non-metastasized post-mastectomy breast cancer patients attending outpatient clinics at the National Cancer Institute, Maharagama. Following systematic random sampling, an interviewer-administered questionnaire collected data on socio-demographic characteristics, EBP use, perceived barriers and QOL aspects.ResultsThe response rate was 94.9%. The current EBP use was 58.9% (n = 93), which was primarily intended to mimic natural breast tissue. More than 75% of them expressed satisfaction with its application at work, social events and outdoor shopping. Higher monthly income and educational status, non-urban living, non-Buddhists, being employed and ≥ 18 months since surgery were associated with current EBP use, however, younger age (18–60 years) (adjusted odds ratio = 2.4; 95% CI: 1.2, 4.9) and being single (adjusted OR = 3.2; 95% CI: 1.1, 9.5) were the only factors significantly associated with their current EBP use after adjusting for confounders.The EBP non-users consisted of 59 (37.3%) who had never used EBP and six (3.8%) who had used previously but now discontinued. Barriers to ever use of EBP included a lack of awareness (78.0%) and accessibility (61.0%), socio-cultural inappropriateness (55.9%), and feeling embarrassed (45.8%) and stigmatised (44.1%) to use. Compared with non-users, current EBP users reported higher satisfaction with the individual QOL aspects (p<0.01).ConclusionsEBP use, driven by younger age and being single, was relatively high in post-mastectomy patients, while continued use was significantly associated with improved QOL in some critical aspects. The findings highlight the importance of incorporating informal EBP supply channels into rehabilitation programs to ensure sustainability, while concurrently addressing barriers among never/discontinued users through enhanced awareness and stigma reduction via patient-support groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04560-6.

Background

Breast cancer is a significant global health concern, affecting approximately one in eight women during their lifetime [1]. Despite the burden, advancements in diagnostic techniques and treatment have drastically improved the survival rates of breast cancer patients, particularly for those with early-stage disease [2].

Surgical interventions, comprising mastectomy and breast conservative surgery, constitute the primary mode of effective curative-intent treatment available for breast cancer [3]. Despite substantial curative gains, mastectomy can result in substantial anatomical changes, affecting body image and feminine identity [4]. Further, it can lead to surgical site pain, body deformation and anxiety, negatively impacting both physically and psychologically [5]. These consequences, along with frequent hospital visits, side effects of drugs, social stigma and disruptions to normal social activities, can diminish the quality of life (QOL) for patients. It is shown that disability-adjusted life years associated with cancer are significantly greater among breast cancer survivors than among those with other cancers [6]. Therefore, postoperative QOL plays a crucial role in achieving the goals of cancer treatment; thus, identified as a critical outcome in the rehabilitation of breast cancer survivors [7].

In developed countries, there is strong evidence supporting the use of external breast prostheses (EBP) to overcome post-mastectomy challenges [4]. EBP, which is designed to simulate natural breast tissue, has been shown to restore body symmetry and improve posture and physical appearance, social interactions, sexual dysfunction, lifestyle, phantom pain and psycho-social status of post-mastectomy patients [8–11]. Recent advances in EBP design aim to further improve anatomical customization, comfort and thermal properties [11, 12]. It is also expected to enhance their overall QOL [9–11]. Consequently, the introduction of an EBP after mastectomy is recognised as an integral part of rehabilitation programs, especially for patients who opt out or undergo delayed breast reconstruction. However, the use of EBP is shown to be influenced by factors such as patient education, affordability, after-sales services, comfort, practicality, and confidence restoration, family support, and age [8]. The evidence on these associated factors is scarce in resource-poor developing countries.

Sri Lanka, a country in South Asia with impressive health indicators, is experiencing a rising burden of non-communicable diseases, especially cancer [13]. Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed among women in the country [13]. During the period 2016–2020, 61% of women diagnosed with breast cancer (73% of those in stages III & IV and 62% in stages I & II) underwent mastectomy compared to only 32% who received wide local excision [14]. This high rate of mastectomy is attributable to several factors, including limited availability of breast- conserving surgical techniques, insufficient radiotherapy capacity to meet service demand and lower prioritisation of aesthetic outcomes in cancer care. In this context, the high prevalence of mastectomy indicates the relevance of EBP use in comprehensive breast cancer care in Sri Lanka.

The National Cancer Control Program of Sri Lanka (NCCP-SL) aims to enhance cancer survival rates and ensure an acceptable QOL with minimal disabilities in cancer survivors [13]. However, in practice, the focus has predominantly been on achieving a cancer-free status, often neglecting QOL, especially after mastectomy. Studies have shown negative aesthetic and intimate relationship outcomes following mastectomy in Sri Lanka [15]. These outcomes, combined with the limited availability of breast reconstruction surgery, make EBP a cost-effective option for minimising post-mastectomy effects in Sri Lanka. However, providing EBP is not routinely practised in the country nor considered an integral component in the current rehabilitation of breast cancer patients. Additionally, there is scarce documentation on the use of EBP, its influencing psychological, cultural, economic and practical factors and the impact on QOL. Bridging these knowledge gaps will enable clinicians, policy makers and public health experts to identify potential barriers and contributors to EBP usage and make strong recommendations towards improving the QOL of post-mastectomy patients. The lessons learnt can also be applied to other low-resourced developing countries with similar socio-cultural backgrounds. This study was undertaken to describe the use of EBP, the factors associated with its use and the relationship with selected QOL aspects among post-mastectomy patients in Sri Lanka.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted among post-mastectomy breast cancer patients aged 18 years and above who were attending the follow-up outpatient clinic at the National Cancer Institute, Maharagama (NCIM). This is the premier tertiary cancer care hospital in Sri Lanka, providing radiotherapy, chemotherapy and long-term follow-up care for post-operative breast cancer patients referred from all parts of the country. As a state-owned institution, the NCIM offers universally accessible, free-of-charge care, enabling patients from diverse socioeconomic and geographic backgrounds to receive specialised oncology services and thereby facilitating the recruitment of a representative sample of post-operative breast cancer patients within the national healthcare system.

All participants had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer without distant metastasis and had undergone surgery with curative intent. Patients in stage IV of the disease or those who underwent palliative or prophylactic mastectomy, experienced disease relapse or had co-existing non–breast malignancies were excluded to ensure a clinically homogeneous cohort with comparable prognosis. In addition, women with upper-limb oedema were excluded as it is associated with functional and psychological impairments, which could confound QOL assessments of the study [16]. Patients who had undergone chemotherapy or radiotherapy within the preceding two months or mastectomy within the previous six months were also excluded to minimise the influence of acute treatment-related effects and allow adequate postoperative recovery before evaluation.

The minimum sample required was 138, calculated to detect an expected proportion of EBP use among post-mastectomy patients of 90% (as per the clinical judgement of consultant oncologists, in the absence of research evidence on the use of EBP), with a precision of 0.05 and a Z value of 1.96. After considering 15% non-response, it was decided to recruit a sample of 166. To obtain this sample, systematic random sampling was adopted over a period of two months in the follow-up clinics conducted daily at the NCIM. After randomly selecting one clinic per day, eligible patients were approached for consent, using a systematic sampling method based on their clinic registration number. The first patient was randomly chosen from the first five eligible patients, and thereafter, every other patient, until a maximum of 10 per clinic day was recruited.

Patient recruitment and data collection were conducted by trained fourth-year medical undergraduates (2 males and 1 female) under the supervision of a medical specialist. Prior to medical consultation, eligible patients were provided with a written information sheet and verbal explanation of the study, after which written informed consent was obtained from those willing to participate. Data were collected using a pre-tested interviewer-administered questionnaire available in both local languages, which was completed verbally by participants in a separate area of the clinic that ensured privacy. Whenever a participant preferred a female data collector, it was done so to ensure patient comfort and adherence to ethical standards.

The questionnaire assessed socio-demographic characteristics of the participants and their EBP use, which was assessed in terms of use (current users, previous/discontinued users and never users), sources of information received on EBP, usage patterns among current users and perceived barriers among non-users. Perceived barriers, particularly of complex constructs such as stigma and embarrassment, were operationalised based on expert opinion and views of the target population.

In addition, the questionnaire assessed their QOL across multiple aspects, namely overall well-being, freedom from back pain, mental status, satisfaction with body shape, ability to wear clothes of their own choice and in a way that fits, overall personality, confidence in interacting with others, sexual satisfaction, and relationship with spouse. A visual 5-point Likert scale (ranging from ‘extremely dissatisfied’ to ‘extremely satisfied’) with facial expressions was used for subjective ratings of each aspect. Initially, questions to be included in the QOL questionnaire were systematically developed to capture EBP-specific psychosocial concerns in this population based on a thorough literature review. Although not directly related to EBP use, the EORTC QLQ-BR45 was also perused to identify the general QOL aspects of breast cancer patients (questions 42 and 46). Face validity of the questionnaire on QOL was assessed using a purposive sample of 10 post-mastectomy women to ensure clarity, relevance and appropriateness of each item. Feedback obtained focused on refining wording for comprehensibility. Content validity of the QOL questionnaire was established by expert opinion using a modified Delphi technique involving a multidisciplinary expert panel comprising a consultant oncologist, a public health specialist and a psychologist. All of them had experience in breast cancer care, survivorship and quality of life research. Two iterative rounds of Delphi were conducted, through which ratings made by experts on the relevance and clarity of each item were reviewed using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant). Suggestions for modification or addition of items were also allowed. Consensus on retained items was defined a priori as at least 80% of experts rating an item as either 3 or 4 on the scale. Accordingly, the initial 12 questions considered for expert opinion were finally reduced to 10 questions. The final questionnaire was pre-tested in another 10 post-mastectomy women to ensure further clarity, flow and cultural appropriateness.

Since the QOL questionnaire did not include psychometrically validated multidimensional domains, an overall QOL composite score was not calculated. Instead, based on the responses given by the participants for each QOL aspect, they were dichotomised into ‘satisfied’ (extremely satisfied, moderately satisfied, mildly satisfied) and ‘not satisfied’ (dissatisfied, extremely dissatisfied) with QOL. This categorisation was developed by the same panel of experts.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 20. EBP use and related practices were described using mean and standard deviation for quantitative variables and percentages for categorical variables. The factors associated with EBP use were identified by comparing the current EBP users with non-current users using Chi-squared test at 0.05 level of significance and calculating crude odds ratios (OR) in univariate analysis. Further, multivariate logistic regression analysis was carried out to determine these factors adjusted for confounders, by calculating adjusted OR. In the model, current EBP use (yes, no) was included as the dependent variable, while their demographic, socio-economic and disease related characteristics were included as independent variables. Backward LR method with removal at 0.1 significance was adopted. Model fitting statistics were derived to assess goodness-of-fit of the model. The association between EBP use and each QOL aspect (satisfied, not satisfied) was also assessed in both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses with adjustments made for confounding effects using the same procedure.

Results

A total of 158 women participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 94.9%. Their age ranged from 23 to 82 years, with a mean age of 60.6 years (SD = 11.7). The majority were above 60 years of age (n = 84; 53.2%), married (n = 136; 86.1%), Buddhists (n = 104; 65.8%), living in urban sector (n = 87; 55.1%), educated beyond secondary school education (n = 101; 63.9%) and unemployed (n = 125; 79.1%) with a monthly family income of less than LKR 50,000 (n = 103; 65.2%). The time since mastectomy was ≥ 18 months in 139 (87.9%), with a mean of 52.1 months (SD = 26.3).

Among the participants, 93 (58.9%) were currently using an EBP, while 59 (37.3%) had never used an EBP, and six (3.8%) had used it previously but now discontinued.

Among the 59 participants who had never used an EBP, the most commonly perceived barrier was lack of awareness of its availability (78.0%), followed by poor access (61.0%) and perceptions that its use is socio-culturally inappropriate (55.9%), embarrassing (45.8%) or associated with social stigma (44.1%) (Fig. 1).

Among participants who discontinued EBP, perceived barriers for continued use included poor access after obtaining the first prosthesis (16.7%), discomfort caused by sweating (50.0%) and challenges associated with maintenance (33.3%). All of them had obtained their first prosthesis from external sources.

EBP usage patterns among current users

The current EBP users (n = 93) obtained information about EBP mainly from hospital staff (58.1%) especially from minor staff members, followed by field-based staff (12.9%) and non-health sources such as the internet and mass media (24.8%). The main reasons for initiating EBP use were to mimic the natural breast (97.8%) and for pain relief at the surgical site (8.6%). Around 35% of the users started using EBP within one-year post-mastectomy. Regarding the EBP that they used for the first time, 21.5.% received it as a donation from a volunteer organization, 70.9% made it themselves at home and 5.4% purchased it from a shop. The majority (76.3%) spent less than LKR 1000, whereas 10.8% spent between LKR 1000–5000. The prostheses currently used by participants were mostly made of non-foam local material (75.3%), with only 24.7% made of foam. Only a single user had silicon external prostheses. The majority of users (81.7%) changed their prostheses once or multiple times after the mastectomy. For maintenance, 72.0% claimed that they clean the prosthesis daily; 3.2% weekly; and 1.1% each either monthly, once every two months or less frequently. Over 90% of the users reported that the prostheses were easy to clean and durable.

With respect to satisfaction with EBP use during various activities (Fig. 2), the majority of current users were satisfied, particularly when used at social events (95.7%), outdoor shopping (95.7%) and at work (77.4%).

Factors associated with EBP use

In the univariate analysis, women aged 18–60 years (67.6%) compared to those over 60 years of age (51.2%: p = 0.03); non-Buddhists (59.3% vs. 58.7%: p = 0.94); being single (77.3% vs. 55.9%: p = 0.05); residing in the non-urban sector (59.2% vs. 58.6%: p = 0.95); with secondary education (61.4% vs. 54.4%: p = 0.39) and monthly family income over LKR 50,000 (67.3% vs. 54.4%: p = 0.12); unemployed (60.0% vs. 54.5%: p = 0.57); and with longer time since surgery (60.4% vs. 47.4%: p = 0.28) showed higher EBP use. Of these relationships, younger age (adjusted OR = 2.4; 95% CI: 1.2, 4.9; p = 0.02) and being single (adjusted OR = 3.2 (95% CI: 1.1, 9.5; p = 0.03) remained significantly associated with current use of EBP after adjustment for confounders in the regression analysis (Table 1).

Quality of life aspects and their relationship with EBP use

The QOL aspects of current EBP users and non-current users are compared in Fig. 3. More than 90% of the current EBP users were satisfied with overall well-being (93.5%), not having back pain (93.5%), mental status (90.3%), body shape (93.5%), ability to wear clothes of their own style (92.5%), cloth fitting (91.4%), overall personality (96.8%) and confidence in interactions with others (97.4%). In contrast, more than 75% of EBP non-users were dissatisfied with their cosmetic appearance, such as body shape (76.9%), wearing clothes of their own style (75.4%), being able to wear fitting clothes (76.9%) and confidence in interacting with others (86.0%).

Discussion

This study sheds light on the relatively high prevalence of current EBP use among post-mastectomy patients in Sri Lanka. It further highlights younger age and being single as significant factors associated with their current use, while lack of awareness, poor access as well as socio-cultural and religious beliefs and stigma have been perceived as barriers among those who had discontinued or never used a prosthesis. A significant relationship was noted between EBP use and all aspects of QOL.

In the present study, the prevalence of current EBP use was 58.9%, exceeding the 40% reported from a tertiary cancer treatment centre in Bangalore, India [17]. This higher level of utilization was observed despite the absence of a formally established prosthesis donation programme in Sri Lanka, and despite more than half of the current users learning about the availability of EBP through informal sources, primarily hospital support staff. The limited role of medical personnel in providing such information aligns with findings from a study conducted in Shanghai, China [18]. Moreover, among women who had never used an EBP, the primary barriers were lack of awareness of its availability (78.0%) and poor access (61.0%), highlighting an unmet need for providing timely information on EBP to all women soon after undergoing mastectomy. In this context, although not currently integrated into the national rehabilitation programme, the Indira Cancer Trust, a non-profit organization, provides the first prosthesis free of charge and offers guidance for fabricating subsequent prostheses. Despite this, only 21.5% of patients accessed their first prosthesis through this channel. To ensure sustainability, it is strongly recommended that the NCCP-SL formalize all existing informal donor channels to ensure a continuous and equitable supply of EBP. Recognition of these donor initiatives by the Ministry of Health, Sri Lanka, is also warranted. Additionally, empowering women to fabricate low-cost prostheses at home could further enhance community integration and strengthen comprehensive survivorship care.

Despite a higher proportion of EBP use seen among those with higher income, higher education, living in the non-urban sector and unemployed, none of these differences were significant (p > 0.05). These findings indicate the need for promotion of EBPs regardless of patients’ socio-economic status. In contrast, younger age (adjusted OR = 2.4) and being single (adjusted OR = 3.2) appeared to be crucial factors associated with their current EBP use. In comparison, similar proportions of EBP use among older and married women were reported in a study of post-mastectomy women in Bangalore [17]. This trend may reflect cultural norms in Asia, where body image concerns are often less emphasized among older and married women, resulting in lower motivation to use a prosthesis solely for aesthetic purposes. However, since all different aspects of QOL have improved in EBP users across all age groups, post-mastectomy patients, especially older women should be motivated towards EBP use during post-mastectomy education.

Internationally, a variety of EBPs are available and preferred by patients. In our study, no participant opted for surgical prosthesis insertions. Usage of silicone external prostheses was also minimal, whereas cloth and cotton prostheses were more commonly used, possibly due to greater comfort in humid conditions. Such practical considerations should be taken into account when developing low-cost prostheses for women in tropical regions. In this context, in low- and middle-income settings, locally manufactured, low-cost prostheses have shown both feasibility and acceptability, although challenges related to affordability, awareness, and access to fitting services persist [19, 20]. Qualitative studies have also highlighted challenges concerning maintenance, cultural acceptability and patient education [21, 22]. In the local context, these issues could be addressed by providing post-mastectomy care leaflets in local languages and establishing information hotlines.

Numerous perceived barriers were cited for never using EBP. A substantial proportion not being aware of EBP as a viable option in post-mastectomy care is noteworthy. This finding is compatible with a study conducted in India, where unawareness was reported among never-users as 8.33% [17]. Our results further indicated that non-use of EBP was influenced by their sociocultural and religious norms. Over 40% of the patients perceived EBP usage as a stigmatizing incident, even though there were no unfounded concerns about cancer recurrence. Educational interventions have been shown to significantly improve knowledge, correct use, and self-esteem among post-mastectomy women [22, 23], highlighting the relevance of establishing patient-support groups. Such groups can provide a platform for post-mastectomy women to openly exchange ideas and experiences and support each other, aiming to dispel stigma and overcome potential barriers through public health sector involvement.

The study revealed a significant beneficial association between EBP usage and QOL in all aspects, highlighting the beneficial influence of EBPs on patients’ well-being. These findings were similar to previous research in the USA, which highlighted the importance of EBPs in repairing body image and providing comfort [4]. Another study conducted in Italy highlighted the negative effects of breast surgeries, in contrast to the satisfaction indicated by 65.3% of EBP users with their enhanced body shape [5]. This highlighted a substantial link between EBP use and improved body shape perception. Notably, due to the low response rate, the influence of EBP use on their sexuality and intimate relationships could not be ascertained.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Although a low response was expected owing to the sensitivity of the topic in the Sri Lankan context, taking adequate precautions, such as having anonymized questionnaires and collecting data in an isolated area to ensure privacy, led to a response rate over 90%. The systematic sampling method used was ideal for selecting a sample from clinics with a dynamic population, thus minimising selection bias. The sample included patients who had been biopsy-proven for cancer, making misclassification unlikely. The assessments of factors associated with current EBP use and its relationship with QOL aspects were adjusted for major confounders, which were considered based on their potential influence on the use of EBP, as suggested by previous literature and clinical considerations. The external validity of the study findings was enhanced by selecting a representative sample of breast cancer patients in Sri Lanka from the NCIM, which serves as a beacon for post-mastectomy follow-up care.

This study had some limitations. The cross-sectional design precluded causal inferences between EBP use and QOL. In addition, self-reported data collected in clinic settings may have introduced social desirability bias. Sampling restricted to outpatient clinics, with exclusions based on QOL-affecting conditions, may have also led to sampling bias with underrepresentation of those with limited healthcare access, advanced disease or recent surgery. Further, the overall QOL was not assessed using a composite score, while the lack of qualitative data limited the exploration of nuanced psychosocial experiences related to EBP use.

Conclusions

In post-mastectomy patients, EBP use was significantly associated with their satisfaction with QOL in all aspects, highlighting its importance in post-mastectomy follow-up care, especially in settings where conservative breast surgery is limited. The use of EBP was relatively high, which was significantly associated with younger age and being single. Barriers such as lack of knowledge on the availability and poor access among never users indicate the need for proper EBP supply by integrating informal EBP channels into rehabilitation programmes, as well as improving patient awareness through hospital- and field-based healthcare staff. Further, patient support groups are recommended to dispel socio-cultural and religious beliefs and stigma, which are identified as the main barriers for never use.
