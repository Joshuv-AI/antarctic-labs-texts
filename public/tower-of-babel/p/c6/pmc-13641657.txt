Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Esophagitis: Clinical and Endoscopic Presentation, Treatment Outcomes, and Complications
Authors: Matthew J. Townsend, Afeerah Mumtaz, Jordan S. Sack, Anne F. Liu, Elad Sharon, Nicole R. LeBoeuf, F. Stephen Hodi, Shilpa Grover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:We characterized features of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) esophagitis.METHODS:This was a retrospective cohort of biopsy-confirmed ICI esophagitis.RESULTS:Among 11,636 ICI-treated adults, 22 of 312 (7%) undergoing esophagogastroduodenoscopy had ICI esophagitis (0.2% overall). Concomitant luminal ICI toxicities occurred in 19 of 22 (86%); 8 of 22 (36%) had normal esophageal appearance. Most experienced symptom resolution with acid suppression, steroids, and/or biologic therapy; however, 1 developed refractory strictures.DISCUSSION:ICI esophagitis is rare, often coexists with other gastrointestinal toxicities, and may seem endoscopically normal, underscoring importance of biopsies. Symptoms are typically treatment-responsive, but histologic inflammation can persist. Rare severe complications highlight needs for recognition and tailored therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have been increasingly used for advanced malignancies, but they carry risks of immune-related adverse events affecting multiple organs. Although gastrointestinal complications including colitis and hepatotoxicity are well characterized, knowledge of ICI esophagitis remains limited to isolated case reports (1–5) and small series (6–8). These previous reports often pooled esophageal and other upper gastrointestinal (GI) toxicities or lacked biopsy confirmation, creating uncertainty about clinical features, real-world management, disease sequelae, and outcomes. Here, we present the largest available cohort of patients with biopsy-confirmed ICI esophagitis, describing their clinical characteristics and endoscopic manifestations, treatment responses, and complications.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with cancer who received at least 1 ICI dose between January 2011 and August 2023 and who underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) while receiving ICIs. ICIs included programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) inhibitors (pembrolizumab and nivolumab), PD-1 ligand (PD-L1) inhibitors (atezolizumab, avelumab, and durvalumab), and/or cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor ipilimumab. Patients who received neck or chest radiation were excluded. Biopsy-confirmed cases of ICI esophagitis were assessed by 2 physicians based on clinical notes, active lymphocytic inflammation on histopathology, and exclusion of alternative causes of esophagitis. Demographic, medical history, endoscopic, and clinical outcome data were obtained from electronic medical record reviews. Patients were followed until September 2023 or death if sooner. The institutional review board approved the study. Wilcoxon rank sum and Fisher exact tests were performed to assess associations for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

RESULTS

Among 11,636 adult patients who received ICIs, 312 (3%) underwent EGD; 22 of 312 (7.1%) were diagnosed with ICI esophagitis (overall prevalence: 0.2%). The median age was 67 years, and majorities were male (68%) and non-Hispanic White (95%) (Table 1). At baseline, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) were used in 36% and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in 9%. A PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitor alone was administered in 18 (82%) patients, and 4 (18%) also received a CTLA-4 inhibitor. Patients developed ICI esophagitis after a median of 3 cycles of ICI, with median follow-up of 81 weeks post-ICI initiation.

Concurrent gastrointestinal luminal toxicities were observed in 19 of 22 (86%) patients: 16 of 22 (72%) with additional upper GI toxicity (gastritis and/or enteritis) and 9 of 22 (41%) with concurrent ICI colitis. Isolated esophageal toxicity was observed in 3 of 22 (14%) patients. 14 of 22 (64%) patients underwent simultaneous lower gastrointestinal evaluation (flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy) alongside diagnostic EGD.

The most common symptoms were diarrhea (64%), nausea/vomiting (32%), dysphagia (27%), abdominal pain (23%), and heartburn (18%) (Table 1). Indications for baseline and follow-up EGDs are presented in Supplementary Table S1 (http://links.lww.com/CTG/B511). Most of the patients were found to have abnormal esophageal appearance endoscopically: 9% ulcers, 18% erosions, and 46% nonerosive mucosal changes. However, 8 of 22 (36%) patients had normal endoscopic appearance of the esophagus with biopsies confirming the ICI esophagitis diagnosis.

Management included hospitalization of 9 of 22 (41%) patients all of whom had concomitant luminal toxicit(ies). A total of 8 (36%) patients were treated with acid suppression alone (PPI or H2RA intensification), including all 3 patients with isolated ICI esophagitis. Of patients who escalated acid suppression, 4 eventually tapered off PPI (median 231 days); the rest did not attempt taper. Steroids were administered in 14 of 22 (64%), infliximab in 3 of 22 (14%), and vedolizumab in 4 of 22 (18%) patients (Table 2). All biologic use occurred among patients treated with steroids. Steroid use was significantly higher in patients with concurrent luminal ICI toxicity than in those with isolated ICI esophagitis (74% vs 0%, P = 0.04). Steroid taper doses and durations varied widely and are summarized in Table 2. Most patients (16/22, 73%) required discontinuation of ICIs without rechallenge, although only 1 patient required discontinuation specifically for ICI esophagitis. The most frequent causes of ICI discontinuation were ICI colitis (4 patients), enteritis (3 patients), and nephritis (2 patients), and cancer progression (2 patients).

Persistent histologic or endoscopic inflammation was seen in 6 of the 10 patients who underwent repeat endoscopy (at median 147 days from index EGD), including 4 of the 7 patients who received steroids. One patient who presented with dysphagia, heartburn, and diarrhea underwent EGD and colonoscopy and was diagnosed with concomitant ICI esophagitis and ICI colitis. The patient was treated with omeprazole, prednisone, and subsequently infliximab resulting in resolution of diarrhea and colitis. Follow-up endoscopy for dysphagia revealed persistent lymphocytic esophageal inflammation and multiple strictures requiring esophageal dilation, unfortunately complicated by perforation. CT demonstrated widely progressive metastatic disease, the patient was felt not to be a surgical candidate, and they elected to transition to comfort measures only (inpatient hospice); they passed several days later.

DISCUSSION

Timely recognition and effective management of gastrointestinal complications from ICIs are essential for patients with cancer who receive these transformative cancer therapies. To our knowledge, this is the largest published cohort of patients with biopsy-supported lymphocytic ICI esophagitis, supporting previous findings that PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors are more frequently associated with ICI esophagitis than are CTLA-4 inhibitors (6,8,10). Like ICI gastritis, ICI esophagitis often co-occurs with other luminal toxicities including colitis (8,11). Some patients experience favorable outcomes with symptom resolution through acid suppression alone (H2RA or PPI) (8,12).

In contrast to the previous cohort of Panneerselvam et al in which 52% of patients did not undergo esophageal biopsies, our cohort assessed biopsy-proven ICI esophagitis and had a higher proportion of repeat EGDs. Furthermore, in contrast to other studies that have reported the presence of ICI esophagitis only in patients with endoscopic mucosal abnormalities (12), we noted ICI esophagitis in 36% of patients with a normal endoscopic appearance, supporting the role of esophageal biopsy—even when endoscopically normal—if ICI toxicity is suspected. This raises questions whether the “microscopic” ICI esophagitis without endoscopically visualized inflammation has a different natural history or treatment response, akin to ICI-induced microscopic colitis (vs typical ICI colitis).

The prevalence of ICI esophagitis is low (diagnosed in 7.1% of patients in our cohort who underwent EGD and 0.2% of those exposed to ICIs). However, prevalence may be underestimated where biopsies are not routinely performed, where patients are managed with empiric medical therapy without diagnostic endoscopy, and due to the potential for some ICI-exposed patients in our cohort to have developed immunotoxicity after the follow-up period.

Limitations include a modest sample size which restricted statistical comparisons and modeling. Our study limited inclusion to patients with lymphocytic inflammation to minimize misclassification of other more common causes of neutrophilic esophagitis (e.g. gastroesophageal reflux disease) as ICI-induced; other variants of ICI esophagitis may exist and are not captured here. The absence of a control group (retrospective design) limits generalizability and precluded characterization of non-ICI lymphocytic esophagitis, although this remains quite rare (13). Racial/ethnic homogeneity in our study may also limit generalizability to other populations.

Although most patients responded well to acid suppression and/or immunosuppression (the latter usually guided by concomitant luminal toxicity), the highlighted case of refractory stricturing esophagitis despite resolution of concurrent nonesophageal luminal inflammation underscores the importance of continued treatment of ICI esophagitis until resolution. Larger future studies may help define high-risk characteristics and predictors of treatment response.
