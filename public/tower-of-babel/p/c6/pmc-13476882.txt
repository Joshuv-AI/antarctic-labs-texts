Targeting mitotic kinesin KIF20A: A differentiation-based therapeutic strategy for glioblastoma stem/progenitor cells
Authors: Runxiang Qiu, Alejandra Velazquez Ojeda, Cesar Gonzalez, Vincente Abatay, Jun Wu, Dina Awabdeh, Renate Starr, Nadia Carlesso, Sarah Shuck, Yun Rose Li, Christine E. Brown, Michael E. Barish, Qiang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) remains refractory to current treatment modalities. Differentiation-based approaches, which force cancer stem/progenitor cells to exit the cell cycle and adopt terminal fates, offer an alternative therapeutic strategy. Cell fate regulators operating during stem/progenitor cell divisions integrate proliferative and anti-proliferative cues and represent particularly attractive points of intervention. Here, we investigated the therapeutic potential of targeting mitotic kinesin KIF20A in GBM stem/progenitor cells. KIF20A is a crucial component of cytokinetic machinery and cooperates with a network of cell fate regulators to balance proliferative and differentiative divisions in neural stem/progenitor cells (NSPCs). Using complementary in vitro and in vivo models, including 2D cultures, 3D organoids, and intracranial xenografts, we show that inhibition of KIF20A drives cell cycle exit and induces a postmitotic/differentiated state in GBM stem/progenitor cells, resulting in a marked suppression of proliferation. Together, these findings establish KIF20A as a key vulnerability in GBM and a promising target for differentiation-based intervention.

Introduction

GBM is the most common and aggressive form of brain cancer in adults. Despite the use of multimodal treatment strategies, including surgical resection and/or radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy, the prognosis remains poor. Median survival is approximately 15 months, and the 5-year survival rate is less than 5% (Louis et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2020). In addition to the challenges posed by the diffuse invasiveness of GBM cells and the blood-brain barrier, a major obstacle in GBM therapy is that the tumor-initiating stem/progenitor cells (Galli et al., 2004; Ignatova et al., 2002; Singh et al., 2003, 2004; Yuan et al., 2004) are inherently heterogeneous (Little et al., 2012; Patel et al., 2014; Snuderl et al., 2011; Sottoriva et al., 2013; Szerlip et al., 2012). This intratumor heterogeneity is a key contributor to therapeutic resistance (Bao et al., 2006; Eramo et al., 2006), which limits the efficacy of current treatments. As a result, there is a pressing need to develop novel therapeutic strategies for GBM.

Sustained proliferation or blocked differentiation is a major pathological feature of malignant cancers including GBM. Cell differentiation process has been proposed as a target to break uncontrolled proliferation of cancer stem/progenitor cells by driving them to adopt a post-mitotic/differentiated cell state. All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)-induced leukemia blast differentiation was one remarkable example (Huang et al., 1988) which revolutionized the treatment for acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) and highlighted the therapeutic potential of differentiation therapy. The success of ATRA-based treatment of APL has inspired extensive efforts to develop additional differentiation-inducing agents (de The, 2018; Lewis and Kats, 2021; Madan and Koeffler, 2021). Many factors or pathways implicated in the control of proliferation and differentiation, such as IDH1/2 in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) (Figueroa et al., 2010; Lu et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2013) and RSPO3 of the PTPRK-RSPO3 axis in colon cancer (Storm et al., 2016), have been explored as potential targets. However, identifying an effective target for differentiation-based strategies has remained a significant challenge despite decades of research (de The, 2018; Lewis and Kats, 2021; Madan and Koeffler, 2021). The primary obstacle in developing an effective differentiation-inducing agent is likely the presence of multiple driver mutations that fuel cancer progression and/or the inherent heterogeneity of cancer stem/progenitor cells.

Cell fate regulators that function during cell divisions are key factors integrating growth stimulating vs. inhibiting pathways; they are expected to directly impact the outcome of a stem/progenitor cell’s division mode: producing daughter cells either staying in proliferation or becoming differentiated. As such, these factors are potentially unique targets for developing differentiation-based therapies as they are expected to lie downstream of major proliferation-promoting (oncogenic) pathways. In our study of how NSPCs control the proliferation vs. differentiation decisions during embryonic mouse brain development, we identified a protein-protein interaction network, comprising EphrinB, RGS3, KIF20A, and SEPT7 (collectively referred to as the “ERKS” axis), which operates at the late stage of cytokinesis to regulate fate specification of nascent daughter cells. We found that the ERKS axis proteins are essential for balancing proliferation and differentiation during brain development. Specifically, genetic loss-of-function (LoF) of any of the four proteins in cortical radial glial cells (RGCs) (Geng et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2008, 2010, 2020) or LoF of KIF20A in cerebellar granule neuron progenitors (GNPs) (Qiu et al., 2021) promoted a shift in these cells from proliferative to differentiative divisions. These findings suggest that the ERKS axis could play a similar role in brain tumor stem/progenitor cells. In one study, we tested the inhibition of KIF20A in a genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of medulloblastoma. Our data showed that genetic LoF of KIF20A in tumor-initiating GNPs induced cell cycle exit and precocious neuronal differentiation, thereby suppressing tumor growth (Qiu et al., 2021). Thus, KIF20A is also critical for balancing proliferation and differentiation in brain tumor stem/progenitor cells. Importantly, our data revealed that blocking the function of the ERKS axis proteins in normal (Geng et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2008, 2010, 2020, 2021) and malignant (Qiu et al., 2021) NSPCs had minimal adverse impact on cell divisions (cytokinesis) of the parental stem/progenitor cells but promoted differentiation of their daughter cells.

The ERKS axis proteins are expressed in most, if not all, types of NSPC populations, including cortical RGCs, cerebellar GNPs, as well as spinal and adult subventricular zone NSPCs. These proteins are expected to play a crucial role in maintaining the proliferation of various NSPC populations. Thus, although the precise mechanisms by which the ERKS axis regulates daughter cell fate remain to be fully elucidated, evidence from our mouse genetic studies suggests that this pathway may represent a shared vulnerability amenable to differentiation-based therapeutic strategies across multiple brain tumor types or subtypes. To assess the translational relevance of targeting this axis in human brain tumors, we employed primary brain tumor (PBT) cells derived from patients with GBM (Brown et al., 2009, 2012). In this study, we focused on inhibiting KIF20A and evaluated its functional impacts on PBT cell proliferation and differentiation. Our data showed that disruption of KIF20A function in human PBT cells promoted differentiation and suppressed proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, consistent with our findings from mouse genetic studies.

Results and discussion

Depletion of KIF20A in PBT cells inhibited proliferation with minimal adverse effects on cell division or survival

We first constructed a plasmid-based short hairpin RNA (shRNA), shKIF20A726, targeting the human KIF20A transcript. This shRNA effectively knocked down endogenous KIF20A expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in PBT017 cells via lentiviral transduction (Figures S1A and S1B). To evaluate the cellular effects of KIF20A LoF, we delivered shKIF20A726 or a control shRNA (shScrambled) into multiple PBT cell lines. Expression of shKIF20A726 significantly suppressed cell proliferation in these cells (Figure 1A). We further performed extreme limiting dilution analysis (ELDA) (Hu and Smyth, 2009) to determine the impact of KIF20A LoF on the self-renewal capacity of PBT cells. To independently validate this finding, we next disrupted the KIF20A gene in PBT017 cells using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing. Two specific guide RNAs (gRNAs) were selected based on their ability to efficiently induce Cas9-mediated DNA cleavage of KIF20A in human cells (Figures S1C and S1D). Individual gRNAs (gRNA-1, gRNA-6, or a control gRNA), Cas9, and mCherry reporter were assembled into a lentiviral expression vector and delivered into PBT017 cells by lentiviral transduction. Following transduction, the cells were divided into two groups. One group was cultured for two days and subsequently harvested for genomic DNA extraction, PCR amplification of the targeting region, subcloning into pBSK vector and sequencing analysis, while the second group was subjected to western blot and cell proliferation analyses. Our data showed that co-expression of gRNA-1 or gRNA-6 with Cas9 in PBT017 cells induced frameshift mutations in the KIF20A gene (Figure 1C), leading to reduced KIF20A protein levels (Figure S1E) and suppression of cell proliferation (Figure 1D).

To address whether the reduced proliferation rate observed upon KIF20A LoF resulted from cytokinesis failure and/or increased cell death, we examined cell cycle-associated DNA content and apoptosis markers in PBT017 cells following KIF20A depletion, achieved either by shRNA-mediated knockdown (Figures 1E and 1F) or CRISPR-mediated gene editing (Figure S1F). Our data showed that KIF20A LoF in PBT017 cells did not cause obvious abnormality in cytokinesis (comparing 4N DNA contents; Figure 1E) or induce apoptosis (comparing expression of activated caspase 3; Figures 1F and S1F).

Depletion of KIF20A in PBT cells induced cell cycle exit and neuronal differentiation

We previously demonstrated, using a GEMM system of medulloblastoma, that KIF20A LoF induced cell cycle exit and promoted neuronal differentiation of tumor-initiating GNPs (Qiu et al., 2021). Based on these findings, we next examined the effects of KIF20A LoF on the differentiation and survival of PBT cells. In one experiment, shKIF20A726 or control shScrambled were delivered into multiple lines of PBT cells via lentiviral transduction. Cells were collected three days post-transduction and assessed for cell cycle exit status following a 24-h labeling of 5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EdU). In a separate experiment, PBT cells transduced by lentivirus of shKIF20A726 or shScrambled were cultured for ten days after lentiviral transduction to assess cell differentiation and survival. KIF20A depletion did not adversely affect the overall viability of the PBT cells (Figures 2C, S2B, and S2C), consistent with apoptosis analyses performed at earlier time points (Figure 1F). Analyses of cellular markers revealed that KIF20A LoF in PBT cells led to an increase in neuronal differentiation (Figures 2C, 2D, S2B, and S2C).

We next employed CRISPR-based gene editing to disrupt KIF20A function in PBT017 cells. Following lentiviral transduction of gRNAs and Cas9, cells were cultured for ten days and then analyzed for differentiation. Consistent with the shRNA results, KIF20A knockout did not compromise cell viability but markedly promoted cellular differentiation (Figures 2E and S2D). Collectively, these data indicate that inhibition of KIF20A function in PBT cells suppresses proliferation primarily by promoting cell cycle exit and inducing differentiation.

Depletion of KIF20A in PBT cell-derived organoids promoted differentiation and inhibited proliferation

To further examine the impact of KIF20A LoF on proliferation vs. differentiation of the PBT cells, we next utilized an established brain organoid system (Ojeda et al., 2025), which provides a more complex structure that better mimics in vivo cellular organizations and interactions. Organoid cultures were initiated by seeding a clone of PBT017 cells, which was engineered to harbor a stably integrated Tet-on inducible shKIF20A726 expression cassette (PBT017-Tet/on-shKIF20A726 cell). We confirmed that the cells were responsive to doxycycline treatment, which inhibited KIF20A expression and suppressed cell proliferation (Figures S3A–S3C).

Thirty days after the initial seeding of PBT017-Tet/on-shKIF20A726 cells, organoids were randomly divided into two groups: one group was treated with doxycycline (2 μg/mL) for three days, while the other group remained untreated as a control. All organoids were labeled with EdU for 24 h before fixation by 4% paraformaldehyde for immunostaining analyses (Figure 3A). After 30 days in culture, organoids derived from the PBT017-Tet/on-shKIF20A726 cells displayed an appearance with peripheral protrusions/bulges. The bulges appeared to be fast growing areas, which were enriched with proliferating cells marked by the expression of Ki67 or labeled with EdU (Figure 3B). Upon doxycycline treatment, the organoids exhibited a significant reduction in proliferating cells both in the interior and in the bulges (Figure 3B), but this was not due to an increase in apoptosis (Figure 3C). Analyses of co-staining of Ki67 and EdU in PBT cells located in the bulges showed that doxycycline treatment induced cell cycle exit (Figures 3D and S3D), consistent with the data from 2D cultures (Figure 2A). Within the population of EdU-labeled cells that had left the cell cycle (EdU+Ki67- cells), co-staining of EdU, Ki67, and neuronal marker NeuN or TBR1 showed that doxycycline treatment resulted in an increase of neuronal cells (EdU+Ki67−NeuN+ or EdU+Ki67−TBR1+ cells) (Figures 3D and S3D). In a separate experiment, some of the organoids were replenished with new media free of doxycycline after three days of doxycycline treatment and were cultured for two more weeks (Figure 3E). Doxycycline-induced KIF20A knockdown in organoids derived from a different PBT line (PBT106 cells) similarly showed that KIF20A inhibition promoted cell cycle exit and differentiation (Figure S4). Together, these data support the idea that inhibition of KIF20A function in PBT cells suppresses proliferation by inducing differentiation.

Depletion of KIF20A suppressed proliferation of GBM stem/progenitor cells in growing tumors

To assess the effect of KIF20A LoF on the proliferation of PBT cells in tumors, we examined a brain tumor xenograft model. NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice were intracerebrally injected with PBT017 cells (105 cells per mouse) carrying stably integrated expression cassettes of Tet-on inducible shKIF20A726 and luciferase. When tumor growth in the recipient mice was evident by bioluminescence imaging, the mice were randomly separated into two groups: one group was treated with doxycycline and the other group was untreated as control. For the treatment group, mice were first fed with doxycycline solution (10 mg/mL, 0.2 mL) by gavage daily for two consecutive days. They were then kept on doxycycline-containing food continuously. The data showed that, in comparison with the control group, mice that received doxycycline treatment displayed a reduced growth rate of brain tumors (Figure 4A) and better overall survival (Figure 4B). To assess the cellular characteristics of brain tumors, we performed immunostaining for Ki67, SOX2, and NeuN on tumor tissue sections. Tumors from control mice exhibited a typical solid tumor cell mass (Figure 4C). In contrast, a substantial portion of tumor tissues from doxycycline-treated mice showed evidence of liquefaction, likely due to necrosis or necrosis-like processes, which compromised tissue fixation and retention in the sections. In the remaining solid areas of these doxycycline-treated tumors, the samples exhibited a decrease in Ki67+ and SOX2+ cells, accompanied by an increase in NeuN+ cells, relative to tumor samples from untreated controls (Figure 4D). Concluding remarks

Driving cancer stem/progenitor cells to exit the cell cycle and enter a differentiated state represents a promising strategy for cancer therapy. Unlike currently available chemotherapeutic agents, which are designed to halt cell division or kill mitotic cells, an effective differentiation agent would ideally avoid disrupting cell division or cytokinesis in proliferating stem/progenitor cells. Instead, it would promote the differentiation of newly generated daughter cells. Differentiation therapy can, therefore, inhibit tumor cell proliferation while maintaining genomic stability and avoiding cytokinesis defects that may lead to aneuploidy. The success of ATRA-induced differentiation of leukemia blasts in the treatment of acute APL has demonstrated the remarkable potency and clinical efficacy of differentiation therapy and has inspired increasing efforts to develop additional differentiation-inducing agents. In this context, the ERKS axis proteins appear to be particularly promising targets for engineering differentiation-based therapies. First, our genetic data in both normal (Geng et al., 2018; Qiu et al., 2008, 2010, 2020, 2021) and malignant (Qiu et al., 2021) NSPCs demonstrated that blocking the function of this axis does not disrupt cell division (cytokinesis) per se but instead promotes nascent daughter cells to exit the cell cycle, thereby reducing the pool of proliferating cells. The present study targeting KIF20A in human GBM stem/progenitor cells provides further support for this concept. Second, the ERKS axis proteins were the first identified regulators of cell division modes that act during the late stage of cytokinesis. It is, therefore, conceivable that they function downstream of most, if not all, proliferation-promoting pathways. Consequently, targeting this axis may suppress the proliferation of diverse subtypes of PBT cells, as well as recurrent tumor cells, without increasing the risk of mutagenesis. This approach may, therefore, offer a unique strategy to address the heterogeneity of GBM stem/progenitor cells. We anticipate that drugs targeting the ERKS axis proteins could be used either alone or in combination with precision medicine approaches, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy for the treatment of GBM.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice were obtained from in house breeding colony. Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were carried out in accordance with NIH guideline and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

PBT cells

PBT lines were derived from patient specimens as previously described (Brown et al., 2009). Cells of passages 12-14 were first expanded for two passages in neural stem/progenitor cell growth media containing DMEM-Ham’s F12 50:50 Mixture with L-glutamine and 15 mM HEPES, 1 x B-27, 1 x Pen/Strep, 20 ng/ml recombinant human EGF and 20 ng/ml recombinant human FGF. The expanded cells were then cryo-frozen and stored as stock cells. When needed, stock cells were thawed, expanded for 1-2 passages to revive growth conditions, then seeded at designated density for each experiment. In general, cells used in experimental studies were passages 16-18.

Method details

qPCR of shRNA inhibition on Kif20A expression and cellular markers

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Ambion, 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was generated with the SuperScriptTMIV First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, 18091050), Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using 2 x DyNAmo color flash SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, F-416). GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization. All data was normalized to the control samples.

Western blotting

PBT017 cells transduced with lentivirus particles of human shKIF20A726 and shScrambled RNAs were cultured for 3 days. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), lysed with 2×SDS loading buffer, and boiled for 5 min. Denatured proteins were resolved by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane for Western blotting detection by KIF20A (L13) antibody (1 : 500), p21 antibody (1 : 200) and horseradish-conjugated donkey anti-goat secondary antibody (1 : 2500) with chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific, Catalog #: 34095).

Flow cytometry-based cell cycle analyses of primary brain tumor cells

For cell cycle analyses, PBT017 cells transduced with either human KIF20A shRNA-726 or shRNA-Scramble lentiviral particles were gently dissociated in cold HBSS (Mediatech) with 5-mM EDTA. After centrifugation with 300 g at 4°C for 10 min, cells were washed twice with cold HBSS buffer. Subsequently, large cell/aggregates were removed with a cell strainer (40 μm). 70% ethanol was added drop by drop and the cells were fixed for overnight at 4°C, cells were then washed in cold PBS twice. For propidium iodide (PI) staining, cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL staining buffer (PBS + 10 μg/mL PI + 0.1% Triton X100 + 0.2 mg/mL DNAse-free RNAse A) and were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Cells were filtered before flow cytometry analysis. Measurements were made using a CyAn ADP instrument (DakoCytomation) in combination with summit software.

Immunocytochemistry and immunohistochemistry

In 2D cultures, PBT cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocked and permeabilized for 2 h at room temperature in blocking solution (1× PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% Donkey serum, 5% AffiniPure Fab fragment Donkey anti-Mouse IgG, 0.2% Sodium Azide), followed by incubation with primary antibody in blocking solution at 4°C overnight. The cells were rinsed three times with 1× PBS and incubated with secondary antibody in 1× PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 dye and rinsed three times with 1× PBS followed by once in water, then the cells were mounted with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech) for imaging with microscope.

In organoid culture system, organoids were fixed at day 33 for 30 minutes in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature and then washed three times (3×) for 15 min with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Tx-100) (TBS wash solution). After washing, samples were permeabilized and blocked for one hour in CAS blocking solution (Invitrogen 008120). Organoids were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in the same CAS blocking solution, then washed 3× (15 min each) with TBS wash solution with gentle agitation. Organoids were stained with neuronal antibody NeuN and TBR1, proliferation antibody Ki-67, and apoptosis antibody Caspase3. After incubating primary antibodies overnight, secondary antibodies were applied in CAS blocking solution and incubated overnight at 4°C. Organoids were then washed 3× with TBS wash solution and mounted on a glass slide with Prolong Glass (Invitrogen P36984). The slides were kept at room temperature in the dark for at least 48hr to allow for curing of the Prolong Glass.

For immunohistochemistry of brain tumor xenografts, brains were fixed with 4% formaldehyde by perfusion, coronally sectioned at 16μm, blocked and permeabilized for 2 h at room temperature in blocking solution (1× PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% Donkey serum, 5% AffiniPure Fab fragment Donkey anti-Mouse IgG, 0.2% Sodium Azide), followed by incubation with primary antibody (mouse anti-Ki67, goat anti-SOX2 and rabbit anti-NeuN) in blocking solution at 4°C overnight. The sections were rinsed three times with 1× PBS and incubated with secondary antibody in 1× PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 dye, rinsed three times with 1× PBS and once in water, then mounted with Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech) for imaging.

CRISPR-mediated KIF20A deletion

Six human KIF20A sgRNAs were designed with IDT custom AIt-RTM CRISPR-Cas9 guide RNA design tool: https://www.idtdna.com/site/order/designtool/index/CRISPR_PREDESIGN. Individual gRNAs, human spCas9, and mCherry reporter were assembled into lentiviral vectors. After lentiviral transduction of human cells, DNA cleavages were tested with AIt-RTM Genome Editing Detection Kit (Cat: 1075931). sgRNA1 and sgRNA6 were selected for human KIF20A gene editing in PBT017 cells. For sequencing validation, gRNA/Cas9-targeting regions were PCR-amplified, subcloned into the pBSK vector, and 12 clones per gRNA were sequenced to determine wild-type and mutant frequencies.

Generation of PBT cells with stably integrated Tet-on inducible shRNA expression system

PBT017 or PBT106 cells were transduced with lentivirus of Tet-on inducible human KIF20A shRNA-726 and GFP-Luciferase prepared from Tet-pLKO-shKIF20A726 plasmid and Hiv7CMVGFP-IRES-Luciferase plasmid, respectively. Following transduction, cells were selected for stable integration using Puromycin (1 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) and GFP fluorescence signal in neural stem cell growth medium (DMEM-Ham’s F12 50:50 Mixture with L-glutamine and 15 mM HEPES, 1 x B-27, 0.84 U/ml Heparin Sodium, 1 x Pen/Strep, 20 ng/ml recombinant human EGF and 20 ng/ml recombinant human FGF). Single PBT017 clones were isolated using glass cloning cylinders (Sigma-Aldrich) in 10cm plates. Multiple clones were expanded and evaluated for growth capacity and doxycycline (0.5 μg/ml) inducibility using growth curve assay, Western blotting, and a luciferase reporter assay system. Clone #2 was selected for further use. For PBT106 cells, multiple clones were pooled and expanded to establish a stable cell line for experimental use.

Growth assay of primary brain tumor cells and the stably integrated Tet-on inducible human KIF20A shRNA-726 tumor cells

PBT cells were seeded into multiple wells of 24 well plates (2x104 or 1x104 cells per well). After overnight culture, lentiviruses expressing scrambled control shRNA or shKIF20A726 were added to the cells. On consecutive days after lentiviral infection, cells in quadruplicate wells were then dissociated and counted using a hemocytometer.

ELDA assay

Limiting dilution assays of PBT017, PBT106 and PBT138 were performed as described (Hu and Smyth, 2009). PBT cells transduced with human shKIF20A726 or control sh-Scrambled lentiviruses were seeded into 96-well plates at 1, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 cells per well. The number of sphere-forming wells was counted under microscope in two weeks. The results were analyzed using the software at http://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda.

PBT cell organoid preparation

PBT cells (PBT017 and PBT106) carrying a Tet-inducible shRNA of KIF20A (shKIF20A726) were cultured to 80-90% confluence beginning at using Neurosphere media including DMEM:F12 (Corning 15-090-CV), GlutaMax (Gibco 35050-061), PenStrep (Gibco 15140122), B27 (Gibco 17504-044), and HEPES (Irvine Scientific 9319), supplemented with EGF (20 ng/mL; PeproTech AF-100-15), FGF (20 ng/mL; PeproTech 100-188), and puromycin (1μg/mL) at 37°C in a 20% O2, 5% CO2 environment. This medium was also used to culture the organoids. Our method for organoid preparation is modified from those described by Hubert et al. (2016) and Lancaster et al. (Lancaster and Knoblich, 2014). Each well of a 6-well plate was prepared by placing a 2.5 × 2.5 cm square of parafilm, sterilizing with 70% ethanol, and placing it uncovered in a culture hood under UV light for approximately 30 min for the ethanol to evaporate. At the same time, 468 μL of growth factor reduced Matrigel was placed in an Eppendorf tube to which 266,000 cells were added and gently mixed. Using a repeating pipettor (Eppendorf 4049-7134), six 13 μL-droplets/well, each containing 7,000 PBT cells, were dispensed onto the parafilm, and allowed to polymerize for 3 hours in the 37°C, 20% O2, 5% CO2 incubator. The parafilm inserts were then gently removed, and 4 mL of Neurosphere medium supplemented as above was added to each well, into which six PBT/Matrigel droplets were added using a sterile spatula. The culture plate was returned to the CO2 incubator. Culture medium was changed once per week for the first two weeks, and then twice or three times per week thereafter. At day 30, doxycycline (2μg/ml) was added to one of the plates for 48 hours. At day 32 EdU was added to PBT organoid plates for 24 hours and then stained using Click-iT EdU Imaging Kit.

Intracranial tumor transplantation and bioluminescence imaging

Animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Beckman Research Institute of the City of Hope. NSG mice (10-15 weeks old) were anesthetized with isoflurane and oxygen and placed under microscope. After exposing the skull with a scalpel, a cell suspension (1x105 cells in 3 μl PBS) of PBT017 cell clone #2 stably integrated with Tet-on inducible human KIF20A shRNA (shKIF20A726) and Luciferase gene was slowly injected into the right front lobe at a depth of ∼2.5 mm using a 10 μl Hamilton syringe with a 26 G needle using a plastic blocker to control the injection depth. After injection and incision closure, the transplanted mice were monitored weekly with luciferase-based bioluminescence imaging for tumor growth. When tumor growth was evident, the mice were randomly separated into two groups, control group and doxycycline-treatment group. For the treatment group, mice were first gavaged with 0.2 ml of doxycycline solution (10 mg/ml) for two consecutive days. Then the mice were placed on doxycycline-containing food (TestDiet, 625ppm; replenished every other day). Tumor growth was monitored biweekly by bioluminescence imaging. Survivals of mice after cell transplantation were recorded and analyzed.

Fluorescent image acquisition and processing

Fluorescent images of 2D cultured PBT cells were taken with Zeiss Observer II or confocal microscope of Zeiss LSM-900. Images of organoids were taken using Zeiss LSM-900 confocal microscopes, and processed and analyzed using Zen 3.6 (Zeiss). Sections of PBT cell transplanted brain tumor xenograft samples were imaged by Zeiss Axioscan 7 microscope with 20x magnification.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was performed and p values indicated in histograms of the figures. Log-rank test was performed for the Kaplan-Meier curve. Zen blue software and IMARIS software were used for quantification of 2D and 3D analyses, respectively. Asterisks indicate levels of statistical significance as follows: ∗ p < 0.05; ∗∗ p < 0.01; ∗∗∗ p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗ p < 0.0001; ns, not significant (p > 0.05).
