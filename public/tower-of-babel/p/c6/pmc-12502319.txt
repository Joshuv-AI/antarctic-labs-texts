Evaluating microbial regulation as a preventive strategy for radiation-related caries: A review
Authors: Hong-xiang Ou, Yu Chen, Da-li Zheng, You-guang Lu, Rui-huan Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiotherapy is a crucial treatment for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma but is associated with several complications, particularly the onset of radiation-related caries (RRC), which severely compromises patients' oral health and quality of life. Most studies have focused on the direct effects of radiation on host organs. Such as radiotherapy/Concurrent Chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) contributing to RRC primarily by inducing salivary gland hypofunction and directly damaging tooth structure. However, emerging evidence implicates additional mechanisms including dietary modifications and oral microbial dysbiosis in driving pathogenic microbial shifts characterized by cariogenic bacterial/fungal proliferation, thereby exacerbating RRC progression. In particular, changes in common cariogenic bacteria/fungi after radiotherapy remain poorly understood. Furthermore, clinical translation of microbial ecology principles into effective RRC prevention strategies remains underexplored. This review centers on radiation-induced oral microbiota alterations, critically analyzing documented microbial shifts characterized by marked proliferation of cariogenic taxa including Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus, Prevotella melaninogenica, Veillonella, and Actinomyces, alongside fungal overgrowth of Candida albicans. We propose a dual-focused intervention protocol: initiating probiotic supplementation at radiotherapy commencement to stabilize microbial ecology and preserve salivary function, combined with standardized oral care encompassing mechanical plaque removal, fluoride therapy, and natural anticariogenic agents. While mechanistically plausible, this paradigm requires rigorous validation through multicenter randomized controlled trials assessing ecological stability maintenance and caries incidence reduction.Keywords: Radiotherapy, Radiationrelated caries, Oral microorganisms, Probiotics, Fluoride

Backgroud

Head and neck cancer (HNC) ranks as the seventh most prevalent malignancy worldwide, constituting a significant global health burden [1]. Contemporary therapeutic paradigms emphasize multimodal approaches combining surgical intervention, radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy, which have demonstrated substantial improvements in oncological outcomes for HNC patients [2]. Radiotherapy remains essential for treating head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), especially when preserving organ function. However, it frequently causes radiation-related caries (RRC) – a severe complication marked by aggressive tooth decay that resists standard dental care [3, 4]. The pathognomonic presentation involves initial enamel hypomineralization and cervical fissuring, progressing through successive stages of enamel delamination, dentinal exposure, and ultimately culminating in catastrophic crown fracture with tooth loss [5].

Epidemiological surveillance reveals RRC incidence rates ranging from 29 to 37% in irradiated HNC populations, with clinical onset typically occurring 3–12 months post-radiotherapy [6, 7]. This condition profoundly compromises quality of life through masticatory dysfunction and dysphagia, resulting in progressive malnutrition and cancer-associated wasting. Additionally, poor dental health can hinder social interactions, potentially affecting patients' ability to form and maintain relationships and even contributing to psychological disorders [8–10].

Dental caries fundamentally arises from microbial dysbiosis (pathogenic imbalance in oral biofilms) and acidogenic metabolic processes [11, 12]. Unlike conventional caries, which progresses slowly and is generally predictable, RRC displays rapid onset and is more challenging to prevent and manage. Current investigations predominantly focus on radiotherapy-induced dental mineralization defects and salivary dysfunction, while systematically neglecting its ecological impacts on oral microbial communities and microenvironmental homeostasis. Emerging evidence suggests RRC pathogenesis may stem from radiotherapy-disrupted homeostasis between bacterial/fungal populations, pH stability, and salivary immunoregulation.

This review aims to explore the changes in oral microbiota following radiotherapy and provide a theoretical basis and novel therapeutic direction for RRC prevention and treatment from the perspective of modulating the oral microbiome(Fig. 1).

Current preventive paradigms should prioritize microbial equilibrium preservation via integrated strategies: mechanical biofilm disruption using optimized brushing protocols with soft-bristled brushes, fluoride-enhanced remineralization through high-concentration formulations, xerostomia mitigation via pH-responsive mucoadhesive hydrogels, and strict dietary control of fermentable carbohydrates. Accumulating evidence elucidates the anticariogenic efficacy of probiotics and natural products through dual mechanisms of competitive pathogen exclusion and microbial homeostasis restoration, underscoring the necessity for translational research to develop microbiota-targeted therapeutics that counteract radiotherapy-induced pathogenic microbial shifts

Epidemiology and clinical characteristics of RRC

While radiation-related caries (RRC) shares histopathological features with conventional dental caries [13], its divergent clinical trajectory manifests in distinct anatomical patterns (Table 1). Characteristically affecting caries-resistant zones—including cervical regions, incisal edges, cuspal tips, and smooth tooth surfaces—RRC demonstrates a predilection for multifocal, rapidly destructive lesions [7, 14]. The pathognomonic presentation involves circumferential cervical demineralization ("annular caries"), progressing through sequential enamel delamination, dentinal necrosis, chromatic alterations (brown discoloration with enamel translucency), and catastrophic structural failure [3, 15]. A rarer variant presents as generalized crown hyperpigmentation with concomitant occlusal/incisal surface attrition [16].

Etiology of RRC

The exactly etiology of radiation-related caries (RRC) remains undefined, though its classification as a caries variant suggests shared pathogenic mechanisms. Radiation therapy constitutes a diagnostic prerequisite for radiation-related caries (RRC), primarily through its dual assault on dental and salivary integrity. Therapeutically relevant doses induce ultrastructural enamel defects including microhardness reduction, prismatic disorganization, and enamel-dentin junction disruption. Atomic force microscopy reveals preferential degradation of interprismatic enamel matrices, generating cariogenic surface roughness through apatite bond destabilization [24–26]. Concurrent dentinal alterations manifest as tubule occlusion and stress fracture propagation, collectively compromising biomechanical resistance while creating bacterial adhesion niches [24, 26, 27].

Radiation therapy critically disrupts salivary gland homeostasis through dual quantitative and qualitative alterations: diminished salivary flow and biochemical composition shifts synergistically establish a cariogenic niche [28, 29]. This iatrogenic damage exhibits dose-dependency, with permanent dysfunction emerging above the 30 Gy threshold [18]. Preclinical models demonstrate dose-progressive glandular atrophy accompanied by microvascular compromise and hypometabolic states [30, 31]. Pathogenic mechanisms involve direct radiocytotoxicity targeting acinar cell membranes, inducing enzymatic extravasation and oncotic necrosis, compounded by radiation-activated apoptotic pathways that perpetuate secretory dysfunction [3, 32].

The cariogenic potential of dietary factors becomes amplified in radiation-related caries (RRC) through a triad of risk augmentation: increased carbohydrate consumption patterns in cancer patients, compromised oral hygiene practices, and diminished salivary pH modulation [33]. This synergistic triad drives accelerated enamel demineralization while promoting pathogenic microbial selection, particularly acidogenic biofilm formation through sustained acidic challenges.

Therapeutic radiation drives RRC development through three interconnected mechanisms: structural compromise of dental tissues, salivary dysfunction, and dietary shifts. Radiation-induced enamel defects and dentin microcracks provide niches for cariogenic biofilm proliferation, while impaired salivary flow disrupts pH buffering and microbial clearance. Concurrent cancer-related dietary changes increase carbohydrate retention, fueling acidogenic metabolism in dysbiotic biofilms. This triad synergistically accelerates mineral loss through persistent acidic challenges, culminating in the rapid, widespread tooth destruction characteristic of RRC.

Microbial factors leading to RRC

The oral cavity sustains a homeostatic balance through reciprocal host-microbe interactions, where commensal organisms actively stabilize microbial community architecture and metabolic equilibrium [34–36]. Imbalance in this symbiotic system, evidenced by pathogen dominance or keystone species reduction, progressively disrupts microbial control systems, elevating risks for varied oral disorders [22].

The oral cariogenic consortium predominantly comprises Streptococcus mutans, S. sobrinus, Lactobacilli, Prevotella, Veillonella, Actinomyces, and Candida species [22, 37–41]. Radiotherapy disrupts structure of teeth, salivary function, dietary patterns and microbial balance, synergistically amplifying acidogenic/aciduric taxa and biofilm acidification—establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of enamel demineralization that drives RRC progression (Fig. 4). Streptococcus

Streptococcus spp. serve as pioneer colonizers in oral ecosystems, with S. mutans emerging as a principal cariogenic pathogen through its acidogenic-aciduric dual pathogenicity [63]. This facultative anaerobe employs dual adhesion modalities: sucrose-independent pellicle binding and sucrose-activated glucan-mediated attachment [22]. Its cariogenic arsenal includes four glucan-binding proteins (Gbps) orchestrating enamel adherence and biofilm maturation, complemented by glycosyltransferase (Gtf)-catalyzed glucan synthesis that enhances biofilm structural integrity and antimicrobial resistance [64, 65].

Numerous studies have indicated an increase in Streptococcus spp. after radiation, with significant rises in S. mutans abundance and cariogenic virulence. Saliva analyses demonstrate that heavy ion irradiation reduces oral microbial diversity while elevating Streptococcus abundance, with this shift exhibiting dose-dependent correlation [43, 66]. Longitudinal analyses of saliva and dental plaque throughout radiotherapy phases reveal significant increases in S. mutans colonization levels [42]. Radiation selectively enhances the ratio of S. mutans to Streptococcus sanguinis in saliva-derived biofilms via transcriptional upregulation of gtfC and gtfD [66]. Furthermore, therapeutic radiation doses drive virulence-modulating genomic mutations in S. mutans populations [67].

Conversely, Serial surveillance demonstrates diminished Streptococcus colonization in oropharyngeal rinses during late-phase radiotherapy [50], persisting across saliva, dental plaque, tongue dorsum, and buccal mucosa at 24-month follow-up [46, 47]. Parallel reductions in S. mutans bioburden occur in oral lavage fluids and subgingival biofilm ecosystems post-irradiation [49]. Additionally, S. sobrinus demonstrates transient salivary proliferation at 6-week follow-up, whereas tongue-associated S. salivarius populations show sustained depletion through 36-month post-radiotherapy intervals [44, 48].

Lactobacillus

Lactobacillus spp. are recognized as the second principal cariogenic microorganisms within the oral microbial ecosystem [68]. These gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacteria demonstrate acidogenic capacity and intrinsic acid-adaptive tolerance mechanisms [69, 70],while encoding glucosyltransferases (Gtfs) for extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) biosynthesis [71, 72]. Elevated detection frequencies of Lactobacillus show positive correlations with carious lesion progression, demonstrating increased prevalence within advanced dentin lesions [73, 74]. Common cariogenic species include Lactobacillus fermentum, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus gasseri, Lactobacillus casei/paracasei, Lactobacillus salivarius, and Lactobacillus plantarum [75].

Current evidence demonstrates significant elevation of Lactobacillus proliferation in HNC patients undergoing radiotherapy, persisting post-treatment with selective enrichment of hyperacidogenic strains [43, 46–48, 53]. Radiation-exposed cohorts exhibit amplified Lactobacillus colonization compared to controls, a phenomenon mechanistically linked to impaired salivary pH regulation.

Prevotella

Prevotella spp., gram-negative anaerobic bacteria [76], constitute prevalent cariogenic microbiota with Prevotella melaninogenica emerging as the predominant cariogenic variant [77–81]. This species demonstrates upregulated collagenase expression associated with dentin caries pathogenesis [75, 80, 82].

Convergent findings indicate radiotherapy-associated Prevotella proliferation, particularly accentuated in P. melaninogenica populations [43, 45]. Temporal analyses of saliva, tongue dorsum, and dental plaque microbiomes demonstrate progressive Prevotella enrichment throughout and following radiotherapy, reaching maximal colonization densities at six-month post-radiotherapy intervals [43, 45]. Oral rinse profiling further identifies Prevotella dominance during advanced concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) phases [50], while comparative analyses reveal approximately 14-fold elevated P. melaninogenica abundance in radiation-related caries specimens versus post-radiotherapy caries-free controls [54].

Contradictory findings emerge in select investigations, with Hou et al. documenting dose-dependent Prevotella reduction correlating with escalating radiation doses [55]. Notably, longitudinal analysis of salivary and plaque microbiomes demonstrated sustained Prevotella depletion persisting through two-year post-radiotherapy monitoring [46].

Veillonella

Veillonella spp. are strictly anaerobic, gram-negative cocci [83] that metabolize short-chain organic acids, primarily lactic acid, rather than sugars [84]. They are prevalent in early childhood caries (ECC)[81, 85]and co-aggregate with Streptococcus spp. [86]. Significant positive correlations exist between S. mutans and Veillonella in carious lesions, with enhanced cariogenic virulence in dual-species biofilms [87].

Radiotherapy induces marked Veillonella proliferation, with elevated colonization densities detected in oral mucosal swabs and plaque microbiomes throughout and following treatment, reaching maximal levels at one-month post-radiotherapy intervals [45, 56, 57].

Actinomyces

Actinomyces spp. are non-acidophilic, gram-positive bacilli and facultative anaerobes that play a pivotal role in dental biofilm formation and pathogenesis [88]. These bacteria colonize the cervical regions of teeth, facilitated by mycorrhizal hair types 1 and 2, which enhance adhesion and interbacterial interactions [89, 90]. Additionally, highly expressed collagen-binding proteins promote Actinomyces colonization by binding to hyphae [91]. Actinomyces metabolize carbohydrates into organic acids and store intracellular polysaccharides, contributing to their cariogenic potential [92].

Accumulating experimental data substantiate post-radiotherapy Actinomyces proliferation within the oral microenvironment, as evidenced through 16S rRNA sequencing of Gracey curette-collected buccal plaque specimens from maxillary first molars [45, 57]. Complementary mucosal microbiome analyses further confirm this radiation-induced Actinomyces dominance, consistent with Zhu et al.'s high-throughput sequencing results [59].

Candida

Candida albicans, a polymorphic yeast implicated in caries pathogenesis [93], interacts with S. mutans through specific cell wall binding sites for Gtf enzymes [94, 95], facilitating indirect tooth surface adhesion. This fungal species enhances cariogenicity by upregulating S. mutans gtfB expression, thereby boosting extracellular polysaccharide production [96]. Demonstrating potent acidogenic capacity, C. albicans primarily generates pH-lowering pyruvate, sustaining viability under extreme acidity (pH 3–4) in carbohydrate-rich environments [97, 98]. Additionally, C. albicans produces proteolytic enzymes that degrade dentin collagen and facilitate pathogen penetration through dentinal tubules, accelerating caries progression [99].

Radiotherapy significantly increases the abundance of fungi, particularly Candida spp. and C. albicans, in the oral cavity [49]. Temporal analyses reveal progressive C. albicans colonization in HNC patients, reaching clinical significance at six-month post-radiotherapy assessments [61]. Compared to controls with normal salivary secretion, patients with reduced salivary flow six months post-treatment exhibit higher quantities and proportions of C. albicans [60]. Similarly, the abundance of C. albicans in supragingival plaque rises three years after radiotherapy [48]. These findings suggest that radiotherapy enhances the colonization of Candida spp., potentially promoting RRC progression through interactions with S. mutans.

Other microorganisms associated with radiotherapy

The abundance of bacterial species significantly decreases during early radiotherapy (weeks 1–3) but stabilizes in later stages (weeks 5–7) [50]. Initially, thick-walled phyla, such as Firmicutes, gradually increase and become dominant by week three [43, 50]. At the genus level, certain gram-negative bacteria, including Neisseria, Capnocytophaga, and Leptotrichia, decline during mid-to-late radiotherapy, while Treponema and Gemella increase [43, 50]. Protective bacterial species, such as Actinomyces defectiva and Leuconostoc mesenteroides, also decrease in late stages [54, 66]. Leuconostoc mesenteroides is known for its antagonistic activity against S. mutans, a characteristic typical of oral probiotics [61]. Overall, radiotherapy increases caries-associated pathogens while reducing their antagonistic counterparts, shifting the oral microbiota towards a cariogenic profile that promotes RRC.

Intriguingly, new microbial genera, such as Acinetobacter, Pasteurella, Pediococcus, and Leuconostoc, emerge in the oral cavity after radiotherapy [100]. Conversely, the abundance of Fusobacterium decreases in the molar region and on the tongue two to three years post-treatment [47, 48]. Additionally, other microbial species, including Staphylococcus, Bacillus, Enterococcus, Pedicoccus, Klebsiella, Staphylococcus kloosi, and Staphylococcus hominis, increase in prevalence following radiotherapy [57, 100]. The oral cavity of patients with mucositis following radiotherapy also displays a higher abundance of Gram-negative bacteria, such as Tannerella, Sneathia, Mycoplasma, Capnocytophaga, Bacteroidales G2, Porphyromonas, and Eikenella [101].

Radiotherapy-induced dysregulation of the oral microbiome, characterized by selective pathobiont expansion and commensal microbiota depletion, demands targeted microbial modulation strategies to restore ecological balance.

Prevention of RRC by modulation of the oral microbiota

Given the central role of oral microbiota in caries pathogenesis, understanding its perturbation post-radiotherapy may open avenues for targeted interventions. Mechanical plaque removal persists as the cornerstone preventive strategy against dental caries, yet demonstrates incomplete eradication efficacy against resilient biofilm communities. Therefore, alternative strategies for controlling oral bacteria are essential. In RRC patients, enamel destruction, reduced salivary secretion, dietary changes, and the presence of cariogenic bacteria collectively complicate the management of oral microbiota. Current antimicrobial drugs lack specificity for biofilm structures and functions, resulting in limited clinical efficacy for caries prevention [37, 102]. Thus, it is crucial to identify substances or methods that specifically target oral microbiota, modulate the biofilm matrix, alter the local pH environment, or eliminate acid-producing cariogenic bacteria to prevent RRC [11].

Probiotics and prebiotics

In October 2013, the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) defined probiotics as "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host" [103]. In December 2016, ISAPP updated the definition of prebiotics to "a substrate that confers health benefits through selective utilization by host microorganisms" [86]. Probiotics utilize prebiotics to produce bioactive metabolites (Fig. 5). The mechanisms of caries prevention by probiotics include inhibition of cariogenic bacteria, synthesis of antimicrobial peptides and enzymes, competitive adhesion and colonization, and modulation of the immune system [104]. When incorporated into oral rinses, lozenges, or chewing gum formulations, probiotics demonstrate antagonistic activity against pathogenic bacteria, influence metabolic pathways, and induce immunomodulatory effects [105], exhibiting therapeutic properties that position them as viable antibiotic alternatives [106].

Schematic diagram of Preventive management of RRC. Three categories of substances have been demonstrated potential in maintaining oral microbial equilibrium for radiation-related caries (RRC) prevention. Probiotics utilize prebiotics to synthesize bioactive metabolites such as antimicrobial peptides and enzymes, while competitively inhibiting pathogenic bacterial adhesion and modulating immune responses. Concurrently, selected natural compounds derived from botanical and insect sources exhibit targeted suppression of cariogenic microorganisms. Additionally, chemical agent exemplified by fluoride exhibit anticaries properties through dual mechanisms involving enzymatic activity suppression and bacterial membrane permeability enhancement, achieving both bacteriostatic and bactericidal outcomes

By metabolizing arginine via the arginine deiminase system (ADS), probiotics generate ammonia to neutralize acidic byproducts from cariogenic bacteria such as S. mutans, effectively maintaining pH homeostasis in oral biofilms [107]. This pH-stabilizing effect is leveraged in clinical formulations like 8% arginine toothpaste, which reduced S. mutans colonization around orthodontic brackets by 40% [108], and 4% L-arginine-enhanced glass ionomer cement (GIC) that combats secondary caries without compromising mechanical integrity [109]. Arginine-fluoride toothpaste further amplifies these benefits, enhancing microbial diversity at both caries-active and healthy sites in long-term trials [110]. Beyond pH regulation, probiotics secrete antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) such as GH12, which disrupt S. mutans virulence by dual targeting: inhibiting glucosyltransferase-mediated insoluble glucan synthesis and suppressing lactate dehydrogenase activity to limit acid production [111–113]. This strain has been shown to lower the incidence of dental caries and mitigate lesion severity in rat models while maintaining balanced oral microbiota [114]. Notably, specific probiotic formulations including Streptococcus salivarius BLIS K12 and M18 exhibit innate colonization capacity within oral ecological niches, biosynthesizing both bacteriocins and bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances that exert targeted antimicrobial activity against cariogenic pathogens [115]. Innovative biotechnological approaches exemplified by precision-targeted antimicrobial peptides are engineered to differentially neutralize cariogenic pathogens while maintaining microbial homeostasis in the oral ecosystem [111, 116]. These actions are complemented by enzymatic biofilm disruption through α−1,3 glucanase and α−1,6 glucanase, which degrade EPS and synergize with proteases or DNases to achieve over 70% biofilm reduction in vitro [117–120]. These enzymes can be incorporated into oral care products (e.g., mouthwash, toothpaste) or administered internally to prevent caries [121]. Clinical applications of enzyme-producing strains like S. salivarius M18—which neutralizes acidity via urease and reduces plaque polysaccharides with glucoamylase—have lowered caries incidence by 35% in pediatric populations [122]. Multi-enzyme therapy is a promising approach for controlling dental biofilms, warranting further research [123].

Notably, probiotics remodel the oral microbiota by suppressing pathogens while fostering beneficial taxa. Lactobacilli inhibit Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans oral colonization through dual mechanisms: impairing adhesive interactions with gingival epithelial cells and modulating host-cell responses [124]. Lactococcin-producing Lactobacillus strains reduce cariogenic flora while restoring microbial diversity [125], and specific combinations like Bifidobacterium animalis ZK-77 with LigiLactobacillus salivarius ZK-88 elevate Firmicutes abundance while diminishing S. mutans, Veillonella, and acidogenic Lactobacillus [126]. Lacticaseibacillus paracasei GMNL-143, when incorporated into toothpaste, physically coaggregates with cariogenic bacteria to limit their colonization [127], while Lactiplantibacillus plantarum CCFM8724 enriches beneficial genera like Granulicatella and Gemella while reducing Proteobacteria-associated pathogens [128]. These microbial shifts suggest probiotics could serve as viable alternatives to chlorhexidine, particularly in high-risk groups such as mechanically ventilated patients [129]. Competitive exclusion further augments these effects, as exemplified by LimosiLactobacillus brevis KCCM 202399, which reduces S. mutans adhesion by decreasing bacterial surface hydrophobicity by 45% and EPS production by 30% [130]. Notably, Akkermansia muciniphila—a keystone gut-derived probiotic symbiont—demonstrates targeted suppression of Fusobacterium nucleatum pathogenicity; this interspecies antagonism correlates with measurable amelioration of periodontal inflammation [131].

Concurrently, probiotics enhance host-derived defenses against caries. Daily consumption of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei SD1 elevates salivary human neutrophil peptides (HNP 1–3) by twofold, correlating with reduced S. mutans levels and slowed caries progression [132]. A six-month trial with SD1-fortified milk similarly boosted secretory IgA (sIgA) by 50%, underscoring its dual role in pathogen suppression and mucosal immunity reinforcement [133].

Collectively, these multifaceted mechanisms spanning direct antimicrobial activity to ecological and immunomodulatory regulation establish probiotics as a valuable complement to established caries prevention protocols.

Natural products

Natural products and their bioactive constituents exhibit significant antimicrobial properties through diverse mechanisms. Phytochemical analyses reveal that most antimicrobial substances are secondary metabolites with specific physiological functions [134]. Experimental and clinical validation confirms the capacity of botanical compounds to suppress both cariogenic bacterial proliferation and virulence expression. For example, raffinose inhibits glucan formation by binding to S. mutans GtfC [135], while pepper leaf extract induce structural disintegration of cariogenic biofilms [136]. Cranberry polyphenols inhibit cariogenic properties without bactericidal effects, suggesting potential for caries prevention and maintaining microbial homeostasis [137]. Other natural compounds, such as stevia rebaudiana bertoni, xanthorrhizol, grape seed extract, tea polyphenols, and xylitol, also exhibit unique caries-preventive properties [138–140].

In addition to plant-derived products, insect-derived substances like honey exhibit caries-preventive properties. Key antimicrobial factors in honey include low pH, high osmolality, hydrogen peroxide, gluconic acid, and antimicrobial peptides [141]. Recent studies highlight that hydrogen peroxide, enzymatically produced by glucose oxidase in bees, plays a major role in antimicrobial activity [142]. Honey combined with cranberry extract or Manuka honey shows stronger antimicrobial effects than commercial mouthwashes [143]. Similarly, honey, myrtle, and pomegranate extracts inhibit S. mutans through synergistic effects [144].

Chemical agent

Fluoride remains central to caries prevention through topical applications (e.g., mouthwash, toothpaste) and systemic methods (e.g., water fluoridation). Fluoride replaces hydroxyl groups in hydroxyapatite to form fluorapatite, which is more resistant to acid dissolution than other tooth minerals (carbonate apatite and hydroxyapatite). Additionally, fluoride enhances remineralization by promoting fluorapatite crystal growth on partially demineralized subsurface lesions [145] Low fluoride concentrations inhibit biofilm enzymes, while high concentrations increase cell membrane permeability by acting as proton carriers [146]. These mechanisms enhance tooth acid resistance and antimicrobial activity against cariogenic bacteria. Fluoride applications have been shown to restore dentin hardness progressively after radiotherapy [148]. However, broad-spectrum bacteriostatic agents like chlorhexidine can disrupt oral biofilm composition and metabolic activity, potentially perturbing the commensal microbiome [148].

Recommendations for prevention of RRC

Synthesizing current evidence on oral microbiota modulation, we recommend the following multidisciplinary strategy for RRC prevention in head and neck cancer patients.

Mechanically remove the plaque

Mechanical plaque removal remains fundamental for RRC prevention. Daily oral hygiene protocols should incorporate twice-daily brushing with small-headed, soft-to-medium bristle toothbrushes, complemented by interdental cleaning. For patients with manual dexterity limitations, ergonomically modified brush handles demonstrate clinical efficacy in optimizing plaque removal [148].

Fluoride application

Fluoride demonstrates evidence-based efficacy in caries prevention throughout the lifespan [148], with robust clinical evidence validating fluoride-containing toothpaste as a cornerstone intervention [148]. Synergistic use of fluoride toothpaste and mouthwash promotes superior enamel fluoridation through ionic exchange mechanisms [148]. Furthermore, high-concentration fluoride formulations demonstrate enhanced remineralization capacity for radiation-compromised superficial enamel, providing adjuvant protection against post-radiotherapy caries development [148].

Alleviates xerostomia

Irreversible salivary gland dysfunction, characterized by diminished or thickened saliva secretion, occurs when radiation doses are accumulated [5], thereby weakening saliva's buffering capacity against acidic environments.

Pilocarpine, a non-selective cholinergic agonist, activates salivary gland muscarinic receptors to mitigate xerostomia, though its use is associated with systemic side effects [148]. While this agent demonstrates efficacy in enhancing salivary flow and alleviating xerostomia, its caries-preventive potential has not been conclusively established [148].

Acupuncture, with incompletely understood mechanisms, demonstrates therapeutic benefits for xerostomia management even in pilocarpine-resistant cases [148]. Its minimally invasive nature and low adverse reaction rate make it a potential treatment for radiation-induced xerostomia [148].

For patients with compromised salivary secretion, gustatory or masticatory stimulants, lubricants, and artificial saliva substitutes may alleviate symptoms, though these conventional interventions exhibit transient therapeutic effects [148]. While microgel-enhanced aqueous lubricants demonstrate potential for sustained efficacy when paired with acidic taste stimulation, showing both prolonged symptom relief and secretory enhancement [148, 148]. Additionally, fibrin hydrogels fortified with FGF-7/10 and laminin-1 peptides have demonstrated regenerative potential in murine salivary gland models, supporting the need for translational clinical investigations [148].

Limit sugar intake

Post-radiotherapy patients frequently exhibit cariogenic dietary patterns characterized by preferential consumption of soft, sucrose-dense foods, attributable to xerostomia-related masticatory limitations and dysregulated sweet taste perception. This pathological dietary shift significantly elevates caries risk by creating prolonged acidic challenges to radiation-compromised dentition [148, 148]. Evidence suggests that limiting free sugar intake to below 10% of total energy reduces caries incidence, with potential benefits for further reduction to below 5% under certain conditions [148, 148]. These recommendations are included in the WHO's guidelines on sugar intake for adults and children [148].

Probiotics and prebiotics

Probiotics demonstrate therapeutic potential in oncology by regulating gut microbiota to boost immune function and alleviate cancer treatment-related side effects, aiming to optimize clinical efficacy and potentiate antitumor responses [168]. Emerging evidence supports the therapeutic potential of probiotic-mediated oral microbiota modulation in radiation-induced oral mucositis (ROM). Clinical studies have documented a characteristic enrichment of the Prevotella genera in the oral microbiota of ROM patients [148, 168]. Notably, probiotic interventions demonstrate microbial restorative capacity by ameliorating these dysbiotic shifts through selective suppression of pathogenic genera such as Prevotella and Candida, while simultaneously preserving commensal Streptococcus populations critical for mucosal homeostasis [148, 168]. Clinical evidence further indicates that probiotic supplementation mitigates oral mucositis severity by dual mechanisms: systemic immune potentiation via gut microbiota modulation and direct suppression of oral pathobionts [148, 168]. Additionally, probiotics additionally stimulate salivary secretion [148], suggesting these combined actions may provide preventive benefits against radiation-related caries (RRC). Established evidence of probiotic efficacy in gastrointestinal infections [168], combined with recent findings demonstrating their ability to improve periodontal indicators such as bleeding on probing and pocket depth reduction [116, 148, 148, 168, 179], supports analogous therapeutic potential for mitigating radiation-related caries (RRC) through shared microbial pathogenesis mechanisms. This approach focuses on stabilizing oral microbiota during the critical pretreatment phase, aiming to disrupt the pathogenic progression from dysbiosis initiation to cariogenic activity. Radiotherapy-induced oral microbiota alterations commence early in treatment, preceding clinical RRC manifestation [45, 50]. Orthodontic trials validating probiotics' capacity to prevent dysbiosis provide rationale for pre-radiation probiotic administration as a preventive strategy against RRC development [148]. While current evidence suggests probiotic supplementation may preserve microbial diversity and maintain ecological equilibrium to reduce RRC risk, these proposed mechanisms require rigorous verification through controlled clinical trials incorporating standardized outcome measures.

Natural products

Plant-derived bioactive constituents demonstrate targeted antimicrobial efficacy through multiple mechanisms. Cranberry polyphenols selectively suppress cariogenic virulence factors while preserving commensal microbiota integrity, suggesting ecological modulation potential for dysbiosis management [137]. Honey, an insect-derived substance, has revealed remarkable anti-caries properties through synergistic effects with cranberry extract [142, 143, 179]. While natural products appear innocuous due to their edibility, further research is needed to evaluate their biosafety and impact on the human microbiota.

Conclusions and future considerations

In addition to the direct damage to dental hard tissues, salivary gland injury, and dietary changes in patients, radiotherapy is known to induce significant alterations in the composition and abundance of microorganisms within the oral mucosa, saliva, and dental plaque. Consequently, RRC remains difficult to control and, in severe cases, may result in the complete destruction of otherwise healthy dentition. This review article primarily focuses on the changes in oral microbiota following radiotherapy, with observed increases in the abundance of cariogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus, Prevotella melaninogenica, Veillonella, and Actinomyces, as well as Candida albicans. Therefore, we speculate that managing the dysbiosis and cariogenic alterations in the oral microbiota following radiotherapy could serve as a preventive strategy against RRC. We propose initiating probiotic supplementation concurrent with radiotherapy initiation to simultaneously prevent oral microbial imbalance and stimulate salivary secretion, thereby alleviating radiation-induced salivary gland impairment. This regimen should be implemented alongside a comprehensive oral care protocol including meticulous mechanical plaque removal (brushing/flossing), topical fluoride therapy, targeted xerostomia intervention when clinically warranted, and supplementary application of natural caries-inhibitory compounds. These coordinated interventions collectively target maintenance of oral microbiome homeostasis and prevention of RRC. Future efforts should focus on identifying substances or methods with specific efficacy against these cariogenic bacteria and targeting biofilm matrices to control cariogenic microorganisms effectively.
