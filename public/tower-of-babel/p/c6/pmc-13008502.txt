Ca2+ signalling in cardiac muscle: the importance of balances
Authors: Ernst Niggli, Ana M Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this narrative review, an example of a cardiac arrhythmia with a mechanistically appealing molecular pathomechanism is highlighted. This example will be used to delineate how recent basic science findings, which are summarized, can be used to obtain a deeper understanding of pathological behaviour from the molecular to the cellular level. The condition in question is the highly lethal catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia resulting from a point mutation of the cardiac ryanodine receptor. For this deep dive, the RyR2R420Q phenotype will be discussed in detail. Interestingly, these findings and the conclusions which could be drawn from them were very much unexpected but could be relevant for pharmacological treatments. To make the transition from the molecular and cellular findings to the patient will require the translation across several layers of complexity. Ultimately, such detailed understanding will lead to improved therapies tailored to each individual case and the specific RyR2 mutation carried by a particular family, in the framework of precision medicine.Keywords: Cardiac electrophysiology, Cellular electrophysiology, Calcium signalling, Ion channels, Ryanodine receptors, Arrhythmias, Sudden cardiac death, Mechanisms

Introduction

This short and narrative review will highlight how the present understanding of cardiac Ca2+ signalling can help to decipher the inner workings of specific pathomechanisms. Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is highlighted as an example of a cardiac disorder, which has gained much recent attention because of its mechanistic appeal. It will be shown how the knowledge and understanding gained from basic research, and the methods and techniques developed for such studies, can help to arrive at an improved recognition of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying a pathological condition. Ultimately, this gives hope that in the future, the therapeutic strategies to approach this and similar syndromes may be improved based on a profound understanding of its mechanistic underpinnings. This would allow a more individually tailored therapeutic approach in the framework of precision medicine.

Milestones of Ca2+ signalling research in cardiac muscle

To delineate a brief historical background covering the evolution of our present concepts of cardiac cellular electrophysiology and Ca2+ signalling, it is worthwhile to remember a few decisive milestones in the history of the related basic research. The inspiring groundwork for the entire research field was laid when Edouard Coraboeuf and Silvio Weidmann recorded the first action potentials from cardiac muscle using glass microelectrodes in 1949,1 while working in the laboratory of Alan Hodgkin in Oxford. According to anecdotal evidence this happened on 16 July 1949, when they received a dog heart that had been used before to teach students.2 It then took several years until the ‘slow inward current’ (Isi) was identified by Harald Reuter coincidentally with Oger Rougier and attributed to the influx of Ca2+ into the cardiac muscle cells, to initiate contraction.3,4 The Ca2+ signal elicited by the Ca2+ inward current is subsequently amplified by Ca2+-induced Ca2+ release (CICR) via SR Ca2+ release channels (also known as ryanodine receptors, RyRs, or RyR2s in cardiac tissue). This mechanism was initially recognized in 1970 in skeletal muscle by Makoto Endo et al., concurrently with Ford and Podolsky.5,6 The crucial importance of CICR in cardiac muscle was later confirmed by Alexandre and Françoise Fabiato in the seventies and eighties.7,8 For these studies, an ingenious experimental superfusion system was used to examine isolated single cardiomyocytes. By that time, CICR was also noted to be able to produce erratic signals, such as Ca2+ oscillations and Ca2+ waves, which could potentially trigger cardiac arrhythmias. In support of these observations, computer models of CICR confirmed a tendency for an oscillatory behaviour, resulting from the strong positive feed-back inherent in this amplification system.9 Nonetheless, the strong amplification of Ca2+ influx signals that could be observed experimentally in normal cardiac muscle seemed to contradict the behaviour of such unstable computer models; hence, this discrepancy was called the ‘cardiac Ca2+ signalling paradox’. This oddity was subsequently scrutinized and finally resolved by exploring a range of mechanistically distinct regenerative designs for such Ca2+ signals (and corresponding computer models). These models were generally referred to as ‘local control theories’. An important milestone was achieved by Michael Stern by replacing the common-pool arrangement of CICR (i.e. a single cytosolic Ca2+ pool) with a localized ‘cluster bomb’ design in such models,9 whereby the Ca2+ release signals remained subcellularly highly confined near each SR Ca2+ release terminal, an anatomical ultrastructure now referred to as ‘couplon’. In 1993, Cheng et al. reported the observation of a biological equivalent of the mathematical ‘cluster bomb’, the Ca2+ spark.10 These elementary and spatially limited Ca2+ signalling events could exhibit a very high Ca2+ signal amplification locally without long-range regenerativity and oscillations. This is the case, because they are normally shielded from neighbouring Ca2+ release terminals by steep concentration gradients due to cytosolic buffering limiting Ca2+ diffusion. With such an extreme local control system for CICR, the cardiac Ca2+ transient (and the cardiac muscle force) can be conceptualized as a system that is regulated by a process of recruitment, whereby fewer or more Ca2+ sparks will be activated, depending on the number of opening sarcolemmal L-type Ca2+ channels. Ca2+ influx via open channels triggers the RyRs on the SR facing them across the dyadic cleft (see Figure 1).

Given the intrinsic simplicity of such a system with near digital (i.e. all-or-none) elementary signalling events combined with recruitment, the question emerged whether cardiac EC coupling is really that simple or whether there are more sophisticated mechanisms regulating the elementary Ca2+ signalling events, the sparks, themselves? Even from early studies it was known that the activity of the RyR2 is regulated by luminal calcium,12 and that depending on the SR Ca2+ load, spatiotemporal features of the Ca2+ sparks could change. For instance, it was recognized that during SR Ca2+ overload the local control process shielding the individual spark sites from neighbours could be overturned. Such conditions could lead to intracellular Ca2+ waves initiated by store-overload-induced Ca2+ release (SOICR)13 and subsequently be driven by CICR along the cardiomyocytes.14

The search for mechanisms regulating the cardiac RyR2 function through posttranslational protein modifications eventually resulted in the discovery of the modulation by phosphorylation by Valdivia et al in 1995,15 and its role in aggravating several abnormal cardiac conditions, including heart failure and arrhythmias by Marx et al. in 2000.16 This landmark publication has inspired an entire research field, not only because of its mechanistic fascination but also because of its potential clinical significance. Since then, a large number of reports exploring RyR2 phosphorylation have appeared from many different research laboratories. Based on numerous and partly quite controversial studies it has been recognized that the functional ramifications of RyR phosphorylation depended on the particular protein kinase involved (PKA, CaMKII, and/or SPEG) and on the specific serine(s) on the RyR2 (e.g. S2808, S2814, S28030, S2367) that becomes phosphorylated.17–19 However, the intricate details of the various phosphorylation sites on the channel and the role of the involved protein kinases and phosphatases are still not yet fully understood. Also, the consequences of altered Ca2+ flux balances across the sarcolemma and across the SR membrane resulting from changes of RyR and SERCA function are not yet clarified.20 Summarizing the multitude of still open questions and related findings is beyond the scope of this review, but some of the issues relevant for the example of CPVT discussed in this analysis will be detailed further below (for reviews see refs11,21)

Parameters to estimate cellular Ca2+ arrhythmogenicity experimentally

Before diving into the detailed discussion of possible molecular and cellular mechanisms for Ca2+-induced arrhythmogenic events, it is worthwhile to briefly look at the experimental techniques and tools used to look at these potentially abnormal signals and the parameters analysed to quantify their severity. For many years, the method of choice to record and analyse Ca2+ signals on the level of single cardiac myocytes has been laser-scanning confocal microscopy, due to its superior spatial and flexible temporal resolution. Various fluorescent Ca2+ indicators have been applied to image these subcellular Ca2+ signals. Several parameters of cellular Ca2+ signalling considered to be related to abnormal electrical activity have found widespread use to assess the predisposition for arrhythmias.

Frequency of spontaneous Ca2+ sparks

One parameter that can be assessed quite easily is the frequency of spontaneous Ca2+ sparks, i.e. sparks during diastole. While a few spontaneous Ca2+ sparks are usually present, an elevated Ca2+ spark frequency is considered to result from a higher RyR open probability. This may result from a variety of causes, such as, for example, a change in the diastolic cytosolic Ca2+ concentration, but also other cytosolic components such as Mg2+ concentration, ATP/ADP levels, or an altered interaction of the RyRs with other proteins. Intrinsically the RyR channel can change its activity or Ca2+ sensitivity due to posttranslational modifications or a RyR mutation.

Frequency of Ca2+ waves

Spontaneous Ca2+ release can also manifest itself as Ca2+ waves, which are essentially chain reactions of a series of Ca2+ sparks propagating in a saltatoric fashion as a fire and diffuse event along or across the cell, driven by CICR. Such a subcellular spreading occurs when the local control mechanisms, which normally confine spontaneous sparks, are impaired or lost. Under these conditions, a spontaneous spark can trigger neighbouring Ca2+ release sites, thereby initiating a wave. While isolated Ca2+ sparks do not affect the membrane potential measurably, this may occur during a Ca2+ wave (see below).

Delay to first Ca2+ wave

Another powerful and easily quantifiable criterion for Ca2+-dependent arrhythmogenicity is the delay to the first Ca2+ wave, immediately following a conditioning stimulation protocol consisting of a train of Ca2+ transients. The delay occurs, because the SR needs to refill and reach a certain SR Ca2+ load, before a wave can occur. The more sensitive the CICR mechanism is, the shorter is this delay. A short delay therefore indicates a high readiness to generate Ca2+ waves, while normal myocytes typically do not generate Ca2+ waves at all after a train of stimulation.

Ca2+ spark recovery analysis

While counting the spontaneous Ca2+ sparks is a convenient way to assess the RyR activity, this parameter is under the influence of many confounding factors and not very specific. An extension of this approach has been developed whereby repetitive Ca2+ sparks are recorded from a single Ca2+ release site.22 This is achieved by applying a very low concentration of ryanodine, in the vicinity of 50 nM. Under such a regime, a few sites exhibiting repetitive Ca2+ spark activity develop in every myocyte. These repetitive sparks are thought to be entrained by a single RyR2 channel that has ryanodine bound and may stay open in a long-lasting sub-conductance state. The repetitive nature of this Ca2+ spark activity allows detailed analysis of the temporal evolution of the spark trigger probability (after termination of a preceding spark). In addition, the time course of Ca2+ refilling of this specific couplon is manifest as the gradual restoration of the spark amplitude with time.

CPVT: a mechanistically intriguing Ca2+ signalling disease

CPVT is a serious inheritable condition that can lead to tachycardias with syncope or even sudden cardiac death, often manifesting itself in young adults.23,24 In these patients, arrhythmia episodes are triggered by emotional or physical stress. So far, in excess of 500 mutations of the RyR2 have been identified, and more than 150 are known to cause CPVT type 1. Mutations of the SR Ca2+ buffering protein calsequestrin can underlie CPVT type 2.25 Patients harbouring a CPVT mutation are usually treated with β-adrenergic receptor (β-AR) blockers, such as propranolol, sometimes supplemented by flecainide. Only in extraordinary cases, implantable cardioverter–defibrillator devices or left cardiac sympathetic denervation surgery are needed.26 To examine the pathophysiology of this condition, a number of transgenic mouse models have been engineered. They allow experimental studies on all levels of complexity. To define the precise mechanism of a given mutation from the molecular level to the intact organism, usually several laboratories with complementary expertise join forces and collaborate. This is also the case for the RyR2R420Q mutation, which will be discussed in detail as a somewhat peculiar, if not idiosyncratic, example below, providing remarkable and unexpected insight.

Conceptualizing possible molecular mechanisms for triggering CPVT using lessons learned from basic research

Given that CPVT episodes can be provoked by physical or emotional stress and are successfully treated with β-AR blockers, it is very likely that the cellular mechanism precipitating these arrhythmias is downstream of the β-AR. Since the root cause is a mutation of the RyR2, it is reasonable to assume that disturbances of cellular Ca2+ signalling are underlying these arrhythmias as key events, although other signalling pathways and molecular interactions with other proteins may contribute. Most (but not all) identified RyR2 mutations are gain of function mutations, manifesting themselves by increasing the open probability and/or Ca2+ sensitivity of the channels for the CICR mechanism. However, on the molecular level, every mutation may affect the function of the RyRs differently and in a site-specific way, also depending on the RyR2 domain, in which the mutation is located. For example, a range of mutation-specific mechanisms have been identified in transgenic mouse models depending on their location on the RyR2 protein. In the C-terminal domain, the RyR2R4496C mutation results in an elevated Ca2+ sensitivity.27 In contrast, the RyR2A4860G mutation, which is located in the pore, results in a loss of function.28 The RyR2R2474S mutation in the central domain interferes with the zipping and unzipping interaction between the N-terminal and central domains.29 This mutation has also been reported to prevent calstabin-2 (also known as FKBP12.6) binding to RyR2, thereby destabilizing it.30 The RyR2R420Q mutation highlighted in this review is located in the N-terminal domain. It interferes with inter-domain communications, notably with the core solenoid, impairing RyR2 channel closure.31 Quite detailed information on functional alterations of mutated RyRs was also obtained from cellular expression studies or lipid-bilayer experiments. Some mutations were found to specifically alter the cytosolic Ca2+ sensitivity of the RyR2, particularly after PKA-dependent phosphorylation (for example RyR2G230C).32 In contrast, other mutations primarily affect the intra-SR luminal Ca2+ sensitivity of the channels (for example RyR2S2246L, RyR2R2474S, and RyR2L422P).33 Based on these and related observations, the exact mechanism linking the altered molecular function of the mutated RyRs to the anomalous behaviour of the cardiac muscle cells and the heart is most likely also diverse, and a generalized concept of the consequences for all mutations would not be suitable.34 Nevertheless, it is conceivable that in most cases, posttranslational modifications occurring after β-AR stimulation, such as phosphorylation, elevate the Ca2+ sensitivity of the mutated RyRs further. Depending on the extent of phosphorylation, this may push the RyRs over the spontaneous activity threshold and even overcome the barrier of local control which normally keeps the SR Ca2+ release events localized as sparks. Notable RyR phosphorylation sites that have been implied in an increase of its activity are serine 2808, 2814, and 2030.11,16,21,35 As a consequence of the resulting loss of local control, the spontaneous diastolic Ca2+ release events can start to propagate along the cardiomyocyte in a chain reaction and as a Ca2+ wave sustained by CICR. During such Ca2+ waves, a limited fraction of the Ca2+ released from the SR will be removed from the cytosol by the electrogenic Na+–Ca2+ exchange (NCX), giving rise to an inward current and a depolarization noticeable as a delayed after-depolarization (DAD) during diastole. In principle, this depolarization could even lead to an extra action potential of an individual cell. How such single cell events could possibly spread into more substantial and multicellular cardiac muscle volumes and ultimately trigger a full-blown CPVT will be discussed below.

Besides the change of RyR activity and Ca2+ sensitivity following its phosphorylation, another mechanism changing the cellular Ca2+ signalling system also needs to be considered. The activity of the SR Ca2+ pump (SERCA) and the rate of SR refilling after a Ca2+ release event are restrained by phospholamban (PLB). After β-AR stimulation, PLB also becomes phosphorylated, predominantly by cAMP-dependent protein kinase A (PKA). This posttranslational modification removes the suppression of the SERCA by PLB, essentially stimulating the SERCA. In turn, this will lead to faster and more dynamic SR Ca2+ reuptake, which in turn may contribute to the arrhythmogenic Ca2+ release via SOICR, particularly so in the presence of mutated RyRs. However, the situation turned out to be more complex. How and to what extent these two important Ca2+ signalling systems are intertwined and mutually interdependent will be described below. In addition, the L-type Ca2+ current will increase during β-AR stimulation thereby possibly increasing the SR Ca2+ load. However, Ca2+ channel inhibitors were found to be less potent than β-AR blockers alone or in combination with flecainide to prevent CPVT episodes (for review see ref.36)

Unexpected role for the CPVT mutation RyR2R420Q in the initiation of arrhythmogenic events

The RyR2R420Q mutation was initially discovered in a family from Spain with several members suffering sudden cardiac death.24 A transgenic mouse line was subsequently engineered as a model system to carry out research projects aimed at understanding the ramifications of this mutation. These animals faithfully recapitulated a corresponding CPVT phenotype.31 In a comprehensive study, many features and parameters were investigated, such as ultrastructural changes, alterations in Ca2+ signalling, protein–protein interactions with the RyRs and single channel properties in lipid bilayer studies. Cellular Ca2+ signalling exhibited abnormalities associated with gain of function mutations, such as higher Ca2+ spark frequencies, longer spark duration, more frequent Ca2+ waves exhibiting shorter delays to the first wave, and faster Ca2+ spark recovery kinetics.31,37,38 However, what remained unclear was the question discussed above, how exactly the functional change of the mutated channels initiated the arrhythmias, in combination with β-AR stimulation.

In the framework of a smaller project focusing on the pathophysiology resulting from the RyR2R420Q mutation, this question was specifically addressed. What is the role of the mutated RyR2 and its posttranslational modification during β-AR stimulation in enabling the transition into arrhythmogenic Ca2+ signalling and ultimately into an arrhythmia? Specifically, we also wanted to examine a possible exacerbation by the alternative trigger mechanism mentioned above, the stimulation of the SERCA after PLB phosphorylation, which occurs in parallel during RyR2 phosphorylation during β-AR stimulation. As already mentioned, SERCA stimulation may increase the intra-SR Ca2+ concentration, with the possible consequence of favouring the cellular arrhythmogenicity via SOICR.

How to address this question experimentally? Both, the phosphorylation of the RyRs and the PLB share the same cAMP and PKA dependent downstream signalling pathway after β-AR stimulation. What would be needed is an experimental tool by which the SERCA could be stimulated independently and without affecting the RyR function directly.

In previous studies, we have used pharmacological tools, such as jasmonate or ochratoxin, to stimulate SERCA function.39 Unfortunately, these chemicals turned out not to be very specific and exhibited various side effects. But several years ago, it was noted that exposure of permeabilized cardiomyocytes to the monoclonal PLB antibody 2D12 was able to relieve the SERCA inhibition by PLB, thus specifically stimulating the SERCA.40 Unfortunately, in these experiments, the time course of antibody-induced stimulation was very slow, and it remained incomplete, presumably resulting from slow antibody diffusion within the permeabilized cardiomyocytes. Incidentally, it was found that enzymatically splitting the PLB antibody to obtain the small antibody-binding fragment (Fab) greatly enhanced its effectiveness and kinetics.41 In a previous study, we found that the Fabs were able to maximally stimulate the SERCA, to the same extent as the application of cAMP to activate PKA and phosphorylate PLB.35

Using this experimental tool, we designed experiments that allowed us to compare the pro-arrhythmic effect of isolated SERCA stimulation with the more common situation where, after β-AR stimulation, the activation of both, the RyRs and the SERCA, occurs simultaneously and in parallel. Our initial hypothesis was that stimulating both mechanisms in tandem would be much worse and result, for example, in higher Ca2+ wave frequencies. This was expected because the RyR2 phosphorylation is thought to increase the wave frequencies by making the RyRs more Ca2+ sensitive, while SERCA stimulation would elevate wave frequencies further via faster SR refilling and SOICR. To our big surprise and contrary to expectations, this was not the case. Ca2+ wave frequency was higher when only the SERCA was stimulated, without RyR2 phosphorylation (see Figure 2). This was the case in both cell types WT and CPVT, but the discrepancy was more pronounced in cells harbouring the RyR2R420Q mutation.

What is going on here? How to explain this unexpected behaviour? To understand the situation, one has to dive a bit deeper into the two potentially arrhythmogenic mechanisms and also look at secondary effects which their activation are likely to have.

The SERCA activation not only fills the SR with more Ca2+, thus favouring store overload and SOICR, but also lowers the cytosolic Ca2+ concentration, thereby potentially reducing the amount of cytosolic Ca2+ that is able to contribute to CICR and allow it two propagate from one Ca2+ release site to the next as a Ca2+ wave. This phenomenon might in fact constitute an anti-arrhythmic feature of SERCA stimulation, as far as cellular Ca2+ signalling is concerned.

Likewise, an elevated diastolic Ca2+ spark frequency may not only favour spreading of spontaneous CICR and Ca2+ waves, but it also constitutes an SR Ca2+ leak, since with every spark, a small amount of Ca2+ is lost from the SR. Thus, this leak might counterbalance to some extent the augmented SR filling by the SERCA and thereby also reduce the arrhythmogenicity. Taken together, each of the two mechanisms, the RyR2 channel openings and the SERCA pumping activity, have proarrhythmogenic consequences in the two different subcellular compartments, the cytosol and inside the SR lumen. The balance of the two activities and the intra-SR concentration and cytosolic trigger sensitivities of the mutated RyRs, and how this balance is disturbed following β-AR stimulation, will ultimately define the propensity for an arrhythmia.

According to this reasoning, we reckoned that in the case of the RyR2R420Q mutation such a scenario could explain our unexpected experimental observation described above. This would mean that the Ca2+ waves are predominantly triggered by elevated SERCA activity when the Ca2+ load is high. Such a behaviour could occur via an intra-SR mechanism activating the RyRs from the SR luminal side on an intra-luminal Ca2+ binding site of the channels.13 In other words, this could result from an elevated Ca2+ sensitivity of the mutated RyRs for intra-SR Ca2+, which in turn would lead to a reduced threshold for SOICR. This also indicates that in the presence of such a pathomechanism, the SR Ca2+ leak via the mutated RyRs might actually be somewhat beneficial, by lowering the SR Ca2+ load.

To confirm this rather speculative hypothesis, we repeated the same experiments, but after loading the myocytes with the low-affinity Ca2+ indicator Fluo-5N, which becomes entrapped inside the SR.42 If the scenario proposed above was correct, this intervention should reveal a lower Ca2+ concentration inside the SR in those cells exposed to cAMP (to activate both the SERCA and the RyRs) when compared to the presence of Fab, which exclusively stimulates the SERCA. Indeed, a lower SR concentration was recorded, confirming that the Ca2+ leak via phosphorylated RyRs is sufficient to reduce the SR content to the extent where it curtails the increase of Ca2+ wave frequencies by SERCA. Taken together, these findings, which may be specific for the RyR2R420Q mutation, reveal that the interplay and balance of SERCA and RyR2 activity are decisive cellular features for the precipitation of arrhythmogenic events.

Open question: transition from cellular arrhythmogenic events to arrhythmias on the organ level

How can the small DAD elicited by a Ca2+ wave in a single cell trigger an arrhythmia on the level of the entire heart? The transition of this abnormal diastolic depolarization from the cellular to the tissue level adds another layer of complexity to the pathophysiology. One obvious possibility is by generating extrasystolic action potentials. However, for this to happen, the electrical source to load mismatch experienced by a single cardiomyocyte facing a certain tissue volume via its gap junctions needs to be overcome. In other words, in a three-dimensional tissue, many cells need to generate DADs concurrently, in order to create a current source that can drive and depolarize a large multicellular load sufficiently to initiate a full-blown ectopic action potential. Some degree of synchronization among cells will always occur by the rather concurrent SR refilling after each Ca2+ transient. In addition, anatomical structures were identified where the source-load mismatch is less pronounced than deep inside the muscle tissue of the ventricles. For example, this condition is present in the Purkinje-myocardial junctions, which were proposed to serve as a possible origin for abnormal electrical activity.43 Furthermore, in the presence of structural or electrophysiological remodelling, such as during fibrosis or heart failure, the protective source–sink mismatch may become markedly degraded, which may favour the development of such arrhythmias.44

More recently, it has also been noted that direct triggering of ectopic action potentials may not even be necessary to initiate an arrhythmia. Subthreshold electrical changes, such as DADs (and EADs), could be sufficient to impair the propagation of cardiac electrical excitation either directly by partial inactivation of the sodium current (INa) of the next action potential or indirectly by cycle-length-dependent refractory mechanisms, thereby precipitating arrhythmias.45,46

Similarly, Ca2+-dependent AP lengthening resulting from late Ca2+ sparks during the decay of the Ca2+ transient and from EADs can contribute to arrhythmias via loss of repolarization reserve.44,47

Conclusion and outlook

The more details we learn regarding the understanding of the molecular and cellular underpinnings of the pathophysiology resulting from RyR2 mutations (and for many other cardiac conditions), the more specifically and more mechanistically causative we will be able to target them for treatments (for a recent review see ref.48). For the RyR2R420Q mutation portrayed above the interplay of SERCA stimulation and RyR2-mediated SR, Ca2+ leak turned out to be quite unexpected. It appears that the finely tuned balance between these two mechanisms is decisive for the stability of Ca2+ signalling. This balance will most likely be quite different for the various RyR mutations, most notably for those causing a loss-of-function. Also, it will be important whether the intra-SR or the cytosolic Ca2+ sensitivity (or both) will be elevated as the result of a particular mutation. In the first case, the SR loading via SERCA may play a decisive role, while in the second case, the hyperactivity of the RyR2 itself may be the main culprit.

So far the pharmacological toolbox to specifically inhibit or stimulate the SERCA49 or RyR250,51 function is very limited, but research is ongoing on both targets and progress may be expected, possibly also from gene therapy approaches. One promising development is the reported beneficial off-target effect of the class 1 antiarrhythmic flecainide, commonly used as a Na+ channel inhibitor. According to several, but not all, studies, flecainide also acts as an open-channel blocker of the RyR2, which may be useful in treating some of the CPVTs52–54 (but see e.g. ref.55). With a better toolbox and the knowledge of the fundamental molecular and cellular dysfunction of Ca2+ signalling specific for each RyR2 mutation, therapies may be optimized and tailored for each individual patient, in the framework of the evolving precision medicine. Such developments may be greatly facilitated by using iPSCs and other experimental systems for specific drug testing and ‘clinical trials in a dish’ models carrying the patient’s own genetic background.56,57 As far as gene therapies are concerned, the large size of the RyR2 will render many strategies unfeasible. However, AAV-mediated expression of CaMKII-inhibitor peptides as well as allele-specific silencing of the mutated RyR2 mRNA may be promising future approaches.58–60
