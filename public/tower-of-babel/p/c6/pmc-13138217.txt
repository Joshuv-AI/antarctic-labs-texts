Smoking after lung transplantation: Incidence and risk factors in the French multicenter prospective tabatransplant cohort study
Authors: Pauline Pradère, Sebastien Couraud, Séverine Feuillet, Adrien Tissot, Véronique Boussaud, Marlène Murris, Xavier Demant, Christel Saint Raymond, Vincent Bunel, Laurence Beaumont, Romain Kessler, Claire Merveilleux, Martine Reynaud-Gaubert, Jérôme Le Pavec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking is a major contributor to respiratory diseases requiring lung transplantation (LTx). Data on smoking after LTx are scarce. Understanding the incidence and determinants of smoking after LTx is crucial for optimizing long-term outcomes.MethodsFor this prospective observational cohort study done from March 2023 to August 2024 in 11 French transplantation (Tx) centers, LTx and heart-lung transplant (HLTx) recipients completed an anonymous self-administered questionnaire during their routine follow-up. Pre- and post-Tx smoking and sociodemographic characteristics were collected and analyzed centrally.ResultsOf 946 recipients with complete data on smoking, 524 (55%) reported having smoked before Tx. Among them, approximately 5% smoked after LTx. Smoking after Tx was significantly associated with several demographic and contextual factors, including female sex, younger age, living alone, having cystic fibrosis, prior use of hand-rolled cigarettes, and regular exposure to smokers among family and friends. By multivariate analysis, younger age, living alone, and smoking among family and friends were associated with post-Tx smoking. Due to the anonymous completion of the self-administered questionnaires, associations between post-Tx smoking and clinical outcomes could not be assessed.ConclusionsThis study provides novel multicenter data about the incidence and determinants of smoking after LTx or HLTx. A small but meaningful proportion of recipients smoked after Tx. We identified several social and demographic risk factors. Our findings highlight the need for targeted smoking-relapse prevention-and-support programs in lung and heart-lung recipients. Further research is warranted to evaluate the impact of smoking after Tx on graft and patient outcomes.Keywords: lung transplantation, smoking relapse, tobacco use, risk factors, cohort study

Tobacco smoking is the main cause of chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)1 and interstitial lung disease,2 which are among the leading indications for lung transplantation (LTx).3 LTx is a life-extending procedure in patients with end-stage respiratory failure. Smoking cessation for at least 6 months is an eligibility criterion for being put on the LTx list.4 Continued abstinence from smoking is crucial to ensure graft survival and good long-term outcomes.5 However, there are no formal recommendations regarding the assessment of this exposure or its monitoring while on the waiting list.4

Smoking after LTx has been documented in several cohorts, with a prevalence ranging from 0% to 15%.6, 7, 8, 9 This behavior is of concern given its established associations with higher risks of infection,10, 11 malignancy,12, 13, 14 cardiovascular disease,15, 16 and lung function deterioration.8 Well-known determinants of smoking relapse after cessation in the general population and among kidney and liver recipients include younger age, psychosocial vulnerability, and lack of social support.17, 18 However, little is known about factors associated with smoking in lung recipients, in whom the therapeutic margin is particularly narrow.

The primary objective of the French nationwide multicenter TabaTransplant prospective cohort study was to estimate the prevalence of smoking after LTx or heart-lung transplantation (HLTx). The secondary objective was to identify demographic and behavioral factors independently associated with post-Tx smoking relapse.

Methods

Study design

This prospective multicenter observational cohort study was done in all 11 French LTx and HLTx centers between March 2023 and August 2024. All adult LTx or HLTx recipients with at least 6 months of follow-up after Tx were eligible.

Table S1 details the smoking management practices in the 11 Tx centers.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Paris-Saint-Joseph Hospital (Groupe Éthique en Recherche Médicale, #IRB548) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. In accordance with French law on studies of de-identified health data, patient-informed consent was not required. Data management complied with the requirements of the French data protection authority (Commission Nationale de l′Informatique et des Libertés).

During the study period, 2849 LTx or HLTx recipients received follow-up in France during the study period. Due to organizational constraints, questionnaire distribution was possible for only 1899 patients. No individual-level clinical data were available for non-invited recipients, precluding formal comparisons with the entire national cohort.

Data collection

During their routine post-Tx follow-up visits, patients were invited to complete a self-administered anonymous questionnaire. Time since Tx was the time from the date of Tx recorded in the Tx-center database to the date of questionnaire completion reported by the patient. Although the date of birth was collected, no direct identifiers were recorded, and the questionnaires were processed centrally without access to clinical identifiers. The questionnaire (Supplementary Appendix) was available in paper format or electronically via a QR code. Completed questionnaires were analyzed centrally at the Marie Lannelongue Hospital.

Outcome measure

The primary outcome measure was the incidence of patients who resumed smoking after Tx.

Statistical analysis

Baseline was defined as the time of Tx. Baseline demographics were described as median [first to third quartiles] for continuous variables and as n (%) for categorical variables. The patients were divided into 2 groups based on whether they had smoked before listing. Former smokers were divided into 2 sub-groups depending on whether they smoked after Tx. The groups and sub-groups were compared by applying the Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on the number of groups, and Pearson's chi-square test for categorical variables.

Logistic regression was used to look for associations linking the baseline factors listed in Table 4 to post-Tx smoking resumption, handled as a binary outcome. A multivariate model was built using the variables associated with P values below 0.05 by univariate analysis. Because the number of outcome events was limited, the number of covariates included in the multivariable model was intentionally restricted to minimize the risk of model overfitting. Age at Tx was entered into the logistic regression model as a continuous variable (per year increase). Missing data were handled using a complete-case approach. Only patients with complete information on smoking status before and after Tx were included in the primary analyses. For secondary variables, analyses were performed using available data, and the number of patients with missing values for each variable is reported in the corresponding tables. No imputation of missing data was performed, given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited number of events in the post-Tx smoking group. Two-tailed P values below 0.05 were considered significant. Relative risks (RRs) were computed with their 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs). All statistical analyses and graphs were performed using the R program v4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org).

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Figure 1 is the patient flowchart. Among the 2849 LTx or HLTx recipients living in France, only 1899 could be invited to participate in the study, due to organizational issues. Of these, 996 (52.5%) completed the questionnaire. The questionnaires of 50 patients were not evaluable, leaving 946 patients for study inclusion. Table 1 reports the baseline features of the overall cohort and of the groups with vs without smoking before Tx. Briefly, compared to never-smokers, smokers were significantly older and were more likely to be living with a partner, to have been factory workers, and to have pulmonary fibrosis or COPD as the reason for Tx.

Baseline characteristics of the patients who smoked before transplantation (n = 524)

Tobacco use after Tx was defined exclusively by patient self-report and therefore reflects admitted behaviors rather than objectively confirmed tobacco use. Table 2 reports the smoking history in the 524 (55.4%) patients who smoked before listing. Most patients in this group had attempted to quit multiple times, although nearly half could not remember the number of attempts. The median time between cessation and Tx was 5.3 years. During the management of their respiratory disease, only one-third of smokers were offered cessation support, with nicotine replacement therapy being the most common method. Among smokers, 90% reported quitting smoking before Tx; in the remaining 10%, the main reported reason for continuing to smoke was lack of motivation (Table S1). Of note, although the policies in all 11 centers required at least 6 months of abstinence before listing, 42/524 (8%) smokers reported continued smoking until Tx (Figure 1).

Features of Pre-transplantation Smokers With vs Without Post-transplantation Smoking

In the group of 524 patients with a history of smoking, 27 (5%) patients smoked after Tx, as determined by self-report (Table 3). All patients who reported smoking after Tx had a history of smoking before Tx: no never-smokers reported initiating smoking after Tx. Compared to the sub-group that did not smoke after Tx, the sub-group that did was significantly younger, had fewer patients living as a couple, and had more patients who had used hand-rolled cigarettes and who had cystic fibrosis. Neither the use of manufactured cigarettes nor the duration of smoking differed significantly between the 2 sub-groups. In the sub-group that smoked after Tx, the time from Tx to questionnaire completion was longer and the proportion of patients whose close family and friends did not smoke was smaller. Risk factors for smoking after transplantation

Table 4 reports the results of the univariate and multivariate analyses to identify variables associated with post-Tx smoking among patients who smoked before Tx. By univariate analysis, cystic fibrosis was the strongest risk factor. Other risk factors were smoking among close family and friends, living alone, having used hand-rolled cigarettes before Tx, and female sex. Older age at Tx was associated with a lower risk of smoking after Tx. By multivariate analysis, the only variables associated with smoking after Tx were smoking among family and friends, living alone, and younger age.

Discussion

In this large multicenter prospective cohort of 946 LTx or HLTx recipients, 524 (55%) individuals had a history of smoking prior to Tx. Among them, 27 (5.2%) reported smoking after Tx, the median interval being 5.0 [1.5-8.5] years. The reported 5.2% prevalence reflects admitted smoking rather than the true prevalence of smoking, and underreporting cannot be excluded. Previous studies using cotinine testing suggest that a substantial proportion of recipients may deny active tobacco use.19, 20 This 5.2% prevalence is low compared with the 25%-30% prevalence reported in the general French population. It is nonetheless clinically important given the potential adverse effects of smoking on graft function, infection risk, and long-term survival. The main strengths of our study lie in its multicenter design, the high response rate to the questionnaire, and the large number of patients included.

Previously reported rates of smoking after LTx or HLTx range from 0% to 15%. In addition to differences in study populations, reasons for this variability may include the method used to detect smoking resumption, i.e., self-report, exhaled CO, cotinine in blood and/or urine, or serum carboxyhemoglobin levels.4, 6, 8, 9, 14, 19 Recipients may be reluctant to report smoking. However, the use in our study of questionnaires that were both anonymous and self-administered probably minimized social-desirability bias, although some underreporting of smoking after Tx cannot be excluded. A study in the general population found that self-reported smoking was validated by blood cotinine levels in over 95% of individuals.21

Pre-Tx smokers in our study reported substantial tobacco exposure of nearly 20 pack-years. Interestingly, 29% of smokers reported more than 2 cessation attempts and only 33% were offered professional support for smoking cessation. These data indicate a substantial unmet need for structured cessation interventions before listing.

Our finding that, compared to never-smokers, pre-Tx smokers were older and were more often male, factory workers, and transplanted due to COPD or fibrosis is consistent with previous reports.19, 21 In a retrospective single-center study of 620 patients on the LTx waiting list, self-reported status regarding smoking and exposure to second-hand smoke was compared to repeated blood or urine cotinine assay results.20 At least 1 assay was positive in 14.8% of patients, most of whom had COPD. Of note, 84.7% of patients with a positive assay falsely declared not consuming any nicotine-based products. These findings, together with the history of smoking in over half of our patients, indicate a need for careful attention to the smoking history of Tx candidates and recipients, given the risk of tobacco use after Tx. Routine biochemical screening and social vulnerability assessments should be performed during and after listing, and support should be offered early to patients at risk for smoking after Tx.

Multivariate analysis identified younger age at Tx, being single, and having smokers among friends and family as independent predictors of smoking after Tx. Neither the number of cigarettes per day nor the years of smoking was significantly associated with smoking after Tx. However, the use of hand-rolled cigarettes, a factor associated with greater nicotine-dependence, predicted smoking after Tx in a previous study.22 These findings suggest that psychosocial factors may be stronger determinants of post-Tx smoking than the degree of previous tobacco exposure. However, earlier studies found that the key risk factors were the underlying disease (notably COPD), cessation for less than 1 year, and higher cumulative tobacco exposure.12, 23

Taken together, our findings suggest that younger, socially isolated recipients with prior use of hand-rolled cigarettes and ongoing close exposure to smokers are at high risk for post-Tx smoking. Although the small number of events precluded accurate absolute-risk estimation, this subgroup may warrant enhanced monitoring, including biochemical screening.

Although organizational constraints allowed the enrollment of only two-thirds of eligible recipients, all 11 French LTx and HLTx centers enrolled patients. The distribution of included patients across centers broadly reflected nationwide LTx and HLTx activity, and no center effect was observed for post-Tx smoking, supporting the overall representativeness of the cohort.

All participating centers required at least 6 months of abstinence before listing. Nonetheless, 8% of smokers reported that they continued to smoke until Tx. This variable was not kept in the multivariable model, given the small number of post-Tx smokers and the risk of model overfitting; therefore, our study cannot determine whether smoking until Tx independently predicts post-Tx smoking. In addition, this finding likely reflects both imperfect enforcement of abstinence criteria in urgent situations and underdetection of ongoing tobacco use despite mandated policies. Improving cessation outcomes in LTx and HLTx candidates and recipients will require adaptation of the interventions used outside the Tx setting. A large meta-analysis of studies involving over 100,000 participants demonstrated that varenicline nearly tripled abstinence rates at 12 months (odds ratio [OR], 2.88; 95%CI, 2.40-3.47) and that nicotine replacement and bupropion were also effective (ORs, 1.84 and 1.82, respectively).24 Another recent meta-analysis suggests that the use of nicotine-containing e-cigarettes may also be an effective aid for smoking cessation.25 None of the patients were LTx candidates or recipients, and the safety and efficacy of these treatments in the LTx setting remains unclear.

The number of HLTx recipients was too small for a reliable comparison of the relapse risk in HLTx vs LTx recipients.

Integrating structured smoking-relapse prevention into routine follow-up protocols may benefit patient outcomes after LTx or HLTx. Targeted strategies could include psychosocial screening to identify recipients who are socially isolated or have smokers among their close contacts. Structured cessation support could be provided before and after Tx. In selected high-risk patients, periodic biochemical screening (e.g., cotinine or exhaled CO) would improve the detection of tobacco use. Embedding these approaches into a multidisciplinary Tx-care program may help to increase pre-Tx cessation and to reduce post-Tx relapse.

Selection bias is a major limitation of our study. Only two-thirds of eligible recipients were invited, and only half of those responded. Patients who resumed smoking may well have been less inclined to complete a questionnaire addressing tobacco use. This potential source of bias may have led to underestimation of post-Tx smoking prevalence. Despite the likely mitigating effect of anonymity, this bias cannot be fully excluded. Second, self-administered questionnaires are imperfect tools for assessing socially sensitive behaviors such as smoking after Tx. However, anonymity and self-completion have been shown to reduce social-desirability bias compared with face-to-face interviews. Biochemical verification was not feasible in our study due to the anonymous design and to organizational and funding issues. Consequently, our results reflect the prevalence of admitted smoking, which may be lower than the true prevalence of smoking. However, in a previous study, self-report showed 85% sensitivity and 100% specificity for detecting smoking.14 Third, anonymity also preventing us from determining whether smoking after Tx was associated with clinical endpoints such as chronic lung allograft dysfunction, malignancy, or event-free survival. Fourth, given the small number of patients who reported smoking after Tx (n = 27), only a limited number of predictors were included in the multivariable model. Nonetheless, overfitting may have occurred, and the estimated effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, although several demographic and social variables were examined, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Important determinants of smoking relapse, such as the severity of nicotine dependence, psychological comorbidities, socioeconomic stressors, and access to structured smoking-cessation support, were not recorded and may have influenced the observed associations. These limitations highlight the need for larger longitudinal studies involving comprehensive behavioral and psychosocial assessments. Fifth, the time from Tx to questionnaire completion varied substantially and, in smokers, was significantly longer in patients who smoked after Tx than in patients who did not. This disparity may have introduced bias, as a longer time since Tx meant more time to return to smoking after pre-Tx cessation. Consequently, the reported incidence of smoking after Tx may underestimate early smoking and overestimate delayed smoking, potentially skewing the observed associations. Finally, all the study centers were in France, potentially limiting the general applicability of our results.

In conclusion, a substantial proportion of LTx and HLTx recipients had a history of smoking. Smoking after Tx, although relatively infrequent, was significantly associated with modifiable psychosocial factors. These findings underscore the urgent need for structured, long-term smoking-cessation and relapse-prevention programs integrated into post-Tx care. Such programs should include psychosocial assessments, family-based interventions, and targeted biochemical screening in high-risk recipients.
