An MRI-based radiomics framework for early identification and progression stratification in knee osteoarthritis: data from the osteoarthritis initiative
Authors: Jiahui Fu, Lin Mu, Dong Dong, Mingyang Li, Zheng Miao, Xiaochen Huai, Yuhao Zheng, Huimao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop a cascaded machine learning model based on MRI radiomics features from cartilage and subchondral bone to predict the incidence and progression of knee osteoarthritis (KOA), thereby addressing the need for early intervention in pre-radiographic stages.Materials and methodsThe study analyzed 456 participants without radiographic OA (Kellgren–Lawrence [KL] 0–1) at baseline were selected from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI) and randomly divided into training and testing cohorts (7:3). Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disease and a major cause of pain and disability worldwide, with the knee joint being the most commonly affected area [1, 2]. Despite its high prevalence and socioeconomic burden, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying knee osteoarthritis (KOA) remain incompletely understood, and current diagnostic and prognostic approaches often fail to capture the early and complex nature of disease progression.

KOA is now recognized as a whole-joint disease, involving structural changes in articular cartilage, subchondral bone, infrapatellar fat pad, periarticular muscles, and meniscus [3, 4]. While degeneration of articular cartilage is considered to be the main factor in the incidence of OA, emerging evidence from preclinical studies suggests that subchondral bone lesions may precede or interact with cartilage degeneration during the early stages of the disease [5–7]. Early-stage KOA is characterized by chondrocyte dysfunction and extracellular matrix degradation, leading to cartilage loss, joint space narrowing, and mechanical pain. Concurrently, subchondral bone exhibits increased remodeling, sclerosis, and bone marrow lesions, contributing to both biomechanical alterations and inflammatory processes that further accelerate cartilage degeneration [8, 9]. The close physical and biochemical interaction between cartilage and subchondral bone highlights their interdependence in maintaining joint tissue integrity and function [10].

Kellgren-Lawrence classification (KLG) based on X-ray is widely used for KOA identification and prognosis [11]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables more detailed visualization of cartilage morphology, bone marrow lesions, and subchondral trabecular structure [12]. However, conventional imaging techniques often lack the sensitivity to detect early microstructural and biochemical alterations imperceptible to visual assessment. Radiomics, a rapidly emerging field that transforms large volumes of digital medical images into quantifiable data to enhance the accuracy of early disease diagnosis, prediction, and personalized treatment [13–15]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of radiomics in characterizing OA severity [16–18]. However, most existing research has focused on single structures or two-dimensional regions of interest, limiting their capacity to capture the spatial heterogeneity and inter-tissue interactions integral to KOA pathogenesis. Moreover, longitudinal investigations applying comprehensive multi-structure radiomics analyses for predicting tissue-level disease progression remain limited.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a two-stage radiomics-based model to predict structural progression in KOA. We further investigate whether radiomic features extracted from cartilage and subchondral bone provide incremental prognostic value for early detection and progression stratification. By establishing an MRI-based radiomics framework, the proposed approach is expected to improve individualized risk stratification, inform timely clinical decision-making, and advance precision medicine in KOA management.

Materials and methods

Study sample

All participants were from the Osteoarthritis Initiative (OAI), a longitudinal, multicenter cohort study that enrolled 4,796 individuals with or at risk of KOA between 2004 and 2006 [19, 20]. The OAI study provides up to 11 years of annually collected clinical, imaging, and biospecimen data related to KOA (https://nda.nih.gov/oai/). Study protocols and informed consent documents were reviewed and approved by the local institutional review boards of all collaborating OAI centers.

Based on longitudinal radiographic assessments using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system, 456 participants were analyzed and categorized into four groups: (i) the incident KOA group (n = 152), which included participants without radiographic KOA at baseline (KL ≤ 1) but developed radiographic KOA (KL ≥ 2) within 48 months of follow-up; (ii) the early progression group (n = 96), encompassing those who developed radiographic KOA (KL ≥ 2) within 12–24 months of follow-up; (iii) the late progression group (n = 56), comprising individuals who developed radiographic KOA (KL ≥ 2) during 36–48 months of follow-up; (iv) the non-KOA group(n = 304), which consisted of participants with no radiographic KOA (KL ≤ 1) throughout the 48 months of follow-up. To minimize baseline differences, participants in the non-incident KOA group were matched to those in the incident KOA group using 2:1 propensity score matching (PSM) based on sex, race, body mass index (BMI), and contralateral knee status at baseline [21, 22]. Figure 1 shows the selection criteria. Additionally, Fig. 2 provides a comprehensive flow chart of this study. For model development, 318 participants (70%) were randomly assigned to the training set and 138 (30%) to the independent testing set to predict incident KOA. Five-fold cross-validation was performed within the training set to optimize model parameters and reduce overfitting. To develop a second-stage model for predicting progression speed, individuals with incident KOA were similarly divided into training (n = 106) and testing (n = 46) cohorts using a 7:3 ratio.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

MRI scans were acquired using 3.0-T Siemens scanners (Magnetom Trio, Siemens Healthcare) with a sagittal intermediate-weighted three-dimensional dual-echo steady-state (3D DESS) sequence with fat suppression (repetition time (TR), 16.3 ms; echo time (TE), 4.7 ms; field of view (FOV), 140 mm; slice thickness, 0.7 mm; number of slices, 160; and total acquisition time, 10.6 min). Supplemental Appendix A provides further details of the sequence parameters and MRI preprocessing procedures.

Segmentation and features extraction

Baseline sagittal 3D DESS MRI images of the knee were used for radiomic analysis. A segmentation model based on the nnU-Net architecture [23] was developed, with its configuration and training hyperparameters optimized according to the spatial resolution, intensity distribution, and anatomical characteristics of the target MRI dataset (Supplemental Appendix B). Images underwent signal intensity normalization via Z-score and were resampled to 0.70 × 0.36 × 0.36 mm³ using linear interpolation, while corresponding segmentation masks were resampled with nearest-neighbor interpolation to ensure isotropic resolution. Six 3D volumes of interest (VOIs) from the cartilage and subchondral bone regions of the femur, tibia, and patella were semi-automatically segmented using ITK-SNAP (version 3.8.0; www.itksnap.org). Cartilage VOIs were manually segmented by two junior radiologists (with 5 and 7 years of experience in musculoskeletal diagnostic respectively), and subsequently reviewed and refined by two senior radiologists with 10 and 15 years of experience. Subchondral bone VOIs were automatically segmented using a binary morphological operation combined with a multi-label constraint algorithm, and then independently reviewed and corrected by the same senior radiologists to ensure anatomical accuracy. Segmentation details are provided in Supplement Appendix C. The nnU-Net-based segmentation achieved a Dice coefficient above 0.94 across all structures in the independent testing set (Table 1). The corresponding segmentation data is publicly available at: https://github.com/lisherlock/13-structure-segmentation-of-knee-osteoarthritis.

Image preprocessing, feature extraction and selection, and model development are all implemented on Radiomics Intelligent Analysis Software (RIAS; https://github.com/lisherlock/RMIT) [24]. Radiomic features were extracted from the 3D DESS MRI images using PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) [25], an open-source Python (version 3.10) package, integrated within the RIAS platform. A total of 1427 radiomic features were extracted from each VOI, including first-order statistics, shape-based features, higher-order features and filter-based features (Supplemental Appendix D).

Feature selection and model development

After feature extraction, radiomic features with inter-observer and intra-observer consistency (ICCs) ≥ 0.75 were retained as relatively stable features (Supplemental Appendix E). Dimensionality reduction was subsequently performed using Student’s t-test, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and principal component analysis (PCA) to minimize overfitting. Detailed features are provided in Supplemental Appendix F.

To predict the risk and timing of KOA progression, a two-stage logistic regression (LR) modeling framework was constructed using radiomic features extracted from MRI. In the first stage, a predictive model was developed to identify individuals at risk of incident radiographic KOA within 4 years, capturing the transition from non-diseased to structurally diseased state. In the second stage, among those who developed KOA, a progression stratification model was implemented to distinguish early (≤ 2 years) and late (> 2 years) structural progressors based on the time to radiographic worsening. Both models used the same feature selection pipeline and were trained using repeated stratified five-fold cross-validation to ensure robustness. These models were then integrated into a cascaded classification framework to enable more refined risk stratification for KOA progression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and R (version 4.3.3). Quantitative data were analyzed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Qualitative data were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was indicative of statistical significance. Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which quantified the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcome. PCA loadings represent the correlation coefficients between original variables and principal components. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis, with the area under the ROC curve (AUC) calculated as the overall performance criterion. Additional indicators included accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, F1 score, and both micro- and macro-averaged ROC curves. The DeLong test was used to statistically compare the AUCs between models.

Results

Participant characteristics

After PSM, no significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between groups. Table 2 presents the demographic data before and after PSM. In the KOA group, 69.1% were female (105 of 152), and the mean BMI (28.70 ± 4.71 kg/m2) and total Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) score (11.91 ± 13.88) were slightly higher compared to those of the control group (28.39 ± 4.87 kg/m2 and 11.26 ± 13.83, respectively). No statistically significant differences in clinical characteristics were observed between the training and testing cohorts in the model predicting KOA onset (Supplemental Table SⅠ). However, in the training cohort for the KOA progression model, significant differences were observed in BMI (P = 0.030) and WOMAC stiffness sub-score (P = 0.042) between the early and late progression groups (Supplemental Table SⅡ). In contrast, no significant differences in demographic or clinical characteristics were observed between these two groups in the testing cohort (P > 0.05).

Radiomic features

For the model predicting incident KOA, 74 radiomic features from cartilage VOIs and 38 from subchondral bone VOIs were retained after feature selection (Supplemental Tables SⅢ and SⅤ). Meanwhile, when combined cartilage and subchondral bone VOIs, 102 features were retained (Supplemental Figure S1A, Table SⅦ). However, for the KOA progression stratification model, 28 and 26 radiomic features were retained from cartilage and subchondral bone VOIs, respectively (Supplemental Tables SⅨ and SⅩ). And 46 radiomic features were retained when cartilage and subchondral bone VOIs were combined (Supplemental Figure S1B, Table SⅪ).

Performance evaluation of predicted incident radiographic KOA

SHAP can illustrate the contribution of radiomic features, thereby enhancing model interpretability. Both PCA_feature0 and PCA_feature1 exhibited robust predictive performance in both cartilage and subchondral bone, as indicated by their positive SHAP values (Supplemental Figure S2A). The PCA loading matrix, which quantifies each feature’s contribution to the principal components, revealed tissue-specific drivers. Specifically, for PCA_feature0, cartilage was predominantly characterized by wavelet-LLL_firstorder_Minimum (loading, −0.1479) from the patella, while subchondral bone was primarily associated with the exponential_glrlm_ShortRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis (loading, 0.2468) from femur (Supplemental Tables SIV and SVI). Notably, when combining cartilage and subchondral bone, PCA_feature0 emerged as the most stable biomarker (Fig. 3A), predominantly influenced by texture features of the femur subchondral bone (square_glrlm_ShortRunLowGrayLevelEmphasis, loading = 0.1505, Supplemental Table SⅧ). Figure 4A shows the ROC curves of each LR model for the prediction of KOA onset. In the testing cohort, the AUC was 0.974 (95% CI: 0.944–1.000) for cartilage VOIs, 0.941 (95% CI: 0.904–0.979) for subchondral bone VOIs, and 0.985 (95% CI: 0.969–1.000) for the combined model. Although the combined model achieved the highest discrimination performance, the DeLong test showed no statistically significant differences among the models (P > 0.05). Performance evaluation of predicted stratification of progression

With the first-stage model predicting the onset of KOA, we conducted a secondary analysis focusing on patients with KOA to construct a second-stage model for predicting the risk of early versus late disease progression. When cartilage and subchondral bone features were combined, wavelet-HHL_firstorder_Minimum derived from the patellar subchondral bone demonstrated the highest predictive contribution to the model and was negatively correlated with the disease progression (Fig. 3B). Among the LR models, the subchondral bone-based model slightly outperformed the cartilage-based model, with AUCs of 0.721 (95% CI: 0.543–0.898) and 0.676 (95% CI: 0.514–0.839), respectively. Notably, the combined model incorporating cartilage and subchondral bone achieved superior diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.738 (95% CI: 0.565–0.911) (Fig. 4B). Other detailed evaluation indicators are shown in Supplemental Table SXIII.

Additionally, among the clinical factors assessed, only the status of the contralateral knee was identified as a significant predictor of KOA progression. However, its predictive performance (AUC: 0.575) was inferior to that of the radiomics-based model (AUC: 0.738). Furthermore, integrating clinical factors with radiomic features did not improve model performance compared to that of the radiomics-based model alone (AUC: 0.718, 95% CI: 0.537–0.900) (Supplemental Appendix F, Figure S3).

Performance evaluation of cascading classification framework

A three-classification cascade model was developed based on the above two-stage binary classification LR models. The confusion matrix is shown in Fig. 4C. As Fig. 4D illustrates, the micro-average AUC was 0.948 (95% CI: 0.901–0.973), while the macro-average AUC was 0.918 (95% CI: 0.876–0.969), indicating robust classification performance. The overall accuracy of the three classifications was 0.791 (Supplemental Table SXIV), further supporting the model’s effectiveness in distinguishing different KOA progression stages.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a novel MRI-based cascade radiomics framework using high-resolution images to identify individuals at high risk of KOA and to detect potential early disease progression. The multi-VOI model integrating both cartilage and subchondral bone outperformed the single-ROI model in predicting KOA incidence (AUC = 0.985) and progression (AUC = 0.738). Furthermore, the two-stage cascaded classification approach effectively stratified patients into different risk categories (overall accuracy = 0.791). This framework offers a reliable and interpretable tool for assist early identification and risk stratification of KOA, thereby enhancing clinical risk assessment and facilitating personalized management strategies.

Our findings support the growing recognition that KOA is a dynamic and heterogeneous disease involving early pathological changes not only in the cartilage, but also in the subchondral bone. Early diagnosis is critical for extending the diagnosis window and informing timely medical decision-making. Väärälä et al. employed 3D texture analysis based on gray-level co-occurrence matrices and found that texture features of the tibial and femoral cartilage could effectively predict KOA onset, showing significant longitudinal changes across different progressive KOA groups [26]. However, texture analysis provides limited information and easily susceptible to image parameters. Radiomics can mine more comprehensive information related to disease diagnosis by analyzing image features [27, 28]. Cui et al. demonstrated that MRI-based radiomics using cartilage from multiple knee compartments achieved high diagnostic accuracy for KOA (AUC: 0.983) [29]. Similarly, Xue et al. found that radiomics models based on tibial and femoral subchondral bone effectively distinguished radiological KOA, outperforming trabecular-based parameters [30]. Our findings are consistent with these results. The radiomics features derived from the cartilage and subchondral bone of the femur, tibia, and patella yielded excellent predictive performance for KOA incidence within 4 years (AUC: 0.974 vs. 0.941). This underscores the potential of combined cartilage and subchondral bone radiomics in capturing early disease-related changes and improving risk prediction.

Integrating a second-stage model to differentiate progression timing (early vs. late) represents a novel aspect of our framework. Identifying the KOA population at risk for early progression is critical for effective disease prevention and personalized treatment strategies. Different from the model predicting KOA onset, we found that the subchondral bone-based model was slightly more effective in stratifying progression risk than the cartilage-based model (AUC: 0.721 vs. 0.676). SHAP analysis further identified square_glrlm_ShortRunLowGrayLevelEmphasis and wavelet-hhl_firstder_minimum from the subchondral bone as key predictive factors for both onset and progression of KOA. These findings are consistent with previous studies. Liu et al. reported that patients with mild OA exhibited reduced trabecular bone volume in the femur and tibia without concurrent cartilage thinning, suggesting that changes in trabecular structure may serve as an earlier biomarker of OA onset [31]. This supports the hypothesis that subchondral bone changes may precede those in cartilage, potentially offering a more sensitive approach for identifying high-risk KOA populations. However, further validation with additional data is necessary.

Articular cartilage and subchondral bone are not only anatomically contiguous, but also engaged in biochemical interactions mediated by various signaling molecules. Fell et al. [32] demonstrated a linear relationship between subchondral bone thickness and cartilage thickness, as well as between cartilage damage and both bone mineral density and bone volume. Similarly, Aizah et al. [33] showed that subchondral bone remodeling and cartilage matrix degradation interact during the early stages of KOA. Consistent with these findings, our study observed that combining cartilage- and subchondral bone-derived features improved predictive performance for both KOA incidence and progression compared to that in single-marker models. These results suggest that cartilage and subchondral bone should be considered as a whole to more comprehensively understand the regulation of joint physiology and the development of pathological changes.

This study also explored the multi-classification challenge of predicting the incidence and progression of KOA by cascading two binary classification models—a methodology rarely explored in previous studies, particularly within the musculoskeletal domain. By separating the tasks of disease incidence prediction and progression rate stratification, we reduced model complexity and better captured the temporal heterogeneity inherent to KOA. The proposed approach demonstrated strong predictive performance, achieving a micro-average AUC of 0.948 and a macro-average AUC of 0.918. However, the data of patients in the late progression group was relatively reduced in this study, which may affects the validation of the model. Meanwhile, in real clinical diagnosis and treatment, the clinical symptoms and imaging findings of patients with late progression within 4 years are easy to be confused with those without KOA, which has certain limitations. Therefore, larger, more diverse datasetsdatasets and multi-dimensional information will be needed to further validate early OA detection.

This study also has some limitations. Although our findings based on multiple substructures, confirm the critical role of osteochondral alterations in KOA progression, the knee is a complex joint comprising numerous interacting tissues. Therefore, incorporating additional relevant substructures may further enhance the predictive performance and robustness of the model. Additionally, the data in this study primarily derived from European and American populations, with insufficient representation of Asian populations. Further validation using more diverse datasets is necessary to strengthen the model’s generalization and improve its performance across various samples.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potential of a two-stage MRI-based radiomics framework for early prediction and stratification of KOA progression. Integrating radiomic features from both cartilage and subchondral bone enhances the sensitivity for early detection and progression stratification of knee osteoarthritis. The approach may offer reliable support for early clinical screening, prognostic assessment, and timely intervention and treatment in KOA.
