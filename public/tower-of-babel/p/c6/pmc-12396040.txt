Asbestos Exposure and Leukemia Incidence: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: KONSTANTINOS DODOS, TSAMPIKA-VASILEIA KALAMARA, PETROS PAPALEXIS, DIMITRIOS AP. KERAMYDAS, EFFIE G. PAPAGEORGIOU, VASILIKI EPAMEINONDAS GEORGAKOPOULOU
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimAsbestos comprises six naturally occurring fibrous minerals known for their health risks, particularly in occupational settings. This systematic review evaluates the association between asbestos exposure and leukemia incidence, synthesizing findings from various studies.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science, adhering to PRISMA guidelines. Studies included participants exposed to asbestos compared to matched controls, focusing on leukemia incidence. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsA total of 1,751,580 participants were included, with 257,572 (14.7%) exposed to asbestos. The incidence of leukemia varied across studies, ranging from 0.02% to 0.45%. Meta-analysis revealed an overall risk ratio of 1.25 (95% confidence interval=0.81-1.94) with significant heterogeneity (I2=86%), indicating no statistically significant difference between exposed and non-exposed cohorts.ConclusionThe findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between asbestos exposure and leukemia, influenced by factors such as exposure type, duration, and confounding variables like smoking. While some studies suggest a potential link, the evidence remains inconclusive, necessitating further research. This review underscores the need for high-quality studies to clarify the association between asbestos and leukemia, informing public health policies to reduce exposure risks and protect vulnerable populations.

Introduction

The term asbestos describes a collection of six naturally occurring fibrous minerals: amosite, crocidolite, tremolite, actinolite, anthophyllite, and chrysotile. These minerals are characterized by their elongated fibers, which have a length-to-width ratio of at least 3:1, often much higher. Asbestos fibers can be categorized into two main groups, amphibole and serpentine, based on their chemical composition and morphology. Amphibole asbestos, which includes amosite and crocidolite, has straight, needle-like fibers, while serpentine asbestos, represented by chrysotile, is known for its wavy structure. Chrysotile accounts for approximately 90% to 95% of all asbestos used worldwide, making it the most prevalent form in industrial applications (1).

Asbestos exposure can occur in various environments and activities, leading to significant health risks. Common sources of asbestos exposure include buildings and construction, where asbestos was widely used as a building material before the 1970s in products such as roofing, insulation, wall cladding, and flooring tiles. Even today, older buildings may still contain asbestos, posing risks during renovations or demolitions. Additionally, workers involved in mining, grinding, and bagging asbestos-containing materials (ACMs), as well as manufacturing asbestos products like asbestos cement and textiles, are at heightened risk due to inhalation of asbestos fibers. Exposure risks arise from contaminated environments, especially for individuals residing near asbestos factories or mines, as they can inhale airborne fibers. Released fibers can cause bystander exposure, affecting family members of workers who carry asbestos home on clothing or equipment. Additionally, deteriorating asbestos cement pipes can contaminate water supplies, leading to potential ingestion (2).

The incidence of asbestos-related diseases differs based on the type of exposure and the sizes of the fibers (3). When asbestos fibers settle in the terminal respiratory bronchioles and alveolar ducts, they trigger inflammation, which can activate fibroblasts, lead to collagen deposition, promote autoimmunity, and cause damage to deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) (4).

Numerous serious health issues, both malignant and non-malignant, are associated with asbestos exposure. Mesothelioma, a rare but aggressive cancer that primarily affects the pleura, is one of the malignant conditions that asbestos exposure almost exclusively causes. Lung cancer is a major concern, particularly among workers exposed to asbestos, and smoking is an additive factor to the risk (3). The non-malignant group is comprised of pneumoconiosis (asbestosis), a chronic lung disease resulting from inhaling asbestos fibers, characterized by lung fibrosis and respiratory issues, as well as other pleural diseases, including pleural effusion, pleural plaques, round atelectasis, and diffuse pleural thickening. Evidence has also linked asbestos exposure to gastrointestinal malignancies, including cancers of the esophagus, stomach, liver, and colorectal region, particularly among workers with prolonged exposure. It is important to note that diseases associated with asbestos exposure can require many years, often decades, to manifest, complicating diagnosis and management (2).

Emerging evidence suggests that asbestos may also be implicated in the development of hematological malignancies, particularly leukemia (5). Initial studies examining mortality among asbestos workers suggested an increased risk of malignancies in the hematolymphoid (HL) system. This finding prompted several case reports exploring the potential association, which proposed that chronic immune system stimulation or disruptions in immunologic function due to faulty immunoregulation related to asbestos exposure could interfere with normal hematopoiesis and potentially lead to leukemia (6).

Epidemiological studies have reported varying associations between asbestos exposure and leukemia (7), with some suggesting a significant increase in risk among occupationally exposed populations, while others present inconclusive results (6,8,9). The complexity of these relationships is compounded by factors such as the duration and intensity of exposure, the specific type of asbestos, and the presence of co-factors like smoking. Additionally, the mechanisms by which asbestos may influence leukemia risk are not yet fully elucidated (7).

Despite the global decline in asbestos use due to increasing awareness of its health hazards, it continues to be mined and utilized in several developing countries, raising concerns about ongoing exposure and its long-term health implications. Understanding the relationship between asbestos exposure and leukemia is crucial for informing public health policies and protective measures, particularly in regions where asbestos remains in use (2).

This systematic review aims to evaluate the current evidence surrounding asbestos exposure and its association with leukemia incidence, synthesizing findings from various studies to clarify this potential link. This analysis may contribute to the understanding of asbestos-related health risks and highlight the need for continued vigilance in managing exposure to this hazardous material.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review is conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (10) and the Meta-analysis Of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines (11).

Search strategy. We performed a search for articles published in the electronic databases PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science from their inception up to April 14, 2025. There were no restrictions regarding sample size, study setting, or publication language. The search strategy included both MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to the exposure (asbestos) and the outcome (leukemia). Specifically, we used MeSH terms such as “Asbestos”, “Asbestos, Amphibole”, “Asbestos, Serpentine” for the exposure, and “Leukemia”, “Leukemia, Myeloid”, “Leukemia, Lymphoid” for the outcome. Free-text terms included "asbestos", "asbestosis", "occupational asbestos exposure", "leukemia", "leukaemia", "acute myeloid leukemia", and "chronic lymphocytic leukemia". Boolean operators “OR” and “AND” were applied to combine these terms appropriately.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria. The literature search strategy was developed based on the PECO framework (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome) (12). We targeted studies involving participants of any age (P) who had been exposed to asbestos (E), in comparison to unexposed or matched control groups (C). The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of any type of leukemia (O). We excluded animal studies, case reports, case series, previously published meta-analyses (if applicable), as well as editorials, opinion pieces, and narrative reviews.

PRISMA process. A total of 332 records were identified through electronic database searches: PubMed (n=155), Cochrane Library (n=1), and Web of Science (n=176). After the removal of duplicates, 290 records remained and were screened based on title and abstract.

Following the initial screening, 272 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The remaining 18 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 14 articles were excluded for the following reasons: full text unavailable (n=2), unrelated outcomes (n=3), studies involving parental or indirect asbestos exposure (n=2), uncertain exposure classification (n=3), or inappropriate study design (n=3). Ultimately, five studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection process.

Quality assessment. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (13) was used by two independent reviewers (T-VK, KD) to evaluate the quality of the included observational studies. The studies were assessed across several broad categories: study groups, comparability between groups, and the ascertainment of either exposure or outcomes of interest. Each study could earn up to 4 stars for selection, 2 stars for comparability, and 3 stars for outcomes, with a maximum possible score of 9 stars. Any differing opinions among reviewers were addressed through discussion, consensus, or arbitration by a third senior reviewer (VEG). The results of the quality assessment are available in Table I.

Data extraction. Data extraction from the eligible reports was performed independently by the same two reviewers (T-VK, KD). Relevant information was recorded on a data collection form developed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The extracted data included details such as the first author, year of study, country of origin, participants’ number, age, and sex, job/task, smoking habits, type of asbestos fibers, method of asbestos exposure assessment, average exposure level, prevalence of leukemia, and type of leukemia.

Data synthesis and analysis. We aimed to evaluate the primary clinical endpoint (leukemia development), which is a dichotomous variable, and thus estimated the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To generate pooled estimates of the outcome, we used the Mantel-Haenszel (M-H) random effects formula. The I2 statistic was employed to assess the degree of statistical heterogeneity in the meta-analysis attributable to differences between studies. Low heterogeneity was indicated by an I2 of 0 to 25%, moderate by 25 to 50%, and high if I2 exceeded 75%. Forest plots were created to visually represent the presence and nature of statistical heterogeneity (14). All analyses were conducted at the 0.05 statistical significance level, using a rapid meta-analysis tool for epidemiological studies by Fekete et al. (15).

Various types of asbestos were assessed, including chrysotile, blue asbestos, and a mix of chrysotile, amosite, and crocidolite for both environmental and occupational exposure. The studies employed different methods for assessing asbestos exposure, such as interviews, Transmission Electron Microscopy, cumulative methods based on historical measurements, questionnaires, and job exposure matrices combined with employment histories.

Exposure durations varied, with some studies reporting long-term exposure [>50 years in Polissar et al. (16) and >20 years in Sadler et al. (18)], while others had shorter durations, averaging approximately 19 months. Average exposure levels ranged widely, from 206.5 ± 152.2 fibers × 104/l to 50-800 fibers/ml/year. The number of participants varied significantly across studies, from 1,393 in the Italian study to over 1.3 million in the U.S. study by Polissar et al. (16) Most participants were adults aged 35 and older, with a mean age of approximately 48.5 years in Reid et al.'s study (17).

Sex representation was noted in several studies, with a nearly equal distribution in the Danish study (50.3% males and 49.7% females) and a similar proportion in the U.S. studies, although some studies did not specify sex distribution. Various types of leukemia were reported, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), and others. The incidence of leukemia varied, with the highest found in the occupational exposure group in Denmark (0.41% among exposed participants). Notably, smoking adjustment was included in only one study (17), indicating a potential limitation in the ability to control for confounding factors in the other studies.

Meta-analysis. Altogether, five studies were examined with a total of 257,572 subjects in asbestos exposed cohort and 1,494,008 subjects in the cohort without asbestos exposure. The corresponding forest plot is shown in Figure 2. Based on the analysis performed using random effects model with the Mantel-Haenszel method to compare the risk ratio, there is no statistically significant difference between the two cohorts; the overall risk ratio (RR) is 1.25 with a 95% confidence interval (95%CI) of 0.81-1.94. The test for overall effect did not show a significant effect. A significant heterogeneity was noted (p<0.01), pointing at uneven effects in extent and/or direction. The I2 statistic was 86%, indicating that most of the variability in the results is due to heterogeneity rather than chance. The funnel plot (Figure 3) does not indicate potential publication bias. The Egger's test does not support the presence of funnel plot asymmetry (intercept=1.47, 95%CI=-7.26-10.2, t=0.331, p-value=0.763).

Incidence of leukemia. In the present systematic review, the incidence of leukemia among participants exposed to asbestos varies significantly across the included studies. In the study by Polissar et al. (16), which analyzed chrysotile exposure using Transmission Electron Microscopy, the incidence of leukemia among exposed individuals was found to be 0.02%. This low incidence, despite a large sample size of over 1.3 million participants, suggests that while there may be some association with asbestos exposure, other factors could influence leukemia risk. Conversely, Reid et al. (17) reported a higher incidence of 0.45% among those exposed to blue asbestos over an average exposure duration of approximately 19 months. This study indicates a stronger link between shorter-term exposure and increased leukemia risk, potentially highlighting the impact of fiber type and exposure duration on disease incidence. Additionally, the study by Sadler et al. (18) found an incidence of 0.11% among exposed participants, further illustrating the variability in leukemia incidence across different populations and exposure assessments. The differences in reported incidence rates underscore the complexities in establishing a direct relationship between asbestos exposure and leukemia, suggesting that factors such as exposure duration, fiber type, and study methodology play significant roles in influencing outcomes. Overall, the findings from these studies highlight the need for careful interpretation of leukemia incidence in the context of asbestos exposure, as well as the importance of considering various contributing factors when assessing the risks associated with different types of asbestos and exposure scenarios.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive evaluation of the current evidence regarding the association between asbestos exposure and leukemia incidence. Our result indicates no statistically significant difference in leukemia incidence between the asbestos-exposed cohort and the non-exposed cohort. Furthermore, the lack of significant findings, despite the observed risk ratio suggesting a potential increase in leukemia risk, underscores the complexities surrounding the relationship between asbestos and hematological malignancies. Factors such as the duration and intensity of exposure, the type of asbestos involved, and the presence of confounding variables could all influence outcomes.

The incidence of leukemia among exposed individuals in the studies reviewed ranged from 0.02% to 0.45%. Polissar et al. (16) reported a low incidence of 0.02% in a large cohort exposed to chrysotile, suggesting that while there may be an association with asbestos exposure, other factors could significantly influence leukemia risk. In contrast, Reid et al. (17) documented a higher incidence of 0.45% among those exposed to blue asbestos (crocidolite), indicating that the type of asbestos and the duration of exposure may play crucial roles in determining risk. The higher incidence reported in this study could reflect the more aggressive nature of blue asbestos compared to chrysotile, highlighting the need for further investigation into the specific risks associated with different asbestos types.

Sadler et al. (18) also found an incidence of 0.11%, further illustrating the inconsistencies across studies. These variations emphasize the importance of considering factors such as exposure duration, fiber type, and the methodologies employed in assessing exposure when interpreting the results. Additionally, the lack of uniformity in reporting and classifying leukemia subtypes in some studies complicates the ability to draw definitive conclusions about the relationship between asbestos exposure and specific hematological malignancies.

From a mechanistic standpoint, asbestos exposure has long been recognized for its carcinogenic effects on mesothelial and pulmonary tissues. Its implication in hematological malignancies, although less established, is biologically plausible. Asbestos fibers can incite chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to DNA strand breaks, genomic instability, and alterations in the bone marrow microenvironment. These effects may disrupt normal hematopoiesis and immune regulation, ultimately promoting malignant transformation of hematopoietic progenitors. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that asbestos can impair immune cell function and produce reactive oxygen species that damage DNA, contributing to a genotoxic environment conducive to leukemogenesis (19-22). Although translocation of fibers from the respiratory tract to systemic sites like the bone marrow has not been definitively proven in humans, animal studies suggest that this may be possible, particularly with thinner and more bio-persistent fibers (23).

Notably, the absence of chemoprevention strategies for hematologic malignancies mirrors a broader gap in asbestos-related cancer prevention. As highlighted by Neri et al. (24), despite the well-established carcinogenicity of asbestos and the long latency of related diseases, no new chemoprevention trials have been initiated in over two decades, even for better-studied outcomes like mesothelioma and lung cancer. This underscores the need to explore preventive strategies and early intervention approaches not only for solid tumors but also for rarer outcomes such as leukemia, particularly in high-risk, previously exposed populations (8).

The lack of stratification by leukemia subtype in the majority of included studies presents a significant limitation to the interpretability of findings. Leukemia encompasses a heterogeneous group of malignancies, including AML, CLL, and ALL, each with distinct etiologies and susceptibility profiles. AML has been linked to environmental carcinogens such as benzene and ionizing radiation, raising the possibility that asbestos might exert differential effects depending on hematopoietic lineage and the type of fiber involved (8). The absence of subtype-specific data in most studies limits our ability to discern whether certain types of leukemia are more closely associated with asbestos exposure.

Another critical consideration is the role of confounding variables. While Reid et al. (17) adjusted for smoking in their analysis, the remaining studies did not, introducing potential bias given the established link between tobacco use and both respiratory and hematologic malignancies. Moreover, several occupational cohorts included in these studies may have had co-exposures to other leukemogenic agents, such as benzene, solvents, or radiation, which were not consistently reported or controlled. This oversight could mask or exaggerate the true effect of asbestos exposure on leukemia risk and may partly explain the heterogeneity observed.

Importantly, none of the included studies provided robust evidence on the existence of a dose-response relationship between asbestos exposure and leukemia. While studies on mesothelioma and lung cancer have shown strong associations with cumulative fiber exposure, such associations have not been replicated in the context of hematologic malignancies. The lack of detailed exposure quantification-especially in terms of duration, intensity, and fiber type-limits the ability to draw conclusions about threshold effects or the influence of cumulative burden on leukemia development. Given the latency and dose-dependent nature of many asbestos-related diseases, future research should employ more refined exposure assessment methods, including fiber-years, environmental concentration monitoring, and histological quantification of fiber deposition.

It is also noteworthy that asbestos has been investigated in the context of other hematologic malignancies beyond leukemia. Several epidemiologic reviews have suggested possible links between asbestos and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma or multiple myeloma, albeit with conflicting evidence (9,25). Asbestos may exert systemic immunotoxic effects capable of influencing multiple hematologic lineages, and it is plausible that the spectrum of asbestos-related hematologic diseases is broader than currently appreciated (26,27). Integrative studies evaluating multiple hematologic outcomes in the same cohort would offer more comprehensive insights.

The implications of our findings are significant from a public health perspective. Although asbestos use has declined in many high-income countries due to regulatory bans and increasing awareness of its health hazards, it remains in active use in several developing nations where occupational safety measures are often limited (2,27). The continued mining and industrial application of asbestos in these regions expose millions to potential long-term risks. Even in countries with strict regulations, legacy exposure from old buildings and infrastructure poses ongoing threats, particularly to construction workers and bystanders. The findings of this review, although inconclusive regarding causality, reinforce the need for continued surveillance of asbestos-exposed populations, especially with respect to rare or underreported malignancies like leukemia.

Methodologically, this review is constrained by the limited number of eligible studies, the predominance of retrospective designs, and the variability in exposure and outcome assessment. The reliance on job-exposure matrices or participant recall for asbestos classification increases the risk of exposure misclassification. Moreover, the lack of uniform reporting of leukemia subtypes and the underreporting of smoking or co-exposures introduce additional layers of uncertainty. While the meta-analysis applied rigorous statistical methods, including random-effects modeling and evaluation for publication bias, the high degree of heterogeneity limits the confidence in the pooled estimate.

In light of these limitations, future research must adopt prospective cohort designs with detailed occupational histories and biomarker-based exposure validation. Studies should stratify results by leukemia subtype, asbestos fiber type, and exposure route (e.g., inhalational vs. environmental). Advances in molecular epidemiology may allow for exploration of gene-environment interactions, including the role of genetic polymorphisms in detoxification pathways that may modify susceptibility to asbestos-induced hematologic malignancies. Finally, international collaboration and data sharing will be crucial to assembling large, well-characterized cohorts necessary to investigate rare outcomes such as leukemia. Proposed molecular mechanisms linking asbestos exposure to leukemogenesis. This schematic illustrates the hypothesized molecular and cellular pathways by which asbestos exposure may contribute to leukemia development. Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a critical appraisal of the current evidence linking asbestos exposure to leukemia risk. While the pooled risk ratio suggests a potential association, the lack of statistical significance, high heterogeneity among studies, and limited control for confounders highlight the need for cautious interpretation. Variability in exposure assessment methods, fiber types, and insufficient stratification by leukemia subtype further complicate the picture. Despite these limitations, the biological plausibility of asbestos-induced leukemogenesis-via chronic inflammation, genotoxicity, and immune dysregulation-warrants further investigation. Future research should prioritize prospective, subtype-specific studies with detailed exposure quantification and control for co-exposures. Given the persistence of asbestos use in certain regions and the latency of related malignancies, ongoing surveillance and precautionary public health policies remain essential to mitigate potential long-term hematologic risks.
