Left ventricular non-compaction in a hypermobile adolescent harboring a novel troponin I (TNNI3) variant (p.K106N): a case report

Introduction

Left ventricular non-compaction (LVNC) is a distinctive cardiomyopathy characterized by prominent trabeculation, deep intertrabecular recesses, and a hallmark non-compacted to compacted myocardial ratio >2:1, reflecting incomplete myocardial compaction during embryogenesis.1 The American Heart Association classifies LVNC as a primary genetic cardiomyopathy, with identifiable genetic causes in approximately 20%–40% of patients, most commonly involving sarcomeric or cytoskeletal genes such as MYH7, MYBPC3, and TTN.2 Despite this broad genetic landscape, only one prior case has linked LVNC to troponin I (TNNI3).3

TNNI3 encodes cardiac troponin I and is usually associated with hypertrophic or restrictive cardiomyopathy and only rarely with LVNC.4 LVNC has been reported almost exclusively in vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (EDS), not in hypermobile EDS (hEDS).5,6 The coexistence of hEDS with an already rare LVNC–TNNI3 association makes this case particularly unusual and raises questions about overlapping phenotypic expression.

Case presentation

A 17-year-old female with joint hypermobility and chronic musculoskeletal pain was evaluated after a right shoulder subluxation. Physical findings raised concern for hEDS, prompting a screening transthoracic echocardiogram whose results led to referral to paediatric cardiology. Her past medical history was otherwise unremarkable aside from a motor vehicle accident and right elbow fracture 11 years earlier that required open reduction and pinning. Family history was notable for hypermobility in her mother and maternal grandmother, neither of whom had undergone formal evaluation. She reported intermittent dizziness, palpitations, brief chest discomfort, and early fatigue.

On examination, she appeared comfortable and well perfused, with a blood pressure of 98/66 mmHg and a resting heart rate of 98 beats/min. Orthostatic vital signs showed a 53 beats/min increase from supine to standing without hypotension. Beighton score was 7/9, exceeding the adolescent threshold for generalized joint hypermobility, with skin hyperextensibility on the pinch test, elbow hyperextension beyond 180°, and passive fifth-finger dorsiflexion beyond 90° (Figure 1); there was no evidence of skin fragility or abnormal scarring. The cardiovascular examination was otherwise normal. The constellation of findings was considered clinically suggestive of hEDS.

Physical examination findings consistent with a hypermobile Ehlers–Danlos phenotype. (A) Passive thumb apposition to the forearm. (B) Skin hyperextensibility (note linear scar from prior elbow fracture). (C) Elbow hyperextension. (D) Passive fifth-digit hyperextension >90°.

The screening echocardiogram demonstrated a spongiform left ventricle with deep apical septal and lateral myocardial crypts and a non-compacted layer exceeding 50% of the compacted epicardium, consistent with LVNC rather than the anticipated connective-tissue findings (Figure 2; Supplementary material online, Video S1A–C). Colour flow imaging demonstrated flow from the LV cavity into the deep intertrabecular recesses within the non-compacted apical and lateral segments. LV size was normal (end-diastolic dimension 4.9 cm, Z −0.4; end-systolic dimension 3.76 cm, Z + 1.38; left atrium 3.6 cm, LA-to-aortic-root ratio 1.2), with mildly reduced systolic function (biplane Simpson ejection fraction [EF] 41%, fractional shortening 21.1%, global longitudinal strain −11.1%) and grade 1 diastolic dysfunction. Right ventricular function, valvular anatomy, and aortic dimensions were normal. Electrocardiogram showed normal sinus rhythm with non-specific T-wave abnormality (Figure 3), and a 48-h Holter monitor showed no evidence of arrhythmia. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing revealed reduced peak oxygen uptake (77% predicted), an early anaerobic threshold, and a markedly elevated VE/VCO2 slope of 41.1, consistent with impaired cardiopulmonary efficiency and early cardiomyopathic physiology rather than deconditioning or isolated dysautonomia; oxygen saturation and oxygen-pulse dynamics suggested a preserved stroke-volume response.

12-Lead electrocardiography showing normal sinus rhythm with non-specific T-wave abnormality.

Given symptomatic LV systolic dysfunction, she was initiated on enalapril and carvedilol. Low-dose aspirin was preferred over anticoagulation given mildly reduced EF, absent thrombus, sinus rhythm, and easy bruising, with planned escalation if dysfunction persisted. At 3-month follow-up, symptoms had modestly improved, and repeat echocardiography demonstrated improved systolic function (biplane Simpson EF 55%; Supplementary material online, Video S2A–C) and global longitudinal strain (−17.4%); aspirin was therefore continued. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging at 6 months following initiation of therapy confirmed LVNC by Petersen criteria (non-compacted-to-compacted ratio 2.51:1 in the distal LV lateral wall; Figure 4A–C) and showed normalized LV volumes, function, and mass (LVEF 60%, LVEDVi 84 mL/m2, LVESVi 29 mL/m2, LV mass index 47 g/m2), preserved myocardial perfusion, and no late gadolinium enhancement.

Cardiac MRI confirmed the diagnosis of left ventricular non-compaction cardiomyopathy with a non-compacted-to-compacted ratio of 2.51:1 in the distal left ventricular lateral wall: (A) parasternal short-axis view; (B) apical four-chamber view; (C) apical two-chamber view.

Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous TNNI3 variant (c.318 G > C; p.K106N), classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS). This missense change replaces a conserved lysine residue at position 106 with asparagine, within a critical regulatory domain of cardiac troponin I that mediates calcium-dependent inhibition of actomyosin ATPase activity. No pathogenic variants were detected in other LVNC-associated genes, and no abnormalities were identified in connective-tissue genes. Cascade genetic testing identified the same variant in the patient's mother, for whom a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiogram has been recommended.

Discussion

This case presents an unusually rare coexistence of LVNC and a TNNI3 variant, with a concurrent hEDS phenotype adding complexity to a genotype-phenotype association not previously reported.

The genetic basis of LVNC is broad, most commonly involving sarcomeric or cytoskeletal genes such as MYH7, MYBPC3, and TTN, with additional contributions from TNNT2, ACTC, TAZ, DTNA, LMNA, LDB3, DMD, and HCN4.2 Despite this diversity, only one prior report has linked LVNC to a TNNI3 variant.3

TNNI3 encodes the inhibitory component of the troponin complex, regulating thin-filament activation in response to intracellular calcium.4 Variants in TNNI3 account for fewer than 5% of inherited cardiomyopathies and are most frequently associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, with fewer reports in restrictive and dilated phenotypes.5–7 Most pathogenic variants cluster in exons 7 and 8; the previously reported LVNC-associated variant involved an Arg192His allele.3 The variant identified in our patient, p.K106N, lies outside this established hotspot within the troponin T-binding region. Although currently classified as a VUS, it affects a conserved residue within a functional regulatory domain (residues 80–136) important for troponin I positioning.8 While this exact substitution has not been previously described, its presence in a patient with clear structural and physiological evidence of LVNC raises the possibility of a broader TNNI3 phenotypic spectrum than currently appreciated.

Clinically, LVNC spans a wide spectrum, from incidental hypertrabeculation with preserved function to overt cardiomyopathy characterized by systolic impairment and arrhythmic risk. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines emphasize that LVNC should be interpreted as a phenotypic trait requiring integration of morphology with ventricular function, symptoms, and objective functional assessment.9 In our patient, the combination of symptoms, ventricular dysfunction, impaired global longitudinal strain, and abnormal cardiopulmonary exercise performance supports genuine myocardial involvement rather than incidental hypertrabeculation. Here, the rare p.K106N TNNI3 finding most plausibly explains ventricular dysfunction, with hypermobility as a coincidental coexisting phenotype.

LVNC has been described in association with EDS, but almost exclusively in the vascular subtype related to COL3A1 mutations.10,11 Hypermobile EDS, which best matches this patient’s clinical features, is not known to be associated with intrinsic cardiomyopathy, and cardiovascular involvement typically includes valvular, aortic, or autonomic involvement. That said, myocardial compaction is influenced by several developmental processes, including sarcomeric maturation, extracellular matrix structure, and mechanical signalling. Experimental studies suggest that changes in matrix organization or mechanotransduction can affect how the ventricle compacts during development.12 While this has not been specifically shown in hEDS, it raises the possibility that connective tissue differences could influence the expression of an underlying sarcomeric process.

Antithrombotic strategy in LVNC remains debated. Consensus favours anticoagulation with significantly reduced EF, intracardiac thrombus, atrial fibrillation, or prior thromboembolism—none of which applied here: EF was only mildly reduced (Simpson 41%, above the <35%–40% threshold), imaging showed no thrombus, sinus rhythm was present, and there was no prior embolic event. Weighed against bleeding risk from easy bruising and connective-tissue laxity, low-dose aspirin was initiated with planned escalation to anticoagulation if dysfunction persisted. With function and strain improving at 3 months, aspirin was continued; reassessment is planned should EF decline, atrial arrhythmia develop, or thrombus arise.

In this patient, the combination of LVNC with the rare p.K106N TNNI3 variant remains the most convincing explanation for her cardiomyopathic physiology. The hypermobile phenotype likely represents a separate but clinically relevant comorbidity, and their coexistence highlights how multiple biological factors may overlap in ways not yet fully understood. This case highlights an uncommon presentation and contributes to the expanding understanding of TNNI3-related cardiomyopathy in young patients.

Conclusions

This case highlights an exceptionally rare association of LVNC and a TNNI3 variant in an adolescent with hypermobile feature, with objective physiological findings supporting clinically meaningful myocardial involvement and the need for guideline-directed phenotyping.

Patient perspective

The patient and her family appreciated understanding the genetic basis and rarity of the diagnosis and the importance of long-term follow-up and surveillance.
