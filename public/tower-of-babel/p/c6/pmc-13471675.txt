The relationship between serum uric acid and gallbladder stones in Korean women: a cross-sectional study of 19,329 cases
Authors: Hoonsub So, Young-Jee Jeon, Doo-Ho Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGallbladder stones represent a common gastrointestinal disorder influenced by various metabolic and lifestyle-related risk factors. Serum uric acid (UA), a byproduct of purine metabolism, has been suggested as a potential contributor to gallstone formation, but the association remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum UA levels and gallbladder stone prevalence in Korean women.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, cross-sectional study of 19,329 Korean women who underwent abdominal ultrasonography as part of general health examinations. Participants were categorized into quartiles based on their serum UA levels. Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between serum UA levels and gallbladder stone prevalence. Adjustments were made for potential confounders, including age, body mass index, lifestyle factors, and metabolic parameters.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 48.4 ± 10.3 years, with a mean serum UA level of 4.23 ± 0.91 mg/dL. The prevalence of gallbladder stones increased significantly across serum UA quartiles, from 4.0% in the lowest quartile to 6.0% in the highest quartile (p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest quartile had a significantly higher risk of gallbladder stones compared to those in the lowest quartile (OR: 1.430, 95% CI: 1.174–1.740, p < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated serum UA levels were significantly associated with an increased prevalence of gallbladder stones in Korean women. This association remained significant after adjustment for multiple potential confounders. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between serum UA and gallstone development and to determine the clinical significance of this association.

Introduction

Gallbladder stones are among the most common gastrointestinal disorders worldwide and represent an important public health concern [1]. Gallstone disease is clinically important because it can lead to several complications, including acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, and an increased risk of hepatobiliary and pancreatic malignancies [2, 3]. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that chronic inflammation plays an important role in gallstone pathogenesis by promoting alterations in bile composition and gallbladder function [4]. The formation of gallstones is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic predisposition, metabolic status, and lifestyle behaviors [5]. In particular, metabolic abnormalities such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia are well-established risk factors for gallstone formation [6, 7]. Recent evidence suggests that serum uric acid (UA), a key metabolic byproduct, may also contribute to gallstone development [8].

UA, the final metabolite of purine degradation in humans, has traditionally been linked to gout and renal disorders [9]. Recent studies, however, have identified a correlation between elevated serum UA levels and various metabolic disorders, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension [10–12]. Furthermore, elevated serum UA levels have been associated with a variety of inflammatory and cardiometabolic conditions, suggesting that UA may serve not only as a metabolic marker but also as a mediator of systemic inflammation. Hyperuricemia is known to promote oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and insulin resistance- mechanisms [9], all of which are biological mechanisms that may contribute to gallstone formation. Despite these biological connections, the relationship between serum UA levels and the formation of gallbladder stones remains ambiguous, particularly in women, who possess distinct metabolic and hormonal profiles that influence gallstone risk.

Limited research has specifically explored the association between serum UA levels and gallbladder stones, with existing studies often constrained by small sample sizes or inadequate adjustments for potential confounding variables [8]. Additionally, while previous research has predominantly concentrated on metabolic risk factors such as obesity, insulin resistance, and lipid profiles [5, 6], the potential contribution of serum UA has been comparatively underexplored. Given its close association with metabolic syndrome and oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, all of which are implicated in gallstone formation, further investigation into the role of serum UA in gallstone disease is warranted.

Given that women exhibit a higher prevalence of gallstones and possess distinct hormonal and metabolic characteristics that may influence gallstone pathogenesis, we focused our analysis on women to reduce biological heterogeneity and better isolate the potential contribution of serum UA to gallstone formation in this subgroup. Therefore, we conducted a large-scale observational study to evaluate the association between serum UA levels and gallbladder stone prevalence in Korean women.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a retrospective, cross-sectional approach, involving 24,545 female participants who underwent abdominal ultrasonography as part of a general health examination at a tertiary referral hospital between March 2014 and March 2020. Of these, 5,216 participants were excluded based on the following criteria: presence of fatty liver disease on abdominal sonography (n = 4,511), a previous history of cholecystectomy (n = 615), insufficient medical records (n = 87), or missing serum UA measurements (n = 3). To reduce potential confounding, women with fatty liver disease identified on abdominal sonography were excluded, given its close metabolic relationship with both hyperuricemia and gallstone disease. After these exclusions, a total of 19,329 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Clinical and laboratory measurements

Electronic medical records were accessed through the institutional electronic health record platform. This system was utilized to gather detailed clinical and laboratory information for all study participants. Anthropometric measurements, including height, body weight, and blood pressure, were obtained during routine health examinations following standardized protocols [13]. Blood specimens collected after an overnight fast underwent comprehensive analysis. All measurements were performed using the Roche Cobas c702 automated analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols and standardized clinical laboratory procedures. The examination encompassed counts of white blood cells, hemoglobin levels, and platelet numbers. Biochemical parameters assessed included total bilirubin, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). The lipid panel consisted of total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides. Additional measurements included UA, total calcium, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Diabetes was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, or a self-reported history of diabetes or treatment with dietary modifications or antidiabetic medication. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or a self-reported history of hypertension or treatment with antihypertensive medication. Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL or a self-reported history of hyperlipidemia or treatment with anti-hyperlipidemic medications [13].

Definition of physical activity

A survey was administered to assess participants’ physical activity levels in accordance with the World Health Organization’s Global Recommendations on Physical Activity for Health 2010 [14]. Individuals were categorized as physically active if they performed moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity for at least 150 min, or vigorous-intensity activity for at least 75 min throughout the week with aerobic activity comprising at least 10 min duration.

Identification of gallbladder stones

Specialized radiologists conducted ultrasound scans using advanced, high-resolution equipment. Participants were required to fast for a minimum of 8 h prior to the abdominal examination. The presence of gallbladder stones was determined based on the presence of echogenic and acoustic shadows and echo movement within the gallbladder that corresponded to changes in participant positioning [15].

Statistical analysis

Participants were stratified into quartiles according to their serum UA levels (Fig. 1). Because several participants shared identical serum UA values at the quartile boundaries, quartile thresholds were defined to avoid assigning identical values to different categories. Consequently, slight differences in group size occurred across quartiles while preserving consistent categorization of participants with the same serum UA values. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies with percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as means and standard deviations. Between-group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for numerical variables, as appropriate. Univariable and multivariable analyses were performed using a logistic regression model to analyze the association between SUA and gallbladder stone on ultrasonography. The selection of covariates for the multivariate analysis was included by both clinical significance and statistical relevance. The covariates were adjusted gradually in four models. The initial model, Model 1, made no adjustments. Model 2 incorporated corrections for age and body mass index (BMI). Building upon this, Model 3 included additional adjustments for lifestyle and clinical factors, such as alcohol consumption, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. The final model, Model 4, expanded the analysis further by integrating various metabolic and biochemical markers, including hemoglobin, albumin, total bilirubin, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, GFR, and serum calcium level. Multi-collinearity among covariates included in the multivariable models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and no evidence of significant multicollinearity was observed (all VIF values < 2.0). Unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for the logistic regression were calculated. All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data manipulation and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 24; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

The mean age of the 19,329 participants was 48.4 ± 10.3 years, with a mean serum UA level of 4.23 ± 0.91 mg/dL. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants, stratified by serum UA quartiles. The serum UA quartiles were defined as follows: Quartile 1 (≤ 3.60 mg/dL), Quartile 2 (3.61–4.20 mg/dL), Quartile 3 (4.21–4.70 mg/dL), and Quartile 4 (≥ 4.71 mg/dL). Participants in the highest serum UA quartile were significantly older than those in the lower quartiles (p < 0.001). BMI was notably higher in the upper UA quartiles compared to Quartile 1. The prevalence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia increased with higher UA quartiles. No significant differences were observed between UA quartiles concerning physical activity and alcohol consumption. Moreover, levels of white blood cells, hemoglobin, platelet count, and liver enzymes (AST, ALT) were significantly elevated in the higher UA quartiles. Total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides also exhibited a significant upward trend with increasing UA levels (all p < 0.001).

Figure 2 illustrates the prevalence of gallbladder stones across serum UA quartiles. The prevalence of gallbladder stones increased with higher serum UA levels, rising from 4.0% in Quartile 1 to 6.0% in Quartile 4. This trend was statistically significant, with a p-value of < 0.001, indicating a positive association between elevated serum UA levels and the increased prevalence of gallbladder stones.

Table 2 presents the association between serum UA levels and the risk of gallbladder stones according to the quartiles of serum UA. In unadjusted Model 1, the odds ratio (OR) for gallbladder stones in Quartile 2 was 1.092 (95% CI: 0.898–1.328, p = 0.376), in Quartile 3 was 1.207 (95% CI: 0.991–1.470, p = 0.062), and in Quartile 4 was 1.527 (95% CI: 1.271–1.834, p < 0.001). Upon adjusting for age and BMI in Model 2, the OR for Quartile 4 remained significant (OR: 1.395, 95% CI: 1.159–1.680, p < 0.001). In the Model 3, which accounted for lifestyle and clinical factors such as alcohol use, physical activity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, the association for Quartile 4 persisted as significant (OR: 1.352, 95% CI: 1.122–1.629, p = 0.002). Similarly, in Model 4, which further adjusted for various metabolic and biochemical markers including hemoglobin, albumin, total bilirubin, triglyceride, HDL cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c, GFR, and serum calcium, the OR for Quartile 4 remained significant (OR: 1.430, 95% CI: 1.174–1.740, p < 0.001). Furthermore, a significant positive trend was observed across increasing serum UA quartiles in the fully adjusted model (p for trend < 0.001).

Discussion

This study is one of the first large-scale investigations analyzing the association between serum UA levels and the prevalence of gallbladder stones in 19,329 Korean women. The findings demonstrated that higher serum UA levels were significantly associated with an increased prevalence of gallbladder stones. Specifically, the prevalence of gallbladder stones was highest in Quartile 4, compared to Quartile 1. Multivariable logistic regression analysis further confirmed that, even after adjusting for potential confounders, participants in Quartile 4 had a significantly higher risk of developing gallbladder stones.

The association between hyperuricemia and gallbladder stones is consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of UA in metabolic disorders. Prior research has demonstrated that hyperuricemia is closely linked to conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia [7, 10–12], all of which are well-established risk factors for gallstone formation. A study based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey in the United States similarly reported that higher serum UA levels were associated with an increased prevalence of gallstones and suggested a nonlinear dose-response relationship [8]. In addition, recent NHANES-based studies have demonstrated that uric acid-related metabolic indices, including the uric acid-to-creatinine ratio and the uric acid-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, are independently associated with increased gallstone risk, particularly among women [16, 17]. These findings collectively support an association between uric acid-related metabolic markers and gallstone disease and further suggest a potential role of uric acid metabolism in gallstone development. However, the majority of prior research has been conducted within Western populations, and several studies relied on self-reported gallstone diagnoses. In contrast, our study provides novel evidence in an East Asian population, specifically Korean women. Furthermore, gallbladder stones in our study were identified using abdominal ultrasonography, which may provide greater diagnostic accuracy than questionnaire-based assessments. Considering potential ethnic and dietary differences in gallstone pathophysiology, these findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between UA and gallstone disease across diverse populations.

Several biological mechanisms may elucidate the observed association between elevated UA levels and gallstone formation. One possible explanation is the role of UA in oxidative stress and chronic inflammation [9], both of which are implicated in bile super-saturation and gallstone pathogenesis. Excess UA can generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may lead to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, ultimately promoting cholesterol crystal formation in bile [18, 19]. Additionally, UA is known to play a key role in insulin resistance by impairing endothelial nitric oxide production and promoting adipocyte dysfunction [20]. Insulin resistance, in turn, has been shown to decrease gallbladder motility and delay bile emptying, creating conditions that favor gallstone formation [21]. Furthermore, hyperuricemia is often associated with dyslipidemia, particularly elevated triglycerides and low HDL cholesterol [22], which can contribute to an increased cholesterol saturation index in bile. These metabolic disturbances collectively create a pro-lithogenic environment, supporting the hypothesis that UA may actively contribute to gallstone formation rather than serving as a mere bystander marker of metabolic dysfunction.

Our study focused exclusively on women, underscoring the necessity of considering sex-specific factors in the formation of gallstones. It is well established that women have a higher prevalence of gallstones compared to men, a disparity primarily attributed to hormonal influences [5]. Estrogen has been shown to increase cholesterol secretion into bile and reduce gallbladder motility, both of which facilitate gallstone formation [23]. Furthermore, progesterone may exacerbate gallbladder contractility impairment, resulting in bile stasis [24]. These hormonal effects may be particularly pertinent for postmenopausal women, who frequently undergo metabolic changes, including elevated serum UA levels. The interaction between serum UA and hormonal changes in women could further heighten the risk of gallstones, rendering this an important area for future investigation.

The strong association between serum UA and gallbladder stones observed in this study may also reflect the broader relationship between serum UA and metabolic syndrome [10]. Participants in the highest serum UA quartile had higher BMI and an increased prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, all of which are metabolic risk factors for gallstones [5]. This suggests that serum UA may not function as an isolated risk factor but rather as a marker of underlying metabolic dysfunction that predisposes individuals to gallstone formation. The modest magnitude of the observed association further supports the interpretation that serum UA may represent one component of a broader metabolic risk profile rather than a dominant causal factor in gallstone formation. Interestingly, serum UA levels were also positively associated with physical activity and inversely associated with fasting blood glucose in our cohort. The positive association with physical activity may reflect the relatively healthier metabolic status and greater muscle mass of physically active individuals, both of which can influence purine turnover and serum UA concentrations [25]. In contrast, the inverse association with fasting blood glucose may be related to the uricosuric effect of glycosuria in individuals with higher glucose levels, which can increase renal uric acid excretion and lower circulating UA concentrations [26]. Similar paradoxical associations have been reported in previous metabolic studies and highlight the complex interplay between serum UA and metabolic health [27].

Meanwhile, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, is closely linked to both hyperuricemia and gallstone disease through shared metabolic pathways [28, 29]. In our study, patients with fatty liver were excluded to reduce confounding, but MASLD remains an important factor to consider in future research on UA and gallstone risk. Taking together, given the established links between serum UA and metabolic disorders, it is plausible that reducing serum UA levels through lifestyle modifications or pharmacological interventions could have beneficial effects not only on cardiovascular and metabolic health but also on gallstone prevention. However, whether serum UA reduction directly influences gallstone risk remains to be determined, necessitating further prospective studies.

From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that serum UA levels could serve as a useful biomarker for identifying individuals at elevated risk of gallbladder stones. Given that serum UA is routinely measured in clinical practice, it could be easily integrated into risk prediction models to guide preventive strategies. Moreover, considering the association between serum UA and various metabolic disorders [10], interventions aimed at reducing serum UA levels, such as dietary modifications, increased physical activity, and the use of UA-lowering agents, may offer potential benefits in mitigating gallstone risk. In our study, serum UA levels exceeding 4.71 mg/dL (the highest quartile) were significantly associated with increased odds of gallbladder stones, indicating a potential threshold for elevated risk. This finding aligns with prior evidence linking elevated serum UA to metabolic disturbances and gallstone formation [8, 10]. However, further prospective studies are needed to validate clinically applicable serum UA cutoffs for gallstone risk across diverse populations, considering that UA levels may vary by ethnicity and environmental factors. Additionally, future research should explore whether lowering serum UA can effectively reduce gallstone risk and identify precise thresholds beyond which risk significantly increases.

While our study possesses notable strengths, such as a substantial sample size and comprehensive multivariable adjustments, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the cross-sectional design inhibits the establishment of a causal relationship between serum UA and gallstone formation. Longitudinal studies are necessary to ascertain whether UA directly contributes to gallstone development or if the observed association is influenced by underlying metabolic factors. Secondly, all participants were recruited from a health screening cohort at a tertiary referral hospital. Individuals who voluntarily undergo health examinations may differ from the general population with respect to health awareness, healthcare utilization, socioeconomic characteristics, and underlying health status. Therefore, the possibility of selection bias cannot be excluded, and caution is warranted when generalizing our findings to the broader population. Thirdly, the study population comprised exclusively Korean women. While this approach enhances internal validity by reducing biological heterogeneity, given sex-specific and ethnic differences in gallstone pathophysiology, it may also restrict the generalizability of our findings. Further studies are warranted to determine whether the observed association between serum uric acid and gallstones is consistent in men and in other ethnic or racial groups. Forthly, the analysis did not incorporate dietary intake, a critical factor in both UA metabolism and gallstone formation. In addition, information regarding medications that may influence serum UA levels, including thiazide diuretics, low-dose aspirin, and other uric acid-altering agents, was not available. Consequently, residual confounding related to medication use cannot be excluded. Additionally, the absence of data on hormonal factors, such as menopausal status and hormone replacement therapy, represents another limitation, as these variables may affect both serum UA levels and gallstone risk. Furthermore, serum UA was measured only once at the time of the health examination and therefore may not accurately reflect long-term UA exposure. Gallbladder stones were identified using routine abdominal ultrasonography, which may not reliably detect biliary sludge or microlithiasis. Therefore, some participants with early-stage biliary disease may have been misclassified as not having gallstones, potentially resulting in outcome misclassification. In addition, information regarding gallstone composition was unavailable; thus, cholesterol stones could not be distinguished from pigment stones, limiting mechanistic interpretation of the observed association. Additionally, participants with fatty liver disease were excluded at the design stage to reduce metabolic heterogeneity. However, this may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings, as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease is closely related to both serum uric acid levels and gallstone disease. Addressing these gaps in future research could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between serum UA and gallstone disease.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that elevated serum UA levels were significantly associated with an increased prevalence of gallbladder stones in Korean women. This association remained significant after adjustment for multiple demographic, clinical, and metabolic confounders. These findings suggest that serum UA may reflect underlying metabolic disturbances associated with gallstone disease. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between serum UA and gallstone development and to determine the potential clinical significance of this association.
