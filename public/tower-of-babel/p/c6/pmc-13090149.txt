Combined minimally invasive lymphatic microsurgery and aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffold for refractory post-traumatic eyelid lymphedema: A case report
Authors: Dimitrios Dionyssiou, Avra Drougou, Anastasios Topalis, Rebecca Ardemagni, Efterpi Demiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLymphedema arises from impaired lymphatic drainage, leading to accumulation of protein-rich fluid in the interstitium and progressive soft-tissue fibrosis. Although eyelid lymphedema is rare, it can cause marked functional and aesthetic morbidity, while often proves refractory to conservative measures. We report a case of secondary facial and eyelid lymphedema following closed head trauma, successfully treated with guided lymphagiogenesis using aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds.A 44-year-old man presented with a 12-month history of non-pitting edema involving the upper and lower eyelids bilaterally and the lower frontal region, following blunt cranial trauma. Cross-sectional imaging excluded fractures or hematoma. Despite 12 months of complete decongestive therapy, there was no meaningful improvement. Indocyanine green lymphography (ICG) demonstrated a diffuse dermal backflow pattern and complete lymphatic flow blockage at both medial canthi.We implanted thread-like aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds subcutaneously, from functional lymphatic collectors at the medial canthi bilateral to the ipsilateral preauricular lymph nodes, creating neocollectors to bypass the fibrotic obstructed segment. At 4 months postoperative, repeat ICG showed restoration of linear lymphatic flow along the scaffold track with drainage of the lower eyelid and zygomatic region, and clinical examination confirmed complete resolution of eyelid heaviness, pain and edema.This case illustrates that guided lymphagiogenesis using aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds can restore lymphatic drainage in highly localized, post-traumatic facial lymphedema that is resistant to conservative therapy, offering a minimally invasive microsurgical alternative to debulking procedures.Keywords: Eyelid lymphedema, Lymphagiogenesis, Nanofibrillar collagen scaffold, Indocyanine green lymphography, Lymphatic microsurgery

Introduction

Lymphedema is caused by failure of lymphatic drainage, resulting in accumulation of protein-rich interstitial fluid, chronic inflammation and progressive fibrosis. The dysfunction may result from abnormal lymphatic anatomy, excessive lymph load or mechanical obstruction of lymphatic pathways.

Common causes of secondary lymphedema include malignancy, lymph node dissection, radiotherapy, trauma and filarial infection.1,2 Traumatic lymphedema after closed soft-tissue injury without skeletal damage or oncologic surgery is relatively uncommon but recognized, and when it involves the face—particularly the periorbital region—it can be functionally disabling and resistant to conservative treatment.

Eyelid lymphedema presents as a persistent, non-pitting edema of the eyelids and surrounding tissues. Conventional management relies on complete decongestive therapy (manual lymphatic drainage and compression), which may be impractical or insufficient in the periorbital region. In cases of complete local obstruction or failure of conservative therapy, surgical solutions are required. Historical approaches based on debulking and excision frequently yielded suboptimal aesthetic results and recurrent swelling.3

Supermicrosurgical techniques, including lymphaticovenous anastomosis, have since expanded the reconstructive options for lymphedema affecting the extremities and, more recently, the eyelids.4–7 In parallel, aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds (BioBridge®) have been developed as resorbable, thread-like matrices that mimic the extracellular matrix and promote lymphagiogenesis, creating subcutaneous neocollectors to bypass areas of fibrosis and obstruction.4,5,8,9 BioBridge® is manufactured as a thread-like construct (approximately 12–15 cm long and ∼0.3 mm in diameter) composed of highly aligned type I collagen nanofibrils, providing parallel micro/nano-architecture that can wick interstitial fluid and guide lymphatic endothelial cell alignment and migration across scarred segments.4,5,9

We report a rare case of secondary post-traumatic facial and eyelid lymphedema successfully treated by guided lymphagiogenesis using aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds positioned with a minimally invasive lymphatic microsurgery procedure between functional periorbital lymphatic collectors and preauricular lymph nodes.

Case report

A 44-year-old man with no significant comorbidities was referred to our plastic surgery department with a 12-month history of progressive facial swelling following closed head trauma. The initial injury consisted of blunt impact to the frontal – parietal region without loss of consciousness.

Over subsequent weeks he developed non-pitting edema of both upper and lower eyelids and the lower frontal area, more pronounced on the left side. He complained of eyelid heaviness, morning swelling and intermittent periorbital discomfort. Neurological examination was normal. Repeated computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging confirmed the absence of facial fractures, intracranial pathology and post-traumatic hematoma, supporting a diagnosis of localized post-traumatic soft-tissue injury with secondary oedema.

After the first 6 months of self-applied cryotherapy and gentle manual massage, he underwent a further 6 months of complete decongestive therapy, including manual lymphatic drainage techniques adapted for the face and use of custom compression garments. Despite good adherence, clinical improvement was minimal, and the lymphedema was considered refractory to conservative measures.

On presentation to our unit, clinical examination revealed marked asymmetric edema of the upper and lower eyelids bilaterally, more pronounced on the left, extending into the lower forehead (Figure 1). The overlying skin was intact without signs of cellulitis or venous congestion. There was no ptosis or lagophthalmos, and ocular motility was full. Indocyanine green lymphography (ICG) was performed after five intradermal injections of 0.2 mL along the midline of the frontal-to-parietal region. Imaging showed severely impaired lymphatic drainage with a “stardust” or “diffuse” dermal backflow pattern and complete flow blockage at both medial canthi that persisted beyond 120 min. No spontaneous drainage from the periorbital region to regional lymph nodes was observed (Figure 2).

Surgical technique

Given the localized obstruction and failure of conservative management, we planned a guided lymphagiogenesis procedure using aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds (BioBridge®). The aim was to create subcutaneous neocollectors bridging functional lymphatic vessels at the medial canthi to the ipsilateral preauricular lymph nodes. In our setting, individual scaffold threads were 15 cm long, matching the required bypass distance.

Under general anesthesia and microscopic guidance, functional lymphatic collectors in the medial canthal region and preauricular lymph nodes were identified using additional indocyanine green injections approximately 5 cm cranial to the ears in the temporal region.

Two incisions were made: a 3-cm preauricular incision to localize the lymph node, and a lower blepharoplasty incision extending to the medial canthus, positioned over the ICG-visualized lymphatic vessels, to facilitate retrieval of the cannula tip. From the preauricular site, a 1.5-mm diameter, 15-cm long single-use tunneling cannula was introduced and advanced in the subcutaneous plane towards the functional periorbital collectors at the medial canthus (Supplementary 1). No scar excision or formal scar release was performed; the tunnel was created bluntly and narrowly, solely to deliver the scaffold with minimal tissue disruption.

A thread-like collagen scaffold was inserted into the cannula with a suction aid from the contralateral side and left in place as the cannula was gently withdrawn, positioning the scaffold within the subcutaneous tunnel between the medial canthus and preauricular region (Supplementary 2). A small puncture wound was created at the afferent lymphatic vessel entry site of the preauricular lymph node using a 23-gauge needle (Supplementary 3), and the distal end of the collagen scaffold was anchored using a 9–0 nylon purse-string suture (Supplementary 4), facilitating lymphatic ingrowth into the node. The proximal end of the scaffold was placed in direct contact with the functional lymphatic collectors at the medial canthus to provide a target for lymphatic ingrowth. The procedure was performed bilaterally using the same technique, implanting two scaffolds on each side.

The skin incisions were closed in layers. There were no intraoperative complications, and the patient was discharged on the first postoperative day.

Postoperative course and follow-up

Beginning on postoperative day 5, the patient resumed gentle manual lymphatic drainage sessions focused on directing lymph along the scaffold path from the medial canthi towards the preauricular nodes. No postoperative infections, seromas or wound-healing complications were observed.

At 4-month follow-up, clinical examination showed near-complete resolution of eyelid and frontal edema with restoration of normal upper and lower lid contour and symmetry (Figure 3). The patient reported complete relief of heaviness and pain and expressed high satisfaction with both functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Clinical findings at 4-month follow-up.

Repeat ICG demonstrated re-established linear lymphatic flow along the scaffold track, with drainage from the right lower eyelid and zygomatic region to the preauricular nodes (Figure 4, Supplementary video 1) and a marked functional improvement on the left side, consistent with successful guided lymphagiogenesis and creation of neocollectors. Discussion

Historically, surgical management of eyelid lymphedema relied on debulking and excisional procedures.3 These techniques were associated with suboptimal aesthetic outcomes, risk of eyelid malposition and, importantly, frequent recurrence due to persistent lymphatic dysfunction.

A significant shift occurred with the advent of supermicrosurgery by enabling anastomosis of tiny lymphatic and venous structures.6,7 Recent reports have successfully applied periorbital lymphaticovenous anastomoses to treat primary eyelid lymphedema, offering symptomatic relief and sustained improvement.6 However, these procedures rely on the presence of suitable proximal lymphatic channels and recipient venules, which may be limited in scarred or severely fibrotic tissues.

Modern aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds, such as BioBridge®, provide a biomimetic extracellular matrix with highly aligned type I collagen nanofibrils that guide endothelial cell migration and vessel formation.4,5,8,9 These scaffolds are resorbable, mechanically robust and designed as thin, ribbon-like structures that can be implanted subcutaneously, creating parallel channels that can support capillary transport of interstitial fluid, to bypass areas of lymphatic obstruction.4,5

Preclinical and early clinical studies have shown that aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds can support lymphatic regeneration and improve outcomes in secondary lymphedema, both as a standalone intervention and as an adjunct to established microsurgical procedures.4,5,8 Imaging studies have demonstrated new lymphatic vessel formation along the implantation track.5,8 In addition, histological evaluation of explanted BioBridge® threads has demonstrated vessel ingrowth and absence of a fibrous capsule, with the scaffold structure remaining identifiable at 10 months, supporting the concept of scaffold-guided neocollector formation.9

Individual BioBridge® threads are typically 12–15 cm long, and longer bypass distances can be addressed by implanting multiple threads in series and/or in parallel, depending on the anatomy and the available proximal and distal basins.4,8 Most reported clinical use has been in upper- and lower-extremity secondary lymphedema, either as an adjunct to vascularized lymph node transfer or combined with other lymphatic microsurgical strategies, where the scaffold is used to bridge fibrotic segments and guide new lymphatic pathways toward a functional nodal basin.5,8 The same principle may be applicable to other localized regions with a defined obstruction and an available functional lymphatic pathway such as post-traumatic or post-oncologic head-and-neck or facial lymphedema; however, evidence in these anatomical regions remains limited.

In our patient, the lymphedema was highly localized to the periorbital and lower frontal region following closed trauma. Indocyanine green lymphography confirmed complete, persistent obstruction at both medial canthi with dermal backflow and absence of spontaneous drainage. The failure of prolonged complete decongestive therapy suggested that the obstruction would not resolve spontaneously, and that purely conservative management was unlikely to restore lymphatic function.

We therefore selected a minimal invasive microsurgical method combined with collagen scaffolds guided lymphagiogenesis by bridging functional medial canthal lymphatic vessels to preauricular lymph nodes. This technique offers several advantages in such a setting:

1.Bypassing a focal obstruction without the need to sacrifice or extensively dissect delicate periorbital tissues.

2.Exploiting residual functional lymphatics at the medial canthus, serving as a physiological “inlet” into the scaffold.

3.Anchoring into a regional nodal basin, providing a robust “outlet” for regenerated lymphatic channels.

4.Minimal invasiveness, as the procedure requires small incisions and tunneling, with low morbidity and rapid recovery.

At 6 months, both objective imaging and clinical findings suggested successful regeneration of lymphatic pathways along the scaffold track, with durable resolution of symptoms. While the follow-up remains relatively short, this case supports the growing body of evidence that aligned nanofibrillar collagen scaffolds represent a promising tool in microsurgical techniques for localized, treatment-refractory lymphedema, including challenging periorbital presentations.

Limitations of this report include its single-patient design, the lack of a control intervention and the limited duration of follow-up. Comparative studies would be valuable to better define the indications, optimal timing, and the role of guided lymphangiogenesis in combination with other microsurgical procedures; however, the rarity of this condition and the small number of eligible patients may limit the feasibility and statistical power of such investigations.
