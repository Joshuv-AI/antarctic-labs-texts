Deep Learning‐Based Tumor Cell Classification in Lung Adenocarcinoma With a Case‐by‐Case Human‐in‐the‐Loop Approach
Authors: Martin Stampe, Pia Klausen, Ida Skovgaard Christiansen, Charlotte Vestrup Rift, Peter Rindom Koffeldt, Rasmus Hartvig, Eric Santoni‐Rugiu, Thomas H. L. Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeep learning networks trained for classification of single cells in hematoxylin and eosin‐stained (H&E) sections are limited in accuracy. We hypothesize the accuracy of a pathologist to be 98%–100%. Digital classification should be on par with this to be relevant. Here is presented a human‐in‐the‐loop approach, where cell classification is fine‐tuned by the pathologist during microscopy. The approach was established and tested on H&E‐stained histological slides of pulmonary adenocarcinomas (LUADs). Networks pretrained on a limited selection of typical cells reached an average true positive rate (TPR) of 0.989 for classification of LUAD tumor cells within a high‐power field (HPF), with 20 additional tumor cells included from the tumor investigated. Results were consistent on three HPFs, yielding means of Dice = 0.991, TPR = 0.987, precision = 0.996, and specificity = 0.988. Networks trained and tested on a smaller dataset of LUADs from The Cancer Genome Atlas demonstrated means of Dice = 0.951, TPR = 0.944, precision = 0.96, and specificity = 0.951. Classification of LUAD tumor cells by networks based and tested on data from the same source is comparable to that of a pathologist. The output is interpretable, validatable, and applicable in routine pathology.

Introduction

Deep learning (DL) networks, such as residual convolutional neural networks (CNN), excel at classifying objects in images [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. DL‐based classification of cells in histological sections as introduced by Graham et al. [6] in 2019, as well as the PanNuke dataset presented by Gamper et al. [7] is expected to have a large impact on the work of pathologists. In addition to paving the way for studies on distribution and morphological details of single cell populations, the approach ensures an abundance of readily available training data, as each hematoxylin and eosin‐stained (H&E) whole slide image (WSI) can contain several thousands of cells. Despite this advantageous circumstance, published cell classification networks display unsatisfyingly low classification accuracies below 80% [3, 6, 8].

Digital classification should be at least on par with humans to be relevant. We hypothesize that the ability of a trained pathologist to correctly classify common, characteristic cells is 98%–100%. To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports on the human accuracy in classifying characteristic cells, such as tumor cells or hepatocytes. Digital accuracy in the range of 80% may seem convincing on paper, but it is not acceptable in clinical diagnostics. Thus, there has been limited clinical interest in commercially available apps relying on cell classification, that is, apps for PD‐L1‐scoring or counting of mitoses [2, 9, 10]. Up to this point, these solutions have yet to prove compellingly more efficient or feasible than standard human interpretation in a clinical setting.

Our own attempts at training a CNN in classifying cells of various types have, so far, been disappointing. Despite > 105 each of various cell classes in various training setups, the accuracy when inferring new microsections were marred with immediately recognizable cell classification errors—the accuracy plateaued firmly at 80%–85%. This is probably caused by variations in processing of histological microsections and biology—that is, one cell‐type in one microsection may be indistinguishable from a different cell‐type in another microsection [11].

In this study, we are putting an allegedly fool‐proof approach at accurate cell classification to the test by means of the same U‐Net segmentation from HoVer‐Net [6]. The approach was introduced in a recent publication [11] and is amply described as “case‐by‐case human‐in‐the‐loop.” In short, we establish a multiclass CNN trained on a limited number of carefully selected cells, infer new microsections with the network, and finetune it by adding cells from the new microsection until a classification on par with a trained pathologist is reached.

We now wish to ascertain the number of added cells that are needed to finetune the multiclass CNN to a targeted true positive rate, that is, ability to correctly classify tumor cells as such, of more than 98%. For this purpose, tumor cells in H&E‐stained sections from one of the most frequently diagnosed cancer types, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), were utilized.

Materials and Methods

Data was collected retrospectively from 30 consecutive cases of resected LUADs. Tumor sections from 30 formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded blocks (FFPE) were stained with H&E. The stained sections were then scanned and converted into NDPI‐files (Hamamatsu Nanozoomer). This workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. In each NDPI file, a high‐power field (HPF), magnification ×40, with the perceived highest density of tumor cells was identified and demarcated. The area was then preprocessed. All cells were localized by instance segmentation of cell nuclei using the U‐net architecture from Hover‐net [6], trained on the expanded PanNuke dataset [7]. An example of demarcation of an area for preprocessing with subsequent single cell segmentation and classification can be seen in Figure 2.

For every case of LUAD, the cells in the annotated HPF were classified manually by one trained pathologist (M.S.) as either LUAD tumor cells, eosinophilic granulocytes, lymphocytes, or mesenchymal cells. Images of these segmented and annotated cells were stored as 256 × 256‐pixel tiles with the nucleus in the center. Examples of the four cell types are outlined in Figure 3. Pictures of the annotated HPFs were taken before and after cell classification to compare results and make audits possible between two pathologists (M.S. and T.H.L.J.) to ensure standardization of the annotations. For disagreements in classification between the two pathologists, the files were reviewed, and consensus was reached for the final cell classifications.

Examples of the four cell types included in the dataset for the basis network. (1) Lymphocyte, (2) eosinophilic granulocyte, (3) mesenchymal cell, and (4) LUAD tumor cell. All magnifications at ×40, scale bar = 20 μm. The illustration was created with Biorender.com.

The tumor specimens were subsequently split evenly and at random, as 15 of the tumors were designated to provide data for establishing a basic training network for LUAD cells. The other 15 LUADs were reserved for testing the baseline capabilities of this network. They were also designated for investigating what effect the addition of 5, 10, or 20 tumor cells from these tumors would have on the network's ability to correctly classify all tumor cells from the HPF of the tumor. As such, the initial investigation on a single HPF of the tumors aimed to assess how many additional tumor cells needed to be added to the basis network's training data to attain a mean true positive rate ≥ 98% for tumor cell classification.

We added 300 mesenchymal cells, 300 LUAD tumor cells from the tumors designated for training (20 tumor cells from each tumor reserved for accumulating training data), 282 eosinophilic granulocytes, and 300 lymphocytes from our established digital image archive to a baseline network for single‐cell classification purposes. These cells were chosen, as they were expected to cover the most abundant types of cells in the WSIs. The baseline model was conceived with a training session of 200 epochs with an 80/20 validation split. Each training session yielded an accuracy, a validation accuracy, and a loss as metrics for the network's performance on the training dataset.

We proceeded to investigate the network's ability to classify LUAD tumor cells in a single HPF of the 15 LUADs that were reserved for testing. After every training session, the performance of the network was evaluated by testing the network's ability to classify all the tumor cells from the HPF of the tumor that was being tested. Performance of the network was evaluated in a one‐vs‐all setting, where LUAD tumor cells were treated as the positive class and mesenchymal cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophilic granulocytes were treated as the negative class. Total number of correctly classified tumor cells (true positives), total number of incorrectly classified tumor cells (false negatives), total cell counts for each class, and the total number of accumulated cells were tracked and registered during the study of a single HPF. For the final iteration of the classification network, that is, the one with 20 tumor cells included, the number of false positives (non‐tumor cells classified as tumor cells), and true negatives (non‐tumor cells correctly classified as such) were also noted. The network generates a probability for every cell prediction with an associated picture of the cell in question. Probability ranges from 0 to 100, where 0 means a 0% belief in the given class, and 100 means a 100% belief in the assigned class. In the same way, the network presents an overview of the cells it classified wrong compared to what the trained pathologists manually assigned the cell (M.S. and T.H.L.J.), making evaluation possible.

Identical tests were subsequently performed on 3 HPFs from the same LUADs in areas with low, medium, and high tumor cell density. This was done to account for possible variations in local staining enrichment and section thickness. Networks with 0 and 20 additional tumor cells added to the training data were tested to affirm generalizability to the remaining slide of a true positive rate above 98%. Total number of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives were registered to calculate true positive rate, specificity, precision, and Dice‐score (mathematically equivalent to the F1‐score in our binary LUAD tumor cell vs. non‐tumor cell setup).

To further investigate and assess the ability and generalizability of the CNN, we tested our findings on the external, open‐source dataset, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). We identified 14 scanned cases of H&E‐stained LUADs scanned at ×40 (same magnification as our in‐house dataset).

Testing on the TCGA dataset was done in two phases. First, we decided to test the performance of the basis network trained on the in‐house data. Following this initial investigation, the 14 cases were split. Seven tumors were reserved for the construction of a basis network with 300 LUAD tumor cells, 300 lymphocytes, 300 mesenchymal cells, and 282 eosinophils. Due to a shortage of eosinophils, it was decided to use the same 282 eosinophils from the initial basis dataset as the eosinophils for the new basis network's training data. The remaining seven cases were reserved for testing. In accordance with our primary investigations on the in‐house dataset, all cells (LUAD tumor cells, mesenchymal cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophils) were extracted as image files and reviewed independently by THLJ and MS to reach consensus and ensure standardization of cell classifications.

The same methodology presented above was applied. Again, only tests for networks with 0 and 20 tumor cells added to the training data were done.

Dice‐score, true positive rate, precision, and specificity were calculated using the following equations [12]: TPR=TPTP+FN Precision=TPTP+FP Specificity=TNTN+FP DiceF1=2×TP2×TP+FP+FN where TP = true positives, FP = false positives, TN = true negatives, FN = false negatives, TPR = true positive rate [12].

Statistics

Data was collected and stored in Microsoft Excel and all subsequent data management, analysis, statistics, and calculations were performed in R. Sample sizes for the study were calculated for continuous means with an aspired power of 0.8, κ = 1 and statistical significance set at 0.05. We estimated that the initial ability (true positive rate) of the base network in correctly classifying tumor cells would be around 0.95 with a standard deviation of 0.025, while we aimed to achieve a true positive rate of 0.99 with a standard deviation of 0.01 in the final network.

To achieve a power = 0.8 with these specifications, we required n = 5 in each group. As our estimates were tinged with a degree of uncertainty, we decided to include all the available 30 cases of LUAD from in‐house in the study to achieve the best possible power. Paired two sample t‐tests were performed to assess statistically significant differences in the means [12] of the evaluation metrics for networks with 20 and 0 additional tumor cells included in the training data. Our null hypothesis for the paired t‐test is that there is no difference in means of true positive rate, specificity, precision, and Dice‐score between the compared networks based on the number of included tumor cells in the training data.

Corrections for multiple significance testings have not been done due to the exploratory nature of the study. Results should be interpreted accordingly.

All training sessions for the networks were done with a validation split of 80/20 with 200 epochs.

Discussion

Our findings for the tests on a single HPF indicate a high degree of mean true positive rate with small standard deviations. This suggests our network can reliably separate LUAD tumor cells from lymphocytes, mesenchymal cells, and eosinophilic granulocytes in a single HPF with a relatively small and feasible amount of training data. With the inclusion of 20 tumor cells from the tested LUADs, the network's mean true positive rate had a 95% confidence interval from 98.3% to 99.5%, which is on par with our hypothesized ability of a trained pathologist. The mean true positive rate rises while the standard deviation falls as more tumor cells are included in the training data.

Results are consistent when we increase the number of HPFs from one to three, accounting for different areas of the LUADs with low, medium, and high tumor cell density. We saw a significant increase in mean Dice‐score (p = 0.0191) and mean true positive rate (p = 0.00589) with paired t‐tests between networks with 0 and 20 tumor cells included, and no significant changes in precision or specificity. True positive rate for the tests on 3 HPFs retained a 95% confidence interval of 0.983; 0.991.

A comparable study on classification of single cells, namely leukocytes in blood smears, from three different datasets, gave similar results for evaluation metrics [14]. The study in question reports mean precision, recall and F1‐score of 0.9812 ± 0.009, 0.9923 ± 0.0389, and 0.9823 ± 0.0003, respectively [14]. While our study's setup is not identical to Kalnoor and Kadrolli [14], together the studies highlight interesting and feasible use cases for single cell classification in different areas of pathology.

In 2022, Ilyas et al. [8] assessed the nucleus detection and classification performances of different dedicated networks on the PanNuke dataset [7]. Metrics for the performance were similar. The best performing network displayed precision, recall (true positive rate), and F1‐score of 0.60, 0.71, and 0.65, respectively, for neoplastic cell classification [8]. Admittedly, the PanNuke dataset is many times the size of our study setup, and tissue sections are presumably more heterogenic. Both factors cumulatively make their task and setup vaster than ours. When all is said, the uniformly low classification performance across several network calls for an alternative approach.

Performance of the networks with training data from the in‐house dataset tested on external tumors was expectedly lower (Table 2). The tests for the network with 0 additional tumor cells added demonstrated lower mean Dice, mean true positive rate, mean specificity, and mean precision compared to the tests done on the in‐house LUADs. When we added 20 tumor cells from the external tumor investigated, we saw a dramatic rise in the mean true positive rate (from 0.807 to 0.984) and mean Dice (from 0.874 to 0.914). However, both precision (from 0.975 to 0.862) and specificity (from 0.961 to 0.692) were significantly impaired by this addition. It seems that the networks in this case overclassify non‐tumor cells as tumor cells. Comparing our in‐house tumors to the TCGA dataset, differences in tissue section thickness and staining intensities are evident. The TCGA microsections are generally thicker and appear more color dense with less conspicuous subcellular features. These discrepancies are most likely causing the network to overclassify tumor cells in the tests. The results from these tests are strikingly inferior to tests exclusively utilizing in‐house data for training and testing.

When we split the TCGA dataset in seven LUADs for establishing a basic network and seven for testing, we saw enhanced performance. Mean Dice, mean true positive rate, mean precision, and mean specificity were all higher and adding 20 tumor cells from the tumor investigated increases both Dice‐score and true positive rate, at the expense of both precision and specificity (Table 3). The network ultimately reaches a mean true positive rate of 94.4% and so fails to reach a true positive rate ≥ 98%.

The single cell classification is not perfect. As evidenced by Figure 2, there are at times misclassifications of cells and outlines of structures in the tissue that are not easily identifiable as individual cells. We have previously shown that thicker tissue sections result in more uniform morphological cell features and thus lower classification accuracy with the CNN used in this study [11]. Along with the lower number of cases used for the basis network, we believe the microsection thickness to be the reason that lower performance was obtained with a network established, trained, and tested on the TCGA tumors.

As the network has only been used for evaluation of what roughly corresponds to 3 selected HPFs, further testing is needed to elaborate on and ensure the applicability and safety of the CNN on the entirety of WSIs for different tasks, not only pertaining to separating LUAD tumor cells from eosinophilic granulocytes, lymphocytes, and mesenchymal cells.

Limitations

There are several limitations of this study. The CNN has been applied on a single entity of disease originating from a single organ, namely LUADs. This relative homogeneity of the training and test data could help produce convincing numbers; however, it also narrows down the explored use cases of the model for this study. Further validation on different entities of disease from different organs is warranted.

All cell classifications have been agreed upon independently by two pathologists to circumvent potential bias. However, it does not eliminate the potential bias of cell classifications by the pathologists.

The study utilizes small sample numbers for statistical analyses, meaning outliers could disproportionately skew results. As such, results should be interpreted accordingly. This is especially true for the last iteration of significance testing on the 50/50 split of the LUADs from the TCGA dataset.

Because of few samples of LUADs from the TCGA dataset with the relevant magnification and a shortage of eosinophilic granulocytes, a 100% similar test of the TCGA LUADs was not possible. An identical trend in classification was observed with addition of cells, but we cannot directly compare the performance. Another possible limitation is the lack of native eosinophilic granulocytes from the tissue sections from the TCGA dataset. Arguably, the general characteristics of eosinophilic granulocytes remain stable across tissue sections, but we cannot know for certain how the cell classification properties of the CNN have been affected.

Finally, training the networks for cell classification takes approximately 10–15 min (depending on the amount of training data). With the ongoing technological advances, this time is expected to be reduced in the future. Whether or not the time is an obstacle for implementation depends on the use‐case of the classification. If the pathologist must wait for the result, a faster analysis is desirable. If the results are input for a downstream analysis, timing may not be an issue.

Conclusion

Our CNN can classify single cells with a high true positive rate within up to 3 HPFs for the categories LUAD tumor cells, mesenchymal, eosinophilic granulocytes, and lymphocytes. We reached an estimated mean true positive rate for classification of LUAD tumor cells of up to 99.1% across 15 different LUADs with five individual tests per case with a human‐in‐the‐loop approach. We were unable to reproduce these findings completely on a smaller external dataset of generally thicker microsections, where performance was overall inferior, albeit much higher than reported in other studies not using a human‐in‐the‐loop approach.

Taken together, the “slide‐by‐slide human‐in‐the‐loop” cell classification approach was successful when utilized as an in‐house solution on areas of tumors in thin microsections using a basic network compiled of at least 15 cases of the intended tumor type. With our results, we hope to pave the way for quantitative single cell AI solutions on H&E‐stained histological slides.
