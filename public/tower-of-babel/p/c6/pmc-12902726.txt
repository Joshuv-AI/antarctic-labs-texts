Effect of Functional Electrical Stimulation Combined With Physical Exercise on Electron Transport Chain Activity in Peripheral Blood Cells of Stroke Patients
Authors: A. Larrea, A. Martín‐Odriozola, E. Astigarraga, C. Rodríguez‐de‐Pablo, G. Barreda‐Gómez, M. Torrecilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStroke is commonly accompanied by motor disorders secondary to the initial injury, and at the mitochondrial level, there is an increase in oxidative stress, causing tissue damage and relating to alteration in motor function. Functional electrical stimulation (FES) therapies can be useful in stroke patients aimed at restoring motor function and improving life quality. Several studies have recently proposed that electrical stimulation modulates mitochondrial biogenesis and physiology; however, the effect of FES on individual complexes comprising the mitochondrial electron transport chain (mETC) remains unknown.MethodsTo study the effect of FES therapy on the activity of the mETC, in vitro analyses were conducted using UV‐spectrophotometry of the activity of the complexes (I, II, and IV), glycerol‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G3PDH), and monoamine oxidase (MAO) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) obtained from stroke patients. Patients' activity of complex II and MAO was also determined in platelets. Analyses were measured before and after 12 rehabilitation sessions based on FES therapy combined with physical exercises.ResultsThe results showed that the activity of the mETC, G3PDH, and MAO was significantly reduced in pre‐intervention stroke patients compared to healthy volunteers, both in PBMCs and platelets, except for complex II in platelets and MAO in PBMCs. Interestingly, FES intervention increased the mETC, G3PDH, and MAO activity in both cell types of stroke patients.ConclusionsThis preliminary study indicates that intervention can restore motor function and mitochondrial activity, which are damaged in stroke patients. However, more research is needed to determine if changes in mitochondrial activity can be used as biomarkers of the pathology and/or the efficacy of intervention.

Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of disability in Europe and the second cause of cognitive impairment and death worldwide, with an estimated incidence ranging from 95 to 190 per 100 000 habitants per year. To highlight that ischemic stroke accounts for 8% of all strokes. Additionally, 50% of individuals who suffer a stroke present some disability in basic activities of daily living [1]. Strokes are those disorders that produce functional and structural neuronal alterations in different areas of the brain due to a maintained hypoxia [2].

Mitochondria is the main driver of energy production in the brain and a key regulator of cellular homeostasis. The mitochondrial electron transport chain (mETC) is a crucial component of cellular respiration, taking place in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It consists of a series of protein complex, including complex I (NADHDH), complex II (succinate dehydrogenase [SDH]), complex III (cytochrome bc1 complex), complex IV (cytochrome C oxidase [CCO]), and complex V (ATP synthase). The mETC plays a fundamental role im generating ATP by transferring electrons from electron donor such as NADH and FADH2, to molecular oxygen (O2), while also contributing to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [3].

The disruption in the mETC following stroke can affect various cells in the body. Neuronal cells are particularly sensitive to mitochondrial dysfunction due to their high demand and vulnerability to oxidative stress. However, mitochondrial dysfunction in stroke extends beyond local brain tissue damage. It triggers systemic responses, including the release of inflammatory cytokines, propagation of oxidative stress, and activation of immune cells. These systemic effects can lead to secondary damage in distant organs and tissues. Consequently, blood cells such as white blood cells and platelets may also experience mitochondrial dysfunction, impacting their respective immune and haemostatic functions.

On the one hand, during ischemic stroke, the acute reduction in cerebral blood flow rapidly compromises mitochondrial function. The mETC, responsible for oxidative phosphorylation, is mainly affected by hypoxia. Ischemia drastically reduces oxygen availability, preventing mitochondria from accepting electrons in the mETC and consequently limiting ATP production. This phenomenon is especially critical in vulnerable brain regions that heavily depend on aerobic metabolism [4]. Moreover, during cerebral ischemia, the accumulation of ROS is a direct consequence of mitochondrial disruption. Oxygen deficiency promotes uncontrolled production of superoxide at complex I and hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction. ROS not only trigger lipid peroxidation and oxidation of structural proteins but also activate cellular stress signaling pathways and programmed cell death [5]. Cytosolic calcium overload induced by ischemia and oxidative stress promotes prolonged opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pores (mPTP) [6], compromising mitochondrial membrane integrity and facilitating the release of pro‐apoptotic proteins such as cytochrome C, which leads to neuronal apoptosis [7]. Experimental studies have shown a significant decrease in the expression of genes encoding mETC proteins [8, 9, 10]. Likewise, mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), especially in key genes such as MT‐ND4 and MT‐CYB, have been associated with a higher risk of stroke and worse functional prognosis [11, 12, 13, 14]. These alterations impair the mitochondrial capacity to generate ATP and manage oxidative stress [14]. In turn, mitochondrial dysfunction not only promotes ROS production and apoptosis but also activates inflammatory responses, including the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB) pathway, amplifying tissue damage and perpetuating post‐ischemic neuronal injury [15]. Overall, changes in mETC biogenesis and function—such as reduced oxidative phosphorylation, decreased ATP production, and increased generation of free radicals—appear to be key determinants in the pathophysiology and progression of stroke [4].

Alterations in ROS levels and redox imbalance have been implicated in the pathophysiological processes following stroke, particularly in the acute phase, where they can contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, and cellular damage [16]. In chronic phases, although acute oxidative stress is usually attenuated, cellular bioenergetic alterations persist, especially at the mitochondrial level, including decreased electron transport efficiency, changes in mitochondrial dynamics (fusion/fission), and impaired coupling between energy demand and muscle activity [17]. These dysfunctions may negatively affect functional recovery by limiting metabolic responsiveness during therapeutic exercise. Additionally, motor function in this phase is modulated by a complex interaction of structural and functional factors, such as neuroplastic reorganization, integrity of sensorimotor circuits, physical activity levels, and the patient's overall functional status [18].

In this context, functional electrical stimulation (FES) has been proposed as a complementary therapeutic intervention. It is a specific neuromuscular stimulation modality where electrical currents are applied to motor nerves, provoking sequential muscle contractions that enable the execution of functional tasks such as walking, cycling, or grasping [19]. FES allows activation of paretic muscles through electrical impulses, which can improve muscle strength, endurance, and, in some cases, overall motor function. Previous studies have shown beneficial effects of FES on muscle trophism, fiber architecture, and contractile properties. Improvements have also been observed in biochemical markers related to oxidative metabolism [20]. From a bioenergetic perspective, FES has demonstrated positive effects on improving skeletal muscle contractile function by promoting biochemical changes that favor mitochondrial biogenesis [21], possibly mediated by overexpression of genes involved in mitochondrial protein synthesis [18]. Consequently, there is an increase in ATP synthesis, oxidative activity, and regulation of antioxidant enzymes [22]. These effects could facilitate arteriolar vasodilation and improve muscle perfusion, optimizing mitochondrial capacity to produce energy in the presence of oxygen, which in turn translates into improved muscle strength and motor function [23]. Finally, it has been observed that the restoration of axonal mitochondrial transport, impaired by mitochondrial depolarization and ATP deficit after stroke, is crucial to meet neuronal metabolic demands [24]. Furthermore, beyond its peripheral effects on skeletal muscle, FES may contribute to the reorganization of central motor control. Through repetitive activation of afferent pathways, cortical plasticity processes are promoted, which can facilitate motor relearning. This influence on the central nervous system is enhanced when FES is combined with targeted functional activities, such as walking or object manipulation, thus reinforcing the connection between motor intention and execution.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the effect of FES therapy combined with physical exercises on the function of specific mETC complexes in blood cells from stroke patients.

Methods

Type of Study

This project was a quasi‐experimental nondrug study. The protocol has been developed under the recommendations of the Healthcare Bioethics Committee of the University Hospital of Donostia (Donostia‐San Sebastián, Spain) and in accordance with Law 14/2007 of Biomedical Research, Ethical Principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and other evaluable ethical principles.

The study has been conducted in a collaborative framework between the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU) (Leioa, Bizkaia, Spain), IMG Pharma Biotech S.L. (Zamudio, Bizkaia, Spain), and Fesia Technology S.L. (Donostia‐San Sebastián, Gipuzkoa, Spain).

Each patient underwent a two‐blood sample collection by venipuncture in the arm before and after the intervention: The first one in the morning prior to start the intervention (pre‐intervention), and the second immediately after completing the intervention (post‐intervention). Blood samples were used for measuring mitochondrial activity as described below (Figure 1).

All patients received 12 rehabilitation sessions with FES, combined with physical exercises. All rehabilitation sessions lasted 60 min and were administered twice a week (for 6 weeks), leaving a minimum of 48 h between them. The sessions were always performed by the same physiotherapist. Depending on the person's condition, the intervention was applied to the upper limb (60 min/session), the lower limb (60 min/session), or both (30 min/session for each limb) (Figure 1).

Intervention of the lower extremity was performed using the Fesia Walk device (Donostia‐San Sebastian, Spain). It is multi‐field (16 cathodes and 4 anodes) electrode‐based FES system designed for lower limb rehabilitation. It allows to stimulate the movements of dorsal flexion, plantar flexion, eversion, and eversion, both in static and dynamic (walking) activities [19]. The first 10/20 min of the session consisted in the stimulation of the dorsal and plantar flexion movements in an alternate way. No voluntary contraction was requested from the person. The rest of the session was performed walking with the FES device on, allowing the stimulation of ankle dorsal and plantar flexion in the corresponding phases of the gait. During this time, treadmill walking training (half of the time) and obstacle agility circuits (for the other half of the time) were performed. The walking speed and complexity of the tasks were adapted depending on the motor and cardiovascular capabilities of each subject.

Intervention of the upper extremity was performed using the Fesia Grasp device (Donostia‐San Sebastian, Spain). It is a commercial FES device designed for hand dexterity rehabilitation which delivers trains of biphasic pulses of different widths and amplitudes to different electrode fields (32 cathodes and 8 anodes) in an asynchronous manner [16]. The first 5/10 min consisted of stimulating different forearm muscles cyclically, generating random movements towards flexion and towards extension. The second 10/20 min of the session consisted in the stimulation of the following movements in an alternate way: wrist flexion and extension (5 min); thumb flexion and extension (5 min); index flexion and extension (5 min); and flexion and extension of the fingers 3, 4, and 5 (5 min). No voluntary contraction was requested from the participants, they were only encouraged to observe the movements that were performed. In the las 15/30 min, the FES was applied combined with therapeutic exercises (execution of several tasks, such as pick up objects of different sizes, move objects around, make different grips on different materials). An electrical current was applied to the paralyzed muscles in the exact moment that it was required, so that the hand opened or closed completely, facilitating the grasping of different objects. It was done manually by the clinician with a software application connected to the stimulator.

The FES waveform was the same for the intervention of upper and lower limbs. A symmetric biphasic rectangular wave was used, applying frequencies of 25 Hz and pulse widths of 250 μs. The amplitude was set for each day and subject at 8 mA above the motor threshold, except for those cases in which the tolerance level was lower than that, for whom amplitude was set at tolerance level above the motor threshold. The motor threshold was defined as the minimum amplitude at which an ankle (Fesia Walk) or wrist or fingers (Fesia Grasp) movement was visually appreciated with one of the fields.

Also, five healthy volunteers were selected, and a blood sample was obtained to determine mitochondrial activity (Figure 2). Mitochondrial activity was compared between the recruited patients, before receiving intervention (pre‐intervention), and healthy volunteers.

Drugs and Reagents

Nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT), 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB), β‐NADHDH sodium succinate dibasic, decylubiquinone (dUQ), sodium azide, cytochrome C from equine heart, glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G3PDH), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and tryptamine were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (San Louis, Missouri, USA).

Peripheral Blood Collection and Cells Preparation

Samples were obtained by peripheral venous blood collection by venipuncture. Two 4.5 mL tubes were drawn from each participant, with sodium citrate to avoid coagulation (BD Vacutainer; BD, Plymouth, UK). The samples were stored at 4°C until usage.

Isolation was performed using the Ficoll gradient (dBiokab, Barcelona, Spain) technique optimized at IMG Pharma Biotech S.L. (Derio, Spain). The volume of blood extracted at venipuncture was mixed with an equal volume of balanced salt solution (pH 7.4). The mixture was deposited on the same volume of Ficoll, forming two distinct phases. The gradient was centrifuged (Multifuge 3S‐R with rotor 75006445, Hareous, Germany) for 40 min at 400 g at 22°C. Afterward, the formation of three phases could be observed: the upper, yellowish‐colored phase corresponding to platelets; a white halo corresponding to PBMCs; and the lower one of red color corresponding to the sedimented hemoglobin.

The first phase (platelets) was transferred into new tubes and centrifuged (Multifuge 3S‐R with rotor 75006445, Hareus Germany) for 15 min at 14 000 rpm (rpm) at 22°C. The supernatant was discarded, the pellet obtained corresponds to the platelets, and it was stored at −80°C until usage.

The second phase (PBMCs) was transferred to new tubes, three volumes of balanced salt solution (pH 7.4) were added to wash possible Ficoll residues and centrifuged (Multifuge 3S‐R with rotor 75006445, Hareus Germany) for 15 min at 14 000 rpm at 22°C. Thus, a pellet of PBMC was formed and stored at −80°C until usage (Figure 3).

Cell isolation process. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Determination of Superoxide Formation in Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells and Platelets

The superoxide formation of complex I and II, as well as G3P and monoaminoxidase (MAO) was determined by adding their respective substrates: NADHDH (0.35 mM), SDH (10 mM), G3PDH (0.35 mM), and tryptamine (5 mM), respectively. PBMCs and platelets (0.1 mg/mL) were incubated in NBT (0.5 mg/mL), in phosphate buffer (5 mM; pH 7.4), in the presence or absence of dUQ (100 μM) and sodium azide (10 mM). The reaction begins with the addition of PBMCs and platelets. The superoxide formation was measured in optical density units (OD) at 2‐min intervals, during 300 min at a wavelength of 595 nm, in a microtiter plate reader, Multiskan FC (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). NBT was used as an indicator of superoxide formation. It is a colorimetric reagent that participates in redox reactions by accepting electrons, passing to its insoluble reduced form (formazan) which is a dark blue color. Thus, in the presence of a mitochondrial inhibitor, it will capture electrons that are diverted from the respiratory chain indicating the presence of superoxide formation.

Determination of Cytochrome C Oxidase Activity in Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells

For the determination of CCO activity (complex IV), PBMCs (0.1 mg/mL) were incubated in DAB (0.05%), NBT (0.5 mg/mL), in phosphate buffer (5 mM; pH 7.4), in the presence of cytochrome C (0.01%). The reaction begins with the addition of PBMCs. DAB oxidation was measured at 2‐min intervals, during 214 min at a wavelength of 450 nm, in a microtiter plate reader, Multiskan FC (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

Data Analysis

Measurements of mitochondrial activity and superoxide formation were obtained by ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometry, in absorbance data. The velocities for each data point were obtained from a linear regression model of superoxide formation assays, thus determining the slope for each data point. The analysis of the data was carried out using Excel Software (version 17.0, Microsoft, USA) and GraphPad (version 9.2; GraphPad, La Jolla, California, USA). The normality of the data was tested by the Shapiro–Wilk statistical test, with α set at 0.05.

The comparison between healthy volunteers and preintervention participants was performed by Mann–Whitney statistical analysis for nonparametric data and the unpaired Student's t‐test for parametric data, both with α set at 0.05.

The comparison between pre‐ and postintervention was carried out using the paired Student's t‐test for the parametric data and Wilcoxon statistical analysis for nonparametric data, both with α set at 0.05.

The processing and analysis of the data from motor and functional assessment scales was carried out using Excel software (version 17.0, Microsoft, USA) and GraphPad (version 9.2, GraphPad, La Jolla, California, USA). The normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk statistical test with α set at 0.05. For data with nonparametric distribution, Wilcoxon statistical analysis was performed with α set at 0.05. Analysis of the data with parametric distribution was performed using a paired or unpaired Student's t‐test according to the comparisons to be made.

Results

To investigate the effect of the therapy on mETC, the basal and maximum superoxide formation of each complex (complex I, II, and IV), the electron shuttle (G3PDH), and MAO were determined by inducing their respective substrates for each patient pre‐ and post‐intervention and compared with those of healthy volunteers.

To assess the influence of intervention on complex I mitochondrial activity, the production of basal superoxide formation induced by NADH in healthy volunteers and stroke patients was determined. The results showed a significant decrease in superoxide formation in pre‐intervention patients compared to healthy volunteers (pre: 0.0022 ± 0.00036 OD, volunteers: 0.0035 ± 0.000056 OD, p < 0.001) (Figure 4A). However, after intervention, it was observed that the superoxide formation had significantly increased in all patients compared to pre‐intervention (post: 0.0030 ± 0.0003 OD; p < 0.05) (Figure 4B).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of a rehabilitation protocol based on FES combined with physical exercise on mitochondrial activity in patients who have suffered a stroke. This objective is grounded in the growing recognition that motor sequelae after stroke are not only a consequence of neurological damage but also involve systemic bioenergetic dysfunctions, particularly affecting mitochondrial performance. To this end, an intervention consisting of 12 sessions of therapy based on a combination of FES and physical exercise was designed, applied to the affected limb, with the dual purpose of promoting neuromuscular reactivation and restoring mitochondrial function. The choice of FES was based on previous evidence indicating that electrical stimulation can promote mitochondrial biogenesis, modulate oxidative stress, and improve energy metabolism in skeletal muscle [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. However, little is known about its effects on systemic mitochondrial levels, particularly in circulating blood cells, which may serve as accessible biomarkers of the organism's metabolic state and oxidative balance.

Next, to investigate the potential effect of the intervention on mitochondrial activity in vitro, assays were conducted in PBMCs and platelets obtained from stroke patients. Thus, superoxide formation was quantified as an indirect measure of the activity of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, the mETC, and MAO, using UV–visible spectrophotometry. As these were in vitro experiments, the mitochondrial antioxidant system lacks the ability to neutralize the excess ROS produced during complex activity induction. Consequently, increased superoxide formation is associated with enhanced mitochondrial complex activity [24]. For this purpose, the basal and maximal superoxide formation of each mitochondrial complex (NADHDH, SDH, cytochrome C oxidase), the electron transport shuttle (G3PDH), and MAO were quantified by inducing their respective substrates in PBMCs from patients before and after intervention. The results are compared with superoxide formation in healthy volunteers to investigate whether mitochondrial activity was disrupted in stroke patients. Furthermore, investigating the activity of MAO, an enzyme present in the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOM) interacting with mETC complexes, could demonstrate that changes induced by the intervention occur in both mitochondrial membranes [25]. The study also examined the formation of basal and maximum superoxide in platelets to determine if the changes caused by the intervention occurred in the mitochondria of both cell types. In platelets, analysis of SDH was conducted, as it is specific to the mETC and MAO, thereby encompassing the study of both the mETC and external mitochondrial enzymes.

Regarding PBMCs, the formation of basal superoxide was significantly reduced in stroke patients when compared with healthy volunteers, in all complexes of mETC and in the electron transport shuttle. However, MAO activity was similar in both groups. Interestingly, after subjecting the patients to 12 sessions of intervention, the formation of superoxide significantly increased, reaching the levels of the healthy volunteers. As expected, when including the maximum formation of superoxide in the presence of dUQ and inhibiting cytochrome C with sodium azide, there was a significant increase in the formation of superoxide, and the same effect described above was observed. Initially, before intervention, patients showed a significant reduction in the production of superoxide compared to healthy volunteers, except in the case of MAO, which subsequently increased significantly after intervention. Additionally, the results showed that the intervention could restore mitochondrial activity in the complexes, as well as in the electron shuttle and MAO. That is, both at the internal and external mitochondrial levels [26]. This could translate into improvements in metabolic function, such as an increase in ATP synthesis, and consequently, a reduction in oxidative stress [27, 28]. In agreement with this, it has been shown that FES induces the regulation of autophagy, primarily through the Sirt3 signaling pathway, and secondarily through Sirt1 and Unc‐51‐like autophagy‐activating kinases 1 and 2 (ULK1/2). Sirt3, an NAD+ dependent deacetylase found in the mitochondria, regulates numerous antioxidant enzymes. Additionally, FES therapy deacetylates components of mECT, including NADHDH, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), and complex III, increasing the removal of ROS [29]. In this context, a recent study demonstrates the antioxidant power of FES by increasing the expression of superoxide dismutase and other antioxidant enzymes [30].

Regarding platelets, both basal and maximum superoxide formation were reduced in patients before the intervention compared to healthy volunteers, with significant changes observed in maximum formation mediated by MAO. After the intervention, there was a significant increase in superoxide formation in patients, mediated by both enzymes (SDH and MAO). These results indicate that the changes induced by the intervention improve the activity of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (mETC), the MAO enzyme, and that these changes also occur at the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) in this cell type. This finding is relevant because it reflects that the intervention can restore not only internal mitochondrial function but also mitochondrial membrane interactions, which are crucial for regulating processes such as apoptosis and ROS production [31]. Furthermore, improved MAO activity may influence oxidative stress regulation and energy metabolism, given that this enzyme participates in neurotransmitter degradation and hydrogen peroxide generation, a type of ROS [32]. On the other hand, the fact that MAO in PBMCs and SDH in platelets do not show statistically significant differences between healthy volunteers and pre‐intervention patients, but do show a significant increase in mitochondrial activity between pre‐ and post‐intervention patients, could indicate that these measures are more useful as biomarkers for monitoring therapy response rather than for initial diagnosis [33]. This suggests that dynamic assessment of mitochondrial function could be a promising tool to personalize and optimize intervention rehabilitation protocols. Finally, these results align with previous studies showing that functional recovery after stroke is associated with improvements in systemic and local mitochondrial function [34], and that modulation of oxidative stress through therapeutic interventions may facilitate neuronal and muscular plasticity [35].

The mechanisms by which cerebral ischemic injury affects mitochondria in skeletal muscle highlight the complexity and interconnectedness of several pathophysiological processes. After stroke, a disconnection is observed in the neuro‐muscular and neural communication that regulates both muscle contraction and energy metabolism in skeletal muscle. This disruption may be mediated by altered neurotransmission, reduced release of neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine, and dysfunction in receptor sensitivity at the motor plate, which compromises muscle activation and local metabolic regulation [36, 37]. Oxidative stress plays a crucial role in post‐stroke pathogenesis, resulting in a significant increase in ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) production. These reactive compounds can diffuse through the bloodstream and directly affect the structure and function of mitochondria in skeletal muscle, inducing oxidative damage at the mitochondrial level and compromising energy efficiency [32, 38]. This mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to impaired cellular energy production, exacerbating the adverse effects of stroke on muscle function. Likewise, the systemic inflammatory response triggered by stroke also plays a significant role in post‐ischemic muscle pathogenesis [28, 39]. The release of proinflammatory cytokines such as alpha‐Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF‐α) and interleukins (IL‐1β and IL‐6), along with the activation of immune cells such as macrophages and lymphocytes, amplifies oxidative stress and dysregulates cellular homeostasis in skeletal muscle [35, 40]. These inflammatory mediators can penetrate muscle tissue through the blood circulation, triggering inflammatory signaling cascades that negatively affect mitochondrial function and contribute to post‐stroke muscle damage [14]. Furthermore, additional metabolic alterations, including hypoxia and reduced supply of glucose and other energy substrates, further compromise the ability of skeletal muscle to maintain oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial electron transport chain activity. These metabolic changes significantly alter ATP production and cellular energy homeostasis, affecting the functional capacity of skeletal muscle [18, 19]. Finally, alterations in cell membrane permeability and mitochondrial membrane function occur during stroke, exacerbating post‐ischemic tissue damage by compromising the structural integrity of mitochondria in skeletal muscle [37]. This mitochondrial membrane dysfunction can also trigger the release of pro‐apoptotic proteins and the activation of programmed cell death pathways, contributing to loss of muscle function.

The main findings obtained in both cell types highlight the reduction of mitochondrial activity in pre‐intervention stroke patients compared to healthy volunteers. In this regard, various studies conducted in the brains of gerbils, humans, and in peripheral blood cells have demonstrated that the activity of mETC complexes was significantly reduced following the onset of an ischemic or haemorrhagic stroke [27, 35, 37]. This can be associated with the role of oxidative stress in the pathophysiological mechanism of stroke [16]. Furthermore, intervention significantly restores the activity of the mETC. The changes are intrinsic to the mitochondria, as they can be observed both internally and externally, and in both cell types. This could promote ATP synthesis, reduction of ROS, and increase in antioxidant systems, leading to improvement in energy production and functional and motor capacity of skeletal muscle [34, 35, 36]. Considering the significant clinical response, it could be proposed to monitor the functional capacity of stroke patients through their energy capacity, as well as the use of mitochondrial activity as a potential biomarker in the pathophysiology of stroke and/or in the efficacy of FES intervention combined with physical exercises.

Therefore, the effect of the intervention on motor function and mitochondrial activity in stroke patients is highly relevant. The results obtained could support the implementation of the methodology described here to deepen our understanding of the biomolecular mechanisms underlying motor sequelae after stroke, thus promoting advances in therapeutic approaches. Moreover, the study opens up new and promising future perspectives in the field of neuromuscular rehabilitation. This suggests its broader integration into post‐stroke rehabilitation protocols, aiming to enhance functional recovery and patient quality of life. Additionally, assessing mitochondrial activity could become a key tool for evaluating the extent of mitochondrial damage after stroke and accurately monitoring the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions, fostering more personalized treatment strategies. Finally, a deeper understanding of how FES combined with physical exercises affects mitochondrial function could inspire the development of new therapies focused not only on functional recovery but also on optimizing cellular bioenergetics and protecting against oxidative stress in a wide range of neuromuscular contexts beyond stroke (Table 3).
