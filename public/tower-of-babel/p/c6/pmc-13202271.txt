Pre-operative predictors for conjoined tendon impingement following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty
Authors: Estelle Wigmore, Max Mackay-Cruise, Stephen Kemp, Joshua G Twiggs, Tiane O'Connor, Mark Taylor, Rami MA Al-Dirini, Sonya R Murray, Minas Petrelis, Benjamin East
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) reliably relieves pain and restores function. Yet anterior shoulder pain persists in some patients, with its underlying cause remaining unclear. One proposed mechanism is altered anterior anatomy leading to impingement of the conjoined tendon. This exploratory study investigates potential anatomical and surgical planning predictors of conjoined tendon impingement following rTSA.MethodsNine rTSA patients with intraoperatively confirmed conjoined tendon impingement against the lesser tuberosity were matched to 9 asymptomatic controls by sex, operative side, and age. Forty anatomical and planned surgical parameters were derived from pre-operative computed tomography–based 3-dimensional planning. Parameters with P < .1 in univariate testing entered exhaustive binomial logistic regression with backward stepwise refinement. Model performance was assessed using leave-one-out cross-validation.ResultsThe optimal model identified 3 potential predictors of impingement: greater retroversion of the planned humeral stem relative to native, greater anteversion of the planned glenoid baseplate, and a more inferior coracoid position relative to the glenoid center. Validation showed 79.0% overall prediction accuracy. Predicted impingement likelihood was 76.1 ± 30.9% in symptomatic patients vs. and 22.2 ± 16.2% in asymptomatic patients (P = .0012).ConclusionAnatomical and surgical planning parameters can increase the risk of conjoined tendon impingement through mechanisms proposed to include altered humeral rotation, tuberosity positioning, and reduced joint distalization. These results provide early insight into potential predictors of impingement, offering a foundation for future research. A model based on anatomical and surgical planning parameters demonstrated feasibility for identifying intraoperatively observed conjoined tendon impingement, achieving a classification accuracy of 79%. Integrating a model derived from these findings into pre-operative planning may help inform implant selection and positioning, with the potential to reduce the risk of post-operative anterior shoulder pain and improve functional outcomes.Keywords: Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Conjoined tendon, Impingement, Post-operative pain, Anterior shoulder pain, Pre-operative planning

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is routinely performed for a broad range of shoulder pathologies. Its use has steadily increased over the past decade, with rTSA accounting for 85.7% of all primary total shoulder arthroplasties in Australia by 2023, up from 57.2% in 2008.3 Similarly, procedure volume in the United States rose by over 190% between 2011 and 2017.21 A study by Mayfield et al15 reported that while osteoarthritis remains the most common indication for rTSA (54%), other significant indications include proximal humerus fractures (12%) and rotator cuff tears (13%),15 reflecting its expanding role across diverse clinical scenarios. While rTSA has demonstrated effectiveness in relieving pain and improving function,14,19 dissatisfaction is reported by up to 21% of patients following the procedure.17 Complications such as instability, infection, component loosening, and periprosthetic fracture are well recognized3; however, persistent shoulder pain continues to pose a considerable clinical challenge.

Anterior shoulder pain following rTSA is a contributor to post-operative dissatisfaction,8,20 yet its etiology is often unclear and challenging to treat.7,20 One proposed mechanism involves altered anatomical and dynamic relationships among anterior shoulder structures, such as the coracoid process, conjoined tendon, and lesser tuberosity, resulting from changes to the glenohumeral joint center. Medialization with distalization, or increasingly, lateralization of the joint center, can alter soft tissue tension and disrupt native function, leading to impingement or overloading of the conjoined tendon,5,13,15 as well as stress reactions in the coracoid.1,2 Impingement between the conjoined tendon and proximal humerus during internal rotation has also been suggested as a potential pain source,5,7,12 with Fleet et al7 identifying rTSA component design parameters and positions, such as larger glenospheres and increased humeral component retroversion, as factors that heighten the risk. Consistent with these hypotheses, several studies have demonstrated that conjoined tendon lengthening relieves anterior shoulder discomfort8,20 and enhances range of motion (ROM),18 indicating that conjoined tendon pathology may contribute to anterior shoulder pain. However, the influence of individual variations in native anatomy on this impingement risk remains yet to be explored.

The ability to assess the risk of pain-inducing conjoined tendon lengthening or impingement during pre-operative planning may enable clinicians to take proactive measures, reducing avoidable complications and optimizing patient outcomes. The purpose of this study is to identify potential anatomical and surgical planning predictors of conjoined tendon impingement following rTSA. It is hypothesized that the size of the humeral lesser tuberosity, distance between the coracoid and the glenoid center, and version and lateralization of the implanted components would affect the incidence of impingement.

Materials and methods

Nine patients were identified intraoperatively based on dynamic evaluation for conjoined tendon impingement. Following component implantation, the operating surgeon performed ROM assessments, positioning the shoulder through forward elevation, adduction, and internal and external rotation to identify any mechanical conflict between the lesser tuberosity of the humerus and the conjoined tendon (Fig. 1A). Conjoined tendon impingement was defined as a distinct displacement or snap caused when the lesser tuberosity passed under the lateral edge of the conjoined tendon before the tendon settled into the bicipital groove (Fig. 1B). In cases where impingement was observed, the tendon was managed via a surgical cascade. The procedure began with debulking of the lesser tuberosity. If necessary, this was followed by conjoined tendon recession, in which approximately half of the leading edge of the conjoined tendon was released and folded back on itself (Fig. 1C). Finally, the folded edge was sutured to its medial border, a technique referred to as conjoined tendon imbrication (Fig. 2). Following surgical correction of the conjoined tendon impingement, the shoulder was re-evaluated through the same ROM to confirm resolution of impingement prior to wound closure. These patients were subsequently classified as exhibiting intraoperative findings consistent with symptomatic conjoined tendon impingement following rTSA. At the start of the study period, intraoperative assessment was performed selectively in cases where impingement was suspected; however, as clinical awareness increased, routine intraoperative and post-operative screening were implemented for all rTSA cases.

A cohort of equal size (n = 9 per group), matched by sex, operative side, and age, was identified from asymptomatic patients who had previously undergone rTSA with no intraoperatively observed conjoined tendon impingement and no post-operative anterior shoulder pain at a minimum of 12 months' follow-up. Inclusion criteria for both cohorts were patients undergoing primary rTSA surgery with the AltiVate Reverse prosthesis (Enovis, Austin, TX, USA) with pre-operative planning. Patients undergoing revision surgery were excluded. A total of 18 patients (9 symptomatic and 9 asymptomatic/control) were included, with 12 (66.7%) female and a mean age of 66.9 ± 7.8 (56-82) (Table I). All procedures were performed by 1 of 3 participating surgeons from the same center.

All patients had undergone a routine pre-operative shoulder computed tomography (CT) scan as part of the standard clinical protocol. The CT scans were segmented and landmarked with a validated protocol. The reverse prosthesis was virtually implanted in the ASTRA surgical planning platform (Enovis, Austin, TX, USA) by the surgeon prior to surgery. Patient-specific instrumentation guides were designed and printed to execute the planned version and inclination of the glenoid component, and the planned version, neck–shaft angle, and osteotomy height for the humeral component.

Pre-operative parameters were taken against the pre-operative CT scan or 3-dimensional segmented bones. In particular, humeral lesser tuberosity size was measured on the axial CT slice corresponding to its peak prominence, as determined in a 3-dimensional view. On this slice, the maximum mediolateral (width) and anteroposterior (depth) distances were then recorded (Fig. 3). To account for body habitus, both measurements were normalized by dividing them by the humeral head diameter. The position of the coracoid was measured as the distance from the origin of the biceps brachii on the coracoid process to the glenoid center. The lateral and superior measurements were taken based on the projection of these 2 points onto the scapular coronal plane (defined by the glenoid center, trigonum spinae, and inferior angle of the scapula) along and perpendicular to the Friedman line, respectively. The anterior measurement was taken perpendicular to the Friedman line in the scapular axial plane (Fig. 4). The measurements for coracoid position were likewise normalized by expressing them as a ratio relative to the length of the Friedman line.

The superior (yellow arrow), lateral (pink arrow), and anterior (orange arrow) position of the coracoid was measured between the origin of the biceps brachii (red dot) and glenoid center (green dot) along or perpendicular to the Friedman line (blue line) in the scapular coronal (A) and axial (B) planes.

Surgical parameters were taken from the planned component orientations or calculated from these values. Planned humeral version was defined as the angle between the implant's neck axis and the anatomic neck of the humerus in the humeral axial plane. Planned glenoid version was defined as the angle between the central screw of the baseplate and the Friedman line in the scapular axial plane.

Descriptive statistics were used to determine means and standard deviations. Balance between the symptomatic and asymptomatic cohorts was evaluated by calculating standardized mean differences for each matching variable (sex, operative side, and age). Normality of variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-sample t tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed to compare means between symptomatic and asymptomatic cohorts for normally distributed and non-normally distributed parameters, respectively. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Generalized linear models using binomial logistic regression were used to predict symptomatic status. An exhaustive model selection process was conducted, evaluating all possible combinations of pre-operative and planned parameters that showed a trend toward significance in univariate analysis (P < .1). For each combination of parameters, model generation and refinement with backward stepwise selection was repeated 10 times. The bias-corrected error was averaged across iterations, and the parameter combination with the lowest error was selected. The performance of the final model was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation to maximize training data usage and provide an unbiased performance estimate. In each iteration, 1 symptomatic and 1 asymptomatic patient were excluded, a model was trained on the remaining data, and predicted probabilities of being symptomatic were generated for the excluded patients. Classifications were considered correct if symptomatic patients had a predicted likelihood greater than or equal to 50% or if asymptomatic patients had a likelihood less than 50%. Model performance was also evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. For each metric, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the exact binomial method.

This study was conducted as a feasibility study. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; the sample was determined by available symptomatic cases meeting inclusion criteria and the need to obtain initial estimates of effect size and variability to inform a subsequent definitive study.

Ethics for this retrospective study was approved by the Bellberry Human Research Ethics Committee (study number 201203710).

Results

The unadjusted standardized mean difference for age between the matched symptomatic and asymptomatic groups was −0.028 (P = .954), indicating minimal imbalance. Gender and operative side were perfectly balanced between the 2 cohorts (P = 1 for both) (Table I).

The generalized linear model with the lowest average bias-corrected error (0.17) trended toward significance (P = .10) and included 3 potential predictors: planned humeral stem version change from native, planned glenoid baseplate version, and the pre-operative normalized coracoid superior position (Table III). The symptomatic cohort tended to have a more retroverted planned stem version, a more anteverted planned baseplate version, and a less superiorly positioned coracoid process relative to the glenoid center. No individual parameter was significantly different between the 2 cohorts (P > .07) (Table II).

Leave-one-out cross-validation produced 81 trained models, corresponding to the number of unique symptomatic–asymptomatic patient pairs. Each model generated 2 predictions per iteration, one for each excluded patient, resulting in 162 total predictions across all iterations. Out of these, 128 classifications (79.0%) were correct. The model achieved a sensitivity of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.63-0.84) and specificity of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.71-0.90), with positive predictive value of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.69-0.89) and negative predictive value of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.66-0.86). The average predicted likelihood of impingement was 76.1% (±30.9) for symptomatic patients, and 22.2% (±16.2) for asymptomatic patients, which was statistically significantly different (P = .0012) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The etiology of anterior shoulder pain following rTSA remains multifactorial and not yet fully understood. One proposed mechanism, conjoined tendon impingement, has previously been described8,20; however, its underlying causes and potential preventive measures remain unclear.

The increasing adoption of lateralized components in rTSA reflects ongoing efforts to enhance joint biomechanics and reduce complication rates.1,4 While the Grammont-style prosthesis remains effective for treating massive rotator cuff tears and cuff arthropathy, it has notable limitations, including a reduced impingement-free ROM, increased scapular notching leading to polyethylene wear, and a higher risk of nerve palsy due to excessive limb lengthening.11,16 In addition, Tashjian et al20 have proposed over-distalization of the humerus as a potential contributor to conjoined tendinitis.

The introduction of glenoid lateralization has helped restore more anatomic biomechanics, leading to improvements in internal rotation and dynamic ROM.9,10,16 However, this shift in design alters the mechanism by which the conjoined tendon may be disrupted. Rather than tendinitis due to excessive tension, the primary concern becomes mechanical impingement of the humerus. This risk is hypothesized to stem from 3 main factors: (1) an increased radius of the humeral rotation arc relative to native anatomy, which may bring the humerus into closer proximity with adjacent soft tissues, including the conjoined tendon (Fig. 6); (2) restoration of internal rotation range, which brings the lesser tuberosity more anteriorly during mid- to terminal internal rotation, increasing the likelihood of impingement (Fig. 6); and (3) reduced distalization with a smaller neck–shaft angle, which preserves a greater volume of bone near the glenohumeral joint and increases the risk of impingement. Furthermore, reduced distalization positions the lesser tuberosity more superiorly and closer to the coracoid, increasing the risk of impingement occurring nearer to the tendon's attachment. This may exacerbate pain and discomfort, as greater force is required to displace the tendon due to a shorter moment arm and increased stiffness near the fixed end.

Although this study did not evaluate the impact of implant design, the positioning of the component was found to influence the risk of conjoined tendon impingement. The symptomatic cohort was more likely to have a humeral component positioned with increased retroversion relative to native anatomy than the asymptomatic cohort. A more retroverted humeral component would displace the humerus anteriorly if humeral rotation remained unchanged, consistent with Kontaxis et al,13 who reported that such displacement can cause extra-articular impingement against the coracoid during glenohumeral scaption. In addition, a more retroverted humeral osteotomy would remove a greater proportion of posterior bone while preserving more of the anterior surface, potentially contributing to both the severity and early onset of impingement against the conjoined tendon.

A more anteverted planned glenoid component was also identified as a potential predictor of impingement. Fleet et al7 reported that a more anteriorly placed baseplate resulted in earlier impingement between the humerus and conjoined tendon. These findings may reflect a common mechanism, in which reduced baseplate retroversion positions the humerus more anteriorly, increasing the risk of early tendon impingement. Nevertheless, determining optimal baseplate version requires balancing the mitigation of conjoined tendon impingement with the maximization of rotational ROM and achieving reliable glenoid fixation.

The final potential predictor contributing to the model was coracoid process position, with symptomatic patients more likely to exhibit a more inferiorly positioned coracoid process. Consistent with the third proposed mechanism of conjoined tendon disruption associated with lateralized implant design, a more inferiorly positioned coracoid reduces the clearance between the lesser tuberosity and the conjoined tendon. This reduction increases the risk of impingement and shifts the contact point closer to the tendon's origin, where impingement is more likely to be symptomatic. Klosterman et al12 similarly found that a decreased subcoracoid distance was associated with increased pain and reduced internal rotation following rTSA, with the authors postulating that this was due to impingement against the coracoid or conjoined tendon.

The size of the lesser tuberosity was hypothesized to increase the rate of impingement by adding bone volume adjacent to the conjoined tendon, thereby raising the likelihood of contact. Similarly, greater component lateralization of the glenoid, humerus, or both was expected to elevate the risk of impingement by increasing the radius of the humeral rotation arc and shifting the humerus further anteriorly. Baseplate lateralization showed a trend toward significance in univariate analysis, but none of these variables reached statistical significance, nor were they retained in the final model. Their contribution may be outweighed by other factors when considered in combination, but it is possible that with a larger sample size, these parameters may achieve statistical significance and be incorporated into future iterations of the model.

The model achieved 79% accuracy in classifying patients at risk for conjoined tendon impingement, with moderate diagnostic performance overall. Sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values ranged from 0.75 to 0.82, indicating reasonable discrimination between symptomatic and asymptomatic patients. However, these findings should be interpreted considering the study's limitations, particularly the small sample size and potential risk of overfitting, discussed below. Nevertheless, the results demonstrated trends towards significance and support the potential utility of a predictive model derived from this work.

This study had some limitations. First, the small sample size reflects the exploratory nature of the study and the low incidence of diagnosed conjoined tendon impingement, limiting statistical power. The number of model parameters relative to the sample size may also increase the risk of overfitting and optimistic estimates of performance. Although cross-validation and backward stepwise selection were applied to reduce this risk, these measures cannot fully eliminate it. Given the infrequency of diagnosed conjoined tendinitis, larger studies may be necessary to validate these results and assess their applicability across diverse patient populations. Second, surgical parameters were based on the planned implant positions and did not account for intraoperative adjustments or deviations from the intended alignment. While the use of patient-specific instrumentation likely reduced variability (particularly in glenoid version and inclination, and humeral version, neck–shaft angle, and osteotomy height) post-operative imaging was not consistently available, preventing verification of the implanted component orientation and limiting the direct clinical applicability of the findings. Future prospective studies could address this limitation by evaluating the final implanted orientation using standardized post-operative imaging, such as multiplanar x-rays or CT scans. Third, native humeral version could not be incorporated as an input parameter due to the limitation of standard imaging protocols, which typically capture only the proximal humerus. The observed influence of the deviation between native and planned humeral version on the model suggests that patient-specific anatomical variations to humeral version may play a significant role and should be included in future models if feasible.

Further work to expand the model's generalizability will also require comparisons across different implant designs. Evaluating how design features influence the likelihood of conjoined tendon impingement can help ensure the model is applicable across a broader range of surgical approaches and patient populations. Furthermore, this study aimed to predict the likelihood of intraoperatively observed impingement. However, to address the clinical issue of post-operative anterior shoulder pain, it is necessary to establish a link between impingement observed intraoperatively by the surgeon and functional impingement and pain experienced by the patient. Future studies could further explore this relationship by assessing subscapularis repair technique and post-operative rehabilitation protocols, as both factors may significantly impact long-term patient outcomes. Prospective studies should validate and refine the potential predictors of conjoined tendon impingement described in this work, and assess their clinical impact, including interventions and patient outcomes in cases with and without pre-operative identification.

Existing approaches to soft-tissue analysis often rely on additional imaging such as magnetic resonance imaging or on complex computational methods like finite element analysis, which can limit their routine, patient-specific applicability.6,22 Models derived from this study may offer a low-cost and low-risk tool to support surgical planning. They could inform implant positioning, guide patient expectation management, and optimize patient selection, which may ultimately help reduce intraoperative interventions and post-operative complications, particularly anterior shoulder pain. Validation studies in larger, prospective cohorts are warranted.

Conclusion

Anterior shoulder pain remains a significant source of dissatisfaction following rTSA, with growing evidence linking it to altered anatomical relationships and mechanical impingement involving the conjoined tendon. This study identified 3 potential predictors of conjoined tendon impingement from pre-operative anatomical and surgical planning parameters and developed a model that classified at-risk patients with 79% accuracy. Integrating a model derived from these findings into pre-operative planning may enable surgeons to proactively optimize implant positioning and reduce the incidence of post-operative anterior shoulder pain.
