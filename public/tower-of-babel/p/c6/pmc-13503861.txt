Stretchable and self-healing copper–iodide scintillator for conformal X-ray imaging and wearable radiation alerting
Authors: Yu Shen, Ting Pan, Xiaoya Cheng, Senyu Zhang, Pu Yan, Jiaxing Li, Tangzhe Chen, Meijuan Ding, Xiangmei Liu, Qiang Zhao, Zhongfu An, Wen-Yong Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible X-ray scintillators are critical for next-generation conformal imaging and wearable radiation monitoring, yet their development is hindered by the persistent trade-offs among particle dispersion, scintillation efficiency, and mechanical robustness in conventional composite designs. Here, we overcome these limitations via a ligand-matrix interface engineering strategy, integrating highly soluble copper-iodide clusters with a self-healing fluorinated block copolymer to produce highly stretchable and autonomously self-healing scintillator films. Interfacial coordination and ion-dipole interactions guide the in situ self-assembly of uniformly dispersed, aggregation-induced emission-active clusters within the polymer matrix, while dynamic dipole-dipole interactions between fluorinated segments confer high stretchability (up to 2000% strain) and room-temperature self-healing capability. The resulting films achieve a high spatial resolution of 16.4 lp mm−1, enabling distortion-free conformal imaging of curved objects and maintaining high-fidelity imaging under severe mechanical deformation or in aqueous environments. Furthermore, dip-coated textiles functionalized with the composite serve as wearable radiation-alert systems that synergize real-time visual detection with passive X-ray shielding. This work provides a versatile materials platform that reconciles long-standing performance conflicts, paving the way for advanced conformal diagnostics and scalable personal radiation protection technologies.

Introduction

Flexible X-ray scintillators are poised to transform radiation detection and imaging by enabling conformal medical diagnostics, large-area inspection of curved surfaces, and wearable safety monitors—applications inaccessible to conventional rigid scintillators1–4. Luminescent metal-halide clusters and perovskites are attractive candidates due to their high X-ray attenuation coefficients, excellent light yields, and low detection limits5–7. A common route to flexibility involves embedding such crystalline scintillators (single crystals, microcrystals, or nanocrystals) into polymer matrices such as poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)8–10. This physical embedding strategy, however, faces two intrinsic limitations that compromise performance: poor dispersion of scintillating crystals induces severe light scattering, leading to imaging vignetting, degraded spatial resolution, and reduced sensitivity11; meanwhile, repeated mechanical deformation and abrasion during use, especially in wearable devices, cause fatigue and damage, introducing signal noise and undermining long-term reliability12,13. Achieving uniform dispersion, mechanical robustness, and autonomous self-healing in a single flexible film is therefore critical for the practical applications.

In situ formation of scintillators within polymer matrices can improve dispersion by controlling precursor concentration and growth during polymerization, thereby reducing light scattering14–16. Nevertheless, scintillators fabricated this way often suffer from defects, residual byproducts, and diminished photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQYs), primarily due to insufficient control over nucleation and growth kinetics17. More fundamentally, the inherent stiffness mismatch between rigid crystalline scintillators and soft polymers compromises both scintillation efficiency and mechanical flexibility of the composite18. Reconciling the trade-offs among high scintillation efficiency, mechanical stretchability, and long-term operational stability remains a pivotal challenge.

Herein, we introduce a ligand-matrix interface engineering strategy to fabricate highly stretchable, autonomous self-healing scintillator films (Fig. 1), utilizing a highly soluble copper-iodide cluster (CuI-PTPP) and a tailored fluorinated block copolymer (FMA-FBA). During the curing of FMA-FBA, molecularly monodisperse CuI-PTPP self-assembles into amorphous, uniformly dispersed clusters within the copolymer matrix. Synergistic interfacial ion-dipole and weak coordination interactions between clusters and the polymer preserve the dual thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF) and phosphorescence emissions of CuI-PTPP, maintaining a high PLQY in the composite. Concurrently, dynamic dipole-dipole interactions within the FMA-FBA matrix endow the film with high stretchability, autonomous self-healing, and robust environmental stability. The resulting large-area flexible film achieves a high spatial resolution of 16.4 lp mm−1, enabling conformal imaging of curved objects and high-fidelity X-ray imaging at strains up to 500%. Furthermore, dip-coating the composite onto textiles creates wearable radiation-alert systems that integrate visual radiation detection with effective passive shielding. Results

Design, structure, and intrinsic properties of CuI-PTPP clusters

Copper–iodide clusters are promising earth-abundant and low-toxicity emitters, yet their practical application is often limited by poor solution processability19–21. To overcome this, we employed a highly soluble, strongly luminescent copper–iodide cluster (CuI-PTPP) synthesized via a synergistic ligand-pair strategy reported previously22,23, using tris(3-methylphenyl)phosphine (P-(m-Tol)3) to confer high solubility (up to 500 mg mL−1 in common organic solvents, Supplementary Table 1), and diphenyl-2-pyridylphosphine (Pyrphos) to rigidly chelate and stabilize the inorganic core via its N and P donors (Fig. 2a). Consistent with previous reports, single-crystal X-ray diffraction reveals a zero-dimensional dimeric Cu2I2 core coordinated by one bridging Pyrphos and two terminal P-(m-Tol)3 ligands, with Cu–I, Cu–P, and Cu–N bond lengths of 2.65–2.74, 2.25, and 2.11 Å, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 2, 3). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that the bond dissociation energy (BDE) values of the Cu–I, Cu–Pyrphos, and Cu–P-(m-Tol)3 bonds are 3.30, 0.84, and 0.55 eV, respectively, indicating strong resistance to bond cleavage under external stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 2, Table 4). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern of the as-synthesized CuI-PTPP crystals exhibits sharp, well-defined diffraction peaks in agreement with the simulated pattern (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4), confirming high crystallinity and phase purity. Our density functional theory (DFT) calculations show that the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is localized on the Cu2I2 core, while the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) resides on the pyridine moiety of Pyrphos (Fig. 2b), facilitating efficient metal/iodide-to-ligand charge transfer (MLCT/XLCT)24.Fig. 2Photophysical properties, solubility, and stability of CuI-PTPP.a Molecular structure of CuI-PTPP (H atoms omitted for clarity). b Frontier molecular orbitals and singlet (S1)/triplet (T1) energy levels. The as-synthesized CuI-PTPP powder exhibits a strong, single-band emission centered at 550 nm with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 120 nm, color coordinates (0.404, 0.564), a large Stokes shift of 170 nm (Fig. 2c), and a high PLQY of 92 ± 1%. Temperature‑dependent time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectra and decay curves were recorded from 80 to 300 K (Fig. 2d, e, Supplementary Fig. 5), and the bi-exponential fits are summarized in Supplementary Table 5. As the temperature increases, the average lifetime decreases from 24.9 µs at 80 K to 10.2 µs at 300 K. The radiative rate constant (kr) of 4.01 × 104 s−1 at 80 K is consistent with efficient phosphorescence enabled by strong spin–orbit coupling (T1–S0 transition)20,25. At 300 K, kr rises to 9.06 × 104 s−1, suggesting thermally assisted population exchange between the S1 and T1 states. A small singlet–triplet energy gap (ΔEST) of 0.028 eV from time-dependent DFT calculation, 0.034 eV from kinetic fitting, and 0.027 eV from steady-state spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 6–10) further supports the thermally activated reverse intersystem crossing (RISC), characteristic of thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF). Benefiting from efficient spin–orbit coupling and a small ΔEST, CuI-PTPP features a dual-emission pathway combining TADF and phosphorescence. At low temperatures, the excited-state population is predominantly frozen in the T1 state, leading to dominant phosphorescence. As the temperature increases, the S1 state is gradually repopulated through the RISC process from the T1 state, activating the TADF channel and shortening the overall PL lifetime. Quantitatively, TADF and phosphorescence contribute 65% and 35% of the total emission at 300 K, respectively (Fig. 2f)26. This dual emission is consistent with the established MLCT/XLCT mechanism in hybrid copper–halide complexes and enables efficient harvesting of triplet excitons for X-ray scintillation.

Having elucidated the photoluminescence (PL) mechanism of CuI-PTPP, we next examined its structural stability and solution processability, both essential for achieving homogeneously dispersed films. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 11, the PXRD patterns of CuI-PTPP after 14 days of ambient air exposure and water immersion are highly consistent with the as-synthesized CuI-PTPP crystals and the simulated pattern, confirming the preservation of its crystalline structure. Furthermore, the PL intensity of CuI-PTPP remained 90% of the initial intensity after 14 days of exposure to ambient air or immersion in water (Supplementary Fig. 12), demonstrating high chemical stability toward oxygen and moisture. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicates high thermal stability, with decomposition temperature exceeding 220 °C (Supplementary Fig. 13). The rational multidentate ligand design and high BDE values between Cu ions and organic ligands endowed the CuI-PTPP with high structural stability. To confirm structural integrity upon dissolution, CuI-PTPP was characterized in deuterated tetrahydrofuran (THF-d8) by 31P nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. The spectrum showed chemical shifts distinct from free P-(m-Tol)3 and Pyrphos ligands, confirming retention of the coordination framework and absence of ligand dissociation during solvation (Fig. 2g). The 31P NMR spectrum of CuI-PTPP is broader than that of free ligands, consistent with increased structural flexibility in the solvated state27. As shown in Fig. 2h, CuI-PTPP in the dispersed state (~20 mg mL−1) exhibits weak luminescence and a broader, red-shifted emission at 620 nm relative to the pristine powder, due to enhanced molecular motion and dynamic equilibria of solvated species28. Optical and structural reversibility were assessed by reprecipitating the complex from THF with n‑hexane. The reprecipitated suspension reproduced the emission wavelength and intensity of the pristine powder, confirming aggregation‑induced emission (AIE) behavior29,30. PXRD of the precipitate shows a broad diffraction peak centered near 8°, indicative of an amorphous assembly of CuI-PTPP clusters (Fig. 2i). In this amorphous solid, abundant intermolecular interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces and π–π stacking) strongly restrict rotational and vibrational motions of the phenyl and methylphenyl rings, thereby restoring the radiative emissive pathway31. This combination of high luminescence efficiency, structural integrity in solution, and AIE characteristic renders CuI-PTPP well-suited for fabricating homogeneous, high-performance composite films via solution processing.

Fabrication, dispersion, and interfacial interactions of CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite films

To provide a stretchable and chemically compatible matrix for luminescent CuI-PTPP clusters, we synthesized a fluorinated block copolymer (FMA-FBA) by UV-initiated (365 nm) copolymerization of 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate (FMA) and 2,2,3,4,4,4-hexafluorobutyl acrylate (FBA) (Fig. 3a)32. The polymer design leverages complementary segment functions: the hard FMA blocks provide mechanical integrity and a confined microenvironment for cluster encapsulation, while the soft FBA segments impart toughness and flexibility. The resulting copolymer possesses a high molecular weight (Mw, ~172,127 g mol−1) and narrow dispersity (PDI, 1.5), ensuring structural uniformity and favorable solution processability. Comprehensive 1H and 19F NMR spectroscopy confirmed successful copolymerization with an approximate 1:1 molar ratio and preservation of fluorinated functionalities (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 15). In the 19F NMR spectrum, characteristic resonances at −76, −118, and −216 ppm are assigned to fluorine in the trifluoromethyl (–CF3), difluoromethylene (–CF2), and monofluoromethylene (–CF), respectively. The pure FMA-FBA film exhibits the following mechanical properties, including Young’s modulus of 23.5 MPa, tensile strength of 2.5 MPa, and elongation at break exceeding 1700% (Supplementary Fig. 16). The high density of terminal –CF3 groups from both comonomers strengthens intermolecular dipole–dipole interactions, which are central to the material’s self-healing capability33. Furthermore, the 0.15-mm-thick FMA-FBA copolymer film shows ~95% optical transparency across the visible spectrum (400–800 nm, Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 17), ensuring minimal attenuation of the scintillation photons.

The high solubility and stability of CuI-PTPP enabled the straightforward fabrication of cluster–polymer composite films by solution blending in THF (Fig. 3c). PXRD of a composite containing 10 wt% CuI-PTPP shows the complete absence of crystalline diffraction peaks from the cluster, with only a broad halo characteristic of the amorphous FMA-FBA matrix centered at ~18° (Fig. 2i). This confirms the molecular-level dispersion of CuI-PTPP without detectable crystallization or phase segregation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal uniformly dispersed CuI-PTPP particles with an average size of ~160 nm and no significant agglomeration (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 18). The homogeneity of the luminescent phase at the microscale was verified by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), which shows spatially uniform green fluorescence without localized bright aggregates or dark voids (Fig. 3e), critical for achieving uniform light emission. To elucidate the interfacial interactions responsible for the homogeneous dispersion, we performed an Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, the IGMH isosurface color map—blue for strong attraction, green for weak van der Waals interactions, and red for repulsion—reveals dominant attractive interactions at the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA interface. The Cu–O distance is 2.41 Å, and the corresponding green and cyan isosurfaces indicate a weak coordination interaction. The Cu–F distance is 3.44 Å, consistent with a weak interaction between CuI-PTPP and the fluorinated polymer segments. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) shows a decreased contribution from the ester C = O component (532.1 eV) upon incorporation of CuI-PTPP in O 1 s, together with corresponding changes in the C 1 s spectrum (Fig. 3g, h), consistent with weak coordination between electron-deficient Cu+ centers and the lone-pair electrons of the ester carbonyl groups. Solid-state 19F NMR further supports interfacial coupling: the –CF2 resonance broadens, and the –CF resonance shifts from −221.4 ppm in pure FMA-FBA to −217.0 ppm in the composite, indicative of ion–dipole interactions between Cu⁺ and the fluorinated polymer segments (Fig. 3i). For comparison, dispersing CuI-PTPP in a non-fluorinated copolymer (ethyl methacrylate-butyl acrylate, EMA-BA) resulted in larger (~250 nm) and more sparsely distributed particles (Supplementary Fig. 20), indicating enhanced compatibility in the fluorinated matrix. These complementary analyses collectively demonstrate that synergistic coordination and ion-dipole interactions (Fig. 3f) effectively enhance the compatibility between CuI-PTPP and FMA-FBA matrix, enabling the fabrication of homogeneous, luminescent composite films with optimal dispersion.

Photoluminescent, mechanical, and environmental stability of composite films

The photoluminescent properties of the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite films were characterized under UV excitation. Films with varying cluster loadings exhibit bright yellow emission centered at 550 nm (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 21), identical to that of pristine CuI-PTPP powder, confirming that the local coordination and electronic structure of the cluster are preserved within the polymer matrix. The composite films show short PL lifetimes of 6.8–8.7 μs and retain high PLQYs of 55–71% at room temperature (Supplementary Fig. 22). This indicates that the intrinsic dual-emission mechanism (TADF and phosphorescence) remains largely unperturbed upon incorporation, with the moderate PLQY reduction attributable to minor non‑radiative channels introduced by the polymer environment. The composite films are highly hydrophobic (water contact angle ~105°, Supplementary Fig. 23), which effectively shields the embedded clusters from moisture and oxygen. Mechanical testing confirms high stretchability. The composite film containing 10 wt% CuI-PTPP, selected as the optimal composition for subsequent studies, achieves an elongation at break of 2100% with a Young’s modulus of 12.8 MPa and a fracture stress of 1.0 MPa (Fig. 4c), significantly surpassing previously reported stretchable scintillators (Supplementary Table 4). Cyclic tensile tests to 1200% strain show nearly overlapping stress-strain curves over five consecutive cycles (Supplementary Fig. 24), indicating minimal hysteresis and good mechanical durability. Crucially, the film maintains bright and uniform luminescence under severe deformation, including bending, twisting, and stretching up to 2000% strain (Fig. 4d). A slight decrease in PL intensity is observed only at the maximum strain (Fig. 4e), likely reflecting transient perturbation of interfacial ion–dipole stabilization rather than irreversible damage. Furthermore, the dynamic dipole-dipole interactions within the fluorinated copolymer matrix confer autonomous self-healing capability. When a cut film is brought into contact at room temperature, the fracture visibly heals within 24 h, and the healed region can withstand stretching up to 900% without re-fracturing (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 25), confirming full recovery of mechanical integrity. Direct scanning electron microscopy (SEM) visualization (Supplementary Fig. 26) reveals progressive closure of the crack interface, with complete disappearance of the damage after 24 h. By contrast, the non-fluorinated EMA-BA copolymer showed no observable self-healing at the damaged interface after 24 h under identical conditions (Supplementary Fig. 27). This control experiment confirms that the self-healing behavior depends critically on the fluorinated –CF3 functionality and the associated dynamic dipole–dipole interactions. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the composite films integrate high luminescence efficiency, high stretchability, durable optical-mechanical stability, and autonomous self-healing, key attributes for robust flexible and wearable scintillator applications.

Scintillation performance and application validation

The inherent heavy-atom composition (Cu and I), high luminescence efficiency, and mechanical robustness of the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite prompted an investigation into its X-ray scintillation performance. We established a performance baseline by characterizing pristine CuI-PTPP powder. The powder exhibits high X-ray attenuation across 40–60 keV, comparable to a commercial Bi4Ge3O12 (BGO) crystal; a 0.6-mm-thick layer attenuates over 99.9% of incident X-ray intensity at 50 keV (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 28). Under X-ray excitation, CuI-PTPP produces intense radioluminescence (RL) centered at 550 nm (Supplementary Fig. 29), spectrally identical to its PL spectrum, confirming that the same MLCT/XLCT states, coupled with TADF and phosphorescence, govern the radiative recombination of X-ray-generated carriers (Fig. 5b). The efficient heavy-atom-facilitated generation of electron-hole pairs and the theoretically 100% exciton utilization via dual emission yield a high light output. Referenced to BGO (10,000 photons MeV−1), the light yield of CuI-PTPP powder is estimated as 26,000 ± 500 photons MeV−1 (Fig. 5c). The limit of detection (LOD) was determined as 26.3 nGy s−1 based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 30), which is approximately 200 times lower than the standard requirement (5.5 μGy s−1) for clinical diagnostic imaging. Continuous X-ray irradiation demonstrated the high radiation tolerance of CuI-PTPP and the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite film. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 31, 32, the relative RL intensity of both samples remained nearly unchanged over 20 min of continuous X-ray irradiation, retaining 97% of the initial intensity at a cumulative dose of 136 Gy. The radiation stability of CuI-PTPP was further supported by the nearly unchanged PXRD patterns and XPS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 33), confirming the preservation of its crystallinity, chemical states, and local coordination environment under high‑dose X‑ray irradiation. The rigid structure and high BDE values of CuI‑PTPP effectively suppress bond breakage, ligand dissociation, and Cu reduction during X-ray irradiation. In addition, the radiation-mechanical coupling stability of the CuI‑PTPP/FMA‑FBA film was evaluated. After 50 cycles of 500% strain stretching coupled with 20 min of X-ray exposure, only a slight decay in RL intensity and mechanical stretchability was observed, indicating robust tolerance to combined mechanical deformation and X-ray radiation. We next evaluated the imaging capability of a 0.5-mm-thick CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite film. Spatial resolution, quantified via the slanted-edge method, reaches 16.4 lp mm−1 at the modulation transfer function (MTF) = 0.2 (Fig. 5e), outperforming most reported stretchable scintillators (Supplementary Table 6). This is corroborated visually by resolving ~14.3 lp mm−1 on a standard X-ray test pattern (Fig. 5f). Proof-of-concept imaging resolves the internal circuitry of a memory chip (Fig. 5g). Notably, the film maintains its scintillation intensity and imaging fidelity even when submerged in water, enabling reliable operation in humid or aquatic environments (Fig. 5h). Owing to its TADF/phosphorescence dual-emission mechanism, short decay lifetime, and high light yield, CuI-PTPP is highly promising for dynamic X-ray imaging.

One key advantage of flexible scintillators is their ability to conform to non-planar surfaces, eliminating the air gaps that cause vignetting and spatial distortion in rigid-detector setups4,34. To demonstrate this superiority, we directly compared imaging in flexible conformal versus rigid planar modes (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 34). When conformally wrapped around a curved copper ribbon cable, the film clearly resolves individual conductor strands and insulation boundaries with high fidelity (Fig. 6b, c). In stark contrast, the rigid planar mode produces severe geometric distortion and blurring; cable edges appear warped (Area II), and adjacent conductors merge into an indistinct array (Area I). Such distortions typically necessitate multiple exposures from different angles for accurate reconstruction with rigid detectors, increasing both imaging time and radiation dose. Furthermore, the intrinsic stretchability of the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA film substantially enhances versatility and resource efficiency for large-area imaging, particularly when accommodating objects of varying dimensions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35, the relative RL intensity decreases rapidly to 45.6% of the original value at 500% strain, and further drops to 18.6% at 1000% strain. This reduction is primarily attributed to the decreased film thickness and areal density upon stretching, which diminishes X-ray absorption efficiency according to the Beer–Lambert law. Upon further stretching to 2000% strain, the RL intensity decreases more slowly, reaching 16.4% of the original. Benefiting from the high light yield and uniform cluster dispersion, a film stretched to 500% strain still maintains high-fidelity X-ray imaging capability. As demonstrated in Fig. 6e, the film maintains high-fidelity visualization of internal structures, such as a metal spring enclosed within a plastic capsule, even when reversibly stretched up to 500% strain, with no perceptible loss in spatial resolution or contrast. This on-demand adaptability eliminates the need for multiple size-specific scintillator panels, reducing material waste and simplifying imaging setups, a crucial advantage for portable or on-site applications where miniaturization and operational simplicity are prioritized35. Equally important for long-term use in demanding environments is the ability to autonomously recover from mechanical damage (e.g., scratches, bending fatigue, or cracks), which otherwise impede X-ray transmission and introduce imaging artifacts13,36. The self-healing capability of the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite, rooted in dynamic dipole-dipole interactions that enable reconstruction of both the polymer network and the cluster-matrix interface, effectively mitigates such degradation. As shown in Fig. 6d, a completely severed film fully recovers after 24 h of contact at ambient conditions. Subsequent X-ray imaging and corresponding gray-value profiles confirm that the healed region exhibits no residual cracks or localized signal loss, restoring imaging performance indistinguishable from the pristine film (Supplementary Fig. 36). Together, the intense RL, high stretchability, environmental stability, autonomous self-healing, and high spatial resolution position this material as a compelling candidate for next-generation portable, wearable, and conformal radiation-imaging systems.

The environmentally benign nature and solution processability of the CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite further enable its seamless integration with textiles to create wearable radiation-alert systems. Direct visual indication of X-ray exposure via radioluminescence offers an immediate and intuitive warning mechanism37,38. Using a scalable dip-coating process, we fabricated luminescent cotton fibers and textiles that emit bright yellow light under X-ray or UV excitation (Fig. 6f, g), providing a real-time optical alert to users before exposure reaches hazardous levels. Beyond visual sensing, the fluorinated polymer matrix and heavy-atom-rich clusters afford the textile substantial X-ray attenuation, delivering lightweight, large-area passive shielding, an advantageous alternative to conventional lead-based garments39–41. This dual function of simultaneous radiation alerting and passive protection is particularly valuable for personnel in nuclear facilities, oil-drilling operations, security checkpoints, and medical X-ray environments. The composite-coated textiles also retain mechanical robustness, environmental stability, and resistance to moisture. Notably, the luminescent performance remains unchanged after repeated hand-washing cycles (Supplementary Fig. 37), confirming practical durability and compatibility with routine maintenance.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have demonstrated a robust, highly stretchable, and autonomously self-healing scintillator film through a ligand-matrix interface engineering strategy that integrates radioluminescent CuI-PTPP clusters with a fluorinated FMA-FBA copolymer. This design concurrently achieves high-resolution X-ray imaging, visual radiation alerting, and passive shielding functionality. Key to this advance is the synergistic interplay of coordination and ion-dipole interactions, which guide the molecularly dispersed CuI-PTPP clusters to uniformly self-assemble within the polymer matrix, effectively suppressing light scattering and preserving high luminescence efficiency. Simultaneously, the hard-soft segmented architecture of the FMA-FBA copolymer, governed by dynamic dipole-dipole interactions, confers high mechanical stretchability (up to 2000% elongation) and autonomous self-healing capability. These combined attributes enable the film to maintain a high spatial resolution of 16.4 lp mm−1 and deliver high-quality conformal imaging of curved objects without vignetting, even under severe mechanical deformation. Furthermore, wearable textiles coated with the composite function as integrated personal protection systems, providing real-time visual radiation alerting coupled with inherent X-ray attenuation. This work thus establishes a versatile material platform that reconciles the traditional trade-offs among dispersion uniformity, scintillation efficiency, and mechanical robustness, opening promising avenues for conformal medical diagnostics, non-destructive inspection of complex surfaces, and next-generation wearable radiation-safety technologies.

Methods

Reagents and materials

All materials were used as received without further purification. Copper(I) iodide (CuI, Aladdin, 99.95%), tris(3-methylphenyl)phosphine (P-(m-Tol)3, Aladdin, 99%), diphenyl-2-pyridylphosphine (Pyrphos, Aladdin, 97%), 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate (FMA, Aladdin, 98%), 2,2,3,4,4,4-hexafluorobutyl acrylate (FBA, Aladdin, >95%), ethyl methacrylate (EMA, Aladdin, 99.5%), butyl acrylate (BA, Aladdin, 99.5%), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMPP, Energy Chemical, 98%), acetonitrile (CH3CN, Aladdin, 99.5%), tetrahydrofuran (THF, Aladdin, 99.5%), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, Aladdin, 99.5%).

Preparation of luminescent Cu2I2[tris(3-methylphenyl)phosphine]2diphenyl-2-pyridylphosphin (CuI-PTPP) cluster

CuI (190.5 mg, 1 mmol), P-(m-Tol)3 (304.4 mg, 1 mmol), and Pyrphos (131.6 mg, 0.50 mmol) were suspended in 10 mL of CH2Cl2 under nitrogen protection and stirred for 2 h in a reaction vial. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 µm poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), and the filtrate was concentrated by vacuum rotary evaporation to give the CuI-PTPP powder with a yield of 93%. Notably, CuI-PTPP powder can be stably synthesized on a large scale (Supplementary Fig. 38). For single-crystal preparation, the CuI-PTPP powder was dissolved in CH3CN at 50 °C to form a hot mixture. The hot mixture was immediately filtered over a PVDF filter (0.22 µm) to obtain a hot solution. The solution was kept at room temperature for 2 days, yielding yellow single crystals.

Preparation of stretchable poly(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl methacrylate-block-2,2,3,4,4,4-hexafluorobutyl acrylate) (FMA-FBA) copolymer

FMA (1.68 g, 10 mmol), FBA (2.36 g, 10 mmol), and HMPP (3.4 mg, 0.021 mmol) were mixed and stirred for 4 h in a reaction vial. Then, the mixture was sonicated for 1 min and injected into a Teflon mold for 365 nm UV polymerization for 4 h. Finally, a stretchable and highly optically transparent FMA-FBA film was obtained.

Preparation of stretchable CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA composite film

The FMA-FBA copolymers (3.0 g) were dissolved in THF (12.0 mL) as a copolymer precursor solution. Different amounts of CuI-PTPP powder were dissolved in THF (0.5 mL). The CuI-PTPP solution was added dropwise to the copolymer precursor solution at room temperature and stirred for 2 h to ensure intimate mixing of the two components. The resulting homogeneous mixture was then transferred to a vacuum oven and thermally annealed at 60 °C for 2 h. After natural cooling to room temperature, CuI-PTPP/FMA-FBA film was obtained.

Characterizations

The contact angle data of water droplets were collected by a drop shape analyzer, Kruss DSA20, at room temperature. 19F solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic measurements were conducted on a 600 MHz Bruker Avance NEO spectrometer. 1H and 31P NMR spectroscopic measurements were conducted on a 400 MHz Bruker Avance NEO spectrometer. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SXRD) was performed on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer using Mo Kα radiation at 100 K. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were conducted on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was recorded on a Shimadzu Axis Supra X-ray photoelectron spectrometer with a monochromatized Al Kα X-ray source. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) tests were conducted on a Hitachi H7700 operated at 200 kV. The refractive index of the polymer was measured using a SE‑2000 ellipsometer in the wavelength range of 200–1200 nm. UV–vis absorption spectra and transmission data were measured on a PerkinElmer Lambda 35 and Shimadzu UV-3600 spectrophotometer. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra were taken using a Shimadzu RF-6000plus fluorescence spectrometer. Time-resolved PL was collected on an Edinburgh FLS920 using a fluorescence lifetime measurement system. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) tests were carried out on a Netzsch STA2500 at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. DSC measurements were recorded on a Netzsch DSC214 with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was carried out on Waters HPLC1515 and e2695, using THF as an eluent. Strain-stress curves were obtained on an Instron 3300 electronic universal material testing system.

DFT calculations

For the theoretical investigation, the Gaussian 16 software package served as the primary computational platform for systematic density functional theory (DFT) simulations42. CuI-PTPP optimizations and vibrational frequency analyses were conducted at the PBE0-D3BJ/def2-SVP level of theory43. Time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) calculations for excited-state properties utilized the PBE0-D3BJ/def2-TZVP level, with consistent basis set partitioning44. Contributions of frontier molecular orbitals to excited states were quantified using Multiwfn 3.8, and electron density distributions of the HOMO and LUMO were visualized using GaussView 6.0. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations were performed at the same level to confirm that each optimized minimum has no imaginary frequencies. To obtain more accurate electronic energies, single-point energy calculations were performed on the B3LYP-D3BJ/def2-TZVP level of theory. The Gibbs free energies were computed by combining the single-point electronic energies with the Gibbs free energy corrections (thermal and entropic contributions) obtained from the frequency calculations at the optimization level. All calculations were performed in tetrahydrofuran (THF) using the PCM solvation model45. In addition, the Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analyses presented in this work were implemented using the Multiwfn program package46.
