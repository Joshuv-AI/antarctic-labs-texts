Counterion Effects of Protic Ionic Liquids on the Conductivity and Stretchability of Free-Standing PEDOT:PSS Films
Authors: Thuy T. Le, Fatima Abbas, Shawn Bourdo, Fumiya Watanabe, Noureen Siraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Influenced by the hard–soft acid–base (HSAB) theory, different hard–soft cations and anions are used to investigate the counterion effects of ionic liquids (ILs) on electrical and mechanical characteristics of the poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonic acid) (PEDOT:PSS) and IL free-standing films. Two protic ILs, i.e., methylimidazolium dicyanamide ([p-MIM]­[DCA]) and imidazolium dicyanamide ([p-IM]­[DCA]), are synthesized and characterized for the first time. Six ions ranging from hard to soft (cations: [p-IM]+, [p-MIM]+, and 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium ([EMIM]+), anions: tetrafluoroborate ([BF4]−), tetracyanoborate ([TCB]−), and [DCA]−) are studied to investigate their impact on advancing conductive flexible films. Herein, PEDOT:PSS with protic ILs [p-MIM]­[DCA] and [p-IM]­[DCA] exhibited tremendous increase in electrical conductivity of 2730 S/cm and 3547 S/cm, respectively. Both protic ILs improved external strain of up to 11%, surpassing aprotic IL [EMIM]­[DCA] (∼7%) and pristine PEDOT:PSS (<4%). This study revealed that ILs containing hard cation and soft anion exhibited superior conductivity and flexibility in the composite films.

Introduction

Flexible electronic devices have attracted tremendous attention in recent years, particularly in energy and biomedical applications such as solar cells, , light-emitting diodes (LEDs), artificial skins, sensors, drug delivery systems,, and in healthcare devices. Stretchable electronics require a high degree of flexibility, typically with a tensile strain of at least 10% along with robust conductivity under bending, twisting, and folding conditions. Thus, the best materials for flexible electrodes must retain their electrical characteristics even when subjected to significant external stress.

Currently, various materials including metal, carbon, and polymer-based compounds such as metal nanoparticles, graphene, carbon nanotubes, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), have been employed as conductive fillers in elastomers to develop flexible electronics. However, a serious drawback of incorporating metal- or carbon-based materials into elastomers is poor processability and the reduced flexibility of the resulting materials. For instance, indium–tin oxide (ITO) has been widely used as a transparent conductive electrode for optoelectronic devices. However, it faces several limitations, including poor resistance to mechanical stress, chemical instability, and high fabrication costs. Polymer-based materials offer a more flexible alternative but typically exhibit low conductivity. Soft polymers such as polyurethane (PU), poly­(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS), poly­(ethylene glycol) (PEG), poly­(ethylene oxide) (PEO), or poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) have been employed as additives to enhance the stretchability of conductors, with some achieving up to 55% strain. However, these polymers significantly impact the overall conductivity of the materials.

An alternative approach is presented by utilizing conductive polymers, which can simultaneously enhance both electrical conductivity and flexibility. Among conductive polymers, poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):poly­(styrenesulfonic acid) (PEDOT:PSS) stands out as a promising candidate due to its high transparency, weak conductivity, tunability, and improved flexibility. Previous studies showed that PEDOT:PSS has relatively low conductivity (>1 S/cm) and a low intrinsic strain of less than 6%. Therefore, several efforts have been made to enhance the conductivity of polymers by incorporating secondary dopants (such as polar solvents, surfactants, and ILs), , while flexibility has been improved by the addition of elastomers or plasticizers into the conductive polymers. Additionally, post-treatment with sulfuric acid has been reported as well to enhance the conductivity of the film, reaching up to 4000 S/cm. , Some additive improved one characteristic but negatively impacted other properties. For example, Zonyl and triton are plasticizers that have been combined with PEDOT:PSS for improvement of stretchability, but their addition caused a decrease in conductivity. , Incorporating appropriate secondary dopants into conductive polymers is key to improve both conductivity and stretchability, which are crucial for the development of flexible electronic devices. Recently, ILs have been employed as both conductive fillers and elastomers to simultaneously enhance the conductivity and stretchability of PEDOT:PSS. However, the mechanism of enhanced mechanical strength and conductivity upon addition of ILs remains unclear.

ILs are molten salts (composed of a cation and an anion) with melting points below 100 °C. They exhibit unique physicochemical properties, such as low vapor pressure (which makes them nonflammable), , high thermal stability, high ionic conductivity, and tunability. − Owing to the tunability of ILs, their physical and chemical properties can be tailored by modifying the combination of anions and cations in ILs. These distinctive characteristics make ILs the solvents of choice for a wide range of study, including catalysis, , electrochemistry, − organic chemistry, polymer processing, nanotechnology, , biomedical, and flexible electronics applications , ILs with conductive characteristics are not elastomers, but they can aid to enhance mechanical strength by cross-linking.

Various hypotheses have been proposed to understand the mechanism behind the enhancement of such properties of PEDOT:PSS upon ILs incorporation, but the exact reason remains under debate. Recently, Izarra et al. applied the principle of hard/soft cations (acids) and anions (bases), developed by Ralph Pearson in the 1960s, to predict the binding interactions between PEDOT:PSS and ILs. According to this principle, hard anions tend to bind strongly to hard cations via ionic bonds, including hydrogen bonding, while soft anions prefer to bind strongly to soft cations through weak interactions. Hard anions are typically small, composed of highly electronegative atoms with localized charge, making them less susceptible to oxidation to their tightly held electrons. On the other hand, hard cations possess high positive charges with high charge densities, which enable them to attract anions strongly. In contrast, soft anions (or cations) are bulkier with delocalized charges. Soft anions exhibit lower electronegativity which can easily oxidize because their electrons are loosely bound. Similarly, soft cations have lower charge densities and more delocalized charges, making them more polarizable. Since PEDOT+ is a soft cation and PSS– is a hard anion, we expect that the ideal ILs should contain hard cation and soft anion for better cross-linking with PEDOT:PSS to enhance the electrical conductivity and stretchability of the resulting composite film. On the hard- and soft-ion theory, it is anticipated that the hard cation of the IL will bind strongly to the PSS– (insulating component, leaving the PEDOT+ (conductive component) free to interact with the IL’s anion, thereby improving the conductivity as well as mechanical strength of the PEDOT:PSS-ILs composite film.

In this study, we investigated three different cations and anions using six different ILs with PEDOT:PSS to understand their effect on the resulting free-standing films. First, we examined the effects of various anions, specifically tetrafluoroborate ([BF4]−), tetracyanoborate ([TCB]−), and dicyanamide ([DCA]−), each containing the same soft aprotic cation, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium ([EMIM]+), in the IL. The trend of the softness of these anions are as follows: [BF4]− < [TCB]− < [DCA]−, where DCA– is the softest and BF4 – is the hardest among the three. Next, we explore the influence of different cations (both protic and aprotic), [p-MIM]+, [p-IM]+, and [EMIM]+ with [DCA]−-based IL, aiming to uncover strategies for enhancing the electrical conductivity and stretchability of PEDOT:PSS-IL composite films. Three cations with different hardnesses are selected, and their hardness follows the trend [p-IM]+ > [p-MIM]+ > [EMIM]+, where [p-IM]+ is the hardest cation among the three. In this regard, we synthesized two new protic ILs, namely, 3-methylimidazolium dicyanamide ([p-MIM]­[DCA]) and imidazolium dicyanamide ([p-IM]­[DCA]), through ion-exchange reactions to investigate the cationic effects on free-standing films. This study aims to unveil strategies to improve the flexibility and conductivity characteristics of free-standing films. Ultimately, the findings will contribute to the development of advanced flexible electrode materials for a wide range of applications, including energy storage, optoelectronic displays, and biomedical devices.

Experimental Section

Materials

PEDOT:PSS 1.3 wt % dispersion, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([EMIM]­[BF4]), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium tetracyanoborate ([EMIM]­[TCB]), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium dicyanamide ([EMIM]­[DCA]), sodium dicyanamide (Na­[DCA]), 1-methylimidazolium chloride ([p-MIM]­Cl), and imidazole hydrochloride ([p-IM]­Cl) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

Protic IL Syntheses

Imidazolium Dicyanamide ([p-IM]­[DCA])

The protic IL [p-IM]­[DCA] was synthesized via an ion-exchange reaction (Scheme S1). First, [p-IM]Cl and Na­[DCA] were dissolved in deionized (DI) water and mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio. After 24 h of vigorous stirring, the solid [p-IM]­[DCA] was formed. The byproduct, NaCl, was removed from the mixture by pipetting the supernatant, and the solid was washed with DI water (10 mL × 3). The solid was further freeze-dried for 24 h to remove excess water. A light purple solid was obtained with a 79.5% yield. The IL was characterized using NMR and mass spectrometry, which confirmed the presence of both p-IM cation and DCA anion in the product. Further details on these characterizations are provided in the Supporting Information.

1-Methylimidazolium Dicyanamide ([p-MIM]­[DCA])

[p-MIM]­[DCA] IL was also synthesized via an ion-exchange reaction (Scheme S1) by mixing [p-MIM]Cl and Na­[DCA] in DI water with a 1:1 molar ratio and vigorously stirring for 24 h. To remove excess NaCl, water was removed from the mixture using a Rotary Evaporator (Rotavap) at 60 °C for 3 days. The NaCl solid was then extracted after centrifugation. The resulting solution was freeze-dried for 2 days to eliminate the remaining water moisture. Since some NaCl remained in the reaction mixture, [p-MIM]­[DCA] was extracted with ethanol, followed by centrifugation to remove the solid salt. Finally, [p-MIM]­[DCA] IL was recovered using a rotary evaporator to remove ethanol, and lyophilization was used to remove any remaining water. A light-yellow liquid was obtained with a 22.6% yield. The IL was analyzed by using NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry, both of which verified the presence of the p-MIM cation and DCA anion in the final product. Additional information on these characterizations can be found in theSupporting Information.

Preparation of IL/PEDOT:PSS Free-Standing Films

PEDOT:PSS is supplied as a 1.3 wt % aqueous dispersion in water, consisting of 0.5 wt % PEDOT and 0.8 wt % PSS. In 10 g of PEDOT:PSS solution, there are 0.13 g of PEDOT:PSS grains. IL was used without dilution. The free-standing films were prepared by using the drop-casting method. First, the solution of PEDOT:PSS is mixed with various ratios (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 wt %) of each IL and stirred at 500 rpm for 24 h. To determine the weight percent (wt %) of IL in the composite, mass of PEDOT:PSS grains and the mass of IL were used, as tabulated in Table S1 (in the Supporting Information). The wt % of IL was calculated using the mass of solid PEDOT:PSS and the mass of IL according to the following eq: wt⁡%⁡IL=mass of IL(g)mass of IL(g)+mass of PEDOT:PSS(g)×100%

After adding the IL to PEDOT:PSS, the mixture was stirred for 24 h to allow ion rearrangement before preparing the film and undergoing the drying process. The homogeneous solution was then drop-casted onto a handmade mold (4 cm × 6.5 cm) using the microscope glass slide wrapped with parafilm and air-dried for several days. During drying, most of the water content evaporated, resulting in free-standing films. After drying, the films were cut into 0.7 × 3.5 cm pieces and peeled off the parafilm substrate. The free-standing films, which were embedded between glass substrates, were then annealed on a hot plate at 130 °C for 10 min to remove excess moisture. The fabrication scheme of the free-standing film is shown in Scheme S2.

Characterizations

1H and 13C Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)

The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of the newly synthesized ILs were recorded by using a JEOL-JNM-ECA Series (Delta V4.3) 400 MHz FT-NMR spectrometer, operating at 400 MHz for 1H NMR and 100 MHz for 13C NMR. DMSO-d 6 was used as the solvent to dissolve the ILs.

Mass Spectrometry

The electrospray ionization mass (ESI-MS) spectra in both positive and negative ion modes of the newly synthesized protic ILs were generated by a Bruker (Billerica, MA) Ultraflex 9.4T.

Physical Characterizations

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

AFM is a powerful technique for evaluating the surface topography and measuring surface roughness by scanning a sample surface with a cantilever. The surface and phase morphologies of the films were investigated using AFM (Bruker Dimension Fastscan with ScanAsyst). The PEDOT:PSS and IL/PEDOT:PSS free-standing films were attached to a glass substrate by using double-sided tape for characterization.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS measurements were conducted using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS system, equipped with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.7 eV) and an output power of 36 W (12000 V at 3 mA). The PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:PSS-IL free-standing films were prepared by using the drop-casting method (Scheme S2). Samples were placed on the sample platen using copper clips, and the regions of interest for analysis were selected by visualizing the samples with a digital optical camera at magnifications between 60× and 200×. The base pressure in the analysis chamber was typically maintained at 2 × 10–9 mbar or lower. All spectra were recorded with the charge neutralization flood gun activated, which employs a mix of low-energy electrons and argon ions to mitigate charge buildup. During the operation of the flood gun, the typical chamber pressure was 2 × 10–7 mbar.

Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDS)

The free-standing films of pristine PEDOT:PSS and PEDOT:PSS/IL were cut to 7 mm × 7 mm and transferred onto double-sided tape mounted on an aluminum cylinder. The films were characterized using a SEM system (JEOL JSM 7000F) operated at 7.5 kV.

Electrical Conductivity

The electrical conductivity of the films was assessed using the four-point probe technique, which is commonly employed to measure the sheet resistance. The free-standing films were annealed at 130 °C for 10 min in ambient air to remove residual moisture. Sheet resistance was measured by using a four-point probe connected to a Keithley 2400 Sourcemeter. The film thickness was measured using a Mitutoyo micrometer and AFM (Bruker Dimension Icon with ScanAsyst), and these values were used to calculate the film’s electrical conductivity. In this method, four probes are aligned in a straight line with an equal spacing between them. Current is passed through the outer two probes, and the voltage drop is measured across the inner two probes. The change in voltage is then used to calculate the sheet resistance (R s) using eq . 1 sheet resistance(Rs)=πln⁡2(VavgIavg) where R s is the sheet resistance (Ω/sq), V avg is the average voltage (V), and I avg is the average current (A).

The conductivity of the film (σ) is the inverse of the sheet resistance and can be calculated using the following relation (eq ). 2 conductivity(σ)=1Rs×t where σ is the conductivity of the thin film (S/cm), R s is the sheet resistance (in Ω/sq), and t is the thickness of the film (in cm). The correction factor is equal to 1 if the thickness of the film is smaller than 1 mm.

Stretchability Test

Mechanical testing was conducted to determine the flexibility of the film, defined as its ability to stretch or bend without losing conductivity. Stress was applied to one end of the film, and the resulting strain was measured to calculate the strain percentage. This stress–strain relationship provides insight into the material’s elastic deformation capacity before failure, which can be quantified by the elongation at break percentage, as given by eq . 3 %elongation at break=ΔLL0×100% where ΔL represents the change in length (mm) and L 0 is the original length (mm). Elongation at break, or fracture strain, is defined as the difference in length between the initial point and the breaking point of the film. This measurement indicates the material’s ability to resist shape change, maintaining integrity without undergoing deformation or cracking. In the stress/strain plot, the Young’s modulus (elastic modulus) Ey=σyεy is obtained from the ratio of the yield stress (σ y ) and the yield strain (ε y ) in the elastic region. This parameter investigates a material’s ability to withstand changes in length under applied stress. In other words, it quantifies the extent to which a material deforms when subjected to an external force. The stress/strain behavior of the film was determined using a tensile strength machine (ADMET eXpert 7600 series), operating at a strain rate of 3 mm/min. The applied force was gradually increased, and the corresponding change in length of the film was recorded until failure occurred.

Results and Discussion

AFM

The AFM measurements were performed to investigate the film morphological characteristics. Figure shows the surface morphology of the IL/PEDOT:PSS composite films compared with the pristine film.

AFM (2D and 3D) images of pristine (A, Aa), [EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS (B, Bb), [EMIM]­[TCB]/PEDOT:PSS (C, Cc), [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (D, Dd), [p-MIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (E, Ee), [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (F, Ff).

The average surface roughness (R a) of the thin films (Figure ) decreased in the following order: PEDOT:PSS (30.7 nm) > [EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS (28.0 nm) > [EMIM]­[TCB]/PEDOT:PSS (27.2 nm) > [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (25.1 nm) > [p-MIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (22.2 nm) > [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS (20.3 nm). The addition of ILs resulted in the formation of smoother surface films. An increase in the surface roughness induces local disruptions in the electric field, leading to greater electron scattering. This, in turn, results in electrons colliding with one another, interfering with the flow of electric current and increasing electrical resistance. Conversely, a smoother surface with fewer irregularities reduces scattering, leading to lower resistance and higher conductivity. , In fact, the conductivity of the PEDOT:PSS electrode materials was enhanced upon incorporation with ILs.

XPS

This experiment was conducted to investigate the ion-rearrangement between IL and PEDOT:PSS. The pristine film of PEDOT:PSS exhibits two distinct peaks in the S­(2p) spectra (Figure ). One doublet, at 163.98–165.28 eV, corresponds to the thiophene group of PEDOT, and the other one at 168.17 eV originates from the sulfonate group of PSS. − In addition, the intensity ratio of PSS/PEDOT is correlated to the concentration of the PEDOT:PSS dispersion in water.

XPS data of the pristine and composite films with different ILs.

Upon ion-rearrangement, the former PSS peak decreases, indicating its decoupling from PEDOT. This reduction in the PSS peak is attributed to the separation of the PSS chain from PEDOT. The PSS/PEDOT intensity ratio (Figure S1) decreased from 2.92 (PEDOT:PSS) to 2.28 ([EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS), 2.16 ([EMIM]­[TCB]/PEDOT:PSS), 1.94 ([EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS), 1.65 ([p-MIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS), and 1.03 ([p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS). As the cation hardness and anion softness increase in ILs, the separation of PSS from PEDOT also increases, resulting in a significant decrease in the PSS peak. These results are very well-correlated with hard and soft ion theory. The separation of PSS from PEDOT indicates better cross-linking of polymer ions with ILs, which subsequently impacts the characteristics of the resulting films.

Electrical Conductivity of IL/PEDOT:PSS

This study is designed to investigate the hard and soft properties of the ions in ILs that impact electrical conductivity of the PEDOT:PSS-IL composite film.

Anion Study

In this study, we investigated the effect of [DCA]−, [TCB]−, and [BF4]− anions, containing [EMIM]+ cation in IL, at various weight percentages (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 wt %) with polymer composite film.

Detailed examination of results revealed that as the weight percent of ILs increased, there was a corresponding rise in the conductivity of the free-standing film, indicating that incorporating ILs into the PEDOT:PSS film significantly enhances its electrical conductivity. The greatest enhancement in conductivity was observed at 2.0 wt % IL. Beyond this concentration, the drying process of the composite films became prolonged; therefore, we limited the IL concentration to 2.0 wt %.

As shown in Figure A, the [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS film exhibited the highest conductivity, approximately 1817 S/cm, while [EMIM]­[TCB]/PEDOT:PSS and [EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS showed conductivities of 1101 and 185 S/cm, respectively, at 2.0 wt % as compared to the pristine PEDOT:PSS film (∼1 S/cm). The conductivity enhancement trend for [EMIM]+-based IL can therefore be ranked as follows: [BF4]− < [TCB]− < [DCA]−. This trend aligns very well with the findings of Saxena et al.

Effect of (A) anions and (B) cations on the conductivity of IL/PEDOT:PSS composite film.

According to the hard–soft acid–base theory, [BF4]− is considered a hard anion due to its relatively small size and high charge density. Fluorine atoms in [BF4]− are highly electronegative with charge primarily localized. In addition, [BF4]− has a highly symmetrical structure with no pi bonds, making it less polarizable. This limited polarizability may result in weaker interactions with the PEDOT soft cation, leading to a smaller improvement in conductivity. In contrast, [TCB]− is considered a moderately hard/soft anion because of electron delocalization within the cyano groups, which increases its polarizability and imparts some degree of softness. Moderate anions (such as [TCB]−) also enhance conductivity but to a lesser extent. Whereas [DCA]−, on the other hand, is classified as a soft anion due to the delocalization of charge within its structure, which makes it highly polarizable. Soft anions like [DCA]− have a greater ability to disrupt PEDOT:PSS ion pairing, promoting to more efficient cross-linking with [PEDOT]+ soft cation, that resulted the significant increase in the conductivity of PEDOT:PSS-[EMIM]­[DCA] composite film. These findings indicate that soft anions are more effective than hard anions in enhancing the electrical conductivity of composite films due to better interactions with the soft PEDOT cation.

Cation Study

In this study, imidazolium-based cations paired with three different anions were investigated with PEDOT:PSS to understand their influence on the conductivity in composite films. Given that the [DCA]− anion demonstrated the highest electrical conductivity among the three anions in the study, therefore three different cations are pair with [DCA]− anion to investigate the effect of cation in the resulting free-standing film. The effect of three different cations ([EMIM]+, [p-IM]+, and [p-MIM]+) with various IL weight percentages of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 wt % were used with PEDOT:PSS to develop free-standing composite films.

The conductivity of the composite films increased as the IL weight percent increased, as illustrated in Figure B. At 2.0 wt %, the [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film demonstrated the highest conductivity of 3547 S/cm, while [p-MIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS and [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS exhibited 2730 S/cm and ∼1817 S/cm, respectively. These results indicate that the conductivity trend for PEDOT:PSS-IL composites follow the order: [EMIM]+ < [p-MIM]+ < [p-IM]+. It suggests that the structure or chemical properties of ILs’ ions play a significant role in the composite material.

[EMIM]+ is considered a soft cation due to the electron-donating groups (methyl and ethyl), which make it more polarizable. This increased polarizability allows it to form stronger covalent interactions with anions. As a result, this soft cation preferentially binds to soft anions like [DCA]− rather than hard anions like [PSS]−, leading to less effective solvation and lower ion dissociation in the PEDOT:PSS, ultimately reducing conductivity. [p-MIM]+ is considered a harder cation compared to [EMIM]+ because it is slightly less polarizable than [EMIM]+. This is attributed to the presence of hydrogen on the nitrogen in the imidazolium based-IL, which contributes to the increased hardness of [p-MIM]+ compared to [EMIM]+. This [p-MIM]+ moderate hard cation interacts slightly better with hard [PSS]− in the PEDOT:PSS composite, leading to some ion dissociation and thus showing a moderate improvement in conductivity compared to [EMIM]+. Among the three cations studied, [p-IM]+ is considered the hardest cation due to the absence of alkyl groups and the presence of two hydrogens on the nitrogen in the imidazolium-based IL. Hard cations do not bind strongly to soft anions, which caused favorable interactions between [PEDOT]+[DCA]− (soft–soft ions) and [p-IM]+[PSS]− (hard–hard ions). Our data suggest that the primary contribution arises from favorable interactions between hard–hard and soft–soft ions. Specifically, the soft p-IM+ cations are expected to remain in their protonated cationic form, as confirmed by our 1H NMR analysis (see Supporting Information) that interact effectively with the PSS part of the matrix, thereby enhancing electrical conductivity. This promotes more effective doping in PEDOT:PSS films, thereby improving the overall conductive performance of the [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film. These findings revealed that hard cations are more effective than soft cations in the electrical conductivity enhancement of PEDOT:PSS-IL composite films, which validate the hard and soft ions theory.

For the flexible electronics, mechanical strength of the film is also a key characteristic as conductivity is. Anion Impact on Mechanical Strength

The Young’s modulus quantifies a material’s stiffness, representing its resistance to deformation under applied stress within the elastic limit. The Young’s modulus values (Figure C–F) of [EMIM]+-based IL in the PEDOT:PSS-IL composite film increased in the order of pristine < [DCA]− < [TCB]− < [BF4]− across all IL weight percentages, indicating an increased in material’s stiffness. A small increase in the stiffness enhances the structural stability of the composite materials, making them more resistant to deformation under moderate stress or during the fabrication process.

Tensile strength represents the maximum stress that a material can handle before failure. Interestingly, we did not observe a uniform increase in mechanical strength as we increased the ILs percentage with PEDOT:PSS. As illustrated in Figure S3, PEDOT:PSS-IL composite film exhibited random variations in strain while tolerating higher stress than the pristine film. As showed in Figure C–F, the tensile strength of [EMIM]+-based IL in the IL/PEDOT:PSS increased in the following order: pristine < [DCA]− < [TCB]− ∼ [BF4]− (at 1.0 wt %) and pristine < [DCA]− ∼ [TCB]− < [BF4]− (at 1.5 and 2.0 wt %). However, an exception was observed with [TCB]−, where its tensile strength fell below both [DCA]− and [BF4]− at 0.5 wt %.

Flexibility can be influenced by more than just Young’s modulus; factors such as ductility, microstructure, and energy dissipation also play vital roles in defining the flexibility of a material. Ductility refers to a material’s capacity to experience substantial plastic deformation under tensile stress without breaking. This characteristic enables materials to be elongated or stretched without breaking. To assess the ductility of the composite materials, we investigated the elongation at break of the aprotic IL [EMIM]+ with three different anions. A slight change in elongation at break was observed for [BF4]− (hard anion), while [TCB]− (moderate hard/soft anion) and [DCA]− (soft anion) exhibited a significant increase in elongation as compared to the pristine film (Figure A).

The incorporation of IL into PEDOT:PSS resulted in increased Young’s modulus, tensile strength, and elongation at break. These findings demonstrate that the addition of an IL effectively extended the elastic limit of the resulting composite films, enhancing their mechanical properties. [TCB]− and [DCA]− exhibited the optimal stretchability at 1.5 and 2.0 wt %, respectively, among the three anions for [EMIM]+-based IL with PEDOT:PSS films. As discussed earlier for the [EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS composite films, the combination of [PEDOT]+ with [BF4]− and [EMIM]+ with [PSS]− (soft–hard ions) is not favorable, resulting in lower stretchability. In contrast, the [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film did not exhibit the highest mechanical strength, likely because both [PEDOT]+ and [EMIM]+ are soft cations that competitively interact with [DCA]− (a soft anion) rather than with [PSS]−. This hinders the proper alignment of the polymer chain with the IL and limits the stretchability. In the case of the [EMIM]­[TCB]/PEDOT:PSS composite, ion-rearrangement between [PEDOT]+ with [TCB]− (soft–moderate hard/soft ions) and [EMIM]+ with [PSS]− (soft–hard ions) is more effective than in the [EMIM]­[BF4]/PEDOT:PSS composite due to the decreased hardness of the [TCB]− anion, leading to improved stretchability. Incorporating ILs into the PEDOT:PSS enhances its ductility. While [TCB]− exhibited optimal stretchability across various weight percentages, [DCA]− anion emerges as particularly promising for flexible electronics, delivering superior electrical performance and good stretchability, while maintaining consistent tensile strength.

Cation Study

In this study, the [DCA]− anion was kept constant while the cations are tuned to understand the effect of the cation in the PEDOT:PSS-IL composite film due to [DCA]− excellent enhancement in electrical performance (approximately 1817 S/cm) and its consistent tensile strength. To investigate the cationic effect, ILs containing the same anion ([DCA]−) were incorporated with PEDOT:PSS to prepare free-standing films at varying IL weight percentages.

While [EMIM]­[DCA] improved the elongation at break, achieving a maximum value of 7.3% at 2.0 wt % (Figure A and Figure S4), the incorporation of protic ILs, [p-IM]­[DCA] and [p-MIM]­[DCA], into PEDOT:PSS delivered a more significant improvement. These protic ILs significantly enhance the film’s elongation at break as much as 11% at 1.0 wt % IL, compared to less than 4% observed in the pristine PEDOT:PSS film, demonstrating the superior stretchability offered by protic ILs. These results revealed that the protic ILs offer a more effective strategy for enhancing the ductility of PEDOT:PSS, making them promising candidates for flexible electronic applications.

[EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film shows hindered ion-rearrangement because of the competitive ion pairing between soft cations ([PEDOT]+ and [EMIM]+) with [DCA]− (a soft anion), rather than with [PSS]− (a hard anion), which limits the film’s stretchability. In contrast to the [p-MIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film, the combination of [PEDOT]+ with [DCA]− (soft–soft ions) and [p-MIM]+ with [PSS]− (moderate hard–hard ions) is more favorable than in [EMIM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS, leading to an improvement in stretchability. Similarly, in the [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composite film, ion-rearrangement between [PEDOT]+ with [DCA]− (soft–soft ions) and [p-IM]+with [PSS]− (hard–hard ions) is the most favorable due to the stronger ionic interaction between cations and anions, leading to enhanced stretchability. Therefore, [p-IM]+ exhibited the optimum stretchable characteristics among the three cations of the [DCA]−-based IL in the PEDOT:PSS film.

The Young’s modulus values (Figure B–E) of the [DCA]−-based IL in the PEDOT:PSS-IL followed the order: pristine < [EMIM]+ < [p-IM]+ ∼ [p-MIM]+ (at 0.5 and 2.0 IL wt%), indicating an increase in material’s stiffness upon IL incorporation into PEDOT:PSS. This increase in stiffness within a certain range enhanced the maximum stress tolerance, improving its resistance to deformation and breaking under applied stress, particularly during the fabrication process of free-standing films.

As illustrated in Figure B–E, the cationic tensile strength of the IL/PEDOT:PSS films increased in the following order: pristine < [EMIM]+ < [p-MIM]+ < [p-IM]+ at 1.0 and 2.0 IL wt%. However, at 0.5 and 1.5 IL wt%, the order was: pristine < [EMIM]+ < [p-IM]+ < [p-MIM]+. The observed variation between [p-IM]+ and [p-MIM]+ at certain concentrations was attributed to film defects, which influenced the mechanical properties of the films. Nonetheless, the incorporation of these ILs, particularly the protic ILs [p-IM]­[DCA] and [p-MIM]­[DCA], significantly enhanced the mechanical strength of the PEDOT:PSS films.

The increase in Young’s modulus, tensile strength, and elongation at break in the composite films indicates an extended elastic limit of IL/PEDOT:PSS, confirming the successful incorporation of IL into PEDOT:PSS. Among these two protic ILs, [p-IM]­[DCA] stands out for its remarkable improvement in conductivity (3547 S/cm) and its ability to withstand an external strain of 10.4% when incorporated into PEDOT:PSS. This enhancement in mechanical strength, coupled with the high electrical conductivity, suggests that [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS composites are promising candidates for flexible and stretchable electronic applications, where both superior electrical performance and mechanical robustness are essential for ensuring durability and functionality.

Characterization of the Optimal Film

To assess the electrical performance of the optimal free-standing [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS film, sheet resistance was measured using the two-point probe technique (Figure A) while subjecting the films to mechanical stresses such as bending and twisting. This experiment is crucial for understanding the material electrical properties under stress condition, which is a key factor for determining its suitability for flexible electronics applications. As shown in Figure A, the sheet resistance of the film increased slightly with increasing bending angles. The sheet resistance was recorded when films were bend at 45°, 90°, and twisted. An increase in sheet resistance corresponds to a decrease in electrical conductivity. The optimal film was further evaluated by using a four-point probe and mechanical testing instrument (Figure S5) to assess its stability under mechanical stress. During this test, the free-standing film was subjected to different strains to measure its relative resistance (R/R 0). A slight increase in relative resistance ratio (Figure S5) under various applied stresses indicated that the composite films are highly stable under stress, with a maximum R/R 0 of 1.07 at 6% strain.

A simple circuit experiment was designed to investigate the flow of electricity of a free-standing film (Figure B,C). The circuit consisted of a battery, an LED light bulb, and conductive wires connecting the battery terminals to the light bulb. The LED light bulb successfully illuminated when connected to [p-IM]­[DCA]/PEDOT:PSS, indicating efficient electricity flow through the material. In contrast, the PEDOT:PSS free-standing film did not light the bulb, suggesting a disruption in the electrical conductivity (Figure S6).

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the significant potential of incorporating protic ILs, such as [p-IM]­[DCA] and [p-MIM]­[DCA], enhancing both the electrical conductivity and stretchability of PEDOT:PSS free-standing films, offering a promising pathway for the development of advanced materials for flexible electronics. The synergy between hard cations and soft anions in ILs has been shown to effectively optimize the conductivity of the film without compromising its mechanical flexibility, making it ideal for applications in wearable devices, flexible sensors, and next-generation display technologies. The findings highlight the importance of carefully tailoring the cation and anion components of ILs to achieve enhanced electrical and mechanical properties. This work paves the way for further research into IL-based materials, opening new possibilities for the creation of durable, high-performance, and flexible electronic systems.
