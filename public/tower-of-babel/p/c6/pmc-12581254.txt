A novel model of dual-network composite hydrogels for application in endoscopic submucosal dissection
Authors: Fei Zhao, YunFang Bai, JunJie Wu, ZhiHao Zhu, JinLei Mao, Aikebaier Aili, HongRun Gu, ShiJie Yu, ZhiFei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsEndoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is a critical technique for treating early-stage gastrointestinal tumors, requiring precise operational skills. Traditional training methods, however, face challenges such as high costs, ethical concerns, and sustainability issues. This study aims to design a novel training model based on dual-network composite hydrogel (DNH) (Jing, et al, Polymers 11:952,2019) to simulate the multilayered structure of gastric tissue, providing a more realistic and effective training environment.MethodsA new DNH model was developed to simulate gastric tissue’s structure and biomechanics. The effectiveness of the model in ESD training was evaluated through structured assessments with participants of different experience levels. Physicians trained with the DNH model were evaluated for technical precision, efficiency, and complication risk.ResultsThe DNH model demonstrated superior performance, with physicians using the model showing significantly higher technical precision and efficiency in ESD procedures. Additionally, the model resulted in a substantially reduced risk of complications compared to traditional methods. Participants rated the model highly for reusability and lower cost, confirming its effectiveness as a training tool.ConclusionsThe dual-network composite hydrogel model offers great potential for ESD training, providing an efficient, safe, and cost-effective alternative to traditional training methods. Its integration into medical education could enhance the development of surgical skills and improve clinical practice outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04341-9.

Introduction

Early diagnosis and endoscopic treatment of gastrointestinal diseases are pivotal for optimizing patient prognosis. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), pioneered in Japan for the en-bloc resection of early gastrointestinal cancers and precancerous lesions [1–4]. represents a paradigm shift from conventional endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR). While EMR is constrained by lesion size limitations and risks of incomplete resection [5, 6]. ESD employs advanced electrosurgical knives and submucosal injection techniques to achieve complete tumor removal with minimal invasiveness, thereby reducing recurrence rates and enhancing pathological diagnostic accuracy [7, 8]. The success of ESD has led to its global adoption and expanded indications, now encompassing esophageal, colorectal, and even duodenal lesions.

Despite its clinical superiority, ESD requires operators to master a complex skillset involving precise anatomical layer identification, controlled dissection of submucosal fibers, and real-time management of bleeding or perforation. These technical demands create a steep learning curve, with studies suggesting a minimum of 30–50 supervised procedures for competency acquisition. Traditional training modalities, such as ex vivo animal organs or live animal models, face significant limitations: porcine stomachs exhibit biomechanical disparities compared to human tissue, synthetic models lack realistic vascular networks and layered tissue resistance, while ethical regulations and costs restrict the widespread use of live animal training [9].

To address these challenges, this study introduces a 3D-printed gastric ESD training model that combines polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)-acrylamide (AM) hydrogels [10, 11] with embedded hyaluronic acid (HA)-gelatin vascular networks [12]. The model replicates human gastric wall stratification (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria) with tunable stiffness that matches clinical measurements [13, 14], while incorporating simulated bleeding responses via pressurized fluid channels (Fig. 1). ‌This study aims to evaluate the model’s effectiveness in improving procedural competency among trainees, as assessed by objective performance metrics and expert validation.Fig. 1Application of the dual-network composite hydrogel model in ESD

Methods

Preparation of the hydrogel ESD model

First, 270 mL of deionized water and 30 g of PVA powder were heated to 90 °C, stirred for 1–2 h until fully dissolved, and cooled to room temperature for storage. Considering cost-effectiveness and the localized nature of surgery, the model was designed for a specific test area and prepared in three main steps:

The mucosal layer was prepared by mixing 20 mL of 2 M acrylamide (AM) solution, 10 mL of 10% (w/v) PVA solution, 16 mg N, N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA), 20 mg ammonium persulfate (APS), 0.1 g sodium chloride, and three drops of food-grade red dye. The mixture was stirred at 300 rpm for 5 min at 25 °C. Immediately before casting, 100 µL of tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) was added to initiate polymerization. The mixture was poured into the mold and allowed to polymerize for 10 min at room temperature (22–25 °C). The layer was subjected to one freeze–thaw cycle (–20 °C for 6 h, thaw at 25 °C for 2 h) to enhance physical crosslinking.

The submucosal layer was prepared by dissolving 0.2 g of gelatin and 0.1 g of hyaluronic acid in 20 mL of distilled water at 50 °C, followed by the addition of 0.6 g of sodium tetraborate under stirring at 250 rpm for 3 min. Forty milliliters of 10% PVA solution was then added, and the mixture was stirred at 300 rpm until a uniform gel-like consistency was achieved.

For vascular simulation, hydrogel tubing (internal diameter 1.5 mm) was embedded within the submucosal layer in a branching pattern around the lesion site. The tubing was connected to an external syringe pump, which delivered dyed water at 25 kPa to simulate pressurized bleeding.

The muscularis layer was prepared by mixing 20 mL of 2 M AM solution, 8 mL of 10% PVA solution, 16 mg MBA, and 12.8 mg APS. After adding 80 µL TEMED, the mixture was stirred for 3 min and poured over the submucosal layer, polymerizing for 10 min at room temperature., We created a total of 15 DNH models with identical structures, all of which were prepared using the same mold and the same batch of materials. Each model contained four lesions, two measuring 1.5 cm × 1.5 cm and two measuring 2 cm × 2 cm; only the 2 cm × 2 cm lesions were used for the training and evaluation experiments.

Evaluation of the ESD model

A hydrogel-based simulation model was prepared to validate the model’s effectiveness. The study involved 20 endoscopists (10 experts, 5 attending physicians, and 5 residents) from Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital, with a median of 8 years of endoscopic experience (range: 2–20 years) (Fig. 2A–C). Participants were categorized into three groups according to the number of prior ESD cases performed: Experts: >100 cases, Attending physicians: 30–100 cases, Residents: <20 cases.Fig. 2Demonstration of the ESD model. Years of endoscopy practice were recorded only as descriptive information and were not used as classification criteria. At the Endoscopy Center, participants performed full-simulation ESD procedures, including lesion localization, marking, submucosal injection, circumferential cutting, and dissection (Figure D-F). The operations were fully recorded, using a novel gastric ESD model based on dual-network composite multilayer hydrogels developed by Yu, Zhao et al.

‌Outcome Assessment‌: Primary outcome:‌ The model’s realism was evaluated using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = extremely unrealistic; 7 = extremely realistic) adapted from surgical simulation validation studies. Participants rated visual realism, anatomical accuracy, and tissue properties (thickness, elasticity).

‌Secondary outcomes:‌ Technical step realism (rated by expert): Lesion marking (surface suitability/visibility), submucosal injection (submucosal elevation/precision), circumferential incision (accuracy/tissue separation), and submucosal dissection (smoothness/tissue clarity).

Content validity (rated by expert): Operational similarity to real procedures, ease of use, training appropriateness, skill enhancement, confidence-building, and risk reduction potential.

Statistical analysis

‌Operation times on the model by physicians of varying experience levels are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Group differences were assessed using the One-way ANOVA, with post hoc multiple comparisons conducted via Tukey’s test. Likert scale responses are reported as median (M) with interquartile range (P25–P75). En bloc resection was defined as complete lesion removal with preserved layer integrity; perforation was recorded if the muscularis layer exposure exposed.

Results

Operation of the hydrogel training model

We created a new ESD training model using various hydrogel monomers. As seen in Fig. 3(A), an endoscope was inserted and advanced to the ESD hydrogel model’s mucosal layer for observation and lesion location. After identifying the lesion, electrocautery markings were made around the target area (Fig. 3B). Next, injection elevated the entire mucosal layer (Fig. 3C), separating the lesion from adjacent tissue. This elevation lasted through the procedure, allowing for precise circumferential incision along the lesion margin (Fig. 3D). Dissection then exposed the submucosal layer and formed a realistic mucosal flap (Fig. 3E). Complete en bloc resection was achieved, as shown in Fig. 3F.

During the fabrication process, vessels were strategically embedded around the lesion area to emulate vessel exposure (Fig. 4B) and bleeding resulting from inadvertent cutting during ESD (Fig. 4C). Observations of vessel closure post-hemostasis through electrocoagulation carbonation were documented in the simulated bleeding scenario (Fig. 4D). This simulated environment serves as a valuable training tool for surgeons, enhancing their confidence in managing potential bleeding situations and equipping them with the requisite skills for decisive action during actual surgical procedures. Tensile testing using a dual-column universal testing machine showed that the average Young’s modulus of the DNH model’s full-layer stomach, muscularis plus submucosal layer, and mucosal layer was 0.533 MPa, 0.401 MPa, and 0.168 MPa, (Fig. 5B) respectively, closely approximating values reported for human gastric tissue in the literature. The mucosal layer exhibited an elongation strain of 325%, deliberately designed to be higher than that of native tissue to enhance the model’s durability during repeated training sessions.Fig. 5A Schematic diagram of the ESD surgery procedure flow. A Schematic diagram of the ESD surgery procedure flow. B Tensile stress-strain curves of the whole layer, mucosal layer, submucosal layer, and muscle layer of the hydrogel model. C Conductivity of different simulated layers of the hydrogel model

Electrosurgical performance was evaluated using a standard ESD knife under clinical settings. The DNH model displayed localized carbonization and cutting resistance highly comparable to those of in vivo gastric tissue, with consistent outcomes across multiple trials.

Conductivity testing revealed that the hydrogel model’s conductivity profile closely matched that of human gastric tissue. This correspondence is attributable to the incorporation of NaCl into the hydrogel, which allows Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions to diffuse uniformly within the prepolymer solution, resulting in a homogeneous ionic distribution throughout the gel. During polymerization, additional interactions—such as hydrogen bonding or electrostatic forces—between polymer chains and ions may induce structural modifications in the polymer network, thereby enhancing conductivity. Notably, the conductivity at all tested layers was close to or exceeded 0.1 S/m, meeting the basic threshold for gel-based sensors as reported in previous studies [15, 16]. This ensures the reliable conductive performance of the model under standard conditions that simulate the use of electrosurgical instruments.

Evaluation of the hydrogel training model

We recruited 5 experts, 5 attending physicians, and 5 residents to perform the procedures on the simulator, and the operative times were recorded (Fig. 6). The results showed that experts had an average circumferential incision time of 5.20 ± 0.84 min, submucosal dissection time of 17.60 ± 2.41 min, and a total operation time of 26.00 ± 2.74 min. Attending physicians required significantly longer times, with 12.60 ± 2.41 min for circumferential incision, 22.60 ± 1.14 min for submucosal dissection, and 37.80 ± 2.17 min in total. Residents took the longest time, with 23.40 ± 2.07 min, 36.80 ± 3.96 min, and 63.00 ± 5.52 min for the respective steps. Post hoc analysis revealed statistically significant differences in stepwise operation times and total operation time between all pairs of physician groups with different experience levels (p < 0.05, Fig. 6).

Discussion

Technical performance of the DNH model

Our findings demonstrate that the DNH model can effectively discriminate between physicians of different experience levels, reflecting the reality that less-experienced physicians often require longer procedure times due to limited exposure to real surgical cases. While slightly less realistic than some high-fidelity commercial or biological models in certain aspects, the DNH model offers distinct advantages in terms of affordability, ease of storage, and strong discriminatory capacity, making it particularly suitable for routine training and skill enhancement among less-experienced or lower-skilled physicians—a key focus of our future validation work.

The model’s electrocautery effects derive from the ionic composition of the NaCl-containing hydrogel, in which Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions form an electrolyte solution. Under high-frequency current, this solution generates conductive heating, producing localized carbonization closely resembling clinical tissue responses. The PVA–borax hydrogel system provides structural stability during submucosal elevation through dynamic, reversible complexation between borate ions and PVA hydroxyl groups. This mechanism preserves submucosal fluid cushions and enables controlled water exchange.

Gelatin’s thermal denaturation during electrosurgery reproduces the carbonization of human tissue, while viscosity enhancement from hyaluronic acid recreates the “stringy” texture encountered in submucosal dissection. High visual and anatomical fidelity scores suggest successful replication of gross tissue morphology, likely due to biomimetic polymer composition and surface texturing. However, lower mucosal thickness scores may reflect material limitations in reproducing the human epithelium’s stratified structure.

Performance variability was also observed. Strong marking performance contrasted with less satisfactory submucosal injection outcomes, suggesting accurate replication of surface properties but weaker simulation of fluid dynamics. Moderate dissection scores may result from limited mechanical contrast between mucosal and submucosal layers. Overall, the model demonstrates strong visual–tactile fidelity but lower biomechanical accuracy for layered tissue mechanics and fluid-mediated expansion. Adjusting PVA–borate crosslink density may enhance viscoelastic mimicry of human tissue.

Comparison with existing training methods

Compared with traditional ex vivo porcine stomach models, the hydrogel model eliminates cold-chain logistics, reduces biosafety risks, and ensures consistent batch-to-batch anatomical properties, while offering reusability and ease of storage. Although porcine tissue provides authentic mechanical feedback, it is single-use and difficult to standardize.

We only compare with KOTOBUKI models from Japan. When compared to the commercial KOTOBUKI model (Japan), the DNH model achieved comparable realism scores (6.0 vs. 6.1 on a 7-point scale) at substantially lower cost (USD 40 vs. USD 100) and with significantly greater reusability (> 5 training sessions vs. 1–2). The preparation time for the DNH model was approximately 4 h, compared to several weeks of manufacturing lead time for KOTOBUKI. Additionally, the DNH model incorporates vascular bleeding simulation and tunable mechanical properties, features absent in KOTOBUKI.

Educational implications

The DNH model demonstrated high content validity, effectively bridging didactic learning and clinical application. It allows safe simulation of complication scenarios without patient risk and supports deliberate practice through progressive task difficulty. However, a usability–performance paradox was noted: while training efficacy was high, operability was only moderate, suggesting that optimizing handling characteristics could better accommodate novices.

The model clearly stratifies operator performance by experience level, with quantifiable differences—such as a 40% gap in complete resection rates and a 20% gap in perforation rates—illustrating the experience-dependent learning curve in ESD. These differences establish competency benchmarks and technical thresholds, such as achieving complete resection while preserving the muscularis propria. Novices’ logarithmic learning curves and attendings’ linear progression reflect distinct skill-acquisition phases. Persistent time differences between trained novices and experts align with Fitts’ three-stage theory, underscoring the model’s value in targeted, phase-specific training.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center investigation with a modest sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Recruitment from a single tertiary hospital could also introduce selection bias. Second, although lesion size was standardized, variability in operator technique may still have influenced the outcomes. Third, the study was exploratory in nature; larger, multi-center trials are warranted to validate reproducibility and further refine the model’s fidelity in simulating layered tissue mechanics and fluid dynamics.

In addition, Likert-scale ratings were only obtained from experts, which limited cross-group subjective comparisons of realism and training utility. Moreover, resected specimens were not preserved for post-procedural measurement, preventing further quantitative assessment of resection completeness. Finally, the lesion resection area was not formally measured, which precluded the calculation of objective dissection speed metrics (e.g., cm²/min). This omission limits the ability to compare operator performance using standardized quantitative benchmarks.

Furthermore, a formal sample size or power analysis was not conducted, given the exploratory design and modest cohort. As a result, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should incorporate area-based dissection speed evaluation and be conducted as multi-center trials with larger sample sizes and predefined statistical power to confirm reproducibility and refine the model’s fidelity.

Implementation: The DNH model can be integrated into structured ESD training curricula as a bridge between classroom learning and live clinical practice. We recommend its use in simulation workshops with progressive difficulty, inclusion of vascular bleeding scenarios, and repeated sessions to maximize cost efficiency. Its reusability, low cost, and strong discriminatory capability make it particularly suited for institutional adoption, especially for novice training programs and resource-limited settings.

Conclusions

Our study comprehensively evaluated the novel dual-network hydrogel (DNH) model for ESD training and demonstrated favorable performance across multiple domains. The model replicated key biomechanical properties of human gastric tissue, with a layered design encompassing mucosal, submucosal, and muscular strata that enhanced the authenticity of the training experience. Within a controlled and safe environment, physicians trained on the DNH model exhibited measurable improvements in technical proficiency, highlighting the model’s potential to support the development of ESD skills.

In conclusion, the DNH model represents a promising innovation for endoscopic training by providing a high-fidelity, safe, and reusable platform. However, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary, given the exploratory design, modest sample size, and single-center setting. Further research—including multi-center studies with larger cohorts, formal power analyses, and long-term validation—is warranted to confirm reproducibility and optimize the model’s integration into surgical education and clinical practice.
