Temporal and spatial convergence: the major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence against women
Authors: Pengfei Guo, Rizhen Wang, Jiacheng Li, Yinghua Qin, Nan Meng, Linghan Shan, Huan Liu, Jingjing Liu, Qunhong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: There is a strong causal relationship between intimate partner violence and major depressive disorder, which partly endangers women's safety across the life course and potentially affects the development of future generations. The international community has placed a high priority on addressing the intimate partner violence and the resulting burden of mental illness. Data collection needs to be captured across the temporal trend and spatial distribution for major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence, to reflect the priorities and expectations of survivors.Method: This research obtained raw disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) information for major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence from the Global Burden of Disease 2019. Using estimated annual percentage change and two-way fixed effects models, a secondary spatio-temporal analysis of the age-standardized DALYs rate from 1990 to 2019 was performed.Results: In 2019, DALYs lost among women experiencing major depressive disorder (3.16 million) accounted for 37.18% of the DALYs lost worldwide due to intimate partner violence. The age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence was 108.57 per 100,000. The highest was concentrated in the menopausal transition (45-55), with 133.61 per 100,000, and particularly distributed in Uganda (429.31 per 100,000). The early reproductive period (15–19) showed the increasing age-standardized DALYs rate from 1990 to 2019, which was mainly driven by Malaysia (3.73% per year). Furthermore, countries with higher initial levels of the age-standardized DALYs rate were growing faster than those with lower levels.Conclusions: The burden of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence showed biological and spatial inequality, prioritized intervention should be targeted at vulnerable stage women in their early reproductive period and menopausal transition. Combined political, socio-cultural as well as medical measures to prevent violence and treat major depressive disorder should be implemented and developed.KEYWORDS: Intimate partner violence, major depressive disorder, women, disability-adjusted life years, Global Burden of Disease Study

Introduction

Intimate partner violence was generally defined as ‘any behaviour perpetrated against a current or former female intimate partner that causes physical, psychological, or sexual harm, including physical violence, emotional abuse, sexual violence, and controlling and coercive behaviours’ (Chang et al., 2005; Oram et al., 2022; WHO, 2022). It is relatively prevalent in the general population and clinical studies. Globally, more than one-third of women have been reported experiencing intimate partner violence in their lifetime (Secretary-General U, 2006). The number of reports of intimate partner violence against women is increasing steeply worldwide, especially in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic (Evcili & Demirel, 2022; Fereidooni et al. 2022). Intimate partner violence adversely affects women's physical health, resulting in an increased risk of injuries, chronic pain, and sexually transmitted diseases (Campbell, 2002; Thomas et al., 2021; Valera et al., 2019). Additionally, it has a negative impact on psychiatric illnesses in women like anxiety, depression, and other mental diseases (Antai & Anthony, 2014; Chandan et al., 2020; Devries et al., 2013). Compared with the negative physical and behavioural consequences among women, the mental disorders caused by intimate partner violence could be more severe and chronic (Aizpurua et al., 2021; Beydoun et al., 2012). Evidence of the strong causal relationship between intimate partner violence and major depressive disorder has been proved. This includes biological vulnerabilities, negative emotionality and cognitive style. The tendency to ruminate may contribute to women developing major depressive disorder (Hyde et al., 2008; Spencer et al., 2019). Associations between mental health problems and experiencing intimate partner violence appear to occur across the life course. The presence of mental health problems also makes individuals more vulnerable to experiencing and committing intimate partner violence than those without mental health diagnoses (Dillon et al., 2013). Elimination and prevention of the intimate partner violence against women epidemic and associated mental health harms are highlighted internationally. Following Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 5.2 on the elimination of violence against women and girls, the World Health Organization (WHO) 13th General Programme of Work (GPW) highlighted the framework and goals for action to address the violence against women (WHO, 2022). Mental health care makes a crucial difference in the path to healing for women survivors of experiencing intimate partner violence. WHO's LIVES principles for first-line response to intimate partner violence can help mental health professionals to recognize and respond to the gendered nature and dynamics of intimate partner violence against women. Data collection needs to be captured at a national level for mental health harms attributed to intimate partner violence against women, which reflects the priorities and expectations of survivors. The ultimate goal is to reduce mental health harms among women caused by intimate partner violence.

Therefore, this research conducted a standardized assessment of major depressive disorder burden owing to intimate partner violence among women taking into account geographic context. Data for the study were obtained from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study 2019. The national differences and trends of convergence for major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence among women aged over 15 years from 1990 to 2019 were added. According to the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW) 2011 criteria framework (Harlow et al., 2012), this research first classified women into five biological stages, including reproductive age (early 15–19, peak 20–34, and late 35–44), menopausal transition(45–55), and post-menopause(56+) stages. The standardized disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) are a measure of major depressive disorder burden owing to intimate partner violence. This research explored and identified the temporal and spatial distribution of high-risk biological stages of major depressive disorder burden in women, as well as trend convergence from 1990 to 2019.

Methods

Overview and definition

Original data about the major depressive disorder DALYs attributed to intimate partner violence was obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019. The GBD 2019 Study, conducted by Institute of Health Metrics and evaluation (IHME), is the largest and most comprehensive effort, to assess epidemiological levels and trends globally. The GBD 2019 study obtained data from multiple data sources, including national vital statistics, cancer registries, verbal autopsy reports, national health surveys, censuses, and published studies (Ilic & Ilic, 2024). GBD 2019 estimated 369 diseases and injuries; 286 causes of death; and 87 behavioural, environmental and occupational, and metabolic risk factors by region, sex, country, and age in 204 countries and territories, 7 super regions, and 21 regions from 1990 to 2019 (Cen et al., 2024). All this data is accessible for free access through the Global Health Data Exchange (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021/sources) (Ferrari et al., 2024).

Within the GBD 2019 framework, intimate partner violence was considered a proportion of the population who had ever experienced one or more acts of physical or sexual violence by a present or former intimate partner over the age 15 years. Major depressive disorder was defined as an episodic mood disorder, incorporating disability from the experience of depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure almost all day, every day, for at least two weeks. According to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases and Injuries (ICD-10), the codes for major depressive disorder were identified as F32.0-9, F33.0-9.

Estimation of DALYs

The indicator of DALYs was used to evaluate major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence among women across different biological stages at the global, regional, and national levels from 1990 to 2019. Age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder due to intimate partner violence was calculated using global standards (WHO 2000–2025) and expressed per 100,000 persons. Convergence analysis

This research finally explored whether countries worldwide reported convergence trends in the age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence, i.e. whether countries with higher age-standardized DALYs rate experienced a correspondingly rapid decline and eventually reached a steady state consistent with countries with lower age-standardized DALYs rate. Initially, the convergence test was used to analyse the disparities in economic growth between countries (Barro & Sala-i-Martin, 1992). And in the last decade, it has been widely used to study convergence effects on environmental quality, efficiency, and prevalence (Balla et al., 2021; Jaunky, 2013; Jin et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020; Zhang & Hao, 2020). Given data availability and the purpose of the study, this paper used absolute β convergence for the convergence test of age-standardized DALYs rate levels across countries globally. Results

The temporal trend of major depressive disorder DALYs attributed to intimate partner violence among overall women at the global level

There were 8.50 million DALYs in women resulting from intimate partner violence and 22.30 million DALYs caused by major depressive disorder worldwide in 2019 (Figure 1(A and B)). The overall DALYs of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence was 3.16 million among women in 2019, accounting for 14.2% of overall cases of major depressive disorder, and showing an increase of 2.9% from 1990 (Table 1, Figure 1(C)). The greatest DALYs of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence were found in women of peak reproductive age (1.03 million), whereas the lowest was in early reproductive age (0.12 million) (Table 1, Figure 2).

The overall age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder owing to intimate partner violence was 108.57 per 100,000 in 2019 and exhibited a downward trend from 1990 to 2019, with an annual average change of 0.42% (Tables 1 and 2). Age-standardized DALYs burden varied by different biological stages of women in 2019. The highest age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder caused by intimate partner violence was observed in menopausal transition (133.61 per 1000), followed by late reproductive age (121.82 per 1000), peak reproductive age (114.44 per 1000), post-menopause (106.6 per 1000), whereas early reproductive age (40.7 per 1000) was the last. Notably, women at early reproductive age showed the only increased age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder caused by intimate partner violence, with a 0.59% increase per year from 1990 to 2019. Other biological stages among women declined slightly and the most significant decrease was observed at peak reproductive age, showing a 0.60% decrease per year.

The spatial distribution of major depressive disorder DALYs attributed to intimate partner violence among high-risk biological stages of women

Compared with other biological stages, women in menopausal transition (having the highest burden in 2019) and early reproductive age (having the fastest burden from 1990 to 2019) were at high risk of suffering from major depressive disorder DALYs attributed to intimate partner violence. This research further explored the spatial distribution of the age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence at the regional and national level in two high-risk biological stages of women, which were in the menopausal transition and early reproductive stage.

The spatial distribution of major depressive disorder burden owing to intimate partner violence among the menopausal transition women was first reported in detail that they showed the highest age-standardized DALYs rate in 2019. The high DALYs burden of menopausal transition women was mainly concentrated in the regions of Central Sub-Saharan Africa (289.58 per 100,000), Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa (250.04 per 100,000), North Africa and Middle East (243.39 per 100,000). Nationally, Uganda (429.31 per 100,000), Iran (337.23 per 100,000) and Liberia (329.40 per 100,000) were countries with the major depressive disorder burden owing to intimate partner violence among the menopausal transition women (Tables 1 and 2, Table S1-2).

Secondly, there is a need to discuss the DALYs burden among early reproductive women, because it showed the only increased trend in age-standardized DALYs rate from 1990 to 2019 compared to other biological stages. The increasing trend among early reproductive women was most possibly driven by (1) regional level: Tropical Latin America (1.30% per year), followed by High-income North America (1.21% per year) and Australasia (0.85% per year). (2) Country level: Malaysia (3.73% per year) showed the highest increased trend, followed by the United Republic of Tanzania (2.16% per year) and Paraguay (1.95% per year) (Tables 1–2, Table S1-2, Figure 3).

The convergence effects of age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence among different biological stages of women

This research analysed the disparities in trend change between countries for age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence among different biological stages of women worldwide by using the absolutely β convergence test. The Hausman test results supported the fixed effects model.

According to empirical results, the global convergence coefficient for age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder owing to intimate partner violence from 1990 to 2019 was significantly positive at the 1% level. It showed a dispersion trend, which meant that the DALYs gap at the global level was gradually increasing and tending towards a state of internal disequilibrium. Specifically, five models were used to test for absolute convergence in the major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence in early reproductive, peak reproductive, late reproductive, transitional menopause and late menopause countries worldwide. The R2 of these five models varied from 0.019 to 0.310, which meant that the explained percentages of the dependent variable's variance in models 1–5 were significantly different. The DALYs β coefficient for each biological stage was significantly positive at the 1% level (Table S3). This indicated that there was no spontaneous absolute β convergence in the age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder across countries globally; That was, the countries with a low initial level of DALYs burden of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence did not have higher growth trend than the countries with high level. The burden levels of different countries were not expected to have a convergence trend.

Discussion

Globally, intimate partner violence remains a human rights issue that cannot be ignored and has a considerable impact on the physical and mental health of women (El-Nimr et al., 2021). Women who experienced intimate partner violence are twice as likely to suffer from depression as other women (WHO, 2022), which reminds the importance of monitoring the mental burden attributed to intimate partner violence in women. To our knowledge, this is the first study of major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence based on standard and comparable methods using global data from GBD 2019. This research systematically described the temporal trend, spatial distribution, time–space convergence and vulnerability at different biological stages in major depressive disorder burden experienced intimate partner violence among women aged over 15 from 1990 to 2019.

A declining trend of age-standardized DALYs rate was observed for major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence among overall women aged 15+ in the past 30 years. Undeniably, the global burden of major depressive disorder in women has been somewhat reduced due to concerted measures from global, regional, and national efforts in violence prevention and mental health services. The United Nations and the Commission on the Status of Women emphasized multi-sectoral cooperation to address the sources of violence against women (García-Moreno et al., 2013). The improvement of social structural factors of intimate partner violence against women, such as female education, female employment, poverty-reduction strategies, laws and policies that discriminate against women and anti-violence, policies to reduce harmful alcohol consumption, social norms against intimate violence, as well as mental health primary care all reduced the exposure women to intimate partner violence and improved mental health outcomes. Meanwhile, governments were gradually incorporating questions about women's exposure to partner violence into their national health surveys, including the demographic and health survey centre for disease, control and prevention reproductive health survey (Health MDDa, Surveys, 2022; Prevention CfDCa, 2022). In addition, mental health policies, programmes, or campaigns have been developed internationally to work toward the global goal of improving mental health through the Comprehensive Mental Health Action Plan for 2013–2030. These efforts have all resulted in a greater or lesser reduction in the burden of disease for major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence among overall women aged 15+ (Organization WH, 2021).

Women at different biological stages showed varying degrees of mental vulnerability when experiencing intimate partner violence, and women at menopausal transition were the main focus group. In 2019, women in menopausal transition experienced the highest age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence, although the major depressive disorder burden in the group decreased. Across multiple studies. Most women during the menopausal transition would experience symptoms, including aging-related biological and hormonal changes, sleep disturbances, mood symptoms, and vaginal dryness (Durvasula, 2014; Ngai, 2019). After experiencing intimate partner violence, their negative affect was higher (Gibson et al., 2019; Schei et al., 2006), and they were more likely to fall into major depressive disorder (Desai & Jann, 2000). The high burden of women with menopause was mainly distributed in the region of Central Sub-Saharan Africa, especially in the nation of Uganda. Intimate partner violence against women was widely accepted by a large proportion of women in Central Sub-Saharan Africa, and they even considered ‘arguing with husband’ and ‘refusing sex’ as valid reasons for wife beating (Rani & Bonu, 2009). The widespread acceptance of intimate partner violence against women caused a considerable hindrance to the success of family planning programmes, care seeking for sexually transmitted diseases or voluntary testing and mental counselling (Uthman et al., 2009).

Notably, the burden of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence showed a significantly increasing trend in women in early reproductive stage from 1990 to 2019. This research speculated that intimate partner violence was experiencing a trend shift toward younger women (Garcia-Black, 2002). Some studies reported younger pregnant women were more likely to suffer from intimate partner violence (Rogers & Gilvert, 2002), with an approaching 30% incidence of intimate partner violence (Decker et al., 2015; García-Moreno et al., 2013). They may not be fully mature in terms of psychological and social relationships. The mental problems arose when they were not aware of seeking help from family, professional anti-violence agencies and psychological diversion agencies after suffering intimate partner violence (Rogers & Gilvert, 2002). The mental burden from early reproductive stage experienced intimate partner violence showed spatial inequality and rapid trends were observed in Tropical Latin America, High-income North America, as well as High-income North America. Higher teenage pregnancy rate (Alina, 2022), higher per-drinker consumption (Defined by WHO as having consumed ≥60 g and equal to at least 5.0 international standardized alcoholic drinks on one occasion.) (Cherpitel et al., 2013), and open sexual attitudes tended to earlier accelerate young women’s exposure to intimate partner violence (Foshee et al., 2005; Moffitt & Caspi, 1999).

Country specifically, the major depressive disorder burden attributed to intimate partner violence in early reproductive age grew fastest in Malaysia from 1990-2019. Inadequate laws may be one of the reasons. The term ‘IPV’ is not formally recognized by Malaysian law. The relevant Malaysian law applies only to spouses and former spouses and other intimate relationships not mentioned, such as cohabiting partners, are excluded from the application of the law (Mohammed Na'aim et al., 2022). World Bank data showed that Malaysia's fertility rate among women aged 15–19 years had been increasing. Moreover, its laws did not explicitly criminalize acts such as marital rape (Anna & Antonio, 2010). There was reportedly a perception among young Malaysians that excessive control over partners was the norm and that violence was just one way to express anger (Hanafi et al., 2022). This may contribute to the social tolerance of intimate partner violence. In turn, the Malaysian intimate partner violence prevention and treatment system, the one-stop centre model, was long and fragmented and had no dedicated budget (Colombini et al., 2011). As a result, there was little evidence that it effectively reduced the negative impact of intimate partner violence on women and girls (Ellsberg et al., 2015). In addition, some staff at its specialty and regional hospitals were sometimes unsure how to deal with intimate partner violence-related mental problems and where to refer women (Colombini et al., 2012; Pau, 2015).

There was a significant dispersion in the burden for major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence across biological stages during 1990–2019 at the global level. The diversity and differences of countries across the globe in ethnically, culturally and religiously diverse economic development and education levels bring obstacles to comprehensively addressing the mental health harms of women owing to intimate partner violence. Interpretation of intimate partner violence by different sources is complex. The relative resource theory proposed relatively fewer female resources could lead to more abuse due to marital dependency (Vyas & Watts, 2009). Whereas relatively more women’s resources could increase violence due to the stress induced by status inconsistencies (Hornung et al., 1981). Multifaceted geographically differentiated may represent some difficulty in biding to change men's unconventional attitudes toward intimate partner violence. In addition, different resource endowments resulted in significant differences in violence prevention and mental health services. WHO showed that 44 of the 132 countries mentioned had limited or no implementation of medico-legal services for victims of sexual violence. For the scope of psychiatrists working in the mental health sector (per 100,000), it was less than one person in 62 countries.

Even so, it is a possible fact that the major depressive disorder harm to women caused by intimate violence remains underreported and heterogeneous. Quantifying intimate partner violence frequency, severity, types, and effects on mental health is challenging. Little consensus has been reached concerning the definition and measurement of significant typologies and constructs of intimate partner violence against women. Assessment of intimate partner violence preference quantifies information about the frequency, and category of experiencing violence given certain parameters, whereas women's subjective experiences are rarely taken into account. This neglects a complex phenomenon as defined by the survivor herself (Bogat et al., 2005). Furthermore, female victims are labelled, such as ‘battered woman’ which may not reflect how the target sees herself, because more than one-third of the women did not accept any of the labels for the acts they had experienced (Hamby & Gray-Little, 2000).

These findings reinforced the importance of intimate partner violence exposure as a significant public health concern and highlighted that the risk of major depressive disorder among female victims of intimate partner violence is still not negligible. Certain targeted interventions are necessary. We based our recommendations on a three-tier prevention framework. Primary prevention means prevention before intimate partner violence occurs. First, policymakers legislate for women's rights in intimate relationships. The second is education and media awareness programmes in the home, school, workplace, and broader community. Advocacy efforts must take into account the importance of sociocultural differences in different countries and biased attitudes toward intimate partner violence perceptions. Secondary prevention emphasizes identification. Conduct intimate partner violence screening in specialized facilities for women of all ages, especially for high-risk groups such as women in the early reproductive years and during the menopausal transition. Tertiary prevention is treatment, rehabilitation, and relapse prevention. There is a need to enhance the capacity of mental health medical personnel. For example, train their ability to cut through the mental problems caused by intimate partner violence. The following principles should guide the violence prevention and health sector in dealing with intimate partner violence incidents: Do not blame or stigmatize women survivors of intimate partner violence when they come for help. They should be given appropriate medical help and their privacy and safety should be guaranteed. In addition to training staff, a clear and accessible referral system needs to be established to ensure that women survivors of intimate partner violence can receive appropriate care and follow-up.

Conclusion

Major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence in women required increasing global attention. The age-standardized DALYs rate of major depressive disorder owing to intimate partner violence showed biological and spatial inequality. Women in the menopausal transition reported the highest age-standardized DALYs rate in 2019, especially in Central Sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and Middle East, Uganda, Iran and Liberia. Women among early reproductive stage showed the only increased age-standardized DALYs rate from 1990 to 2019, and the increase was probably driven by Tropical Latin America, High-income North America and Australasia, Malaysia, Tanzania and Paraguay. The age-standardized DALYs rate levels of major depressive disorder attributed to intimate partner violence worldwide were not expected to have a convergence trend from 1990 to 2019.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. GBD 2019 has some key limitations of its own. For example, some estimates may be based on low-quality data from sources that may not be representative of the country as a whole; estimates of disease burden are limited to standard epidemiological measures and do not take into account other economic and socially comprehensive effects (Bhatta et al.). More detailed information on limitations is detailed in other studies (Collaborators GMD, 2022; Vos et al., 2020). In addition, an in-depth analysis is not feasible, given data availability issues. Therefore, the speculated causes and associations in this study need to be treated with caution.
