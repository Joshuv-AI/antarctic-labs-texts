Bilateral retinal rescue through unilateral AAV8-CYP4V2 gene delivery in Bietti crystalline dystrophy
Authors: Laura A Jenny, Yao Li, Stephen H Tsang
Published: 2025

Main text

Gene supplementation therapy has gained regulatory approval and reimbursement by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for the treatment of inherited disorders, paving the way for application in precision medicine. Luo et al. described preclinical pharmacology and toxicology data for an AAV8 therapy (VGR-R01) for Bietti crystalline dystrophy (BCD)1 a form of autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa (arRP). BCD (Figure 1) is due to loss-of-function mutations in the cytochrome P450 family 4 subfamily v member 2 (CYP4V2) gene (OMIM 210370) and causes patients to accumulate lipid crystalline deposits in the retina before leading to retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptor atrophy causing severe blindness in middle-aged patients.2,3,4 Importantly, this study demonstrated bilateral rescue of vision in animal models of BCD receiving a unilateral subretinal injection of VGR-R01. As such, using the contralateral eye as a control in gene therapy clinical trials in ophthalmology may not be an ideal comparison as there may be effects from therapy, which confound the results. Notably, this study provides important takeaways about off-target biodistribution, which can be applied to other ocular gene therapy programs that use AAV8 or other adeno-associated virus vectors.

Color fundus image of a patient with BCD showing yellow crystalline deposits in the left eye retina

There have been several gene therapy clinical trials for inherited retinal dystrophies and, in particular, BCD (see Table 1). The first clinical trial of a gene therapy for BCD was conducted on 12 patients who received rAAV2/8-CYP4V2 at a dosage of 7.5 × 1010 vector genomes (VGs) via subretinal injection.5 Patients showed modest improvements in best corrected visual acuity (BCVA), electroretinography (ERG), microperimetry (MP), and the Visual Function Questionaire-25 at 180 days post-injection with rAAV2/8-CYP4V2. Notable adverse events included anterior chamber/conjunctival and corneal inflammation and edema, macular hole, and cataract. Overall, the mean increase in BCVA at 1 year post-treatment was 11 letters.5

Since this study, another open-label phase 1/2 clinical trial has been conducted to examine the safety and efficacy of AAV2-hCYP4V2 delivered at a dosage of 1.5 or 3 × 1011 VG/mL to BCD patients.6 This study found modest improvement in BCVA in treated and untreated eyes from BCD patients. Notably, patients who received the low dose had a higher mean BCVA gain (19.7 letters) compared to patients who received a high dose (6.8 letters). Examinations of the patients for the safety profiling of AAV2-hCYP4V2 were notable for six patients with ocular hemorrhage post-administration and one patient with anterior chamber inflammation. To measure efficacy, patient contrast sensitivity (CS), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and BCVA were collected. CS and OCT findings were not consistent with improvements in BCVA,6 suggesting that improvements in BCVA could be due to learning effects, rather than direct improvement from the gene therapy.

Luo et al. demonstrate a novel approach to gene therapy in BCD with VGR-R01, an AAV8-CYP4V2 vector, evaluated in both in vitro and in vivo BCD models.1 VGR-R01 was administered at various doses to ARPE-19 cells and CYP4V2−/− patient-derived RPE cells, where it successfully induced CYP4V2 expression. In Cyp4v3-deficient mice, subretinal delivery of VGR-R01 resulted in significant improvements in ERG b-wave amplitudes at 3, 6, and 9 months post-injection. Interestingly, functional improvements were detected not only in the directly injected eye but also in the contralateral, non-injected eye, suggesting that a non-therapeutic dose of the vector reached the fellow eye through systemic or trans-synaptic routes.7 This finding has broad implications for ocular gene therapy clinical trials, as it indicates that the contralateral eye may not be an ideal control. Future clinical trials of AAVs may need to carefully measure off-target biodistribution of vectors in preclinical animal models and design clinical trials accordingly if similar biodistribution is found in the contralateral non-injected eye. If ocular gene therapy treatments are found to have effects on the non-injected contralateral eye, then clinical outcome measures of visual function and retinal structure would have to be compared only to baseline values in the respective eye rather than comparing results in injected and non-injected eyes.

However, despite the observed functional rescue, Cyp4v3-deficient mice do not exhibit the hallmark progressive RPE degeneration seen in human BCD,2 limiting their utility as predictive models for long-term therapeutic efficacy. To bridge this translational gap, the safety and tolerability of VGR-R01 were further evaluated in higher-order species. In both rabbits and non-human primates, VGR-R01 was well tolerated—an essential milestone toward advancing to human clinical trials. Unexpectedly, CYP4V2 mRNA expression was detected in the contralateral, non-injected eyes of rabbits, suggesting that subretinal delivery of AAV8 may result in vector or transgene spread beyond the injected eye. This phenomenon aligns with prior hypotheses that astrocyte-mediated interocular trafficking may facilitate the movement of viral particles, RNA transcripts, or their metabolic products between eyes.7

Luo et al.’s report challenges a key assumption in the design of Food and Drug Administration ophthalmic gene therapy trials, namely, that the contralateral, untreated eye can frequently serve as a valid internal control for assessing anatomical efficacy. Luo et al.’s observation of contralateral transgene expression following unilateral subretinal injection raises important concerns about the reliability of this within-subject control design. This is especially relevant in light of similar bilateral effects observed in Cyp4v3−/− mice. Taken together, these results underscore the need for further investigation into interocular AAV8 distribution and its functional consequences in patients with BCD. They also highlight the importance of re-evaluating trial designs to ensure appropriate control selection in future clinical studies of VGR-R01 and other subretinal gene therapies.

Finally, Luo et al. provide safety data related to administration of VGR-R01 in New Zealand rabbits and cynomolgus monkeys showed as measured by ocular examination, OCT, ERG, fundus photography and intraocular pressure. Notably, rabbits injected with 1.6 × 1011 VGs/eye had a decrease in ERG amplitude. CYP4V2 DNA was detected in the optic tract, optic chiasma, and spleen of rabbits treated with VGR-R01, while monkeys treated with 2.4 × 1011 VGs/eye had CYP4V2 DNA in the spleen, thymus, lymph nodes, liver, heart, kidney, optic tract, and gonads.1

Overall, Luo et al.’s report in this journal demonstrates promising efficacy and safety data for AAV8-CYPV42, providing another potential therapeutic treatment for BCD patients.1 These findings—combined with a favorable safety profile and early signals of functional benefit—support advancing AAV8-CYP4V2 to a phase 3 clinical trial to rigorously evaluate long-term efficacy, durability of response, and therapeutic impact in a larger and more diverse patient population.
