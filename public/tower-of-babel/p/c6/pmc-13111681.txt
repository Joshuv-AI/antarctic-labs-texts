Artificial intelligence-assisted spatial omics-based biomimetic nanoplatform for intelligent and precise intervention in the immunosuppressive core region of ovarian cancer
Authors: JinKe Li, Weilin Liu, Yu Mu, Xiaoxue Wang, Hefeng Zhang, Kexin Tang, Dandan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvarian cancer (OC) ranks among the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) and pronounced spatial heterogeneity significantly restrict the efficacy of immunotherapy. Recent advances in single-cell omics and spatial transcriptomics (ST) have enabled the identification of immunosuppressive core regions within the TME at molecular and spatial levels. These regions often contain “exclusion structures” composed of regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in hypoxia-enriched niches. To achieve precise therapeutic modulation of these core microregions, researchers have developed biomimetic nanodrug platforms such as cell membrane-coated systems and exosome-based carriers. These platforms deliver immunoregulatory agents targeting programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), with enhanced efficiency and adaptability through multi-responsive mechanisms. The paper provides a comprehensive review of the spatial organization mechanisms, omics-based identification methods, and signaling network pathways that define the immunosuppressive core regions in OC. It also summarizes the design principles and adaptive strategies of biomimetic platforms and introduces an artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted closed-loop therapeutic framework encompassing identification, delivery, and feedback to support individualized and precise immunotherapy. Although the approach demonstrates strong potential for multidimensional integration, several challenges remain, including the standardization of spatial atlases, the stability of delivery systems, and cross-platform compatibility, all of which require further technological advancement.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Immunology

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) ranks among the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system and remains the leading cause of death among gynecological cancers, earning the name “silent killer”1. The disease often presents with subtle clinical symptoms, and more than 70% of patients are diagnosed at stage III-IV. The five-year survival rate remains approximately 45%, while the recurrence rate exceeds 70%2. Although standard treatment methods (surgery combined with chemotherapy) have prolonged survival in some patients, high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) still exhibits significant resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy, severely limiting treatment efficacy3.

Immunotherapy has produced major advances in several solid tumors; however, its efficacy in OC remains limited, with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) achieving an objective response rate of less than 15%4. Evidence indicates that the immunologically “cold” nature of OC, T cell exhaustion, and hypoxia-induced exclusion structures are primary factors contributing to immunotherapy resistance5. Immunosuppressive cells, such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), form localized immune barriers in hypoxic regions, preventing effective immune activation6. In addition, the pronounced spatial heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) hinders immune drugs from covering all pathogenic microregions7.

The fundamental obstacle to improving OC treatment lies in the exclusion structures that develop within hypoxia-enriched regions8. Achieving precise identification and targeted intervention of these “core microregions” has become a critical challenge for precision medicine9. The present review focuses on the integration of spatial omics and biomimetic nanodrug delivery as a promising approach to address these challenges and explores its underlying scientific rationale and translational potential.

Single-cell multi-omics technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq), together with spatial transcriptomics (ST) platforms, including 10× Visium and NanoString CosMx, have enabled multidimensional reconstruction of the TME9,10. These tools elucidate immune cell subpopulation functions at single-cell resolution and identify immunosuppressive hotspots and hypoxia-driven core regions within tissue space, representing key barriers to drug penetration. Meanwhile, developing biomimetic nanodrug systems offers a physical carrier for the “region-specific delivery” of immunoregulatory agents. By integrating exosomes, cell membrane coating, and multi-responsive mechanisms, these systems achieve precise intervention in immune barriers11,12. Therefore, establishing a closed-loop therapeutic framework based on omics-driven identification, targeted localization, controlled drug delivery, and efficacy feedback has emerged as an innovative strategy for achieving precision immunotherapy in OC.

Although the strategy integrating spatial omics with biomimetic drug delivery shows considerable potential, several critical challenges remain. First, the compatibility and standardization of ST data across different platforms have not been fully established13. Second, the actual targeting efficiency of biomimetic nanoplatforms in the complex TME is limited by delivery stability and immune recognition mechanisms9. Third, establishing a closed-loop system from atlas identification to therapeutic feedback still requires the deep integration of multi-omics and algorithmic models14.

This review focuses on the hypoxic immunosuppressive core regions within the OC TME. It systematically summarizes recent advances in the use of multi-omics technologies for spatial identification, analyzes the design principles and targeting mechanisms of biomimetic nanoplatforms, and proposes concepts for future artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted individualized delivery systems15. The review is organized around a three-stage therapeutic framework comprising identification, delivery, and feedback, and addresses three central scientific questions. First, at the identification stage, it examines the spatial organization of immunosuppressive structures within the TME and explores how spatial omics enables precise recognition of immunological hotspots. Second, at the delivery stage, it investigates how biomimetic nanosystems employ omics-based atlases to achieve targeted delivery within hypoxic immune microregions. Third, at the feedback stage, it analyzes how omics information informs the optimization of delivery pathways and supports the development of an AI-assisted individualized closed-loop therapeutic system16.

The review aims to provide multi-omics evidence, spatial localization frameworks, and nanodrug delivery strategies to advance precision immunotherapy for OC, promoting a transition from fragmented progress to an integrated therapeutic paradigm14,15. Given that the main barrier within the current OC TME is the hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α)-mediated immune exclusion axis, this pathway is considered the key focus for spatial omics identification, biomimetic platform construction, and AI feedback regulation9,14. The entire analysis centers on the three interconnected stages of HIF axis identification, blockade, and feedback regulation (Fig. 1A).

Immunosuppressive mechanisms and spatial heterogeneity in the OC TME

The section provides a systematic analysis of the formation mechanisms underlying immunosuppressive core regions within the OC TME from three perspectives. First, it describes the spatial organization and composition of immunosuppressive cell populations. Second, it examines the regulatory influence of hypoxia and metabolic signaling axes within these regions. Finally, it explains how intercellular signaling networks coordinate to establish spatial immune barriers.

Immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (iTME) and key cellular populations in OC

The OC TME exhibits significant immunosuppressive characteristics, primarily constructed by Tregs, TAMs, and MDSCs17. These immune cells suppress antitumor responses through both direct and indirect mechanisms, establishing a “cold tumor” phenotype inherently resistant to immunotherapy18,19. Tregs limit effector T cell activity by expressing high levels of Forkhead box P3 (FOXP3), Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte Antigen 4 (CTLA-4), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β)20. Clinically, increased Treg infiltration correlates with poor prognosis in OC21. In multiple advanced OC cohorts, patients with high Treg density exhibit shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) following chemotherapy or immunotherapy, underscoring the pivotal role of Tregs in immune tolerance17.

TAMs are among the most abundant immunosuppressive cells in the TME, predominantly consisting of M2-type macrophages22. These cells are induced by factors such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) and express high levels of arginase-1 (Arg-1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), and CD206, which promote angiogenesis, tissue remodeling, and immune evasion23. TAMs cooperate with Tregs and secrete chemokines such as CXCL12 to initiate immunosuppressive cascade reactions24. In animal models, targeting TAMs with CSF1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitors significantly suppresses tumor growth and enhances T cell infiltration25. In addition, MDSCs also play a critical immunosuppressive role in the OC TME21,26. They inhibit antitumor T cell functions by releasing reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and Arg-1, while simultaneously promoting Treg expansion17. Multiple single-cell studies have found that MDSCs are significantly enriched in chemoresistant OC tissues and co-localize with tumor stem cell regions, suggesting their involvement in establishing immunosuppressive core regions27.

These immunosuppressive populations display spatial clustering and dynamic evolution correlated with tumor progression19. Early-stage TMEs are dominated by inflammatory immune responses, whereas advanced stages show a transition toward immunosuppression28. Multi-omics studies suggest that the reprogramming of the immune microenvironment driven by these suppressive cells may represent a critical mechanism underlying OC refractoriness15. Therefore, targeting these cell populations or their mediated signaling pathways is a critical strategy to reverse immunosuppression and improve the response rate to immunotherapy.

Hypoxia signals and the immune-metabolic coupling network driven by HIF

The hypoxic state within the TME is a key microenvironmental factor driving the establishment of immunosuppressive core regions29. Among these, HIFs HIF-1α and HIF-2α, as oxygen-sensing transcriptional regulators, play central roles in modulating immune cell polarization, immune checkpoint expression, and metabolic adaptation30. In OC, HIF-1α is widely expressed in ischemic marginal regions and upregulates immunosuppressive molecules such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), VEGF, and IL-10. The distribution of HIF-1α closely coincides with immune “cold zones,” highlighting its central role in establishing exclusion structures8,31. In vivo models, deletion of HIF-1α expression significantly reduces Treg infiltration density and enhances the efficacy of ICIs32.

HIF-2α, though less frequently studied, also contributes to maintaining cancer stem cell characteristics and promoting the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells33. Evidence indicates that HIF-2α modulates tumor cell expression of CXCL8 and CSF1, facilitating the accumulation of TAMs and MDSCs and reinforcing the hypoxia-driven immunosuppressive feedback loop34. In addition, the HIF pathway influences glutamine metabolism, lactate production, and other metabolic axes, affecting TME pH and immune-metabolic coupling35.

Clinically, multiple ST studies have confirmed that HIF-1α-enriched regions in OC tissues highly overlap with immune “cold zones,” supporting its potential role as a spatial biomarker for immune-targeted drug delivery36. Combining HIF inhibitors and immunotherapy strategies is being tested in early-phase clinical trials. Several small-molecule inhibitors, such as PT2385 targeting HIF-2α, have shown promising effects in enhancing T cell infiltration in prostate and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, indicating potential applicability in OC treatment37,38.

Construction of spatial signaling axes and mechanisms for maintaining immune barriers

The formation of immunosuppressive core regions in OC depends on the spatial clustering and metabolic adaptation of immune cells and a highly coordinated intercellular signaling network39,40. Immunosuppressive cells, primarily TAMs, Tregs, and MDSCs, interact with tumor cells and effector T cells through various ligand-receptor pathways to construct a sustained immune evasion network within the TME40,41.

In the OC TME, single-cell communication analysis tools such as CellPhoneDB have revealed that M2-type TAMs activate tumor cell immune evasion pathways via the SPP1-CD44 axis, inducing the upregulation of T cell exhaustion markers such as LAG-3 and TIGIT39,42. TAMs also cooperate with Tregs through CXCL12–CXCR4 and CXCL13–CXCR5 axes to regulate T cell localization and inactivation40,43. Tools like NicheNet and CellChat have further revealed that MDSCs secrete factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and TGF-β to induce tumor cell PD-L1 expression and construct an “inhibitory signaling loop,” forming a spatially selective immune negative regulation circuit39.

ST studies have also confirmed that the activity of these signaling pathways is significantly enhanced in hypoxic marginal areas and the stroma-tumor transition zones, overlapping significantly with HIF-1α expression regions. These findings suggest that immunosuppressive signaling exhibits strong spatial clustering and radiative distribution patterns36,39. In multi-omics integrated analyses, M2-type TAMs have been identified as hub cells within the signaling network. Their negative regulatory signals extensively affect Tregs, CD8+ exhausted T cells, and tumor stem-like cells, forming core microregions with clear spatial localization and potent functional blockade40,44.

These intercellular communication pathways not only reveal the spatial topological features of the immune barrier in OC but also provide a theoretical basis for subsequent spatial omics-based precise localization of signaling hotspots, target identification, and spatial drug delivery39,45. However, most current network models rely on established ligand–receptor databases and may not capture non-classical or unidentified signaling axes, emphasizing the need for systematic optimization and experimental validation46. As multiple immune cell subpopulations jointly construct the immunosuppressive signaling network, defining their key molecular axes is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies47,48. Current studies have systematically elucidated multiple signaling pathways, including PD-1/PD-L1, IL-6-STAT3, CXCL12-CXCR4, and SPP1-CD44, with promising results in both preclinical and clinical investigations48–53. Applications of spatial omics and single-cell omics in identifying immunosuppressive cores

Multi-omics has been widely applied in the diagnosis and treatment of OC, playing a significant clinical role in its prediction and early detection, including genomics, metabolomics, single-cell omics, transcriptomics, and proteomics54 (Fig. 2). Notably, the emerging single-cell and spatial omics technologies in recent years have provided new technical approaches for targeted intervention and therapy within the TME of ovarian cancer45. The following sections mainly discuss their applications in the prevention and treatment of OC.

Applications of multi-omics in the prediction and early diagnosis of OC.

Functional role of scRNA-seq in deciphering immune heterogeneity

scRNA-seq quantifies mRNA expression at the single-cell level, enabling the construction of detailed functional maps of cellular subpopulations within the TME. This technique is particularly effective for analyzing immune heterogeneity and dynamic immune states in OC22. Compared with bulk RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq captures state-specific variations among individual immune cells, including exhaustion, activation, and migration, providing critical insights into immune regulatory mechanisms in OC51,55.

Extensive studies have employed scRNA-seq to profile immune cell populations in OC tissues, revealing distinct subsets such as CD8⁺ T cells (cytotoxic, exhausted, and interferon-responsive), regulatory Tregs, natural killer (NK) cells, and TAMs. These analyses have elucidated differences in functional states, immune evasion pathways, and prognostic relevance25. For example, single-cell transcriptomic analyses of ovarian cancer have revealed distinct CD8+ T cell subpopulations within the tumor microenvironment, and their proportions are closely associated with the response to immunotherapy56.

Immune subpopulations, particularly FOXP3⁺ Tregs and CD163⁺ TAMs, are highly enriched in hypoxic regions that coincide with elevated HIF-1α expression22. This observation indicates that scRNA-seq not only defines functional immune states but also provides indirect spatial clues for identifying immunosuppressive core regions.

Animal model experiments have further validated the dynamic analytical value of scRNA-seq. Temporal tracking of CD8⁺ T cell states revealed a marked expansion of PD-1⁺ TOX⁺ exhausted T cells in lactate-treated groups, suggesting the potential of this approach for monitoring therapeutic responses and functional reprogramming57.

In summary, scRNA-seq serves as a foundational tool for mapping immune heterogeneity, characterizing functional immune subpopulations, and guiding the development of spatially targeted therapeutic strategies. Its application is essential for elucidating the complex immune microarchitecture of OC.

Mechanism of scATAC-seq in identifying epigenetic regulation in hypoxic immunosuppressive regions

scATAC-seq detects open chromatin regions within individual cell subpopulations using Tn5 transposase, enabling the prediction of potential regulatory elements and transcription factor (TF) activities. The technique plays a critical role in elucidating cell fate decisions and immune epigenetic regulation58,59. This technology has been widely applied in OC to reveal upstream driving mechanisms of immunosuppressive formation.

A recent scATAC-seq study identified AP-1 and STAT3 binding sites specifically open in TAMs from OC patients, demonstrating their activating roles in immunosuppressive signaling60. More importantly, regulatory regions open in Tregs within hypoxic areas are enriched for HIF-1α response elements and are accompanied by the co-expression of immunosuppressive factors such as CTLA-4 and IL-10, suggesting a close association between chromatin accessibility and immunosuppressive signaling axes22,61.

Clinical studies further support these observations. In some OC patients, CD8⁺ T cells display open enhancer regions for RUNX3 and TCF7, indicating a functionally active or potentially reversible state. In contrast, other patient samples show TOX-driven chromatin remodeling, leading to a transcriptional exhaustion phenotype associated with increased therapeutic resistance62,63.

Functional validation using CRISPR interference has confirmed the biological relevance of regulatory elements identified by scATAC-seq. Inhibition of the STAT3 enhancer in TAMs markedly reduces tumor growth and enhances CD8⁺ T cell activation60. Such findings establish a coherent framework linking chromatin accessibility mapping, functional target identification, and therapeutic intervention.

In summary, scATAC-seq is one of the core technologies for identifying epigenetic regulatory mechanisms within the OC TME, elucidating the immunosuppressive mechanisms of hypoxic core regions, and guiding the design of targeted interventions.

Multi-omics data integration strategies and spatial localization approaches

ST retains the spatial positions of gene expression within tissue sections, integrating morphological context with molecular profiles. It is particularly effective for identifying functionally heterogeneous regions such as immunosuppressive hotspots and hypoxic cores64,65. Mainstream ST platforms, including 10× Genomics Visium, Slide-seqV2, and Stereo-seq, offer resolutions ranging from tens of micrometers to the subcellular level, enabling simultaneous analysis of subpopulation localization and tissue architecture66.

In OC samples, detection using the Visium platform has shown that Tregs, M2-type TAMs, and exhausted CD8+ T cells are predominantly concentrated at the tumor-stroma boundary and highly overlap with HIF1A expression regions67,68. These findings confirm the spatial co-localization of immunosuppressive cores and hypoxic niches, establishing a structural foundation for spatially targeted therapeutic strategies5,32. In addition, spatial clustering analysis can identify “immune hot regions” (rich in activated T cells) and “immune cold regions” (enriched with MDSCs and Tregs), indicating that spatial distribution patterns may serve as a basis for patient stratification and response prediction69,70.

The Slide-seq approach enhances spatial resolution through a nanobead array, allowing precise mapping of local signaling axis hotspots within densely packed tumor regions. For example, the CXCL12-CXCR4 pathway shows extremely high expression in certain areas, indicating that these are regions enriched with drug delivery targets71,72. Stereo-seq, a recently developed high-throughput technology, achieves broader tissue coverage and subcellular precision, enabling the identification of layered metabolic–immune heterogeneity in endometrial and gastric cancers, and its applications in OC are rapidly increasing73.

From a translational perspective, spatial omics data can provide coordinate references for the precision design of biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems74. For example, spatial transcriptomics approaches such as Visium have been applied to identify molecular markers that influence nanomedicine distribution within tumor tissues, thereby providing guidance for the design and optimization of targeted nanoparticle delivery strategies75. Integrating spatial omics data into clinical and surgical pathway planning and individualized treatment regimens holds significant promise for advancing precision immunotherapy in OC.

Reconstruction of cell–cell communication axes and mechanistic insights into immune target aggregation

Cell–cell communication represents a fundamental mechanism shaping the functional organization of the TME. Analytical frameworks based on single-cell and spatial omics data have enabled systematic elucidation of ligand–receptor interaction pathways across cellular populations45,76. Widely used tools such as CellPhoneDB, NicheNet, and CellChat, which rely on statistical correlations, regulatory inference, and signaling network reconstruction, respectively, have been extensively applied to studies of the OC TME39,77,78.

CellPhoneDB uses a random permutation algorithm to assess the significance of ligand–receptor pair enrichment between interacting cell types. Analyses in OC have revealed that M2-type TAMs activate immune evasion mechanisms in regulatory Tregs and tumor cells through signaling pathways such as SPP1–CD44 and TGFβ1–TGFBR245. NicheNet integrates TF regulation with target gene expression to identify which cell populations drive specific transcriptional responses. The method is particularly suited to spatial omics datasets, allowing dissection of signaling axis formation within defined microenvironmental hotspots76.

CellChat provides structural insights into intercellular signaling topologies by reconstructing the global architecture of communication networks. Applications in multiple OC single-cell datasets have identified TAMs as central hub nodes transmitting the majority of immunosuppressive signals, whereas exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and Tregs function as major receiver populations4,79. The hub–periphery organization of these networks provides a conceptual framework for biomimetic drug delivery, emphasizing the strategy of targeting hub-driven signaling axes. To enhance spatial resolution, recent integrative workflows have been developed to map functional cellular states to precise spatial coordinates through multi-omics data alignment (Fig. 3A)4,79.

Construction logic and functional modules of biomimetic nanodrug platforms

Targeting the immune barrier formed in HIF-1α-enriched regions, biomimetic nanosystems are emerging as a key technological approach to overcome spatial constraints and the complexity of target distribution87,88. These systems achieve precise drug intervention through the “identification-delivery-response” process by integrating with omics-derived coordinates.

Structural types and delivery design mechanisms of biomimetic nanosystems

Biomimetic nanocarriers integrate synthetic nanostructures with biological membrane characteristics, offering high biocompatibility, immune evasion capability, and precise targeting. These systems have become essential tools for spatial drug delivery due to their ability to penetrate immune barriers and achieve site-specific accumulation89,90. Compared with conventional liposomes or polymer nanoparticles, biomimetic systems demonstrate superior efficiency in crossing complex immune microenvironments and hold broad potential for tumor immune modulation91,92. Current biomimetic platforms can be broadly divided into three major categories:

Cell membrane biomimetic systems utilize membranes derived from red blood cells, T cells, macrophages, or tumor cells to coat synthetic nanocores, forming a core–shell structure. These systems leverage intrinsic self-recognition properties to evade immune clearance and achieve homologous targeting. For instance, T cell membrane-coated carriers enhance immunological synapse formation and locally increase the concentration of therapeutic agents such as PD-L1 inhibitors93.

Surface functionalization plays a critical role in improving targeting precision. Common strategies include antibody conjugation (e.g., anti-PD-L1, anti-TGFβ, and anti-CSF1R), peptide modification (e.g., RGD and CRV), and viral membrane camouflage97. Studies have shown that CRV-modified nanoparticles can precisely deliver STAT3 inhibitors to M2-type TAM regions, significantly enhancing their conversion to the M1 phenotype97,98. Moreover, adenovirus membrane-modified systems possess dual functions of “delivery and activation” by activating the TLR9-NFκB signaling pathway to boost immune responses (Fig. 4)99.

Drug delivery strategies targeting immunoregulatory pathways

Biomimetic nanosystems enable the precise release of therapeutic agents targeting key regulatory pathways within the iTME. By delivering antagonistic molecules such as anti-PD-L1, anti-TGFβ, indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) inhibitors, and CSF1R inhibitors, these platforms can effectively alleviate immune suppression and reprogram the TME toward an antitumor state26,100.

Recent research has demonstrated the effectiveness of biomimetic nanoplatforms targeting the PD-L1 axis8. Red blood cell membrane-coated nanoparticles loaded with anti-PD-L1 antibodies have shown controlled release within macrophage-rich regions, leading to enhanced activation of CD8⁺ T cells and improved immune surveillance101,102. Blocking the TGF-β pathway has also attracted considerable attention. Agents such as Galunisertib exhibit stronger inhibitory effects under hypoxic conditions, suggesting that spatial omics-guided delivery to hypoxia-enriched “hotspot” regions could further improve therapeutic precision103,104.

Combined intervention strategies have also emerged as a central focus in recent years8. Studies have shown that the simultaneous delivery of anti-PD-L1 and lactate oxidase can block both immune and metabolic axes in T cell exhaustion core regions, significantly improving therapeutic responses105–107. Such combination platforms enhance drug efficacy and significantly reduce systemic toxicity and side effects, providing a foundation for clinical translation.

Multi-responsive nanodrug delivery strategies driven by physiological barriers in the OC TME

The OC TME is characterized by multiple physiological barriers, including hypoxia, acidity, and enzyme overexpression. Exploiting these conditions to design responsive release systems enhances the regional selectivity and bioactivity of biomimetic nanoplatforms.

The hypoxia-responsive (HIF) mechanism involves incorporating hypoxia-sensitive linkers such as 2-nitroimidazole or AZO aryl groups, which undergo cleavage in HIF-1α-enriched regions. For instance, hypoxia-activated nanoparticles delivering a TGF-β inhibitor have been shown to induce macrophage repolarization and restore immune activation105,106.

Integrating multiple responsive mechanisms has become a key trend. Systems combining hypoxia, MMP, and pH responsiveness can achieve spatiotemporally controlled release within the tumor core, markedly improving therapeutic precision110–112.

Omics-guided spatial mapping provides essential coordinates for the design of such responsive platforms. ST technologies, including Visium and Slide-seq, identify immunosuppressive hotspots associated with targets such as CXCL12, PD-L1, and HIF-1α, enabling targeted delivery design8,71. Previous studies have combined Slide-seq with scRNA-seq to locate M2-TAM regions and successfully constructed CRV-exosome systems to achieve immune restoration113–115.

Precision drug delivery strategies enabled by multi-responsive platforms integrated with omics

Integration of multi-responsive mechanisms with spatial coordinates derived from omics atlases enables the construction of highly precise and dynamic drug delivery systems112,116,117. To address the spatial heterogeneity and dense immunosuppressive networks within the OC TME, biomimetic nanosystems are being developed into multi-responsive platforms coupled with spatial omics technologies, forming an “atlas-guided and multi-axis responsive” delivery framework71,110,118,119.

Design strategies for multi-responsive systems focus on TME characteristics such as hypoxia, acidity, and enzyme overexpression8,120. For example, HIF structures (e.g., 2-nitroimidazole, AZO aryl groups) undergo reduction and cleavage in regions with high HIF-1α expression, allowing site-specific release of inhibitors targeting TGF-β or PD-L1. Enzyme-responsive carriers exploit MMP or Cathepsin overexpression to initiate shell degradation in stromal cell-rich zones. Acid-responsive systems employ polymers such as PEG-b-PDEAEMA and PLGA to enable drug release under the mildly acidic conditions of the TME121–123. Composite nanosystems combining hypoxia-, enzyme-, and pH-responsive mechanisms have achieved synergistic triple-stimulus release, resulting in superior localization and antitumor efficacy compared with single-responsive systems in murine models124,125.

Spatial omics data provide a “spatial coordinate system” for drug delivery platforms71. Technologies such as 10× Visium and Slide-seq can map the spatial distribution of immunosuppressive pathways, including CXCL12, HIF1A, and PD-L1, offering a target map for designing delivery routes and carriers126,127. A representative study applied Slide-seq to identify enrichment regions of M2-type TAMs and regulatory Tregs, followed by CRV peptide-modified exosome delivery of a STAT3 inhibitor, effectively reversing local immune suppression113.

AI-assisted spatial atlas modeling and multi-modal omics integration are being increasingly applied to enhance the spatial recognition and target-matching capacity of multi-responsive delivery platforms128,129. Integration of scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq, and ST enables AI algorithms to reconstruct multidimensional expression maps of immunosuppressive niches, providing accurate spatial coordinates for regions co-enriched with hypoxic and immunosuppressive signals128,129.

A new paradigm of biomimetic nanodrug delivery for precision intervention in oc spatial heterogeneity

In addressing HIF-enriched immunosuppressive core regions, spatial drug delivery strategies can be broadly divided into three pathways: precision delivery targeting immune cells, synergistic interventions integrating metabolic axes, and closed-loop regulatory mechanisms guided by omics coordinates. The following sections provide a systematic discussion of each pathway, highlighting representative delivery systems, key studies, and their potential for clinical translation.

Limitations of traditional delivery systems in addressing spatial heterogeneity and the breakthrough advantages of biomimetic systems

Although ICIs have achieved success in several solid tumors, their therapeutic efficacy in OC remains limited. The main reason lies in their inability to effectively reach the “immunosuppressive microregions” shaped by pronounced spatial heterogeneity40,113. These regions, typically aggregations of Tregs, TAMs, and MDSCs, are distributed in hypoxic margins, perivascular areas, and fibrotic bands, where signaling molecules such as PD-L1, TGF-β, and CXCL12 are highly enriched113. Conventional systemic administration fails to achieve sufficient spatial precision, leading to suboptimal therapeutic responses and significant systemic toxicity137. In contrast, biomimetic nanoplatforms construct delivery coordinates based on spatial atlas data and provide three major advantages: spatial mapping with target focusing, structural integration for multimodal delivery, and individualized optimization through AI, collectively addressing key spatial therapeutic barriers138.

The immunosuppressive microenvironment in OC involves intricate signaling axis networks such as the HIF-1α–VEGF–PD-L1 and TGFβ–Smad–STAT3 pathways, whose active regions display strong spatial clustering and structural dependence139,140. Without spatial targeting capability, conventional carrier systems fail to cover these signaling “hotspots,” resulting in local failure of the immune blockade and the so-called “local escape phenomenon”8,141.

Biomimetic nanosystems overcome these challenges by integrating spatial omics data to construct a comprehensive “delivery coordinate system,” achieving three principal breakthroughs4. First, high spatial localization precision is realized through platforms such as Visium and Slide-seq, which identify immunosuppressive hotspots and map delivery targets with spatial accuracy71,136. Second, functional modular integration allows nanoplatforms to combine targeting ligands, environment-responsive elements, and therapeutic payloads within a unified design, ensuring simultaneous targeting, release, and regulation132. Third, individual adaptation potential is achieved through AI algorithms that align spatial atlas data with drug targets, enabling personalized carrier optimization and avoiding generalized therapeutic approaches that limit efficacy142.

In summary, traditional drug delivery systems encounter spatial therapeutic limitations including insufficient targeting, unstable efficacy, and delayed feedback. In contrast, biomimetic nanoplatforms guided by omics data possess the transformative potential to achieve precise identification, targeted delivery, and dynamic regulation, offering an innovative strategy to overcome spatial heterogeneity in OC143.

Spatially targeted delivery systems for Tregs, TAMs, and MDSCs

In the OC TME, the three major immunosuppressive cells, including TAMs, Tregs, and MDSCs, play critical roles in immune evasion and treatment resistance113. Targeted delivery systems employ antibodies, peptides, or aptamers to recognize these cells, achieving dual functions of “directional delivery and functional blockade”144.

In TAM-targeted repolarization strategies, CRV peptide-modified exosomes have been designed to deliver TLR7 agonizts, enabling selective intervention in M2-type TAMs and promoting their polarization toward the M1 phenotype, which enhances T cell activation145. Other studies have used MnO₂ as a nanocarrier to co-deliver a CSF1R inhibitor and an immune adjuvant, achieving localized release through an H₂O₂-responsive mechanism and reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment17,146. Clinical studies have shown that folate-decorated nanoparticles can encapsulate drugs and effectively target cancer cells, addressing the problem of chemoresistance in ovarian cancer147. Scientists have sought to enhance the innate immune response by co-delivering cyclic GMP–AMP (cGAMP) and monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA) within specialized nanocarriers. This formulation has been demonstrated to promote the expression of CD86 and the secretion of key proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-β and IL-6. Furthermore, studies indicate that delivering synergistic adjuvants and peptide neoantigens using a pH-responsive nanoparticle platform can enhance dendritic cell activation and improve peptide antigen presentation on MHC-I molecules, thereby potentially boosting the CD8⁺ T-cell response148

Targeting strategies for Tregs and MDSCs often focus on signaling axes such as FOXP3, CTLA-4, IDO, and Arg-1131. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-based nanoplatforms delivering immunoregulatory agents, including anti-CTLA-4 antibodies or IDO inhibitors, have been validated across multiple tumor models, demonstrating improved Teff/Treg ratios and enhanced antitumor immunity. Metal-organic framework (MOF)-based nanostructures, characterized by high ROS sensitivity and metal ion loading capacity, have also been applied to deliver Arg-1 inhibitors for suppressing MDSC activity149,150.

Spatial omics and scRNA-seq data indicate that immunosuppressive cells preferentially accumulate in hypoxic margins, perivascular niches, and fibrotic stromal areas. These spatial features highlight the necessity of integrating targeting mechanisms with spatially guided delivery strategies to achieve efficient and precise therapeutic coverage45,151.

Synergistic delivery strategies for immunosuppressive core regions combining metabolic intervention

In the OC TME, metabolic reprogramming phenomena such as lactate accumulation, dysregulated fatty acid metabolism, and amino acid metabolic competition significantly suppress the activity of immune effector cells152–154. The primary, ascitic, and metastatic tumors are closely interconnected within the same pathological continuum. The primary tumor acts as the origin. When it arises within the abdominal cavity, as in peritoneal mesothelioma, it progressively induces ascitic fluid accumulation, forming the “primary ascitic tumor,” composed of tumor cells that adapt to a relatively simple and weakly immunosuppressive environment. Conversely, when the primary tumor develops outside the abdominal cavity, such as in colorectal cancer, tumor cells can disseminate into the peritoneal cavity and establish “metastatic tumors.” To survive in this new environment, these cells remodel the ascitic microenvironment by secreting immunosuppressive factors, forming physical and metabolic protective barriers, and altering energy pathways. As a result, the ascitic tumor evolves into a “metastatic ascitic tumor,” characterized by an immunosuppressive and metabolically adaptive milieu that promotes further dissemination. This process creates a self-reinforcing cycle of “metastatic tumors expanding through a remodeled ascitic environment,” signifying the shift from localized proliferation to widespread metastasis. In advanced ovarian cancer, ascites formation and tumor metastasis frequently occur, accompanied by significant alterations in the TME. Ascites represents the pathological accumulation of fluid within the peritoneal cavity and serves as a conduit for tumor cell dissemination across pelvic and peritoneal organs. Through transcoelomic spread, cancer cells adhere to serosal and omental surfaces, leading to secondary implantation within the peritoneal cavity. The ascitic fluid contains both tumor and non-tumor cells, together forming a distinct microenvironment that modulates the malignant potential of tumor cells155. Compared with ascitic fluid, the metastatic TME is enriched with metastasis-associated proteins such as mucin-16 (MUC16) and MMPs. Building on this understanding, increasing research has focused on co-delivering metabolic pathway inhibitors and immunomodulators to achieve precision therapy across ovarian cancer subtypes by selectively targeting immune core regions156.

One study designed a HIF polymeric nanosystem for the co-delivery of an MCT4 inhibitor and a STING agonist, enabling synergistic release in HIF-1α-activated zones. This dual delivery significantly enhanced type I interferon signaling and CD8⁺ T cell activity157,158. Another study utilized an exosome-based delivery system to co-load the fatty acid synthase inhibitor Orlistat with an anti-PD-1 antibody, thereby restoring T cell function and reducing tumor burden in lipid metabolism–active regions159. Preclinical research has shown that superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles effectively target metastatic breast cancer160. As no universal cancer biomarker has been identified, research has increasingly focused on multiplex detection strategies. One study applied a nanotechnology-assisted fine-needle aspiration technique to quantitatively assess the expression of nine known cancer biomarkers and one leukocyte marker. A four-marker panel, including MUC-1, EGFR, HER2, and EpCAM, achieved a diagnostic accuracy of 96% compared with pathological results from matched patient biopsies161. Nanotherapies exhibit distinct treatment behaviors and can be classified according to the origin of their self-assembly stimuli as either endogenous or exogenous. Endogenous stimuli include overexpressed enzymes, acidic pH, and altered redox environments. Compared with normal tissues, TMEs often display elevated enzyme activity, lower pH, and distinct redox conditions, which can trigger the self-assembly of small-molecule drugs. Under these physiological conditions, drug precursors undergo cleavage or conjugation reactions that modify their chemical structures and facilitate the formation of nanomedicine assemblies. Exogenous stimuli, in contrast, are external physical or chemical factors such as temperature variation or induced reactions. Differences in assembly mechanisms can result in variable drug-loading capacities across nanotherapeutic systems162.

Spatial omics analyses have revealed that metabolism-immune co-regulated microregions are typically located within tumor stem cell niches, perivascular areas, and hypoxic boundaries. Integrating metabolic maps with immune spatial expression profiles enables greater precision in drug delivery and enhances overall therapeutic efficacy163,164.

These limitations restrict their ability to meet the precision treatment requirements of heterogeneous microregions, individualized targets, multiple signaling pathways, and dynamic regulation174. The emergence of intelligent feedback biomimetic platforms, driven by spatial omics-derived coordinates and AI-assisted mechanisms, is establishing a closed-loop architecture of “identification–response–regulation–evaluation,” which is poised to become a central component of next-generation tumor drug delivery strategies163.

Design of intelligent nanostructure platforms with feedback regulation capability

With the rapid accumulation of multi-dimensional data from single-cell omics, ST, proteomics, and metabolomics, building cross-omics integrated models for target identification and patient stratification has become an essential component of precision medicine182. In OC, these approaches are particularly essential due to the pronounced spatial heterogeneity and complex immune evasion mechanisms. Traditional pathological indicators alone cannot accurately identify patients who may benefit from immunotherapy183. Multi-omics models integrate multiple-level information to provide a precise decision-making foundation for individualized drug selection184.

In one representative study, researchers applied the Tangram algorithm to align scRNA-seq data with spatial transcriptomic coordinates on the 10× Visium platform, thereby constructing a functional mapping between cell types and spatial pixels to generate a predictive model of immunosuppressive microregion distribution185. Based on the spatial atlas, a CNN model was then trained to recognize heatmap features of FOXP3⁺ Treg and M2-TAM clusters, achieving an AUC greater than 0.92 in an independent validation cohort and demonstrating strong discriminative capacity for immunosuppressive core regions183. The model further enabled inverse prediction of optimal delivery landing zones, guiding localized release of anti-CTLA-4 and anti-CSF1R combinations, which markedly enhanced intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and reduced immune “cold zones”183.

In practical applications, scRNA-seq resolves cell composition and state, spatial omics determine tissue localization, proteomics reveal target expression patterns, and metabolomics elucidate the metabolic coordination underlying immune suppression186. For instance, an integrated analysis of scRNA, CITE-seq, and Visium data from patients with HGSOC compared immune activation and metabolic profiles between responders and nonresponders to immunotherapy. The study identified the STAT3–TGFβ–HIF1α signaling axis as a key axis of immune exclusion, suggesting its suitability for intervention using nano-TAM repolarization strategies184. To systematically integrate AI with omics atlases, current research explores a four-step closed-loop mechanism of “spatial expression-target recommendation-platform regulation-efficacy feedback”182. In this framework, scRNA provides cell state labels, the spatial atlas provides tissue localization, and the AI model identifies atlas features and recommends the optimal drug-carrier-adaptive region combination, achieving a data-driven full-pathway control from identification to design to release to feedback182.

Representative models include Cellformer, a cross-modal atlas–target prediction network; Vis-AI, a CNN-based spatial feature recognition system; and Drug-SpatialNet, a drug–target–delivery pathway matching framework (Fig. 7)182. In these models, scRNA-seq assigns functional cell labels, ST determine localization, AI algorithms integrate multi-omic inputs to predict target–carrier configurations, and feedback from therapeutic efficacy data, such as CD8⁺ T cell activation curves, enables iterative optimization, forming a “data–platform–efficacy” tri-axis interaction mechanism182.

Omics atlas decoding and delivery pathway optimization strategies based on AI models

AI technologies, particularly deep learning (DL), GNNs, and generative adversarial networks (GANs), have rapidly advanced applications in precision medicine, spatial omics, and targeted nanodrug delivery79,114. Within spatial atlas–driven drug delivery systems, AI has shown strong capabilities in feature extraction, pathway prediction, and feedback regulation, serving as the computational engine for multimodal omics integration and delivery structure optimization187.

In one representative study, a CNN was trained on ST images to identify immune-active regions, including Treg⁺TAM clusters and metabolically dysregulated zones. The network generated a “target spatial heatmap” that informed delivery design and enabled intelligent simulation of drug-loading strategies188. In mouse models, the atlas-driven prediction of nanocarrier distribution showed high concordance with actual fluorescence imaging distributions, verifying its spatial prediction accuracy189.

Advanced models such as DeepST, TransST, and SpaGCN have been widely applied for spatial data decoding and region label prediction, achieving automatic identification of regions of interest (ROI) and cross-modal target matching across various cancer types79,190. These models further enhance the spatial correspondence between omics atlases and drug target regions.

Another key application of AI in optimizing delivery pathways involves multi-task learning (MTL) and generative modeling. A comprehensive study integrated data from more than 200 nanoplatforms, together with omics profiles, immune scores, and pathway activity datasets, to train a multi-input neural network and establish Drug-SpatialNet, an AI framework that recommends optimal three-dimensional combinations of delivery platforms, signaling pathways, and target region coordinates114. This system has been used to design multi-axis responsive nanoplatforms with adaptive drug release rhythm adjustment capabilities.

AI plays a central role in advancing quantitative oncology. Through advanced algorithms, AI can extract extensive radiomic features, including texture, density, and volume, from medical imaging modalities such as CT and MRI. By integrating multi-omics data encompassing genomics and transcriptomics, AI enables quantitative analyses linking tumor molecular subtypes with gene mutation profiles, such as RAS mutations in colorectal cancer and EGFR expression in lung cancer191. These capabilities allow precise differentiation of tumor genotypes and molecular subtypes, establishing a strong foundation for molecular-level quantitative oncology research. AI also supports the development of predictive models for therapeutic responses, facilitating quantitative evaluation of tumor sensitivity to various regimens, including chemotherapy and immunotherapy. By comparing radiomic feature changes before and after treatment, AI can quantitatively predict clinical outcomes such as PFS and OS, thereby strengthening the assessment of treatment efficacy192. In radiation oncology, AI enhances the precision of target volume delineation, optimizes treatment planning parameters, and predicts risks of treatment-related adverse effects with quantitative accuracy193. Moreover, through natural language processing (NLP), AI integrates clinical data to refine critical quantitative parameters in clinical trial design, including sample size determination and endpoint thresholds, ultimately improving data reliability and supporting more precise and efficient cancer research194.

Feedback-driven AI systems further enable inverse analyses based on efficacy atlases to reconstruct the dynamic causal relationship between drug administration and therapeutic response195,196. For example, an algorithm integrating convolutional networks with immune activation time-series features can predict spatial drift and signaling variation in target regions during secondary administration, providing a computational foundation for dynamic pathway refinement and closed-loop immune delivery189,197,198.

In summary, AI in spatial atlas-driven targeted nanodrug delivery systems is evolving from “assistive analysis” to “pathway leadership plus feedback learning,” offering an actionable intelligent technology backbone for constructing individualized and multi-axis therapeutic regimens for highly heterogeneous solid tumors such as OC.

Construction of an individualized drug suitability model supported by multi-omics integration

Traditional nanodrug delivery systems mainly rely on “passive” delivery, where drug release follows a predetermined program once administered, limiting responsiveness to microenvironmental changes199,200. In contrast, intelligent nanodrug platforms incorporate feedback sensing, dose modulation, and environmental adaptation capabilities to achieve real-time immune state-responsive regulation201. This “intelligent intervention” is particularly critical in the complex TME of OC.

In immunosuppressive core regions, the system released high doses of anti-PD-L1 while simultaneously blocking TAM recruitment pathways203. In contrast, in marginal immune-active zones, it delivered only low doses of immune adjuvants to prevent excessive immune activation201. Animal experiments showed that this system precisely modulated T cell activation amplitude and significantly reduced inflammation-related toxicity204.

More advanced systems incorporate feedback-loop mechanisms, allowing drug release rates to respond dynamically to fluctuations in ROS or enabling multiple delivery cycles triggered by cytokine expression205. For example, a CRISPR-Switch-controlled nanosystem could self-activate or terminate TGF-β antagonist release according to local IFN-γ expression, forming a true “self-regulated release loop”206. Clinical translation pathways and perspectives on omics database development

Although spatial omics and biomimetic drug delivery have achieved considerable progress at the research stage, their clinical translation remains constrained by several major challenges, including sample heterogeneity, lack of standardized datasets, and limited interpretability of spatial atlases in clinical practice. Studies have shown that the heterogeneity and late diagnosis of OC significantly complicate treatment, underscoring the barriers that must be addressed before clinical implementation45. The following section analyzes key translational barriers, optimization pathways, and future development strategies to advance spatial delivery systems from “research prototypes” to “clinical deployment.”

Challenges of spatial omics atlas standardization and clinical adaptation strategies

Although spatial omics and single-cell technologies have been widely applied in basic research, their transformation into clinically applicable systems for target identification and individualized treatment design remains constrained by multiple technical and translational challenges. Studies have shown that ST and scRNA-seq technologies hold great potential for revealing tumor heterogeneity, but their application in clinical samples still needs further validation7,45.

On one hand, technologies such as scRNA-seq and ST enable high-resolution mapping of immune cell heterogeneity and tissue spatial architecture. Nevertheless, their reproducibility, data stability, and visualization consistency in clinical contexts remain limited. Different commercial platforms, including 10x Visium, NanoString CosMx, and Slide-seq, vary significantly in resolution, staining depth, and spatial mapping methodology, making cross-platform data integration difficult. For instance, although the 10x Genomics Visium platform provides relatively high spatial resolution, its reproducibility across laboratories still requires systematic evaluation208. Additionally, clinical sample preservation methods (e.g., FFPE vs fresh-frozen), tissue thickness, and annotation consistency also affect the accuracy and comparability of spatial coordinates209.

More importantly, the candidate therapeutic targets identified by multi-omics analyses must undergo rigorous validation through patient subgroup stratification, treatment response correlation, and suitability screening in clinical cohorts5. For example, in an integrated omics study on HGSOC, the combination of scRNA-seq, Visium, and CITE-seq analyses revealed synergistic activation of the STAT3–TGFβ–HIF1α signaling axis in immune “cold zones,” suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for low-response patient subgroups210. However, clinical implementation still faces challenges such as small sample sizes, high tissue heterogeneity, and short follow-up periods211. Studies have shown that the heterogeneity of HGSOC and immunosuppressive microenvironment significantly affects treatment response, emphasizing the need for methodological standardization before translation to clinical practice5,212.

To overcome these challenges, current optimization strategies focus on three key directions. First, the development of standardized expression matrices and atlas integration algorithms has improved cross-platform comparability. Methods such as the Tangram algorithm, based on spatial colocalization weighting, and spatial gene set scoring frameworks enhance data harmonization. The BayesSpace algorithm, for example, refines spatial resolution in ST datasets, improving the detection of transcriptional heterogeneity within tumor tissues209. Second, the establishment of visual clinical annotation templates facilitates more intuitive interpretation of omics results. Creating “spatial immune heatmap and targeting recommendation” systems allows clinicians to correlate atlas-derived molecular patterns with actionable therapeutic strategies. AI-guided ST analyses have demonstrated improved identification of prognosis-associated biomarkers and enhanced precision in clinical decision-making213. Third, large-scale “atlas-driven drug-matching” databases are under development to promote standardized, multi-center integration. Initiatives such as OvIMAP and SPATIAL-OMICS aggregate cross-institutional datasets to construct target recommendation engines and clinical decision support systems. For instance, the OVsignGenes model integrates multi-omics data to predict HGSOC molecular subtypes with high accuracy, supporting individualized treatment design214.

The long-term goal is to establish a fully integrated workflow encompassing personalized atlas construction, target inference, delivery pathway design, and efficacy prediction. Multi-omics integration provides the essential structural data foundation for seamless convergence between AI algorithms and biomimetic nanoplatforms. Recent studies further emphasize that the combined application of ST and single-cell analyses can decode HGSOC heterogeneity, providing new insights for precision therapeutic strategies45.

Cross-platform and multi-center strategies for constructing OC TME atlases

ST and single-cell omics have emerged as core technologies in precision oncology; however, their clinical translation continues to face major obstacles such as data fragmentation, high interpretation thresholds, and a lack of standardized workflows4,213. Current clinical diagnostics still rely heavily on traditional approaches, including tissue sectioning, immunohistochemistry, and PCR, which cannot meet the demands of spatially resolved, multi-dimensional expression profiling or the clinical interpretation of omics atlases211,213. Presenting spatial omics results in a clinically interpretable format that integrates seamlessly with pathology systems has become a fundamental prerequisite for practical application213,215,216.

A prominent challenge is the lack of data standardization214. Different platforms (such as 10× Visium, NanoString CosMx, and Stereo-seq) vary in resolution, expression quantification, section thickness, and gene selection, making direct cross-sample or cross-platform comparisons difficult. These inconsistencies hinder the reconstruction of unified or shared spatial atlases214. Current efforts include the development of consensus-based normalized expression matrices, unified coordinate systems, and AI-assisted image registration algorithms. Despite these advances, achieving clinical consensus and interoperability remains a long-term goal214.

Another critical issue is the absence of standardized clinical interpretation templates217. Clinicians often find it difficult to derive actionable insights or make treatment decisions from thousands of spatial expression data points. To address this, emerging platforms have begun developing automated annotation systems, including AI-generated “spatial immune heatmap and treatment recommendation” reports. Although promising, these systems still require rigorous validation for reliability and generalizability across tumor types and clinical settings217. Additionally, the absence of national-level databases for spatial omics storage and sharing, coupled with unresolved data privacy and interoperability issues, further limits clinical adoption. Therefore, advancing spatial omics toward clinical translation requires coordinated optimization across five dimensions: data standardization, atlas annotation, platform generalizability, visualization tools, and clinician decision-support systems.

Given the current challenges of inter-platform variability, lack of standardization, and pronounced regional heterogeneity in atlas construction, future progress will depend on integrating multi-center, high-throughput platforms and regional biobanks to establish a collaborative framework for OC spatial omics and nanotherapy mapping. A proposed OvaMAP Consortium could serve this role, promoting data sharing, AI model generalization, and the co-development of treatment pathways to enhance translational efficiency217.

In the long term, a standardized, atlas-based omics-delivery co-construction platform will form the central infrastructure of spatially targeted immunotherapy. Multi-center collaboration should be prioritized to establish a shared OC TME atlas consortium, build a regional immune hotspot database (OvaMAP), develop standardized annotation interfaces, and define AI-access interface protocols. Such initiatives will enable the creation of an integrated spatial omics–delivery targeting framework that is interoperable across platforms and accessible to intelligent computational systems.

Prospects for synergistic application with ICIs, CAR-T, and vaccine therapies

Building a high-quality, multi-center, cross-platform OC TME atlas database is a foundational infrastructure project for achieving future individualized nanodrug-based immunotherapy218,219. Such an atlas should cover multi-dimensional indicators, including immune subgroup distribution, signaling pathway activity, metabolic status, and spatial coordinates, to support various scenarios such as target screening, delivery strategy fitting, and efficacy evaluation22,127. Several international consortia, including HuBMAP, the Human Cell Atlas (HCA), and SPATIAL-OMICS, are already advancing this goal, while national collaborations such as the Tumor Spatial Multi-Omics Atlas Consortium, jointly led by Fudan University, UCAS, and CAS, are building similar frameworks in China220.

The key to multi-center integration lies in unified data standard protocols and computational frameworks221. Current mainstream approaches include cross-atlas clustering based on GNNs, cross-platform alignment techniques based on variational autoencoders (VAEs), and the construction of immune microenvironment spatial coordinate embedding models (IM-Space), enabling unified expression of immune structures across tissue samples from different sources222,223. A representative study integrated spatial omics data from 230 OC cases across six centers to construct the Ovarian Cancer Immune Signal Topography (OvIMAP), which stratified TMEs into nine structural subtypes, providing a framework for adaptive multi-nanocarrier design4.

The long-term objective is to establish a National Ovarian Cancer Spatial Immune Database embedded within clinical diagnostic and therapeutic systems222. Once a patient sample is collected, the system could automatically match the spatial immune subtype, recommend suitable nanocarrier platforms and drug formulations, and generate an atlas-guided individualized treatment plan. Such a database would also enable retrospective efficacy analyses, prospective clinical trial design, and real-world evidence (RWE) studies, forming a critical foundation for intelligent precision therapy (Fig. 9).

Roadmap of future development and an overview of key research directions.

Future development of spatial drug delivery systems is expected to achieve breakthroughs in three domains. First, the creation of preoperative individualized platforms combining spatial atlases and patient-derived organoids will allow preclinical validation of therapeutic efficacy. Second, AI-based prediction systems are anticipated to evolve into active back-propagation mechanisms capable of inferring optimal target combinations from treatment feedback. Third, the establishment of real-time closed-loop intelligent nanoplatforms will enable continuous efficacy monitoring and adaptive drug release regulation. Together, these innovations will transform spatial delivery systems from static structural designs into dynamically controlled therapeutic platforms, defining a new paradigm for precision oncology14,224.

Summary and future perspectives

Core value of spatial omics in deciphering oc immune heterogeneity

Over the past decade, significant progress has been achieved in tumor immunotherapy. However, OC, recognized as a prototypical “immune cold tumor,” continues to exhibit persistently low response rates to treatment22. The iTME in OC displays profound spatial heterogeneity, characterized by regional variations in immune cell composition, activation states, and inhibitory signaling intensity219. Therefore, traditional bulk omics methods fail to accurately identify immunosuppressive core microregions, severely limiting target identification and the design of precise drug delivery strategies.

The rise of ST and single-cell multi-omics has provided key tools to address the above challenges. scRNA-seq reveals the functional states, exhaustion signatures, and signaling network distributions of immune subpopulations, while scATAC-seq maps chromatin accessibility and TF activity to define regulatory origins of immune phenotypes22. Spatial omics technologies (such as Visium, Slide-seq, and CosMx) further integrate “molecular signals” with “tissue localization,” enabling the reconstruction of microenvironment heterogeneity maps218. In OC, recent spatial multi-omics studies have successfully localized the PD-L1, CXCL12, and HIF-1α signaling axes, precisely identifying immunosuppressive hotspots and providing accurate guidance for individualized therapeutic intervention22.

Spatial and single-cell omics are increasingly integrated with broader bioinformatics frameworks, including immunomics, metabolomics, and proteomics, establishing a multi-dimensional analytical system for TME characterization224. Through cell–pathway–spatial triple-layer analysis, a system-level atlas can be constructed to guide all stages of immunotherapy, from drug design and target selection to patient stratification and treatment monitoring222. The emerging omics-driven atlas–target–intervention framework is expected to replace current empirical treatment approaches, advancing OC immunotherapy toward a new stage characterized by spatial precision and dynamic regulatory control.

Functional advantages and application prospects of biomimetic nanoplatforms as the main platform for immune drug delivery

Conventional systemic immunotherapy faces major challenges in addressing complex iTME, including inaccurate drug delivery, unstable efficacy, and severe adverse effects218. Biomimetic nanodrug delivery systems have emerged as next-generation therapeutic platforms with superior targeting precision, biocompatibility, and controllable responsiveness22. In multiple studies on OC, nanocarriers engineered through cell membrane coating, exosome derivation, or hybrid assembly have enabled precise delivery of multi-pathway inhibitors such as PD-L1, TGF-β, CSF1R, and IDO, effectively remodeling the TME and enhancing antitumor immune activation225.

Compared with conventional carriers, biomimetic nanoplatforms demonstrate higher functional intelligence. Through receptor–ligand recognition on membrane surfaces, signaling axis matching, and responsiveness to local microenvironmental stimuli (e.g., hypoxia, acidity, enzyme activation), these systems achieve refined delivery modes including region-specific targeting, site-selective release, and dose-feedback regulation224. For example, in HIF-1α-enriched regions, HIF carriers can autonomously release anti-PD-L1 antibodies and lactate metabolism inhibitors to overcome metabolic and immune suppression. In Treg- or MDSC-dominant regions, chemokine-guided peptide-modified nanoplatforms precisely localize delivery, restoring local immune equilibrium14,22.

Once deeply integrated with omics data, biomimetic nanoplatforms evolve beyond the role of simple drug carriers to act as precise delivery executors. Spatial omics define target coordinates, AI algorithms identify adaptive delivery regions, and intelligent systems autonomously regulate transport pathways, forming a preliminary closed-loop framework. With continued advances in industrial standardization, Chemistry–Manufacturing–Control (CMC) system development, and clinical pathway integration, biomimetic nanoplatforms are expected to serve as the core infrastructure of precision OC therapy, leading to a transformative shift in clinical treatment paradigms.

From individualized delivery to an “AI + Omics”-driven intelligent closed-loop therapeutic paradigm

Future OC immunotherapy will move beyond the traditional uniform treatment approach toward a highly integrated system characterized by individualized, region-specific, and dynamically adaptive interventions powered by spatial omics, AI prediction, and biomimetic drug delivery. At the individualized level, each patient will possess a personalized spatial immune atlas that precisely identifies immunosuppressive cores, dominant inhibitory axes, and therapeutic sensitivities. At the regional level, spatial omics will define multiple functional intervention zones, such as PD-L1 expression areas, MDSC-enriched regions, and metabolically dysregulated sites, allowing different delivery systems to operate in parallel with distinct therapeutic strategies. At the dynamic level, AI learning mechanisms combined with feedback-responsive nanoplatforms will enable autonomous dose regulation, sequential drug release, and functional reprogramming, forming a continuous recognition–intervention–evaluation–re-recognition treatment cycle.

In the clinical setting, spatial omics atlases are expected to be embedded within diagnostic and treatment systems as central indicators for therapeutic planning. Physicians will no longer depend solely on imaging or staging standards but will design personalized treatment regimens based on immune atlas profiles and AI-generated recommendations. Biomimetic delivery platforms will provide modular capabilities for drug combination, regional adaptation, and individualized dosing, enabling integration with CAR-T therapy, ICIs, vaccines, and radiotherapy to construct a comprehensive, multidimensional, and precise tumor management framework.

In summary, OC immunotherapy stands at a crucial stage of transformation, evolving from systematized approaches to intelligent, multi-dimensional therapeutic strategies. The analytical depth of single-cell and spatial omics, together with the precise execution capability of biomimetic nanodrug systems, is expected to open a new era of precision immunotherapy. Such integration offers a promising solution. However, limitations such as the high cost of spatial omics, the need for rigorous standardization across clinical centers, and the complexity of large-scale nano-manufacturing must be addressed before widespread clinical adoption.
