A multi-decadal 1 km gridded database of continental-scale spring onset products
Authors: Emma Izquierdo-Verdiguier, Raúl Zurita-Milla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The timing of spring onset is a particularly sensitive climate change indicator that also allows the study of weather interannual variations and extremes. This indicator can be derived from a suite of phenological models called the Extended Spring Indices (SI-x). These models transform daily minimum and maximum temperatures into a set of consistent indices that track the timing of first leaf and first bloom for key indicator plant species. The SI-x also enables the calculation of the so-called frost damage index. Using new computational technologies and high spatial resolution gridded weather data, here we present and evaluate a multi-decadal 1 km spatial resolution version of the SI-x models, which cover North American (1980 to 2022) and European areas (1950 to 2020). Because of the generally good agreement with ground phenological observations, this continental-scale and high spatial resolution product can support both scientists and decision makers in their quest to hind- and forecast weather and climate change impacts.

Background & Summary

Almost half of the global population lives in regions highly vulnerable to climate change (https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_SYR_SPM.pdf). Therefore, developing climate change indicators is imperative to better manage human and natural systems. Phenological observations are one of the most sensitive indicators1 of climate change since they robustly link changes in local weather and climate to biological and ecological processes. In this respect, the Start of Spring indicator is particularly relevant because it illustrates the impact of our changing climate on the timing of the vegetation growth, which in turn is directly linked to the “reactivation” of many ecological processes. This phenological indicator is derived from a suite of thermal time models known as the extended Spring Indices (SI-x: http://www.globalchange.gov/explore/indicators).

The original Spring Indices (SI-o) models were formulated by Schwartz et al.2, who later removed the chilling requirements to be able to calculate them over wider geographical areas3. For more information about the SI-x models, see4.

Initial SI-x studies were done at plant and weather observation sites4. However, the SI-x products soon transitioned from point to continuous data layers. Due to the increasing availability of gridded weather datasets and the wider interest spring onset indicators, the SI-x products transitioned from one degree grids5, to 25 km6 and then to the four kilometers product currently used by the USA National Phenology Network7. Nevertheless, higher spatial resolutions are needed for regional and local studies, as well as to facilitate the synergic use of this spring onset indicator with additional datasets such as remote sensing derived global land cover8 or plant traits databases9, and in-situ sensors and observations. For that reason, in 2018, we developed and released a 1 km gridded database of the SI-x over the Contiguous United States, CONUS10.

New computational technologies and data products have allowed us to cover bigger study areas and more extended study periods. Hence, here we introduce and validate a new 1 km gridded continental-scale phenological database that covers both North American (southern Canada, the contiguous United States, and Mexico) and European (including Turkey and European Russia) territories11. Ground phenological observations corresponding to the three plant species used to create and calibrate the SI-x models were used to validate our database, which is available as an Image Collection in Google Earth Engine. To facilitate its exploration and re-use, we created the following (interactive visualization), and uploaded the code and products, as Cloud Optimized GeoTiffs, to this (trusted repository).

Methods

Extended Spring Indices (SI-x)

The SI-x models provide two primary products, namely the “First Leaf” (LF) and “First Bloom” (BL) indices as well as the derived “Damage Index” (DI). These products are available for three plant species: one lilac (Syringa vulgaris) and two honeysuckle plants (Lonicera tatarica (LT) “Arnold Red” and Lonicera korolkowii (LK) “Zabeli”). The two primary indices are calculated by a linear combination of predictors derived by accumulating short- and long-term variables. These predictors are based on Growing Degree Hours (GDH) calculated from daily minimum and maximum temperatures and the day length as described in Ault et al. in 20154. The regression coefficients were calculated by Schwartz et al.3 in 2013. Table 1 shows the linear combinations used to calculate both, the leaf and bloom indices per plant. These plant-specific values are then averaged to produce the LF and BL indices. The “Damage Index” (DI) is the difference between the day of LF and the day of Last Freeze, defined here as the last day of a given calendar year whose minimum temperature is equal to or lower than 28 °F ( ~ − 2.22 °C). The DI provides an indication of the risk of frost damage. Thus, negative and small DI values indicate a high probability of frost damage12. Computational upscaling the SI-x models

The SI-x models were computationally upscaled using cloud computing via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. This platform was chosen for its specialized capabilities in cloud-based geospatial processing, availability of relevant weather data, and open accessibility14. This upscaling was done using as a reference the verified and validated MATLAB code provided in4. Some restructuring of the code was required to harness the power of the cloud architecture provided by Google. The main distinction between the local and cloud implementations comes from their differences in processing geospatial data. The local (i.e., MATLAB code focuses on point data– typically weather station data) whereas the cloud implementation natively deals with gridded datasets. Because of this, minor (computational) differences arise between both model implementations. For instance, when the maximum daily temperature is below 0 °C, the local implementation skips the calculation of the model predictors, and the cloud implementation assigns zero values to the grid cells affected by those low temperatures. In addition, the cloud implementation uses the map function to replace all for loops, and the number of maximum days to be processed by the cloud implementation was increased from 240 to 300 to accommodate for colder regions in America and Europe.

Technical Validation

In this section first, the ground phenological observation (GDOs) databases used to validate the results are described. After that, two approaches to characterize the quality of our high spatial resolution phenological database are used. First, we verified the SI-x GEE model by comparing local and cloud results at GPOs sites. Then, we validated the cloud-based LF and BL indices by comparing them to actual leafing and blooming dates, as provided by the GPOs.

Ground phenological observations (GPOs)

Two ground phenological databases were used to validate our database. For the North American region, we use a dataset provided by the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS); for detailed information on the observation protocol, refer to Table 1 in15. This database, which includes 2288 GPOs sites spread around the CONUS over the period 1980–2014, contains the latitude and longitude where leafing and blooming events were observed, the name (species) of the observed plant, and the day of the year (DOY) and year when the observation took place. A total of 15, 833 GPOs were collected over the study period for the lilac and honeysuckle varieties modeled by the SI-x. A second lilac species is also available in this dataset. The number of leafing and blooming observations depends on the year and the observation site.

For Europe, we use the Pan European Phenology PEP725 database (http://www.pep725.eu/). This database includes 10, 930 GPO sites over the period 1950 − 2016 with a total of 167, 041 observations. Similarly to the USGS, this dataset contains the location (i.e., latitude and longitude), the name of the plant, and the DOY and year when the observation took place. Unlike the North American dataset, PEP725 only contains observations for a type of lilac Syringa vulgaris (SV).

Figures 3 and 4 show the observation sites over CONUS and Europe. In CONUS, the observation sites for leafing and blooming events are evenly spread (Fig. 3(a),(b)). On average, each site has five years with leafing and/or blooming observations. In Europe, sites are mostly concentrated around specific areas. Leafing sites are mostly found in Slovakia, Croatia, and Montenegro (Fig. 3(c)) with a high number of observations per location, while blooming observations are mainly concentrated in Germany and England (Fig. 4(b)) with a lower number of observations per site. Comparison between local and cloud implementations

Here, we analyze potential differences between the results produced by the legacy implementation of the SI-x models using Matlab16 toolbox4 and our cloud-based results. For this, results are split based on the type of phenological event and region. Potential differences in model precision are quantified through the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and the mean absolute error (MAE), in model bias through the mean error (ME), and in the goodness of fit through the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R).

Table 4 depicts the results using all the available GPOs (i.e., a total of 182, 874 observations for CONUS and Europe). Pearson’s correlation equals one for both phenological events and regions, and the RMSE is close to zero. Our analysis at the plant level reveals better results for the honeysuckle when looking at the LF index and more favorable results for lilac in the case of the BL index. If we look at the two lilac varieties, we see that Syringa chinensis “Red Rothomagensis” (SC) exhibits notably more favorable results than Syringa vulgaris (SV). This is expected because the former species was used to calibrate the SI-x models and also, it is a cloned plan whereas Syringa vulgaris is not2.

An extra comparison between our results and the ground observations was done by means of a violin plot (Fig. 5). The violin plot shows that the European BL index has the lowest bias, as represented by the close distance between its mean (asterisk) and the median value (line of the box plot). The violin plot also shows that outliers are present in both regions and indices with absolute differences exceeding 40 days. These outliers, which substantially contributed to the errors reported in Table 5, are linked to a few sites and observation years. As an example, the two North American and European GPO sites with the highest absolute errors and having a minimum of 20 years of observations were selected for further analysis. Our validation results confirm that the SI-x database presented in this paper provides a robust and realistic view of plant phenology and this, in turn, confirms its usefulness for weather and climate change impact studies.
