Clinical impact of guideline-directed medical therapy in patients with left ventricular assist device: an international multicentre study
Authors: Miloud Cherbi, Clément Delmas, Paul Gautier, Karim Benali, Kerstin Bode, Elena Efimova, Alexey Dashkevich, Jackson Liang, John Larson, Blandine Mondesert, Jacinthe Boulet, Pierre-Emmanuel Noly, François Picard, Jean Luc Pasquié, Jean-Baptiste Gourraud, Sandro Ninni, Laurence Jesel, Alexandre Sebestyen, Marylou Para, Jean-Claude Deharo, Frederic Anselme, Laure Champ-Rigot, Soundous M’Rabet, Bertrand Pierre, Romain Eschalier, Mathieu Echivard, Anne-Céline Martin, Nicolas Lellouche, Matteo Pozzi, Pierre Groussin, Erwan Flecher, Vincent Galand, Raphael Martins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) is recommended for left ventricular assist device (LVAD) recipients, real-world evidence supporting its clinical impact remains limited. This study evaluated the association between GDMT prescription and clinical outcomes in LVAD patients.MethodsThis international retrospective multicentre study included 875 LVAD patients from 22 centres. Patients were categorized based on the number of GDMT (ACE-I/ARBs, beta-blockers, MRAs) prescribed. Primary outcome was 6-month all-cause mortality. Secondary outcome was late ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) (>30 days post-implant). Multivariable Cox regression and ordinal logistic regression analyses were performed.ResultsOverall, only 261 patients (29.8%) received triple GDMT, while 97 (11.1%) received no GDMT. After multivariable adjustment, the number of prescribed GDMTs was independently associated with improved survival, with aHRs for all-cause mortality of 0.51 (0.33–0.75, P < .01) for triple therapy, 0.39 (0.26–0.59, P < .01) for dual therapy, and 0.45 (0.30–0.67, P < .01) for single therapy, all compared with no GDMT. Similarly, ACE-I/ARB were associated with a lower risk of late VAs (aHR 0.65 [0.50–0.84], P < .01). Female sex, diabetes, early VAs, and higher bilirubin levels were associated with lower GDMT prescription rates. Major LVEDD improvement (≥10 mm reduction) increased progressively from 51.5% without GDMT to 66.1% with triple therapy.ConclusionIn this large international study, the use of GDMT in LVAD patients was associated with improved survival, with benefits observed even with single-agent therapy. Despite these benefits, only 30% of patients received optimal triple therapy, highlighting the need for improved implementation strategies and standardized protocols in this population.Keywords: Left ventricular assist device, Guideline-directed medical therapy, Ventricular arrhythmia, Heartmate, Heartware

Introduction

As advanced heart failure (HF) remains a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality, left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) have become a cornerstone in the treatment of end-stage HF, offering significant improvements in both survival and functional status. These devices are increasingly utilized within two primary therapeutic strategies: bridge to transplant (BTT) and destination therapy (DT), each supported by robust clinical evidence.1 Given the ongoing disparity between organ availability and transplant demand, LVAD implantation rates are expected to continue rising in the coming years.2 Thanks to successive technological advancements, patients supported with the latest-generation device—HeartMate 3 (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, US)—now achieve a median survival approaching six years,3 with 5-year survival exceeding 70% in patients younger than 50 years.4 This growing reliance on LVAD therapy underscores the critical need to better characterize medical management in this population, both to enhance patient outcomes and to identify areas where further clinical evidence is needed.

While the benefits of GDMT are well established in HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) through multiple randomized trials, comparable trial evidence is lacking for LVAD recipients. Current practice is largely informed by consensus statements, such as the 2023 HFSA guidelines, which recommend extrapolating HFrEF therapies to this population.1,5 GDMT typically includes angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE-Is), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs). Mechanistically, LVAD patients may still benefit from these therapies despite mechanical unloading, as persistent neurohormonal activation, increased vascular resistance, and ongoing myocardial fibrosis contribute to adverse remodelling and clinical events. Expert opinion supports the use of GDMT in LVAD recipients to reduce afterload, mitigate neurohormonal activation, and facilitate reverse remodelling, while also partially lowering the risk of ventricular arrhythmia (VA).6–9 However, these recommendations are based on low levels of evidence, and real-world data on the clinical impact of GDMT in patients supported by LVADs remain scarce.

Hence, this study aims to evaluate clinical outcomes in LVAD recipients based on the number of GDMT prescribed, using data from the VT-LVAD Consortium, a large international multicentre cohort.

Methods

The VT-LVAD Consortium is an international, retrospective, multicentre observational study of durable mechanical circulatory support devices, conducted across 22 tertiary centres in France, Germany, United States, and Canada.10,11

Briefly, patients implanted with LVAD between 2006 and 2019 were included in the registry. The type of pump implanted depended on the local heart team’s decision at each centre, as well as the availability of different devices depending on the year of implantation. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who died or underwent heart transplantation (HTx) during the index hospitalization, those who received a total artificial heart or a pulsatile-flow LVAD, and patients with a history of HTx.

Baseline assessment and follow-up

Baseline data were obtained from hospital records for all enrolled patients and included demographic information, underlying cardiac and HF conditions, prior implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), history of supraventricular or ventricular arrhythmias, laboratory values, and echocardiographic parameters, especially left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD). HF treatment was also reported, including beta-blockers, ACE-Is/ARBs, and MRAs. GDMT exposure was defined based on medications initiated during the index hospitalization and confirmed at early post-discharge follow-up visits. Patients were categorized according to the number of GDMT classes (beta-blockers, ACE-I/ARB, MRA) they were receiving at the time of hospital discharge. To minimize immortal time bias, follow-up for outcome assessment began at hospital discharge, and patients who died or underwent heart transplantation during the index hospitalization were excluded from the analysis. Follow-up was conducted in accordance with the protocols of each participating centre. Patients who underwent HTx during follow-up were censored at the time of transplant to minimize competing risk bias.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 6-month follow-up. Secondary outcomes included the occurrence of late VAs, defined as any VA occurring more than 30 days after LVAD implantation, and LVEDD improvement defined by the change in LVEDD between baseline and 6 months follow-up. VA was defined as sustained (>30 s) ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation without an acute reversible cause, managed either medically, by external electrical cardioversion, or by appropriate ICD therapy (antitachycardia pacing or shock).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages. Quantitative variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or the Fisher exact test when appropriate. To analyse trends across groups, we employed the Cochran-Armitage test for qualitative variables and the Jonckheere–Terpstra test for quantitative variables, both described in the text and tables as ‘Ptrend’. Additionally, a multivariable Cox regression model was used to assess the association between all outcomes of interest and the number of GDMTs, adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, cardiovascular risk factors (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking), HF aetiology, history of atrial fibrillation or VA before LVAD implantation, early VA post-implantation (defined as any VA occurring within the first 30 days after implantation), pre-implant LVEF and LVEDD, baseline creatinine and bilirubin levels, LVAD type, LVAD strategy (BTT, DT, or other), GDMTs before LVAD, post-implant use of amiodarone, presence of ICD or CRT prior to implantation, INTERMACS classification, year and centre of implantation. These variables were selected based on clinical and pathophysiological relevance, in accordance with the Prognosis Research Strategy (PROGRESS) guidelines.12 Additionally, individual analyses were performed for each GDMT medication class. To further characterize GDMT implementation patterns, we performed a supplementary analysis examining treatment trajectories by comparing continuation, de novo initiation, and discontinuation for each drug class. Kaplan–Meier time-to-event curves for the occurrence of each outcome of interest were used to assess the influence of the number of GDMTs. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test, and adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) were computed through Cox regression with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A sensitivity analysis restricting the cohort to patients implanted as BTT or destination therapy DT was also performed. To assess the robustness of our findings and explore potential effect modification, we performed predefined exploratory subgroup analyses according to age (≥60 vs <60 years), sex, HF aetiology, baseline LVEF (≤15% vs >15%), and LVAD strategy (BTT vs DT), based on gaps identified in the existing literature and relevant pathophysiological considerations.1,5,10 To identify factors independently associated with the number of GDMTs prescribed after LVAD implantation, we performed an ordinal logistic regression using a proportional odds model. The analysis included descriptive comparisons across GDMT categories and estimation of adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs. For the purposes of multivariable analyses, missing values were addressed using multiple imputations with chained equations, performed with the R package mice, generating 10 imputed datasets. Eventually, an exploratory assessment of LVEDD reduction between baseline and 3 months follow-up was performed, categorizing patients into four groups: deterioration (≥+5 mm), stable (±5 mm), moderate improvement (−5–−9 mm), and major improvement (≤−10 mm).

All tests were two-tailed. The value of P ≤ 0.05 was accepted as statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software [version 4.3.2 (2023-10-31)].

Ethics

This retrospective international study was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee of each participating centre and conformed to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent requirements were waived by the ethics committees or obtained according to local institutional guidelines.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Figure 1, the analysis included 875 LVAD patients (after exclusion of 141 patients due to death or HTx during the index hospitalization and 293 due to incomplete medication lists). Overall, 97 patients (11.1%) received no GDMT, 194 (22.2%) were treated with one agent, 323 (36.9%) with two agents, and 261 (29.8%) with all three GDMT classes (Figure 1, Supplementary Figure S1). Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the cohort. The median age was 59.2 years (51.1–65.7), with a predominance of males (87.0%). Nearly half of the patients (48.1%) received LVAD as a BTT, while 36.7% were implanted in a DT strategy. Patients receiving triple GDMTs exhibited a greater prevalence of hypertension (69.3% vs. 40.2% in the no GDMT group, P < .01) and dyslipidemia (62.9% vs. 50.0%, P = .02), with statistically significant trends observed across GDMT categories (Ptrend < 0.01). Additionally, a history of VA prior to implantation was more frequent in this group (41.8% vs. 26.8%, P = .04), alongside a borderline increase in atrial fibrillation prevalence (Ptrend = 0.049) and a notable decrease in early VA occurrence post-implantation (Ptrend = 0.02). Bilirubin levels before LVAD placement were lower in patients with higher GDMT exposure (Ptrend < 0.01). Patients receiving a higher number of GDMT were more likely to have prior ICD implantation (62.9% to 70.9%, Ptrend = 0.04), whereas CRT use was less frequent in these groups (35.2% to 46.5%, Ptrend = 0.01).

Mortality and late VAs

Overall, 49 patients (5.6%) died during the 6-month follow-up period. Six-month mortality rates differed markedly across GDMT groups: 19.6% (19/97) with no GDMT, 3.6% (7/194) with single therapy, 3.4% (11/323) with dual therapy, and 4.6% (12/261) with triple therapy, corresponding to absolute risk reductions (ARDs) of 16.0%, 16.2%, and 15.0%, respectively, compared with no GDMT. After adjustment for potential confounders, the number of prescribed GDMTs was independently associated with improved survival, with no evidence of violation of the proportional hazards assumption (P = .20). Compared with patients not receiving GDMT, aHRs for all-cause mortality were 0.51 (0.33–0.75, P < .01) for triple therapy, 0.39 (0.26–0.59, P < .01) for dual therapy, and 0.45 (0.30–0.67, P < .01) for single therapy (Figure 2A). Individually, patients receiving beta-blockers had a 6-month mortality rate of 3.7% versus 11.0% without beta-blockers (ARD −7.3%), and patients receiving MRAs had a mortality rate of 3.9% versus 7.7% without MRAs (ARD −3.8%). Correspondingly, beta-blockers (aHR 0.33 [0.18–0.61], P < .01) and MRAs (aHR 0.48 [0.26–0.89], P = .02) were associated with improved overall survival, whereas the association for ACE-I/ARB (mortality rate 5.3% vs 6.0% without ACE-I/ARB, ARD −0.7%; aHR 0.98 [0.55–1.75], P = .94) did not reach statistical significance (Figs 2B–D). Conversely, ACE-I/ARB were the only GDMT component associated with a reduced risk of late VA (18.6% without ACE-I/ARB versus 11.2% with ACE-I/ARB [ARD −7.4%], aHR 0.65 [0.50–0.84], P < .01) (Figure 3). Lastly, exploratory subgroup analysis revealed a consistent protective effect of GDMTs across all subgroups tested, except for women and patients above 60 years (Figure 4). The sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with BTT or DT indications also yielded consistent results with the main analysis (Supplementary Figure S2), confirming the robustness of these associations.

LVEDD improvement

A significant positive relationship was observed between GDMT number and LVEDD reverse remodelling, with patients receiving optimal medical therapy demonstrating markedly superior left ventricular dimensional recovery compared to those without GDMT (P < .01) (Figure 5). Patients receiving triple GDMT exhibited the highest proportion of major LVEDD improvement (≥10 mm reduction) at 66.1%, which progressively increased across GDMT groups from 51.5% in patients receiving no medical therapy. Similarly, any significant improvement (≥5 mm reduction) was higher in patients receiving GDMT compared to those without therapy, ranging from 71.9% to 85.7% across treated groups versus 69.7% in untreated patients, with the highest rate observed in the triple GDMT group (85.7%).

GDMT treatment trajectories

Beta-blockers showed the highest continuation rate (47.8%), while MRAs were most frequently initiated de novo (30.7%). Discontinuation rates ranged from 21.7% for beta-blockers to 30.7% for MRAs (Supplementary Figure S3). In multivariable Cox regression analyses, both de novo initiation and continuation of beta-blockers and MRAs were associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality compared with no treatment (beta-blockers: de novo initiation aHR 0.49 [0.27–0.91], P = .02; continuation aHR 0.53 [0.28–0.98], P = .04; MRAs: de novo initiation aHR 0.42 ([0.17–0.98], P = .04; continuation aHR 0.43 ([0.18–0.99], P = .04) (Supplementary Figure S4). In contrast, neither de novo initiation nor continuation of ACE-I/ARB therapy reached statistical significance.

Factors associated with GDMT prescription

After multivariable ordinal logistic regression, several factors were independently associated with GDMT prescription patterns (Table 2). Female sex (aOR 0.65 [0.44–0.96], P = .03), diabetes mellitus (aOR 0.70 [0.52–0.94], P = .02), early VAs (aOR 0.60 [0.44–0.83], P < .01), and higher total bilirubin levels (aOR 0.91 per 10-point increase [0.85–0.98], P = .01) were associated with lower GDMT prescription rates. Conversely, hypertension (aOR 1.74 [1.27–2.39], P < .01), history of VA (aOR 1.37 [1.02–1.83], P = .03), and specific LVAD devices (HeartWare aOR 3.03 [2.21–4.17], P < .01; HeartMate 3 aOR 1.89 [1.26–2.83], P < .01) were associated with higher prescription rates compared to HeartMate II.

Discussion

We present here a large international multicentre study evaluating the clinical impact of standard GDMT for chronic HF in LVAD recipients. Our main findings are as follows: (1) only 30% of LVAD recipients were treated on optimal triple GDMT; (2) GDMT prescription was strongly associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality, with a clear benefit observed even with a single agent; (3) ACE-I/ARB therapy was associated with a lower risk of late VAs; (4) GDMT was associated with enhanced LVEDD reverse remodelling during follow-up.

With the growing burden of advanced HF, LVADs have emerged as a cornerstone of long-term therapy, offering substantial benefits in terms of both survival and functional outcomes.1 In parallel, the persistent shortage of organ donors has led to an increasing number of LVAD implantations, compelling the medical community to confront new challenges.13,14 One of the most pressing among these is the optimization of medical therapy in LVAD recipients, a population for whom no randomized data currently exist to provide high-level evidence, despite the rising burden of advanced HF. Our study, based on a large international cohort, confirms the relevance of GDMT commonly used in chronic HF for patients with LVADs. Specifically, the prescription of GDMT was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality and improved reverse remodelling, with benefits observed as early as the initiation of a single GDMT agent.

Despite mechanical unloading by the LVAD, multiple deleterious pathophysiologic processes persist and contribute to adverse outcomes. The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system remain activated, leading to increased systemic vascular resistance, impaired right ventricular function, and persistent neurohormonal stress.5,15 Moreover, myocardial fibrosis continues to progress even after LVAD implantation, with elevated circulating pro-fibrotic markers contributing to the progressive deterioration of residual native heart function.16 These ongoing maladaptive processes—including sustained neurohormonal activation, pro-inflammatory signalling, extracellular matrix remodelling, and electrophysiologic instability—perpetuate the risk of clinical events despite the haemodynamic support provided by mechanical circulatory support. GDMT targets these persistent pathophysiologic processes through complementary and class-specific mechanisms. Clinical and histopathological evidence demonstrates that adjuvant pharmacologic therapy produces favourable effects on the structure and function of the unloaded myocardium that extend beyond LVAD-induced unloading alone.17 However, the divergent associations observed across individual GDMT classes—ACE-I/ARB with arrhythmic endpoints but not mortality, beta-blockers and MRAs with mortality but not arrhythmias—likely reflect both the distinct pathophysiologic targets of each medication class and the temporal dynamics of outcome manifestation. ACE-I/ARB therapy primarily targets neurohormonal activation and myocardial remodelling through renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibition, reducing systemic vascular resistance, mitigating fluid retention, and potentially lowering bleeding risk.18 Critically, these effects directly influence arrhythmic substrate formation through modulation of myocardial fibrosis, ion channel expression, and electrophysiologic stability,19 which may explain the observed association with reduced late VAs. Beta-blockers provide complementary benefits through sympathetic nervous system modulation, reducing sympathetic overdrive, decreasing heart rate, and allowing improved diastolic filling and ventricular recovery,20 mechanisms that more directly impact haemodynamic stability and survival. MRAs further attenuate aldosterone-mediated myocardial fibrosis and inflammation,21 with effects on congestion, right ventricular function, and end-organ protection that similarly translate into survival benefits. These findings underscore that GDMT medication classes should not be assumed to exert equivalent ‘class effects’ in LVAD recipients, and that individualized therapy selection based on specific clinical targets and temporal considerations may be warranted pending further mechanistic investigation.

In this perspective, our findings are consistent with data from the INTERMACS registry,22 where patients receiving triple therapy with an ACE inhibitor or ARB, a beta-blocker, and an MRA exhibited the lowest mortality rates, as well as reduced levels of NT-proBNP and serum creatinine. While intensive GDMT up-titration following acute heart failure hospitalization has demonstrated survival benefits in non-LVAD populations,23 the applicability of this approach to LVAD recipients remains uncertain. In the immediate postoperative period, clinical priorities focus on haemodynamic optimization and avoidance of right ventricular failure,5,24 and premature GDMT initiation may risk hypotension or acute kidney injury.25 Conversely, data from the RESTAGE-HF study26 suggest that combining mechanical unloading with systematic GDMT up-titration can promote reverse remodelling and improve outcomes in haemodynamically stable patients. The ENVAD-HF trial27 further demonstrated the safety of angiotensin-neprilysin inhibition in stable LVAD recipients. Future randomized trials incorporating systematic haemodynamic monitoring are needed to define optimal GDMT implementation strategies, including timing, sequence, and dose titration protocols specific to the LVAD population, as the absence of dosing information and time-varying treatment patterns in our dataset precluded assessment of dose-response relationships.

Moreover, our study highlights that despite being managed in tertiary expert centres, only 30% of LVAD patients were treated with triple GDMT, and as many as one in ten received no HF therapy at all. Several factors may explain this observation. Most importantly, haemodynamic tolerability likely represents the primary determinant of GDMT prescription in LVAD recipients.15 While LVADs typically provide sufficient haemodynamic support to allow the reintroduction of neurohormonal therapies, achieving a stable balance between blood pressure, filling pressures, and cardiac output may remain precarious, particularly in patients with persistent hypotension, right ventricular dysfunction, or compromised end-organ reserve. In such cases, physicians may be appropriately reluctant to introduce or up-titrate GDMT, fearing destabilization of an already fragile haemodynamic state.14 Unfortunately, the absence of systematic post-implant blood pressure measurements, right ventricular function assessment, and device-related haemodynamic parameters in our retrospective dataset prevents us from directly evaluating the contribution of haemodynamic constraints to GDMT underutilization.

Beyond haemodynamic considerations, other factors may also contribute to suboptimal GDMT uptake. These include therapeutic inertia, with numerous studies having identified critical delays in the initiation of GDMT.28,29 Failure to start GDMT at the time of hospital discharge is associated with a substantial risk that these therapies will either never be introduced or will be delayed significantly, both of which are linked to worse outcomes.30 In contrast, improved survival has been shown to correlate directly with the number of GDMTs initiated.31 Our analysis also revealed that GDMT prescription was less frequent in women—a gap previously identified in non-LVAD HF populations32—and in patients with diabetes mellitus,33 potentially related to concerns about side effects such as hyperkalaemia, acute kidney injury, or orthostatic hypotension related to dysautonomia. While guideline recommendations for systolic HF are identical across these subgroups, and these patients often have multiple overlapping indications for renin–angiotensin–aldosterone inhibition, the clinical significance of these prescribing disparities in the context of haemodynamic constraints remains unclear. Overall, in this retrospective setting, GDMT prescription may partly reflect patient stability and haemodynamic tolerability, and prospective studies incorporating systematic haemodynamic monitoring, comprehensive assessment of right ventricular function, and rigorous documentation of reasons for GDMT withholding are needed to better disentangle modifiable practice gaps from appropriate clinical judgment.

Lastly, our exploratory subgroup analyses suggested a potential attenuation of GDMT benefit in women and patients ≥60 years, though these findings require cautious interpretation given limited sample sizes and wide 95% CIs. Potential explanations could include sex-specific pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences in response to neurohormonal blockade, distinct patterns of myocardial remodelling, differential effects of body size on LVAD haemodynamics and medication tolerability in women, as well as age-related factors such as increased susceptibility to adverse effects (hypotension, hyperkalaemia, renal dysfunction), greater comorbidity burden, polypharmacy concerns, and potentially different HF phenotypes with reduced responsiveness to GDMT in older patients. However, prior studies in non-LVAD HFrEF populations have generally demonstrated consistent GDMT benefits across demographic subgroups, including women and elderly patients.11 Therefore, our findings may reflect chance variation or unmeasured confounding rather than true effect modification. These observations warrant confirmation in larger prospective cohorts with adequate statistical power before any clinical implications can be drawn.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, echocardiographic assessment was limited to LVEDD measurement due to the retrospective nature of the registry and heterogeneity in data collection across centres. LVEDD reduction may reflect mechanical unloading or pump-related effects rather than intrinsic myocardial recovery. We did not have access to additional echocardiographic parameters (right ventricular function, septal position, aortic valve opening patterns) or biomarkers that could have provided more comprehensive insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed associations with GDMT and their structural benefits. Second, our analysis did not systematically capture right ventricular dysfunction or heart failure hospitalizations, which represent important clinical outcomes in LVAD recipients and could have further characterized the impact of GDMT on overall cardiovascular morbidity. Additionally, novel drug classes such as ARNi and SGLT2 inhibitors were not captured in our cohort; although they have emerged as cornerstones of contemporary HF management and growing evidence suggests potential benefits in LVAD patients, their use was still limited during the study period and warrants further investigation. Third, our cohort spans 2006–2019, predating recent advances in LVAD technology and perioperative management strategies. Additionally, the multicentre design spanning 22 institutions introduced heterogeneity in clinical practices, data collection protocols, and measurement standardization. However, device type, year and centre of LVAD implantation were included as covariates in multivariable analysis, though further prospective studies reflecting contemporary clinical practice are needed to validate these findings. Fourth, despite performing comprehensive multivariable adjustment, we cannot fully exclude confounding by indication. Patients not receiving GDMT may have been haemodynamically more fragile (hypotension, right ventricular dysfunction, end-organ dysfunction), making GDMT prescription potentially a marker of clinical stability rather than an independent therapeutic intervention. Although the magnitude of the observed associations is consistent with prior registry data, residual confounding cannot be excluded and these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence within the inherent constraints of the observational design. Finally, we did not have access to medication dosing information or systematic documentation of subsequent treatment modifications, precluding assessment of titration adequacy, achievement of guideline-recommended target doses, and potential exposure misclassification over time, although the exclusive follow-up in tertiary expert centres and the short 6-month follow-up period limit the likelihood of significant undetected treatment changes. Longitudinal key laboratory parameters (potassium, creatinine) were not systematically collected, which limited our ability to identify adverse events such as hyperkalaemia or acute kidney injury, and to evaluate whether safety concerns influenced prescribing patterns.

Conclusion

In this international multicentre study, GDMT was associated with better outcomes in LVAD recipients, including both all-cause mortality and the occurrence of late VAs while improving reverse remodelling. Despite these benefits, only 30% of patients were treated on optimal triple therapy. Future studies are warranted to identify barriers to GDMT implementation and assess strategies to improve its use in this high-risk population.
