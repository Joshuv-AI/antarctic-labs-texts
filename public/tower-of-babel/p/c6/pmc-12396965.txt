On the role of interface strategy in multi-scale hybrid additive manufacturing
Authors: Alan Burl, Zaky Hussein, Venkata Surya Karthik Adapa, Melissa Foley, Nicole Van Handel, Matthew McCoy, Thomas Kurfess, Christopher Saldaña, Kyle Saleeby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs multiple macro scale directed energy deposition (DED) processes begin to be industrially adopted for large scale component manufacture, it is imperative that interface strategies between the processes are fully understood. The present work investigates the asynchronous deposition of a wire component (DED-arc), followed by a powder-based deposition (DED-LP) with varying surface treatments which were evaluated for flatness, porosity, hardness, and Charpy impact energy. The self-regulation effect of DED-LP was fully realized with up to 55% reduction in surface variation relative to the DED-arc surface. Contrarily, as surface contaminants were not removed between each process, the resultant DED-LP porosity was significantly reduced from 99.5% to 92.4%. Albeit the reduction in density did not negatively impact the impact toughness as evidenced by a low correlation coefficient of −0.46. As such, the overall manufacturing costs and application space must be considered for selection of the different interface strategies presented in the current work.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Metals and alloys

Introduction

Directed energy deposition (DED), a metal additive manufacturing (AM) process, has quickly risen in popularity to manufacture geometries that are otherwise challenging to fabricate through conventional methods1. DED commonly forms geometries in a layer-wise approach by utilizing a feedstock, either in wire or powder form, that is continually fed into a melt pool generated by an electric arc (DED-arc) or laser (DED-L) energy source2. The selection of a specific energy source and feedstock combination results in unique strengths and challenges that must be carefully considered3. Critical factors influencing this selection include the form in which raw material is available and its compatibility under a specific energy source, material deposition rates, and required feature resolution.

For example, the material deposition rate is a key factor towards industrial adoption of DED-arc, commonly achieving rates as high as 240 mm3s−14,5. In contrast, most commercially available powder-based processes (DED-LP) typically reach deposition rates of only 20 mm3s−1. Albeit, the resultant surface form of DED-arc depositions is largely heterogeneous with deviation of over 1 mm being commonplace6. As such, geometries produced through DED-arc often require time-consuming post-processing to achieve intricate details, whereas the lower scale deposition of DED-LP can readily produce components with feature sizes of 0.6 mm or smaller7. Additionally, wire-based systems are constrained to a set of commercially available feedstocks, such as mild steels8, high strength low alloys9, stainless steels10, titanium alloys11, and nickel based super alloys12. A limitation due, in part to the complex thermomechanical properties of advanced alloys that pose significant challenges to the transformation of raw materials into usable feedstock13. Contrarily, DED-LP is renowned for advanced materials development, with more feasible customized powder manufacturing methods, including high entropy alloys14 and functionally graded materials15,16. Further, a plethora of material systems are readily available and have significant support in the literature for process parameter selection for DED-LP, making it an ideal candidate for localized and intricate depositions. A distinguishing feature of DED-LP is the unique self-regulation effect (SRE), which enables the autonomous regulation of layer height by dynamically adjusting capture efficiency (the ratio of material captured in the melt pool to the material delivered). This remarkable phenomenon is an inherent property of DED-LP that is realized through the dynamically changing melt pool to nozzle distance, and consequently, the laser-powder convergence point, thus driving the process towards passive stability during depositions17,18. In contrast, the SRE is absent in DED-arc, as the electric arc established between the substrate and wire feedstock is largely impervious to subtle changes in contact tip to workpiece distance. However, variation in this distance can have a marked impact on overall deposition quality19.

To this extent, large-scale components often require precise material properties only at the regions of interest, such as boundaries or in high-stress critical regions, while the interior material primarily serves for heat dissipation and structural support. This highlights the potential advantage of utilizing DED-arc for bulk deposition and DED-LP for localized material control in applications requiring spatially tailored properties. However, a critical knowledge gap remains in understanding how the interface strategy between these two macro-scale processes influences the final part quality. Recent studies on combining laser-based powder bed fusion (PBF) and DED processes have revealed that interfacial defects occurred due to surface roughness variations (highly dependent on the relative deposition directions) and oxidation of meltpool during deposition20. These defects affect the bonding characteristics and, consequently, part properties, with disparities in cooling rates and microstructural evolution leading to variations in hardness and crack propagation behavior. Additional studies have investigated the use of varying wire-based processes with a secondary deposition of DED-LP21–23. Albeit, these works have primarily leveraged a machined interface thus mitigating much of the variability seen in DED-arc or, if the DED-LP surface is deposited on the as-printed wire surface, there is limited discussion of how surface quality (i.e. topology or surface contaminants) correlates with the final interface. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing deposition strategies to mitigate defects and ensure robust mechanical performance in hybrid AM components.

In the present work, two commonly used materials in DED processes were utilized, specifically H13 (X40CrMoV5-1) and ER70S-6 were asynchronously deposited by DED-LP and DED-arc, respectively, as displayed in Fig. 1. H13 is widely used as a hot work tool steel due to its exceptional hardness, toughness, and resistance to thermal fatigue, making it ideal for high-temperature tooling and die applications24. However, H13, with its high carbon equivalent, is prone to cold cracking during the arc deposition process25. This is primarily due to the formation of micro-segregation-induced brittleness and hard martensitic structures, which increase internal stresses and lead to crack formation. To mitigate this issue, strategies have been developed by introducing Al-Si-Ti composite oxides to promote ferrite formation, thereby improving toughness and thus leading to compositional modification. Instead, numerous studies26–28 have widely investigated and characterized the DED-LP process and have demonstrated the good processability of H13, including both repair applications and the fabrication of novel geometries with ideal compositions, making H13 a suitable choice for DED-LP in this study. In contrast, ER70S-6, a low-carbon steel filler material, is cost-effective, readily available, and exhibits good weldability. As such, it is commonly used in DED-arc29. However, due to the addition of deoxidizers to welding wire feedstock, the deposition often leads to significant silicate formation on the final surface, which can impact interfacial bonding and post-processing requirements30,31. Understanding the interactions and interfacial characteristics of these materials for combined macro scale processes is essential for optimizing deposition strategies and mechanical performance.

Visualization of asynchronous DED-arc and DED-LP processes.

A key feature of the present work is the applicability to the manufacture or repair of large-scale components by utilizing DED-LP for localized control and DED-arc for rapid bulk material deposition. This approach not only enhances process efficiency but also optimizes manufacturing time for large components, making it a more viable solution for industrial adoption. To this extent, the present work investigated a myriad of novel interface strategies including mapping the DED-LP standoff plane to the DED-arc surface topology and maintaining a constant standoff distance through localized machining of the interface. Additionally, the first-layer direction of the DED-LP relative to the DED-arc surface was investigated.

Results

To reduce the number of process planning decisions such that only parameters impacting the interface quality between the DED-arc and DED-LP depositions were investigated, nominal processing conditions from previous experiments were implemented. This reduction in parameter space gave way to the key parameters in the present work; namely, first-layer orientation of the DED-LP deposition relative to the DED-arc surface, selection of the DED-LP standoff plane, and preparation of the DED-arc surface. The selected interface conditions are displayed in Fig. 2. As a serpentine toolpath was utilized for both the DED-arc and DED-LP processes, the first layer of the DED-LP deposition could be oriented either perpendicular to the print direction of the DED-arc deposition (against lay) or parallel to the direction of printing (with lay). This orientation was identified as a critical parameter since deposition over the heterogeneous DED-arc surface could lead to localized variation in melt pool morphology. For selection of the laser standoff plane (focal plane), three cases were considered based on the geometry of DED-arc printed surface: topology mapped, least square fit (plane of best fit), and maximum plane. The mapped case represents a varying DED-LP laser plane by maintaining a constant standoff distance (10 mm) from the surface of the mild steel deposition. The least squares fit case represents a constant DED-LP standoff distance from the least squares derived plane of best fit. The reference plane was derived from the topological data of the DED-arc surface by forming the best fit solution to z = Ax + By + C by minimizing equation (1) where A, B, and C are the parameters of the plane that minimize ∇ E (total error between the sampled points (xi, yi, zi) and the fit plane).

The maximum plane case represents selecting the tallest point of DED-arc printed surface as the reference point with the remaining degrees of freedom limited by a machined locating corner. For the least-squares fit case and the maximum plane, this interface condition leads to a varying DED-LP standoff distance relative to the DED-arc surface. The minimum reference plane was not considered to avoid potential crashes with the DED-LP deposition head. The top row of Fig. 2 illustrates the deposition orientation of the first DED-LP layer, while the bottom row illustrates the reference plane and standoff plane. Each standoff plane was tested with each lay direction resulting in six depositions with distinct interface conditions; additionally, a machined DED-arc surface was tested as a control interface condition. This resulted in a total of seven samples with the standoff and print direction for each sample enumerated in Table 1.

The samples were evaluated for geometric uniformity both before and after the deposition of the DED-LP surface. The initial scan data was used in conjunction with the machined locating corner for localization in the computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) software and within the machine tool. A key component of the present work is to investigate the direct transition from DED-arc to DED-LP with no intermediary processing. As such, the surface contaminants (silicate and other oxides) that formed during the DED-arc process were not removed prior to evaluation nor before the deposition of the DED-LP structure, as displayed in Fig. 3a. While the surface contaminants slightly alter the DED-arc surface topology, the overall variation was dominated by valleys from the step over distance and corner acceleration defects. These variations are visually evidenced by the obvious remnants of the toolpath in the topology data. For samples two (Fig. 3a) and three (Fig. 3b) the combined effect of the valleys and acceleration defects accumulate into surface topology variations of 1.8 mm and 1.6 mm respectively for the area of interest, as outlined in blue.

a and c display the as deposited surface of the DED-arc for samples two and three respectively. b and d display the DED-LP surface for samples two and three respectively.

It should be noted that the DED-arc in sample three was machined to a uniform surface only for the area where DED-LP would be deposited, that is, the entire surface was not machined flat. This decision was made to ensure that a similar thermal mass was present for all samples. As expected, the DED-LP deposition for sample three, as displayed in Fig. 3d, was highly uniform with subtle deviation from the programmed height (2.4 mm) at the corners due to machine acceleration/deceleration. Contrarily, the surface topology of sample two had significant variability with the edges being higher than the center section. Albeit, the extent of the variation in sample two (Fig. 3c) is significantly decreased relative to the deviation of the DED-arc surface with the complete obscuration of the DED-arc toolpath. This result is likely tied to the self-modulating effect that DED-LP exhibits as the focal point of the powder and laser converge/diverge (bulk powder capture in melt pool/significant decrease melt pool capture). From a qualitative perspective, sample two also displays a much less uniform surface coloration when compared to sample three. As the variation in color was seen in all the non-machined samples, it is thought to be a result of oxides that readily dissolved during the DED-LP process and rapidly floated to the surface of the melt pool before resolidification.

Porosity in the DED-LP section of the samples was evaluated by optical methods to gain insight into how depositing on the silicate and oxide contaminated surface impacted the density of the H13 section. Figure 4 displays the pore area fraction relative to the equivalent diameter of the pore as extracted using OpenCV and Python, with the solid bars being used to represent the contribution from each pore and the transparent bars displaying a 25 μm accumulator. A threshold of 100 μm was used to classify pores into those mainly caused by gaseous inclusions, as are commonly seen in DED-LP, or were a result of surface contaminants remaining from the DED-arc process that were melted and absorbed into the melt pool before resolidifying. The threshold was selected from a culmination of prior works32,33 that have investigated porosity in DED-LP under ideal conditions and from analysis of previous experiments that achieved greater than 99% density for the same material system. Using this threshold, sample three was found to have no pores larger than the threshold, an expected result as the surface was machined to a uniform surface free of DED-arc contaminants prior to deposition. Contrarily, a bulk of the porosity in the remainder of the samples was attributed to inclusions from the DED-arc processes due to the equivalent diameters ranging between 100 and 425 μm. It should be noted that, while a bulk number of the pores for these samples were still less than 100 μm, singular large pores are more heavily weighted in this analysis: as is evident by over 50% of the porosity in sample four being linked to a single 300 μm pore. The evaluated density of the component followed a similar trend to the size comparison with sample three exhibiting greater than 99% density with the remainder of the samples ranging from 92.4% to 97.5% for sample two and four/six respectively.

Comparison of pore fraction percentage between interface strategies for DED-LP section.

The pore shape factor, a ratio of the pore area to the pore circumference, was analyzed to delineate the most probably cause of each pore. Below the classification threshold (100 μm), a bulk of the pores were attributed to gaseous inclusions from entrapped gas into the feedstock due to the near-circle shape. However, for pores larger than the threshold, a mixture of lack of fusion and irregularly shaped pores was noted. The implications of these findings are found in Section 3; however, the major conclusions can be summarized as 1.) the lack of fusion porosity was most probable to be a result of variability in standoff distance due to the differing interface strategies that led to insufficient energy for full fusion of the feedstock to the previous layer and 2.) the large irregular shaped pores were likely caused by the rapid resolidification of surface contaminants that did not have sufficient time to diffuse to the surface of the DED-LP melt pool.

Figure 5 displays the Microvickers hardness relative to H13 top surface where each point represents an average of forty equally spaced points. Due to the cyclical heating and rapid cooling rates of the DED-LP process, the top surface is very near the maximum hardness of H13 for all samples (≈ 650 HV) with a gradual reduction in hardness due to an inherent heat-treatment effect as additional layers are deposited. Similarly, the measured hardness in the DED-arc section was very near the expected value (≈200 HV) with little variation. Contrarily, at the interface, significant variation in the hardness profile is noted between samples. In the span of one indent spacing, samples two and three transition from the maximum hardness value to the mild steel, thus indicating a sharp interface with limited variability. Whereas samples five and seven have a gradual transition between the hardness levels, thus indicating more intermixed material at the interfaces. This is attributed to the first-layer interface strategy being with the direction of lay as the melt pool more readily flowed to the extrema of the surface topology on the DED-arc surface. Sample one again serves as an outlier in these trends as the transition occurs closer to the H13 top surface.

Average hardness value of specimen relative to DED-LP top surface.

As the materials selected for the current work were chosen based on the ever growing need to develop new and novel methods for repairing high value tooling, any new approaches must withstand the high-strain-rate applications that are likely to be seen in the application space. To this extent, Charpy impact testing was utilized to evaluate both interface strategies, with the results displayed in Figure 6. In general, a high Charpy impact energy would indicate a ductile material that absorbs a significant amount of energy prior to breaking, while the converse is true for brittle materials. Analysis of the data in Fig. 6 shows little geometric variation between samples cut from edges or the center section of the specimens, thus indicating that the thermal softening, and by extension decreased brittleness, of the H13 clad structure was not prevalent in the tests. This result is likely a function of the size and geometry of the component as larger depositions would trend towards decreased hardness at the center of the structure due to increased thermal softening.

Charpy impact energy comparison between tested interface strategies.

Contrary to the minimal geometric variation, significant differences in absorbed energy were observed between the different interface strategies. It should be noted that sample one does not conform to the previously discussed trends. The cause of this variability will be discussed in section 3. Additionally, it should be noted that only one edge sample was evaluated for sample one due to significant inclusions in the DED-arc section that were not electrically conductive and thus prevented the electro-discharge machining (EDM) of the second edge sample. Excepting sample one, a variation of over 25% was measured between the lowest and highest absorbed energy, sample three with 105 J and sample five with 145 J, respectively. Interestingly, while sample three had a uniform surface condition to start with, the error associated with the lowest and highest averages was nearly the same range. The similarity in performance between the machined sample and others indicates that while interface strategy does contribute to the impact readiness of the material, other more stochastic defects, such as pore/inclusion location, are more likely to dominate. Additionally, when comparing samples that were deposited under the same standoff but with different first-layer directions, the samples deposited with the first layer against the DED-arc lay had higher impact energy. However, the average values were within the margin of error of the study, and thus it is difficult to draw conclusive results from this observation regarding the first layer of DED-LP orientation on Charpy energy.

Discussion

The present work explored the implications of different interface strategies between two macro scale DED processes. To investigate this paradigm, a DED-LP structure was deposited on a DED-arc surface utilizing distinct standoff plane formulations and varying first-layer directionality. Furthermore, a sample was locally machined to a uniform surface for comparison with the state-of-the-art methodology.

During the interface selection process, a careful balance of resources must be considered for industrial applications. Significant time and resources were expended to generate the unique topologically mapped surface for a given sample. At scale, this process is not feasible due to a lack of generalizability, as each new component necessitates a geometry-specific mapping strategy. Although not extensively discussed in this work, the added complexity of registering the mapped sample within multiple software and machine tools, coupled with the lack of an apparent advantage over the other investigated interfaces, significantly impedes widespread industrial adoption. In contrast, the plane of best fit and maximum point interface could be approximated using touch probes that are commonly integrated into machine tools. While the machined interface is the simplest to implement, it is the most time consuming from a machine operation standpoint due to the added material removal.

A strong negative correlation coefficient of 0.83 between the DED-LP fraction and Charpy impact energy suggests that as the portions of DED-LP decreased due to material differences, an increase in Charpy energy was exhibited. This aligns with previous studies showing that the Charpy energy for ER70S-6 is approximately 160 J8, while the Charpy energy of as-deposited H13 typically exhibits around 10 J34,35. Thus, a higher fraction of DED-arc material in the cross section correlates with increased impact toughness. Contrarily, weak correlations were observed between the Charpy toughness and both DED-LP flatness (0.21) and DED-LP density (-0.46). However, the largest impact on Charpy energy, as indicated by a correlation coefficient of 0.93, was DED-arc flatness. Indicating that as the DED-arc surface became less uniform, an increase in Charpy energy occurred. As the Charpy samples were cut against the lay of the DED-arc surface, there was a higher chance that the Charpy impact test occurred at a crest of the DED-arc bead, thus resulting in a reduced fraction of DED-LP. Which as was discussed above, would lead to an increased Charpy impact energy. This is corroborated by the high correlation between DED-LP fraction and DED-arc flatness (−0.88). As the negative correlation would indicate that as the DED-arc surface became less uniform, the fraction of DED-LP material would decrease.

Prior work has identified the SRE17 as a mechanism in DED-LP, where powder capture efficiency, melt pool stability, and laser standoff distance interact to regulate layer height. Interestingly, the SRE begins developing after just four layers, as indicated by the weak correlation (0.19) between DED-arc and DED-LP flatness. Qualitatively, the SRE is evident in Fig. 3 from the uniformity of the DED-LP surface, despite the surface topology of the underlying DED-arc depositions. While the scale of flatness deviation (from 1.5 mm to 2.7 mm) is consistent with prior works in multi-bead depositions in DED-arc36, it represents a considerable deviation for DED-LP to overcome by the SRE17,18. However, when considered sample one, the SRE reduced the DED-LP surface flatness by over 55% relative to the DED-arc surface. The remaining samples saw reductions of flatness changes ranging from 10–33% for sample seven and five, respectively. From a standoff plane selection perspective, the highest point methodology achieved the smallest average reduction in flatness (≈11%), whereas the mapped surfaces were reduced on average by ≈ 49%. This is somewhat perplexing as the mapped surface maintained a constant standoff distance from the DED-arc surface and thus should have experienced minimal effects from the SRE. A possible explanation for the result may be that as the mapped surface traversed in the vertical direction at the extrema of the DED-arc topology, the melt pool remained molten for longer, thus allowing surface tension and gravitational forces to dominant and act as a self-leveling mechanism.

The high variability in Charpy results may be attributed to interfacial discontinuities such as gaseous pores, oxide inclusions, or lack of fusion. In this study, the exact location of interfacial discontinuities was not tracked with respect to the Charpy test samples. An H13-mild steel interface with many discontinuities would exhibit a lower impact energy. However, the more ductile mild steel can absorb more energy obfuscating the propensity of defects to cause lower impact energies than the nominal H13. In an ideal scenario with sufficient fusion (i.e., a rigidly bonded interface), the more brittle H13 is prone to crack at a lower impact energy, exhibiting significantly less deformation than the mild steel. The fracture of H13 induces high stresses at the interface, subjecting the surrounding mild steel to additional stress. This rapid deflection from the H13 cracking will either cause the interface to fail (delamination) allowing the mild steel to absorb more energy before cracking as well, or the interface will transfer the stress from cracking to the mild steel with some energy absorption. Figure 8 displays images taken of the fracture surface of sample one and sample three illustrating the ductile response of the DED-arc deposition as compared to the H13. For sample three, the Charpy results indicate that the interface strength was sufficient to resist delamination with the mild steel rigidly bonded to the H13. Partial delamination of the interface was observed in the case of sample one as highlighted in red.

In summary, this work suggests that while machining DED-arc reduces variation caused by surface impurities, use-case-specific performance may not degrade from depositing on an as-printed surface. Porosity analysis demonstrates that printing directly on the as-printed DED-arc surface results in lack of fusion porosity from insufficient material dilution. Despite the machined case having the highest density and smallest porosity, as-printed samples demonstrated equivalent or higher impact toughness. Regardless of standoff or lay direction, the self-regulating effect of powder DED-LP improved the flatness of the final surface. Finally, impact toughness was more strongly correlated to the DED-arc surface flatness than any other factor, with performance variation largely attributed to surface defects.

For practical applications, the choice of interface processing strategy should depend on the final use case. For high value, low production components such as tooling die and molds that prioritize durability over processing speed; machining or thorough cleaning can be justified to remove surface contaminants and ensure a high fatigue life. For low value, high production components, where process efficiency may outweigh process quality outcomes, a faster interface strategy without intervention such as a best fit plane may be acceptable. Without intervention such as machining, the density will likely decrease leading to shortened tool life.

Characterization

A Keyence VR-6200 optical profilometer was used to measure the top surface of the DED-arc and the DED-LP deposition for geometric uniformity with a XY resolution of 95 μm and a Z repeatability of 4 μm. Python 3.11, FreeCAD, and OpenMind hyperMill 2024.2 were used to manipulate and extract various values and statistics from the scans as well as generate the appropriate toolpaths for the various interface strategies. Additionally, the flatness of each scan was calculated using an orthogonal least squares regression to understand the self-regulation effects of the DED-LP process on the resultant geometry.

Hardness mapping of the interface region of the samples was performed with a Buehler VH3300 microvickers hardness tester. A 25 mm region at the center of the specimen was mapped with using a load of 0.5 kg and dwell time of 10 s. Indents were spaced on a 750 μm grid starting 200 μm from the top surface of the DED-LP, a spacing that was determined the be sufficient relative to the ASTM standard spacing of two and half times the largest indent (due to the significant differences in hardness between the two materials, the spacing was dominated by the mild steel indents). The indents were evaluated with a 40x objective with indents having less than 6% asymmetry on average.

Full size ASTM E23-24 Charpy impact samples were cut from the deposited samples with the 55 mm length being normal to the lay of the DED-arc sample. This orientation was selected to ensure that the maximum variability in DED-LP thickness was present across each sample. A total of four samples were cut from all the specimens, two coming from the boundary and two coming from the center section to illicit and spatial variability the different interface strategies had on the absorbed energy. The samples were machined as v-notch samples with the impact surface being the DED-LP surface (the notch was cut into the DED-arc sample) as this closely mimics the impacts the tooling would see in practice. This orientation has also been extensively utilized for the testing of bimetallic junctions in other applications37,38. The impact testing was performed on an Instron SI-1K3 at room temperature with all samples being completely broken.

To delineate the boundary between the DED-LP and DED-arc materials, the cross-sectioned samples underwent standard metallographic preparation. The samples were mechanically polished using progressively finer silicon carbide (SiC) abrasive papers, ranging from 320 grit to 1200 grit. Subsequently, the polished samples were chemically etched with a 5% Nital solution to reveal distinct microstructural features resulting from compositional variations. The samples were then imaged using a Keyence VR6200 with an estimated spatial resolution of 15 μm per pixel. Afterward, the images were analyzed using Python and OpenCV. The process is graphically illustrated in Fig. 9, where the region of analysis is shown in Fig. (a), encompassing a 50 mm wide central region. Geometrical defects, such as non-uniform edges caused by melting collapses, an inherent characteristic of the DED process, were excluded from the analysis, while the entire sample height was considered for evaluation. Figure (b) presents the raw images acquired for all seven conditions.
