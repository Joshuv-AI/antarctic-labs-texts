An X-ray Dataset and Benchmark for AI-Based Diagnosis of Monteggia Fractures
Authors: Ning Li, Zhu Xiong, Suigu Tang, Zhenhui Zhao, Shuai Xue, Chaohao Jiang, Ajian Liu, Yanyan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonteggia fractures exhibit a high missed diagnosis rate of over 20%, largely attributable to their subtle radiographic presentation and frequently low clinical suspicion. While AI-based diagnostic methods hold considerable potential to enhance detection accuracy, their development has been hampered by the absence of a dedicated, well-annotated imaging dataset. To address this gap, we introduce MFXR, a publicly available X-ray dataset designed to facilitate research in AI-based diagnosis of Monteggia fractures. The dataset comprises 4,586 X-ray images from 1,482 patients, including 2,793 Monteggia fracture images and 1,793 normal or other fracture controls. All cases have been annotated and validated by board-certified orthopedic surgeons, ensuring high-quality labels. Furthermore, we provide benchmark results based on eight representative deep learning models, offering performance baselines to support and guide future research. This public dataset and its accompanying benchmarks are expected to accelerate the development of reliable AI diagnostic tools, thereby helping to reduce diagnostic errors and improve patient care in the management of Monteggia fractures.

Background & Summary

Clinical Motivation

The Monteggia fracture1–5 is defined as a proximal ulnar shaft fracture with a concomitant radial head dislocation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. It is a relatively uncommon injury, with a markedly higher incidence in children compared to adults2–4. In a normal forearm, the proximal ulna maintains its alignment with the trochlea of the humerus, and the radial head articulates smoothly with the capitellum. Both bones move in unison during pronation and supination, with no evidence of fracture or dislocation, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In practice, Monteggia fractures are notoriously overlooked in initial diagnosis, often due to occult ulnar fractures or overlooked radial head displacement in anteroposterior views, resulting in missed diagnoses in over 20% of cases2,5. The consequences of missed diagnoses can be severe. Chronically dislocated radial heads may become entrapped by soft tissue or scar formation, resulting in elbow joint deformities and requiring complex surgical procedures such as ulnar osteotomies2,3. In pediatric cases, delayed recognition can impair bone growth, leading to permanent functional disabilities and lifelong quality-of-life impacts2,4. The emerging field of AI-assisted diagnostics offers a promising pathway to augment radiographic analysis. Recent deep learning models have achieved expert-level accuracy in automated fracture classification for regions like the spine6, calcaneus7, and wrist8–10. Advanced methods further enable precise fracture localization11–14 and morphological segmentation15. Clinically, these tools match specialist performance16,17 and significantly improve junior residents’ diagnostic accuracy12. However, the development of such AI tools for Monteggia fractures has been fundamentally constrained by a critical bottleneck: the lack of a dedicated, high-quality imaging dataset.

Limitations of existing datasets

While the clinical need is evident, existing radiographic resources are inadequate for developing specialized diagnostic tools for Monteggia fractures. Current public orthopedic datasets are typically large-scale and multi-purpose, prioritizing breadth over the granular detail required for complex joint injuries. Specifically, these datasets vary widely in their anatomical focus, ranging from craniofacial structures18,19 (e.g., the mandible) and the spinal column20 (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar) to distal joints14,17,21–24 (e.g., wrist and ankle) and major extremities10,25–27 (e.g., humerus, forearm, and knees). While comprehensive in their respective domains, these resources often lack a sufficient density of Monteggia cases to capture its diverse clinical presentations, such as subtle ulnar bowing or occult radial head displacements. This gap in specialized, high-quality data remains the primary obstacle to training robust deep learning models and achieving reproducible benchmarks in AI-based detection for this specific injury.

MFXR dataset

To address this critical gap, we present MFXR28, the first public X-ray dataset dedicated to advancing AI research for Monteggia fractures. The dataset is structured to provide a comprehensive resource for model development. It is characterized by three key attributes: (1) Its scale and diversity, comprising 4,586 images from 1,482 patients, including 2,793 confirmed Monteggia fractures and 1,793 controls, with the data collection workflow shown in Fig. 2; (2) Its expert-validated annotations, where all labels have been meticulously verified by board-certified orthopedic surgeons, as shown in the Labeling panel of Fig. 2, ensuring high-quality ground truth for diagnosis; and (3) The establishment of a foundational benchmark, achieved by evaluating eight representative deep learning models. We provide these benchmarks to offer the community performance standards, delineate current challenges, and furnish a reproducible framework for future research. By releasing MFXR, we aim to accelerate the development of reliable AI tools, ultimately contributing to a reduction in diagnostic errors and an improvement in patient care.

Methods

The MFXR dataset28 was developed using pediatric arm radiographs and expert-derived annotations. The annotations were established through a direct analysis of the radiographic content, supplemented by insights from the corresponding clinical reports. The dataset construction followed a six-stage pipeline, ranging from initial data collection to the final dataset splitting, as detailed in the systematic workflow shown in Fig. 2.

Data collection

The dataset for this study was retrospectively obtained from Shenzhen Children’s Hospital, comprising radiographic studies and corresponding clinical case reports from 1,565 patients over an 11-year period (2013 to 2024). The patient cohort, aged 0–14 years, represented a clinically diverse case mix that included normal findings, Monteggia fractures (encompassing all four Bado types, I-IV1), other fracture types, and post-operative follow-up images. As typical of real-world clinical radiographs, the original images presented several challenges: they frequently included irrelevant anatomical regions (e.g., torso or unaffected limbs) and exhibited quality variations such as motion artifacts and improper exposure.

Data preprocessing

The original DICOM files were converted to JPG format for broader compatibility. All images subsequently underwent a rigorous manual curation process to exclude studies with significant motion artifacts, improper exposure, or incomplete anatomical coverage. Furthermore, images depicting irrelevant regions (e.g., torso) or those containing surgical implants were manually excluded to prevent confounding features in model training. This expert-driven quality control resulted in a final curated dataset comprising studies from 1,482 patients.

Labeling & data masking

To ensure the diagnostic accuracy of the MFXR dataset, all X-ray images were rigorously annotated through a multi-stage process. First, an experienced pediatric orthopedist provided an initial diagnostic label based solely on radiographic findings. This assessment was then reconciled with the full clinical documentation by the same specialist. Finally, all cases were reviewed and verified by a second board-certified orthopedic surgeon to establish the definitive consensus-based final diagnosis. In case of discrepancies, a third senior expert would mediate to reach a final consensus, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the national committees on human experimentation and the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Children’s Hospital (Reference number: 202505902). Due to the retrospective nature of this study and the absence of additional risk to patients, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. To ensure patient privacy and allow for secure data distribution, the dataset was fully anonymized in strict compliance with the HIPAA Safe Harbor standard. This process involved the removal of all 18 specified categories of protected health information, including case numbers, patient names, addresses, and identifiers within image headers.

Data augmentation

To enhance model training, we retained all original radiographs and generated additional region-focused augmented samples. Specifically, we generated new samples by cropping image patches around the key anatomical landmarks (the proximal ulna and radial head) identified in the annotations. We applied region-focused cropping to direct the model’s attention toward the radiocapitellar joint and ulnar shaft, ensuring the capture of subtle cues essential for identifying Monteggia-specific anomalies.

Dataset splitting

The primary objective of the MFXR dataset is to facilitate the detection of Monteggia fractures, a task that aligns naturally with the anomaly detection paradigm29–34. In this paradigm, Monteggia fracture cases are treated as the ‘anomaly’ class, while all other conditions are collectively defined as the ‘normal’ class. The dataset was partitioned into training and test sets in a 2:1 ratio, stratified by class label (i.e., ‘normal’ vs. ‘anomaly’) to preserve the distribution of classes across both sets. Crucially, the split was performed at the patient level to ensure that all images from a single patient were exclusively assigned to either the training or test set, thereby preventing data leakage and ensuring a rigorous evaluation of model generalization. Training: 1,794 Monteggia fracture images and 1,199 normal (non-Monteggia) images. Test: 999 Monteggia fracture images and 594 normal images. Comprehensive benchmark

To better enable subsequent researchers to utilize the proposed MFXR dataset, we provide a comprehensive benchmark and detailed experimental procedures and parameters. We evaluate eight widely adopted deep learning models as feature extractors, each paired with a unified classifier for performance assessment. The selected frameworks represent the current mainstream approaches in medical computer vision: three convolutional neural networks (ResNet1835, ResNet5035, Wide-ResNet5036), three vision transformers (ViT-Small37, ViT-Base37, ViT-Large37), and two contrastive language-image pretraining models (CLIP-ViT-B/1638, CLIP-ViT-B/3238). This diverse selection enables systematic comparison across fundamentally different architectural approaches while maintaining clinical relevance for orthopedic trauma imaging. All benchmarked models were systematically evaluated at two input resolutions (256 × 256 and 518 × 518 pixels) to assess how sensitive different architectures are to input resolution, a factor that may particularly affect the detection of subtle Monteggia fracture patterns. Evaluation metrics

We employed five key metrics to comprehensively assess model performance for Monteggia fracture detection: Area Under the Curve (AUC), Accuracy (ACC), Precision, Recall, and F1-score. The evaluation metrics used in this study are derived from the agreement or disagreement between model predictions and ground-truth labels. We therefore first define the four basic classification outcomes used in the following calculations. In this task, Monteggia fracture images were treated as the fracture class, while control images were treated as the control class. TP denotes Monteggia fracture images correctly classified as Monteggia-positive; FN denotes Monteggia fracture images incorrectly classified as non-Monteggia controls; FP denotes control images incorrectly classified as Monteggia-positive; and TN denotes control images correctly classified as non-Monteggia controls.

AUC measures the model’s ability to distinguish Monteggia fracture images from control images based on the model’s predicted fracture probability. This metric is important because subtle Monteggia fracture patterns may resemble normal anatomical variations. AUC is defined as the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (true positive rate vs. false positive rate across all classification thresholds). Data Records

The MFXR dataset28 has been deposited in figshare and is publicly available with no registration required for access. After the curation process described in the Methods section, the released collection comprises 2,993 training images and 1,593 test images from 1,482 unique patients. Image resolutions varied widely, from approximately 138 × 129 to 2,405 × 2,862 pixels, as summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 4. All images underwent a rigorous curation process to ensure consistency and clinical relevance, as detailed in the pipeline illustrated in Fig. 2. The dataset is distributed as a ZIP archive. Upon extraction, users will find two main directories: training and test. Each directory contains two subfolders: anomaly (representing Monteggia fractures) and good (encompassing all other normal and non-Monteggia fracture cases). Both the anomaly and good folders include the original images as well as their corresponding augmented samples. Each patient was assigned a unique, non-identifiable identifier to fully protect privacy. Different radiographic views and their augmented derivatives are distinguished by a suffix after a hyphen in the filename (e.g., 440359-1.jpg). Formulated as an anomaly detection task for Monteggia fracture detection, the dataset is organized into two categories: ‘anomaly’ and ‘normal’. The ‘anomaly’ folder contains all Monteggia fracture X-rays, encompassing the four classic types. The ‘good’ folder includes non-Monteggia cases, comprising normal forearm images and other fracture types. All images in the dataset are stored in JPG format. We provide examples of the dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 5.

Examples of MFXR dataset.

Overall, the dataset is structured to support robust supervised learning through a clear training-test split, balanced anatomical coverage, and multiple imaging views per case. This design promotes both operational simplicity and improved model generalizability by enabling the learning of a wide spectrum of features associated with Monteggia fractures.

Technical Validation

The technical quality of the MFXR dataset and the rigor of its construction process were validated through the following measures:

Validation of data labeling

The diagnostic labeling of Monteggia fractures is clinically challenging, with reported misdiagnosis rates as high as 20%. To quantitatively validate the reliability of our annotation protocol, we measured the inter-observer agreement between the two board-certified surgeons on a randomly selected subset of 100 cases prior to the final consensus. The result was a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.92, indicating an almost perfect level of agreement and providing strong statistical evidence for the consistency and reliability of our diagnostic labels.

Validation of deep learning-based models

The functional utility of the MFXR dataset is demonstrated by the performance of a comprehensive benchmark comprising eight diverse deep learning models (Tables 2 and 3). The performance across different architectures, ranging from classical CNNs to modern vision transformers, indicates that the dataset contains robust and learnable features for Monteggia fracture detection. The consistency of high AUC values provides strong evidence that the dataset is of sufficient quality to support the development of effective models. This benchmark not only validates the dataset’s technical soundness but also establishes a solid baseline performance for future research.

Validation of salient features

To provide qualitative evidence that models learn clinically relevant features from our dataset, we visualized the attention maps of the ResNet-18 model (trained on 256 × 256 pixel images) using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM). As shown in Fig. 6, the model consistently localizes its decision-making focus to critical anatomical regions, with high activation observed at the proximal radioulnar joint (site of radial head dislocation) and the fracture site on the proximal ulna. The strong spatial correlation between the model’s attention and the established radiographic landmarks of a Monteggia fracture confirms that the model is learning valid pathological features from the dataset, rather than relying on spurious correlations. This provides additional, visual proof of the dataset’s clinical relevance and technical soundness.

Conclusion

The release of MFXR addresses an important bottleneck in AI-assisted Monteggia fracture diagnosis: the absence of a dedicated, expert-validated imaging benchmark. Our benchmark results indicate that subtle Monteggia fracture patterns remain challenging for current models. In particular, recall remains below the level needed for autonomous clinical use. We therefore view MFXR not as an endpoint, but as a foundation for future studies on fine-grained fracture localization, multi-view fusion, and prospective clinical validation. In the long term, this work may help reduce missed diagnoses in pediatric care.
