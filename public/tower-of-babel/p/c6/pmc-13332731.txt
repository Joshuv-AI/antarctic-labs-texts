Therapeutic Potential of Ziyuglycosides from Sanguisorba officinalis: Anticancer and Immunomodulatory Properties
Authors: Jiaying Qi, Ying Zhong, Nana Huang, Hang Du, Jianchao Li, Xiaolu Li, Rong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer represents a major global health threat, and the development of effective anticancer therapies is urgently needed. Sanguisorba officinalis (DY), a traditional Chinese herbal medicine, possesses properties including cooling blood and promoting blood circulation, detoxifying pathogenic toxins, and astringing sores. Increasing evidence suggests that DY and its bioactive constituents exhibit notable anti-tumor effects, with mechanisms that align with traditional Chinese medical principles of enhancing Zhengqi (fuzheng) and removing Xieqi (quxie). However, a comprehensive review that systematically summarizes the antitumor potential of DY and its bioactive constituents is still lacking. In this review, we summarize the anticancer effects of DY extracts and Ziyuglycosides. In vitro studies demonstrate that DY and its active components can inhibit tumor cell proliferation, induce cell cycle arrest, trigger mitochondria-mediated apoptosis, enhance autophagic flux, and suppress invasion and metastasis by regulating Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and EGFR/NF-κB signaling pathways, as well as downregulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition-related transcription factors and matrix metalloproteinases. In vivo studies confirms DY and its active components inhibit tumor growth and metastasis, regulate the tumor immune microenvironment, modulate Th17/Treg balance, activate CD8+ T cells, and enhance the anti-tumor immune responses. Notably, clinical application evidence indicates that Diyu Shengbai Pian, a preparation derived from DY, have been proven effective in alleviating leukopenia caused by chemotherapy or radiotherapy, highlighting DY’s dual therapeutic potential in tumor suppression and mitigating treatment-related adverse reactions. However, current research mainly focuses on in vitro cellular studies and in vivo animal models, lacking large-scale, standardized clinical trials to validate its long-term efficacy and safety in humans. This review systematically summarizes the mechanisms of the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory effects of DY and its active components, and outlines advancements in improving their in vivo delivery efficiency, providing a theoretical basis and research reference for the translational application of DY in cancer therapy.Keywords: Sanguisorba officinalis, ziyuglycoside I, ziyuglycoside II, anticancer, immunomodulatory

Introduction

Cancer remains a significant challenge to global public health, with high incidence and mortality rates posing a persistent threat to human health. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, approximately 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million deaths occurred worldwide in 2022. Projections indicate that by 2050, the annual number of cancer-related deaths will rise to 35 million.1 Conventional anti-tumor modalities, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy, constitute the cornerstone of clinical cancer management. However, these approaches are increasingly limited by inherent constraints like drug resistance and severe off-target adverse events.2 Moreover, the exorbitant costs of these regimens impose a substantial socioeconomic burden on both patients and healthcare systems. The urgent imperative to identify novel, safe, and efficacious anti-cancer agents is underscored by these converging challenges.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has played a long, pivotal role in the management of cancer, underpinned by its theoretical foundations in traditional Chinese medical principles and its systematic approach to therapeutic intervention. Within the TCM system, the term “cancer” was originally used to denote liu, tumor, and mass, which were systematically articulated in the Huang Di Nei Jing.3 It was believed that the occurrence of cancer was primarily related to an imbalance between Zhengqi and Xieqi. When Zhengqi, which also means healthy qi, was insufficient, it was prone to being affected by Xieqi, the unhealthy, eventually leading to disease. Therefore, the treatment principle of cancer in TCM lies in enhancing Zhengqi (fuzheng) and removing Xieqi (quxie), that is, by promoting and maintaining the circulation of Zhengqi, such as enhancing immunity in tumor treatment, and eliminating Xieqi, such as killing tumor cells. Both fuzheng and quxie exhibited co-anti-tumor effects.4

TCM consists of three categories: herbal medicine, animal medicine, and mineral medicine, which complement each other and together provide diverse strategies for cancer therapy. Plant-based TCM are not only abundant in resources but also highly effective. Paclitaxel, derived from Taxus chinensis, blocks tumor cell division by interfering with microtubule function and has been developed into a clinical anti-cancer drug and widely applied.5 Animal-derived TCM have the characteristic of strong penetrating power. Bufalin, derived from toad venom extract, can inhibit tumor cell proliferation, reverse chemotherapy resistance, and provide strong pain relief.6 It is used in the treatment of various tumors. Marine animals and plants are an emerging field of anti-cancer research. The unique living environment of Marine organisms enables their metabolites to have novel anti-cancer activities. At present, it has been found that a variety of Marine active ingredients, such as molecular glue degradants derived from Marine organisms, can selectively regulate pathogenic proteins through the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which provides candidate drug support for targeted anti-cancer.7 Aaptamine, derived from Aaptos sponge, inhibits angiogenesis and inhibits renal cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma by degrading HIF-1α.8 Bryostatin-1 derived from Bugula neritina, has a regulatory effect on immune function and can induce apoptosis of multiple myeloma and leukemia cells.9 Xyloketal B, derived from Marine fungi, has also shown antitumor activity in solid tumor models such as liver cancer.10 Phycocyanin extracted from Spirulina maximum has also been confirmed to have clear anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative stress and anti-cancer activities, showing broad research and development prospects. In addition, mineral TCM has the effect of clearing heat and detoxing, softening and dispersing, showing a unique anticancer effect.11 Arsenic trioxide can treat acute promyelocytic leukemia by inducing apoptosis of leukemia cells and inhibiting abnormal proliferation, significantly improving the remission rate of patients.12 At the same time, it also has a certain adjuvant treatment effect on solid tumors such as liver cancer and lung cancer.

In addition to natural active ingredients, chemical modification and green synthesis strategies based on the active ingredients of TCM have shown broad prospects in the development of new anticancer drugs in recent years. Alyaa A. Alkhafaji et al have successfully synthesized silver nanoparticles and zinc oxide nanoparticles using the extract of the TCM plant Borago officinalis as reducing and stabilizing agents, which have selective cytotoxicity against colon cancer HT-29 cells and can promote cancer cell death by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production.13 Samar M. Shakir et al, using the active backbone of TCM coumarin as the lead structure, obtained new derivatives by chemical modification. The thiourea group (C=S) and Schiff base structure (-CH =N -) in the structure can enhance the binding ability to metal ions or target proteins, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of cancer cells.14 The pyrazole derivative (S2) synthesized from chalcone as precursor by Yasameen K. Al-Majedy et al has a strong binding affinity with Serologically defined colon cancer antigen 10, which can effectively inhibit the proliferation and induce apoptosis of colon cancer HT-29 cells15 The above studies indicate that the active ingredients derived from TCM have important potential in anticancer drug development after rational modification or nano-modification.

Besides the active products, TCM extracts can form complexes with synergistic effects through scientific ratios. The study by T. Abiraamavalli et al, Acorus calamus, Tinospora cordifolia, Coscinium fenestratum, Terminalia chebula, SmilaxFive extracts of Chinese medicinal materials were prepared into a complex, and their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and other biological activities and biocompatibility were evaluated. The results showed that the biological activities of the metabolic complex were superior to those of the single extract, and the biocompatibility was good.16 Krithika Shree Sivasuriyan et al prepared an ethanol extract of Mukia maderaspatana combined with chitosan and starch to prepare a novel biocomposite and evaluated its antioxidant and antibacterial biological activities. The results showed that its biological activity was better than that of the original extract, and it had good thermal stability, structural stability and biocompatibility. Among them, enhanced antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capabilities can effectively inhibit tumor-related oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, which provides a low-toxicity, multi-target complex construction strategy for the development of anti-cancer adjuvant agents.17

Sanguisorbae Radix (DY, also named DiYu in Chinese), derived from the dried roots of Sanguisorba officinalis L. or Sanguisorba officinalis L. var. longifolia (Bert). Yü et Li of the Rosaceae family is one of the oldest and most significant medicinal herbs, first documented and systematized in the Shen Nong Ben Cao Jing during the Han Dynasty. The distribution of DY is primarily confined to Europe, the northern temperate regions of Asia, and China. It is recorded that DY grows in grasslands, meadows, grasslands on mountain slopes, in thickets, under sparse forests, at altitudes of 30–3000 meters, especially in the provinces of Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, etc, in China. According to TCM theory, DY is characterized by a “bitter, sour, and slightly cold” nature, with tropism to the liver and colon meridians. It was recorded that the classic therapeutic effects of DY were cooling blood and promoting blood circulation (凉血活血), detoxifying pathogenic toxins and astringing sores (解毒敛疮). Based on its traditional applications, the holistic perspective of TCM in DY implied that DY cleared pathogenic heat and toxins to counteract Xieqi while astringing and nourishing the body’s tissues to support Zhengqi. This “fighting Xieqi without damaging Zhengqi” feature is a core advantage of TCM in cancer treatment. According to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, DY was commonly used for hematochezia, hemorrhoidal bleeding, hemorrhagic dysentery, uterine bleeding, burns, scalds, carbuncles, and swelling toxins. Numerous pharmacological investigations have revealed that DY exerts notable effects on antioxidant,18 anti-inflammatory,19 antiviral,20 antibacterial,21 hemostatic,22 and anticancer.23 In addition, bioactive triterpenoids isolated from DY, named ziyuglycoside I (ZYGI) and ziyuglycoside II (ZYGII), demonstrated broad-spectrum antitumor and immunomodulation activity. DiYu ShengBai Pian (DYSBP), a drug composed of DY and other excipients, is also considered a candidate for improving blood tumors and promoting immunity.24 However, there is still a lack of systematic research on the anti-cancer effects of DY, its preparations, and active ingredients.

This study systematically reviews current research on DY, its marketed drugs, and representative ziyuglycosides, providing a comprehensive overview of its applications in cancer treatment and revealing its potential in anticancer and immune regulation. We sincerely hope that this study will provide new evidence for the future application and translation of DY and its derivatives in anti-tumor therapy. Phytochemical Constituents of DY

The chemical constituents of DY are rich and structurally diverse. To date, more than 120 compounds have been isolated and identified from this plant genus, which can be classified into four major categories. Among them, triterpenoids and phenolic compounds are the main active ingredients, while flavonoids and other auxiliary components work synergistically to exert pharmacological effects. The structural characteristics and distribution of various components exhibit clear species-specificity and medicinal relevance. In the roots of DY, it mainly contained triterpenoids and their glycosides, as well as a small amount of monoterpene glycosides, with a content of approximately 2.4% to 4.0%. To date, a total of 75 triterpenoids and nine monoterpene glycosides have been isolated from DY. Pentacyclic triterpenoids were the main type of triterpenoid represented by ZYGI (C41H6O13) and ZYGII (C35H56O8), which were the core markers for the quality control of DY. The structural formulas of ZYGI and ZYGII are shown in Figure 2. Tannins, with a total content reaching up to 17%, are another type of ingredient that cannot be overlooked in DY. It is suggested that DY primarily comprises hydrolyzable tannins and condensed tannins. To date, 62 tannin compounds have been isolated from DY, including hydrolyzable tannins such as gallic acid, which is another marker for quality control of DY, with a minimum required content of 1.0%. Flavonoid constituents in DY are predominantly flavonol and anthocyanin derivatives, present at concentrations of approximately 3.0%-4.0%. Major structural types include quercetin, catechins, kaempferol, and their glycoside derivatives, such as rutin and 7-O-galloyl-(+)-catechin, which exhibit strong antioxidant activity. In addition to the three primary classes of bioactive compounds, DY also contains lignans, organic acids, polysaccharides, and other components that contribute to its nutritional value. Furthermore, DY contains steroidal compounds such as β-sitosterol and soyasapogenol, as well as essential trace elements, including magnesium, calcium, and zinc.25

Identification and Metabolism of Ziyuglycosides

To identify ZYGs and their secondary products in vivo and in vitro, numerous studies were undertaken through ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS), liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-Q-TOF/MS), or the sectional multiple filtering (SMF) scheme. In the full-scan spectra, intense peaks for ZYGI were observed at m/z 784.5 → 437.4 via a 150V fragment and 10 V collision energy in positive polarity. After oral administration of DY to rats at different concentrations, plasma concentrations were measured approximately 1 hour post-dose, which was attributed to plasma solubility.26–28 Due to gastric emptying fraction, enterohepatic circulation, and a separate “absorption window”, the second peak was observed at 4–8 h.29 However, elimination rates of ZYGI were inconsistent across concentrations, with a t1/2 ranging from 5.02 h to 19.76 h. To further describe the absorption, distribution, and excretion of ZYGI, tail vein injection and subcutaneous injection of ZYGI were carried out in rats. The results indicated that ZYGI was rapidly eliminated and had low total recovery in bile, urine, and feces, suggesting potential biotransformation.30 ZYGI was metabolized into various substances in the lung, liver, kidney, and colon via deglycosylation, oxidation, deoxidization, and dehydration. Recent studies have demonstrated that ZYGI is biotransformed into approximately 35 metabolites in rats, particularly ZYGII. The majority of ZYGI-metabolites found in urine originate from the liver and kidney, while feces contain all the metabolites produced by intestinal microbiota. Furthermore, ZYGI was also found to be converted into 3β,19α-dihydroxyurs-12-en-28-oic-acid 28-β-d-glucopyranosyl ester and pomolic acid, unidirectionally.31

ZYGII, which could be bio-transformed from ZYGI, has a similar identification method as m/z 603.6 → 585.4 for an intense peak and 39 eV collision energy. It was indicated that after administration of 2 g/kg YGII for 2.67 hours, the plasma ZYGII reach peak concentration, followed by a t1/2 at 12.16h, which was all later than ZYGI.32 Jingcheng Xiao et al use the SMF scheme to distinguish metabolites in Phase I and Phase II redox reactions and their deglycosylated derivatives. A total of 17 ZYGII metabolites were identified in the liver homogenate. Several metabolites of ZYGII, especially the glucuronic-, dehydrate-, decarboxylation-, dearabinosylation- and oxidative products, were the same as ZYGI.33 The metabolic derivatives of ZYGI and ZYGII are shown in Figure 3.

Anti-Tumor Effects

The anti-cancer activity of DY does not rely on a single mode of action, but rather involves a coordinated multi-target and multi-pathway attack that disrupts key processes essential for tumor survival and progression. Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated that DY interferes with multiple stages of malignant tumor development by modulating critical cellular events, including the induction of programmed cell death (PCD), cell cycle arrest, immunomodulation, inhibition of metastasis, and metabolic reprogramming.

Induction of Programmed Cell Death

Regulated cell death (RCD) refers to a class of programmed cell suicide pathways characterized by distinct signaling cascades involving multiple molecular effectors and exhibiting unique morphological and biochemical features, including apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and others.34 In the past 20 years, targeting RCD in cancer has become a necessary complementary approach to cancer treatment strategies. Multiple studies have shown that DY extract and its purified ziyuglycosides can effectively induce apoptosis and autophagy in various cancer cell lines.

Apoptosis, a form of non-immune cell death, has been extensively studied due to its critical role in cancer defense mechanisms and has been leveraged in the development of targeted anticancer therapies. As shown in Figure 4, the primary mechanism involves activating both mitochondrial-initiated endogenous and Fas/FasL-initiated exogenous apoptotic pathways. In apoptosis, Bcl-2 (anti-apoptotic) and Bax (pro-apoptotic) coordinately regulate mitochondrial membrane permeability: Bax facilitates the release of cytochrome c, whereas Bcl-2 suppresses it. Caspases are subsequently activated as executioner proteases, driving the apoptotic cascade and constituting a central regulatory axis in programmed cell death.35 Protein kinase B (AKT) is a primary effector of phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K), and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a downstream target of AKT, regulates cellular processes linked to proliferation, growth, and apoptosis.36 It was reported that the aqueous extract of DY induces S-phase cell cycle arrest and activates the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway by suppressing the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, thereby inhibiting breast cancer cell proliferation and metastasis and promoting apoptosis.37 Studies have demonstrated that the alcoholic extract of DY inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells and activates the ROS/caspase-3/ Poly(ADP-Ribose) Polymerase 1 (PARP1) axis, thereby inducing apoptotic cell death.38 Myeloid cell leukemia-1 (Mcl-1), a critical anti-apoptotic protein, is frequently overexpressed in cancer cells and contributes to chemotherapy resistance and tumor progression. It also modulates the activation and oligomerization of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, which are crucial events in initiating the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway.39 The methanol extract of DY suppresses Mcl-1 expression, enhances Bax levels, and promotes its oligomerization, thereby triggering the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway. This molecular mechanism accounts for the inhibition of PC3 prostate cancer cell proliferation and the induction of apoptosis, supporting the potential of the aqueous extract of DY as a therapeutic candidate for prostate cancer.40 Furthermore, evidence demonstrates that the aqueous extract of DY downregulates Mcl-1 and induces apoptosis in oral cancer HSC4 cells.41 The polysaccharide SRP purified from DY specifically induces apoptosis in the leukemia cell line HL-60 through a mitochondrial-mediated, endogenous, caspase-dependent pathway by affecting mitochondrial function, upregulating the Bax/Bcl-2 protein expression ratio, and activating the caspase cascade.

Molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory effects of DY and its ZYGs. DY and its representative constituents, including ZYGI and ZYGII, exert anti-tumor activity through multiple signaling pathways. In tumor cells, DY inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, downregulating proliferation-related proteins such as GSK-3β, DDK2, and mTOR, while increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels to promote apoptosis. Both ZYGI and ZYGII modulate the Bcl-2, Mcl-1, and Bax, triggering mitochondria-mediated apoptosis via cytochrome c release and caspase-9, caspase-7, and caspase-3, which ultimately cleaves PARP to execute cell death. Additionally, ZYGI upregulates p21 to arrest the cell cycle and induces apoptosis via the ROS-AIF pathway, while ZYGII activates the Fas/FasL-JNK1/2 pathway to initiate the extrinsic apoptosis cascade. Both ZYGI and ZYGII inhibit EGFR phosphorylation and NF-κB signaling to suppress tumor survival and inflammation. Notably, ZYGII also downregulates ESRRG to further enhance anti-tumor effects. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in cellular proliferation, self-renewal, and stem cell regulation and is strongly associated with tumors.42 In the absence of Wnt ligands, β-catenin is targeted for degradation by a destruction complex composed of Axin, adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), and glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β).43 GSK3β phosphorylates the N-terminal domain of β-catenin, thereby promoting its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing the expression of Wnt target genes. Evidence indicates that both the aqueous extract of DY and fractions eluted with 40% ethanol or 40% ethanol in combination with 40% acetonitrile upregulate caspase-3 and PARP expression and increase the BCL2-Associated X (Bax) /Bcl-2 ratio in HT29 colorectal cancer cells. These interventions also inhibit the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway by modulating downstream effectors such as dickkopf WNT signaling pathway inhibitor 2 (Dkk2) and GSK3β, thereby triggering intrinsic apoptosis.44

Through bioactivity-guided fractionation, gallic acid and ellagic acid were identified as the principal active constituents underlying the anti-breast cancer effects. These compounds induce apoptosis and inhibit angiogenesis, thereby contributing to the therapeutic potential of DY. Gallic acid induces apoptosis of colorectal cancer cells by increasing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and enhancing caspase-3 and caspase-9 expression, while simultaneously inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis.45 3,3’,4’-trimethylellagic acid (TMEA) can also significantly inhibit tumor cells such as HepG2, A549, and SW620 by up-regulating the protein expression ratio of Bax/Bcl2 and the expressions of caspase-3 and caspase-9, and down-regulating PI3K/AKT/mTOR.46 Studies also have shown that the active ingredient in DY, α-l-arabinopyranosyl oxy]-URS-12,18(19)-dien-28-oic acid β-d-glucopyranosyl ester, Gallic acid, catechinic acid, and ellagic acid can up-regulate the Bax/Bcl2 protein expression ratio and induce cell death through the intracellular mitochondrial-caspase dependent apoptotic pathway and autophagy.47 Ellagic acid (EA) allosterically modulates phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) activity, thereby activating the PTEN/ focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway, inhibiting the MAPK signaling cascade, and stabilizing p53 expression. EA exerts multi-target effects on B16F10 melanoma cells, inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. Furthermore, combination therapy with cisplatin demonstrates synergistic anti-melanoma effects.48 For ziyuglycosides, Xue Zhu et al proved that ZYGI significantly suppressed triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 proliferation and promoted apoptosis through the p53 pathway, which was also confirmed in human retinoblastoma WERI-Rb-1 cells.49,50 Multiple studies have shown that ZYGII promoted caspase-dependent or independent apoptosis. It increased Bax/Bcl-2 in mitochondria, decreased MMPs, and increased membrane permeability, resulting in the release of intrinsic apoptotic factors, cytochrome-c. In the process of bone resorption, estrogen-related receptor gamma (ESRRG) mediates the expression of transcription factors such as c-Fos by regulating the downstream nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) pathway, activates caspase-3, mediates osteoclast differentiation, bone resorption, and inflammatory response, and accelerates osteoclast apoptosis.51,52 Studies have shown that ZYGII can up-regulate p53 and ESRRG to inhibit cell proliferation, arrest cell cycle, and promote apoptosis.53 On the other hand, ZYGII activated Fas, which, when bound to its ligand, forms a death-inducing signaling complex, releases caspase-8, and induces extrinsic apoptosis. Ultimately, activated apoptotic factors and caspase-8 further stimulated caspase-3 activation, initiating a caspase cascade that results in cancer cell apoptosis.49,54 What is recognized is that the apoptosis-inducing factor (AIF) was a mitochondrion-localized flavoprotein for caspase-independent apoptosis, with NADH oxidase activity.55 Furthermore, Khaliunaa Lkhagvasuren et al also proved that ZYGII caused reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and AIF translocation, which led to colon cancer cell apoptosis.56 However, the generation of apoptosis signals by ZYGII in different cancer cells was conflicting, including ROS/c-Jun NH2-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway, p53/p21 upregulation, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/NF-κB signaling, and EGFR/extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK) signaling.50,57–60

Autophagy

Autophagy, as a downstream cellular response to ROS, plays a critical and dual regulatory role in determining cancer cell fate by mediating the delivery of damaged organelles and misfolded proteins to lysosomes for degradation.61 Autophagy enables cancer cells to survive under a variety of internal and external stress conditions, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and the accumulation of oxidized or aggregated proteins. Hypoxia inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) is a key regulator of autophagy. It is indicated that HIF-1α induction of BH3-only proteins (BNIP3 and BNIP3L) to release autophagy regulator Beclin-1, promotes autophagy.62 Furthermore, Hif-1α contributes to autophagy-associated glucose metabolism by regulating the expression of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1). Loss of Caveolin 1 (Cav-1) in stromal cells stabilizes HIF-1α, which, in turn, drives glycolysis and lactate efflux via upregulation of monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4) while promoting a catabolic phenotype with enhanced macroautophagy, reflecting their regulatory crosstalk in mediating the Warburg effect and stromal-carcinoma metabolic synergy.63 Notably, studies have demonstrated that DY water extract suppresses EMT and metastasis in triple-negative breast cancer by down-regulating HIF-1α-mediated Cav-1 expression, impairing lysosomal function, and blocking the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, thereby preventing substrate degradation.64 For ziyuglycosides, ZYGII attenuated phosphorylation of AKT and a mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR), and downstream substrates to enhance the conversion of LC3-I to lipidated LC3-II in a dose-dependent manner, finally promoting colorectal cancer autophagy both in vitro and in vivo, as shown in Figure 5.65,66

Induction of Cell Cycle Arrest

The division, growth, and cell cycle progression of tumor cells mediate critical biological processes, including tumor initiation, development, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance, and serve as the central driving forces behind the malignant behavior of cancers, as shown in Figure 5.67 While normal cell proliferation and cell cycle progression are tightly regulated, tumor cells often exhibit dysregulated signaling through pathways such as PI3K-Akt and MAPK. This dysregulation leads to the overexpression of core regulatory proteins, including cyclin D1 and c-Myc, disrupting the balance of the G0–G1 to S phase transition, accelerating cell cycle progression, and amplifying proliferative signals.68,69 Integrated analysis based on network pharmacology and transcriptomic sequencing indicates that the aqueous extract of DY suppresses colorectal cancer cell proliferation and migration by modulating the PI3K-Akt, HIF-1, and MAPK signaling pathways, and induces G0–G1 phase cell cycle arrest in colon cancer cells and breast cancer cells.37,70 For ZYGII, it was demonstrated that ZYGII blocked cells in the G0/G1 or G2/M phases; the latter was mediated by decreases in cyclin A2 and cyclin B1. MYC Proto-Oncogene (MYC) is a proto-oncogene commonly implicated in various human cancers that accelerates the transition from G1 to S phase and enhances tumor cell proliferation. Moreover, overexpression of Axin 2 has been shown to activate the Wnt signaling pathway, thereby accelerating colorectal cancer (CRC) development and promoting its metastatic progression. Additionally, Dickkopf Wnt Signaling Pathway Inhibitor 1 (DKK1) has been identified as an antagonist of the Wnt signaling pathway in CRC. Another compound of DY, called AGE, exerts antitumor effects by inducing cell cycle arrest at the G0–G1 phase through the downregulation of MYC, FGF20, and AXIN2 expression, concomitant upregulation of DKK1, and inhibition of the Wnt signaling pathway.71

Inhibition of Migration

Cell metastasis is a pivotal event in cancer progression, primarily driven by the aberrant activation of molecular signaling pathways that enable cancer cells to acquire invasive and migratory capabilities. As a result, targeting these metastasis-associated pathways has become a key therapeutic approach for improving patient prognosis and enhancing treatment efficacy. In colorectal cancer, high invasiveness is primarily mediated by the EMT72,73 (Figure 5). During this process, cancer cells undergo a phenotypic shift characterized by the loss of epithelial features, such as cell polarity and intercellular adhesion, and the gain of mesenchymal traits, including motility and invasive potential.74 This transformation facilitates detachment of tumor cells from the primary tumor mass, enabling them to infiltrate the intestinal mucosa and submucosa and subsequently invade adjacent blood or lymphatic vessels, thereby contributing to poor clinical outcomes and increased mortality. Activation of the MAPK pathway leads to the phosphorylation of transcription factors, which in turn induce the expression of EMT-related genes and MMPs, modulate key regulators such as neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally down-regulated 9 and Bmi-1, promote mesenchymal phenotype acquisition, and accelerate the degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Similarly, within the PI3K/AKT pathway, upregulation of AKT3 is strongly correlated with elevated expression of EMT marker genes, indicating its functional involvement in promoting chemoresistance.75 Specifically, MMP2 and MMP9 serve as key effector molecules in tumor invasion and metastasis. Their activity facilitates ECM degradation, releases growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and epidermal growth factor, and activates EGFR signaling, thereby promoting EMT and enabling tumor dissemination. Studies have demonstrated that ZYGII can inhibit the EGFR/NF-κB signaling pathway, downregulate the expression of MMP2 and MMP9, and consequently suppress the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of hepatocellular carcinoma cells, confirming the potential of ZYGII as an anti-metastatic agent in liver cancer.57 Another study has indicated that ZYGII inhibits the integrin subunit beta 4 / FAK complex, as well as downstream activation of Akt and p38MAPK, in a proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase Src /EGFR-dependent manner, thereby contributing to the inhibition of breast cancer cell metastasis.76,77 The high invasiveness of osteosarcoma stems from the adaptability of tumor cells to hypoxic conditions. Recent research indicates that the key component of the multi-comb inhibitory complex 1, staining box homologous 4 (CBX4), plays a significant role in driving these phenotypes by stabilizing β-catenin and increasing its membrane localization.78 Studies have confirmed through in vivo and shRNA experiments that ZYGII effectively inhibits the growth of OS tumors and lung metastasis in mice. Its role and reduces CBX4 expression thereby inhibit the Wnt/beta-catenin signaling pathway.79

Immune Regulatory Effects

As shown in Figure 6, immune escape represents a fundamental biological characteristic of tumor cells, enabling them to evade detection and elimination by the host immune system through diverse mechanisms. This capability allows tumors to achieve sustained proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.80 Moreover, immune escape serves as a critical driver in tumorigenesis, disease progression, recurrence, metastasis, and the development of therapeutic resistance. Chronic infiltrative inflammation is a key driver in the initiation and progression of CRC. Sustained inflammation in the colorectal mucosa activates macrophages to secrete tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which binds to receptors on epithelial cells and triggers activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. This cascade promotes nuclear translocation of the transcription factor p65, leading to the transcriptional regulation of downstream target genes.81 On one hand, this results in the upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators such as cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which perpetuate intestinal mucosal barrier dysfunction and establish a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that supports tumor development.82 On the other hand, the pathway induces overexpression of proliferation-associated genes, including cyclin D1 and c-Myc, and simultaneously suppresses apoptosis-related genes, thereby promoting uncontrolled expansion of intestinal epithelial cells. The persistent imbalance in cell turnover drives the transition from hyperproliferative states to precancerous lesions and ultimately culminates in malignant transformation.83 Research has demonstrated that triterpenoid extracts from DY significantly ameliorate clinical symptoms, reduce disease activity index scores, and alleviate pathological changes in CRC. These effects are mediated by downregulation of TNF-α, NF-κB p65, COX-2, and iNOS protein expression, thereby inhibiting aberrant activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. Furthermore, these extracts induce CpG island methylation of TNF-α and NF-κB p65 genes, promoting epigenetic silencing of cancer-related genes and markedly enhancing intestinal barrier integrity.84 Tumor cells evade immune surveillance by expressing immunosuppressive checkpoint molecules, secreting inhibitory cytokines, and recruiting regulatory T cells and tumor-associated macrophages, thereby constructing an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.85 Studies indicate that triterpenoid extracts from DY enhance T cell receptor (TCR) signal transduction and NFAT-mediated luciferase activity, activate splenocytes and tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells, promote the secretion of IL-2 and perforin 1, and augment the cytotoxic capacity of T cells against hPD-L1-expressing MC38 colorectal cancer cells. In a humanized PD-1 mouse model, DY extract dose-dependently suppresses tumor growth and exhibits synergistic antitumor effects when combined with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy, thereby further enhancing tumor inhibition.86 Additionally, Lin Zhang et al isolated four polysaccharide fractions from DY via hot-water extraction followed by gel filtration chromatography. These polysaccharides are primarily composed of glucose, fructose, xylose, arabinose, and rhamnose and are largely bound to proteins. Experimental evidence demonstrates their strong DPPH radical scavenging activity, antioxidant capacity, and immunomodulatory effects. They effectively stimulate murine mononuclear macrophage J774A.1 cells to produce nitric oxide (NO) and TNF-α, supporting their potential use as natural antioxidants, immune modulators, and candidate antitumor agents.87

Immunomodulatory mechanisms of DY and its bioactive components in the tumor microenvironment. Polysaccharides from DY exert antioxidant activity by reducing DPPH radicals and activate macrophages to secrete NO and TNF‑α, thereby enhancing innate immune responses. ZYGI regulates the balance between Th17 and Treg cells via the AKT/mTOR pathway, inhibiting the differentiation of Th17 cells and reducing the secretion of IL‑17. ZYGII activates the STING‑TBK1‑IRF3 signaling axis, which promotes the production of IFN‑γ and inhibits NF‑κB‑mediated inflammatory responses. Additionally, triterpenoids from DY upregulate the expression of NAFT, IL‑2, and PRF1 in CD8+ T cells, enhancing their cytotoxic function against tumor cells. For ziyuglycosides, the spleen serves as the primary pivotal organ for initiating adaptive immune response. In collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mice, T cells were detected after 28 days of ZYGI treatment. ZYGI significantly reduced thymus index, level of plasma IL-17, and T cell viability, most importantly, regulated the Th17/Treg balance by increasing Treg cells and reducing Th17 cells. The Akt-mTOR signaling pathway has been demonstrated to exert an impact on the metabolic profiles of T cells, like glycolysis, thereby modulating the formation of both Th17 and Treg cells.88 Impairment of mTOR activity has been linked to reduced Th17 cell development while facilitating the differentiation of Treg cells.89 ZYGI decreased the phosphorylation of Akt1 and mTOR in T cells, which has the potential to interact with Akt via hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and non-covalent interactions, as predicted by molecular docking.90 For further research, Hanfei Sun et al suggested that ZYGI has little effect on Treg cell differentiation due to ineffective plasma TGF-β, which is accepted as a regulator of Treg cells.91 However, noteworthy inhibition of B cell viability was observed in CIA mice, which drew attention to the function of preventing excess plasma cell generation by ZYGI. ZYGI triggered ERK phosphorylation, then decreased the maturation protein 1 in B cells and increased the activation-induced cytidine deaminase in the spleen, both of which exerted a positive influence on B cell formation and activation.92–94 Previous studies have shown that ZYGII is a novel small-molecule stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist. It is widely recognized that STING is a nucleic acid sensor that acts in autoimmune diseases and immune-oncology, which could bind in two C-terminal domains (CTD) to generate a V-shaped ligand binding pocket by its ligands.95–97 ZYGII can bind hSTING CTD in a dose-dependent manner and stimulate NF-κB signaling, the phosphorylation of recruiting TBK1, and the expression of interferon β, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 and interferon-stimulated gene 56 in THP-1 cells.98

Chinese Patent Drug of DY

In modern pharmacological research, the therapeutic potential of Sanguisorba officinalis has been significantly expanded. Its ability to regulate hematopoietic function and to elevate peripheral blood neutrophils represents one of the most notable advances in understanding this herb, referred to herein as DY. Based on these properties, DYSBP, formulated exclusively with DY as the active ingredient, have been developed. DYSBP are clinically indicated for the treatment of leukopenia and are also used to manage thrombocytopenia, immunodeficiency, and aplastic anemia. Leukopenia, a common hematological disorder characterized by a peripheral white blood cell count below 4.0 × 109/L,99 frequently occurs in cancer patients due to bone marrow suppression caused by chemotherapy- or radiotherapy-induced damage to hematopoietic stem cells.100,101 Meta-analyses indicate that DYSBP exerts significant preventive and therapeutic effects against chemotherapy-induced leukopenia. Prophylactic administration reduces the incidence of leukocyte suppression by 26%. It increases the mean white blood cell count by 1.12 × 109/L, while therapeutic use elevates the average white blood cell count by 1.20 × 109/L and decreases the risk of grade III–IV leukopenia. These effects are associated with the promotion of bone marrow stromal cell proliferation and the upregulation of hematopoietic growth factors and their receptors by sanguisorbin and phenolic constituents. Furthermore, DYSBP can reduce the required clinical dose of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) by an average of 1.57-fold across two chemotherapy cycles.24 Erbing Wang et al integrated network pharmacology, microbiome-metabolome joint analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations to identify hypericin, quercetin-3-galactoside-7-glucoside, and related compounds as the primary bioactive constituents of DYSBP. Hypericin exhibits strong binding stability with core targets such as PIK3CA and SRC. Moreover, DYSBP treatment significantly reduces serum levels of G-CSF and IL-6, reverses 133 differentially expressed metabolites in the spleens of model mice, modulates key metabolic pathways, and restores the abundance of Lactobacillus species diminished by cyclophosphamide to baseline levels. Evidence indicates that DYSBP modulates the splenic metabolic profile in mice, improves intestinal microbiota composition, and exerts therapeutic effects on leukopenia by regulating linoleic acid metabolism and PI3K-Akt signaling. These findings provide robust scientific support for the clinical application of DYSBP.102

Processed of DY

Sanguisorbae Radix Carbonisata is a processed derivative of DY and represents another significant form of clinical application of DY. Over the past millennium, processing methods for DY have evolved to include roasting, stir-frying, slow stewing, as well as vinegar, wine, and carbonization treatments.103 Among these, carbonization remains a widely used method today. According to the 2015 edition of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, the preparation of Sanguisorbae Radix Carbonisata is defined as follows: “Take clean slices of DY, place them in a preheated pan, and stir-fry over high heat until the surface turns dark brown and the interior becomes brownish. Immediately spray with a small amount of water to extinguish any sparks, remove from the heat, and allow to dry”. From a modern pharmacological perspective, carbonization significantly modifies the physical characteristics and therapeutic properties of DY, notably enhancing its astringent, hemostatic, detoxifying, and wound-healing effects, which are closely associated with bioactive constituent transformations during thermal processing.104 Wang et al systematically investigated the compositional differences between DY and Sanguisorbae radix Carbonisata using UHPLC-MS/MS combined with multivariate statistical analysis, and further evaluated their in vitro effects on colon cancer cells via MTT assays and cell migration tests. The results revealed that 29 differential saponins were identified in the carbonized extract, with three compounds disappearing and 48 new compounds formed. Additionally, the levels of di-substituted and mono-substituted saponins decreased, whereas the overall content of aglycones increased. It is hypothesized that high-temperature processing induces chemical transformations such as cleavage of sugar chains, hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds, and ring-opening reactions in saponins, leading to component conversion. These changes suggest that the pharmacological activity of DY is optimized through processing, forming a distinct pharmacological substance system in conjunction with trace elements. In vitro studies, including MTT and wound healing assays, demonstrated that both DY and carbonized extracts could inhibit the proliferation and migration of HCT116 and RKO colon cancer cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with the carbonized extract exhibiting superior inhibitory efficacy. Specifically, it showed a lower half-maximal inhibitory concentration and greater suppression of cell motility across both cell lines.105

Modification and Delivery of Ziyuglycosides for Better Therapeutic Potential

Modification of Ziyuglycosides

To improve the potency of ZYGs, various modifications were introduced, particularly for ZYGII, including a unique carboxylic group. 2D structures of ZYGII functional derivatives are shown in Figure 3. Esterification occurs at the carbohydrate site of ZYGII to obtain ZG02-ME. The study confirmed that ZG02-ME decreased serum glucose, glycated hemoglobin, insulin, leptin, C-peptide, low-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, and cholesterol, which is better than ZYGI in db/db Mice. Additionally, ZG02-ME also improved diabetes mellitus complications in the liver and kidney by decreasing lipid peroxidation.106 In another research, 1,2,3-triazole was integrated into -COOH in ZYGII to synthesize 15 ZYGII derivatives. Among these derivatives, Z-15 (28-O-((1-((4-nitrophenyl)-1H-1,2,3-triazol-4-yl)methyl)-3-O-(α-L-arabinopyranosyl)-19-hyd-roxyursolic acid ester) exhibited prominent anti-cancer function in MCF-7 cells. Z15 inhibited proliferation and migration and promoted mitochondrial pathway apoptosis and autophagy.107

Different Delivery Systems of Ziyuglycosides

Previous studies have suggested that ZYGI has poor solubility and a short half-life which caused limited bioavailability and efficacy, due to its chemical structure. In order to enhance the therapeutic potential of ZYGI, different drug delivery systems were established. Inorganic nanoparticles significantly increase the therapeutic efficacy of drug combinations by reducing dose, enhancing efficacy, preventing resistance development, and decreasing adverse effects, which have become novel nanocarriers nowadays.108 Hong Wang et al synthesized a novel mesoporous silica medication delivery system consisting of ZYGI and anti-viral drug oseltamivir and modified with polydopamine, called ZgI@MSNs-OST@PDA. Under these circumstances, oseltamivir was permitted for rapid release in targeting the killing of the virus, while ZYGI was for slow release in anti-inflammation. ZgI@MSNs-OST@PDA reduced NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome to prevent and treat the potential and continued lung damage and inhibited viral pneumonia in H1N1-infected mice.109 To increase the absorption of ZYGI, the self-microemulsifying drug delivery system was used, which was investigated as a transparent, a surfactant, and a cosurfactant.110 Mixing of Obleique CC497, Tween-20, and Transcutol HP in the ratio of 0.25:0.45:0.30 improved the solubility of ZYGI to 23.93 mg/g. This new mixture increased the stability, release, and absorption of ZYGI and enhanced its leukocyte-increasing effect.111 TPGS are compounds composed of hydrophilic polyethylene glycol and lipophilic tocopherol succinate, which are broadly used to increase the dissolution of hydrophobic drugs and extend the half-life of drugs.112,113 ZYGI was first assembled with liposomes and then enveloped with TPGS to form ZgI-TPGS-liposomes. The results revealed that ZgI-TPGS-liposomes enhanced ZYGI uptake by RAW264.7 cells and enhanced ZYGI release and treatment function on myelosuppression in CTX-induced mice after intravenous injection.114 AuNPs (gold nanoparticles, AuNPs), as a biocompatible and modifiable novel drug delivery system, are also considered as autonomic modulators.115,116 Combining ZYGI and A makes the concentration of ZYGI as high as 160.63 ± 1.35 μg/mg, which indicates that the ZYGI release rate was reduced and the function of promoting haematopoietic stem cells’ autophagy was enhanced, compared with ZYGI alone.117

Discussion

To date, cancer remains a major global public health challenge, posing a significant threat to human life and well-being. Conventional therapeutic approaches, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, are frequently compromised by the emergence of drug resistance and the occurrence of adverse effects, underscoring the critical need for the clinical development of novel and effective anticancer agents.118 For thousands of years, TCM has accumulated extensive theoretical knowledge and substantial clinical experience in the management of tumors. According to TCM theory, the pathogenesis of tumors is fundamentally attributed to an imbalance between Zhengqi and Xieqi, along with the accumulation of toxins, blood stasis, and phlegm.119 The core therapeutic principles of tumors in TCM are considered as Fuzheng (restoring healthy qi) and Quxie (eliminating evil qi), which provide a valuable theoretical framework for identifying anti-cancer bioactive compounds from herbal medicines. The discovery and development of anticancer constituents derived from TCM not only expand the repertoire of potential anticancer drugs but also facilitate the integration of traditional medical wisdom with modern pharmacological research, paving the way for innovative strategies in tumor therapy.

DY has the effects of cooling blood and promoting blood circulation, detoxifying pathogenic toxins and astringing sores. Modern pharmacological studies have shown that DY has pharmacological effects such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, and immune regulation, which fully embodies the treatment principle of TCM of “estoring healthy qi and eliminating evil qi”. Modern pharmacological studies have confirmed that DY and its active components exert multifaceted anticancer effects through multiple mechanisms.120 DY enhances the anti-tumor immune response and improves the tumor immune microenvironment by regulating the function of immune cells, such as T lymphocytes, macrophages, and natural killer cells, consistent with the theory of “restoring healthy qi” in TCM. In regulating cell proliferation and metabolism, DY targets cyclins, cyclin-dependent kinases, and other regulatory factors to induce the cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 or G2/M, thereby blocking the uncontrolled proliferation and metastasis of tumor cells. The process by which DY and its active components inhibit tumor cell invasion and metastasis involves targeting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and down-regulating the expression of EMT-related transcription factors and MMP to maintain the epithelial phenotype of tumor cells. In addition, DY can enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to cell death signals by regulating autophagic flux in tumor cells, the regulatory balance of protective autophagy and excessive autophagy in tumor cells. Finally, DY can regulate the expression of Bcl-2 family proteins, change the mitochondrial membrane potential, and promote the release of cytochrome c, thus initiating the caspase cascade to induce the apoptosis of tumor cells to play a role in “eliminating the pathogenic factors”. It is worth noting that the processed product of DY, Sanguisorbae radix Carbonisata, also retains significant anti-tumor activity, which verifies the scientific validity of the TCM processing theory. In addition, the approved DY-derived drug DYSBP can effectively treat leukopenia induced by chemotherapy or radiotherapy, highlighting the potential of DY to reverse treatment-related side effects and improve the efficacy of comprehensive treatment of cancer.

Despite the promising antitumor potential of DY and its active components, the clinical translation of DY still faces some challenges. The main active components of DY are saponins, such as ZYGI and ZYGII. Due to their inherent physicochemical properties, including poor water solubility and low membrane permeability, the oral bioavailability of these saponins is low, which limits their in vivo efficacy and clinical applications.121 Although it has been explored, further optimization is needed to improve its stability in biological systems. Some formulation strategies, such as nanocellulose and pre-drug modification, are needed to improve its drug delivery efficiency, prolong the circulation time in vivo, and achieve targeted drug delivery to tumor tissues. In addition, the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) characteristics of DY and its saponins in vivo have not been fully clarified, and systematic pharmacokinetic studies are needed to provide a scientific basis for the design of drug administration regimens and clinical rational use of drugs. Another critical gap in current research is the insufficient toxicological data on DY and its components. For the development of anticancer drugs, comprehensive toxicity evaluation is a necessary condition to ensure the safety of drugs, including acute toxicity, subacute/subchronic toxicity, long-term toxicity, genotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity.122 At present, most studies on DY are focused on pharmacodynamics, while systemic toxicology is relatively rare. Future studies should prioritize the use of in vitro cell and in vivo animal models for systematic toxicologic evaluation to elucidate the toxicity characteristics of DY and its components, identify potential toxicity targets, and establish effective toxicity control strategies, thereby providing reliable safety evidence for its development as an anticancer drug.

Conclusion

Cancer continues to be the leading cause of global mortality and a major threat to public health security, with its incidence and mortality remaining high worldwide. TCM holds that the occurrence and progression of tumors are closely related to the imbalance between Zhengqi and Xieqi, and the core principle of tumor treatment is to strengthen Zhengqi and eliminate Xieqi, which provides a unique theoretical basis and practical strategy for cancer intervention.

By integrating the research results of multiple studies, this review systematically indicates that DY and its various active components have significant and extensive anti-cancer effects. Specifically, DY extracts, ZYGI, ZYGII, tannins, polysaccharides and other components can inhibit tumor cell proliferation, induce apoptosis and autophagy, block cell cycle progression, suppress tumor invasion and metastasis, and regulate the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple signaling pathways including Wnt/β‑catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK and NF‑κB. These findings enrich the molecular mechanism research of anticancer agents derived from natural medicines, Which fully embodies the TCM therapeutic concept of promoting and maintaining the circulation of Zhengqi, and eliminating Xieqi. In addition, DYSBP, a clinical preparation developed from DY, can effectively alleviate leukopenia caused by chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

However, several key scientific questions remain to be systematically explored. First of all, existing studies mainly focus on in vitro cell experiments, while in vivo verification and clinical evidence are relatively insufficient. Second, the direct targets of DY and its active components against tumors remain unclear, ignoring the complex cross-interactions between molecular networks mediated by its active components, which limits the explanation of its mechanism of action as well as further clinical translation studies. In addition, although DY and its active components have shown superior anti-tumor activities in a variety of cancer types, the specific mechanisms of anti-tumor as well as the common core signaling pathways still need to be systematically investigated and compared across cancers. Therefore, future studies should identify the direct molecular targets of DY and its active components, and systematically reveal its anti-tumor molecular network and global regulatory characteristics by combining single-cell and multi-omics technologies. For active ingredients such as ZYGs, the structure-activity relationship should be explored and the drug delivery system should be optimized to give full play to the unique advantages of DY in anti-tumor, lay a solid theoretical foundation for mechanism research and structure optimization, and accelerate the transformation and application of DY in cancer therapy.
