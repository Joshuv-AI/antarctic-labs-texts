Atf3 Deficiency Promotes Mesodermal Commitment and Enhances Endothelial Differentiation in Embryonic Stem Cells
Authors: Zongyue Jiang, Lide Su, Cuiting Chen, Ronghai He, LiLi Jiang, Yanyan Shu, Dandan Dai, Man Wu, Ailin Guo, Jianlei Liu, Suhuan Liu, Zonghong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Ischemic diseases have become a major threat to global health, with endothelial cell (EC) damage closely associated with their pathogenesis and progression. Cell therapies targeting endothelial repair have thus become a treatment approach of great interest, yet the procurement of clinically approved ECs for these applications has not been fully established. Modulating the expression of Atf3 (activating transcription factor 3) represents a potential strategy for deriving ECs from stem cells; however, its precise function in the development and differentiation of ECs from stem cells remains elusive. In the present study, we sought to elucidate the potential role of Atf3 in the differentiation of embryonic stem cells into ECs.METHODS:CRISPR-Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat–associated 9) system was used to knockout Atf3 (Atf3KO [Atf3 knockout]) in mouse embryonic stem cells. EC differentiation was initially induced using the hanging drop method to promote embryoid bodies formation, followed by embryoid bodies attachment onto culture slides. The expression changes of EC markers during differentiation were assessed by RNA sequencing, Western blotting, immunofluorescence staining, flow cytometry, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Functional comparisons of differentiated ECs were performed by assessing LDL (low-density lipoprotein) uptake and NO production. Potential molecular mechanisms were further explored via bioinformatic analysis of RNA sequencing data.RESULTS:Atf3KO led to a significant upregulation in the expression levels of progenitor and mesoderm cell markers on days 3 and 6 of differentiation. By day 9, the expression of mature EC markers also exhibited a notable increase. Moreover, Atf3KO enhanced the functional properties of differentiated Atf3KO ECs. In addition, our findings revealed that the activation of the Rap1 (Ras-related protein 1) signaling pathway, triggered by Atf3KO, contributed to ECs development and maturation.CONCLUSIONS:Atf3KO directs embryonic stem cells toward the mesodermal lineage and activates the Rap1 signaling pathway, thereby promoting ECs development. These findings highlight a key role of Atf3 in regulating early stage of vascular endothelial development.

Regenerative medicine aims to repair damaged tissues, either through cell replacement therapy or other regeneration strategies, thereby facilitating the restoration of normal organ function. With respect to cardiovascular diseases, ischemia is a prominent pathology, entailing the interruption of oxygenated blood delivery to the tissues.1 One approach to treat ischemia, therefore, is to promote angiogenesis within the existing vascular network, leading to the reestablishment of proper blood flow, and subsequently, the alleviation of ischemic symptoms.2 Within these blood vessels, a single layer of endothelial cells (ECs) forms their innermost layers, serving as a natural barrier between blood and surrounding tissues. Consequently, these cells are an indispensable component for blood vessel formation, and significantly affect vessel structure and function via finely regulated interactions with mural cells, such as smooth muscle cells and pericytes.3,4 Consequently, providing a sufficient supply of ECs has been considered an essential approach for cell therapy–based treatment of ischemic diseases; however, multiple hurdles are present for obtaining and purifying the required number of ECs directly from patients.

To overcome these hurdles, embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells have become critical sources for ECs generation, due to their unlimited self-renewal capabilities and multipotency.5 Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) possess the unique capacity to differentiate into any cell type derived from the 3 germ layers. This pluripotent property, in turn, offers copious possibilities for treating various refractory diseases in the field of regenerative medicine.6,7 This in vitro simulation of embryonic development involves ESCs forming 3-dimensional aggregates known as embryoid bodies (EBs), which subsequently differentiate into various specialized cell types. ECs originate from the mesoderm germ layer, where multipotent vascular progenitor cells could differentiate into either hematopoietic or ECs.8,9 ECs differentiation is tightly and precisely regulated, a characteristic that has facilitated the development of multiple ECs differentiation protocols. However, these protocols frequently yield highly heterogeneous cell populations, which fail to meet the stringent criteria for clinical application.10–12 Therefore, developing strategies to obtain clinical-grade homogeneous ECs is critical for facilitating their application in cell therapies. These strategies include identifying the key molecules and their regulatory mechanisms governing ESCs differentiation into mesodermal and subsequent endothelial lineages. This would optimize differentiation conditions to improve the purity and maturity of ESC-derived ECs, thereby facilitating their clinical application.13

A key molecule that modulates ECs development is AP-1 (activating protein-1), a transcription factor complex involved in regulating multiple cell cycle–related processes, including proliferation, apoptosis, senescence, differentiation, and migration, as well as immune and inflammatory responses.14,15 AP-1 comprises proteins from the Fos (encoding AP-1 component c-Fos), Jun (Jun proto-oncogene), ATF (activating transcription factor), and MAF (musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene) protein families, which bind to specific DNA sites. In particular, one ATF family member, Atf3, exhibits significantly upregulated expression under external stress, reflecting its capacity to respond and adapt to environmental changes.16,17 It was shown that Atf3 is linked to a protein network that can be activated by TGRL (triglyceride-rich lipoprotein) metabolites, which in turn trigger vascular cell apoptosis and inflammatory responses that aggravate vascular damage.18 In addition, Atf3 is associated with homocysteinemia-related ECs dysfunction, in which the metabolite homocysteine could activate the JNK/SAPK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase/stress-activated protein kinase) signaling pathway to induce Atf3 expression in ECs.19 This is further supported by observations showing that Atf3, as well as its T4-5 (splice isoforms ATF3-T4 and ATF3-T5) variants, significantly promotes brain microvascular ECs apoptosis and oxidative stress in a lipotoxic environment, thereby reinforcing the association between Atf3 activity and abnormal lipid metabolism.20 Atf3 upregulation has also been found to promote endothelial-to-mesenchymal transformation, via enhanced TGF (transforming growth factor)-β2/Smad (sma and mad-related signal transducer) signaling, triggered by Sema7A (semaphorin 7A).21 Collectively, these findings indicate that Atf3 plays a significant role in ECs development and associated pathophysiological processes, such as vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis, although the specific molecular mechanisms remain largely unclear.22

In this study, we aimed to clarify the specific roles of Atf3 in ECs differentiation from ESCs. First, key genes were identified via analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data sets, followed by Atf3KO (Atf3 knockout) in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) using CRISPR-Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat–associated 9) gene editing.23 EBs formation was subsequently induced by culturing these mESCs in suspension with an EC-specific differentiation medium for 6 days, and then transferred into an adherent culture system from day 7 to day 9. We found that Atf3 expression levels exhibited an upward trend during endothelial maturation, while Atf3KO promoted differentiation toward both mesodermal and endothelial lineages. Therefore, suppressing Atf3 expression could foster ECs differentiation from ESCs, rendering it an attractive target for cell therapies.

Materials and Methods

All RNA-seq data have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE306049). Detailed information on key reagents is provided in the Major Resources Table in the Supplemental Material. The data supporting the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Single-Cell RNA-Seq Data and Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data analysis was performed using data set GSE216970 from the Gene Expression Omnibus database.24 Initial data processing and visualization were conducted in RStudio using the Seurat package (v5.2.1). Quality control filters retained cells with mitochondrial gene counts <20% and 200 to 6000 unique genes. Subsequent data processing included normalization (NormalizeData), variable feature identification (FindVariableFeatures), dimensionality reduction (RunPCA, RunUMAP), and marker gene detection (FindAllMarkers) using Seurat functions.

After the data set import, pseudotime trajectory analysis was initiated by constructing a CellDataSet object in RStudio. Data normalization and preprocessing were executed using Monocle3 (v1.3.7) functions, followed by dimensionality reduction via uniform manifold approximation and projection or principal component analysis. Cells were subsequently clustered, and highly variable genes were selected for trajectory reconstruction. Pseudotime trajectories were built using these selected genes, with trajectory-initiating cells manually designated based on their differentiation status. Spatial autocorrelation analysis was then performed on the principal graph using Moran I statistic (graph_test() function; neighbor_graph=“principal_graph,” cores=6), testing the null hypothesis that gene expression is randomly distributed along the trajectories. Genes exhibiting significant spatial dependency (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted Q value <0.001) were retained as trajectory-dependent regulators. Finally, key regulatory genes were identified through differential expression analysis across distinct cellular states or branches.

mESC Culture, Atf3KO, and In Vitro Differentiation From EBs

mESCs (AisenGene Bioscience) were cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2, in a high-glucose DMEM (Hyclone), containing 15% FBS, 100× penicillin-streptomycin (BasalMedia), 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate (GIBCO), 1 mmol/L nonessential amino acids (GIBCO), 1 mM GlutaMAX (GIBCO), 0.1 mmol/L β-mercaptoethanol (GIBCO), 1000 U/mL LIF (leukemia inhibitory factor; Millipore), as well as 3 µM CHIR99021 and 1 µM PD0325901 (Sigma). Cells were maintained in an undifferentiated state by daily replacement with fresh medium and passaging every 3 days.

Atf3KO was achieved using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, in which mESCs were transfected with a pair of PX330-sgRNA-Cas9 (plasmid X330 expressing single guide RNA and Cas9) plasmids (AisenGene Bioscience), expressing L- and R-sgRNA, and targeting both upstream and downstream regions of the Atf3 exon, alongside a donor vector PGK-Puro-BPA (phosphoGlycerate kinase promoter-puromycin resistance gene-biotin porter acceptor tag) plasmid (AisenGene Bioscience), carrying a puromycin resistance gene and homologous arms upstream (5arm) and downstream (3arm) of Atf3, for recombination. This resulted in Atf3 gene deletion, involving homologous recombination repair. After puromycin was applied to remove un-transfected mESCs, homozygous knockout cell lines were identified by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Table S1 provides the primer sequences for L- and R-sgRNA (left and right single guide RNA), as well as 5 arm and 3 arm.

Both wild-type (WT) and Atf3KO mESCs were induced to form EBs, in which adherent mESCs were dissociated into single cells with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (GIBCO); these cells were resuspended in a differentiation medium, comprising high-glucose DMEM (Hyclone) with 20% FBS, 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate (GIBCO), 1 mmol/L nonessential amino acids (GIBCO), 1 mmol/L GlutaMAX (GIBCO), 0.1 mmol/L β-mercaptoethanol (GIBCO), and 100× penicillin-streptomycin solution (BasalMedia). The hanging drop culture method was subsequently applied25,26; in brief, hanging drops of mESCs (600 cells/30 µL differentiation medium) were dropped onto the lid of a 10 cm Petri dish, which was placed back onto the dish, containing 10 mL PBS (BasalMedia), and cultured for 6 days. Afterward, uniformly sized EBs that had formed were transferred to a 12-well plate, with 2 drops of differentiation medium per well. Differentiation was continued for 3 days (days 7–9) under conditions of 5% CO2 and 37 °C.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA from mESCs on days 0, 3, 6, and 9 of differentiation was extracted using a FastPure RNA rapid extraction kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA (complementary DNA) using a HiScript IV cDNA reverse transcription kit. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was performed using the ChamQ Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (all from Vazyzme, China). Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene, actin, and quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers are listed in Table S2.

Immunofluorescence Staining and Flow Cytometry

On day 9 of differentiation, adherent WT and Atf3KO EBs grown on chamber slides were washed 3× (5 minutes each time) with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 minutes, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes, and blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 minutes. Cells were then incubated with the following antibodies: Cd31 (3528S; 1:300; Cell Signaling) and Atf3 (ab254268; 1:300; Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, then incubated with the Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (ab150116; 1:500; Abcam) at room temperature for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Coverslips were mounted onto the chamber slides, and cells were visualized under confocal microscopy (LSM 880, Zeiss).

For flow cytometry analysis, the same procedure as used for immunofluorescence staining was followed. Primary antibodies against Cd31 (562939, 1:300), Cdh5 (cadherin 5; 562242, 1:300, both from BD Biosciences) were utilized. Subsequent data acquisition was performed using a flow cytometer (Cytoflex LX, Beckman Coulter), and analysis was conducted with FlowJo_v10.8.1_CL software.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from mESCs using RIPA (radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer) lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were quantified with a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher). Proteins were denatured by boiling in a loading buffer for 10 minutes. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 hour at room temperature. The membranes were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against Cd31 (platelet/endothelial cell adhesion molecule 1; 3528S; Cell Signaling) and Rap1 (ras-related protein 1; 2399S; Cell Signaling) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein expression was detected using an ECL (enhanced chemiluminescence) detection reagent, and bands were visualized using a Qinxiang imaging system (Clinx Science Instruments, Co, Ltd). Protein expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping proteins Gapdh (60004-1-ig; Proteintech) and β-tubulin (10094-1-AP; Proteintech).

Dil-Ac-LDL Uptake Assay

Cells were incubated with Dil-Ac-LDL (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine acetylated low-density lipoprotein; 20–50 µg/mL) in growth medium at 37 °C for 4 hours. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were washed repeatedly with probe-free medium to remove excess dye. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope equipped with a rhodamine filter set, with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 549 nm/565 nm.

Detection of NO

The culture medium was removed, and an appropriate volume of the prepared DAF-FM DA (4-amino-5-methylamino-2′,7′-difluorofluorescein diacetate) staining solution was added to completely cover the cells. The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 20 minutes in a humidified incubator. After incubation, the cells were washed 3× with PBS (pH 7.4) to remove any residual extracellular DAF-FM DA. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope.

RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from mESCs on days 0, 3, 6, and 9 of differentiation using TriZol reagent, collected into 5 mL centrifuge tubes, and high-throughput RNA-seq was carried out by HaploX Biotechnology Co to establish an RNA-seq library. The original image files obtained were subjected to base identification and error filtering to obtain sequencing data. Filtered clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2 software to identify uniquely mapped reads. Based on those unique reads, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (log fold change>1, adjusted P<0.05), followed by cluster analysis and plots to identify sample differences. Pathways and processes enriched with DEGs were analyzed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, Gene Ontology, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses, with statistical significance determined by Fisher exact test.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0.0). Data are presented as mean±SEM. Independent 2-group comparisons were conducted using Student t test for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed data sets. For multigroup comparisons, 1-way or 2-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey honestly significant difference post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Results

Atf3 Regulates the Differentiation of mESCs Into ECs

We analyzed a single-cell RNA sequencing data set (GSE216970) encompassing key stages of mouse vascular development (E9.5, E12.5, and E15.5), which was retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. Dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed to classify cells based on endothelial markers, including Cd31, Cd34, Cdh5, Flt1 (fms-like tyrosine kinase 1), and Kdr (kinase insert domain receptor27; Figure S1A), yielding 2 major populations: ECs and non-ECs (Figure 1A). Notably, Atf3 expression was significantly enriched in ECs (Figure S1B). Subsequent hierarchical clustering of the ECs population identified 17 distinct subclusters (Figure 1B). Based on the expression patterns of endothelial markers Vwf (von willebrand factor), Cd31, S1pr1 (sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1), and Nos3 (nitric oxide synthase 3) in endothelial subpopulations, EC2 (endothelial cell cluster 2) was identified as the most developmentally immature cluster within the EC lineage (Figure S1C) and designated as the root node for subsequent pseudotime trajectory reconstruction (Figure 1C). The trajectory revealed a progressive increase in Atf3 expression during endothelial maturation (Figure 1D). We also assessed Atf3 expression levels in WT cells at differentiation days 0, 3, 6, and 9 using RT-qPCR. This analysis revealed an initial decreasing trend in Atf3 expression during the earliest phase of differentiation, followed by a stepwise increase during mesoderm and early endothelial development (Figure 1E), indicating dynamic changes in Atf3 expression throughout the differentiation process. To further elucidate the role of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation, we generated an Atf3 knockout mESCs using CRISPR-Cas9 targeting all 3 exons (Figure 1F). Knockout efficiency was confirmed via Sanger sequencing, PCR, RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence (Figures 1G; Figure S2A through S2D). During maintenance culture, both WT and Atf3KO mESCs exhibited similar spherical morphologies (Figure S3A), and CCK8 (cell counting kit-) assays showed that Atf3 deletion did not affect cell viability (Figure S3B). For endothelial induction, we used the classical hanging drop method to mimic early embryogenesis. By day 3, no significant differences in EB morphology or size were observed between WT and knockout groups. On day 6, the proportion of spherical EBs was comparable between groups (Figure S3C). Continued culture revealed morphological divergence by day 9: Atf3KO cells displayed a radially spreading, tree-like phenotype, whereas WT cells maintained a compact structure (Figure 1H). RT-qPCR analysis of pluripotency markers (Oct4 [octamer-binding transcription factor 4], Sox2 [SRY-box transcription factor 2], Nanog [nanog homeobox], and Esrrb [estrogen-related receptor beta]) on days 0, 3, and 6 showed no significant differences between WT and knockout cells (Figure 1I).28 These findings confirm the successful knockout of Atf3 and underscore its regulatory role in endothelial differentiation of mESCs.

Atf3KO Promotes Mesodermal Differentiation

mESCs undergo initial differentiation into the 3 germ layers—endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm29—which subsequently give rise to all cell types in the body. ECs are primarily derived from the mesoderm. To further evaluate whether Atf3 knockout affects mESC differentiation and lineage specification, we assessed the expression levels of representative mesodermal markers. On days 3 and 6 of differentiation, the early core mesodermal markers T, Mixl1 (mix paired-like homeobox 1), and Eomes (eomesodermin) were significantly upregulated in the Atf3KO group compared with the WT group (Figure 2A). In addition, the lateral plate mesoderm marker Hand1 (heart and neural crest derivatives expressed 1) and the cardiac mesoderm marker Mesp1 (mesoderm posterior bHLH transcription factor 1) showed significantly higher expression levels in the knockout group on day 6 (Figure 2B and 2C). The paraxial mesoderm marker Tbx6 (T-box transcription factor 6) was also significantly elevated in the knockout group on both days 3 and 6 (Figure 2D). Moreover, Atf3 deletion markedly enhanced the expression of the intermediate mesoderm marker Pax2 (paired box 2) on day 3, but significantly suppressed its expression on day 6 (Figure 2E). Regarding endodermal differentiation, the expression of Foxa2 (forkhead box A2) was significantly higher in the Atf3KO group than in the WT group on day 6. In contrast, the expression of another endodermal marker, Sox17 (SRY-box transcription factor 17), remained unchanged between groups at both time points, which is consistent with previous reports30 (Figure 2F). For ectodermal differentiation, only Sox1 (SRY-box transcription factor 1) exhibited a significant difference, with reduced expression in Atf3KO cells on day 3. No significant differences were observed for Pax6 (paired box 6) or Nestin on either day 3 or 6, nor for Sox1 on day 631 (Figure 2G). Collectively, these findings indicate that Atf3 knockout promotes mesodermal differentiation while exerting limited effects on endodermal and ectodermal lineages.

Atf3KO Promotes Mesodermal Differentiation Into EC Lineages

We then further examined whether Atf3KO had any impact on endothelial lineage differentiation. We found that on days 3, 6, and 9, the expression levels for endothelial progenitor markers Vegfr2 (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2), Cd34, and Etv2 (ets variant transcription factor 2)32–34 were significantly higher in Atf3KO cells compared with WT cells (Figure 3A). This was further supported by examining endothelial marker expression levels,12,27,35 where Cd31, Enos, Tie2 (TEK receptor tyrosine kinase), and Flt1 generally showed increased expression in Atf3KO compared with WT cells across days 3 to 9 of differentiation (Figure 3B). In addition, on days 6 and 9 of differentiation, Cdh5 and Vwf levels were significantly higher in Atf3KO versus WT cells (Figure 3C). This was further confirmed by Western blot (Figure 3F) and immunofluorescence staining (Figure 3D and 3E) of Cd31 on days 6 and 9 of differentiation, when Atf3KO cells exhibited significantly higher expression levels than WT cells. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis revealed that on day 9, the expression levels of both Cd31 and Cdh5 expression were significantly higher in Atf3KO cells (21.4% and 22.3%, respectively) compared with WT cells (8.1% and 5.75%, respectively; Figure 3F); this corresponded with higher mean fluorescence intensities for both Cd31 and Cdh5 in Atf3KO cells (Figure 3G and 3H). To avoid experimental specificity, we knocked out the Atf3 gene in mESCs with an OG2 (Oct4-GFP 2) background and found that both endothelial progenitor and endothelial-specific marker genes were significantly upregulated compared with the WT group (Figure S4A through S4C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Atf3KO promoted endothelial differentiation from days 0 to 9 of differentiation.

Atf3KO Enhances the Functional Maturation of Differentiated ECs

To evaluate the functional capacity of differentiated ECs, we assessed NO production in Atf3KO and WT cells on day 9 of differentiation. Atf3KO-derived ECs exhibited significantly higher NO production compared with those from WT cells. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a broader distribution of NO-producing cells in the knockout group (Figure 4A), and quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity confirmed a significant increase in NO levels in the knockout group relative to WT group (Figure 4C). To further validate endothelial functionality, we examined the uptake of LDL (low-density lipoprotein) in Atf3KO and WT cells on day 9 of differentiation. Results showed that Atf3KO-derived ECs had a significantly greater capacity for LDL uptake compared with WT cells. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a broader LDL uptake area in the knockout group (Figure 4B), and fluorescence intensity quantification again indicated a significantly higher level in knockout cells relative to WT cells (Figure 4D). Collectively, these findings suggest that Atf3 deficiency not only promotes endothelial differentiation but also enhances the functional properties of the resulting ECs.

RNA-Seq Analysis Reveals That Atf3KO Leads to the Upregulation of Endothelial-Related Signaling Pathways Atf3KO

To gain insights into the mechanisms by which Atf3 regulates endothelial differentiation, we collected Atf3KO and WT cells at days 0, 3, 6, and 9 and subjected them to RNA-seq. We identified 286 and 87 DEGs at days 0 and 3, respectively, while 853 and 1026 DEGs were identified at days 6 and 9 (Figure 5A). Endothelial marker genes, including Cd31, Cd34, Cdh5, Tie1, Tie2, and Mcam, were significantly upregulated in the knockout group at both the late mesodermal stage (day 6) and the early endothelial differentiation stage (day 9; Figure 5D). These findings indicate that Atf3KO regulates mesoderm development and promotes EC differentiation.

We also conducted Gene Ontology enrichment analysis on DEGs at days 6 and 9 of differentiation. At day 6, these DEGs were enriched for Notch signaling pathway, mesoderm development, EC proliferation and migration, as well as angiogenesis. At day 9, in addition to angiogenesis, the enriched DEGs shift to heart morphogenesis and blood circulation (Figure 5B and 5C). All these processes are closely related to endothelial proliferation, differentiation, and development. This was further supported by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analysis, which identified the 10 most significant signaling pathways associated with the DEGs. At day 6 of differentiation, the most prominent pathways were steroid biosynthesis, Hedgehog signaling, and basal cell carcinoma pathways (Figure S5A), whereas at day 9, the enriched pathways shift to dilated and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, as well as cardiac muscle contraction signaling pathways (Figure S5B). These findings were further validated by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, which revealed that at day 9 of differentiation, DEGs associated with angiogenesis, vasculogenesis, EC migration, and blood vessel EC migration were all upregulated (Figure 5E). In addition, the Notch, Apelin, and Rap1 signaling pathways, along with vascular smooth muscle contraction, were all upregulated (Figure 5F). All these processes were related to endothelial differentiation and development. Collectively, these results demonstrate that genes positively enriched following Atf3 knockout are primarily associated with endothelial differentiation, migration, and angiogenesis.

Atf3KO Promotes ECs Development by Regulating the Rap1 Signaling Pathway

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis enrichment analysis revealed activation of the Rap1 signaling pathway after Atf3KO at day 9 of endothelial differentiation (Figure 6A). RT-qPCR results indicated that, compared with the WT group, the expression of Rap1a and Rap1b—key genes in Rap1 signaling pathway—showed an upward trend in the Atf3KO group (Figure 6B). To investigate whether Atf3 deficiency affects endothelial differentiation and development via modulation of the Rap1 signaling pathway, we treated both Atf3KO and WT groups with the Rap1-specific inhibitor ESI-09 and activator 8-pCPT-2′-O-Me-cAMP-AM, starting at the initiation of differentiation. Morphological observation of suspended and adherent cells at days 3 and 6 of differentiation showed no apparent differences. However, as differentiation progressed, EBs treated with the activator exhibited a phenotype similar to the Atf3KO group on adhesion, characterized by denser and more extensive outgrowth. Conversely, EBs treated with the inhibitor exhibited delayed differentiation, with morphology more closely resembling the WT group (Figure S6A). To validate the efficacy of the Rap1 activator and inhibitor, we performed RT-qPCR analysis. Interestingly, in activator-treated WT cells, Epac1 and Epac2 gene expression decreased, whereas Rap1a and Rap1b expression was significantly upregulated. Conversely, inhibitor treatment led to downregulation of all tested genes (Figure S7A and S7B). Although the Rap1 activator/inhibitor does not directly regulate Epac1/2 transcription, the activator binds to Epac (exchange protein directly activated by cAMP) protein to activate the downstream Rap1 pathway. This binding may lead to excessive consumption of Epac protein, potentially triggering a translational compensation mechanism for Epac mRNA and ultimately manifesting as decreased gene expression levels. Western blot analysis at day 9 of differentiation showed that the expression of Rap1 protein and the endothelial marker Cd31 protein was upregulated in the Atf3KO group compared with the WT group. After treating the WT group with the activator, the expression of Rap1 and Cd31 proteins was significantly increased. Conversely, treatment of the Atf3KO group with Rap1 inhibitor significantly downregulated the expression of both proteins (Figure 6C through 6E). These results demonstrate that activating the Rap1 signaling pathway in WT cells promotes endothelial differentiation, whereas inhibiting the Rap1 pathway in Atf3KO cells suppresses endothelial development. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that inhibiting the Rap1 signaling pathway in the Atf3KO group significantly reduced the expression of endothelial markers, such as Cd31, and weakened intercellular junctions. In contrast, activating the pathway enhanced the integrity of EC-cell junctions (Figure 6F). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity also showed that pathway activation significantly upregulated Cd31 fluorescence intensity, whereas inhibition significantly downregulated it (Figure 6G). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that activator treatment tended to upregulate key Rap1 downstream genes involved in endothelial development (Vegfr2, Cdh5, Vwf, and Tie2), whereas inhibitor treatment induced their downregulation (Figure 6H). In summary, utilizing both gain-of-function and loss-of-function approaches (activator/inhibitor treatment), we demonstrate that Atf3 deletion promotes endothelial development by activating the Rap1 signaling pathway during the transition from late mesoderm to early endothelial differentiation.

Discussion

Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of Atf3 in vascular regeneration and remodeling,36–38 positioning it as a key transcription factor during embryonic development. However, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation remain poorly understood. In this study, we investigated the function of Atf3 using a mESC-based endothelial differentiation model. Our findings reveal the critical role of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation and development: it inhibits differentiation during the early stages of mesoderm specification and endothelial lineage commitment, and also suppresses the functional maturation of ECs. This discovery provides valuable insights into the efficient induction of mESCs into mature ECs, which holds significant potential for cardiovascular disease therapy. By modulating a single transcription factor to guide stem cell differentiation toward mature ECs, this approach could have profound implications for both basic research and clinical applications.

In recent years, the use of inducible TFs (transcription factors) to control cell lineage differentiation has gained momentum, offering the advantage of precise temporal control over TF expression. For instance, Lee et al39 demonstrated that transient activation of NKX3.1 (NK3 homeobox 1) could rapidly drive the differentiation of human mesodermal progenitor cells into functional stem cell–derived vascular progenitor cells. Similarly, Wang et al40 achieved efficient differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells into ECs by regulating ETV2 expression via mRNA modification. The major advantage of using inducible TFs lies in their ability to simultaneously regulate multiple cell lineages in a controlled manner, thereby generating different cell types. For example, Ng et al41 used transient activation of ATOH1 (atonal homolog 1) and SOX9 (SRY-box transcription factor 9) to drive the differentiation of induced stem cells into neurons and oligodendrocytes, whereas Skylar-Scott et al42 used transient activation of ETV2 and NGN1 (neurogenin 1) to induce differentiation into both ECs and neurons. Our study uncovers the regulatory role of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation, offering a new perspective on mechanisms underlying endothelial differentiation and cellular fate decisions.

Stem cell–derived ECs hold tremendous potential for treating cardiovascular diseases. However, their clinical application is hindered by several challenges. First, the efficiency and safety of EC induction remain inconsistent, with many protocols leading to genetic and epigenetic alterations.43 Second, immune rejection remains a major risk when these allogenic ECs are transplanted.44 Third, stem cell transplantation may disrupt the host microenvironment, potentially increasing the risk of carcinogenesis.45 Therefore, identifying key regulators of endothelial differentiation is critical. The AP-1 transcription factor family, comprising Jun (c-Jun, JunB, JunD), Fos (c-Fos, FosB, Fra-1, Fra-2), and ATF (ATF-2, ATF-3, ATF-4, ATFa), plays a pivotal role in vascular formation and development. While the roles of Jun and Fos in vascular development and maturation have been extensively studied,46,47 the role of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation remains unclear. Studies have shown that in zebrafish, Atf3 acts as an essential developmental regulator of cardiac and hematopoietic progenitor cells in the mesoderm, coordinating their differentiation by regulating glucose metabolism.48 In this study, we explored the role of Atf3 in mesodermal and endothelial differentiation using a mESC-based model. Our results reveal that Atf3 plays a role in endothelial differentiation. This finding not only opens new avenues for clinical applications of stem cell–derived ECs but also deepens our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying endothelial differentiation.

The differentiation and development of ECs are regulated by multitude of transcription factors.49 By analyzing the single-cell RNA-seq data set GSE216970, which captures key stages of endothelial development in mice, we found that the transcription factor Atf3 exhibits a progressively increasing expression trend during endothelial differentiation. Furthermore, Atf3 expression was highest in EC clusters compared with other cell populations, suggesting tit may play a critical regulatory role in endothelial development, though its precise function remains unclear. Using an Atf3KO mESC model, we demonstrated that Atf3 functions as a negative regulator essential for the early stages of mesoderm and endothelial lineage differentiation. Loss of Atf3 not only promoted overall mesodermal development but also significantly influenced the specification of mesodermal sublineages. Mesoderm is primarily subdivided into 3 subsets, including the lateral plate mesoderm, paraxial mesoderm, and intermediate mesoderm, which give rise to the cardiovascular system, coelomic mesothelium, and limb mesenchyme;50 somites, which eventually differentiate into skeletal muscle, dermis, and axial skeleton,51 and the urogenital system, including the kidney and gonads.52 In our study, Atf3KO significantly upregulated the expression of the lateral plate mesoderm marker Hand1 and the cardiac mesoderm marker Mesp1 on day 6 of differentiation. With respect to the paraxial mesoderm, Tbx6 expression was markedly elevated in the knockout group on both day 3 and day 6, indicating enhanced development of this lineage. Interestingly, the expression of the intermediate mesoderm marker Pax2 was significantly upregulated on day 3, but notably downregulated by day 6, suggesting a transient promotive effect followed by suppression. Although Atf3 deficiency exerted inhibitory effects on certain mesodermal sublineages, its overall impact was predominantly stimulatory, especially on mesodermal lineages associated with cardiovascular development.

Transcriptional profiling revealed a significant upregulation of endothelial-specific markers (such as Cd31, Cd34, Cdh5, Tie1, Tie2) in Atf3KO cells compared with control cells during the late mesodermal and early endothelial differentiation stages. In addition, Atf3-deficient mESCs exhibited significant upregulation of several signaling pathways associated with endothelial differentiation, including the Notch, Apelin, and Rap1 pathways. The Notch signaling pathway is involved in critical processes such as tip-stalk cell specification, arteriovenous differentiation, vascular stabilization, and maturation.53–55 The Apelin signaling pathway regulates EC proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis,56,57 whereas the Rap1 signaling pathway also positively influences angiogenesis and development.58 These findings suggest that Atf3 may inhibit endothelial differentiation by suppressing the activation of these key signaling pathways. Our results not only offer valuable insights into the role of Atf3 in endothelial differentiation but also highlight potential therapeutic targets for the treatment of vascular diseases.

Based on our transcriptomic analysis, we further validated key signaling pathways potentially involved in endothelial development and identified the upregulation of core genes within the Rap1 signaling pathway. Western blot analysis confirmed that Atf3 deficiency led to increased Rap1 protein expression on day 9 of differentiation. Rap1 is a small GTPase known to play an essential regulatory role in angiogenesis, as well as in EC migration and adhesion. The Rap1 signaling pathway exerts its regulatory function by activating a cascade of downstream effectors, including Rap1a and Rap1b, which influence cellular adhesion, morphology, and motility, thereby promoting vascular development and stability.59 Transcriptomic data revealed that Atf3KO specifically upregulated the Rap1 signaling pathway on day 9 of differentiation, a critical stage for the transition from mesoderm to endothelial lineage. The upregulation of Rap1 suggests that the Rap1 signaling pathway may play a pivotal role in mediating the changes in endothelial development induced by Atf3 deficiency. To further investigate the role of the Rap1 signaling pathway in endothelial development, we administered both Rap1 pathway agonists and inhibitors to the cells starting at the early stages of differentiation. These compounds function by promoting the conversion of Rap1-GDP to Rap1-GTP, thus activating the Rap1 signaling axis, although they typically do not directly increase Rap1 protein levels. Interestingly, we observed that by day 9, both the agonist and inhibitor influenced Rap1 protein expression, suggesting that short-term (<24 hours) modulation may not significantly alter Rap1 protein levels, whereas long-term exposure (>24 hours) could regulate Rap1 protein expression through feedback regulation. Using a Rap1-specific pathway agonist, we found that activation of the Rap1 signaling pathway significantly enhanced EC differentiation and maturation by day 9, indicating a promotive role of Rap1 activation in endothelial development. Notably, under Atf3-deficient conditions, activation of Rap1 signaling pathway further enhanced the expression of endothelial-specific markers, such as Cd31 and Cdh5, suggesting that Rap1 signaling may accelerate endothelial differentiation in the absence of Atf3. Conversely, inhibition of the Rap1 pathway led to markedly impaired endothelial development on day 9, with reduced expression of endothelial markers. These findings indicate that the Rap1 signaling pathway plays a critical positive regulatory role in EC differentiation and functional maturation, and that suppression of this pathway significantly disrupts endothelial development. Together with Atf3 deficiency, our results further support the mechanism by which Atf3 modulates endothelial development via the Rap1 signaling pathway. Specifically, the absence of Atf3 may promote endothelial differentiation by enhancing the activity of the Rap1 signaling pathway. Our study elucidates a novel mechanism through which Atf3 deficiency enhances endothelial development by regulating Rap1 signaling, providing new theoretical insights for stem cell–based therapy and targeted treatment of vascular diseases. Future studies will aim to further dissect the regulatory crosstalk between Atf3 and the Rap1 signaling pathway, as well as their functional roles across different cell lineages.

Although this study provides new insights into the role of Atf3 in EC regulation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we have not yet purified differentiated ECs or transplanted them into a mouse model of myocardial ischemia to verify their ability to ameliorate disease symptoms. Therefore, the functional properties of these differentiated ECs remain unvalidated. Second, our study primarily focused on the impact of Atf3KO on early endothelial differentiation efficiency, without further investigating the regulatory mechanisms governing the differentiation and development of endothelial sublineages. These limitations will be addressed in future studies, with a particular focus on exploring the mechanisms of endothelial differentiation and validating their therapeutic potential in animal models of myocardial ischemia.

Conclusions

Our study delineates the critical role of Atf3 during the early stages of mesoderm and endothelial differentiation, demonstrating that Atf3 functions as a key negative regulator in the differentiation of mESC into ECs. Atf3KO significantly promoted mESC differentiation into the mesodermal lineage and further facilitated endothelial development. Moreover, Atf3 deficiency enhanced endothelial differentiation by upregulating the Rap1 signaling pathway during the transition from late mesoderm to the early endothelial lineage. Although the regulatory role of Atf3 in ECs has been a subject of ongoing debate, our findings offer a new perspective on how Atf3 influences EC fate determination.
