Climate-driven malaria mortality among children in malaria-endemic areas of Uganda
Authors: Agnes Kiragga, Flavian Otieno, Betty Nabukeera, Maureen Ng’etich, Yesim Tozan, Dan Kajungu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria is a major disease in Africa and is among the leading causes of mortality among children. The disease is highly sensitive to climate change. Prior studies have established a link between climatic factors and malaria mortality, but the evidence specifically related to children in sub-Saharan Africa remains inconsistent and requires further investigation. This study examined the impact of climate variables on malaria mortality in the Iganga and Mayuge districts, a malaria-prone area in Uganda. We used weekly malaria mortality data from a population-based cohort in the Iganga Mayuge Health Demographic Surveillance Site (IMHDSS). In this study, we employed a time series model using a distributed lag non-linear approach to assess the association of weekly climatic factors on malaria mortality across all ages, including children under 5 years and children aged between 5 and 14 years. We also conducted a subgroup analysis based on sex to determine whether susceptibility to climate-related malaria mortality differs between females and males. We found an increased mortality risk across all ages at a lag of 11 to 12 weeks following exposure to rainfall above 646 mm. Higher risks of malaria mortality were also observed at a lag of 5 to 11 weeks when temperatures ranged between 25.2 °C and 29.9 °C. Notably, the relative risk of malaria mortality in children under 5 years and children aged between 5 and 14 years was more sensitive to temperature than to rainfall. We found that male children aged between 5 and 14 years were more vulnerable to temperature-related malaria mortality compared to females in that age group and children under 5 years. Rainfall did not have a significant association with malaria mortality in children. A nuanced understanding of climate-malaria relationships has the potential to inform the implementation of malaria prevention and control strategies through early warnings of climate-driven risks, ultimately reducing premature deaths due to malaria in this vulnerable age group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23678-0.Keywords: Climate, Malaria mortality, Children, Distributed lag models, Time series, Delayed effects, Africa

Introduction

Malaria remains a severe global life-threatening disease with high morbidity and mortality rates despite the mitigation measures that were taken in the past decades [1, 2]. The latest World Malaria Report indicates that in 2023, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases worldwide, an increase of 11 million from 2022, and 597,000 malaria-related deaths globally [1]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is burdened with 95% of the total malaria mortalities, of which 76% are children below the age of 5 years [1]. SSA bears the highest burden of malaria, with a particularly severe impact in countries such as Uganda, where malaria is endemic in 96% of the country [3], with 29.1% of outpatient visits and 39.5% of hospital admissions [4]. Annually, the estimated malaria deaths in Uganda are over 17,556 cases, with the highest incidence occurring in children under 5 years of age [1, 5].

Numerous efforts have been adopted to control and prevent malaria in the SSA. These measures include: the widespread distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying, and malaria chemoprevention, which have contributed to a decline in malaria cases and deaths in the region [1, 6, 7]. Approximately 235 million ITNs are distributed annually to SSA by National Malaria Control Programs (NMCP) [1]. Countries in SSA, including Uganda, have embraced these measures over the years to reduce and eliminate malaria mortality by 2030 [3, 6]. For instance, the 2018-19 Uganda Malaria Indicator Survey showed a 30% decrease in malaria infection and disease markers in Uganda following the introduction of interventions. Unfortunately, these declines have not been uniform across Uganda due to inadequate intervention coverage in high-prevalence areas [8]. While progress has been made in reducing malaria mortality, the disease remains the leading cause of mortality in SSA. These persistent high mortality rates are attributable to factors such as mosquitoes becoming resistant to insecticides, limited healthcare access, failure to replace worn-out bed nets, socioeconomic challenges, and climatic factors [1, 9, 10].

Climate change is widely recognized as one of the greatest threats to global health and the fight against malaria [1]. Malaria is climate-sensitive, and changes in temperature, rainfall patterns, and relative humidity affect the dynamics and intensity of malaria transmission as they influence the habitats of mosquitoes and parasites and their growth cycle [11–13]. There remains a dearth of knowledge on the direct causal relationship between climate and malaria mortality. Climate remains an indirect cause of malaria mortality by affecting parasite development during periods of high rainfall and temperatures, which then leads to increased malaria transmission, morbidity, and severe malaria, consequently leading to over 569,000 deaths in Africa and remaining the number one cause of death among children under five [1, 14]. The presence of competing causes of death among children, such as HIV and malnutrition, coupled with limited post-mortem services, could mask the direct impact of climate on mortality in children [15, 16]. This paper explores the indirect causes of climate on malaria mortality.

Numerous studies have explored the correlation between environmental conditions and malaria incidence [9, 12, 13, 17–20]. However, there is a notable lack of research examining how climatic factors affect malaria mortality. One of the studies that have examined the relationship between climatic conditions and malaria mortality in SSA found that temperatures above 28.2 °C and rainfall levels exceeding the 99th percentile are likely to increase malaria mortality [14]. Sewe et al. [21] found that the risk of malaria mortality rises when weekly total precipitation is more than 20 mm. The findings from these studies may not apply to all the settings in SSA due to differences in altitude, vector species, socioeconomic disparities, temporal variability, and human behavior, among others [22, 23].

Uganda is a tropical country with the highest malaria mortality burden. It ranks as the third contributor to global malaria cases and the tenth to global malaria deaths, with high prevalence observed in Northern and Eastern Uganda, where health infrastructure is often inadequate [24, 25]. Children below 5 years of age contribute the highest proportion to malaria mortality in this region due to the underdeveloped immunity to the disease, delayed diagnosis, limited access to treatment, and increased climate variability [24]. School-aged children, 5 to 14 years old, have higher malaria prevalence, with 70% carrying the malaria parasite asymptomatically in high transmission settings [26]. The number of studies exploring the impact of climatic factors on malaria mortality in this vulnerable group (children less than 14 years) has been largely inconsistent in SSA and warrants further exploration that will inform targeted interventions.

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between temperature and rainfall on malaria mortality in an endemic region in Uganda, particularly among children under 5 years and those aged between 5 and 14 years. We also evaluated the gender disparities in climate-driven effects on malaria mortality. The main aim was to better understand how climatic factors influence malaria mortality patterns in these vulnerable age groups.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Iganga-Mayuge Health and Demographic Surveillance Site (IMHDSS) is situated in the Iganga and Mayuge districts in the Busoga region, the Eastern part of Uganda. The HDSS is about 120 km from Kampala, bordered by Lake Victoria to the South. The area has a malaria prevalence rate of 39.4% in children under five years old, making it one of the areas in Uganda most severely impacted by the disease and the leading cause of mortality in children [8, 27]. The climate is typically tropical with bimodal rainfall: March to May, September to November, and two dry seasons: June to August and December to February [28]. The HDSS has a maximum elevation of approximately 1210 m above the sea level (S1 Figure). The IMHDSS population cohort collects data from 65 villages located within an area of 155 km square and with a population of 94,568 as of the end of 2022. The area is predominantly rural, with a few peri-urban regions. The HDSS has 23 health facilities, including two general hospitals, 15 level II, 6 level III, and 1 level IV health centers located within 2 to 5 km from the demographic surveillance area [29]. Malaria screening is provided at no cost in public health centers and at a fee in private health centers. The village health teams provide additional malaria testing services at the community level. Health-seeking behavior is moderate, with 51% of children being taken by their guardians for malaria testing at the onset of fever [8]. Key demographic events of birth, death, migration, and sociodemographic characteristics are routinely collected at individual and household levels within this population.

Data sources

Malaria mortality data

The dataset used in this analysis included daily all-cause mortality records for residents of the IMHDSS from January 2008 to December 2022. The data went through quality control and assessment before being used for analysis. We extracted daily malaria death records using R statistical software and aggregated them into weekly time series data.

Malaria deaths were identified using the InterVA method, a probabilistic tool designed to determine the likely cause of death based on a Verbal Autopsy (VA) questionnaire. The InterVA method employs a Bayesian statistical technique to estimate the probabilities of various causes of death based on pre-coded signs and symptoms into binary categories. Three different VA tools provided by the WHO (version 2009) were utilized, tailored explicitly for neonates (aged 0–28 days), children (aged 29 days-14 years), and adults (aged 15 years and older) [30].

Climate data

Due to the lack of ground weather data in the study area, we utilized remotely sensed climate data. Daily maximum, average, and minimum temperature as well as rainfall, were extracted from the ERA5-Land daily aggregated-EMCWF climate reanalysis, which has a spatial resolution of 0.1° X 0.1° from January 2008 to December 2022 [31]. Rainfall, originally recorded in inches, was converted to millimeters (mm). The daily climatic variables were then aggregated into weekly time series data: weekly averages were computed for temperature, while weekly totals were calculated for rainfall. Finally, the weekly malaria deaths were then merged with the corresponding weekly climatic variables for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

A time-series regression analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between weekly climatic variables and malaria mortality. The distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) was employed to account for the delayed and non-linear effects of climatic variables on malaria deaths. Weekly malaria deaths were assumed to follow a quasiPoisson family to account for the potential overdispersion in the data. The DLNM was fitted using the dlnm package in R statistical software, following the methodology outlined in [32].

Prior studies on malaria mortality have used a maximum lag of 12 weeks to account for the population dynamics of malaria vectors and the life cycle and incubation period of malaria parasites within malaria vectors and human hosts [18, 21, 22]. Therefore, we adopted a maximum lag of 12 weeks in this study in the analysis. This timeframe accounts for the whole chain of events, from environmental changes, mosquito breeding, parasite growth, human infection, and death.

Model Building and selection

This study utilized a stepwise approach, particularly the backward elimination method in building the models [33]. During model selection, the focus was on identifying a model that included one temperature parameter and rainfall. This decision stemmed from the high correlation observed among all three temperature parameters, which warranted the exclusion of models with two or more temperature parameters. The model selection process prioritized the model with the smallest AIC (Akaike Information Criterion), as this criterion helps to balance model fit and complexity. Ultimately, the model incorporating maximum temperature and rainfall was identified as the best-fitting model with an AIC value of 1243.231 (S1 Table), and all subsequent analyses were performed using this model.

Subgroup analysis

The association of temperature and rainfall on malaria mortality were investigated among children under 5 years and children aged between 5 and 14 years. Gender differences in malaria mortality risk across these age groups were also investigated to determine whether susceptibility to climate-related malaria mortality differed between males and females. Figures 6 and 7, and Table 3 present the association of weekly total rainfall and weekly average of daily maximum temperatures on the relative risk of malaria mortality for children under 5 years and children aged between 5 and 14 years, respectively, at 0 to 12 weeks lags. We found that the risk of malaria mortality was highly sensitive to weekly average daily maximum temperatures for both children under 5 years and children aged between 5 and 14 years, exhibiting markedly higher mortality risks compared to the association of weekly total rainfall (Figs. 6 and 7; Table 3). The risk of malaria mortality was significantly higher for children under 5 years when exposed to a weekly average daily maximum temperature ranging between and at a lag of 8 weeks. Additionally, we observed a significant negative association between weekly average maximum temperatures ranging between and and malaria mortality risk at a lag of 0 weeks (Fig. 7). Children aged between 5 and 14 years also had a significantly increased risk of malaria mortality when exposed to weekly average maximum temperatures. At lags of 4 to 8 weeks, they had a significantly higher risk of malaria mortality when exposed to weekly average maximum temperatures ranging between to (Fig. 7). No significant lagged association was observed between malaria mortality and weekly total rainfall among both children under 5 years and children between 5 and 14 years.

Discussion

This study explored the association of climatic factors on malaria mortality in the Iganga-Mayuge HDSS site in Uganda. It focused on all age groups, with particular emphasis on children under 5 years and those between 5 and 14 years, as well as any gender differences in mortality risk. We observed that the association between temperature and rainfall varied across the lags with malaria mortality risk across all ages. Maximum temperature was associated with an increased risk of malaria mortality in both children under 5 years and those aged between 5 and 14 years. In terms of gender-specific vulnerability to malaria, male children aged between 5 and 14 years had the highest risk of malaria mortality due to temperature. In contrast, rainfall did not show a significant association on malaria mortality across the demographic groups studied.

Our results indicate that weekly average maximum temperatures ranging from to were associated with an increased risk of malaria mortality across all ages, with a lag period of 5 to 11 weeks. The development of the malaria parasite within mosquitoes is highly temperature-dependent. Warmer temperatures accelerate the development of malaria parasites in mosquitoes, thereby increasing parasite abundance and, ultimately, malaria transmission rates [34]. Our findings align with previous research indicating that the ideal temperature for mosquito breeding and transmission falls between and [35–37]. Similarly, a study from Western Kenya reported that the risk of malaria mortality increased after 9 weeks of temperatures exceeding 28 °C [21]. Zhao et al. [37] found that malaria incidence risk was associated with increased temperatures at a lag of 4 to 6 weeks. Another study conducted in Ethiopia showed a correlation between maximum temperatures and malaria cases at a lag of 2 to 3 months [38].

The association of rainfall was found to be more delayed, with a significant increase in malaria mortality risk observed at lag periods of 11 and 12 weeks when weekly total rainfall exceeded 646 mm. Rainfall contributes to the formation of water pools, which serve as suitable breeding grounds for Anopheles mosquitoes [39]. A study conducted in Eritrea showed that malaria incidence is positively associated with rainfall at a lag of 1 to 4 months [40]. Another study reported that malaria mortality risk increased after 12 weeks when weekly total precipitation exceeded 20 mm, peaking at 80 mm [21].

A key finding of our study is the stronger positive association between maximum temperature and malaria mortality in children under 5 years and those aged between 5 and 14 years. We found a lagged effect on malaria mortality, with a maximum temperature at 8 weeks for children under 5 years and a lag of 4 to 8 weeks for those aged between 5 and 14 years. In particular, temperatures between 25 and 30 °C were associated with an increased malaria mortality risk in these age groups. A study done in Western Kenya found that the risk of death due to malaria or anemia among children under 5 years of age increased with temperatures above 24 °C at a lag period of 9 to 16 weeks [41]. In Iganga and Mayuge districts in Uganda, where malaria is endemic, children under 5 years and those aged between 5 and 14 years account for the majority of malaria-related deaths. These age groups are disproportionately affected because of their high exposure to mosquito bites. They are most at risk of increased transmission rates due to their vulnerable immune systems, which make them more susceptible to malaria-related deaths [2]. Climatic factors, such as temperature and rainfall, significantly influence malaria transmission dynamics in endemic areas, highlighting their importance in the fight against malaria. Overall, existing studies have shown that children are most vulnerable to the association of temperature and rainfall on malaria [2, 42]. However, our findings contrast with those of Krefis et al. [43], who found a significant association between rainfall and malaria mortality in children under 15 years of age at a 9-week lag. We found no association between rainfall and malaria mortality risk in this age group. The possible explanation for this observation could be the high use of control measures, such as increased bed nets used among children during the rainy season, which could be attributed to a decline in malaria transmission.

This study showed that malaria mortality in male children aged between 5 and 14 years was positively associated with temperature, unlike in females in this age group and also children under 5 years. While malaria impacts both genders, to our knowledge, no study has investigated the climate-related risks on malaria mortality, specifically by gender. Previous research indicated that male children aged between 6 and 15 years were found to have a higher risk of testing positive compared to female children in this age group [44]. In our study, the observed increase in malaria mortality in male children aged between 5 and 14 years may be attributed to behavioral differences and cultural and social factors, including gender roles [45]. Boys often spend more time outside playing and are more likely to be bitten by mosquitoes, particularly in the evenings when mosquitoes are most active. Furthermore, since farming is the primary source of income in the study area [21], boys may be more involved with farming activities, further exposing them to mosquitoes. Further investigation is needed to understand these relationships.

A significant strength of this study is that the data were collected at the community level, providing a good comprehensive record of all documented deaths, including those from malaria. It has also focused on vulnerable groups who are particularly susceptible to climate-related health risks from malaria. However, the study had some limitations. The dataset is specific to the study population residing in the IMHDSS site, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions in Uganda and SSA. The malaria mortality data used in the study were obtained using the InterVA method. Although widely used for cause of death determination in settings where vital registration systems are weak, this method may misclassify deaths due to diseases with similar symptoms. An example is malaria, which shares overlapping symptoms with other severe infectious diseases, such as typhoid and dengue fever. When performing the verbal autopsy, the number of deaths caused by malaria may be overstated or underestimated. Even though verbal autopsies are the most readily available sources of determining causes of death, the absence of post-mortem services might not ascertain other causes of death, potentially increasing the number of malaria-related deaths in the study.

This study has quantified the association between climatic factors and malaria mortality in the Iganga and Mayuge districts. The findings indicate that temperature and rainfall contribute to increased malaria mortality, underscoring the need to incorporate climate considerations into malaria prevention and control strategies in Uganda. Notably, higher temperatures were associated with a greater risk of malaria mortality in children, with the association being more pronounced at certain lag times. A nuanced understanding of climate-malaria relationships can strengthen preparedness and response by providing early warnings of climate-driven risks while informing resource mobilization, which is critical in resource-limited endemic settings. This proactive approach not only addresses the increased risk of malaria associated with specific climatic conditions but also improves the effectiveness of prevention and control efforts, ultimately reducing morbidity and premature deaths due to malaria, particularly in vulnerable populations such as children.
