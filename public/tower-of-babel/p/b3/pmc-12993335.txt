Evaluation of quality characteristics, antioxidant activity, functional groups of sweet potato by-products and their usage in sandwich bread processing
Authors: Nguyen Chi Dung, Nguyen Minh Thuy, Tran Ngoc Giau, Hong Van Hao, Nguyen Van Thanh, Vo Quang Minh, Ngo Van Tai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA substantial quantity of waste is generated during sweet potato processing, posing environmental and economic challenges. This study aimed to valorize sweet potato by-products (SPB) by drying and milling them into powder and incorporating the material into sandwich bread formulations as a partial replacement for wheat flour at levels of 10–25% (corresponding to formulas B1 to B4) along with control sample B0. The physicochemical, structural, and functional properties of SPB powder were evaluated to determine its suitability as a functional ingredient. The results indicated that SPB powder possesses high nutritional value, with considerable amounts of dietary fiber, bioactive compounds, and strong antioxidant activity. Scanning electron microscopy revealed predominantly oval and polygonal particles with relatively smooth surfaces and intact structures. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy confirmed a polysaccharide-rich lignocellulosic composition, including starch, cellulose, hemicellulose, and phenolic compounds. Among the tested formulations, bread containing 15% SPB (B2) was identified as optimal, as it maintained desirable moisture content, loaf volume, and color characteristics while significantly enhancing nutritional quality. Compared with the control, the B2 formulation showed substantial increases in dietary fiber and total polyphenols. These findings highlight the potential of SPB powder as a sustainable, high-fiber functional ingredient for bakery product fortification.Keywords: Antioxidants, FTIR, Texture, Bread, By-products, Sweet-potato

Introduction

Processing sweet potatoes (SP) into various products generated a large amount of solid organic waste, including peels and trimmings from the tubes. According to Liu et al. (2024), sweet potato by-products contain polyphenols (chlorogenic acid, dicaffeoylquinic acids), lutein, and a variety of useful carbohydrates (pectin, polysaccharides). These chemicals are found in the peel and byproducts of sweet potatoes and contain biological actions such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and other properties. Agubosi et al. (2022) reported that sweet potato peel contains a lot of starch, which can be used as an energy source for animals and humans or as fertilizer. The peel also includes protein (about 5% by dry weight), which is distributed irregularly and this content is higher in the flesh layer closest to the peel (Akoetey et al., 2017). The peel and pulp are both high in fiber and minerals (potassium and iron), while being low in fat and cholesterol (Taiyeba et al., 2020). Diets high in fiber improve health, particularly glycemic status, blood pressure, cholesterol reduction, and insulin resistance (Khalid et al., 2022; Porter Starr et al., 2019). Their recovery and reuse in the post-harvest food chain can be viewed as a sustainable strategy, while also addressing present issues as the global industry has advanced dramatically. Furthermore, sweet potato by-products (SPB), with their nutritional composition and bioactive compounds, will serve as a source of essential nutrients for humans if used correctly. Concerns about the potential of sweet potato leaves have been published by several groups of authors (Alam, Rana, et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2024). Utilizing sweet potato wastes is a critical component of long-term management of this material. This field's study trends are highly numerous and multifaceted, including bioethanol production (Ojewumi, Akwayo, et al., 2018), fiber-rich flour (Julianti et al., 2021), bread making (Valino et al., 2020), solid fuel (Chen et al., 2018), high fiber-rich antioxidant biscuits making (Bakar et al., 2022), nano-synthesis (Das et al., 2019a). Determination of phytochemical compounds and bioactive properties of sweet potato peels according to variety and drying technique performed by Ćetković et al. (2025).

However, there is currently no standardized database for by-products, which may make industrialization problematic owing to material instability. Furthermore, there are restrictions in the technological performance of SPBs in food when added to flour systems, such as the observed weakening of the gluten network. Many other questions remain unanswered, such as unknown materials on the shell, pollutants, or chemical compounds found in the polymer matrix of SPB. Besides, bread is a common and popular commodity that can be used because it is a convenient food. White bread contains macronutrients such as carbohydrates, proteins, and fats, but it lacks critical amino acids, micronutrients, bioactive substances, and dietary fiber. The food industry strives to meet consumers' needs and expectations by providing foods that improve nutritional status and promote health. As a result, a percentage of refined wheat flour must be replaced with products containing important nutritional components in order to improve nutritional status while retaining sensory quality, physical and chemical qualities.

Because of these outstanding concerns, the purpose of this research is to provide relevant information on the quality of SPB, develop convenient flour products, and incorporate them into sandwich bread recipes to improve the nutritional and physicochemical properties of the finished product. This study also looked at nutritional properties, bioactive compounds, and in-depth analyses such as identifying functional groups in the by-product powder using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy technology and observing the microstructure of the powder and processed products (using the by-product powder). From a nutritional standpoint, developing a product that combines sweet potato by-products as a source of nutrients (particularly fiber), beneficial bioactive chemicals, and high antioxidant capabilities will provide numerous health benefits to people. This is also an appropriate response in a developing country where obesity and chronic diseases are on the rise.

Materials and methods

Preparation of sweet potato by-products and its powder

During the processing of various products, sweet potatoes were peeled, and their ends were cut off. In addition, when shaping the potatoes, the scraps were also removed. All parts including the peel and the scraps from the processing process are collectively called by-products. The SPB (Fig. 1) were collected and washed with 0.1% baking soda solution for 10 min (Kelagiri et al., 2017) to remove pesticide residues (used by farmers during cultivation). SPB were cleaned with clean water, drained, and crushed using a Bluestone CHB-5138 (300 W, Singapore). They were then stored in PE/Al bags in a freezer at −25 ± 1 °C for study of their physicochemical properties and antioxidant activity.

The by-products of sweet potatoes.

To avoid enzymatic browning, SPB was blanched with steam at 100 °C for 3 min prior to drying. Thuy et al. (2024) used a Memmert drying oven, UN260 (Germany), set to 70 °C, to dry SPB for 10 h with a moisture content of 6–7%. The dry SPB was pulverized with a laboratory-grade micromill and the powder was collected through a 100 μm screen. The finely powdered powder was placed in plastic boxes and stored (lightproof) at room temperature (25 °C) for future investigation.

The sandwich bread preparation

This study used the following fixed ingredients: 27 g sugar, 2.7 g salt, 54 g eggs, 27 g margarine (Tuong An, Vietnam), 117 g fresh milk (TH true milk, Vietnam), and 1.8 g instant dry yeast (Lesaffre-Red Saf, Marquette-lez-Lille, France). SPB powder was used to substitute wheat flour in four recipes, B1, B2, B3, and B4, with proportions of 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25%, respectively, in addition to the control sample B0 (100% wheat flour), which used 261 g of wheat flour (Bakers' Choice flour, Interflour company, Vietnam). The total weight of one recipe was 490.5 g. All ingredients were placed into an automatic sandwich maker (PE9600, PETRUS, China). The total bread-making time was 3 h and 20 mins, including 4 kneading cycles (45 mins), 3 proofing cycles (120 mins), and baking (165 °C for 35 mins). The physicochemical characteristics, including moisture, expansion, hardness and color of the resulting sandwich bread were analyzed.

Nutritional composition, bioactive compounds, and antioxidant activity analysis

Proximate compositional analysis followed the AOAC (2005) standard method, while carbohydrates were analyzed using AOAC (1980). Vitamin C content was analyzed by the spectrophotometric method (Alam, Sams, et al., 2020). Mineral content was determined by the atomic absorption spectrophotometric method (AOAC, 2010).

Total polyphenol content (TPC) was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu assay (Philpott et al., 2004). 0.1 mL of the appropriately diluted extract was mixed with 1.5 mL of 10% (v/v) Folin–Ciocalteu reagent. After 5 min, 4.0 mL of 20% (w/v) Na2CO3 solution was added. The mixture was incubated at room temperature (25 °C) for 30 min in the dark, and the absorbance was measured at 738 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer (UV1200, YOKE, China). Gallic acid was used as the calibration standard, and results are expressed in mg gallic acid equivalent per gram of sample (mg GAE/g).

Meanwhile, according to Chang et al. (2002), total flavonoid content (TFC) was analyzed by recording the absorption reaction of the sample extract with AlCl3 solution and expressed in mg QE/g. The 0.5 mL of the extract was mixed with 0.1 mL of 10% (w/v) AlCl3 solution, 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate solution, 1.5 mL of 80% methanol, and 7.8 mL of distilled water. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 415 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer. Quercetin was used to construct the calibration curve, and the results are expressed in mg quercetin equivalent per gram of sample (mg QE/g).

The scavenging activity of 1,1-diphenyl 2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free radical was used to determine antioxidant activity (Brand-Williams et al., 1995). The 0.5 mL of the extract was added to 3 mL of methanol and 0.5 mL of 0.6 mM DPPH prepared in methanol. The sample is kept in the dark for 30 min, then the absorbance is measured at 517 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Vitamin C is used as a reference standard. The free radical scavenging activity is calculated as follows:

DPPH (%) = [(Abscontrol − Abstest)/Abscontrol] × 100.

The ABTS (%) was determined through the scavenging activity of the 2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) ABTS•+ free radical (Awika et al., 2003), along with the determination of IC50. The eppendorf tubes were sealed to protect against light. The test sample was a methanol-diluted extract or control solution with varying volumes. 10 μL of the test sample was then added to the eppendorf tubes containing 990 μL of ABTS•+ solution, which were also sealed to protect against light, resulting in a final volume of 1 mL. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 6 min, after which the absorbance was measured at 734 nm.

Texture analysis, color measurement, expansion volume

The hardness of sandwich bread was determined using a TA.XTplusC Texture Analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, UK). Slices 2 cm thick were placed on the platform and compressed with a cylindrical probe at a strain of 50%, pre-test speed (1.0 mm/s), test speed (1.0 mm/s), post-test speed (5.0 mm/s), trigger force (5 g), time between cycles (5 s) and probe type (P/36R). The color (L*, a* and b*) of the sample was measured using a colorimeter (Hunter Lab, USA). The expansion volume (times) of the bread was determined by the volume ratio of the sandwich bread before baking and after finishing the product (Thuy et al., 2023).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy technology

FTIR is based on the sample's absorption and transmission of infrared radiation. The scanning process produces a spectrum that generates a molecular fingerprint of the sample. This fingerprint represents the absorption peaks produced by different sample components, providing direct information about the amount of material in the sample. To perform an FTIR measurement, 100 mg of SPB powder was directly deposited onto the ATR (Attenuated Total Reflectance) crystal, and a uniform pressure was applied using the pressure arm. Absorption spectra were obtained in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 with 30 scans per spectrum, using IR spectroscopy (Iraffinity-1S, Shimadzu, Japan) (Cao et al., 2014).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Microstructural images of the sample were prepared and acquired by scanning electron microscope (Hitachi S-4800, Japan) in accordance with the approach of Zambelli et al. (2018). The microscope has a magnification of 20 to 800,000 and an SE image resolution of 2.0 nm at 1 kV and 1.0 nm at 15 kV. The samples were placed on Si wafers, dried, and introduced into the instrument without further coating. Measurements were performed at energy levels ranging from 5 to 10 keV.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were repeated three times, and the findings are presented as mean value±standard deviation (STD). Stratigraphic Centurion XV·I software (USA) was used for analysis of variance, and LSD was used at a 95% confidence level to compare mean individuals.

Nutritional composition

The protein content of SPB was determined to be 2.31% FW (≈8.49 g/100 g dry weight (DW)) (Table 1), which is higher than the value of 1.38 g/100 g DW for the peel reported by Shaari et al. (2021). Ahmed (2019) found higher protein levels in the flesh and peel, ranging from 3.26 to 5.6 g/100 g DW and from 5.38 to 8.85 g/100 g DW, respectively. Sweet potatoes are often regarded as low-protein crops, with levels ranging from 1% to more than 10% (Bovell-Benjamin, 2007). This difference may be due to the processing method and characteristics of sweet potato varieties.

SPB had a fat content of 0.3% FW (≈1.10 g/100 g DW) (Table 1), comparable to Vannini et al. (2021) finding of 1.16% DW. Ahmed (2019) reported that the fat content of the flesh ranged from 0.38 to 0.95 g/100 g DW, whereas that of the peel ranged from 1.02 to 1.86 g/100 g DW. This finding demonstrates that SPBs are a low-fat food source suited for processing good diets for human health, hence preventing excess fat accumulation in the body.

The ash concentration of the examined SPB was 1.45% FW (Table 1) (≈5.33 g/100 g DW), which was lower than that reported by Shaari et al. (2021), which was 6.44 g/100 g DW. Ojewumi, Job, et al. (2018) found 5.6% ash in SP peel and 4% in flesh. SP peel with a high ash content also contains substantial amounts of inorganic elements such as potassium, sodium, calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium (Czech et al., 2020).

About 80% of SP peels are carbohydrates (Maloney et al., 2014). SPB had a carbohydrate content of 22.4% FW (≈ 82.32 g/100 g DW) (Table 1), greater than sweet potato flesh (30.13–42.19 g/100 g DW) and peel (76.4–77 g/100 g DW) (Liu et al., 2024). This study's results align with Ahmed (2019) findings that the carbohydrate content of meat and peel ranges from 68.20 to 83.54% DW and 61.37 to 65.72 g/100 g DW. These observed changes could be due to differences in protein, fat, ash, and fiber levels between SPB types.

The fiber content of SPB was 3.10% FW (≈ 11.39 g/100 g DW) (Table 1), which is similar to the fiber content of sweet potato flesh (9.15–14.26% DW) and higher than the peel (4.74–4.76% DW), as reported by Liu et al. (2024). Ahmed (2019) reported that fiber content ranged from 10.87 to 21.97% DW (in flesh) and 20.09–23.32% DW (in peel). The presented findings showed that SPB are a fiber-rich dietary source that can be used as a raw material in the manufacture of nutritious products. Rosell et al. (2024) found that by-products, such as the peel and root clippings, contain 77.6% insoluble fiber. Fiber is an essential factor in functional foods that can prevent cardiovascular disease and obesity (Mandalari et al., 2010).

The vitamin C content in the SPB mixture was 1.19 mg/100 g FW (≈ 4.37 mg/100 g DW) (Table 1), within the range of values published by Rosell et al. (2024), with 0.74 mg/100 g (peel) and 63.4 mg/100 g (tuber flesh). Our investigation found higher vitamin C content in SP peel and flesh than the value of Shaari et al. (2021) reported, with values of 0.74 mg/100 g DW and 1.17 mg/100 g DW, respectively. Sweet potatoes are rich in vitamin C, with larger quantities found in the flesh and lower levels in the peel (Rosell et al., 2024). Vitamin C contributes to collagen biosynthesis, lipid metabolism, iron absorption, and antioxidant activity (Valdés, 2006).

SP and its by-products contain a high concentration of vital minerals for the body. Minerals are abundant in both the flesh and peel, with the peel containing higher levels of most minerals compared to the body (Rosell et al., 2024).

SPB had a calcium content of 13.6 mg/100 g FW (≈ 49.98 mg/100 g DW) (Table 1), lower than sweet potato flesh (98.2 mg/100 g DW) and peel (134.3 mg/100 g DW) as reported by Rosell et al. (2024). However, the examined value was more than that reported by Etim et al. (2023) for the calcium content of peel (40.41 mg/100 g DW) and flesh (16.84 mg/100 g DW). During sweet potato storage, more inorganic materials may adhere to the outer skin rather than the inside flesh.

The magnesium content of the SPB examined was 5.58 mg/100 g FW (20.51 mg/100 g DW) (Table 1). This is lower than the magnesium amount reported by Ahmed (2019), with the flesh 35–68 mg/100 g DW, and similar to the peel analysis results (12–94 mg/100 g DW). According to Rosell et al. (2024), the flesh has 120.4 mg/100 g DW of magnesium, whereas the peel has 49.7 mg/100 g DW.

SPB had a potassium content of 92.93 mg/100 g FW (≈ 341.53 mg/100 g DW) (Table 1), similar to the potassium content of flesh (340–542 mg/100 g DW) and peel (105–1139 mg/100 g DW) reported by Ahmed (2019). However, this value is substantially lower than that reported by Rosell et al. (2024), with potassium content of the flesh and peel at 1200 mg/100 g DW and 5572 mg/100 g DW, respectively. The peel samples have a higher potassium concentration than the flesh samples.

The phosphorus level in SPB was 20.66 mg/100 g FW (≈75.93 mg/100 g DW) (Table 1). According to Ahmed (2019), the phosphorus level of the tuber flesh ranged from 12 to 98 mg/100 g DW, whereas the peel ranged from 37 to 189 mg/100 g DW. Etim et al. (2023) showed relatively high amounts of 98.95 mg/100 g DW (in the peel) and 72.95 mg/100 g DW (in the flesh).

By-products collected from the peel, trimmed, and discarded parts of the tuber during processing (due to incorrect use) resulted in analytical values within the range of those obtained from the peel and tuber flesh. The variations in their content might be contributed cultivars, climate, soil types, and location…

Bioactive compounds

Many plants include chemicals called phenols and flavonoids that have antioxidant and antibacterial properties (Parashar et al., 2014). The analysis results showed that SPBs included TPC of 1.08 mg GAE/g FW (≈3.97 mg GAE/g DW), remarkably close to the data of Franková et al. (2022) with TPC (sweet potato peel) ranging from 1.68 to 7.03 mg GAE/g DW and flesh was 0.53–5.60 mg GAE/g DW. Darwish et al. (2020) reported TPC ranging from 0.43 to 1.22 mg GAE/g DW (peel) and 0.25 to 0.58 mg GAE/g DW (tuber flesh).

The TFC in SPB was 0.45 mg QE/g FW (≈ 1.65 mg QE/g DW). Darwish et al. (2020) found that the TFC of the peel and tuber flesh ranged from 0.08 to 1.06 mg QE/g DW and 0.06 to 0.62 mg QE/g DW, respectively. These contents were relatively lower than our analytical findings. Makori et al. (2020) recorded quite similar amounts, ranging from 1.16 to 4.87 mg QE/g DW (in the peel) to 1–4.6 mg QE/g DW (in the flesh).

Antioxidant activity

ABTS•+ and DPPH radicals are the two most frequent chromogen chemicals used to assess antioxidant activity in biological materials (Arnao, 2000). SPB have an IC50 of 2.25 ± 0.63 mg/mL for DPPH and 1.89 mg/mL for ABTS•+ (Table 1). Das et al. (2019b) reported that IC50 DPPH values of SP ranging from 0.107 to 0.120 mg/mL and IC50 ABTS•+ values ranging from 0.323 to 0.327 mg/mL. Fidrianny et al. (2018) reported that the IC50 DPPH values from tuber extracts of four sweet potato varieties with different peel and flesh colors in West Java, Indonesia ranged from 0.045 to 0.098 mg/mL. In addition, in the study by Manphae et al. (2022), the IC50 DPPH and IC50 ABTS•+ values of sweet potato extracts from Thailand were determined to range from approximately 0.15–0.37 mg/mL and 0.09–0.14 mg/mL, respectively. The differing values that have been published can likely be ascribed to the varying sources of the by-products obtained, and may also be linked to differences in cultivar, drying temperature, solvent polarity, and whether free or total phenolics were examined, as all of these factors have a significant impact on antioxidant measurements (Dai & Mumper, 2010). Antioxidant efficiency depends not only on total phenolic content but also on molecular structure. Flavonoids lacking ortho-dihydroxy substitution or present in glycosylated forms generally exhibit lower radical scavenging activity (Prior et al., 2005). Therefore, structural differences in SPB phenolics may contribute to higher IC50 values. In addition, SPB is a fiber-rich matrix. Insoluble fiber and particle size distribution can restrict solvent penetration and mass transfer, while matrix turbidity may influence DPPH assay kinetics (Prior et al., 2005).

In-depth research on the sources of by-products collected (instead of being dumped in landfills) from this scientific report can create potential for developing new food products using SPB, while also helping to protect the environment with reduced waste sources. This is also seen as a crucial step forward in food and nutrition research, as it introduces important bioactive compounds that promise to benefit both consumers and the food business.

Scanning electron microscopy of SPB powder

Scanning electron microscopy images of SP and SPB powder samples dried by the convection drying method (presented in the previous section). SEM images were observed at magnifications of ×1000 and ×5000 (Fig. 2). It can be observed that SP powder particles (Fig. 2a) have different sizes (these observations are descriptive), mainly oval and polygonal shapes, quite smooth surfaces and intact structures. Meanwhile, the shape of the SPB powder particles (Fig. 2b) is not uniform, flake-like with sharp fracture edges and a rough surface, but other particle shapes such as oval and polygonal are also present. The components' bonds are slightly slack, the microstructure of the surface is fractured, and the individual starch granules are surrounded and held together by a flat phase. This disrupted and porous morphology increases surface area and exposes structural polysaccharides from peel fractions. Such features enhance water-binding capacity due to abundant hydroxyl groups, leading to stronger water immobilization and competition with gluten proteins for hydration (Rosell et al., 2010). SPB is made up of a succession of individual starch granules, the size of which varies according on the processing technique used and the ratio of peel components to tuber trimmings in the mixture.

Determination of functional groups of SPB powder

The functional group composition of SPB powder was validated by FTIR analysis (Fig. 3). The FTIR analysis revealed that SP powder has seven spectral peaks ranging from 3304.06 cm−1 to 418.55 cm−1. Starch in SPB typically produces absorption bands at 3304.06 cm−1 for O—H stretching and 2922.16 cm−1 for C—H stretching (Guo et al., 2014). The hydroxyl groups (O—H) were sensitive to moisture. These groups establish hydrogen connections with water molecules, affecting the peel's conductivity as humidity levels fluctuate. The observed differences in conductivity reflect this sensitivity.

FTIR analysis of functional groups of SPB powder.

The strong absorption around 1020–1000 cm−1 corresponds to C–O–C and C—O stretching vibrations of polysaccharides, confirming that SPB is rich in starch and non-starch polysaccharides. In FTIR studies of starch and polysaccharide systems, the region between 1200 and 1000 cm−1 is widely recognized as the characteristic “fingerprint region” of carbohydrate backbones, associated with C—O and glycosidic C–O–C vibrations of polymeric structures (Fanari et al., 2022). These functional groups are directly responsible for swelling, viscosity development, and interaction with gluten proteins through hydrogen bonding. Compared with sweet potato pulp, SPB exhibits relatively stronger polysaccharide-associated peaks, indicating a higher proportion of structural carbohydrates and possible structural modifications during processing, which may increase surface exposure of hydroxyl groups. This structural difference could explain its enhanced water-binding capacity and its influence on dough hydration behavior and consistency.

Wang et al. (2018) identified the peak at 1618 cm−1 as the C—O group of uronic acid. The week peak at 1006.84 cm−1 is due to the O-C-O stretching of hemicellulose (Si et al., 2023). The residue was primarily made up of starch and cellulose, with a low concentration of hydrophobic lignin, indicating that it was generally hydrophilic. Sweet potato pomace differs from typical starches due to the presence of lignin, pectin, and protein, making it a potentially major waste that warrants additional research (Vannini et al., 2021).

In short, the structural modifications observed in SEM micrographs revealed partial disruption of the gluten matrix upon incorporation of sweet potato by-product powder. This structural rearrangement results in reduced gas cell uniformity and increased crumb density, which directly affects textural hardness and specific volume. Furthermore, FTIR analysis suggests enhanced hydrogen bonding interactions, potentially contributing to improved water retention capacity. These structural changes were closely associated with functional outcomes, including altered starch digestibility and improved antioxidant retention, highlighting the structure–function relationship in composite bread systems.

The expansion of sandwich bread after baking varied significantly between samples (Table 3), with sample B0 having the maximum expansion (4.57 times) and sample B4 having the lowest (2.24 times). This result is also similar with the findings of Elkatry et al. (2023), who reported that increasing the adding of OFSP peel powder reduces bread expansion. When the quantity of SPB powder in the dough is increased, the sandwich bread gets drier and expands less. This could be because the dough in sample B4 has a low moisture content, and the outer layer is dry, preventing the bread loaf expansion during fermentation and baking.

The incorporation of SPB powder, which is gluten-free, reduces the overall gluten content of the flour blend. As the level of wheat flour substitution increases, the gluten network becomes weaker and less elastic due to the dilution of gluten-forming proteins. This weakened structure results in loose bonding within the dough matrix, thereby decreasing its ability to retain CO2 during fermentation and ultimately leading to reduced fermentation efficiency and lower bread volume (Makinde & Akinoso, 2014). In addition, the high fiber content of SPB powder further impairs gluten network development. Dietary fiber competes with gluten proteins for water absorption, limiting proper gluten hydration and network formation. Consequently, gas retention capacity is further reduced, contributing to poor dough expansion and low-rise bread (Ma et al., 2021). Wang and Zhou (2004) found that increased fiber content can lower bread volume by weakening the dough structure, resulting in a loss in the dough's ability to hold CO2. Yeast converts the carbohydrates in the dough into carbon dioxide gas, which diffuses toward the air particles in the dough during mixing. The final gas volume of bread can account for more than 70% of the loaf's volume, and the size and density of the gas cells can vary, resulting in considerable differences in the texture and sensory properties of the completed product (Chevallier et al., 2012).

The amount of SPB powder used in sandwich bread recipes altered the hardness of the bread. The hardness was highest in sample B4 (103.41 g force) and lowest in sample B0 (19.97 g force) (Table 3). Bread hardness increased in tandem with fiber content, resulting in reduced air cell expandability and a more compact bread structure (Srivastava & Semwal, 2015). Esteller et al. (2004) proposed that the increase in bread hardness was caused by starch degradation, inhibition of gluten network development, reduced dough gas retention, and decreased water content in the dough after baking.

TPC of sandwich bread increased with increasing SPB powder replacement ratio (Table 3). TPC was lowest in bread prepared from sample B0 (1.72 mg GAE/g) and highest in sample B4 (36.11 mg GAE/g). Elkatry et al. (2023) reported that increasing the proportion of sweet potato by-product flour blend in the formulation could significantly increase the polyphenol content of sandwich bread. Polyphenol content in sandwich bread is known to have antioxidant properties and beneficial effects on human health such as hepatoprotective, cardioprotective, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, anti-hypertensive, lipid and glucose metabolism regulation (Naveed et al., 2018).

The color of sandwich bread showed significant differences between samples (Table 3). The L* value was highest in sample B0 (69.40) and lowest in sample B4 (55.66). Similar results have been reported. The color of bread products is significantly affected by the replacement of wheat flour with the addition of other flours (Elkatry et al., 2023; Thuy et al., 2023). Wheat flour is generally considered white due to all brans have been removed (Thuy et al., 2023).

Based on the above results, sample B2 was selected because it demonstrated a reasonable balance between technological quality and nutritional value. Although the expansion volume decreased compared to the control, B2 still maintained acceptable leavening ability and texture (sensory evaluation results not presented here), while the hardness did not increase excessively. At the same time, the TPC increased significantly compared to B0 and B1, showing a clear improvement in functional value. Therefore, B2 is considered the best replacement, ensuring increased nutritional value without seriously degrading bread quality.

Quality comparison of sandwich bread samples (control sample B0 and B2 - sample replaced 15% of wheat flour with SPB powder)

Two sandwich bread samples, the control sample (B0) and the sandwich bread sample (B2), were tested and compared for quality (Table 4). The results showed that replacing some of the wheat flour with SPB powder had a substantial effect on the quality of the sandwich bread. The sandwich bread sample (B2) made with 15% SPB powder had a higher carbohydrate and ash content (52.15% and 2.30%) than sample B0 (48.48% and 1.47%, respectively). The total fiber concentration was particularly high, at 4.76 times that of the control sample. According to Liew (2020), fiber content lowers the risk of cardiovascular illness, digestive tract problems, colon cancer, high blood sugar, and obesity. Furthermore, sample B2 had a lower moisture content than the control sample, whereas the protein and fat contents were the same. It is also obvious that the TPC of sample B2 has improved greatly (20.88 mg GAE/g) compared to sample B0 (1.72 mg GAE/g). Polyphenols have a crucial function in protecting the organism from external stressors and eliminating reactive oxygen species (ROS), which cause a range of illnesses (Rana et al., 2022). As a result of this increasing and decreasing tendency, the quality of sandwich bread B2 has substantially increased, making it a product that is beneficial to human health when ingested.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of sandwich bread

SEM images of the bread samples prepared from all-wheat flour (sample B0) and the sample with 15% SPB powder replacement are shown in Fig. 4. Fig. 4a shows that the sandwich bread sample B0 has a well-distributed gluten structure and pores. The sponge-like crumb structure of the bread shows that the gluten framework forms a solid shape. The gluten network of this sample is well developed with a few starch granules prominent at ×100 magnification.

SEM of sandwich bread (a) sample B0 (control sample) and (b) sample B2 (15% SPB powder), particle size 1 mm (×50) and 500 μm (×100).

The interaction of gelatinized starch with the gluten network was clearly observed in sandwich bread with a part of SPB powder replacing (Fig. 4b), the surface of the sandwich bread crumb was rougher. Most of the air pockets were spherical or oval in shape due to cross-linking between the gluten networks. This connection made the cell wall more stable and stronger. This finding is consistent with the results of Rojas et al. (2000). At the same time, a large number of protruding particles were observed on the surface of the sandwich bread crumb with SPB flour adding samples, probably due to the dilution of the gluten content after adding ingredients containing high fiber content (Xin et al., 2022). When replacing part of the flour with SPB powder, the fiber content is higher, so it absorbs water more strongly than gluten, so the water between the flour components moves to the fiber, causing the gluten to not bind with enough water, resulting in a fragile and brittle gluten matrix.

Furthermore, physical interruption of the gluten–starch matrix by fiber particles weakens gas cell walls, reducing gas retention efficiency during proofing and baking (Wang et al., 2002). The sandwich bread surface can be observed to be rough and uneven; the gluten matrix is somewhat agglomerated and interrupted by starch granules. In addition, fragmentation of the gluten–starch matrix may accelerate moisture redistribution, promoting amylopectin retrogradation and crumb firming (Gray & Bemiller, 2003).

Bread crumb can be regarded as a thermally set starch-based foam formed during fermentation and baking. Gas cells nucleate and expand within the viscoelastic gluten–starch matrix during proofing, and baking fixes this porous structure through starch gelatinisation and protein denaturation. Shogren et al. (1998) characterized baked starch foams as systems with porous interiors and denser outer regions, linking foam density and cell-wall thickness to starch composition. Thus, bread crumb is more appropriately interpreted as a stabilised starch foam rather than a simple heterogeneous matrix.

The final gas cell morphology depends strongly on formulation and processing. Crumb porosity, cell size distribution, and wall thickness are governed by gas generation, expansion, coalescence, and stabilisation phenomena (Rathnayake et al., 2018; Scanlon & Zghal, 2001). In wheat dough, gas cell stability is mainly controlled by the gluten network, which forms elastic films around expanding bubbles (Gan et al., 1995).

Therefore, SPB enrichment acts as a structural modifier of the foam matrix. The addition of fiber-rich, non-gluten components dilutes and disrupts gluten continuity, weakening gas cell walls and reducing gas retention. Consequently, loaf expansion decreases and crumb density increases. Recent studies on extrusion-foamed starch systems and 3D-printed porous starch-based foods (Esposito et al., 2024; Lombardi et al., 2025), confirm that starch-rich materials behave as engineered foams whose structure is governed by composition and rheology. Accordingly, SPB-enriched bread can be viewed as a modified starch–protein foam in which fiber incorporation alters cell wall properties, foam density, and pore structure.

Conclusion

The novelty of this research lies in utilizing sweet potato by-products through processing to create functional flour and incorporating them into bread recipes. The study integrated physicochemical characterization, microstructure evaluation (SEM), and molecular structure analysis (FTIR) to clarify the structure-function relationship of sweet potato flour in a wheat-based system. Furthermore, determining the optimal substitution level of 15% provides a scientifically proven balance between enhanced nutritional value and maintained technological quality. These findings contribute new insights into the interaction mechanisms between lignocellulose-rich by-products and the gluten network, promoting the sustainable application of agricultural by-products in functional bread products. Research on the efficient use of other parts of the sweet potato for processing useful products to minimize environmental pollution and promote biodegradability is also a societal concern. This research is also one of the circular economy solutions in Vietnamese agriculture, aiming for sustainable development.
