Epidemiology of Aspergillosis Diagnoses in US Adults Using a National EHR Database, 2013–2023
Authors: Brittany L Morgan Bustamante, Elijah G Martinez, Aidan Lee, Natalie J Kane, Simon K Camponuri, Rose M Reynolds, Theo T Snow, Juliana G E Bartels, Mark Hoffman, Theodore C White, Justin V Remais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAspergillosis is a fungal infection associated with rising hospitalizations and substantial morbidity and mortality. In the United States, data remain fragmented due to the absence of centralized surveillance. This study aimed to evaluate demographic, geographic, and temporal trends in aspergillosis diagnoses across the United States and evaluate changes in those patterns following the emergence of COVID-19.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using electronic health record data from 142 US healthcare systems (Oracle Health), including adults aged ≥18 years who received care between 2013 and 2023. The cohort included over 76 million patients and 127 million person-years. Aspergillosis prevalence was calculated using post-stratification weights. Adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were estimated via quasi-Poisson and Bayesian spatiotemporal regression. COVID-19-related shifts were evaluated using estimated marginal means.ResultsFrom 2013 to 2023, aspergillosis prevalence increased by 5% annually, peaking in 2022. Rhode Island had the highest state-level aPR; Utah the lowest. Diagnosis was higher among males (aPR 1.37), older adults (≥65 years vs 18–24 years: aPR 4.95), and urban residents (rural aPR 0.86). Following the emergence of COVID-19, prevalence increased disproportionately among Hispanic or Latino patients and several racial minority groups. A nonsignificant upward trend was also observed among rural residents.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive national assessment of aspergillosis diagnosis patterns in the United States, revealing rising prevalence and shifts in affected populations following the emergence of COVID-19. These findings may aid earlier clinical recognition, especially among groups not traditionally considered high-risk, and support efforts to expand diagnostic access and improve fungal disease surveillance.

Aspergillosis is an infection caused by Aspergillus spp., a ubiquitous mold found indoors and outdoors [1]. Over 24 Aspergillus spp. can infect humans, most commonly Aspergillus fumigatus, Aspergillus terreus, and Aspergillus flavus [2], causing a range of syndromes depending on host immunity status [3]. Most individuals inhale Aspergillus spores daily without consequence, but immunocompromised individuals or those with comorbidities can develop infections from mild to life-threatening [4]. Invasive pulmonary and allergic aspergillosis pose serious risks to immunocompromised individuals, whereas immunocompetent individuals may experience less severe forms, such as developing aspergillomas [5, 6].

Despite the ubiquity of Aspergillus, aspergillosis epidemiology remains incomplete, partly due to variability in diagnosis and reporting [7]. Global estimates suggest 250 000 cases of invasive aspergillosis and >3 million cases of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis annually [8, 9]. In the United States, the absence of centralized surveillance has made it difficult to assess national trends, even as aspergillosis-related hospitalizations increased by more than 44% between 2004 and 2013 [10]. Growing concerns about antifungal resistance [11], rising healthcare costs (>$670 million annually and $36 867 per hospitalization) [12], and persistently high mortality—∼20% overall and nearly 100% if the infection becomes systemic [13, 14]—underscore the need for more comprehensive monitoring and analysis.

Aspergillosis is more often diagnosed in urban patients [15] and is one of the few fungal infections in which one disease state, pulmonary aspergillosis, is more common among higher-income individuals [16]. Allergic forms of aspergillosis appear to be most prevalent in the southern United States, disproportionately affecting Black or African American populations [17]. Combined, these patterns suggest potential influences from environmental exposures, structural inequities, and healthcare access. However, these factors remain underexplored, in part due to fragmented data. Without a comprehensive epidemiologic picture, it is difficult to disentangle the role of mediating and moderating factors in disease prevalence.

The COVID-19 pandemic heightened aspergillosis awareness, as COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA) emerged as a severe complication among critically ill COVID patients [18]. This brought renewed attention to the clinical significance of aspergillosis and underscored the importance of timely diagnosis and treatment [19]. The pandemic also introduced substantial shifts in healthcare utilization, including changes in hospital admission patterns, care-seeking behavior, and diagnostic practices that could have influenced the observed incidence of fungal infections. Understanding how these shifts may have affected aspergillosis diagnoses is critical for interpreting trends and preparing for future respiratory disease outbreaks.

A major challenge in assessing aspergillosis burden in the United States is the lack of surveillance [20]. Prior studies have largely been limited to insured populations [17, 21], inpatient vs outpatient comparisons [22], specific disease states [10, 23, 24], and state- or facility-specific assessments [25–27]. This study aims to provide updated, population-wide estimates of aspergillosis diagnoses in US adults using a large, multistate electronic health record (EHR) dataset spanning various care settings.

By identifying geographic regions with rising case counts and estimating demographic differences in prevalence, this study offers insights into the spatial and social patterns of aspergillosis in the United States. It also examines how the COVID-19 pandemic shaped diagnostic patterns, laying the foundation for understanding how social and structural dimensions of the pandemic, such as differential susceptibility, disrupted healthcare access, and uneven exposure, may have influenced incidence. Together, these analyses contribute a comprehensive, population-wide perspective of this opportunistic infection's evolving epidemiology.

METHODS

Study Design

Data were extracted from Oracle EHR Real World Data (OERWD), a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant database covering >76 million patients across 142 health systems. OERWD contains longitudinal EHR data, allowing patients’ encounters and diagnoses to be tracked over time within participating health systems; however, it does not include information that identifies whether all of a patient's care occurs within those health systems, so periods without encounters may reflect care received elsewhere rather than absence of care. We created a retrospective cohort by querying OERWD for all patients who had at least one healthcare encounter with any recorded diagnosis each year from 1 January 2013 to 31 December 2023. Patient totals per year were stratified by self-identified sex/gender (male or female), race (American Indian/Alaskan Native, Black or African American, Asian/Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, White, and some other race), ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino and non-Hispanic or Latino), residence type (urban or rural, as classified according to the US Department of Agriculture's Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes) [28], age (18–34, 35–64, ≥65 years), and state of residence. Aspergillosis cases were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th or 10th revision codes (Supplementary Table 1). Because this study estimates prevalence rather than incidence, both new and chronic cases were included in the analysis. To avoid counting follow-up visits as separate cases, we collapsed any aspergillosis-related encounters occurring within 12 months of the most recent code. Encounters >12 months apart were treated as distinct cases, which may reflect persistent or recurrent disease. Since ICD-9 codes do not differentiate between disease manifestations, all aspergillosis codes were included in the analysis. To assess the potential impact of this approach, we conducted an exploratory review of ICD-10 codes (Supplementary Table 1)—which differentiate between syndromes—for the years in which they were available.

Statistical Analysis

To adjust for differential subgroup representation in OERWD relative to the US population, we calculated nationally representative population-based prevalence estimates using post-stratification weighting [29]. To generate population-based estimates, the OERWD cohort was aggregated to match the American Community Survey (ACS) strata (year, sex/gender, race/ethnicity, age group, and state) [30], and post-stratified weights (Supplementary Equation 1) were applied to adjust for demographic differences [31]. Weights were based on ACS 5-year population estimates, ensuring that all reported prevalence estimates reflect population-adjusted rates, not raw case counts.

To examine geographic and temporal patterns in the continental United States, we implemented a Bayesian hierarchical spatiotemporal model using Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) [32]. Weighted annual case counts per state were modeled using Poisson regression. Spatial dependence was accounted for using a Besag-York-Mollié model, which incorporates structured and unstructured spatial random effects based on a state boundary adjacency matrix. This modeling approach helps account for unmeasured area-level factors and spatial autocorrelation in prevalence that may arise from geographic variation in unmeasured confounders. Temporal trends were modeled using a first-order random walk latent effect, allowing for smooth year-to-year variation. State-specific random slopes captured differential temporal patterns across states. The model was adjusted for sex, race/ethnicity, and age group. Expected cases in each state, computed via indirect standardization using national age, year, and race/ethnicity stratified rates in the SpatialEpi package [33], were included as an offset and served as the population-based reference for estimating state-level adjusted prevalence ratios (aPR) and 95% credible intervals (CrIs), relative to the national average.

To compare prevalence across demographic groups, we fit a multivariable quasi-Poisson model for all patients with complete demographic data with the log of the OERWD cohort as an offset. Because post-stratification weights could not be calculated for residence type strata, we used robust standard errors to generate population-based estimates [34]. aPRs and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated from a model including fixed effects for sex, race, ethnicity, residence type, age group, state, and calendar year. To assess COVID-19's impact on prevalence trends, we centered year at 2019 and included interaction terms between a binary COVID-19 indicator (0 = pre-COVID-19, 1 = COVID-19 years) with race, ethnicity, and residence type to examine subgroup-specific changes, along with a COVID-by-year interaction to adjust for shifts in temporal trends during the pandemic. Post hoc analyses estimated subgroup changes in prevalence (post- vs pre-COVID-19) by demographic and residence group. Additional method details can be found in Supplementary A.

RESULTS

OERWD Cohort and Aspergillosis Patient Characteristics

The OERWD cohort included 76 192 643 patients and 127 414 388 person-years over the 11-year study period (Table 1). Most of the cohort self-identified as White (73.7%), non-Hispanic or Latino (75.9%), female (58.5%), aged 35 to 64 years (46.8%), and resided in urban areas (81.2%; Table 1). A total of 20 764 aspergillosis cases were identified in the cohort. Aspergillosis cases were more likely to be male (50.5%) compared to the cohort (41.4%) and were older, with nearly half aged 65 years and over (47.9% vs 27.9% in the cohort; Table 1). While the racial distribution was similar, Asian/Pacific Islander individuals were slightly overrepresented (3.4% in the cases and 2.5% in the cohort), as were individuals identifying as “some other race” (10.5% in the cases and 8.0% in the cohort; Table 1). Urban residence was more common among aspergillosis cases (83.7%) than the cohort (81.2%; Table 1). Geographically, the highest percentage of cases was reported in California (23.9%), and the lowest in Louisiana (<1%; Supplementary Figure 1). Among patients with ICD-10 codes differentiating disease manifestations, the distribution of demographic subgroups was similar to that observed for all aspergillosis cases (Table 1).

Aspergillosis Prevalence and Spatiotemporal Trends

The population-based aspergillosis prevalence among US adults from 2013 to 2023 was 15.26 diagnoses per 100 000 person-years. Prevalence peaked in 2022 at 18.04 diagnoses per 100 000 person-years, with the lowest recorded prevalence in 2013 (11.99 diagnoses per 100 000 person-years; Supplementary Figure 2). Prevalence was highest in Rhode Island and Arizona, and lowest in Utah and Nevada (Figure 1A). There was substantial variation in aspergillosis across the continental US Rhode Island's prevalence, while decreasing over the study period, substantially exceeded the national average each year, ranging from an aPR of 11.04 (95% CrI: 6.74–17.09) in 2013 to 6.20 (95% CrI: 3.75–9.59) in 2023 (Figure 1B), indicating an observed prevalence 6 to 11 times higher than expected. In contrast, Utah remained well below the national average, with aPR decreasing from 0.20 (95% CrI: 0.09–0.38) in 2013 to 0.17 (95% CrI: 0.07–0.35) in 2023 (Figure 1B). Alabama experienced the largest aPR increase from 0.31 (95% CrI: 0.20–0.46) in 2013 to 1.42 (95% CrI: 1.14–1.72) in 2023 (Figure 1B). California exhibited a steady upward trend, increasing from an aPR of 1.00 (95% CrI: 0.92–1.09) in 2013 to 1.81 (95% CrI: 1.71–1.91) in 2023 (Supplementary Table 2). Several other states—including Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri, Nebraska, New Jersey, New Mexico, and South Dakota—had substantial prevalence increases (Supplementary Table 2). While the magnitude varied, most states showed rising aPRs over the study period, peaking between 2020 and 2022 (Supplementary Figure 3).

Aspergillosis Prevalence by Sex, Race, Ethnicity, Age, and Residence Type

A total of 111 203 140 person-years in the cohort (87.3%) had complete demographic data and were included in the multivariable analysis. Over the study period, prevalence was significantly higher in males compared to females (aPR: 1.37; 95% CI: 1.32–1.42) and among patients aged 65 years relative to those aged 18 to 34 years (aPR: 4.95; 95% CI: 4.65–5.28; Table 2). Prevalence among American Indian/Alaska Native and Asian/Pacific Islander patients was not significantly different from White patients (Table 2). Patients identifying as Black or African American and “some other race” had slightly higher aspergillosis prevalence compared to White patients (aPR: 1.08; 95% CI: 1.01–1.16 and aPR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.16–1.33, respectively, Table 2). Hispanic or Latino patients were less likely to be diagnosed with aspergillosis than non-Hispanic or Latino patients (aPR: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.87–0.99), as were rural residents when compared to those living in urban areas (aPR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.84–0.93; Table 2).

Aspergillosis Prevalence and the COVID-19 Pandemic

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, aspergillosis prevalence increased steadily by ∼5.3% per year (95% CI: 4.0%–6.9%; Table 2). Following the emergence of COVID-19, the annual increase in aspergillosis prevalence stabilized, with a trend decrease of 5.2% (95% CI: 2.6%–7.7%; Table 2) during the post-COVID period compared to the pre-COVID period. Annual cohort size remained stable through 2020 (Supplementary Figure 4). Accounting for this shift in trend, post hoc analyses revealed disproportionate increases in certain demographic subgroups (Supplementary Table 3). Within all racial groups, Hispanic or Latino patients experienced a larger relative increase in prevalence post-COVID-19 compared to pre-COVID-19 (aPR: 1.36; 95% CI: 1.15–1.61) than did non-Hispanic or Latino patients (aPR: 1.15; 95% CI: 0.99–1.34; Figure 2A), after adjusting for age, sex, year, residence type, and state of residence. Rural residents of all races also had a slightly greater increase in prevalence post-COVID-19 relative to the prepandemic period (aPR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.09–1.53) than urban residents (aPR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.05–1.42; Figure 2B). Patients identifying as Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Hispanic or Latino (any race) experienced significantly greater post-COVID-19 increases in prevalence compared to White and non-Hispanic or Latino patients, with relative increases of 32% (95% CI: 26%–39%), 18% (95% CI: 11%–24%), and 21% (95% CI: 16%–25%), respectively (Supplementary Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study provides a comprehensive analysis of aspergillosis trends in the United States over an 11-year period, leveraging a large, diverse cohort weighted to generate nationally representative estimates. Results suggest an overall increase in aspergillosis prevalence between 2013 and 2023, peaking in 2022. Previous research has documented both increasing and decreasing trends of Aspergillus-associated infections in the United States [17, 22, 23, 35]. However, these studies were limited to specific forms of the disease, focused solely on inpatient populations, or restricted to high-risk groups. Our work builds upon these efforts by providing a broader evaluation of aspergillosis prevalence across a longer timeframe, diverse healthcare settings, demographic subgroups, and all-payer types, while applying national weighting to improve representativeness.

Consistent with prior research [24, 36], non-Hispanic or Latino White patients were most likely to be aspergillosis cases. This pattern may reflect differences in underlying conditions that predispose individuals to aspergillosis, such as transplantation, pulmonary disease, and immunosuppressive therapies, which are more common in this population [14, 37]. Aspergillosis was also more common in urban than rural patients, which may reflect socioeconomic factors. Previous studies have shown aspergillosis is more prevalent in individuals from higher-income areas [16]—populations more often concentrated in urban regions—possibly due to greater access to specialized healthcare, higher rates of transplantation, and better management of chronic conditions like cystic fibrosis [15, 35].

From 2013 to 2019, aspergillosis prevalence appeared to increase steadily, with trends persisting after adjustment for demographic and geographic factors. After the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, this trend in prevalence significantly slowed. The reasons for the prepandemic increase remain unclear but may reflect changes in underlying host risk (eg, rising use of immunosuppressive therapies, aging population with more at-risk patients) [10], diagnostic practices [38], or heightened clinical awareness [39]. In contrast, the decreasing postpandemic trend may be attributable to changes in healthcare utilization [40], shifts in healthcare priorities [41], public health interventions (eg, masking, reduced travel) [42], or a possible “saturation effect” (ie high-risk populations captured early in the pandemic) [43].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify COVID-19-associated shifts in aspergillosis prevalence patterns. We observed notable shifts in the demographic groups most likely to be diagnosed following the pandemic, with disproportionate increases among Hispanic or Latino patients, several racial minority groups, and suggestive increased prevalence among rural residents. These differences may reflect variations in baseline health, access to timely diagnosis and treatment, or the disproportionate impact of COVID-19 [44, 45]. Hispanic and rural patients may have faced greater exposure risks or delayed healthcare access, compounding existing trends [46]. Additionally, surveillance and ascertainment bias may have affected pandemic-period estimates of aspergillosis burden in these populations. Hispanic and rural patients experienced disproportionately high rates of hospitalization [47, 48], settings in which more extensive diagnostic workups are routinely performed. Increased access to high-acuity care during the pandemic may therefore have elevated the likelihood of detecting aspergillosis independent of changes in underlying risk. The higher post-COVID-19 prevalence observed among Asian/Pacific Islander and American Indian/Alaska Native patients compared to White patients further underscores the importance of considering structural and social determinants of health in future studies [49, 50]. Evidence suggests that conventional risk factors for invasive aspergillosis are uncommon among individuals with CAPA [51], and recent studies demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 infection itself is a risk factor for CAPA through several pathophysiological mechanisms, including virus-mediated immune dysregulation and lung tissue damage [52–54]. These patterns suggest that pandemic-associated demographic shifts in aspergillosis risk may reflect a combination of changes in healthcare access, differential rates of severe disease, and variations in hospitalization and intensive care unit admissions, surveillance effects, and biological changes in susceptibility directly related to COVID-19 pathophysiology. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these patterns and to identify targeted interventions to mitigate risk.

Our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the spatial and temporal patterns in aspergillosis prevalence at the state level across the continental United States. A recent analysis using three-digit ZIP code and county-level data reported substantial geographic heterogeneity in invasive aspergillosis incidence, with the highest incidence observed in parts of the western and central United States [55]. Consistent with their findings, we observed higher prevalence in California, Arizona, and New Mexico, as well as in Kansas, Nebraska, and South Dakota. We identified higher prevalence in several southeastern states and in Rhode Island, and lower prevalence in Nevada, Wyoming, and Colorado, patterns that were not observed in the regional analysis [55]. This may be due to slight differences in the outcomes examined, the spatial scale of analysis, and the underlying data sources, as the prior study relied on Medicare claims capturing primarily older, insured adults, whereas our analysis used longitudinal EHR data from a broader patient population. Rhode Island consistently had the highest aPR throughout the study period, which may reflect a true increase in disease prevalence, given its population structure aligns with groups identified in the literature as being at higher risk. However, this may also reflect the state's small population size or uneven healthcare system coverage in the dataset. Although post-stratification weights were applied to enhance representativeness, residual bias may persist due to unmeasured factors not included in our weighting scheme, such as chronic disease burden, diagnostic practices, or healthcare-seeking behavior. Importantly, exclusion of Rhode Island in sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 6) did not materially alter state-level or national estimates, and all substantive interpretations remained unchanged. Several other states—including Alabama, Delaware, Kentucky, Missouri, Nebraska, New Jersey, New Mexico, and South Dakota—experienced statistically significant increases in prevalence over time. These findings underscore the need for research on how healthcare access, socioeconomic conditions, and urbanization shape aspergillosis distribution [16, 56–58].

As with all EHR-based analyses, variability in coding practices, case definitions, patient populations, and geographic representation may introduce misclassification or bias. A strength of this study is the use of post-stratification weights to enhance generalizability and conducted data quality checks, including excluding implausible records and restricting to diagnosis-linked encounters for weighting. These steps help reduce biases related to health system-specific data flows and differential completeness across contributing sites [59].

Our findings should be interpreted considering the clinical heterogeneity of aspergillosis. Allergic, pulmonary, and invasive forms differ in pathophysiology, risk factors, and management but were combined into a composite outcome due to coding limitations. To evaluate whether aggregating clinical manifestations could obscure meaningful differences, we conducted descriptive comparisons (Table 1) and a sensitivity analysis restricted to years with complete ICD-10 capture, examining demographic patterns separately for allergic, invasive, and pulmonary aspergillosis. Across most demographic covariates, associations were directionally consistent and varied modestly in magnitude (Supplementary Table 5). For example, estimated aPRs across subtypes differed by <20%–40% for sex, African American/Black race, Asian/Pacific Islander race, and rural residence. Larger variation in effect magnitude was observed for American Indian or Alaska Native race, consistent with sparse subtype-specific counts and wide uncertainty. For Hispanic ethnicity, effect estimates varied modestly across subtypes, which may reflect differences in the clinical contexts in which allergic vs invasive aspergillosis is diagnosed, rather than statistical instability. Age demonstrated greater heterogeneity in effect magnitude across subtypes, with substantially stronger associations observed for invasive aspergillosis than for allergic or pulmonary forms, consistent with the known age- and immunosuppression-dependence of invasive disease. Importantly, however, the direction of association with older age was consistent across all subtypes. Overall, we did not observe evidence of fundamentally different demographic patterns across clinical subtypes that would alter the primary conclusions of the study (Supplementary Table 5).

Individuals were not followed over time to assess when, or if, they became immunocompromised and thus at risk for invasive aspergillosis. While risk for invasive aspergillosis depends on host factors, our goal was to describe diagnostic prevalence patterns, not estimate time-to-event risk. Case definitions relied on ICD codes, which are subject to potential inaccuracy [60]. Previous studies have estimated these codes have ∼84% sensitivity and 65%–67% positive predictive values for aspergillosis [61, 62]. This implies that some misclassification and false-positive diagnoses are expected. Although heightened clinical awareness during the COVID-19 pandemic could have increased coding of suspected cases, the absence of a sharp, short-term spike in prevalence suggests that any such effect and misclassification did not drive the overall temporal trends observed and is likely to be relatively nondifferential over time. While future studies should aim to replicate these analyses among culture-confirmed cases, culturing rates in the United States remain low [62]. As such, a comprehensive understanding of the disease's epidemiology will require integrating findings from both clinical and microbiologically confirmed datasets. Finally, because aspergillosis is not a nationally reportable disease, no surveillance system or gold standard currently exists to validate our estimates.

These findings provide one of the most comprehensive, nationally representative assessments to date of aspergillosis prevalence across US adults, revealing important temporal, geographic, and demographic patterns. We documented an overall increase in aspergillosis diagnoses over the past decade, peaking in 2022, with shifts in groups most affected during the COVID-19 pandemic across race, ethnicity, and geography. These patterns suggest a shift in the populations receiving an aspergillosis diagnosis compared with historical assumptions about those at greatest risk. By characterizing current demographic and geographic patterns of diagnosed cases, these findings provide baseline data that could inform the design of future surveillance efforts, guide outreach to healthcare settings serving newly affected populations, and inform clinician education on atypical presentations. Together, these efforts may support earlier consideration of aspergillosis and reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment.
