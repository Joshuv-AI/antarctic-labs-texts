The unscientific nature of the arguments of “Restorative Reproductive Medicine” and why we need to understand them
Authors: Richard J. Paulson
Published: 2025

There is nothing new about the malignment of in vitro fertilization (IVF). Perhaps the most vilified of all was Robert Edwards, whose accomplishments in developing the techniques of IVF were eventually rewarded with a Nobel Prize. Before Louise Brown was even born, the lay press turned him into the subject of endless cartoons and criticisms, which ridiculed his efforts and painted him as a modern-day Dr. Frankenstein. Over the years, many ideologically based organizations have expressed their opposition to IVF, most of it stemming from concerns about the formation of embryos in the laboratory. It is well established that human reproduction is inefficient and that very few embryos succeed in forming a viable pregnancy; nowhere is that inefficiency more clearly visible than when those embryos are observed in the IVF laboratory. Simplistic reasoning ignores embryo losses in vivo and concludes that since embryos are lost during the IVF procedure, then IVF must therefore be unethical.

Most antagonism to IVF stems from the belief that human embryos are human beings and that they possess “personhood.” Thus, any harm that comes to them is tantamount to battery or even murder. The unsophisticated and unscientific view that life begins with fertilization also implies that life is somehow created in the IVF laboratory. This concept stems from an ignorance of the biological reality that IVF laboratories do not create life, but rather, facilitate its continuation (1, 2). It is worth pointing out that no holy book has ever posited the concept that life begins at fertilization, because the writers of these books had no concept of the mechanics of reproduction. All contemporary prohibitions and limitations on reproduction were written by human beings, interpreting history and presenting their view of how the world should be run. However, hostility toward fertility treatment goes beyond the simple antipathy to seeing human embryos in the laboratory. As practitioners of fertility-promoting care, we should have a perspective on the arguments that threaten it.

When IVF was relatively new, there were no specific prohibitions or religious guidelines for what part of fertility care would or would not be acceptable. It was known that in Rome, Pope John Paul II charged then Cardinal Ratzinger (3) (later Pope Benedict) with drafting a suitable document. Fertility specialists at the time were skeptical that the conservative pope would give his blessing to the IVF procedure. However, most were optimistic about the acceptability of intrauterine insemination, especially when only the sperm of the husband were to be used. There was also general optimism about the gamete intrafallopian tube transfer (GIFT) procedure. In the GIFT procedure, eggs were collected after ovarian stimulation, mixed with sperm, and the resulting combination was injected into the end of the fallopian tube via laparoscopy. In practice, it was possible to separate the eggs from the sperm in the transfer catheter by simply maintaining a small air bubble between the two columns of culture medium. In this manner, the eggs and sperm would not “meet” until they were back inside the body of the female partner. This small modification, we thought, would mollify the concerns of those who did not want fertilization to take place in the laboratory.

Alas, our optimism was misplaced. The resulting document, “Donum vitae,” was released on February 22, 1987, by the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith and signed by Cardinal Ratzinger (3). Its official title was “INSTRUCTION ON RESPECT FOR HUMAN LIFE IN ITS ORIGIN AND ON THE DIGNITY OF PROCREATION; REPLIES TO CERTAIN QUESTIONS OF THE DAY.” It specified (quoting Pope John XXIII from 1961) that “The transmission of human life is entrusted by nature to a personal and conscious act and as such is subject to the all-holy laws of God: immutable and inviolable laws which must be recognized and observed. For this reason one cannot use means and follow methods which could be licit in the transmission of the life of plants and animals." The basic message of the document was that no manipulation of the gametes was ever permissible. I like to paraphrase these words in the language of the Ten Commandments, “Thou shalt not touch the gametes.”

Our program for IVF was in operation in 1987, and we had a patient in the ovarian stimulation phase of her IVF treatment. Midway through her cycle, Donum Vitae was released. The patient, whose family was very religious, returned to the office, and with tears in her eyes, returned the stimulation medications and abandoned further IVF treatment. I was struck by the amount of distress and personal sacrifice that her faith demanded of her.

Therefore, it is not surprising to me that we are once again faced with a reboot of opposition to IVF, this time from a well-known, politically well-connected source. The Heritage Foundation, famous for its Project 2025, has decided to wade into the question of fertility treatment and has coined the term “Restorative Reproductive Medicine.” In the published document, entitled “Treating Infertility: The New Frontier Of Reproductive Medicine (4),” the investigators state, “Infertility is not a disease or condition. Instead, it is a symptom of underlying reproductive dysfunction and can often present with other symptoms.” This statement directly contradicts the World Health Organization (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/infertility), the American Medical Association, and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, all of whom define infertility as a disease.

Neither the arguments against IVF nor the simple fertility treatments proposed by Restorative Reproductive Medicine are novel. The Heritage Foundation Report is garnering attention primarily because the Heritage Foundation has demonstrated significant influence on the current administration. This makes the scientific inaccuracy of the publication much more dangerous. The investigators do not admit that their conclusions are based on their faith. Instead, they claim that their conclusions are scientifically accurate and use misleading language that implies that mainstream medicine is flawed and that patients need to be “restored.” They ignore the irrefutable evidence that simple fertility treatment cannot bypass severe male factor infertility or produce a pregnancy in a woman without functional fallopian tubes.

As physicians and scientists, our duty is to uphold the integrity of medical care by defending it against ideologically driven distortions masquerading as science. “Restorative Reproductive Medicine” is not a novel clinical paradigm—it is an unscientific, politically motivated rejection of modern reproductive science. Although we must respect individual faith-based decisions, it is imperative that public health policy and patient care remain rooted in empirical evidence. Allowing religious doctrine or political agendas to dictate medical standards endangers not only patients facing infertility but also the broader principle that medical care should be determined by what is safe, effective, and evidence-based (5).
