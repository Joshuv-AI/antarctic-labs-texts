Antimicrobial resistance in bacterial pathogens from postoperative surgical site infections in a tertiary care centre in India
Authors: Atul Singh, Mukta Sharma, Ashok Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections (SSIs) remain a significant cause of postoperative morbidity and are often associated with multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms. This study aimed to isolate, identify, and evaluate the antibiotic susceptibility patterns of bacterial pathogens from postoperative wound infections in a tertiary care center.MethodsA postoperative cross-sectional study conducted on 104 wound swab samples were collected from patients aged 10–90 years who underwent surgeries including exploratory laparotomy, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, and Colectomy, Hernia, Ulcer, and Cancer surgeries. Bacterial identification was carried out using selective culture media and biochemical tests, while antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method in accordance with Clinical and laboratory standard institute (CLSI) guidelines.ResultOut of 104 samples, 96 (92.31%) showed positive bacterial growth, while 8 exhibited no growth. Among the culture-positive samples, Staphylococcus aureus was isolated in 18 cases (13.10%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated in 24 cases (17.40%). Further, 33.33% isolates of S. aureus and 37.50% isolates of the P. aeruginosa showed multidrug-resistant. However, antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that S. aureus showed 100% sensitivity to linezolid and 72.22% sensitivity to tetracycline while P. aeruginosa showed 75% and 70.83% sensitive to ofloxacin and piperacillin, respectively.ConclusionLinezolid and ofloxacin emerged as the most effective agents against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa respectively. These findings highlight the importance of continuous antimicrobial resistance surveillance to guide appropriate empirical therapy for SSIs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04480-9.

Introduction

Surgical site infections are infections that develop at surgical incisions following an operation. They are among the most common postoperative complications, contributing significantly to morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs [1]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), SSIs typically occur within 30 days of surgery and may involve superficial tissues, deeper structures, or even multiple organs [2]. The infection might be superficial or deep, as well as one that affects multiple organs or body parts. After surgery, postoperative SSIs are among the most common complications that patients experience [3, 4]. The risk of SSIs is particularly high following procedures such as exploratory laparotomy, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, colectomy, hernia repair, ulcer, and cancer surgeries. Pathogens can gain access to surgical wounds through various routes, including direct contact, self-contamination, and airborne transmission. Inadequate sterilization practices and poor hand hygiene among healthcare workers are considered major contributing factors [5, 6]. The most commonly implicated organisms include Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, coagulase-negative staphylococci (CONS), Streptococcus pyogenes, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter cloacae, Escherichia coli, and Acinetobacter baumannii [7–9].

The growing prevalence of multidrug-resistant bacteria in SSIs poses a serious clinical challenge. Contributing factors include the widespread use of prophylactic and empirical antibiotics, increasing severity of illness, and a rise in immunocompromised patients [10]. Additional host-related risk factors include smoking, alcohol use, diabetes mellitus, hypothermia, prolonged steroid use, and poor skin hygiene [11]. The World Health Organization has recognized nosocomial infections, including SSIs, as a major global health concern, with an estimated 2 million cases annually [1]. The burden is especially severe in developing countries where inappropriate antimicrobial prescribing practices are more common [12–15].

Recent surveillance data emphasize the rising threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in hospital settings. The ICMR Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (AMRSN) reported data from 99,492 culture-positive isolates collected across tertiary care hospitals in India. Gram-negative bacilli, particularly E. coli, K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa, were the most frequently isolated pathogens, while S. aureus predominated among Gram-positive isolates from pus/exudates. Significant declines in antibiotic susceptibility were observed, including reduced efficacy of carbapenems, piperacillin-tazobactam, and fluoroquinolones. Carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii remained critically high (88%), and metallo-β-lactamase-producing P. aeruginosa isolates increased in prevalence. Among S. aureus isolates, methicillin-resistant strains were frequent, although vancomycin and teicoplanin retained near-universal effectiveness. These findings underscore the need for robust antibiogram-guided empirical therapy, strengthened infection prevention and control (IPC) measures, and targeted antimicrobial stewardship, especially in intensive care units (ICUs) [16].

Recent surveillance data from India and other regions underscore the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance in healthcare and community settings. Reports indicate high resistance rates among S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and other pathogens, highlighting the urgent need for robust AMR monitoring, diagnostic stewardship, and infection prevention strategies [17]. Integrating AMR surveillance into routine clinical workflows is critical to guide empiric therapy, optimize antimicrobial use, and prevent the spread of resistant strains. This study aimed to isolate and identify bacterial pathogens associated with postoperative SSIs and to evaluate their antibiotic susceptibility profiles, with a particular focus on the prevalence of multidrug-resistant S. aureus and P. aeruginosa isolates.

Methods and Materials

Study design and setting

This is a cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of surgery, the pus/wound samples were collected from the Lala Lajpat Rai Memorial (LLRM) Medical College and Hospital, Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, India from October 2023 to May 2024, a tertiary care hospital serving both urban and rural populations. The hospital follows an institutional antibiotic policy aligned with the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) antimicrobial stewardship framework. According to this policy, broad-spectrum antibiotics such as carbapenems and higher-generation cephalosporins are reserved for severe or resistant infections, while beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones are routinely prescribed as first-line agents in surgical site infections. Restricted use of higher-end antibiotics may influence the local resistance patterns observed in this study.

Collection and transport of the samples

A total of 104 patients underwent surgical procedures of exploratory laparotomy, Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy, Colectomy, Hernia, Ulcer, and Cancer for the department of surgery. Swab samples were collected from patients with postoperative SSIs. The samples from the wounds were collected using sterilized cotton swab sticks. The surgical wound cleaned with 70% ethanol, and exudates from the infected area were carefully swabbed and sent to the microbiological laboratory. Samples were promptly transported to the Microbiology laboratory at IIMT University, Meerut, for processing under specific conditions.

Patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients of all ages and sexes who developed sign and symptoms of surgical site infection following surgery during the study period were eligible. SSIs were defined according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria: deep incisional SSIs were defined as infections involving the fascia or muscle layers of the incision, occurring within 30 days after surgery.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Specimens were inoculated on selective and differential media, including mannitol salt agar, blood agar, cetrimide agar, and Mac-Conkey agar, and incubated aerobically at 37℃ for 24 h. Isolates were identified on the basis of colony morphology, Gram staining, and standard biochemical tests such as catalase, indole, coagulase, oxidase, mannitol, lactose, citrate, and urease test, respectively (Table 1) [18–24].

Antibiotic sensitivity test

Antibiotic susceptibility was determined by the Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar in accordance with the CLSI guidelines [25, 26]. Standard reference strains (S. aureus ATCC 25923, and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853) were obtained from Dr. Lal Path Labs Diagnostic center, Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, India, for use in this study. Following the zone of inhibition’s diameter test was determined, and the outcomes were categorized as sensitive (SE) and resistant (RE). The following antibiotics were used in this study to treat the isolates: for S. aureus: levofloxacin (5 𝜇g), ampicillin/sulbactam (10/10 µg), tetracycline (30 𝜇g), ciprofloxacin (5 𝜇g), linezolid (30 𝜇g), and gentamicin (10 𝜇g); for Pseudomonas aeruginosa, ampicillin/sulbactam (10/10 𝜇g), piperacillin (100 𝜇g), ciprofloxacin (5 𝜇g), ofloxacin (5 𝜇g), gentamicin (10 𝜇g), and amikacin (30 𝜇g). Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) prior to inoculation. The diameter of inhibition zones was measured and interpreted as sensitive or resistant based on CLSI breakpoints.

Statistical interpretation

The statistical analysis of the data, which was conducted using SPSS software version 20.0.1, and the findings were calculated using the Chi-square test. All statistical data were considered statistically significant if the p-value ≤ 0.05. Logistic regression has been included to explore association between risk factors and multidrug resistance.

Ethical aspects

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committees of IIMT University and LLRM Medical College Hospital, Meerut. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or from parents/guardians in the case of minors.

Antibiotic susceptibility and multidrug resistant pattern of Staphylococcus aureus

S. aureus isolates’ antibiotic sensitivity pattern revealed that all strains were highly sensitive to Linezolid 18 (100%), followed by Tetracycline 13 (72.22%), Levofloxacin 11 (61.11%), Ampicillin/sulbactam 9 (50.00%), Gentamicin 8 (44.44%), and Ciprofloxacin 2 (11.11%) antibiotics (Fig. 2). The Linezolid antibiotics were the most efficient antibiotic against Gram-positive S. aureus. The IIMT-SA17 strain showed highly sensitive, indicated by the large zone of inhibition followed by, IIMT-SA18, IIMT-SA10 and IIMT-SA09 strains, respectively. In contrast, the S. aureus showed highest multi-drug resistant against Ciprofloxacin 16 (88.89%), followed by Gentamicin 10 (55.56%), Ampicillin/sulbactam 9 (50.00%), Levofloxacin 7 (38.89%) and Tetracycline 5 (27.78%), respectively. The Linezolid antibiotic is highly sensitive (100%) to multi-drug resistant against S. aureus. The bar graph shows the resistance rate (%) of each antibiotic with values labeled on top of each bar and heatmap highlights the resistance level using a color gradient, making it easy to spot high and low resistance antibiotics (Fig. 3). The IIMT-SA11 strain exhibited the highest resistance profile that is resistant to more than three antibiotics, producing the smallest zone of inhibition, followed by strain IIMT-SA06, IIMT-SA07 and IIMT-SA04 strains, respectively.

IIMT-PA12 strain exhibited the highest level of resistance against more than three antibiotics because it forms the minimal zone of inhibition, followed by IIMT-PA13, IIMT-PA11and IIMT-PA07, respectively.

Visualization of an antibiotic sensitivity test on Mueller-Hinton agar

For the antibiotic sensitivity test, Mueller-Hinton agar is utilized, a standard culture medium suggested by the CLSI for antimicrobial susceptibility testing. This medium provides reliable and reproducible results due to its non-selective nature and ability to support the growth of a wide range of bacteria. The Plates ‘A’ and ‘B’ display the zone of inhibition for S. aureus and P. aeruginosa against a panel of antibiotics viz.: ampicillin/sulbactam, piperacillin, tetracycline, ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, levofloxacin, linezolid, gentamicin, and amikacin (Fig. 6). The diameter of inhibitory zones was used to determine the sensitivity or resistance test in accordance with CLSI guidelines. The linezolid was the most effective antibiotic and showed 100% sensitivity against S. aureus followed by tetracycline, levofloxacin, ampicillin/sulbactam, and gentamicin. Ciprofloxacin showed the least effectiveness against S. aureus, indicating a high level of resistance (88.89%). In case of P. aeruginosa, ofloxacin was the most effective it was 75% sensitivity, followed by piperacillin, amikacin, gentamicin, and ampicillin/sulbactam, respectively. The ciprofloxacin showed the least effectiveness against P. aeruginosa, indicating a high level of resistance (87.50%).

Discussion

Despite advancements in preventive measures, (SSIs) remain a significant healthcare challenge due to their high rates of morbidity and mortality. In this study, 104 samples were collected, of which 92.31% showed significant bacterial growth while 7.69% showed no growth, indicating a high incidence of SSIs. This reflects a considerable bacterial load in surgical wounds and highlights a serious concern for postoperative care. Similar finding was reported [27], in Kathmandu, Nepal, although the bacterial growth rate observed here was higher than those reported [28, 29]. These differences may be attributed to variations in hospital hygiene, environmental conditions, and infection control practices. According to [30] reported 77.4% of isolates as MDR, while in our investigation, the proportion was comparable, reflecting the ongoing challenge of antimicrobial resistance in tertiary care settings.

S. aureus was most frequently isolated from patients aged 31–50 years, while P. aeruginosa appeared more often in those aged 10–50 years. Overall, male patients showed higher infection rates than females, a trend also noted [31], who suggested that occupational exposure and lifestyle differences may contribute to this gender disparity.

S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were identified as the two predominant pathogens in surgical site at wound infections. The emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains has made the treatment of these infections increasingly difficult in both hospital and community settings [32]. SSIs are among the most common healthcare-associated infection, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 2.5 to 41.9% depending on the hospital and healthcare systems [33].

Gram-staining in this study revealed 28.98% Gram-positive and 71.02% were Gram-negative bacteria, which is consistent with findings [34], who reported higher prevalence of Gram-negative bacteria (65.7%) compared to Gram-positive bacteria (34.3%). The rate of infection varies from different country, healthcare settings and even different areas. In our study, S. aureus was 13.10% and P. aeruginosa accounted for 17.40% of infections, findings that were comparable to those reported [35]. Multiple bacterial isolates were observed in 48.08% of the samples, while 44.23% showed single isolates, and 7.69% showed no growth. In contrast [36], reported multiple isolates in only 12.14% of cases. These variations may reflect differences in patient populations, sample sources, and infection control standards. Comparable prevalence of P. aeruginosa (13%) was reported in human pus samples in Egypt, highlighting the consistent global relevance of this opportunistic pathogen in postoperative infections. Variations in prevalence across studies may reflect differences in hospital hygiene, environmental conditions, patient populations, and infection control practices [37].

Biochemical characterization confirmed that all isolates of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were catalase-positive, citrate-positive, and indole-negative. The catalase positive reaction observed in both organisms aligns with their well-known capacity to produce the catalase enzyme, which essential for protecting against oxidative stress by converting hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water [18]. Their capacity to use citrate as their only carbon source, further highlights their metabolic flexibility, particularly under nutrient limited conditions, a feature that is especially notable in opportunistic pathogens like P. aeruginosa [21]. Mannitol fermentation, a classical trait of S. aureus. The negative results for the indole test across all isolates reaffirm the established biochemical profiles of these organisms, as neither S. aureus nor P. aeruginosa generally possess the enzyme tryptophanase required for indole production [38].

The antibiotic sensitivity profile of S. aureus isolates revealed complete susceptibility to Linezolid (100%), establishing it as the most effective antibiotic. Moderate susceptibility was observed with Tetracycline, Levofloxacin, Ampicillin/Sulbactam, and Gentamicin, while Ciprofloxacin showed markedly poor activity (11.11%). Among the strains, IIMT-SA17 exhibited the highest sensitivity, as reflected by a large zone of inhibition on the antibiogram. The consistent sensitivity to Linezolid is encouraging and aligns with previous findings [39, 40]. Tetracycline also showed promising activity against the isolates, suggesting that tetracycline resistance genes like tetK and tetM may not be widely prevalent in this region. This is consistent with reports indicating that tetracycline resistance varies significantly depending on local antibiotic usage patterns [41]. Fluoroquinolones such as Levofloxacin showed intermediate sensitivity, while Ciprofloxacin showed the highest resistance rate among all tested antibiotics. This high degree of resistance to Ciprofloxacin most likely reflects mutations in DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV genes (gyrA, parC), or the operation of efflux pumps, which actively remove the antibiotic from bacterial cells [42]. Ampicillin/Sulbactam showed 50% effectiveness, possibly due to beta-lactamase inhibition by Sulbactam, although resistance suggests the presence of alternative mechanisms such as modified penicillin-binding proteins [43]. Gentamicin was likely due to aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, commonly reported in nosocomial strains [44]. Multidrug resistance was particularly notable in isolates such as IIMT-SA11, SA06, SA07, and SA04, corroborating earlier reports of S. aureus as a major MDR pathogen in SSIs [45].

Among P. aeruginosa isolates, the highest susceptibility was to Ofloxacin (75%), followed by Piperacillin, Amikacin, Gentamicin, Ampicillin/Sulbactam, and Ciprofloxacin, respectively. Ofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone, appeared to be the most effective, likely due to fewer resistance-conferring mutations in the local population [42, 43]. Piperacillin and Amikacin also showed good activity, consistent with previous reports indicating that β-lactam antibiotics and aminoglycosides remain partially effective against Gram-negative isolates in this region [35].

However, a high level of Ciprofloxacin resistance (87.50%) was observed, which aligns with global reports of P. aeruginosa resistance mediated by gyrA/parC mutations, efflux pump overexpression (e.g., MexAB-OprM), and reduced outer membrane permeability [46, 47]. The most resistant strains (IIMT-PA12, PA13, PA11, and PA07) exhibited resistance to more than three antibiotics, reflecting extensive MDR likely associated with prolonged empirical therapy in clinical settings. These findings are consistent with [36], who reported complete resistance of P. aeruginosa to Erythromycin, Amoxicillin, Colistin, and Cephradine, with retained sensitivity to Imipenem (100%), Apramycin (92.31%), and Amikacin (84.61%).

The high incidence of SSIs and MDR pathogens in this study also points to critical issues in hospital practices and infection control. Lapses in perioperative asepsis, sterilization protocols, and hand hygiene compliance may contribute to elevated infection rates. In addition, widespread empirical use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in tertiary care hospitals likely exerts selective pressure, accelerating resistance development. These findings underscore the urgent need for robust antibiotic stewardship programs, guided by routine antibiogram surveillance and strict adherence to institutional antibiotic policies. Restricting overuse of critical drugs, reinforcing staff training in aseptic techniques, and strengthening postoperative wound care protocols could help mitigate the SSI burden.

Limitations of the current study

There are a few limitations on the current study. It was carried out in a single tertiary care facility with a small sample size, which might limit how broadly the results can be applied. The lack of facilities prevented the molecular characterization of resistance genes, which limited our understanding of the genetic basis of antibiotic resistance. Furthermore, a wider variety of antibiotics and patient-related variables were excluded from the analysis. Notwithstanding these drawbacks, the study offers important new information about the patterns of antibiotic resistance in bacterial pathogens linked to surgical site infections following surgery. However, we are interested in conducting this analysis in the future if appropriate funding support becomes available.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that surgical site infections in a tertiary care hospital are predominantly caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, with a high prevalence of multidrug resistance. Ciprofloxacin resistance were widespread, while linezolid, ofloxacin, and piperacillin retained good activity. These findings call for restricting fluoroquinolone use and adopting antibiogram-guided empirical therapy in surgical units. Strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and infection control are essential to reduce resistance and treatment failures. Future research should include molecular resistance profiling, multicenter surveillance, and exploration of plant-based or novel therapeutic options to improve SSI management and preserve antibiotic efficacy.
