The effects of moisture and aging conditions on the cohesive and adhesive debonding of asphalt mixture using thermodynamical and mechanical methods
Authors: Gholam Hossein Hamedi, Himan Rahmani, Hamed Hosseini, Farhad Sakanlou, Mohsen Sohrabi, Bernhard Hofko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The two mechanisms of bitumen-aggregate adhesive and cohesive failure are generally affected by the bitumen’s characteristics and its compatibility with aggregates. Bitumen aging is one of the most critical factors with the most destructive impact on both these mechanisms, especially when associated with moisture conditions. By capturing asphalt mixtures’ behavior in critical states, i.e., aging and moisture penetration, it is possible to choose the most compatible bitumen-aggregate combination, offer helpful solutions to improve the properties of aged bitumen by rejuvenating it with additives, and eventually, change the rate of occurrence of both failure mechanisms. Accordingly, this study investigated adhesive and cohesive failures under the impact of moisture and aging conditions with mechanical and surface free energy (SFE) methods and provided helpful solutions using additives to minimize these two major failures. While the efficacy of antistripping additives like Wetfix BE and Teragrip AN is recognized, their performance mechanism under combined environmental stressors remains poorly understood. This study provides a novel mechanistic evaluation by subjecting asphalt mixtures to simultaneous aging and moisture conditioning. The research advances the field by correlating fundamental SFE properties with direct mechanical pull-off strength measurements. This integrated approach allows for the first time to deconvolute the adhesive and cohesive failure mechanisms and quantitatively evaluate how each additive modifies the bitumen-aggregate interface thermodynamics to enhance durability under severe field-realistic conditions. This study used the rolling thin-film oven and pressure aging vessel tests to simulate the environmental conditions and bitumen aging, Universal Sorption Device and sessile drop tests to measure the SFE elements of aggregate and bitumen, and the Pull-Off test to simulate the failure mechanism of bitumen and aggregate. To this end, 36 combinations of asphalt mixture, comprising two bitumens (PG 58–22 and PG 64–16), two aggregates with basic and acidic properties (limestone and granite), and two rejuvenators (Teragrip AN and Wetfix BE ), were utilized. The results revealed that despite the adhesion reduction due to moisture and aging, the two additives successfully compensated for the decline in adhesion, which can also be ascribed to positive changes in the debonding (stripping) free energy. Moreover, despite the high rate of changing of cohesion due to aging, these additives played an undeniable role in cohesion. Finally, statistical analyses indicated that the correlation coefficient between the cohesion Pull-Off in the dry state and the cohesion free energy increased from 0.84 (without additives) to above 0.9 (modified states), suggesting the high accuracy of the laboratory data when using the additives.

Introduction

The evolution of transportation systems has led to a rising need for advanced asphalt technologies. At the same time, increased socio-economic activity has caused a surge in traffic density and vehicle weight, underscoring the importance of enhancing asphalt mixture properties [1–3]. One key strategy for achieving this is the use of performance-enhancing additives. These substances improve resistance to cracking, boost flexibility, and provide better thermal and moisture stability, making pavements more resilient under heavy traffic. Commonly used additives—such as polymers, fibers, and waterproofing agents—are chosen to meet the specific demands of each project. Their application helps extend the lifespan of roads, minimize maintenance, cut costs, and improve safety overall [4–6].

The parameters influencing the moisture resistance of asphalt mixtures can be broadly categorized into two main groups: internal and external factors.Internal factors, such as binder type, aggregate type, binder content, and air void percentage, are related to the properties of the constructed and applied mixture.External factors, including traffic, aging, weather conditions, and the amount and characteristics of surface runoff, are elements outside the pavement structure [7–9].

Bitumen, a viscoelastic substance, is commonly employed in civil engineering for road construction purposes. Due to its notable economic efficiency, high durability, and recyclability, this material has been extensively utilized worldwide to combine with aggregates to prepare asphalt mixtures. Despite its numerous advantages, due to the change in its microscopic and macroscopic characteristics during service life, its proper use requires further investigation [10]. The chemical composition and rheological properties of bitumen are greatly affected by aging. Chemical transformations during aging involve the creation of carbonyl compounds and sulfoxides, alteration of generic fractions, and an increase in the quantity of larger molecules or molecular associations, as well as changes in molecular weight and polydispersity. The changes imposed on asphalt components due to aging subsequently alter bitumen cohesion and adhesion, demonstrating the aging process’s important effect on cohesion and adhesion changes [11].

Some influential factors, such as heat, ultraviolet and visible light (UV–VIS) radiation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) gases, and moisture penetration during road operation, alter the aging process due to increased evaporation of volatile substances and the beginning of the oxidation reaction. This indicates a relationship between aging and moisture penetration, which in turn alters the cohesion and adhesion strength [12]. Given that the influence of aging and moisture on the cohesion and adhesion mechanisms can affect asphalt behavior and the occurrence of various failures, it is crucial to explore the changes in the mentioned mechanisms under the effect of aging and moisture. Given the significance of this issue, the aging process has been analyzed by considering the effects of moisture.

Evaluations performed with mechanical tests revealed the impact of ageing and moisture conditions on the cohesion and adhesion failure mechanisms and occurrence of different asphalt mixture failures. Lu and Isacsson [13] reported that the bitumen-aggregate adhesion can decrease due to aging. Based on another study, aging under UV rays increased the fracture potential in the Pull-Off test, which is conducted to examine the adhesion and cohesion strength [14]. Liu et al. [15] investigated the effect of different levels of short-term aging conditions on the resistance of WMA against cracking at the contact surface. The adhesion energy and debonding energy of bitumen-aggregate was determined by contact angle tests under aging process. The results of this research show that there is a relatively close relationship between adhesion energy and debonding energy, but the application of aging causes this relationship to weaken. Also, increasing in the time of short-term aging from 5 to 7 h has led to decrease the strength of asphalt mixture against cracking at the contact surface between bitumen and aggregate. It is believed that loss of adhesion between aged bitumen and aggregate is connected to the decrease in the polar components of bitumen. Wu et al. [16] explored the effect of aging conditions on the performance characteristics and chemical compositions of bitumen. The Thin Film Over Test (TFOT) and Pressure Aging Vessel (PAV) was conducted on the bitumens to applying the short-term and long-term aging conditions. The results of DSR test showed that the complex modulus, storage modulus, loss modulus, and rutting factor increased after aging process. Also, the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) test revealed that the carbonyl aging indicator in both TFOT and PAV states has been increased. In a study by Rahim et al. [17], the Pull-Off mechanical test result showed that aging and moisture conditions direct the tested samples’ behavior towards more brittleness, ultimately reducing the strength. Using mechanical tests, Zhai et al. [18] concluded that temperature has a marked impact on adhesion on the bitumen and aggregate surface. Xu et al. [19] evaluated the impact of aging on the damping properties of rubberized porous asphalt mixture and rubberized bitumen by dynamic modulus, temperature scanning, and free vibration damping tests. In addition, the effect of aging on morphology properties and chemical compounds of bitumen was investigated by FTIR, gel permeation chromatography, and fluorescence microscopic (FM) test. This study revealed that the aging process decreased the stress damping capability of mixes. Also, the ratio of the large molecules in bitumen increased considerably after applying aging conditions.

One of the important solutions to reduce the destructive effects of aging and moisture on asphalt mixtures is to use anti-aging and anti-stripping additives. Many researches have been conducted regarding the use of different additives to reduce the negative effects of aging and moisture in order to improve the performance of asphalt mixes against various failures. In a study by Aksoy et al. [20], the impact of Wetfix I, Lilamin VP 75P, Chemcrete, and rubber on the moisture susceptibility of asphalt mixtures was evaluated under aged and unaged conditions. The properties of bitumens were determined by conventional tests and the specifications of the mixes were obtained using Marshall, indirect tensile strength (ITS) and modified Lottman tests. The results of the conducted tests showed that all four additives decreased the level of failures in asphalt mixes and bitumens after applying moisture and aging conditions. Fatemi et al. [21] investigated the effect of calcium lignosulfonate (CLS) at four quantities (i.e., 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%) as anti-aging additives on the conventional and rheological properties of bitumen. To simulate the aging process, the rolling thin film oven test (RTFOT) was conducted on control and modified bitumens. The conventional tests (softening point, penetration, and ductility) and rheological tests (rotational viscosity (RV), dynamic shear rheometer (DSR), multiple stress creep recovery (MSCR), and linear amplitude sweep (LAS)) was used to determine the characteristics of control and modified bitumens in two states of aged and unaged. The results of conducting tests proved that adding CLS prevents bitumen oxidation reaction and improved the rutting and fatigue performance of bitumen. In addition, the FTIR test revealed that carbonyl groups and sulfoxide groups increase with aging. Tabasi et al. [22] investigated the effect of short-term and long-term aging conditions on the fracture performance of asphalt mixture modified with nano reduced graphene oxide by semi-circular bending (SCB) test. The results of this research showed that the use of nano reduced graphene oxide has decreased the negative effects of aging on fracture energy and fracture toughness, especially at medium temperature. Zhang et al. [23] used layered double hydroxides (LDHs) as bitumen modifier to improve the performance of HMA against ageing. The results of FTIR test showed that the bitumen structure was effectively degenerated. The adding of LDHs in the bitumen balanced the oxygenic groups produced after aging, and retarded the gelatinization of colloidal structures. In other words, LDHs significantly decreased the destructive effects of aging on bitumen, and raised the strength of HMA against aging. In a study by Zhang et al. [24], DSR, MSCR, FTIR, and FM tests were used to determine the characteristics of modified bitumen by polyurethane under aging conditions. The results of the tests demonstrated that the 6% polyurethane improved the anti-aging performance of bitumen.

The lack of a detailed study of failure mechanisms and solutions in mechanical methods has led to concepts such as thermodynamic parameter measurement and its application in asphalt pavements. Based on thermodynamic concepts, the most compatible the bitumen and aggregate system can be designed for various conditions based on bitumen-aggregate adhesion and bitumen cohesion. Thus, according to the correlation between mechanical and thermodynamic tests, based on the basic characteristics of pavement materials, thermodynamic methods can be employed to assess the impact of bitumen aging and rejuvenator additives on the mentioned asphalt mixture failures. In other words, asphalt behavior can be correctly explored by conducting thermodynamic tests, along with mechanical tests, to evaluate the influence of bitumen aging on adhesion and cohesion failure mechanisms, and an improved performance can be induced in the mixture by choosing the appropriate additive. Izadi et al. [25] investigated the effect of aging on the performance of HMA and WMA against cracking at intermediate temperature. The main goal of this study was the prediction of the fatigue life of asphalt mixes under aged and unaged conditions. The results of this research show that models based on mechanical tests regardless of mixture components, loading conditions and aging conditions, cannot predict asphalt against cracking potential. According to Rahmani et al. [26], by using SFE, the bitumen aging impact on asphalt mixtures can be adequately investigated, and the most compatible bitumen-aggregate combination can be obtained. Hossain et al. [27] examined the cohesive and adhesive bond energies of asphalt-aggregate systems in dry and wet conditions in both unaged and aged asphalt mixtures. The study findings indicated that the rejuvenators appeared to be effective in enhancing the cohesive energy of the asphalt binder, indicating an enhancement in the mix’s resistance to moisture damage. Al Masri et al. [28] applied the SFE concept to evaluate the impact of binder aging, aggregate types, and recycled mineral fillers on the susceptibility of asphalt-aggregate systems to moisture. The energy ratio parameter was used to assess the moisture damage resistance of each mastic-aggregate combination. Additionally, various asphalt aging conditions—including nonaged, short-term aged, and long-term aged mixes—were analyzed to investigate the influence of asphalt aging on moisture susceptibility. The findings indicated that the use of recycled mineral fillers, particularly from recycled concrete aggregates (RCA) and recycled asphalt pavement (RAP), notably decreases the moisture damage resistance of asphalt-aggregate systems.

Statement of the present study and objectives

The primary focus of this research is to investigate the adhesive and cohesive properties within asphalt mixtures. Numerous common pavement failures, including low-temperature cracking, fatigue cracking, and stripping, occur due to deficiencies in adhesive and cohesive properties. This ultimately leads to failure within the mastic phase or at the bitumen-aggregate interface. A review of previous studies indicates that moisture and aging are two primary factors that directly and adversely affect the adhesive and cohesive properties of asphalt mixtures. Consequently, asphalt mixtures exposed to moisture and aging conditions gradually lose their adhesive and cohesive strength. This results in adhesive/cohesive failures within the pavement mix, ultimately reducing its overall performance. Furthermore, the literature review reveals that two additives, WBE and TAN, have been effective in improving the properties of asphalt mixtures under aging and moisture conditions. However, the mechanism by which these additives function under such conditions to influence the mixture’s adhesive and cohesive properties has not been sufficiently explored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effect of WBE and TAN additives on the adhesive and cohesive properties of asphalt mixtures under simultaneous aging and moisture conditions. The use of both mechanical and thermodynamic methods in this research to measure these properties will help achieve two goals: first, to understand the mechanical mechanism of moisture, aging, and additives; and second, to evaluate the chemical interaction mechanism at the bitumen-aggregate-additive interface under moist and aged conditions. In summary, the perspective of this research will help assess how and to what extent critical field factors—namely moisture and aging—contribute to various pavement failures. It will also clarify the role each of the aforementioned additives plays in mitigating these two factors.

Accordingly, the current study investigated the effect of moisture conditions and aging to improve material assessment in terms of adhesion and cohesion. This simulates the real-world environment of a pavement, which is constantly exposed to oxidative aging while also being subjected to rain, humidity, and freeze–thaw cycles. Consequently, the results of this research will be highly representative of actual field performance. Besides the Pull-Off test, thermodynamic methods were adopted to explore the trend of changes in the respective failure mechanism; in this way, the factors affecting this process could be investigated separately and accurately. Since most of the studies focusing on the important aging-related failures in asphalt cannot suggest a solution to lower the aging rate, in addition to the evaluations under critical conditions, the present study utilized two additives, TAN and WBE, as rejuvenators in bitumen to accurately overcome the mentioned problems. Finally, statistical analyses explored the magnitude of effect of influential factors, such as the aging process and the type of bitumen, aggregate, and additives on cohesion and adhesion mechanisms.

Aggregates

This study involved granite and limestone aggregates. Their choice was mainly motivated by their difference in hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity. The goal was to enable evaluation of the effects of aggregate types on the mentioned failures.

Rejuvenator anti-stripping additives

Rejuvenator anti-stripping additives are added to asphalt mixtures to improve their properties and enhance their performance against damage induced by aging and moisture by increasing the aggregate-bitumen adhesion and bitumen cohesion. These additives affect both failure mechanisms, so their correct selection is critical. Therefore, two additives, WBE and TAN, were selected to change critical conditions (moisture penetration and aging) positively. This study evaluated the two additives separately, considering their importance.

WBE is a rejuvenating anti-stripping liquid that promotes bitumen-aggregate adhesion by creating thermal stability. For example, adding this substance to the asphalt mixture slightly increases the mixture’s density, which reduces the voids between the aggregates; eventually, the asphalt mixture performs better against water penetration. Moreover, according to the literature, adding WBE at 0.3% by the weight of the bitumen makes the bitumen better coat the aggregate surface, which increases the asphalt mixture’s stability index. This additive, which is abundant in the market in liquid form, is economical and efficient for several reasons. Since it is used in small amounts in asphalt mixtures and can be mixed with a broad range of aggregates, it improves the pavement’s performance and durability. This substance is often used at 0.1% to 0.5% of the bitumen weight for hot-mix and warm mix asphalt; this percentage is selected according to the type of bitumen and aggregate [17]. In this research, 0.2% of this substance was used. Tests

Bitumen aging tests

In this study, the samples were subjected to aging conditions using the RTFO [29] and PAV [30] tests. The PAV test was performed to simulate aging that occurs during 5–10 years of asphalt pavements’ service life. Since bitumen in asphalt pavement undergoes STA during production and installment, the RTFO test was performed before PAV to simulate STA during installation.

Asphalt mix aging test

The AASHTO R30 was utilized to simulate aging in the asphalt mixes. Per this standard, extended aging needs to be implemented on specimens that have already undergone STA treatment. In the STA process, loose mixtures are laid out in a container with a thickness of 1–2 inches for 4 h at 135 °C. It is crucial not to remove the samples from the mold post-compression, as in the last phase, both the samples and the mold are kept at 85 °C for 120 h [31].

Pull-off test

The Pull-Off test is widely used to check bitumen-aggregate adhesion and the internal cohesion of bitumen, yielding promising results. Based on previous projects, it has been determined that this practical and replicable test can be carried out in any situation in a simple procedure. In fact, based on the correlation between Pull-Off results and other moisture sensitivity tests, previous studies confirmed the high validity of the data extracted from this test. To conduct this experiment, the bitumen is first heated to 135 ℃. It is then spread onto the surface of a smooth-surfaced aggregate at the desired thickness and allowed to penetrate and bond for 1 h. During this time, the bitumen-aggregate temperature reaches ambient temperature. Subsequently, to apply moisture conditions, the samples are immersed in water at 40 ℃ for 1 h. Following immersion, samples undergo a one-hour acclimatization period under standard laboratory conditions. After moisture conditioning, samples are stabilized for one hour at their designated test temperatures. Finally, direct tensile strength testing is conducted using the Pull-off test method at a tensile rate of 66 kP/sec until failure occurs [32]. The mechanisms of different failures of asphalt mixtures are considered in two states of cohesive and adhesive failure [7, 33–36]. Based on previous studies, the bitumen film thickness on the surface of aggregate has been selected 0.5 mm for adhesive failure and 1 mm for cohesive failure. When the bitumen thickness is high, cohesive failure occurs, and when the bitumen thickness is low, adhesive failure occurs in the bitumen-aggregate interface [34, 37–41]. The bitumen film thicknesses (0.5 and 1 mm) has been determined based on trial and error. It has been observed that in similar samples, if the bitumen film thickness is less than 0.5 mm and more than 1 mm, most of the failures will be adhesive and cohesive failure, respectively. To ensure the type of failure that occurs, the failure surface was visually inspected after each test. If it is observed that adhesive failure occurs at 1 mm bitumen film thickness and cohesive failure occurs at 0.5 mm thickness, the test will be repeated. In other words, if the failure is not the same as the desired failure, the specimen was remade and the test was repeated [34, 38]. In order to ensure the determination of the bitumen film thickness in the pull-off test, a cylinder with an inner diameter of 20 mm and a height of 10 mm has been used. Then the mentioned cylinder is placed on the surface of the aggregate. Measuring SFE elements

Several methods, such as Wilhelmy plate (WP), SSD, and USD, have been employed to measure the SFE elements of bitumen and aggregates. Due to the sensitivity of measurement in these tests, this study used methods that were reported to be more accurate than other commonly used tests based on previous research. The SFE of bitumen and aggregates used in this research was measured by USD and SSD tests developed by Bhasin et al. [16].

(A) Measuring the bitumen’s SFE elements

Measuring the SFE elements using SSD is a common method used for solid materials by measuring the static contact angle. This method can be used for aggregates and bitumens (in solid form), provided that the aggregate’s SFE elements are small. When performing this test, to prepare the bitumen samples, an aluminum or glass plate should be inserted into the bitumen so that a layer of bitumen is formed on it. Then, the bitumen sample should be placed between the light source and the camera. Subsequently, a microsyringe is filled with the test liquid, and its tip is placed 5 mm above the sample’s surface to spread a drop of the test liquid on the bitumen’s surface. After a few seconds, the camera captures a photo of the drop on the surface. For each test liquid, at least three samples should be tested, three angles should be recorded, and their average represents the final contact angle. Two common methods are used to determine the contact angle between bitumen and a probe liquid: the SD method and the WP method. The WP method typically employs four samples to ensure result repeatability, whereas the SD method uses three [42–44]. Consequently, in this study, three samples per test were utilized to achieve repeatable results. Three test liquids (water, glycerol, and formamide) were used in this study. (C) SFE theory

The SFE theory is the foundation for calculating the parameters used in an integral part of this research to predict asphalt mixture cohesion and adhesion strength against moisture. Previous studies determined that key parameters for predicting the mentioned mechanisms can be obtained by measuring the SFE elements of bitumen, aggregate, and water. Several SFE theories describe materials based on their molecular structure, the most important of which is the acid–base theory [15]. The application of the acid–base theory in asphalt is rooted in the research by Bhasin et al. [16], which investigated and developed laboratory and analytical tools to determine the significance of the effective fundamental characteristics of pavement materials that influence asphalt mixture composition. According to this theory, the total SFE of each substance is divided into three elements based on the type of surface molecular forces: (1) the non-polar, the Lifshitz-Van der Waals (LW), or the dispersed element, (2) the Lewis acid element, and (3) the Lewis base element. Results and discussion

Pull-off test results

Pull-off adhesion strength

The results of the current study indicate that bitumen modification by two additives, WBE and TAN, increased the bitumen-aggregate adhesion strength and the cohesion of bitumen molecules; still, considering the aging factor and the type of aggregates, the trend of increase differed in different bitumens. The results of bitumen-aggregate adhesion strength in dry and wet conditions from the Pull-Off test for granite and limestone aggregates are shown in Figs. 5–8, respectively. According to these figures, although these additives constantly improved the bitumen-aggregate adhesion strength, the slope of this rising trend decreased during the aging process and exposure to moisture conditions. Regardless of the type of aggregates, STA caused a decrease of about 14% in dry conditions and 36% in wet conditions, on average; as for LTA, the trend experienced a decline of 22% and 72%, respectively. In fact, it can be generally acknowledged that long-term exposure to water and further aging of bitumen severely impacted the adhesion strength; nevertheless, both additives still performed successfully despite the strong impact of moisture conditions and aging. Another notable point was the trend of variations in the two types of tested aggregates. Thermodynamic tests

Cohesion free energy

In general, STA and LTA of bitumen increases its cohesion, and these changes can be expressed by raising the cohesion free energy. Although this rise in cohesion free energy is somewhat positive and causes the bitumen to harden, if the elevation is too high, it generally makes the bitumen become brittle, which is undesirable. Therefore, it is crucial to present the results of cohesion free energy to assess this phenomenon. For this purpose, the measurement results of bitumen cohesion free energy in different states (unaged, STA, and LTA) are displayed in Fig. 17. Based on the findings, the incorporation of additives improved the cohesion. For the unaged and LTA states, the additives in both types of bitumen raised the cohesion free energy by an average of 25% of the initial value; for the STA state, this value was increased by 28% on average. Since the percentage of increase in cohesion free energy in the LTA state did not considerably differ from the STA state, the additives controlled this increasing process, which mitigated the bitumen’s brittleness. Furthermore, according to this figure, the cohesion free energy of PG 64–16 (with and without additives) was higher than that of PG 58–22 bitumen (with and without additives).Fig. 17Cohesion free energy of unaged, STA, and LTA specimens.

Debonding free energy in the presence of water

In general, there are significant variations in asphalt mixtures’ adhesion strength after water penetration; as such, it is necessary to include the impact of water as the third material in adhesion calculations to correctly predict the most reasonable changes during the roads’ service life. Since the effect of water is included in determining the debonding free energy according to Eq. 5, this factor plays a key role in predicting the variations of asphalt mixtures’ adhesion strength in the event of stripping. Energy is generally released during the stripping process. Consequently, the numerical value of the debonding free energy is negative; the magnitude (negativity) of this number indicates its higher sensitivity and, as a result, the high risk of moisture damage, especially after aging. For clarification, the absolute value of this factor is depicted in Figs. 20 and 21.Fig. 21Debonding energy in the presence of water for specimens with granite aggregates.

Aged bitumen and aggregate surfaces (especially siliceous aggregates like granite) are both highly polar and often have a negative charge. Like charges repel, which breaks the adhesive bond in the presence of water. Amidoamines & Polyamines (The Adhesion Promoters) in Wetfix BE and Teragrip AN have a long hydrocarbon tail that is compatible with the bitumen. The amine group (-NH₂) is a powerful, positively charged (cationic) head that is strongly attracted to the negatively charged aggregate surface. The molecule orients itself at the interface between the bitumen and the aggregate: the tail buried in the bitumen, the head bonded to the stone. This creates a powerful, permanent covalent bond that is not broken by water. It acts as a "molecular bridge," drastically reducing moisture-induced stripping.

Additionally, Surfactants (Surface Active Agents) are key components in many rejuvenators, especially those delivered as emulsions (Wetfix BE and Teragrip AN). Asphaltenes are flocculated (clumped together) in aged bitumen. Surfactants have a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and a lipophilic (oil-loving) tail. The lipophilic tail surrounds and penetrates the asphaltene clusters. The hydrophilic head faces outwards, creating a protective shell around each cluster. The repulsion between these charged heads prevents the asphaltenes from clumping back together (peptization). This re-disperses the asphaltenes, restoring the bitumen’s colloidal structure. This improves flexibility and homogeneity. In emulsion-based rejuvenators, surfactants are also what allow the rejuvenator to be mixed with water and evenly coat the aggregate.

In the presence of water, the functional groups of bitumen and aggregates dissociate from their surfaces, generating localized electrical charges around their molecules. Bitumen’s acidic functional groups (phenolic, anhydride, carboxylic acids, and pyrrolic structures) release H⁺ ions to create positive surface charges. Table 3 shows the high SiO₂ (an acidic functional group) content of granite produced like-charged with bitumen. The electrostatic repulsion between bitumen and granite surfaces weakens their bond, causing adhesive failure in HMA.

According to adhesion science principles, limestone aggregate—being predominantly basic (alkaline) rather than acidic—exhibits excellent bonding with bitumen, which is acidic in nature. This is due to the polar bonds formed between the acidic and basic components. This results in improved bonding through dipole–dipole and hydrogen bonding mechanisms, increasing surface free energy and adhesion strength. Furthermore, limestone’s hydrophobic properties make it less likely to bond with aqueous solutions in a three-phase system of bitumen, aggregate, and water.

Statistical analysis of thermodynamic parameters and the mechanical test

Many recent studies adopted the SFE method, along with mechanical methods, to determine the moisture sensitivity of asphalt mixtures. The decline of bitumen-aggregate adhesion and bitumen membrane cohesion in the presence of water are the two main factors of asphalt mixture moisture damage; by examining their correlation with the Pull-Off test, these failure mechanisms in asphalt mixtures can be investigated in more detail. Accordingly, the effect of additives on the results was inspected by performing statistical analysis and checking the correlation coefficient between the results of SFE and Pull-Off tests in modified and unmodified states. The results of these analyses are illustrated in Figs. 22–24. In these results, the Pull-Off cohesion strength in the dry state is compared with the cohesion free energy in unmodified and modified states. The correlation coefficient rose from 0.84 in the unmodified case to 0.91 and 0.9, respectively, in the cases modified with TAN and WBE, showing that the validity of data increased when additives were incorporated. Conclusions

Adhesive and cohesive failures play a key role in the spread of moisture damage, especially after aging. Thus, this study evaluated the occurrence of these failures under moisture and aging conditions; then, attempts were made to reduce the rate of occurrence of these failures by adding proper rejuvenators. Finally, statistical analyses assessed the effectiveness of influential factors, including additives, in preventing these failures.
