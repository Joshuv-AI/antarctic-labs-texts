ELAPOR1 mediated vesicle traffic is required for acrosome biogenesis and male fertility in mice
Authors: Runze Ding, Qinghua Guo, Li Wang, Ningyuan Tang, Xiao Fang, Zhixiang Xin, Wenzhen Zhao, Yue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpermiogenesis is the post-meiotic differentiation and morphological change of the haploid spermatids. Acrosome biogenesis is one of the key events of spermiogenesis, which generates a specialized membranous organelle crucial for shaping the balanced morphology of sperm head and ensuring sperm fertility. Defects in acrosome biogenesis can result in globozoospermia or teratozoospermia, causing male infertility. Although acrosome biogenesis is known to derive from the Golgi apparatus, the processes that trafficking, targeting and fusion of the Golgi-derived vesicles are yet to be fully understood. In this study, we demonstrated that ELAPOR1 abundantly expressed in spermatids was required for acrosome biogenesis during spermiogenesis. By selectively deleting Elapor1 in premeiotic germ cells, we found that ELAPOR1 deficient mice were male infertile and exhibited round-head, absence of acrosome, disorganized mitochondrial sheath and reduced sperm motility, phenotypically resembling human astheno-teratozoospermia. We further demonstrated that ELAPOR1 could recruit clathrin via the cytoplasmic adaptor-binding motif YSKL, and consequently contribute to the assembly of clathrin coated onto the vesicles derived from trans-Golgi network, which further mediated vesicle traffic and ultimately succeeded acrosome formation. Taken together, our findings revealed a novel role of ELAPOR1 in mediating the clathrin assembly and thereby involving in the transport of vesicles required for acrosome biogenesis during spermiogenesis.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Acrosome biogenesis, Spermiogenesis, Vesicle traffic, Globozoospermia, Male infertility

Introduction

Spermiogenesis, the terminal phase of spermatogenesis, is a differentiation and metamorphosis process for the round spermatids transforming into the slender spermatozoa. Mouse spermiogenesis consists of 16 steps characterized by morphological changes including the condensation of nuclear chromatin, formation of acrosome, elongation of the sperm head, assembly of the flagella and removal of the cytoplasm [1–3]. This morphological transformation of spermatids is an evidently critical process ensuring sperm morphology, motility and fertility. Disruptions in spermiogenesis can cause mis-shaped sperm head or defective sperm tail, termed teratozoospermia, which is always associated with primary male infertility.

Acrosome biogenesis is an essential and initial step of spermiogenesis. It generally divides into four distinct phases according to the morphology of the acrosome: the Golgi phase, the cap phase, the acrosome phase and the maturation phase [3]. In the Golgi phase, pro-acrosomal vesicles (pro-AVs) derived from the trans-Golgi network (TGN) begin to fuse with each other and form a large acrosomal structure on the acroplaxome, a cytoskeletal scaffold on the nuclear envelope. In the cap phase, with continuously infused with pro-AVs, the acrosomal structure enlarges and spreads over the nucleus to form a cap-like structure. In the acrosomal phase, the acrosome spreads toward the ventral surface of the elongating spermatid nucleus, and concurrently condenses. Finally, in the maturation phase, the acrosome finishes spreading and covers nearly the entire nuclear surfaces. The acrosome is a specialized organelle similar to the lysosome and can be release from sperm by exocytosis during fertilization process, which disperses the oocyte coverings and enables sperm to fertilize the oocyte.

Acrosome biogenesis requires coordination between various organelles (e.g. endoplasmic reticulum and TGN) and cytoskeletal structures (e.g. acroplaxome and manchette system) [4–9]. A number of Golgi-associated proteins, such as GM130 [10], GMAP210 [11], GOPC [12] and SMAP2 [13, 14], and vesicle transport and fusion proteins, such as PICK1 [15], VPS54 [16] and VPS13b [17], have been shown to be involved in acrosome biogenesis. Deletion of these proteins in mice results in abnormal acrosome development, globozoospermia or round-headed spermatozoa, and male infertility. However, the complicated molecular machinery for the traffic and fusion of the pro-AVs remains incompletely elucidated.

Endosome-lysosome associated apoptosis and autophagy regulator 1 (ELAPOR1), also known as estrogen-induced gene 121 (EIG121) or KIAA1324, is a highly conserved transmembrane protein localizing in various cellular membranes, including the plasma membrane, lysosome, endosome, and Golgi apparatus [18]. The N-terminal domain of ELAPOR1 shares structural similarities with the mannose 6-phosphate receptor (M6PR), both the cation-dependent (CD-M6PR) and the cation-independent (CI-M6PR, also known as IGF2R). Meanwhile, its C-terminus at the cytoplasmic side contains a lysosomal targeting motif YXXΦ, which is a consensus binding motif for the adaptor proteins and therefore involves in intracellular transport of clathrin-coated vesicles between the TGN and endosomal-lysosomal system [19]. The function of ELAPOR1 has yet to be fully studied. In human tumors, the expression of ELAPOR1 has been previously described in association with favorable prognosis in endometrial, ovarian, prostate, pancreatic neuroendocrine, and breast cancer [20–24]. Additionally, ELAPOR1 has been found to regulate the proper formation and maturation of secretory granules in glandular cells [25].

Interestingly, there is high similarity in the origin and function between secretory granules and acrosome: both are large membranous organelles with abundant contents derived from Golgi apparatus, reside near the plasma membrane undergoing a maturation process and are released by exocytosis when receiving the signals. Bilekova et al. reported that ELAPOR1 (termed Inceptor) facilitated acrosomal vesicle formation in spermatids [26]. In our previous study (unpublished), we found that ELAPOR1 was downregulated in round spermatids of acrosome defective mice by comparative proteomics. Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to investigate the role of ELAPOR1 in acrosome biogenesis by using a germ cell specific Elapor1 gene knockout mice model. Our findings demonstrated that ELAPOR1 in spermatids contributed to acrosome biogenesis which involved in clathrin-mediated vesicle traffic, underlying the regulatory mechanism of transport and fusion of clathrin-coated vesicles in acrosome formation.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals, and the experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (JUMC2023-097-A). All mice were maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions of ad libitum water and food with temperature- and humidity-controlled and constant light-dark cycles, in the Animal Center of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine.

Elapor1 flox mice (Strain S-CKO-07286) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China). Two loxP sites were inserted into intron 2 and intron 6 respectively, and the resulting deletion of exon 3 to 6 would cause frameshift and loss function of Elapor1 gene. To achieve the germ cell-specific gene knockout, Elapor1 flox/flox mice were crossed with Stra8-Cre mice to generate Elapor1 flox/flox; Stra8-Cre positive mice (Elapor1 cKO). Elapor1 flox/flox, Cre negative mice were used as control in all experiments. All mice were maintained on a consistent C57BL/6J genetic background.

Fertility assessment and sperm parameters analyses

To assess the male fertility, individually housed, sexually mature male mice (10-week-old, either cKO or control) cohabitated with two randomly selected adult wild-type female mice. The females were checked for the presence of vaginal plugs as evidence of successfully mating. Approximately, three weeks after the last day of cohabitation, the number of pups delivered by each mated female mouse was counted and calculated.

Sperm parameters of male mice were performed on epididymal mature sperm. The cauda epididymides were dissected and placed in prewarmed (37 °C) Tyrode’s buffer (T2397, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min to allow dispersion of mature sperm. Then, sperm suspensions were collected and sperm concentration, sperm motility and progressive motility were analyzed by computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) (IVOS2, Hamilton Thorne, USA).

For sperm morphology analysis, sperm suspensions were smeared on glass slides and then stained as described in the Diff-Quick method (BRED-015, BRED Life Science Technology Inc., China). The stained slides were viewed under a microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan) equipped with an UPlanFN N 40×/0.75 objective (Olympus).

Western blotting analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as described [27]. Mice testes or cultured cells were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (#PC101, EpiZyme, China) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (#GRF101, EpiZyme) on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12 000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The proteins in the supernatant were collected, and the protein concentrations were determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (#ZJ101, EpiZyme). Protein samples (20 µg) were separated using 4–15% denaturing polyacrylamide gels and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and immunoblotting was performed overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies against Elapor1 (1:5000 dilution, #NBP2-81949, Novus Biologicals, USA), Flag tag (1:5000 dilution, #66008-4-Ig, Proteintech) AP1M1 (1:5000 dilution, #12112-1-AP, Proteintech), AP2B1 (1:5000 dilution, #68349-1-Ig, Proteintech), AP3B1 (1:5000 dilution, #13384-1-AP, Proteintech) and β-actin (1:5000 dilution, #66009-1-Ig, Proteintech), respectively, followed by incubation with secondary antibody conjugated to HRP (#SA00001-1/2, Proteintech). Signals were generated by enhanced chemiluminescence (#SQ101, EpiZyme) and detected by luminescent image analyzer (GE imagination LAS 4000; GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences, Sweden).

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

Mice were euthanized with CO2 and then, their testes were harvested and fixed in Bouin’s solution. Subsequently, paraffin microtomy was performed with standardize processes. Testicular sections with 5-µm thickness were then performed hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and observed under a microscope (Olympus BX53).

For immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, testicular paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval by boiling for 15 min in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0). Then, the Histostain LAB-SA Detection kits (#85-9843, Invitrogen, USA) were applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibody against Elapor1 (1:200 dilution) and the normal rabbit IgG (1:200 dilution, #30000-0-AP, Proteintech) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, respectively. Digital images were captured under a microscope (Olympus BX53).

Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis

IF staining was performed as described [27]. Specifically, 8-µm-thick frozen testicular sections, or sperm smears, or testicular cell smears were prepared and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The unspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% goat serum in PBS and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies against Elapor1 (1:200 dilution), ACRV1 (1:200 dilution, #14040-1-AP, Proteintech), GM130 (1:1000 dilution, #610822, BD Biosciences), Clathrin (1:200 dilution, #26523-1-AP, Proteintech), and TGN46 (1:200 dilution, #66477-1-Ig, Proteintech), respectively. After washing, the slides were incubated with responding secondary antibodies or fluorescein conjugated antibodies/dyes including coraLite594-conjugated acetylated tubulin (1:1000 dilution, #CL594-66200, Proteintech), AF488-α-tubulin (1:200 dilution, #8058, CST) and FITC-PNA (1:500 dilution, #L7381, Sigma), followed by incubated with DAPI Fluoromount (#36308ES, Yeasen, China). The fluorescence signals were detected under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss LSM-510, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and immuno-electron microscopy (IEM)

Both TEM and IEM analyses were performed as described [27]. For TEM analysis, small pieces of testicular tissue or epididymal tissue immersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. The specimens were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon 618. Ultrathin Sects. (70–90 nm) were stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, and finally examined under a transmission electron microscope (Philips CM-120, Philips, the Netherlands) at 100 kV.

Pre-embedding colloidal gold-labeled IEM analysis was employed to determine the clathrin localization. Small pieces of testicular tissue were fixed with a buffered solution of 1% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde for 1 h at room temperature, and then neutralized the aldehydes with 1 M glycine solution and blocked unspecific binding sites with 5% goat serum. Subsequently, 100 μm-thick slices were prepared by a vibrating slicer and stained with primary antibodies for clathrin (1:100 dilution) and colloidal gold conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:50 dilution, #111-205-144, Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA). Finally, the labelled specimens were postfixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and processed according to the TEM procedure.

Flowcytometry analysis

Mouse sperm were collected from the caudal epididymis and suspended in Tyrode’s buffer at 37 °C. Then, the sperm suspensions were stained with JC-1 probe (5 µM, #40706ES60, Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions to assess mitochondrial membrane potential. After washing with PBS, JC-1 signals of sperm were analyzed by flow cytometer (Cytoflex S, Beckman, USA). A total of 10 000 events were collected for measuring fluorescence intensity. The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated by the green fluorescence intensity (JC-1 monomers indicate depolarized mitochondria) and red fluorescence intensity (JC-1 aggregates indicate polarized mitochondria).

Plasmid construction

To determine the effect of ELAPOR1 on the recruitment of clathrin, we constructed 3×Flag-ELAPOR1 (a.a. 801–1013) which contains the M6PR domain, the transmembrane region and the cytoplasmic terminus, 3×Flag-ELAPOR1-ΔC (a.a. 801–931) which lacks the cytoplasmic C-terminus, and 3×Flag-ELAPOR1-mut (a.a. 801–1013 with mutant) which mutates YSKL to ASKA. The DNA fragments were synthesized by Sheng Gong Biotechnology Limited Company (Shanghai, China) and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector. All the constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Cell culture and transient transfection

The HEK-293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM Medium (Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 500 µg/ml penicillin, and 500 µg/ml streptomycin, in 5% CO2 incubator at 37 ℃. We regularly tested the cell line for mycoplasma contamination and clean the incubator once per week.

For transient transfection, HEK-293T cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes until reaching 70%-90% confluence, then transfected with plasmids using Liposomal 2000 Transfection Reagent (#40802ES, Yeasen) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h transfection, the cells were lysed to collect total proteins.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Since the target proteins contain 3×Flag tags, we used anti-Flag Magnetic Beads (#YJ007, EpiZyme) for co-IP to enrich the target proteins. Before use, the magnetic beads were pre-washer three times with RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein samples extracted from cells or testes were incubated with beads with rotation at 4 ℃ overnight. Then beads were washed with RIPA lysis buffer three times. Proteins were released from beads after denatured and boiled, further separated with immunoblot analysis.

Intra-testicular treatment of pitstop2

Pitstop2 (#HY-115604, MedChemExpress, USA) is a novel inhibitor of clathrin, which specifically inhibits the clathrin-dependent endocytosis by blocking AP2/β-arrestin 2 binding to clathrin. At postnatal day 21 (P21), mice under anesthesia received bilateral intratesticular injections: one testis was injected with 5 µM pitstop2 (10 µl, experimental group) while the contralateral testis received physiological saline (10 µl, control group). Morphological analyses were performed at P28, encompassing critical stages of spermiogenesis (step 5–9), to assess acrosome formation and vesicle trafficking.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by SPSS software (SPSS Statistic 23, Chicago, USA), and the data are reported as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were made using Student’s t-test appropriately. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used assuming a two-tail hypothesis with P < 0.05. Differences were considered statistically different when P < 0.05.

Results

ELAPOR1 abundantly expressed in the spermatids

To investigate the role of ELAPOR1 in spermatogenesis, we evaluated its expression and localization in the testis. IHC analysis revealed that ELAPOR1 expressed mainly in the spermatids and exhibited as a conspicuous granule near the nuclear, in both mouse and human testicular sections (Fig. 1A and B). IF staining demonstrated that ELAPOR1 localized in the acrosome in both round and elongating spermatids (Fig. 1C).

Subsequently, the expression of ELAPOR1 in the testis during mouse postnatal development was analyzed by immunoblotting. ELAPOR1 was continuous expression during testicular development, and more interestingly, it was increasingly expressed in the testis at P21 when the round spermatids were initially appeared during the first wave of spermatogenesis, which indicated the abundant expression of ELAPOR1 in spermatids. (Fig. 1D). This temporal expression pattern of ELAPOR1 in spermatids suggested that it may play a potential role in spermiogenesis, particularly in acrosome formation.

ELAPOR1 was essential for spermiogenesis and male fertility

To investigate the role of ELAPOR1 in spermiogenesis, we generated the germ cell specific knockout mice (Elapor1flox/flox; Stra8-cre, termed as Elapor1 cKO). Western blot analysis of ELAPOR1 in the testicular samples confirmed successful knockout of ELAPOR1 in Elapor1 cKO mice (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, IHC and IF analyses also showed significantly reduced expression of ELAPOR1 in spermatids in Elapor1 cKO testes (Fig. 2B and C).

Male Elapor1 cKO mice exhibited complete infertility (fertility rate = 0%) when mated with wildtype females. Notably, testis index (the ratio of testis weight to body weight) had no statistical difference between Elapor1 cKO and control Elapor1flox/flox littermates (Fig. 2D), suggesting Elapor1 deficiency does not result in obvious change in testicular development or spermatogenesis in testis. Sperm parameter analysis of caudal epididymal sperm revealed that there was no statistical difference in sperm concentration between Elapor1 cKO and control mice, whereas the cKO mice exhibited a dramatic reduction in sperm motility and severely elevated sperm malformation rate, that is 100% sperm in cKO group were round-head (Fig. 2E-H). In a word, Elapor1 deficiency in spermatids caused globozoospermia associated with asthenozoospermia.

To gain insights into the structural alterations in sperm, the epididymal sperm were subjected to detailed IF and TEM analysis. The results of IF staining on sperm smears showed that epididymal sperm from Elapor1 cKO mice lacked PNA staining, suggesting the absence of acrosome. Meanwhile, the sperm nucleus was nearly round and the tail tended to be coiled (Fig. 2I). Parallelly, TEM analysis of the mature sperm in caudal epididymis also showed that the sperm from Elapor1 cKO mice exhibited aberrant head curling (> 90° curvature) with distorted nucleus and missing acrosome (Fig. 2J), as well as frequently vacuolated and irregularly arranged mitochondria in the tail (Fig. 2K). Due to the defects of mitochondrial morphology, JC-1 staining indicated a lower mitochondrial membrane potential in sperm from Elapor1 cKO mice (Fig. 2L and M).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that ELAPOR1 deficiency causes impaired structure with characteristic globozoospermia, especially absent acrosome, ultimately leading to complete male sterility.

ELAPOR1 was indispensable for acrosome biogenesis

Since the acrosome deficiency always originates in spermiogenesis, a precisely stage-specific analysis of the seminiferous epithelium was applied to investigate the acrosome formation. Histological analysis of HE stained testicular sections revealed that Elapor1 cKO testes contained all types of spermatogenic cells throughout the stages I to XII during spermatogenesis, but elongating spermatids exhibited severe nuclear malformations, including loss of characteristic sickle-shaped nuclei and aberrant spherical nuclear condensation (Fig. 3A). IF analysis of ACRV1 (an acrosome marker) revealed complete absence of acrosome signals in Elapor1 cKO testes, while control mice showed stage-associated acrosome development (Fig. 3B).

To elucidate the phenotype of acrosome deficiency resulting from Elapor1 cKO, we investigated the vesicular dynamics in acrosome biogenesis. In Elapor1 cKO spermatids, Golgi-derived pro-AVs were produced normally, but the pro-AVs failed to form a large acrosomal granule (Fig. 3C and D). Meanwhile, parallel to defective acrosome formation, the microtubule-based manchette structure responsible for nuclear shaping was also disorganized in Elapor1 cKO spermatids during step 8 to 12 of spermiogenesis (Fig. 3E).

Furthermore, the ultrastructure dynamics detected by TEM delineated the disruptions in acrosome biogenesis and nuclear remodeling caused by Elapor1 cKO. During the Golgi phase and cap phase of Elapor1 cKO spermatids, pro-AVs (about 200 nm diameter) were secreted from the Golgi apparatus, but unfortunately, these vesicles could not be transported and tethered to the acroplaxome on the nuclear envelope. Instead, they accumulated at the space between the Golgi apparatus and the acroplaxome, persisting as dispersed small vesicles other than fusing with each other to form a single large acrosomal structure (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, severe nuclear malformation was observed in Elapor1 cKO spermatids during late spermiogenesis, resulting in abnormal sperm head shape (Fig. 4B).

ELAPOR1 was crucial for the assembly of clathrin coated vesicles derived from the TGN

According to the characteristic of the cytoplasmic domain as an anchor for the adaptor proteins (APs), ELAPOR1 was found to facilitate clathrin-mediated intracellular vesicle traffic. To investigate whether ELAPOR1 was involved in the recruitment of clathrin through the YSKL motif in its cytoplasmic C-terminus, we expressed the recombinant ELAPOR1 either lack of C-terminus (ELAPOR1-ΔC) or inducing a site-specific mutation which changed the consensus APs-binding motif YSKL to ASKA (ELAPOR1-mut) in HEK-293T cells (Fig. 5A), and then investigated the protein interaction between recombinant ELAPOR1 and clathrin by co-IP analysis. We found that either lacking of C-terminus or mutating YSKL to ASKA disrupted the binding activity of clathrin to ELAPOR1, and their interaction involved adaptor proteins such as AP1 and AP3 (Fig. 5B), suggesting that the YSKL motif in cytoplasmic terminus of ELAPOR1 provided the binding site for adaptor proteins and was indispensable for clathrin assembly.

To confirm the crucial role of ELAPOR1 in the recruitment of clathrin onto the surface of vesicles, we investigated the localization of clathrin in Elapor1flox/flox and Elapor1 cKO spermatids by IF and IEM assays. In control spermatids, clathrin was co-localized with TGN46 positive Golgi-derived vesicles, indicating clathrin anchored onto Golgi-derived pro-AVs (Fig. 5C). In contrast, in Elapor1 cKO spermatids clathrin exhibited rare co-localization with TGN46 positive vesicles, suggesting its impaired vesicle budding (Fig. 5C). Moreover, immuno-colloidal gold labeling of clathrin in IEM assays also proved the association between ELAPOR1 and clathrin. Normally, colloidal immunogold signals of labeled clathrin abundantly distributed on the surface of most pro-AVs, consistent with the established role of clathrin in vesicle formation and transportation. Whereas, immunogold labeling of clathrin was significantly diminished on pro-AVs in Elapor1 cKO spermatids, accompanied by aberrant pro-AVs aggregation and failed acrosome formation. (Fig. 5D).

Together, these results demonstrated that ELAPOR1 could recruit clathrin via the APs-binding motif YSKL and it was indispensable for clathrin anchoring to pro-AVs, which may further involve in vesicle traffic or fusion, and ultimately contribute to acrosome formation.

Clathrin mediated vesicle transport was essential for acrosome biogenesis

In the very early research, based on cell imaging, it was proposed that clathrin-coated vesicles involved in acrosome formation [10]. However, the biological activity of clathrin in acrosome formation is still unknown. Herein, we performed intratesticular injection of pitstop2 to pharmacologically inhibit clathrin-mediated vesicular trafficking. Pitstop2 is a clathrin inhibitor which binds directly to the terminal β-propeller domain of the clathrin heavy chain, causing an arrest of clathrin coated pit dynamics [28]. The treatment of pitstop2 was ranged from P21 to P28, corresponding to the Golgi, cap and early acrosome phases of acrosome biogenesis during the first wave spermiogenesis (Fig. 6A). IF staining of ACRV1 to indicate acrosome in spermatids showed that acrosome signals were significantly reduced in pitstop2 treated testis (Fig. 6B). Specifically, IF analysis on spermatid smears revealed that acrosome signals in spermatids (step 8–9) were significantly attenuate when suffered pitstop2 treatment (Fig. 6C).

Meanwhile, the ultrastructural dynamic of the developing acrosome detected by TEM showed that a large number of abnormal acrosomes at the cap phage appeared in the pitstop2-treated testes (Fig. 6D and E). Upon further analysis of the acrosomal ultrastructure in spermatids, we found that pitstop2-treated spermatids, both the cap phase (step 5–6) and the acrosome phase (step 8–9), exhibited fragmented and immature acrosome-like structures. More importantly, this acrosome-like structure was lack of acrosomal granule, an electron-dense core rich in hydrolytic enzymes, which suggested that clathrin-coated vesicles had a key role in sorting and trafficking acrosomal proteins (Fig. 6F).

Overall, the ultrastructural difference in round spermatids caused by clathrin inhibition was remarkably similar to those in Elapor1 cKO spermatids, confirming that ELAPOR1 in association with the assembly of clathrin coated vesicles was crucial for the traffic of pro-AVs and acrosome formation.

Discussions

In this study, we explored the function of ELAPOR1 in spermatogenesis, and found that ELAPOR1 is necessary for maintaining sperm morphology, especially the acrosome biogenesis, and therefore assuring sperm fertilization. The expression of ELAPOR1 persisted throughout the development of spermatids and intensively localized to the acrosome. As a transmembrane protein, the cytoplasmic terminus of ELAPOR1 provided the anchor site for recruiting the adaptor proteins and thereby ensuring the clathrin assembly on the surface of pro-AVs derived from the Golgi apparatus. Therefore, ELAPOR1 participated in the transport of pro-AVs to form the acrosome in spermatids. In contrast, ELAPOR1 deficiency blocked the acrosome biogenesis and led to globozoospermia and male infertility (Fig. 7).

Schematic depiction of ELAPOR1 involved acrosome biogenesis. ELAPOR1 is localized in the pro-AVs derived from Golgi apparatus and promotes clathrin assembly by the YSAL motif in its cytoplasmic C-terminus, thereby mediating traffic and fusion of clathrin-coated vesicles during acrosome biogenesis. The absence of ELAPOR1 disrupts the clathrin assembly and interrupts acrosome biogenesis, therefore causing a cascade of morphological abnormalities in sperm including round-head and absence of acrosome

ELAPOR1, a transmembrane protein conventionally localized in both the Golgi apparatus and endosomes, was mostly reported to participate in the maturation of the secretory granule in glandular cells [25]. Heiko Lickert group has been consistently investigating the function and mechanism of ELAPOR1 (termed Inceptor) in insulin secretion in pancreatic β-cell [19, 29]. Recently, they also reported that ELAPOR1 facilitated acrosome formation in spermatids [26]. Moreover, the last study of ELAPOR1 reported by Shao et al. showed that ELAPOR1 was characterized as a tethering factor regulating pro-AVs fusion and critical for acrosome biogenesis [30]. However, their studies used the whole-body Elapor1 knockout mice to investigate the effect of ELAPOR1 on spermatogenesis and male fertility. Notably, ELAPOR1 is widely expressed in several tissues and most abundant in gastrointestinal tract, prostate and salivary gland. Thus, systemic knockout of Elapor1 in mice leads to hypoglycaemia and hyperinsulinaemia [19], which introduces confounded factors, such as metabolic disorder and hormone disturbance, that may affect spermatogenesis. To overcome such shortage of the whole-body knockout, we generated an Elapor1 conditional knockout mice in current study, which accurately deleted Elapor1 in spermatogenic cells and was appropriate for investigating its function in spermatogenesis.

In this study, we found that germ cell specific Elapor1 cKO male mice exhibited absolute infertility in association with some evident reproductive phenotypes including globozoospermia, asthenozoospermia and absence of acrosome in sperm, consistent with that reported in whole-body KO mice. Thus, our results confirmed the exact role of ELAPOR1 in the development of spermatids and especially in acrosome formation. However, the clinical evidence for the association of low-level expression or mutation of ELAPOR1 with globozoospermia or poor sperm fertility remains unclear. Previous studies reported that some proteins essential for acrosome biogenesis, such as ACTL7A [31], DPY19L2 [32], FAM71F1 [33], FSIP2 [34], and PICK1 [15, 35], were related to the occurrence of male infertility or sperm defects analyzed by gene screening on testicular tissues [36–41]. Therefore, it is worthy to explore the prevalence of ELAPOR1 variants in male infertile patients in the further.

Interestingly, our findings showed that ELAPOR1 deficiency in spermatids caused a defining phenotype of complete acrosome loss, due to the transport barrier between the Golgi-derived pro-AVs to the destination, acroplaxome, where pro-AVs fuse to form the acrosomal structure. The biogenesis of acrosome started from Golgi-derived pro-AVs can generally divide into three steps including vesicle formation, transport, and fusion. Notably, Shao et al. discovered the role of ELAPOR1 in the priming fusion of pro-AVs, especially its N-terminus can form a homophilic tetramer through a copper-dependent interaction, and meanwhile bind to the SNARE protein STX12, therefore mediating vesicle tethering and fusion [30]. Meanwhile, Shao et al. also discovered the dual membrane orientation of ELAPOR1, which indicates its potentially multiple roles in vesicle transport and fusion. Therefore, we believe that ELAPOR1 probably interacts with several partner proteins and involves in different processes of acrosome formation, especially depending on the different functional domains either in its N-terminus or in C-terminus. Comparing the studies on the gene knockout mice with failure in acrosome formation, we found that the ultrastructural details of the developing acrosomal granule with defective vesicle fusion exhibited the attachment of many vesicles onto the acroplaxome structure [42–45], which is different from that we found in Elapor1 cKO mice. In Elapor1 cKO spermatids, the vesicles could be successfully produced from the Golgi apparatus but unable to undergo the subsequent vesicular trafficking process. Thus, a great deal of Golgi-derived pro-AVs accumulated unusually between the Golgi apparatus and the acroplaxome, neither traffic to the acroplaxome nor fusion with each other to form a large vesicle. This phenotype is close correspondence to the localization of ELAPOR1 in the pro-AVs and acrosome in early-stage spermatids [26], suggesting its direct involvement in the formation of acrosome by mediating transport of pro-AVs.

Moreover, the underlying mechanism of ELAPOR1 participating in the vesicle transport during acrosome biogenesis was yet to be confirmed. Previous studies have demonstrated that various Ras-related proteins (RAB) and RAB associated proteins, such as RAB2, RAB6 and FAM71F1, regulate vesicle trafficking during acrosome biogenesis [33, 46]. However, structural analysis reveals that ELAPOR1 lacks GTPase catalytic domains which is the characteristic of RAB, indicating that the mechanism of ELAPOR1 in vesicle trafficking is different from that of RAB. Ansarullah et al. reported that the C-terminus of ELAPOR1 in the cytoplasmic side contained an AP-binding motif YXXΦ (Φ indicates a bulky hydrophobic amino acid and X indicates any amino acid), which was responsible for AP-clathrin anchoring, and therefore participating in the formation and maturation of secretory granules in pancreatic β-cells [19]. Due to the similarities between the acrosome and the glandular secretory granule, we speculated that ELAPOR1 played a crucial role in the formation and trafficking of clathrin-coated pro-AVs in spermatids.

Clathrin is a highly conserved scaffolding protein composed of heavy chains and light chains that were assembled into triskelion structures [47]. Clathrin can facilitate vesicle budding from the plasma membrane, TGN and endosomes, mediating endocytosis and intracellular vesicle traffic process. Clathrin can interact with several cargo adaptor proteins to form the clathrin-coated vesicles at the TGN and subsequently transport these vesicles to endosomes or secretory granules through the involvement of RAB and effector proteins [48, 49] Our study revealed that ELAPOR1 with the YSAL motif in its cytoplasmic C-terminus was crucial for recruiting clathrin onto the vesicle surface. Whereas, in ELAPOR1 deficient spermatids, the vesicles produced from the Golgi apparatus were lack of clathrin coating, and therefore disabled the further vesicular traffic and fusion.

Although clathrin is well known as the core effector in endocytosis and participated in a broad range of trafficking pathways, its function in acrosome biogenesis remains unestablished. The only study related to clathrin in acrosome biogenesis determined by gene knockout mice was that SMAP2, an GTPase activating protein that binds to clathrin, involved in acrosome formation [50]. To confirm the role of clathrin in acrosome biogenesis, we performed intratesticular injections of Pitstop2, a clathrin inhibitor, to transiently suppress the activity of clathrin. Our results showed that Pitstop2 treatment impaired the acrosome biogenesis, demonstrating that clathrin mediated vesicle trafficking was essential for this process. However, there was significant difference of ultrastructural morphology between Pitstop2-treated and Elapor1-cKO spermatids. Elapor1 deficient spermatids were completely absence of acrosome, whereas Pitstop2 treated spermatids still developed a smaller acrosome-like structure without the characteristic electron-dense acrosomal granules. This result indicated that clathrin-coated vesicles represent only one subset of pro-AVs, and more importantly, these clathrin-coated vesicles were the main carrier responsible for delivery of acrosomal proteins into the acrosome. On the other hand, the mechanism of ELAPOR1 in vesicle traffic or fusion is not just interacted with clathrin, but also has other partners, for instance, it interacted with STX12 required for the fusion of pro-AVs during acrosome biogenesis [30].

In this study, we focused on the function of cytoplasmic C-terminus of ELAPOR1. However, it doesn’t represent the comprehensive function of ELAPOR1, especially its possible role in the cargo sorting during the formation of pro-AVs which determines the kinds of proteins transported into the acrosome. Notably, the N-terminus of ELAPOR1 contains a M6PR domain known to participate in protein sorting. Recently, Siehler et al. demonstrated that ELAPOR1 function in insulin or proinsulin sorting through the Golgi-lysosomal pathway in pancreatic β-cells [29]. Based on these protein sorting mechanisms, we hypothesize that ELAPOR1 may similarly facilitate protein sorting and trafficking during acrosome biogenesis, which is a compelling direction for our future investigations.
