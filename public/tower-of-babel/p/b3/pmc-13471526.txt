Association of pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism and insulin resistance with assisted reproductive technology outcomes and pregnancy complications in women with PCOS: a retrospective propensity score matching study
Authors: Xiangting Shen, Jihong Zou, Junxia Song, Juan Gui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is frequently accompanied by insulin resistance (IR) and abnormal glucose metabolism, impairing fertility and pregnancy outcomes. This retrospective study aimed to investigate their associations with assisted reproductive technology (ART) outcomes and pregnancy complications in women with PCOS.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 1815 women with PCOS who underwent ART at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University from January 2019 to January 2025. Patients were stratified into two groups according to pre-pregnancy glucose and insulin status: a study group with abnormal glucose metabolism or IR, and a control group with neither condition. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to balance baseline characteristics. Multivariate logistic regression was used to screen factors associated with live birth, preterm birth, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), and pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH). A multivariate exploratory prediction model and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were constructed to preliminarily evaluate the predictive efficacy and discriminative performance for GDM and PIH.ResultsAfter PSM, the study group had a significantly lower live birth rate and a higher miscarriage rate than the control group (both P < 0.001). Pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR emerged as an independent associated factor of reduced live birth (OR = 0.551). For preterm birth, female age (OR = 0.843), GDM (OR = 2.789), and the number of embryos transferred (OR = 2.174) were identified as independent associated factors. Female age (OR = 1.305) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) (OR = 1.865) were significant associated indicators of GDM, while body mass index (BMI) (OR = 1.506), embryo transfer type (OR = 6.406), and FPG (OR = 2.224) were associated with PIH. Two preliminary exploratory multivariate prediction models for GDM (AUC = 0.746 pre-PSM, 0.712 post-PSM) and PIH (AUC = 0.741 pre-PSM, 0.711 post-PSM) showed moderate cohort-specific predictive efficacy for the respective complications.ConclusionPre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism or IR is independently associated with adverse ART and pregnancy outcomes in women with PCOS, including increased miscarriage risk, reduced live birth rate, and elevated odds of GDM. We identified distinct independent associated factors for live birth, preterm birth, GDM, and PIH. Furthermore, we developed preliminary exploratory multivariate prediction models for GDM and PIH. These models demonstrated moderate discriminative ability in our cohort but require external validation before any clinical application can be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09449-8.Keywords: Polycystic ovary syndrome, Insulin resistance, Assisted reproductive technology, Pregnancy outcomes, Propensity score matching

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders among women of childbearing age, with a prevalence rate of approximately 5% to 20% [1]. The core characteristics of PCOS include elevated androgen levels, ovulatory disorders, and polycystic ovarian morphology, with anovulation being the primary cause of PCOS-related infertility [2]. In addition to infertility, women with PCOS commonly present with insulin resistance (IR), hyperinsulinemia (HI), and obesity, which elevate their risk of metabolic and cardiovascular disorders including impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3–5].

The widespread application of assisted reproductive technology (ART) has enabled an increasing number of PCOS patients to achieve pregnancy via in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). However, the inherent pathophysiological features of PCOS may increase perinatal risks for both mothers and offspring. This contributes to adverse pregnancy complications such as miscarriage, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), pregnancy-induced hypertension (PIH), preeclampsia (PE), preterm birth, and cesarean delivery, findings supported by numerous studies and meta-analyses [6, 7]. Consistently, studies focusing on PCOS patients undergoing ART have confirmed that this population faces significantly higher risks of maternal complications such as GDM, PIH, and PE. Additionally, they are also at increased risk of adverse perinatal outcomes, including preterm birth, miscarriage, low birth weight, and neonatal complications [8, 9].

Current evidence indicates that these adverse ART outcomes are linked not only to the underlying metabolic syndrome of PCOS but also to comorbidities including obesity, IR, and HI [10–12]. Notably, IR and subsequent metabolic abnormalities not only participate in the pathogenesis of PCOS but also impair reproductive function by affecting follicular development, oocyte quality, and endometrial receptivity, thereby increasing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as miscarriage [11, 13, 14]. Furthermore, the prevalence of IGT and T2DM in PCOS patients is significantly higher than in the general population. Even after weight matching, this risk remains approximately 2- to 4- fold higher [15, 16], which is closely associated with adverse maternal and infant outcomes. However, the adverse effects of pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism and IR on long-term pregnancy complications in PCOS patients undergoing ART remain poorly elucidated, and whether such metabolic derangements potentially influence maternal and neonatal outcomes needs further cohort exploration.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the association of pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism and IR with ART outcomes and pregnancy complications in women with PCOS. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to improve baseline comparability, and a logistic regression model was established to evaluate the main factors correlated with live birth, preterm birth, GDM and PIH. Preliminary exploratory multivariate prediction models were conducted to evaluate the predictive efficacy and discrimination for GDM and PIH among patients with PCOS. These findings may provide exploratory references for risk stratification and assist in selecting PCOS patients who require closer antenatal monitoring to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study patients and characteristics

This retrospective study included women with PCOS who underwent IVF/ICSI and embryo transfer at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University from January 2019 to January 2025. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University and ethical exemption was granted due to the anonymous retrospective analysis of clinical data without individual intervention (2026ACCR-M089).

PCOS was diagnosed according to the Rotterdam criteria: patients met at least two of the following three criteria, after excluding other endocrine causes: (1) Clinical and/or biochemical evidence of hyperandrogenism; (2) Polycystic ovarian morphology on transvaginal ultrasound; (3) Oligo-ovulation or anovulation [17]. Patients with the following conditions were excluded: (1) Uterine malformations; (2) Severe cerebrovascular, cardiac, hepatic, renal, or hematopoietic system diseases; (3) Other medical conditions contraindicating ART and/or pregnancy.

Most patients underwent at least part of the following glucose metabolic screening prior to ART initiation: (1) Fasting plasma glucose (FPG); (2) 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with measurement of 2-hour plasma glucose; (3) Fasting insulin for the calculation of homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR).

The following clinical and laboratory variables were extracted from the electronic medical record system: female age, male age, infertility type, infertility duration, body mass index (BMI), anti-mullerian hormone (AMH), white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), FPG, free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyrotropin (TSH), thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb), anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO), endometrial thickness on the day of embryo transfer, embryo transfer cycle type, embryo transfer type, number of transferred embryos, pregnancy type, birth weight, and primary pregnancy outcomes.

Primary pregnancy complications (PIH, PE, placental and membrane abnormalities, fetal growth restriction [FGR]), neonatal complications (congenital malformation, jaundice, neonatal intensive care unit [NICU] admission), and offspring childhood development were collected via questionnaire surveys and telephone follow-ups. Childhood outcomes were assessed by parental report rather than clinical examination, which may introduce potential reporting bias.

The primary study outcome was live birth. Secondary outcomes included biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, preterm birth and pregnancy complications.

Sample size calculation

The primary outcome was live birth rate. Based on local clinical data, the estimated live birth rate was 50% in PCOS patients without abnormal glucose metabolism or IR and 35% in those with abnormal glucose metabolism/IR. Using a two-sided test with α = 0.05 and 1 − β = 80%, and a 10% missing data adjustment, the minimum required sample size was 380 (190 per group).

A total of 1815 eligible patients were enrolled from January 2019 to January 2025, which was far larger than calculated, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Groups

Based on pre-pregnancy glucose and insulin status, patients were stratified into two groups: (1) Control group: no pre-existing abnormal glucose metabolism or IR; (2) Study group: presence of either abnormal glucose metabolism or IR.

Definitions

Abnormal glucose metabolism was defined as the presence of pre-pregnancy diabetes, IGT, or impaired fasting glucose (IFG). Pre-pregnancy diabetes was diagnosed by any of the following: FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour OGTT ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%, or random serum glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with classic hyperglycemic symptoms or a hyperglycemic crisis. In this study, pre-pregnancy diabetes was diagnosed primarily based on FPG/OGTT results. In the absence of a prior diabetes diagnosis, IGT was defined as 2-hour OGTT 7.8–11.0 mmol/L, and IFG was defined as fasting plasma glucose 6.1–6.9 mmol/L with 2 h OGTT < 7.8 mmol/L. Normal glucose tolerance was defined as FPG < 6.1 mmol/L and 2-hour OGTT < 7.8 mmol/L.

IR was evaluated using the HOMA-IR index, calculated by the formula: HOMA-IR=[FPG(mmol/L)×fasting insulin (µU/mL)]/22.5. In this study, IR was defined as a HOMA-IR value > 1.78 [18, 19]. Patients in the IR group were strictly screened to exclude those with glucose metabolic abnormalities (pre-pregnancy diabetes, IGT, IFG). All subgroups were mutually exclusive with no overlapping cases.

According to the developmental stage of the embryo, embryo transfer type was categorized as cleavage-stage embryo (day 2–3 after fertilization) and blastocyst (day 5–6 after fertilization). Reproductive outcomes were defined as follows: Live birth was the delivery of a viable infant after 28 weeks of gestation, with the live birth rate calculated as the proportion of live birth cycles among all transfer cycles. Biochemical pregnancy was a positive serum beta human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) test before ultrasound evidence of a gestational sac. Clinical pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of a gestational sac on transvaginal ultrasound. Miscarriage was defined as pregnancy loss before 28 weeks of gestation, further stratified into early miscarriage (< 12 weeks) and late miscarriage (12–28 weeks). Preterm birth was the live birth of an infant between 28 and 37 weeks of gestation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were described as means ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or medians (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data between the two groups, while independent t-test/Mann‑Whitney U test was applied for continuous variables.

PSM was used to balance baseline characteristics between the two groups, with the following covariates included: female age, infertility duration, infertility type, BMI, AMH, embryo transfer type, and number of transferred embryos. A 1:1 nearest neighbor matching method with a caliper width of 0.001 was adopted for PSM. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate the balance of covariates before and after PSM. An absolute SMD value < 0.1 indicated a well-balanced covariate.

Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify the associated factors for live birth, preterm birth, GDM and PIH before and after PSM, with results presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A preliminary exploratory multivariate combined prediction model was conducted and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted to evaluate the overall predictive efficacy and discrimination ability of the model for GDM and PIH in PCOS patients undergoing ART. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For post-hoc multiple comparisons following chi-square or Kruskal-Wallis tests, the statistical significance threshold was adjusted to P < 0.017.

Results

A total of 417 PCOS patients with pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism or IR and 1398 patients without either condition were enrolled in the study, all of whom underwent IVF/ICSI and embryo transfer. After PSM, 378 patients were included in each group. We evaluated baseline characteristics, pregnancy outcomes, maternal and neonatal complications, influencing factors for key pregnancy outcomes, and high-risk subgroups for GDM and PIH before and after PSM.

Patient baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the comparisons of baseline characteristics between the two groups before and after PSM. Marked baseline differences were noted between the two groups pre-matching: the study group had older female and male ages (both P < 0.001), significantly higher BMI (P < 0.001), PLT count (P = 0.01), and FPG level (P < 0.001), as well as elevated FT3 concentration (P = 0.004) and greater endometrial thickness on the day of embryo transfer (P = 0.005) compared with the control group. To further clarify the association between different pre-pregnancy glucose and insulin status and the risk of developing GDM, an exploratory subgroup analysis was performed. Patients were categorized into four groups: normal, pre-pregnancy diabetes, IGT/IFG, and IR. The results demonstrated marked inter-group differences after PSM, with the IGT/IFG group showing the highest risk of progressing to GDM (25.8% vs. 48.5% vs. 17.4%, P = 0.003).

Factors associated with live birth, preterm birth, GDM and PIH

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify the independent factors associated with pregnancy outcomes and complications in PCOS patients undergoing IVF/ICSI.

Live birth

Infertility duration (OR = 0.899, 95%CI [0.834,0.968], P = 0.005) and pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR (OR = 0.551, 95%CI [0.384,0.792], P = 0.001) were independently negatively associated with live birth. In contrast, endometrial thickness on the day of embryo transfer (OR = 1.106, 95%CI [1.014,1.208], P = 0.024) and the number of transferred embryos (OR = 1.704, 95%CI [1.147,2.532], P = 0.008) were independently positively associated with live birth. Preterm birth

Female age was independently inversely associated with preterm birth risk (OR = 0.843, 95%CI [0.739,0.962], P = 0.011), while GDM (OR = 2.789, 95%CI [1.425,5.458], P = 0.003), FGR (OR = 11.977, 95%CI [1.034,138.711], P = 0.047), and the number of embryos transferred (OR = 2.174, 95%CI [1.110,4.257], P = 0.024) were independently positively associated with preterm birth risk. Male age was marginally associated with preterm birth (OR = 1.107, 95%CI [0.992,1.235], P = 0.07). ROC curve analysis for predicting GDM and PIH

ROC curve analyses for GDM and PIH were performed in both the total cohort before PSM and the matched cohort after PSM. A binary logistic regression exploratory prediction model was constructed in both pre‑PSM and post‑PSM populations using female age, BMI, FPG, infertility duration, and anti-TPO to calculate individual predicted probability of GDM. Then, ROC curves were plotted based on the predicted probability. For prediction before PSM (Fig. 1A), the prediction model yielded an AUC of 0.746 (95%CI [0.689, 0.803], P<0.001). Using the maximum Youden index (0.414), the optimal threshold for the combined predictive probability was determined as 0.18 specific to this cohort, with 78.9% sensitivity and 62.5% specificity. After PSM, the model achieved an AUC of 0.712 (95%CI [0.614, 0.809], P<0.001) for GDM prediction (Fig. 1B). The optimal cutoff value of the combined predicted probability was 0.24, with a sensitivity of 60.0%, a specificity of 75.6%, and a Youden index of 0.356. All statistically derived thresholds are cohort-specific exploratory indicators rather than definitive diagnostic criteria, given the lack of external validation.

Discussion

In this retrospective PSM study of PCOS patients undergoing IVF/ICSI and embryo transfer, we explored statistical correlations between pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR and ART outcomes, pregnancy complications, as well as neonatal and childhood developmental outcomes. After PSM to minimize confounding bias, our findings suggest that pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR is correlated with relatively lower live birth rate, higher miscarriage rate, and elevated risk of placental abruption in PCOS women undergoing ART. We further identified associated factors for key pregnancy outcomes and established preliminary exploratory moderate predictive efficacy models for GDM and PIH, providing exploratory references for the management of PCOS patients undergoing ART, but not for definitive clinical decision-making.

Pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR and reproductive outcomes

Our results demonstrated that PCOS patients with pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR had a significantly lower live birth rate and a higher miscarriage rate than those without these metabolic abnormalities, which is consistent with the majority of previous studies. Chen et al. observed in a retrospective cohort of PCOS patients undergoing their first embryo transfer cycle that the early miscarriage rate increased from 7.14% to 16.22% and the live birth rate decreased from 63.03% to 47.88% with increasing HOMA-IR values [20]. A meta-analysis also indicated that high BMI (OR = 1.48, 95%CI [1.32,1.67]) and IR (MD = 0.32, 95%CI [0.15,0.49]) were associated with an increased risk of spontaneous abortion in PCOS patients undergoing ART [12]. By contrast, Luo et al. [11] reported no significant association between IR and live birth rate in PCOS patients. However, their study was retrospective and failed to adjust for confounding factors related to clinical interventions. These unadjusted factors may underlie the discrepant findings between their study and ours.

Our study also found that endometrial thickness on the day of embryo transfer and the number of transferred embryos were independent positive associated indicators for live birth, while longer infertility duration was an independent negative associated indicator. Zhu et al. showed in a study of 1242 PCOS patients that female age, high BMI, infertility duration, number of transferred embryos, and embryo transfer type exert significant independent impacts on live birth in fresh embryo transfer cycles using antagonist protocols [21]. A retrospective analysis of 1920 PCOS patients undergoing fresh embryo transfer found that endometrial thickness on the trigger day, number of transferred embryos, and infertility duration were associated with live birth in univariate analysis, but only infertility duration remained significant in the multivariable model [22]. Another study of 1018 PCOS patients using the gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist protocol indicated endometrial thickness as a positive factor for live birth, but found no significant independent association between infertility duration and live birth [23]. These findings indicate that the relationship between infertility duration and live birth remains controversial, underlining the need for further large-scale prospective studies to validate its independent contribution and clarify potential causal mechanisms.

Pre-pregnancy abnormal glucose metabolism/IR and pregnancy complications (GDM and PIH)

GDM and PIH are the two most common pregnancy complications in PCOS patients undergoing ART. Our logistic regression analysis indicated that female age and FPG are independent associated indicators of GDM, while BMI, embryo transfer type, and FPG are independent associated indicators of PIH. These findings are consistent with previous research and further highlight the importance of pre-pregnancy metabolic optimization in this population as a potential preventive strategy.

Risk factors and evidence for GDM

Kouhkan et al. identified higher BMI (25–29.9 kg/m², OR = 3.27; ≥30 kg/m², OR = 5.14) and elevated FPG (OR = 1.40) as significant risk associated indicators for GDM in a nested case-control study of 270 pregnant women undergoing ART, and the GDM group had a significantly higher mean female age than the non-GDM group [24]. Mustaniemi et al. also found in a multicenter case-control study that obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², OR = 5.14) and advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years, OR = 2.58) are associated with an increased risk of GDM in PCOS patients [25].

Li et al. demonstrated in a prospective cohort of 248 PCOS women that elevated early pregnancy FPG (≥ 4.86 mmol/L) is independently associated with a higher risk of subsequent GDM (OR = 2.80), further supporting the potential indicative value of FPG for GDM [26]. However, FPG screening alone may be insufficient to detect abnormal glucose metabolism in PCOS patients, and the OGTT remains the gold standard for diagnosis [27, 28]. Therefore, a combined screening approach of FPG and OGTT is recommended for the detection of prediabetes in PCOS patients before ART.

Risk factors and evidence for PIH

A positive correlation between BMI and PIH has been consistently demonstrated in numerous studies. Jia and colleagues found that PCOS women with GDM complicated by PIH had significantly higher BMI and FPG levels than healthy controls, and these variables were identified as independent associated indicator of both GDM and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [29]. A meta-analysis by Palomba et al. reported that PCOS women have a 3–4 fold increased risk of PIH without BMI adjustment [30].

Our study identifies embryo transfer type as an independent associated indicator of PIH, a finding not previously reported specifically in PCOS populations. While a recent meta-analysis [31] noted a higher PIH risk in programmed frozen embryo transfer (FET) cycles compared to natural cycles (OR = 1.40)—specifically limited to blastocyst transfers (OR = 1.87)—no prior study has directly examined the association between transfer stage (cleavage vs. blastocyst) and PIH risk within the PCOS subgroup. Notably, although opinions remain inconsistent, most current high‑quality evidence shows no significant difference in PIH risk between cleavage‑stage and blastocyst transfer [32, 33]. By contrast, FET, particularly artificial cycle FET without a functional corpus luteum, was strongly linked to an increased risk of PIH and PE [34, 35]. The absence of corpus luteum–derived progesterone and relaxin may impair vascular adaptation and elevate PIH risk, particularly in metabolically vulnerable PCOS women [36, 37]. In our study population after PSM, 86.1% of blastocyst transfers were FET cycles, among which 58.3% used artificial cycles and 26.5% used down‑regulated artificial cycles for endometrial preparation. Those may account for the substantially elevated risk of PIH observed with blastocyst transfer in our study.

Our data suggest this association requires further validation. The identification of BMI, FPG, and embryo transfer type as associated indicators highlights the complex interplay between maternal metabolism and ART procedures in driving hypertensive disorders.

Cutoff values of predictive probability and clinical recommendations

For GDM prediction, the model included female age, BMI, FPG, infertility duration, and anti-TPO; for PIH, the model further incorporated the number and type of transferred embryos. All models showed moderate discriminative ability before and after PSM, with AUCs ranging from 0.71 to 0.75.

Consistent evidence has linked advanced age, elevated BMI, and higher FPG levels to an increased risk of GDM, both in the general obstetric population and among women with PCOS. A previous study of 434 PCOS women established a multivariable logistic model for GDM with age, BMI, HbA1c, total cholesterol and family history, achieving an AUC of 0.937 and optimal cutoff of 0.186 (sensitivity 83.3%, specificity 92.3%) [38]. Another prospective multicenter pre-pregnancy cohort in 189 PCOS women established a multivariable logistic model incorporating first-degree relatives with T2DM, FPG, fasting insulin, and other metabolic factors, with an AUC of 0.87, supporting feasible and effective GDM prediction [39] in their respective cohorts.

Zhou et al. identified that age ≥ 27 years, BMI ≥ 24 kg/m², family history of hypertension or hypertensive disorders complicating pregnancy (HDCP), adverse pregnancy history, and oral contraceptive use were independent risk associated indicators for HDCP in women with PCOS, with these factors showing good predictive value on ROC analysis (n = 188) [40]. Furthermore, incorporating ART-related variables—specifically, the number and type of embryos transferred—into our PIH prediction model aligns with recent evidence. This evidence suggests that ART protocols and embryo transfer strategies may independently be associated with an increased risk of hypertensive and metabolic complications in PCOS pregnancies [8, 41].

Collectively, these findings emphasize that precise pre-ART management—specifically targeting weight control, rigorous glucose screening (combined FPG/OGTT), and careful consideration of embryo transfer strategies—is essential to potentially reduce the risks of GDM and PIH in women with PCOS.

Preterm birth and long-term offspring outcomes

Our regression analysis revealed an inverse correlation between female age and preterm birth risk. This contrasts with most previous studies, which show that advanced maternal age (especially ≥ 35 or ≥ 40 years) increases preterm birth risk [42, 43]. A large cohort study of over 160,000 deliveries identified a U-shaped relationship between maternal age and preterm birth, with increased risk in women aged ≥ 40 years and younger women (20–24 years), and the lowest risk in the 30–34 years age group [44]. The median age of PCOS patients in our study was only 30 years, and the inverse association between age and preterm birth is likely attributable to the limited age range of our cohort, where the well-established risks of advanced maternal age have not yet emerged. Furthermore, the mean female age was 30.73 years in the double-embryo transfer group and 29.80 years in the twin delivery group, both lower than in the single-embryo transfer (31.34 years) and singleton delivery (30.91 years) groups. Preterm patients also showed a younger mean age compared to term birth patients (30.02 vs. 30.94 years). Therefore, the inverse association may be partly attributed to the higher prevalence of double‑embryo transfer and twin pregnancies in younger patients in our cohort, both of which are strong risk associated indicators for preterm birth [45, 46]. However, there were also studies suggesting that a higher risk of preterm birth among women aged 30–34 years [47, 48]. Inconsistencies in previous studies may also stem from differences in study design, population characteristics, and adjustment for confounding factors.

Importantly, our study found that preterm birth is significantly associated with adverse neonatal and long-term childhood outcomes, including higher NICU admission rates, delayed physical development, and childhood developmental and behavioral disorders. Preterm birth is a well-recognized risk factor for offspring neurological development. Compared to full-term infants, preterm infants face higher rates of behavioral problems (e.g., attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, depression, autism) and greater impairments in language, cognitive, sensory, emotional, and motor domains [49, 50]. These findings highlight the long-term developmental consequences of preterm delivery and the importance of implementing preventive strategies for preterm birth in high-risk PCOS pregnancies.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several clinical implications for the management of PCOS patients undergoing ART. First, despite PSM, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded due to incomplete baseline balance and retrospective design; thus, our results indicate an association rather than causation between pre-pregnancy metabolic abnormalities and adverse outcomes. Second, the prediction models and ROC-derived cutoffs are strictly exploratory and lack external validation. They should not be used as diagnostic criteria but may serve as theoretical references for risk stratification in research settings. Third, targeted pre-ART interventions (e.g., glucose control, weight management) are recommended to optimize metabolic status and reduce perinatal risks. Finally, given the link between preterm birth and long-term developmental risks, future prospective studies should include regular offspring follow-up for early intervention.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite applying PSM to balance baseline characteristics, residual differences remained, indicating incomplete balance. Combined with the retrospective design, this precludes causal inference; our findings demonstrate a statistical association between pre-pregnancy metabolic abnormalities and adverse outcomes, but cannot prove causality. Second, the single-center setting may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations. Third, the small sample size for rare outcomes (e.g., FGR, placental abruption) resulted in wide 95% CIs and unstable estimates. Fourth, reliance on parental reports for childhood developmental data may introduce recall bias. Fifth, data on concurrent autoimmune diseases and endometriosis—known risk factors for adverse outcomes—were not systematically screened. Sixth, combining abnormal glucose metabolism and IR into a single exposure group may reduce the interpretability of our results. Large-scale prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant association between pre-pregnancy metabolic abnormalities and adverse ART outcomes in PCOS patients. We identified independent risk factors and established prediction models for GDM and PIH with moderate discriminative ability. These findings underscore the importance of routine pre-ART metabolic screening and targeted interventions. However, given the retrospective nature of the study, our prediction models remain exploratory and warrant rigorous validation in large-scale prospective cohorts before clinical implementation.
