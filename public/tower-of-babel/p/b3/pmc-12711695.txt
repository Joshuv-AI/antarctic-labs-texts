Age-(in)dependent altered molecular mechanisms in Parkinson’s disease through extracellular vesicle proteome and lipidome
Authors: Yu-Ting Zhang, Hao Zhang, Weichao Su, Weiguo Liu, Ya-Ting Chen, Hui-Ying Ren, Maoqin He, Yan-Xi Zhang, Yu-Ping Fan, Wei Liu, Meng-Han Li, Ya-Xin Shi, Qiu-Yi Tang, Yi Liu, Sheng-Hua Zong, Anke Wesselius, Maurice P Zeegers, Yun Xu, Cheng-Xiao Ma, Yang Zhang, Evan Yi-Wen Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryParkinson’s disease (PD) remains insidious and clinically elusive at early stages due to the lack of precise, non-invasive biomarkers. Given their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier, extracellular vesicles (EVs) offer a promising platform for biomarker discovery in neurodegeneration. Using an affinity-based EV isolation method, we profile EV proteomes and lipidomes from plasma across life stages, followed by targeted validation via parallel reaction monitoring (PRM). We identify both age-independent and age-dependent EV biomarkers predictive of prodromal PD and capable of distinguishing PD from multiple system atrophy (MSA). Functional analyses reveal protein-lipid interactions contributing to PD pathophysiology, including lipid-mediated modulation of APOE signaling. Machine learning models integrating putative EV proteins achieve robust classification across age groups. Our findings demonstrate the diagnostic potential of EV-derived molecules for early PD detection and mechanistic insights, advancing a clinically applicable, non-invasive strategy for risk stratification and disease intervention.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, extracellular vesicles, proteomics and lipidomics, early and distinct detection, machine-learning-based diagnosis

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the fastest-growing neurodegenerative disorder globally,1,2 driven largely by an aging population and lacking both curative treatment and reliable early diagnostic strategies.3,4,5 To address these challenges, recent research has focused on discovering reliable and specific biomarkers for early PD diagnosis, aiming to identify high-risk individuals before the onset of severe symptoms, particularly based on less-invasive biofluids.6,7

In recent years, blood-based biomarkers have garnered increasing attention due to their accessibility and the minimally invasive nature of collection compared to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).8 Nonetheless, the biochemical heterogeneity and instability of those emerging PD biomarkers—e.g., synuclein aggregates in blood—coupled with the presence of confounding peripheral proteins, pose significant analytical challenges. Although α-synuclein seed amplification assays (SAAs) demonstrate robust diagnostic performance in CSF,9 their limited stability and reproducibility in peripheral blood substantially hinder their clinical applicability and translational potential.10,11 In this context, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as a particularly promising avenue for neurodegenerative biomarker discovery,12,13 owing to their unique ability to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and relatively higher stability, thereby enabling peripheral access to molecular signatures that originate within the CNS.14,15 Growing evidence has highlighted EVs, containing remarkable proteins and lipids, as playing vital roles in various physiological and pathological processes16,17,18 with strong potential for early detection of PD.19,20 However, the integration of those two major molecular moieties in the underlying mechanistic pathways for PD has yet to be well demonstrated.

Beyond their diagnostic value, EV-derived proteins and lipids also reflect dynamic molecular changes across the disease continuum, allowing differentiation between physiological aging and disease-specific alterations.21,22 Notably, age-independent EV signatures may serve as more robust indicators of PD pathology and are less confounded by aging effects. Additionally, certain EV biomarkers may have predictive value in prodromal PD (pPD), underscoring the potential of EVs not only for cross-sectional diagnosis but also as prospective biomarkers capable of capturing disease onset and trajectory. While EV biomarkers show promise for early PD detection, differentiating PD from atypical parkinsonism,23 particularly multiple system atrophy (MSA), remains a major clinical challenge due to their substantial phenotypic overlap.24 Identifying EV-based signatures that distinguish PD from MSA is critical for enhancing diagnostic specificity and informing targeted interventions.

In this study, we employed the in-house EVlent (extracellular vesicles isolated efficiently, naturally, and totally)25 approach to identify plasma EV-derived protein and lipid biomarkers for PD, incorporating prodromal stage and MSA to assess diagnostic performance. Integrated proteomic-lipidomic profiling with targeted validation revealed robust age-independent and age-dependent candidates. These findings support the development of blood-based EV biosignatures for early and differential PD diagnosis and offer mechanistic insights with therapeutic relevance.

Results

Characterization of participants and isolated EVs

To investigate the role of EVs, as across-BBB molecules in PD development, we applied a sequential strategy among multiple groups of PD, pPD, and MSA with across-age controls, by integrating high-throughput multi-omics, targeted detection, plasma-to-CSF comparison using rather large sample sizes in both discovery and validation cohorts (Figure 1; Table S1). A total of 131 participants were included in the discovery cohort, with PDs (n = 40) aged 52–77 years, ACs (aged controls, n = 31) aged 42–71 years, and YCs (young controls, n = 60) aged 23–40 years (Figure S2A). For the validation cohort, we included 63 additional participants comprising 33 PD cases (aged 51–74 years), and 30 controls split into 15 ACs (aged 62–77 years) and 15 YCs (aged 21–30 years) (Figure S2B). We additionally included 19 pPD cases (aged 46–72 years) and 10 MSA cases (aged 50–74 years) as independent cohorts (Figure S2C). Moreover, an additional 5 PD cases with plasma-CSF paired samples were included.

We employed an in-house-developed affinity-based EV isolation strategy to maximize high-throughput, broad-coverage, and effective purification using small sample volume, as previously described25 (Figure S1). Isolated plasma EVs were characterized for size, morphology, and specific biomarkers; these nanovesicles showed the typical EV-like round morphology by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure S3A); nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed concentrated EVs particle size of 60–70 nm (Figure S3B); and western blotting (WB) analysis confirmed the presence of canonical EV biomarkers CD9, TSG101, and HSP70 while confirming the absence of the negative biomarker Calnexin, demonstrating specificity and reproducibility of the isolation protocol (Figure S3C).

EV proteomic profiles

We quantified 3,795 EV proteins across 112 samples. Proteins with >50% missing values were excluded to construct a high-confidence proteomic matrix. The measured EV protein list was compared against a reference list of EV proteins obtained from reference repositories Vesiclepedia (http://microvesicles.org/extracellular_vesicle_markers)26 and ExoCarta (http://exocarta.org/).27 Remarkably, 92.0% and 94.0% of the top 100 EV proteins listed in these databases, respectively, were identified in our study, including well-known markers such as CD63, CD9, and CD81. These findings confirm the efficiency and robustness of our EV isolation and proteomic profiling methods (Figure S4; Table S2).

Age-independent EV proteomic biomarkers

We performed differential analysis on the plasma EV proteome between groups with different ages (i.e., PDs vs. ACs vs. YCs) (Figure 2A). This analysis identified 179 EV proteins (114 up-regulated and 65 down-regulated) that were differentially expressed (false discovery rate [FDR]-p < 0.05) in PDs compared with both ACs and YCs (Figure 2B; Table S3). Verifying these findings against pooled data from ACs and YCs produced consistent results, suggesting that these identified EV proteins influence PD development irrespective of age stages, thereby categorizing them as age-independent EV differentially expressed proteins (AI-EVDEPs).

To validate the stability of these findings, we repeated the analyses at the peptide level. The majority of AI-EVDEPs could be verified, with more than 2 peptides per corresponding EV protein exhibiting significant signals, supporting the robustness of the data (Table S5). Notably, the identified key AI-EVDEPs, such as A2M, APOE, and SERPINA1, were highly replicated with pronounced peptide-level signals (Figure 2C). We performed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis, which showed significant enrichment of these AI-EVDEPs in pathways including cholesterol metabolism (Figure 2D; Table S6).

Age-dependent EV proteomic biomarkers

Through differential analysis of EV proteome across the three groups (i.e., PDs vs. ACs and then ACs vs. YCs), we identified 7 EV proteins that showed no significant difference in PDs vs. YCs but were consistently differential in PDs vs. ACs and ACs vs. YCs (FDR-p < 0.05). These EV proteins were categorized as age-dependent EV differentially expressed proteins (AD-EVDEPs) (Figure 2E; Table S7). Specifically, we found that the levels of SEMA3C, VNN2, PTPN23, STX4, and CCT8 decreased in ACs compared to YCs (FDR-p = 0.020, 0.008, 0.020, 0.028, and 0.048, respectively) but increased again in PDs compared to ACs (FDR-p = 0.014, 0.009, 0.025, 0.004, and 0.002, respectively). To conceptualize the role of these biomarkers, we developed a framework for AD-EVDEPs, illustrated in Figure 2F, to inform high-risk PD prevention strategies across different age stages. In addition, 3 other AD-EVDEPs showed a distinct pattern: levels were up-regulated in ACs compared to YCs but subsequently down-regulated in PDs compared to ACs. This transition in EV protein levels highlights a dynamic shift that warrants careful consideration when evaluating the role of these biomarkers in PD progression.

Identification of key EV proteins in the significant co-expression module

We applied WGCNA and identified 8 modules of co-regulated proteins ranging from 2 to 67 AI-EVDEPs per module (Figures S6A and S6B; Table S8). To identify hub proteins within each co-expression module, we calculated the module membership (kME) values, defined as the correlation between individual protein expression profiles and the corresponding module eigengenes. The distribution of kME values was plotted for each module to evaluate intra-module connectivity (Figure 2I). We then selected the top-ranked proteins with the highest kME values (|kME| > 0.8), reflecting strong correlations with the module eigengene and suggesting their potential central roles in the module’s biological functions (Figure 2J). Among these, we identified 2 hub proteins in the M1 module (PDGFD, kME = 0.926; PDGFB, kME = 0.863), 2 hub proteins in the M2 module (HBG1, kME = 0.984; HBG2, kME = 0.982), 8 hub proteins in the M3 module (BLVRB, kME = 0.946; HBD, kME = 0.942; HBB, kME = 0.934), 11 hub proteins in the M4 module (RPL7A, kME = 0.948; NACA, kME = 0.921), 5 hub proteins in the M5 module (H1-5, kME = 0.953; MNDA, kME = 0.951), 2 hub proteins in the M6 module (LALBA, kME = 0.990; F11, kME = 0.988), 52 hub proteins in the M7 module (C3, kME = 0.973; SERPING1, kME = 0.972; SERPINC1, kME = 0.968), and 1 hub protein in the M8 module (IFT140, kME = 0.851) (Figure 2J; Table S9).

In addition, the association analysis between these EV protein modules and PD-associated clinical traits was performed. Among the PD-associated modules, M6, M7, and M8 showed positive associations with clinical traits; specifically, M6 was positively associated with Fatigue Severity Scale and PD Questionnaire-8 scores (FDR-p < 0.001 and <0.05, respectively), M7 with Hoehn-Yahr stage and Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQ) (FDR-p < 0.05), and M8 with NMSQ and PD Sleep Scale (FDR-p < 0.001 and <0.01, respectively) (Figures S6C and S6D).

EV lipidomic profiles

Given the strong association between EV protein and lipid metabolism suggested by our analysis, we further explored whether lipid metabolism is altered by differences in protein regulation for PD development based on EV levels. For this, we conducted a high-throughput plasma EV lipidomics analysis. We quantified 2,236 EV lipids across 98 samples, constituting 1,667 lipid molecules in positive ion mode and 569 lipid molecules in negative ion mode.

Age-independent EV lipidomic biomarkers

We performed partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) and observed a distinct pattern across the three groups, with PD samples showing more pronounced separation from ACs and YCs (Figures 3A and 3B; S7A and S7B). Differential analysis revealed significant differences in EV lipid profiles between PDs vs. ACs and PDs vs. YCs, with 45 up-regulated and 91 down-regulated EV lipids (FDR-p < 0.05) (Figures 3C and 3D).

The up-regulated EV lipids were predominantly composed of four major lipid sub-classes: neutral lipids, sphingolipids, phospholipids, and fatty acyl and other lipids. Most of these were triglycerides (TGs) and diglycerides (DGs), both classified as neutral lipids (38/45) (Figure 3E). On the other hand, the down-regulated EV lipids comprised five major lipid sub-classes: phospholipids, sphingolipids, neutral lipids, derivatized lipids, and fatty acyl and other lipids. Most of these were phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylcholine (PC), all classified as phospholipids (38/91) (Figure 3F).

A total of 136 EV lipids were identified as age-independent EV differentially expressed lipids (AI-EVDELs), among which 123 showed a variable importance in projection (VIP) score greater than 1 based on the PLS-DA model (Figures 3G and 3H; Tables 10 and S11). We further performed Pearson correlation analysis between the identified AI-EVDEPs and AI-EVDELs, retaining only EV protein-lipid pairs with significant correlations (FDR-p < 0.05) (Figure 3I; Table S12). Notably, we found certain correlations of EV proteins, i.e., APOE, APOH, and APOD, with a wide range of EV lipids (Figure 3I).

Age-dependent EV lipidomic biomarkers

Similar to AD-EVDEPs, we performed a differential analysis of samples from the three groups, comparing PDs vs. ACs and then ACs vs. YCs. We identified 37 EV lipids, defined as age-dependent EV differentially expressed lipids (AD-EVDELs), that showed no significant differences between PDs and YCs but were consistently differential when comparing PDs vs. ACs and ACs vs. YCs (FDR-p < 0.05) (Figures 3J and 3K; Tables S13 and S14). A total of 20 EV lipids with a VIP value > 1 were identified through the PLS-DA model. Additionally, we performed Pearson correlation analysis between identified AD-EVDEPs and AD-EVDELs, retaining only EV protein-lipid pairs with significant correlations (FDR-p < 0.05) (Table S15). Among the identified associations, we found that EV proteins, i.e., CCT8 and STX4, were positively correlated with SPH(d12:0) (FDR-p = 0.010 & 0.016, respectively) (Figure 3L).

Identification of key EV lipids in the significant co-expression module

Similarly, the distribution of kME values was plotted for each module to assess intra-module connectivity (Figure 4A). The top-ranking lipids with the highest kME values (|kME| > 0.8) from each of the 6 modules were selected and presented as representative hub lipids (Figure 4B). Among these, we identified 19 hub lipids in the M1 module (PS(40:7e), kME = 0.948; PE(16:1e_20:4), kME = 0.939), 24 hub lipids in the M2 module (TG(12:1e_6:0_16:0), kME = 0.954; TG(12:1e_8:0_16:0), kME = 0.946), 3 hub lipids in the M3 module (PS(20:0e), kME = 0.897; MLCL(48:4), kME = 0.852; cardiolipin [CL](80:6), kME = 0.821), 5 hub lipids in the M4 module (DG(36:3e), kME = 0.921; DG(36:0e).1, kME = 0.882), and 1 hub lipid in the M5 module (TG(4:0_14:0_16:0).1, kME = 0.810), while no hub lipids were detected in the M6 module (|kME| < 0.8) (Figure 4B; Table S17). An association analysis was also conducted between the EV lipid modules and PD-associated clinical traits, among which M3, M4, and M5 showed positive associations (FDR-p < 0.001) (Figures S8C and S8D).

Comparative profiling of PD and MSA to identify disease-specific EV biomarkers

PD-specific age-independent EV proteomic biomarkers

Despite substantial clinical overlap between PD and MSA in the early symptomatic stage, accurate differential diagnosis remains essential due to their distinct neurodegenerative mechanisms and therapeutic implications. Building on our initial identification of PD-associated AI-EVDEPs by comparing PD cases to healthy controls, we incorporated an additional cohort of 10 MSA cases to define MSA-specific signatures. To ensure disease specificity, we performed cross-disorder comparisons and excluded proteins that were shared or concordantly regulated in both PD and MSA groups. Proteins uniquely expressed or exhibiting opposite trends were retained as disease-specific AI-EVDEPs. This stepwise refinement yielded distinct PD- and MSA-specific EV biomarker sets, accounting for shared molecular features while enhancing diagnostic discrimination (Figure 4C). Among the 805 AI-EVDEPs identified in MSA (Table S18), 55 overlapped with the 179 PD-associated AI-EVDEPs, including 2 EV proteins that displayed opposite expression patterns between the two disorders (Table S19). After excluding the 55 shared EV proteins from the PD dataset and retaining the 2 EV proteins with opposite expression patterns, a total of 126 PD-specific AI-EVDEPs were identified (Table S20). Similarly, after excluding the 55 overlapping proteins from the MSA dataset and retaining the 2 proteins with opposite regulation, a total of 591 MSA-specific AI-EVDEPs were identified (Table S21).

PD-specific age-independent EV lipidomic biomarkers

We also performed PLS-DA on EV lipidomic profiles of MSA, AC, and YC groups and observed a clear separation of MSA samples from both ACs and YCs (Figures 4D and 4E; S9A and S9B). We applied the same comparative approach used in the proteomic analysis to the EV lipidome to define disease-specific lipid biomarkers. In positive ion mode, 550 MSA-associated AI-EVDELs (Figure S9C; Table S22) and 94 PD-associated AI-EVDELs were identified, with 37 EV lipids overlapping between groups, including 7 with opposite expression patterns (Table S24). After excluding shared lipids and retaining those with opposite trends, we identified 64 PD-specific (Table S26) and 418 MSA-specific AI-EVDELs (Figure 4F; Table S28). In negative ion mode, 193 MSA-associated (Figure S9D; Table S23) and 42 PD-associated AI-EVDELs were identified, with 5 overlapping lipids, including 1 showing an opposite expression trend (Table S25). After adjusting for overlap, 38 PD-specific (Table S27) and 169 MSA-specific AI-EVDELs were retained (Figure 4G; Table S29).

Evaluation of EV biomarker concordance between plasma and CSF

To assess concordant EV protein and lipid expression between plasma and CSF, we conducted proteomic and lipidomic profiling in paired plasma-CSF samples from 5 additional PD cases. Among the 126 PD-specific AI-EVDEPs identified in the discovery cohort, 27 were detected in both plasma and CSF (Figure S10A), showing a consistently co-regulated pattern across the two biofluids (Figures S10C and S10D; Table S30). In parallel, EV lipidomic analysis of the same 5 PD cases revealed a similar consistently co-regulated pattern for the majority of 15 AI-EVDELs measured in both plasma and CSF (Figure S10B), based on the 102 PD-specific AI-EVDELs identified in the discovery cohort (Figures S10E and S10F; Table S31). Overall, our findings suggest that plasma and CSF show overlap and concordant expression in EV protein and lipid profiles, implying that molecular alterations in the brain may be partially reflected in blood-based EVs.

PRM validation of PD-specific EVDEPs in an independent cohort

To validate candidate EVDEPs, we established a high-throughput, multiplexed parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) assay in an independent cohort. As a targeted proteomics approach, PRM offers high resolution and specificity and is widely recognized for robust biomarker verification.29,30 In the validation cohort, 8 EVDEPs previously identified as up-regulated in PD were successfully quantified by PRM. Among them, 7 proteins—APOE, KNG1, KLKB1, APCS, APOD, CFH, and GC—exhibited consistent upregulation in PD compared to controls, aligning with the discovery cohort trends (Figure 5A; Table S32). Of the 7 EVDEPs previously found to be down-regulated in PD, HBA1/HBA2 and HBB showed consistent down-regulation in the PRM results (Figure 5B; Table S32). Further differential expression analysis revealed that AMBP, APOD, and APOE tended to be up-regulated in PD compared to YCs (Figures 5C and 5D), while APOE also showed a trend of elevation in PD compared to ACs (Figures 5E and 5F). These findings support the reproducibility and robustness of selected PD-specific EVDEPs in an independent cohort and highlight APOE as a particularly consistent biomarker candidate across comparisons.

Validation of early alterations in PD-specific EV biomarkers for the prodromal stage

Early alterations of PD-specific age-independent EV proteomic biomarkers in pPD

To assess whether the identified PD-specific AI-EVDEPs exhibited early alterations and predictive potential, we performed validation in an independent cohort of 19 pPDs with longitudinal follow-up. This cohort included 9 participants who later progressed to clinically diagnosed PD (pPD-T) and 10 pPDs who had not yet progressed to a clinical PD diagnosis at the time of analysis (pPD-F).

Unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) based on the expression profiles of PD-specific AI-EVDEPs revealed a notable separation between pPD-T and pPD-F groups (Figure 5G), suggesting underlying molecular differences prior to clinical conversion. Subsequent differential expression analysis between pPD-T and pPD-F individuals identified APOE, GC, and APOD as significantly up-regulated in the pPD-T group (p < 0.05), consistent with their elevation in established PD cases (Figure 5H; Table S33). Conversely, RALB, CFL1, SNCA, and PIP4K2A were significantly down-regulated in pPD-T (p < 0.05), aligning with their patterns in PD (Figure 5I; Table S33). These findings indicate that a subset of PD-specific-AI-EVDEPs undergo early alterations during the prodromal stage, especially in individuals who subsequently progress to PD, highlighting their potential utility as early biomarkers.

Early alterations of PD-specific age-independent EV lipidomic biomarkers in pPD

To investigate whether PD-specific AI-EVDELs exhibit early alterations during the prodromal stage, we performed EV lipidomic profiling on the same cohort of 19 prodromal individuals (9 pPD-T and 10 pPD-F). Quality control assessments showed that the median coefficient of variation for detected lipid species was <20% under both positive and negative ion modes (Figure S11A), indicating good technical reproducibility. PCA of the lipidomic profiles revealed substantial overlap between pPD-T and pPD-F groups (Figures S11B and S11C), suggesting limited global separation at the lipidome level. Consequently, we retained the original groupings for differential analysis. However, no lipid species demonstrated statistically significant differences between pPD-T and pPD-F groups that were consistent with the expression trends of the original PD-specific-AI-EVDELs (Tables S34 and S35).

Machine-learning-based identification of a novel diagnostic panel

To develop a diagnostic panel, we selected 6 robust AI-EVDEPs that demonstrated consistent performance across multiple validation layers, including (1) reproducible expression patterns in the pPD cohort, (2) exclusion from MSA-specific profiles to ensure disease specificity, and (3) confirmation by PRM-based targeted proteomic analysis (Figures 6A and 6B). Data from both the protein discovery cohort (112 individuals) and validation cohort (63 individuals) were randomly split: each cohort into an 8:2 ratio into training and test sets. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied, using the mean squared error criterion to determine the penalty regularization parameter (λ) (Figure S12). Subsequently, we combined the training and test sets to evaluate the overall model performance. Classified accuracy was assessed by comparing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the ROC curves (AUCs) for each model, providing a robust measure of diagnostic performance.

The results showed that models incorporating both up-regulated and down-regulated AI-EVDEPs identified two key biomarker combinations for distinguishing PDs from both ACs and YCs: one consisting of APOE, APOD, and HBA1/HBA2, which achieved an AUC of 0.684, and another using APOE alone, which achieved a higher AUC of 0.721 (Figure 6C). When using only up-regulated AI-EVDEPs, a combination of APOE and APOD was identified, yielding an AUC of 0.700 (Figure 6D). Next, we developed separate models to distinguish PDs from ACs and YCs: for PDs vs. ACs, the model identified a combination of APOE and APOD, achieving an AUC of 0.710 (Figure 6E). For PDs vs. YCs, the model selected APOE, APOD, and KLKB1 as key variables, yielding an AUC of 0.719 (Figure 6F). These findings underscore the potential of AI-EVDEPs in uncovering biological mechanisms and facilitating early and more precise prevention strategies for PD.

Discussion

PD is a global neurodegenerative disease for which early, precise, and non-invasive detection remains a significant challenge. Given the ability to cross the BBB, EVs and their content offer promising biomarkers for early PD diagnosis. In our study, we comprehensively profiled the proteomic and lipidomic signatures of circulating EVs, revealing distinct molecular alterations associated with PD. Notably, we observed concordant expression of these EV biomarkers between plasma and CSF, suggesting their relevance across biofluids and potential translational utility.

We identified both age-independent and age-dependent EV biomarkers that can facilitate early detection of PD across distinct life stages. In particular, some of these biomarkers demonstrated predictive value in individuals at the prodromal stage of PD, with altered expression patterns preceding clinical diagnosis. The inclusion of pPD individuals—representing a stage where pathophysiological processes precede motor symptom onset—enhances the translational relevance of this study.31 By incorporating pPDs, we were able to investigate EV biomarkers in a preclinical context, evaluating their potential as longitudinal biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification of PD. Moreover, comparative analysis with the MSA cohort enabled the delineation of PD-specific EV biomarkers, thereby improving diagnostic resolution among synucleinopathies. Clinically, distinguishing PD from several diseases with overlapping symptoms, particularly MSA, can be challenging, especially in the early stages.32,33 The identification of distinct EV-derived proteomic and lipidomic signatures between PD and MSA suggests underlying disease-specific molecular trajectories. In particular, the differential enrichment of EV lipoprotein species in PD-specific EVs may reflect its unique pathology through lipid metabolism.34 These findings not only support the utility of EVs in refining early diagnostic discrimination between PD and MSA but also highlight their potential to capture mechanistic divergences that could inform future stratified therapeutic strategies.

Beyond individual molecular alterations, we emphasized the interactions between EV proteins and lipids, suggesting that such crosstalk may play an essential role in PD pathogenesis. In particular, our findings highlight lipids not merely as structural vesicle components but as dynamic mediators that may modulate protein function remodeling within the neurodegenerative environment of PD.

Diagnostic specificity through comparative and age-stratified analyses

Given the complexity of aging and neurodegenerative diseases, we propose that single-analyte or single-omics approaches are unlikely to yield robust diagnostic or therapeutic biomarkers.35 Instead, our study supports the application of multi-omics frameworks to capture the complex molecular landscape of PD. Within this context, our identified AI-EVDEPs and AI-EVDELs emerged as robust biomarkers capable of distinguishing PD cases from both young and aged control groups. These EV biomarkers reflect stable and disease-relevant proteomic and lipidomic alterations, demonstrating both diagnostic utility and predictive potential across diverse age stages. Notably, aberrant expression patterns in these EV signatures may serve as early indicators of increased PD risk, offering a foundation for future risk stratification and early intervention strategies.

To further enhance clinical utility, we integrated data from both the discovery and PRM validation cohorts and applied least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) to derive an optimized diagnostic panel of key AI-EVDEPs. This panel demonstrated high classification accuracy in differentiating PD from controls. Among all biomarkers, APOE and APOD consistently demonstrated significant classification power across all models, underscoring their potential as pivotal biomarkers for PD.

In the study by Hällqvist et al.,36 the both PD and the iRBD groups exhibited up-regulated expression of serine protease inhibitors SERPINA3, SERPINF2, and SERPING1, as well as the central complement protein C3. In this study, we also observed significantly up-regulated expression of plasma SERPINA3, SERPING1, SERPINA5, as well as complement components C3, C7, and others in the PD group. This suggests that certain biological characteristics are shared between plasma total- and EV-specific proteins, highlighting the replication of our study to Hällqvist et al.

For AD-EVDEPs, we noted distinct expression patterns that varied with age and PD onset. Notably, EV proteins such as CCT8, which typically decrease with aging, showed an anomalous increase in PDs. These findings suggest a need to prioritize prevention strategies tailored to age-specific molecular profiles.

Interpretation of EV protein-lipid interactions

In our analyses, APOE consistently demonstrated robust performance across both the discovery and validation cohorts, highlighting its potential as a reliable EV-based biomarker with functional relevance through its interactions with EV-associated lipids. Notably, APOE showed significant positive associations with triglycerides (TGs) such as TG(18:1_12:0_12:0).1 and TG(52:1), suggesting a potential role of apolipoproteins, including APOE and APOD, in lipid transport and homeostasis in the CNS, which has also been implicated in α-synuclein uptake and spreading.37,38 Perturbations in lipidostasis, including triglyceride storage in lipid droplets and cholesterol-dependent membrane organization, are thought to influence α-synuclein aggregation and toxicity, highlighting a potential link between apolipoprotein-lipid interactions and PD-related pathology.39,40,41

Extending our protein-lipid association analysis to other lipid classes, CL(80:6) showed positive correlations with several EV proteins, including IFT140, CERKL, and APOH. These associations may reflect subtle interactions between AI-EVDEPs and AI-EVDELs, potentially involving impaired neurogenesis and neuronal dysfunction.42,43,44 Given that CL is low abundance, its biological significance requires further study.

Lipid-mediated mechanisms linking EV proteins to PD pathology

To further explore and validate the internal interaction mechanisms linking EVDEPs and EVDELs in PD pathology, we performed mediation analysis to assess whether EVDELs mediated the association between key EVDEPs and PD outcome. Several EVDELs, including TG, DG, CL, PS, sphingosine (SPH), and monolysocardiolipin (MLCL), were identified as full or partial mediators in these associations (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that EVDEPs may influence PD progression through lipid modulation, with the nature and extent of this mediation varying across different lipid species.

Results revealed full mediation models for the relationship between APOE to PD through specific lipid intermediaries. Specifically, TG(18:1_12:0_12:0).1 and TG(52:1) fully mediated the association between APOE and PD (Figures 6G and 6H). These results indicate that APOE may exert indirect effects on PD outcome by modulating the levels of specific TGs.

We also identified 5 TGs, 1 DG, 1 PS, 1 CL, 1 MLCL, and 1 SPH that partially mediated the relationship between APOH and PD (Figure S13), suggesting that APOH also influences PD indirectly through lipid dysregulation. This dual mechanism underscores the complex interplay between EV protein and lipid biomarkers in PD progression. Beyond APOH-mediated effects, our analysis uncovered additional EV protein-lipid interactions in mediating disease-associated pathways of PD, which further illuminate the molecular complexity of PD pathogenesis. Specifically, CERKL was found to fully mediate the effect of CL(80:6) on PD (Figure 6K), in line with the partial mediation of APOH on CL(80:6), suggesting a potential role of EV protein-CL interactions in PD. Furthermore, LALBA, F11, and SORT1 fully mediated SPH(d14:0).1 on PD (Figures 6I, 6J, and 6L).

These findings not only validate the significant associations between EVDEPs and EVDELs observed in previous analyses but also highlight the potential mechanistic pathways through which EV lipids mediate protein effects in PD. This suggests that lipid metabolism disruptions may play a critical role in PD pathogenesis, offering new insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Emerging PD biomarkers and implications

While our study focused on the identification and validation of EV-associated biomarkers in plasma and CSF, we recognize that the current landscape of PD research is rapidly evolving, with the emergence of promising biomarkers, such as α-synuclein SAA. Nevertheless, the current technical issues, including sample collection, inter-laboratory variability, and uncertain disease specificity across synucleinopathies, limit its clinical application.45

This study employs a multi-omics EV profiling strategy that captures a broader range of pathophysiological signals, providing complementary insights into biological changes in PD. These findings underscore the importance of expanding biomarker discovery efforts to encompass molecular features beyond α-synuclein (SNCA) that have been studied.46 Moreover, it remains conceivable that disease-relevant α-synuclein species, particularly those with pathological conformations, are not sufficiently resolved by conventional mass spectrometry approaches.47,48 This may partially account for its limited discriminatory performance in our dataset. Future investigations employing conformation-sensitive or amplification-based techniques may provide more insights into the diagnostic potential of EV-associated α-synuclein.

One of the key strengths of this study is the use of a novel affinity-based method for isolating EVs. The method facilitates the rapid and efficient isolation of EVs from relatively small sample volumes, enabling high-throughput measurement of EV molecules. Moreover, it outperforms traditional ultra-centrifugation methods by significantly reducing contamination from albumin and apolipoprotein in plasma.25

Limitations of the study

While this study provides valuable insights into aging and neurodegenerative disease progression, several limitations should be acknowledged, particularly in terms of clinical relevance and future applications. Despite the use of PRM for validation of the identified EV proteins, the efficiency and consistency of PRM for PD detection require further validation. Additionally, it would be beneficial to perform cross-validation in larger, multicenter cohorts to increase statistical power, particularly for the establishment of the diagnostic model in clinical settings. Importantly, the current findings remain at the discovery and early validation stage, and their clinical relevance—such as predictive value for intervention, scalability to routine testing, and robustness in diverse patient populations—remains to be determined.

Although we identified potential EV protein and lipid biomarkers, their roles in PD remain to be clarified through mechanistic studies. Low-abundance lipids such as CL require more rigorous validation, as their significance may be affected by fluctuations in more abundant lipids. Future work with larger cohorts and deeper profiling is needed, and it should be acknowledged that some low-abundance molecules may have escaped detection in the current datasets.

Finally, although the identified molecules in this study are likely to be biologically associated with EV-like particles, their exact sub-vesicular localization—whether as internal cargo, surface-bound corona,49 or co-isolated structures (e.g., exomeres50)—remains uncertain. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to delineate these possibilities.

Overall, this study highlights the potential of EV-based multi-omics, combined with multi-biofluid samples, an across-age design, comparison of multiple disease states, and machine learning, to significantly enhance the non-invasive early differential diagnosis of PD. By providing deeper insights into the biological mechanisms driving PD development and progression, these findings pave the way for future applications in identifying individuals at risk across different age stages. With further validation, this approach could enable more precise stratification for tailored prevention strategies, ultimately improving early detection and intervention in PD.

Participants’ recruitment and sample collection

Our study was organized around the illustrative role of plasma EV biomarker molecules in revealing the mechanisms of PD development, and the overall design flow is shown in Figure 1. In the plasma EVs discovery cohort, we recruited 131 participants, including PD cases (n = 40), aged controls (ACs group, n = 31), and young controls (YCs group, n = 60). We then recruited 5 additional PD cases with plasma and CSF pairwise samples, and 63 additional participants with 33 PD cases and 30 controls split into ACs (n = 15) and YCs (n = 15), as the validation cohort. Moreover, to enhance the specificity of identified EV biomarkers and their translational utility for longitudinal prediction of PD occurrence, we additionally recruited 10 MSA cases and 19 pPD cases, respectively. All enrolled PD cases were diagnosed as clinically established PD according to the 2015 diagnostic criteria established by the Movement Disorder Society,60 with assessment on family history, physical examination, and imaging evaluation. The brain MRI scans were carefully reviewed to exclude any structural abnormalities, such as significant atrophy of cortical, cerebellar, or other pathological features indicative of non-PD conditions. To minimize potential confounding from young-onset and monogenic PD, our study exclusively enrolled sporadic PD cases over the age of 50.

All the MSA cases were diagnosed with clinically established MSA or clinically probable MSA according to the 2022 criteria of the Movement Disorder Society (MDS).33 In addition to the clinical assessment, all MSA cases also underwent an MRI scan of the brain to assist in diagnosis. Neuroimaging results were reviewed independently to ensure accurate diagnosis and exclude other neurodegenerative diseases. A standardized procedure was used to identify prodromal Parkinson’s disease (pPD) based on the MDS-pPD research criteria. Demographic information and PD-related risk factors were collected through structured interviews. Assessments included motor and non-motor symptoms, olfactory testing, and RBD screening. Posterior probability was calculated, and individuals with a probability ≥80% were classified as pPD.61,62 Longitudinal follow-up data were obtained retrospectively for all pPD cases from enrollment to the time of analysis.

The healthy controls were recruited primarily from physical examination centers, who reported no symptoms associated with PD and were clinically evaluated by two experienced neurologists, without comorbidities such as neurodegenerative diseases, stroke, psychiatric disorders, or major systemic diseases (e.g., malignancy or chronic kidney disease).

We collected plasma samples (10 mL) when participants were officially enrolled in the study and stored them in sterilized containers before transferring them to a −80°C facility within 2 h of collection. All samples were processed separately according to the statistical principles of the experimental design, including replication, randomization, and appropriate blocking. In addition, we collected 5 additional CSF samples for CSF traceability according to the protocols mentioned above.

Clinical information assessment

The clinical information and medical scales were assessed by trained doctors, including demographics, PD course, PD Hoehn-Yahr (H-Y)63 staging, Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III (UPDRS-III),64,65 Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQ),66 Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE),67 Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA),68 Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD),69 Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ),70 Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale (PDSS),71 Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS),72 Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-8 (PDQ-8),73 Constipation Scoring System (CSS).74

Method details

Plasma EV isolation

The EVlent beads were provided by EVLiXiR and were used as described previously.25 EVlent (Extracellular Vesicles isoLated Efficiently, Naturally, and Totally), as functional affinity magnetic beads, enables the rapid and efficient isolation of EVs from plasma samples. The plasma EV affinity extraction kit (EVLiXiR#EV02-05-01) was used to extract EVs as follows: 1) First, plasma samples were centrifuged (2,500 g, 10 min) to take the supernatant; 2) Take 200 μL of plasma for each sample, add 800 μL of pre-cooled Incubation buffer Ⅰ and mix at room temperature; 3) Add 40 μL of EVlent magnetic beads to each sample and incubate for 2–3 h at room temperature with shaking; 4) Use a magnetic rack to magnetically absorb for 3 min, separate and discard the supernatant, then add 1 mL of Incubation buffer Ⅱ, shake up and down for 20 times, and magnetically absorb to separate and discard the supernatant; 5) Add 1 mL of Washing Buffer, shake up and down for 20 times, separate and discard the supernatant by magnetic suction for 3 min.

EV characterization

EVs were characterized by utilizing western blotting, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The morphology of EVs was observed by TEM (HITACHI, Japan). Briefly, the EVs were fixed with 1% glutaraldehyde, and a volume of 5–10 μL was added to the copper grid. Subsequently, the sample was stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid solution (pH 6.5) at room temperature for 2 min. Finally, TEM was used to capture the image. Nanocoulter instrument (RESUNTECH, China) was employed for NTA detection. As positive markers for EVs, CD9, TSG101, HSP70, and the negative marker Calnexin were detected by western blotting (WB).

EV proteomics measurement

EV protein extraction and digestion

The extracted EVs were lysed with a suitable EV lysis solution (EVLiXiR#EV02-08-01) by incubating for 10 min at 95°C. Lys-C enzyme (EVLiXiR#EV01-02) was added at a ratio of 1:100 (enzyme: sample) and then incubated at 37°C for 3 h. Trypsin (EVLiXiR#EV01-01-02) was then added at a ratio of 1:50 (enzyme: sample) and incubated overnight in a 37°C water bath. This was followed by desalting using a desalting column, and the eluted samples were freeze-dried in a refrigerated-vacuum centrifuge and stored at −80°C for later use.

Mass spectrometry acquisition of proteomics

The lyophilized sample was reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid, and 500 ng of peptide was taken for chromatographic separation using a nanoliter flow rate Easy-nLC 1200 chromatography system. Configure the buffer: liquid A (0.1% formic acid aqueous solution), liquid B (80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid). After peptide separation, the peptides were analyzed by DIA mass spectrometry on a QE HF-X mass spectrometer. The analysis time was 60 min. Detection mode: MS1 scan range: 400–1,200 m/z, resolution: 60,000, AGC target: 1e6, Maximum IT: 60 ms; MS2: resolution: 15,000, AGC target: 1e6, Maximum IT: 20 ms. The samples were lyophilized and concentrated, and the lysate was added, and the proteins were quantified at 95°C and boiled for 10 min; 1 μL of each sample was taken and quantified by NanoDrop. The MS data were analyzed using the Direct-DIA analysis function of Spectronaut 18.0 (Biognosys, Schlieren, Switzerland), using the human source database downloaded from the Uniprot website (https://www.uniprot.org) as the database for Direct-DIA analysis.

EV lipidomics measurement

EV sample preparation for lipidomics measurement

The preprocessing for EV lipidomics measurement from plasma samples is as follows: (1) Remove the sample from the −80°C freezer and place it on prepared dry ice. Weigh 30 mg of the sample, add 300 μL of MeOH and 2 zirconia beads, and homogenize the sample using a tissue grinder. (2) Add 1000 μL of MTBE, vortex for 2 min (4°C, 2,000 rpm), then incubate at 4°C for 30 min. (3) Add 300 μL of water, vortex for 2 min (4°C, 2,000 rpm), then incubate at 4°C for 10 min. (4) Centrifuge the sample at 4°C, 12,000 rpm for 15 min. Transfer equal volumes of the supernatant into EP tubes and dry under vacuum. (5) Add 100 μL of the solution (IPA: ACN = 9:1) for re-dissolution, vortex for 30 s, and sonicate in an ice-water bath for 5 min. (6) Centrifuge the sample at 4°C, 12,000 rpm for 10 min. (7) Take 20 μL of the supernatant and transfer it to a sample vial for analysis. (8) Take 10 μL of the supernatant from each sample and combine them to create a QC sample for analysis.

Mass spectrometry acquisition of lipidomics

The samples were placed in an autosampler at 8°C throughout the analysis. The samples were separated using an ACQUITY Premier CSH C18 column in an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system. The injection volume was 2 μL, the column temperature was set to 50°C, and the flow rate was 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phases used for chromatography were: A: ACN/H2O (10 mM ammonium formate) = 3:2, and B: IPA/ACN (10 mM ammonium formate) = 9:1. The gradient elution program was as follows: (i) Negative ion mode: 0–3.5 min, B was linearly increased from 40% to 43%; 3.5–4 min, B was linearly increased from 43% to 50%; 4–7 min, B was linearly increased from 50% to 60%; 7–13 min, B was linearly increased from 60% to 75%; 13–17 min, B was linearly increased from 75% to 99%; 17–19 min, B was maintained at 99%; 19–19.5 min, B was linearly decreased from 99% to 40%; 19.5–24 min, B was maintained at 40%. (ii) Positive ion mode: 0–3.5 min, B was linearly increased from 40% to 43%; 3.5–4 min, B was linearly increased from 43% to 50%; 4–7 min, B was linearly increased from 50% to 60%; 7–13 min, B was linearly increased from 60% to 75%; 13–17 min, B was linearly increased from 75% to 99%; 17–19 min, B was maintained at 99%; 19–19.5 min, B was linearly decreased from 99% to 40%; 19.5–24 min, B was maintained at 40%. QC samples were inserted into the sample queue to monitor and assess the stability of the system and the reliability of the experimental data.

Each sample was analyzed by electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes. After separation by UHPLC, the samples were analyzed by mass spectrometry using a Thermo QE HF-X mass spectrometer. The mass spectrometry conditions were as follows: ionization source: ESI ion source; sheath gas flow rate: 30; auxiliary gas: 10; spray voltage: 2.5 kV (+)/2.5 kV (−); S-Lens RF: 50; capillary temperature: 325°C; auxiliary gas temperature: 300°C; second-stage collision energy (NCE): 30; isolation window: 1.5 m/z, Top N = 10. Scanning range: 200–1800 m/z, scanning mode: positive and negative ions scanned separately. Analysis software: LipidSearch version 4.2.

CSF EV isolation and proteomic and lipidomic analysis

All frozen samples were thawed and centrifuged at 2,500g for 10 min to remove cell debris, apoptotic bodies, and other large particles. The supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C until further use. The isolation of CSF EVs and subsequent proteomic and lipidomic analyses were conducted following the methods previously described.

Expression analysis of EV proteomic biomarkers in targeted PRM validation

We recruited an additional 63 participants with plasma samples for validation based on the same diagnostic procedure described above, who were classified as PD cases (n = 33) and controls stratified for age (i.e., ACs = 15, and YCs = 15). Then we validated the identified EVDEPs by using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM), in which the procedure was constructed as following steps.

PRM analysis

For each sample, 500 ng of peptides were separated using an Easy-nLC 1200 chromatography system (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with nanoliter flow rates. The mobile phases were prepared as follows: Solution A (0.1% formic acid in water) and Solution B (80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid). Peptide separation was followed by PRM mass spectrometry analysis using a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The analysis time was 30 min with a resolution of 120,000, AGC target of 3e6, and a maximum IT of 100 ms. The peptide list included those manually selected and compared to PeptideAtlas30,75 (Table S36). PRM data were manually curated using Skyline (64-bit) version 25.1.0.142.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Proteomics analysis

PPI networks were obtained using STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/), and proteins were sub-clustered by finding a defined number of clusters based on their centroids (k-means clustering).28 We used CytoNCA to compute betweenness centrality (BC), which assesses protein node importance by measuring the fraction of shortest network paths traversing each node. We performed functional enrichment analysis using the ‘ClusterProfiler’ R package (v 4.10.1), applying the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) to interpret the biological functions of identified EVDEPs with significant differences across the three groups (FDR-p < 0.05) through pathway enrichment.

Lipidomics analysis

We used Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) to classify lipid molecules and calculated Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) scores to evaluate the contribution of each EV lipid to the PLS-DA model.

WGCNA

We performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using the R package ‘WGCNA’, applying dissTOM (1−TOM) as the distance metric. EV protein features were hierarchically clustered, resulting in 8 distinct modules. We analyzed the associations of extracted module-associated EV proteins with PD-associated clinical traits, respectively. Again, we applied WGCNA to the EV lipidome dataset using dissTOM as the distance metric, yielding 6 modules. We then analyzed the associations of extracted module-associated EV lipids with PD-associated clinical traits. Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) was applied for multiple comparison correction.

Biomolecular correlation analysis

Associations between EV protein and lipid molecules were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. Data visualization was performed using the R package ‘ggplot2’ (v 3.5.1).

Mediation analysis

We performed mediation analysis in a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework to identify if the significant associations between EVDEPs and PD outcome were mediated by EVDELs. We tested the mediation of each EV biomarker variable with the causal chain: EVDEPs to EVDELs to PD outcome. We performed bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations to estimate the indirect effects, generating an empirical sampling distribution, which was subsequently used to derive confidence intervals (CIs) for these effects. To quantify the magnitude of mediation, we estimated the proportion of the association mediated by EVDELs (indirect effect/[direct effect + indirect effect]) if the indirect effect is significant. All p values were 2-tailed, and values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Mediation analyses were conducted using the “lavaan” package (v 0.6–18), using bootstrapping to calculate the standard errors in R.

Classification modeling analysis

To identify the best overall set of predictors for PD, we performed the LASSO regression analysis. LASSO shrinks coefficients for weaker predictors toward zero, with the degree of shrinkage determined by an optimal parameter λ. We calculated optimal λ, a penalty factor for penalized maximum likelihood estimation, using 10-fold cross-validation at its minimum level. We split the discovery and validation cohorts into 80% training and 20% testing subsets, which we then combined to form the overall training and testing datasets. Statistical analyses were performed in R (v 4.3.1) using the ‘glmnet’ package (v 4.1–8) for LASSO selection. We calculated a final summary model using the full cohort population. We assessed the performance of the predictive models by plotting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using the ‘pROC’ R package (v 1.18.5).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses

Prior to comparing group differences, the proportion of missing values was estimated, and partial imputation was performed using the KNN imputation function from the ‘impute’ package version 1.76.0. Statistical analysis was conducted using R software version 4.3.1. Between-group comparisons were analyzed based on the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) correction.
