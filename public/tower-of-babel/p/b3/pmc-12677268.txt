Wind Instrument Training and Sound-Based Interventions for Sleep Quality and Obstructive Sleep Apnoea: A Narrative Synthesis
Authors: Yi Ding, KaiLi Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Tolerability issues with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy for obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) have spurred interest in nonpharmacological alternatives like sound-based interventions (wind instrument training, singing and music therapy) for improving upper airway function and sleep quality.Methods:A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (through March 2024) identified studies evaluating the effects of wind instrument training, vocal interventions (singing or speech therapy) or music therapy on OSA-related outcomes and sleep quality. Eight studies, specifically, four randomised controlled trials, one before–after quasi-experimental study and three cross-sectional observational studies, were included.Results:Wind instrument training, particularly that with high-resistance instruments, showed moderate effectiveness in reducing OSA severity and improving sleep outcomes, although no significant improvements in pulmonary function were reported. Music-based interventions (e.g., passive bedtime listening) had mixed effects on sleep quality. Some evidence indicated that structured vocal exercises (singing or speech therapy) can reduce snoring and enhance outcomes when combined with CPAP, but results varied across studies. Overall, sound-based interventions demonstrated potential benefits for OSA, although heterogeneity amongst studies was high.Conclusions:This review highlights sound-based interventions as a unique and promising nonpharmacological treatment for OSA. In contrast to CPAP, which has a mechanical approach, these strategies aim to improve airway patency via upper airway muscle training and may serve as alternatives or adjuncts for patients intolerant of CPAP. Although moderate evidence supports their ability to improve sleep quality and OSA symptoms, outcomes remain inconsistent. The noninvasive, low-cost nature of sound-based interventions underscores substantial clinical potential. Large multicentre trials with long follow-ups and direct comparisons with CPAP are warranted to validate efficacy and clarify underlying mechanisms.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process that is essential for cognitive, metabolic and immune regulation. However, sleep disturbances, including insomnia, primary snoring and obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), are increasingly prevalent across populations and are associated with heightened cardiovascular risk, impaired daytime function and diminished quality of life.[12] Nonpharmacological interventions for improving sleep, especially in individuals seeking alternatives to conventional treatments, such as continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or sedative medications, have received growing attention.[345]

In recent years, sound-based interventions, including music therapy, structured singing and breathing techniques inherent to wind instrument practice, have been examined as low-cost, noninvasive strategies to improve sleep quality and reduce OSA-related symptoms.[67] These interventions are best regarded as acting primarily via oropharyngeal/respiratory muscle conditioning, which may enhance upper airway stability through repeated phonatory and breath control tasks.

However, the putative effects of these interventions on parasympathetic tone and respiratory pattern were not measured in previous trials; they were instead presented only as hypotheses to be tested in future mechanistic studies.[8910] Nonetheless, preliminary studies across diverse modalities − including wind instrument practice, speech therapy exercises and relaxing music listening − provide initial support for the hypothesis that such interventions may enhance autonomic regulation and influence breathing patterns. To explore possible mechanisms, we propose two plausible mechanistic pathways by which these interventions could exert these effects: (i) oropharyngeal/respiratory muscle conditioning; and (ii) autonomic down-regulation induced by auditory stimuli or paced breathing exercises. Mindfulness-only programs (with no vocal or auditory component) are beyond the scope of this review and were excluded by design.

Wind instrument training, such as didgeridoo and brass playing, has been hypothesised to reduce airway collapsibility by enhancing peripharyngeal muscle tone. However, epidemiological findings remain inconsistent; for instance, some studies reported no significant association between wind instrument playing and reduced OSA risk. These discrepancies highlight the need for further controlled trials to clarify causal effects.[1112] Similarly, vocal exercise interventions, such as singing training or speech therapy, and receptive music-based therapies have been hypothesised to improve sleep initiation and continuity by modulating cortical arousal and autonomic function. Preliminary studies across diverse modalities, including wind instrument practice, speech therapy exercises, singing exercises and relaxing music listening, provide initial support for this concept.[13141516] A growing body of empirical studies, ranging from randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to observational cohorts, has explored these approaches in diverse populations. However, substantial methodological heterogeneity across study designs, intervention protocols, target populations and outcome measures limits the direct comparability of findings across studies.

Our systematic review seeks to provide a comprehensive summary of the available evidence concerning wind instrument-, vocal- and music-based interventions for improving sleep-related outcomes. It also aims to highlight research gaps and methodological limitations to inform future clinical trials and nonpharmacological approaches for sleep health.

METHODS

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic literature search to identify peer-reviewed studies that investigated the effects of wind instrument training or sound-based interventions (e.g., vocal training or receptive music therapy) on sleep quality or OSA-related outcomes. The search covered major biomedical databases, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), from database inception to March 2024. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings included combinations of terms, such as ‘wind instrument’, ‘didgeridoo’, ‘singing’, ‘vocal training’, ‘music therapy’, ‘sleep quality’, ‘insomnia’, ‘snoring’, ‘sleep-disordered breathing’ and ‘OSA’. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to expand or narrow the search as appropriate. Boolean operators were applied as follows: (‘wind instrument’ OR ‘didgeridoo’ OR ‘brass instrument’ OR ‘vocal training’ OR ‘singing’ OR ‘oropharyngeal exercise’ OR ‘music therapy’ OR ‘receptive music therapy’) AND (‘sleep quality’ OR ‘insomnia’ OR ‘snoring’ OR ‘sleep-disordered breathing’ OR ‘OSA’ OR ‘OSA’). The reference lists of included studies and related reviews were manually screened to capture additional relevant publications not indexed or missed during database searching. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, then reviewed the full texts of potentially eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Risk of bias for RCTs was assessed by using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool. For nonrandomised studies, including quasi-experimental and cross-sectional designs, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was applied to evaluate methodological quality and potential bias.

Study Designs and Rationale

We included RCTs, quasi-experimental before–after studies and cross-sectional observational studies. Quasi-experimental designs were retained to capture real-world feasibility, acceptability and implementation signals where randomisation is impractical. Observational studies were included to describe exposure–outcome patterns (e.g., habitual wind instrument use) that inform clinical practicality.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria:

Population: Human participants aged ≥18 years with self-reported sleep complaints, primary snoring, diagnosed with OSA or no known sleep disorder but assessed for sleep-related outcomes.

Intervention: Use of wind instrument training (e.g., didgeridoo, brass or double reed instruments), structured vocal sound interventions (e.g., singing or vocal exercises) or passive listening to music or audio content at bedtime.

Comparator: Studies included either a noninterventional group, usual care, sham intervention (e.g., audiobook) or active control when applicable.

Outcome Measures: Quantitative data on sleep quality (e.g., the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [PSQI], Epworth Sleepiness Scale [ESS] and sleep diaries) or apnoea-related outcomes (e.g., the apnoea–hypopnea index [AHI], Berlin Questionnaire and snoring frequency) were required for inclusion. Studies needed to report outcome measures allowing extraction or summaries of sleep-related improvements or OSA severity.

Study design: RCTs, quasi-experimental studies, before–after designs, or cross-sectional studies that provided comparative sleep outcome data.

We excluded articles if they met any of the following: (1) qualitative study reports, case reports, reviews or editorials; (2) reported interventions not involving respiratory or sound-based modulation (e.g., mindfulness-only or cognitive behavioural therapy [CBT]; (3) reported animal studies; (4) had full text in non-English languages or (5) reported studies lacking quantitative data or relevant outcome reporting. Mindfulness-only or CBT programmes without auditory exposure or respiratory muscle components were excluded to maintain focus on sound- or breathing-based modalities.

Study Inclusion and Subgroup Extraction

Following screening, eight studies were deemed eligible and included in this review. These studies involved a total of 2438 participants from diverse geographic regions and age groups. Interventions varied across studies and were broadly categorised into two types: active respiratory muscle training (e.g., didgeridoo practice or vocal exercises) and passive sound-based exposure (e.g., music-assisted relaxation at bedtime).

For the purpose of narrative synthesis, included studies were grouped in accordance with intervention modality. Active interventions, such as wind instrument playing and structured vocal practices, involved the voluntary engagement of the upper airway or respiratory musculature. Passive interventions, such as recorded music or guided audio sessions, involved nonexertional exposure to auditory stimuli.

When multiple sleep-related outcomes were reported in a single study, the primary endpoint most directly reflecting the effect of the intervention, such as changes in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) scores, Berlin Questionnaire classification, or AHI, was summarised. In cases wherein event counts or exact values were unavailable, outcome trends and percentages were noted as described by the authors. No pooled statistical estimates were calculated. Some studies included in this review, such as the work of Majeed et al.,[16] employed non-validated scales, like the Groningen Sleep Quality Scale. While these scales can provide valuable insights, their use introduces potential reporting bias. We highlight this situation as a limitation in the discussion. Additionally, we reviewed the protocols of the included studies to check whether outcome measures were prespecified. We referred to the protocols when available to ensure that the outcomes were not selectively reported. However, in cases where such information was unavailable, we noted the potential for selective reporting bias.

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers by using a structured form, which included study characteristics (design, setting and sample size); participant information (age and clinical profile); intervention protocol (type, duration and frequency); outcome variables and relevant findings. Most studies did not report standardised effect sizes, such as relative risks or odds ratios. Instead, descriptive comparisons were conducted on the basis of the narrative summaries of the clinically relevant changes reported by each study. Authors were contacted when additional information was required, although some did not respond. Our systematic review does not involve new experiments or participant data collection. As such, ethical approval and participant consent were not required. The included studies were all published prior to this review, and ethical approval was obtained by the authors of these primary studies, as indicated in their respective publications.

Statistical Analysis

Owing to significant heterogeneity amongst the included studies, such as differences in study designs (e.g., RCTs vs. observational studies); intervention modalities (e.g., wind instrument training vs. passive music interventions); intervention duration and outcome measurement tools, we chose the narrative synthesis approach over a meta-analysis. We did not calculate pooled effect estimates and compute between-study heterogeneity statistics (e.g., I2 and τ2). Future trials should standardise protocols and include objective outcomes to enable pooling. Interventions should be standardised, and objective evaluation tools, such as polysomnography (PSG), should be employed in RCTs to enhance the reliability of future studies. Given the differences in study design and intervention modalities, future research could consider conducting subgroup analyses to compare the effects of active interventions (e.g., wind instrument training and vocal exercises) with passive interventions (e.g., bedtime music listening) to clarify which type of intervention is more effective than others. Our review did not formally assess publication bias through methods, such as funnel plots or Egger’s test. While we included all relevant studies available through comprehensive database searches, we acknowledge that the possibility of unpublished negative results cannot be ruled out because of the limited number of studies included in our review. For each study, outcome data related to sleep quality (e.g., PSQI); apnoea severity (e.g., AHI and Berlin Questionnaire); daytime sleepiness (e.g., the ESS and Stanford Sleepiness Scale [SSS] and snoring severity (e.g., frequency or patient-reported severity) were reviewed. The most clinically relevant endpoints were prioritised in each case on the basis of the authors’ reporting. When outcomes were reported categorically (e.g., proportion of participants achieving PSQI ≤ 5 or classified as high-risk on the Berlin Questionnaire), values were summarised descriptively as presented in the original articles. No pooled effect estimates or conversions (e.g., risk ratios or standardised mean differences) were calculated. Metrics used to assess statistical heterogeneity, such as I2 or τ2, were not applicable in this review. All data were extracted and cross-checked independently by two reviewers by using a standardised extraction form to ensure clarity and consistency across the synthesised results.

RESULTS

Literature Search and Selection

A systematic search of four bibliographic databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science) and one trial registry (Cochrane CENTRAL) was conducted through March 2024 to identify relevant studies and yielded 1247 unique records. After the removal of duplicates (n = 416), a total of 831 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of the studies reported by these articles, 791 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria primarily because of their irrelevance to sleep-related outcomes and lack of acoustic or respiratory interventions or because they were nonhuman studies. Full-text assessment was conducted on 40 articles reporting potentially eligible studies. Following detailed full-text evaluation, 32 studies were excluded for the following reasons: insufficient outcome data (n = 12), lack of a control group (n = 6), qualitative study design (n = 5), duplicate population or overlapping datasets (n = 4) and inappropriate intervention form (e.g., studies where the intervention did not meet the predefined criteria for wind instrument training or music therapy [n = 5]. Ultimately, eight studies met all inclusion criteria and were retained for qualitative synthesis. Study selection is illustrated in Figure 1, which presents the PRISMA flow diagram.

Study Characteristics

The final set of eight articles included a cumulative sample size of 2438 participants, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 17 to 1105. These articles were published between 2000 and 2021 and represented multiple geographic regions, including Europe (n = 4), Asia (n = 2), North America (n = 1) and South America (n = 1). The final set comprised four RCTs, one before–after quasi-experimental trial and three cross-sectional observational studies.

Intervention modalities were categorised into two broad types, as follows:

Active acoustic interventions included wind instrument training (e.g., didgeridoo playing in Puhan et al.),[10] singing-based vocal training (Ojay and Ernst)[15] and speech therapy (Diaferia et al.).[14]

Passive acoustic interventions comprised bedtime music listening interventions (Harmat et al.[17]; Majeed et al.[16].

The remaining observational studies (Subramanian et al.[11]; Brown et al.[12]; Ward et al.[13] compared habitual wind instrument players with nonplayers in naturalistic settings without direct experimental manipulation and were analysed separately. These cross-sectional studies were included to explore exposure–outcome patterns but were not interpreted as causal evidence.

The study populations also varied. Some studies focused on young adults or student populations with subjective sleep disturbances (Harmat et al.[17]; Majeed et al.[16]; Ojay and Ernst[15], whereas others included professional musicians or community-based samples (Brown et al.[12]; Ward et al.[13]. Several studies specifically recruited participants diagnosed with OSA or habitual snoring (Puhan et al.[10]; Diaferia et al.[14].

Outcome measures included subjective sleep quality (e.g., PSQI), apnoea severity (e.g., AHI), daytime sleepiness (ESS and SSS), snoring frequency and OSA risk classification (e.g., Berlin Questionnaire). For each study, the most clinically relevant endpoints were extracted and summarised descriptively. No effect size conversion or quantitative synthesis was conducted. The assessment revealed considerable heterogeneity in methodological quality, particularly in terms of study design, intervention modalities and outcome reporting. Amongst the RCTs, most studies showed a low risk of bias in areas, such as randomisation and outcome measurement (e.g., Harmat et al.[17]; Majeed et al.[16]. However, some studies (e.g., Puhan et al.[10] exhibited high risk due to the lack of blinding and incomplete outcome reporting. The NOS scores of the observational studies (Subramanian et al.[11]; Brown et al.[12]; Ward et al.[13] were moderate (6–7/9), indicating acceptable methodological quality but with certain limitations in controlling for confounders or ensuring representativeness. Specifically, heterogeneity in study design arose from different data collection methods (cross-sectional vs. cohort-based), whereas intervention variability stemmed from differences in the nature of the interventions (e.g., active wind instrument training vs. receptive music therapy). Additionally, outcome measures varied significantly, with some studies relying on subjective self-reports (e.g., PSQI and ESS), whereas others incorporated objective measures, like the AHI or lung function tests. Despite these variations, no studies were deemed to be of poor quality. Six studies (e.g., Brown et al.[12]; Diaferia et al.[14]; Ojay and Ernst[15] achieved either full or near-full scores in at least two major quality domains, indicating methodological rigour. Given the heterogeneity across study designs, intervention modalities and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis approach was employed, and no quantitative pooling or effect size calculation was conducted. Measures of statistical heterogeneity (e.g., I2 and τ2) were therefore not applicable in our review. Although most studies demonstrated consistent directions of benefit, the risk of residual confounding and reporting bias, particularly in observational studies, must be considered when interpreting the results. This review did not apply funnel plots or Egger’s tests to evaluate publication bias. Consequently, the presence of unpublished negative studies cannot be excluded. Future systematic reviews should employ statistical methods to test for publication bias once a large body of research becomes available.

Results of the Included Studies

The included studies reported various measures of sleep improvement and OSA-related outcomes following sound-based and wind instrument interventions. Clinical improvements were reflected in indicators, such as reductions in PSQI scores, AHI, Berlin Questionnaire classification and daytime sleepiness scores (ESS and SSS). Several studies demonstrated favourable outcomes following passive acoustic interventions. For instance, Harmat et al.[17] reported that bedtime music listening normalised sleep in 86% of participants with poor sleep quality. Majeed et al.[16] also observed a marked improvement in PSQI scores after bedtime music exposure, supporting the feasibility of passive music-based therapies. Active interventions showed similarly positive findings. Puhan et al.[10] found that didgeridoo training improved AHI, ESS and partner-reported sleep disruption in patients with moderate OSA. Diaferia et al.[14] reported that speech therapy added to CPAP treatment led to further improvements in AHI and PSQI scores. Ojay and Ernst,[15] using singing-based vocal exercises, reported a reduction in snoring time over the course of the intervention. However, evidence from the small before–after quasi-experiment conducted by Ojay and Ernst[15] should be interpreted cautiously and not equated to RCT-level certainty. Observational studies suggested potential protective associations between habitual wind instrument use and OSA risk. Notably, observational studies, such as the work of Subramanian et al.,[11] were not designed to assess intervention efficacy and were interpreted cautiously. Brown et al.[12] and Ward et al.[13] reported similar trends, though outcome variability was noted across cohorts. In keeping with our narrative-only approach, we did not compute pooled effect sizes or risk reductions. Qualitatively, excluding studies at high risk of bias or nonrandomised designs did not change the overall direction of findings reported by the randomised trials. The results from the remaining studies were consistent with the original findings, suggesting that the overall conclusions of this review are not significantly influenced by these exclusions. Overall, while the strength of findings differed amongst studies, most investigations supported the beneficial role of acoustic or respiratory muscle interventions over the control or usual care. In consideration of the heterogeneity in study design, target populations, intervention type and outcome metrics, we summarised the findings descriptively through narrative synthesis rather than quantitative pooling.

Summary and Future Directions

This review summarises the available evidence on the role of sound-based interventions − vocal, instrumental and auditory − in improving sleep quality and alleviating symptoms associated with OSA. Across a range of study designs, populations and geographic settings, most interventions demonstrated favourable outcomes, indicating their potential as nonpharmacological adjuncts to conventional treatments.[16171819] However, heterogeneity across studies remains a major limitation and is attributable to differences in intervention protocols, exposure duration and outcome assessment methods.[20] Future research should aim to standardise intervention parameters; incorporate objective evaluation tools, such as PSG and assess the durability of intervention effects over time. Additionally, direct comparisons between active interventions (e.g., wind instrument training and vocal exercises) and passive approaches (e.g., music listening) would be valuable for determining optimal strategies tailored to specific patient populations or clinical settings.[212223] In conclusion, current evidence supports the clinical relevance of incorporating wind instrument and vocal-based strategies into broad sleep health programmes. Their accessibility, affordability and generally favourable safety profiles make them particularly suitable for use in community-based or resource-limited environments.[24]

DISCUSSION

Our systematic review synthesised evidence from eight studies evaluating the effect of wind instrument training, vocal exercises and other sound-based interventions on sleep quality and OSA-related outcomes. While the findings of our review suggest that wind instrument training and music therapy may offer promising interventions for improving sleep quality and OSA symptoms, the small sample sizes and observational designs of many studies limit the strength of the evidence. These factors suggest that the clinical relevance of the interventions remains inconclusive. Large, well-designed studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of these interventions and support their use in clinical practice. The findings provide moderate support for the clinical utility of these nonpharmacological approaches. Across most included studies, participants receiving acoustic-based interventions demonstrated improvements in various sleep-related indicators compared with controls. These findings align with a growing body of literature underscoring the physiological and psychological benefits of acoustic interventions, particularly in improving sleep structure and modulating upper airway function. The proposed mechanisms, such as enhanced parasympathetic tone, are based on plausible physiological pathways. However, direct evidence linking these pathways to the interventions discussed in our review is limited. Some studies, such as those on heart rate variability, provide indirect support for the role of autonomic regulation in music therapy. However, additional mechanistic research is needed to clarify how these interventions influence autonomic function. Future trials should incorporate mechanistic sub-studies to explore the effect of interventions like wind instrument training and music therapy on parasympathetic tone, respiratory control and other related physiological factors.

The interventions assessed in the reviewed studies ranged from receptive music therapy to active respiratory muscle engagement through vocal or wind-based activities. Despite differences in methodology and population characteristics, a majority of the studies reported clinically meaningful improvements in outcomes, such as sleep quality, snoring or daytime alertness. Passive interventions, like music therapy, may act primarily through relaxation and autonomic regulation. Music has been shown to reduce sympathetic nervous system activity, lowering heart rate and blood pressure and promoting parasympathetic tone, effects that may improve sleep quality. Passive interventions, like music therapy, may also reduce anxiety and stress, which are known to affect sleep quality negatively. However, the mechanisms of these interventions are likely less direct than the physical muscle training involved in active interventions. Two of the included studies were observational investigations of wind instrument players (Brown et al.[12]; Ward et al.[13], and one trial evaluated a speech-therapy-based oropharyngeal exercise (Diaferia et al.[14]. Although these therapies are not traditional ‘singing’ or ‘music listening’ interventions, we included them as active sound-based therapies targeting upper airway muscle function, an approach that is consistent with our broad inclusion of respiratory muscle training modalities. Active interventions, particularly those involving controlled breathing or vibratory airflow (e.g., didgeridoo practice), may contribute to enhanced pharyngeal muscle tone, which is relevant in reducing airway collapse amongst patients with OSA. Benefits were noted across various age groups and clinical profiles. Improvements were documented in young adults with subjective sleep disturbances, community-dwelling adults and patients with mild-to-moderate OSA. This review included several studies with small sample sizes, such as the study by Ojay and Ernst,[15] which had only 17 participants. Such an inclusion increases the risk of type II error (false negatives). Small samples can lead to a failure to detect true effects, particularly when investigating complex interventions, like sound-based therapies. While some studies, such as the work of Puhan et al.,[10] were adequately powered, many studies had limited sample sizes, which may affect the robustness of their findings. Additionally, the outcome measures used to assess the effectiveness of the interventions (e.g., the AHI, the PSQI and ESS) were not standardised across studies. This variation in measurement tools could contribute to the differences in the observed effects. For example, studies using subjective outcomes, such as the PSQI, may be more prone to bias than those including objective measures, such as the AHI. Therefore, while the trends observed in this review are promising, future studies with large sample sizes and adequate statistical power are needed to confirm the effects of these interventions on sleep outcomes and OSA. Notably, observational studies, such as that by Subramanian et al.,[11] are susceptible to residual confounding. Key factors, like the fitness levels of musicians compared with those of controls, as well as unmeasured lifestyle variables (e.g., smoking and Body mass index (BMI)), could influence the outcomes of sleep quality and apnoea severity. These confounders might lead to bias in interpreting the effects of wind instrument playing on sleep health. While some primary studies attempted to adjust for certain confounders, such as age and sleep disturbances, not all studies included comprehensive adjustments for potential confounders, like smoking and BMI. Future studies should ensure that such variables are systematically controlled to mitigate residual confounding and provide accurate estimates of intervention effects. Nonetheless, several methodological limitations should be considered. Heterogeneity across studies was substantial, including differences in study design (e.g., RCT vs. observational), intervention duration and frequency and outcome measurement tools. Many studies relied on subjective sleep scales (e.g., PSQI and ESS), which may introduce reporting bias. Objective sleep assessments, such as PSG or actigraphy, were infrequently used, limiting the precision of physiological outcome evaluation. Additionally, most studies evaluated only short-term outcomes, with follow-up typically limited to several weeks. The absence of blinding in behavioural and auditory interventions also raises concerns about potential placebo effects, although some studies mitigated this concern through the use of comparison arms, such as audiobooks or no-treatment controls. Long-term exposure to environmental noise is significantly associated with an increased risk of depression and anxiety symptoms in adults aged 35 years and older. However, the results should be interpreted with caution because some studies, particularly those with a high risk of bias (e.g., lack of blinding), may overestimate effects. Future studies should focus on multicentre RCTs with large sample sizes to improve the reliability and generalisability of results and thus enhance the evidence base for wind instrument training and music therapy in the management of OSA and sleep quality. These trials should include extended follow-up periods (6–12 months) to evaluate the sustainability of the interventions. Subjective and objective outcome measures (e.g., PSG and actigraphy) should be used to improve assessment accuracy and reduce self-report biases. Future studies should explore the effectiveness of noise reduction strategies in improving mental health outcomes, with a particular emphasis on conducting sensitivity analyses and improving study design rigour. Differences between wind instruments likely contribute to heterogeneity. High-resistance or sustained-pressure playing (didgeridoo, brass and double-reed) may impose considerable upper airway muscle loading, tongue/palatal recruitment and breath control demands (including circular breathing), plausibly enhancing pharyngeal dilator endurance. Moderate/low-resistance instruments (single reeds or flutes) may provide weak training stimuli. Future trials should prespecify instrument type, quantify practice dose and airway pressures and compare high-resistance instruments with low-resistance instruments head-to-head with objective outcomes (e.g., AHI and airway collapsibility indices) to clarify differential effects.

Despite the above limitations, our review has notable strengths, including a rigorous search strategy, clear inclusion criteria and systematic outcome data extraction. While heterogeneity precluded statistical pooling, the consistency of directionality in findings across diverse settings supports the clinical relevance of the interventions of interest. Structured acoustic-based strategies, particularly those involving music or vocal training, may serve as meaningful adjuncts for patients who are unwilling or unable to pursue pharmacological or device-based treatments, such as CPAP. Their simplicity, affordability and high acceptability make them especially suitable for use in community or primary care contexts. Future studies should aim to standardise intervention protocols, employ objective evaluation tools (such as PSG) and include long-term follow-up to mitigate the effects of heterogeneity. Furthermore, subgroup analyses comparing active and passive interventions would help clarify which types of interventions are effective in improving sleep quality and alleviating OSA symptoms. Comparative studies between different modalities (e.g., wind vs. vocal vs. passive music) would be valuable in determining population-specific applicability. Mechanistic studies examining neural and autonomic responses to these interventions may also enhance our understanding of their physiological pathways and therapeutic potential.
