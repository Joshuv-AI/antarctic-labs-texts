Complex Patient Scores Meaningfully Affect 2022 Medicare Merit-Based Incentive Payment System Bonus Payments for Orthopedic Surgeons
Authors: Shirley H Fung, Timothy E Stump, Adam Ciarleglio, Norman W Gill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Merit-Based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) affects Medicare reimbursements for over 600,000 clinicians, including ~ 15,000 orthopedic surgeons in the United States. MIPS policies are rapidly evolving, yet limited research exists to guide orthopedic surgeons in optimizing performance. This study evaluates whether recent MIPS policy changes have led to increased payment adjustments for orthopedic surgeons and examines the role of MIPS in enhancing care quality.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 2021–2022 MIPS performance data from nearly 9000 orthopedic surgeons assessed the impact of policy changes on payment adjustments using McNemar’s test and mixed effects logistic regression.ResultsRaising the MIPS performance threshold to 75 points significantly reduced the likelihood of receiving bonus payments (χ 2 = 803.21, degrees of freedom = 1; P < .01). Surgeons in smaller practices had 60% lower odds of earning bonus payments (odds ratio, 0.40; 95% confidence interval, 0.33–0.48; P < .008), though this disparity was smaller than previously reported. The updated complex patient bonus scoring policy showed the strongest positive effect on the likelihood of receiving bonus payments (odds ratio, 6.49; 95% confidence interval, 3.31–12.76; P < .017). Nonetheless, MIPS continues to fall short in encouraging the reporting of specialty-specific, outcome-based, and patient-experience measures.DiscussionRaising the MIPS performance threshold further may lead to greater dissatisfaction. Despite some improvements, equity gaps remain for small and rural practitioners. However, the revised complex patient bonus policy effectively rewards clinicians caring for high-risk populations.

Introduction

Under the Medicare Access and CHIP [Children’s Health Insurance Program] Reauthorization Act of 2015, the Quality Payment Program (QPP) was launched in 2017 to restructure Medicare’s clinician payment system to prioritize value over volume. 1 In QPP, there are 2 payment tracks: Advanced Alternative Payment Models and the Merit-Based Incentive Payment System (MIPS). 2 MIPS has consolidated 3 previous incentive payment programs and serves as the main quality payment program for clinicians in Medicare. 3 In 2022, over 620,000 clinicians were eligible to participate in MIPS. 4 MIPS includes 4 performance categories: quality, cost, improvement activities, and promoting interoperability. Its final score is a composite score derived from the weighted performance in these categories. Based on how the final score compares to the MIPS performance threshold (PT), clinicians receive positive, neutral, or negative payment adjustments 2 years later. 5 The statute has provided additional MIPS adjustment for exceptional performance from payment years 2019 through 2024, with the 2022 performance year/2024 payment year being the final year for providing additional adjustment for exceptional performance. 6

In 2022, ~ 15,000 MIPS-eligible orthopedic surgeons participated in the QPP. 7 Though clinicians are increasingly held accountable for quality and cost of care, research on how orthopedic surgeons perform in MIPS is limited. Moreover, MIPS policies are fast evolving in response to statutory requirements, ongoing criticism, and political pressure to repeal or scale back the program. 8–11 For instance, the MIPS PT has gradually increased since 2019, and starting in performance year 2022 (PY2022), the PT was raised from 60 to 75 points. 12 It is unknown whether this change would impact the percentage of surgeons receiving positive payment adjustments in PY2022. Additionally, MIPS regulations have been updated to improve equity for small practices and relieve their burden of participation. Specifically, beginning with PY2019, eligible clinicians in small practices who submit data to MIPS receive bonus points to boost their MIPS final scores. 13 Additionally, starting with PY2022, small practice clinicians receive automatic reweighting of the Promoting Interoperability category to 0%. 14 Furthermore, in PY2022, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) updated the scoring methodology for the complex patient bonus to include 2 distinct calculations: the average Hierarchical Condition Category (HCC) risk score of a clinician’s Medicare patient population and the percentage who are dually eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid. 15,16 To earn bonus points, clinicians must have at least 1 risk factor (average HCC risk score or dual eligibility ratio) at or above the previous year’s median. 16,17 This policy change was prompted by an Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation Report to Congress on Social Risk and Medicare’s Value-Based Purchasing Program. 18 Last, there was public interest in knowing who received sizable bonuses in PY2022 because that was the first year during which clinicians who performed well actually obtained a maximum of 8.26% payment adjustments. 19 Fast-evolving policies continue to widen the current knowledge gap in how orthopedic surgeons perform under MIPS.

Hence, the authors aimed to answer 3 key questions in this study. 1) After CMS raised the PT to 75 points in PY2022, among the surgeons who received payment adjustments in both PY2021 and PY2022, is the proportion of surgeons who earned incentives in PY2021 different from PY2022? 2) Which practice and clinician characteristics, including practice size and complex patient bonus, are associated with receiving positive payment adjustments in PY2022? 3) Do surgeons in the top 20th percentile for payment adjustments in PY2022 have different practice characteristics, and which quality measures did they frequently report?

To the authors’ knowledge, this study provides the first empirical assessment of how raising the MIPS PT to 75 points affects orthopedic surgeons’ MIPS payment adjustments in PY2022. Additionally, this study is the first to evaluate whether policies designed to provide relief to small practices have been effective in reducing disparities. It also examines whether the new complex patient bonus policy adequately rewards surgeons who treat a higher proportion of complex patients, and this evaluation is a crucial indicator of whether the complex patient bonus score could effectively compensate for additional challenges involved in treating more socially and medically complex patients.

Study Data and Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study was conducted using several publicly available US databases, including the 2021 QPP Public Use File (QPP PUF), 20 2022 QPP PUF, 21 and 2022 Doctors and Clinicians National Downloadable File. 22 The George Washington University institutional review board determined the study was not human participant research and, therefore, exempted it from further review. All CMS data were publicly available and de-identified before analysis; study data were stored in a secure platform or drive for data protection.

Data and Study Population

The 2021 and 2022 QPP PUFs pertain to clinician MIPS performance during those performance years. They provided measure-level scores, performance category scores, final MIPS scores, MIPS payment adjustment percentages, and certain clinician and practice characteristics at the Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN)/National Provider Identifier (NPI) level. Importantly, the 2021 and 2022 QPP PUFs only included TIN/NPI level data contributing to the MIPS final score. 23 The 2022 Doctors and Clinicians National Downloadable File provided additional clinician and practice characteristics.

The authors performed data cleaning and preparation, and a description of data inclusion and exclusion procedures is in Supplementary Appendix A1 and Supplementary Appendix A2. The study population included orthopedic surgeons who actively participated in MIPS by submitting data and receiving final scores and payment adjustments. The data used in Question 1 included an orthopedic surgeon’s unique NPI, as well as a composite weighted-average payment adjustment percentage and its corresponding dichotomized version (> 0 or ≤ 0), for a given performance year. The final sample size with matched pairs in both PY2021 and PY2022 was 6441. In Supplementary Appendix B, the Study Population Construction section describes how the authors constructed the composite weighted-average payment adjustment. The authors also computed a second composite payment adjustment percentage using a simple average to conduct a sensitivity analysis. The data used for Question 2 included TIN/NPI, a payment adjustment percentage and its corresponding dichotomized version, and clinician and practice characteristics. The final sample size with matching records in the 2022 QPP PUF and 2022 Doctors and Clinicians National Downloadable File was 8734.

Study variables

MIPS Payment Adjustment Percentage

MIPS payment adjustment percentages were determined by comparing the final score to the PT and then scaled to ensure budget neutrality. 23 The authors assessed the binary MIPS-payment-adjustment-percentage variable: receipt of a bonus (or positive) payment adjustment (payment adjustment percentage > 0%), or not receiving a bonus (or non-positive) payment adjustment (payment adjustment percentage ≤ 0%). A description of how the authors transformed the outcome variable and a histogram showing a non-normal distribution with a partial ceiling effect in this continuous variable can be found in Supplementary Appendix B and the Study Variables Preparation section.

Clinicians and Practice Characteristics

The authors studied the association and impact of multiple predictor variables: state, practice size, participation option, rural status, safety net status, complex patient bonus score, gender, and years of practice. The variable “number-of-Medicare-patients” was removed from the final list due to its strong correlation with practice size. These predictor variables were selected because a previous study has demonstrated that they are significant factors in receiving positive payment adjustments. 24 Complex patient bonus score, years of practice, and practice size were transformed into categorical variables; detailed operations can be found in the Study Variables Preparation section of Supplementary Appendix B. The updated complex patient bonus scoring methodology, including calculations, can be found in the 2022 Complex Patient Bonus Fact Sheet. 16

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.1.2. 25 The authors computed descriptive statistics on a national sample of orthopedic surgeons (N = 8734), as well as the top 20th, best-performing orthopedic surgeons (n = 1764).

McNemar’s test was used to assess whether the proportion of surgeons who earned positive payment adjustments differed between PY2021 and PY2022. Mixed effects logistic regression was used to examine the factors associated with orthopedic surgeons receiving positive payment adjustments. Using the PY2022 data, the authors fit a set of bivariate mixed effects logistic regression models with positive (> 0) vs non-positive (≤ 0) payment adjustment as the response, a random NPI-specific intercept to account for correlation between repeated measures from the same NPI, and clinician and practice characteristics as predictors. The authors also fit a mixed effects logistic regression model that included all the predictors simultaneously. For both the bivariate and multivariate models, the authors calculated relevant odds ratios (ORs), their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values. An alpha (α) value of 0.05 was used as the benchmark of statistical significance throughout the study. After significant effects were found in the mixed effects logistic regression model, the authors performed pairwise comparisons to compare the levels in each predictor variable that had > 2 levels. Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels (0.05 divided by the number of pairwise comparisons) were used to determine statistical significance for pairwise comparisons. Last, the authors conducted χ 2 and Fisher’s exact tests to assess potential correlations between gender and practice size.

Study Results

Question 1: Effect of Raising the PT from 60 to 75 Points

Study Samples

The authors used a sample of 6441 orthopedic surgeons participating in MIPS and receiving payment adjustment percentages in both PY2021 and PY2022. Sample characteristics can be found in Supplementary Appendix C.

Study Results

In PY2021, 94.9% of the surgeons earned positive payment adjustments, falling to 79.3% in PY2022. Of those earning bonus payments in PY2021 (n = 6110), 18.4% (n = 1123) no longer earned bonus payments in PY2022. The authors found evidence of an association between an increased PT of 75 points in PY2022 and the proportion of surgeons who received positive payment adjustments (χ 2 = 803.21, degrees of freedom = 1, P < .01). The unweighted risk difference was –15.04, with 95% CI, –16.05 to –14.03. Further, a sensitivity test demonstrated that the results based on the simple average were comparable to the weighted average.

Question 2: Factors Associated With Receiving Bonus Payments

Study Samples

A total sample of 8734 orthopedic surgeons who actively participated in MIPS, received payment adjustments in PY2022, and had matching records in the 2022 QPP PUF and the 2022 Doctors and Clinicians National Downloadable File was used. The mean payment adjustment percentage was 1.03% (Table 1). Overall, 12.1% of surgeons were solo or small practice clinicians, with the majority being from medium (32.3%) and large (55.6%) practices. About 34.1% of surgeons practiced in the south, with the rest distributed similarly across the northeast (18.1%), midwest (21.5%), and west (26.3%). Most surgeons came from nonrural areas (87.4%) and non-safety net practices (90.7%); the majority had a complex patient bonus score of zero (76.0%).

Surgeons from large practices (≥ 100 clinicians) experienced significantly greater odds of earning positive payment adjustments than solo (1 clinician), small (2–15 clinicians), and medium (16–99 clinicians) practice clinicians. Surgeons in small practices showed a 60% reduced odds of earning positive payment adjustments (OR, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.33–0.48; P < .008), compared to those in large practices (Tables 2 and 3). Small practice clinicians also had a 29% decreased odds of earning positive payment adjustments compared to those in medium practices (OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.60–0.85; P < .008). However, no significant differences were found between solo and small practice clinicians, or between solo and medium practice clinicians (Table 3).

Surgeons who participated as individuals exhibited a 34% reduced odds of earning positive payment adjustments (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.57–0.77; P < .01), compared to those who participated as a group. Orthopedic surgeons who practiced in rural areas experienced a 34% reduced odds of earning positive payment adjustments (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.56–0.77; P < .01) compared to those in nonrural areas (Table 2).

Surgeons with high complex patient bonus scores (≥ 8 points) had 6.5 times higher odds of receiving positive payment adjustments, compared to those having a complex bonus score of zero (OR, 6.49; 95% CI, 3.31–12.76; P < .017; Bonferroni-adjusted α = 0.05/3 = 0.017). Those with some complex patient bonus scores (> 0 to ≤ 8 points) had 2.7 times higher odds of earning positive payment adjustments (OR, 2.74; 95% CI, 2.27–3.31; P < .017), compared to those with zero points (Tables 2 and 3). Surgeons with some complex patient bonus scores had 58% reduced odds of receiving positive payment adjustments (OR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.22–0.81; P < .017), compared to those with high complex patient bonus scores (Table 3).

Female orthopedic surgeons experienced a 42% higher odds of earning positive payment adjustments (OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.10–1.84; P < .01) compared to males (Table 2). Additionally, there was a significant relationship between gender and practice size (χ2 = 63.98, degrees of freedom = 3; P < .0001). In this sample, nearly 70% of female surgeons were employed in large practices, while only 55% of male surgeons were.

Question 3: Characteristics of Top-Performing Orthopedic Surgeons

Study Results

The authors analyzed a sample of 1764 orthopedic surgeons who ranked in the top 20th percentile for payment adjustment percentages in PY2022. All received positive payment adjustments ranging from 1.63% to 8.26%, including the additional adjustment for exceptional performance. 26 Their mean payment adjustment percentage was 5.10%, compared to 1.03% in the overall sample. There was a higher proportion of solo or small practice clinicians (16.6%), compared to 12.1% in the overall sample. Proportions of both safety net practitioners and surgeons with high complex patient bonus scores (15.50% and 5.10%, respectively) were higher than in the overall sample (9.6% and 2.9%, respectively).

For top performers, 18 quality measures were most frequently reported, including administrative claim measures that were automatically calculated by CMS. 27 Roughly 33% (6/18) of the most frequently self-reported quality measures were orthopedic surgery specific, and ~ 28% (5/18) were not related to their specialty (Table 4). In the overall sample (N = 8734), 24 quality measures were most reported. Roughly 25% (6/24) were related to orthopedic surgery, and ~ 46% (11/24) were not (see Supplementary Appendix D).

Discussion

The authors analyzed detailed data from the most up-to-date MIPS performance years, finding a significant drop in the proportion of orthopedic surgeons who earned bonus payments in PY2022 (79.3%), compared to PY2021 (94.9%). Raising the MIPS PT from 60 to 75 points created a substantial impact on orthopedic surgeons’ ability to earn bonus payments; their chance of receiving a positive payment adjustment in PY2022 was between 14% and 16% lower than in PY2021.

The authors found that region, practice size, participation option, rural status, complex patient bonus score, and gender were significantly associated with receiving positive payment adjustments, with complex patient bonus score showing the strongest association. Specifically, surgeons who either practiced in the west, were from a nonrural, large practice, reported MIPS as a group, provided care to more medically and/or socially complex patients, or were female showed increased odds of receiving positive payment adjustments. In a study using 2018 performance data, Cwalina et al also found that surgeons who reported in groups, practiced in larger practices, were located in the west or south, or were female had decreased odds of receiving a penalty, while those caring for patients with greater HCC risk scores had increased odds of a penalty. 24 There are 2 main differences in this study’s results, highlighting the potential influence of MIPS policy changes. First, this study’s findings indicate that the updated complex patient bonus policy effectively rewards surgeons who treat more high-risk patients, with the reward increasing linearly as the risk level rises. In contrast to the authors’ findings, the Cwalina study indicated that, under the old policy, surgeons who treated Medicare patients with high HCC scores had increased odds of a penalty (OR, 2.31; 95% CI, 1.35–4.01; P = .002). 24 Second, this study’s results suggest that the effect of practice size on receiving positive payment adjustments was minimized due to MIPS policies aimed at improving equity for small practice clinicians. The Cwalina study showed that small practices (1–49 clinicians) had roughly a 95% (OR, 1/22.2 = 0.05; 95% CI, 0.012–0.12; P < .001) reduction in the odds of receiving neutral, positive, or exceptional bonus payment adjustments compared to large practices (≥ 50 clinicians). 24 In contrast to the Cwalina study, the authors observed smaller reductions in the odds of receiving positive payment adjustments for solo (48%), small (60%), and medium (45%) practice clinicians compared to those from large practices. However, surgeons from large practices continue to experience significantly greater odds of earning positive payment adjustments than any other practice size. The strong correlation between practice size and gender may explain why female orthopedic surgeons had a 42% higher likelihood of receiving positive payment adjustments compared to their male counterparts. Notably, nearly 70% of female surgeons worked in large practices.

The authors also found that the top performers had higher proportions of small practice and safety net practitioners, as well as surgeons caring for more medically and/or socially complex patients, compared to the overall sample. This indicates that surgeons from small practices and/or high-risk social or patient settings can still perform well under MIPS. Additionally, top performers had a higher tendency to report specialty-specific quality measures; however, even among top performers, almost a third of the most frequently reported quality measures were not orthopedic surgery specific. In the overall sample, nearly half of the most reported quality measures were unrelated to their practice.

Limitations

The present study had 3 potential limitations. First, the authors excluded 5482 unengaged orthopedic surgeons who did not submit MIPS data, as well as those who participated as an Advanced Alternative Payment Models entity 29 in PY2022 (6214 exclusions in PY2021). However, systematic bias from these exclusions is unlikely because they were not the study’s population of interest. Second, national databases provided the most current data in PY2021 and PY2022; however, this period covered the Public Health Emergency for COVID-19, which expired in May 2023. 30 Hence, this may have had an overall effect on clinicians’ MIPS performance. To address the issue of confounding, which is a common concern in observational studies, the authors have incorporated all relevant variables as predictors in this study’s multivariable model based on the available data set. However, it is still possible that some important confounders were not included in the CMS data set and, therefore, could not be part of the multivariable model.

Policy Implications

This study’s findings have several implications for developing future MIPS policies. First, CMS attempted to increase the PT from 75 to 82 points for PY2024. 31 However, the results suggest that future MIPS PT should not be raised, as doing so could further reduce the number of surgeons receiving incentive payments, potentially leading to greater dissatisfaction. However, the authors recognize that raising the PT is a potential tool CMS can use in a budget-neutral program to reward performance with higher payment adjustment percentages, making incentive payments more attractive to clinicians. CMS should explore strategies to make high performance more challenging while ensuring a meaningful reward for high performance, instead of simply raising the cut-off score for positive payment adjustment.

Second, although this study’s results suggest that the small-practice-bonus policies may have minimized the effect practice size had on payment adjustments, the authors’ analysis also highlights that these policies have not yet fully closed the inequity gap for orthopedic surgeons working in solo, small, or rural practices. CMS must continue to explore policy options to eliminate inequity for practitioners working in practice settings with limited technology, staffing, and financial resources.

Third, the results of this study indicate that orthopedic surgeons receive substantially better rewards when they manage a higher proportion of complex patients, highlighting the importance and success that stem from cross-agency collaborations. CMS should continue to collaborate with agencies like the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation to strategize and improve MIPS scoring and payment adjustment structures that can close policy gaps and contribute to meaningful reform.

Fourth, the authors’ analysis raises concerns about whether MIPS incentivizes orthopedic surgeons to report specialty-specific quality measures that would have the greatest impact on outcomes and patient experience. CMS should develop policies to motivate and reward reporting on more challenging and relevant metrics and move away from allowing clinicians to select measures and activities they already perform well but that do not relate to their practice. The present study provides conceptual support to CMS’s gradual transition from MIPS to the MIPS Value Pathway (MVP), which is a new reporting option developed for specific specialties. MVP offers a smaller, more focused selection of meaningful measures and activities, 32 for example, the MVP ID: G0058 (Improving Care for Lower Extremity Joint Repair) provides clinicians with 8 quality measures. 33 Notably, quality measures unrelated to orthopedic surgery (such as statin therapy, influenza immunization, or diabetes control) are not included. 33

Last, future research could use this observational design and analysis framework to assess if policy-associated changes can be observed in other specialties nationwide.
