Tumor acid-triggered sericin prodrug colloidal system for enhanced photodynamic and ferroptosis therapy of breast cancer
Authors: Wei Huang, Menglin Zhang, Jingting Wang, Ruohan Xiao, Zhigang Xu, Fanpeng Ran, Shucheng Wan, Xiaoxiao Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImage-guided tumor therapy is an integrated approach for visualized and targeted delivery, which synchronizes diagnosis and treatment to achieve complete tumor eradication. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) show promise for cancer therapy by provoking neoplastic oxidative damage; however, their therapeutic efficacy is substantially constrained by the intracellular antioxidant defense system. Inhibiting solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, also known as xCT) has been demonstrated to downregulate intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels, thereby not only enhancing oxidative stress but also triggering ferroptosis, a recently identified form of programmed cell death. Here, we proposed a multi-faceted ROS amplification strategy to induce tumor oxidative damage by constructing cinnamaldehyde (CA)- and ROS-producing purpurin 18 (P18)-conjugated sericin prodrug (SNCPS) encapsulated with xCT inhibitor sulfasalazine (SAS). It was confirmed that the ROS-induced oxidative damage by 660 nm laser-assisted SNCPS was multilaterally amplified by virtue of the down-regulation of SAS-based anti-oxidative stress responses in tumor cells, ultimately leading to highly efficient photodynamic and ferroptosis effects. Furthermore, the near-infrared fluorescence properties of the fluorescent molecule P18 were exploited for in vivo imaging to track the distribution of its nano-formulation. In vivo assays showcased the strong antitumor potency of SNCPS in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, with no apparent systemic toxicity observed. This work presents a highly promising strategy for designing antitumor agents via the multilateral amplification of oxidative stress.Keywords: Sericin prodrug, ROS amplification, Photodynamic therapy, Ferroptosis, Synergistic therapy

Introduction

Oxidative stress, defined by an imbalance in the oxidant-to-antioxidant ratio that favors oxidants, predisposes mitochondria to damage, thereby jeopardizing cell survival and strengthening both inflammatory and immune responses [1,2]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), typically consisting of hydroxyl radicals, singlet oxygen (1O2), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and other related radicals, are regarded as major inducers of oxidative stress [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Dynamic ROS-based cancer therapies involve treatment approaches that amplify the production of intratumoral ROS from endogenous or exogenous perspectives, such as photodynamic therapy (PDT), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and others [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Nevertheless, numerous cancer cells have a heightened intrinsic antioxidant capacity, allowing them to maintain cellular redox homeostasis and thus ensure their survival [[12], [13], [14]]. Specifically, intracellular antioxidant systems, which comprise antioxidant enzymes and non-enzymatic antioxidants like glutathione (GSH), effectively scavenge ROS, safeguarding active components from oxidative damage and ultimately mitigating ROS-induced cellular injury. Accordingly, the therapeutic effectiveness of conventional ROS-based cancer therapies would be greatly impaired [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]].

The solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, also known as xCT) is a well-established component of the antioxidant defense system, responsible for facilitating cystine import to maintain cellular redox homeostasis [16,[20], [21], [22]]. Growing evidence suggests that either depleting extracellular cystine or inhibiting cystine uptake through xCT inhibitors, such as sulfasalazine (SAS), is sufficient to provoke the depletion of intracellular cysteine and GSH pools [[23], [24], [25]]. The GSH exhaustion is conducive to the amplification of oxidative stress within tumor cells, further enhancing ROS-mediated oncotherapy [26,27]. Beyond that, the depletion of GSH by xCT inhibitors, an essential catalytic co-factor for glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a phospholipid hydroperoxidase, can bolster the occurrence of lipid peroxidation (LPO) and contribute to ferroptosis induction, further improving tumor therapy [[28], [29], [30]].

Herein, we developed an acid-responsive sericin-based prodrug nanoformulation (SNCPS) to enhance oxidative stress for photo-ferroptosis therapy (Scheme 1), by simultaneously conjugating hydrophobic cinnamaldehyde (CA) and photosensitizer purpurin 18 (P18) to sericin, followed by further loading with SAS [31,32]. The nanoformulation is enriched in tumor cells, as evidenced by tracking the near-infrared fluorescent signal of P18, which can be excited by light in the 700–900 nm NIR region [33,34]. In the meantime, SAS encapsulated in the hydrophobic core was released to further deplete GSH and fortify ROS amplification via xCT inhibition, synchronously inducing LPO and ferroptosis of tumor cells. Benefiting from the multi-pronged ROS amplification achieved by CA and SAS, laser-aided SNCPS caused severe mitochondrial oxidative damage, inhibited tumor cell proliferation, and triggered immunogenic cell death (ICD). Transcriptome sequencing analysis substantiated effective ferroptosis induction by downregulating the expression of the SLC7A11. The CA and SAS-based ROS amplification and ferroptosis strategy also resulted in significant inhibition of tumor growth in 4T1-bearing mice. Overall, the multi-faceted ROS amplification strategy opens a novel avenue for the advancement of enhanced photo-ferroptosis therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sericin (SS) with average molecular weight of 10 kDa was procured from Hongxin Ruiyu Fine Chemical Co., Ltd (Hubei, China). Purpurin 18 (P18) was obtained from Frontier Scientific Inc. (Utah, USA). Sulfasalazine (SAS) was sourced from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA), and cinnamaldehyde (CA) was acquired from Adamas (Basel, CH). Dimethyl sulfoxide (anhydrous, DMSO), triethylamine (TEA), and other chemicals were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The primary biological reagents, including Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), were purchased from Gibco (Tulsa, USA). The Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, DCFH-DA), Alexa Fluor®488 Phalloidin (AF-488), 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT), and Mito Tracker Green Dye were all provided by Life Technologies (Carlsbad, USA).

Synthesis of SSNH

SS (600 mg) was fully dissolved in 4-morpholineethanesulfonic acid (MES) buffer (60 mL). Subsequently, 1-ethyl-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC·HCl, 20.0 mmol) and N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide (sulfo-NHS, 5.0 mmol) were added to the sericin solution under stirring for 30 min. Following this, 1.0 mL of hydrazine hydrate (85%, 17.5 mmol) was added dropwise to the mixture under darkness and allowed to react for 24 h. The resultant solution was then dialyzed against deionized water for three days using a dialysis tube with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) of 3.5 kDa. After dialysis, the solution was filtered and subsequently freeze-dried to obtain SSNH powders.

Synthesis of SNC and SNP prodrugs

Briefly, P18 (6.0 μmol) was dissolved in DMSO and activated with EDC (12.0 μmol) and sulfo-NHS (12.0 μmol). After stirring for 30 min, the activated P18 solution was slowly introduced into the SSNH (20 mg/mL) solution, which had been pre-dissolved in DMSO, using a micro-syringe under an argon atmosphere and in the dark over a period of 24 h. The resulting product was dialyzed (MWCO = 3.5 kDa) against DMSO for 24 h to yield the amphiphilic SSNH-P18 (SNP) solution.

Similarly, CA (30.0 μmol) and TEA (10 μL) were dissolved in DMSO and gradually added to the SSNH (20 mg/mL) solution via a micro-syringe under dark conditions and argon atmosphere for 24 h. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was dialyzed (MWCO = 3.5 kDa) against DMSO for 24 h to obtain the amphiphilic SSNH-CA (SNC) solution.

Synthesis of SNCP and SNCPS nanomicelles

For the preparation of SNCP, SNP (500 μL) and SNC (500 μL) solutions were mixed in a centrifuge tube and then added dropwise into 5 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring in the dark for 12 h. The resulting mixture was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO = 3.5 kDa) for 24 h to obtain SNCP nanomicelles.

For the preparation of SNCPS, SNP (500 μL) solution was mixed with SNC (500 μL) solution in a centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of SAS (6.0 μmol). The mixture was then added dropwise into 5 mL of deionized water with continuous stirring in the dark for 12 h. Finally, the resultant mixture was dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO = 3.5 kDa) for 24 h to acquire SNCPS nanomicelles.

Quantification of the amino content in SSNH

The content of free amino groups in SS and SSNH was quantified using a modified ninhydrin colorimetric method. Solution A was prepared by mixing disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na2HPO4, 20 mL, 0.067 mol/L) with potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4, 5 mL, 0.067 mol/L). Solution B was obtained by dissolving ninhydrin (2.0 g) and stannous chloride (SnCl2, 80 mg) in deionized water under continuous stirring for 30 min, followed by storage in an amber bottle at 4 °C for 24 h to ensure reagent stabilization.

Subsequently, 1 mL of either SS or SSNH solution (10 mg/mL) was transferred into a glass vial and mixed with 0.5 mL of Solution A and 0.5 mL of Solution B. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 15 min to promote the chromogenic reaction. After cooling to ambient temperature, the absorbance of the resultant solution was measured at 570 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2550, Shimadzu, Japan).

Characterization of SNCPS

The particle size distribution and zeta potential of the SNCPS were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Nano ZS90, UK) measurements. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1230EX, JPN) was utilized to examine the structural morphology of the SNCPS.

The pH triggered release assay of SNCPS

The SNCPS were placed in different pH solutions to benchmark their pH-triggered drug release behaviors. Specifically, SNCPS were suspended in 1 mL of PBS and transferred into a dialysis bag (MWCO = 3.5 kDa), which was immersed in 30 mL of medium with differing pH levels (pH 7.4 and pH 5.0). At specific time intervals, 1 mL of the release medium was withdrawn and measured for absorbance at 360 nm using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (UV-2550, Shimadzu, Japan). To ensure a constant total system volume, each removed aliquot was replaced with 1 mL of freshly prepared buffer solution at the corresponding pH.

In vitro evaluation of photodynamic effect of SNCPS

DPBF was employed as a probe to confirm the generation of 1O2. Initially, free P18 and SNCPS, each at a P18 concentration of 5 μg/mL, were separately incubated with 100 μL of DPBF solution (0.5 mg/mL). These mixtures were subjected to intermittent irradiation using a 660 nm laser (Laserwave Optoelectronics, 0.3 W/cm2), and the absorption of DPBF at 420 nm was recorded at various time points using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. In addition, SOSG was also used as an alternative fluorescent probe for 1O2 to further assess photodynamic effect of SNCPS. Specifically, SOSG (5 μM) was incubated with either free P18 or SNCPS at a P18 concentration of 5 μg/mL. Changes in SOSG fluorescence were monitored via fluorescence spectroscopy, both in the presence and absence of 660 nm laser irradiation (0.3 W/cm2).

In vitro cell uptake

To evaluate the cellular uptake of SNCPS, 4T1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 104 cells/well and incubated overnight to allow for cell attachment. Subsequently, SNCPS (P18 concentration: 1.0 μg/mL) were added to designated wells and cultured for 2 h and 6 h. The progression of intracellular fluorescence signals was monitored using CLSM (Zeiss 800, Germany) to characterize the internalization dynamics of SNCPS.

For quantitative analysis of cellular uptake, flow cytometry (FCM) was employed. 4T1 cells were plated in 12-well culture dishes at a density of 1.0 × 105 cells/well and allowed to stabilize overnight. Thereafter, SNCPS (P18 concentration: 5 μg/ml) was introduced into the designated wells. At time points of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h post-treatment, the cells were washed with PBS to remove extracellular nanoparticle fluorescence. After thorough washing, the cells were analyzed by FCM (NovoCyte 2060R, China). Live cell populations were gated using FSC-SSC scatter plots, and 10,000 events were collected per sample. Fluorescence signals were detected using the APC-Cy7-H channel, and compensation was optimized using single-stained controls. The extent of drug internalization was then quantified.

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment

Specifically, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight to ensure attachment. The cells were then assigned into six experimental groups: PBS, SAS, SNCPS, P18 (+L), SNCP (+L), and SNCPS (+L), with P18 administered at concentrations of 0, 0.6, 1.2, 2.4, and 4.8 μg/mL. After 6 h of drug administration, the L-treated groups were irradiated with a 660 nm laser (0.3 W/cm2, 3 min). Following an additional 18 h of incubation, MTT reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for another 4 h. Subsequently, the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance of cells was measured at 490 nm using a microplate (SPARK-10 M, CH) reader to determine cell viability.

Evaluation of cellular photodynamic effect

2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a fluorescent probe, was utilized to assess intracellular ROS levels. 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight. After 6 h of co-incubation with various formulations, the laser group was exposed to laser irradiation (660 nm, 0.3 W/cm2, 3 min); then all formulation groups were further incubated for 2 h, followed by treatment with DCFH-DA. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS, and fluorescence imaging was performed using CLSM to visualize ROS generation.

Given the Type II photosensitizer characteristics of P18, SOSG was selected as a probe for 1O2 detection. 4T1 cells were plated in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well overnight. After 6 h of treatment with the respective formulations, the laser group was exposed to laser irradiation (660 nm, 0.3 W/cm2, 3 min); all groups were then further incubated for 2 h, followed by the addition of the SOSG probe and CLSM imaging was conducted to capture 1O2-specific fluorescence signals.

In vitro examination of mitochondrial membrane potential

For the quantitative assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential disruption, 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and allocated into six experimental groups: PBS, SAS, SNCPS, P18 (+L), SNCP (+L), and SNCPS (+L). The concentrations of P18 and SAS were maintained consistently across all groups at 1 μg/mL and 5 μg/mL, respectively. Following a 6-h drug exposure, the laser-treated groups received light irradiation (660 nm, 0.3 W/cm2) for 3 min. Following this, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with the JC-1 probe (5 μM) for 20 min to visualize mitochondrial depolarization via CLSM.

In vitro GSH consumption

The ThiolTracker Violet probe was employed to evaluate SNCPS-induced alterations in intracellular GSH levels. 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight. The adherent cells were then divided into four groups: PBS, SAS, SNCPS, and SNCPS (+L). After 6 h of drug treatment, the cells in the L-treated group were exposed to 660 nm laser (3min), and all treatment groups continued to incubate for another 4 h. Thereafter, ThiolTracker Violet staining was carried out under light-protected conditions for 30 min. Finally, the stained cells were used for CLSM observation.

Detection of intracellular GPX4 expression

4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well overnight. After 6 h of drug treatment with PBS, SAS, and SNCPS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. Subsequently, cell permeabilization was carried out using 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubation with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h. Afterward, the primary antibody (anti-GPX4) was added and incubated with cells overnight at 4 °C. After washing off the primary antibody, an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody was applied for 30-min incubation at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min. Multichannel fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM.

In vitro assessment of lipid peroxidation

An oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe, C11-BODIPY581/591, was utilized for the dynamic monitoring of intracellular lipid peroxidation. 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured overnight. The cells were treated with PBS, SAS, SNCPS, P18 (+L), SNCP (+L), or SNCPS (+L) for 6 h. For groups requiring laser irradiation, irradiation (660 nm, 0.3 W/cm2, 3 min) was performed at 6 h post-treatment, and all groups were incubated for an additional 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with C11-BODIPY581/591 probe (2.5 μM) for 30 min in the dark, followed by DAPI staining for 15 min. CLSM imaging was performed to quantify the resulting fluorescence intensities.

In vitro RNA sequencing

A total of 1 × 107 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured overnight, followed by treatment with either PBS or SNCPS (+L) for 24 h. The SNCPS (+L) group received laser irradiation (660 nm, 0.3 W/cm2) for 3 min at 6 h post-incubation. The samples were then analyzed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (BMKcloud, Beijing, China).

In vitro penetrability of SNCPS in 4T1 multicellular tumors spheroids

Agarose and 4T1 cells were used to construct 4T1 multicellular spheroids (MCSs) to simulate the solid tumor model. Following the establishment of the model, SNCPS (P18 concentration: 5.0 μg/mL) was added to the MCSs and incubated for 1, 6, and 12 h, respectively. Thereafter, CLSM was employed to observe the infiltration behavior of SNCPS at varying depths within the MCSs.

In vivo tumor penetrability

Female Balb/c mice (aged 6-8 weeks, weighing 18-22 g) were inoculated with 4T1 cells to establish a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 120 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were administrated with P18 and SNCPS at an equivalent dose of P18 (1.0 mg/kg) via tail vein injection. In vivo fluorescence signals were then acquired at predetermined time points over a 24-h period post-injection using a fluorescence imaging system (IVIS Lumina III, USA).

In vivo therapeutic effect

A 4T1 tumor model was established in female Balb/c mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) using a blinded method: Saline, SAS, SNCPS, P18 (+L), SNCP (+L), and SNCPS (+L). All drugs were administered intravenously to the tumor-bearing mice (P18: 1 mg/kg, SAS: 5 mg/kg), and 4 h post-administration, the L-exposed groups received 660 nm laser irradiation (0.3 W/cm2, 3.0 min). All treatments were implemented at two-day intervals, for a total of four cycles. During the experimental period, tumor volumes and body weights of mice were measured daily. The tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: Tumor volume (mm3) = (length×width2)/2. On day 16 of treatment, all mice were euthanized, and tumors along with major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested for histopathological and immunofluorescence analysis. All animal experiments were strictly conducted in accordance with the National Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee of Southwest University (Approval No. IACUC-20240920-15).

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), or repeated-measures two-way ANOVA where appropriate (e.g., tumor volume data). The levels of statistical significance were defined as follows: ns indicates no significant difference, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of SNCPS nanoparticles

SAS, a well-known inducer of ferroptosis mediated by xCT inhibition, has been extensively substantiated to suppress tumor cell proliferation. To enhance its therapeutic and delivery efficiency, a multifunctional nanoplatform was developed through the conjugation of sericin (SS) with GSH-depleting CA via pH-sensitive hydrazone bonds and ROS-elevating P18 via EDC/NHS-catalyzed amidation [35]. Initially, SS was chemically functionalized with hydrazine groups to enable drug release in response to the acidic microenvironment of tumors (Fig. 1A). The chemical structure of the hydrazinylated sericin (SSNH) was validated using a ninhydrin (NHN) colorimetric assay (Fig. 1B) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1E), confirming characteristic peaks at δ 1.3 ppm corresponding to the introduced hydrazine moieties [36]. Following covalent grafting of CA or NHS-activated P18 onto SSNH, two prodrug conjugates (SNC and SNP) were successfully obtained while preserving the native conformational stability of the sericin protein, as evidenced by gel electrophoresis analysis (Fig. 1C and D). 1H NMR spectroscopy further confirmed the successful synthesis of SNC and SNP prodrugs. For SNPs, aromatic proton signals at δ 6.5–8.0 ppm were attributed to P18 and SSNH moieties, while for SNCs, olefinic protons from CA exhibited distinct peaks between δ 6.8–7.2 ppm (Fig. 1E) [37,38].

To identify the optimal size of SNCP nanoassemblies, a series of SNP/SNC composite nanosystems with varied component ratios were prepared and characterized via dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis to determine their hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI). As the molar ratio of hydrophilic SSNH to hydrophobic P18 increased from 1:1 to 1:4, the hydrodynamic diameter of SNP micelles expanded from 300 nm to 600 nm, accompanied by a gradual increase in PDI values (Fig. 1F). Similarly, incrementing the SSNH:CA molar ratio from 1:3 to 1:15 resulted in progressive enlargement of micelle sizes from 180 nm to 300 nm, coupled with a PDI increase from 0.1 to 0.3 (Fig. S1A). When SNC and SNP components were self-assembled at a stoichiometric ratio of SSNH:CA = 1:15 and SSNH: P18 = 1:3, the resulting SNCP nanomicelles exhibited the smallest hydrodynamic diameter (150 ± 5 nm) and narrowest PDI (0.15) (Fig. 1G and Figs. S1B and S2), qualifying them as the optimal carrier for SAS encapsulation. Upon SAS incorporation into SNCP, the size of the final formulation (SNCPS) was increased to approximately 160 nm (PDI = 0.11) (Fig. 1H), revealing a monodisperse spherical nanostructure as observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The modest increase in size preliminarily suggests the successful encapsulation of SAS within SNCP nanomicelles. Further verification was obtained through UV-vis absorption spectroscopy, which confirmed the presence of SAS and P18 in SNCPS by the emergence of corresponding characteristic absorption peaks (Fig. 1I). Notably, SNCPS retained hydrodynamic diameters in between 150 and 160 nm and PDI values of 0.1 to 0.2 over 15 days, showcasing robust colloidal stability (Fig. S3A). When administered intravenously, the weak negative surface charge of SNCPS can prolong the in vivo circulation time, thereby enhancing delivery efficiency (Fig. S3B). These data suggest the promising potential of SNCPS as an ideal nanoplatform for tumor therapy.

In vitro responsive decomposition and photodynamic effect of SNCPS

Tumor cells display distinctly different GSH content and acidity levels from normal cells, making it indispensable to explore the GSH- and pH-dependent changes in diameter and morphology of SNCPS. As shown in Fig. 2A, the hydrodynamic diameters of SNCPS nanomicelles increased upon the addition of GSH or a decrease in pH solution. The PDI values also rose above 0.3, showing a correlation with the hydrodynamic diameter. Furthermore, the zeta potentials of the SNCPS exhibited an upward trend (Fig. S3B). More intuitively, we observed swollen SNCPS in high-concentration of GSH solution and decomposed SNCPS in response to a pH 5.0 solution (Fig. 2B and C). The responsive mechanism of SNCPS decomposition primarily relies on the pH-cleavable hydrazone bond of SNC, as illustrated in Fig. 2D.

Effective release of SAS from SNCPS is a prerequisite of achieving xCT-mediated ferroptosis. Given the robust pH-dependent decomposition of SNCPS, we inspected the release behavior of SAS under in vitro conditions, including the simulated normal physiological environment and the acidic tumor intracellular environment. It was determined that approximately 84.2% of SAS was released from SNCPS after incubation with a pH 5.0 buffer solution for 24 h (Fig. 2E). In contrast, within the simulated physiological environment (pH 7.4), SNCPS exhibited negligible SAS release within hours and sustained slow release, amounting to approximately 24.9%, over 24 h. These results demonstrate that, compared with the normal physiological environment, SNCPS can release a substantial amount of SAS under acidic conditions, thereby inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells.

Furthermore, the integration of the P18 photosensitizer endowed SNCPS with the capacity to produce ROS during PDT [39]. As illustrated in Fig. 2F, the SNCPS demonstrated a markedly higher yield of 1O2 when compared to traditionally unmodified P18 under identical 660 nm laser irradiation conditions, as reflected by electron spin resonance (ESR) spectra using 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) as a spin trap [40]. In addition, the quantitative analysis of 1O2 production using SOSG as a fluorescence probe under varying laser irradiation durations is presented in Fig. 2G. Indeed, the 1O2 generation by SNCPS was significantly superior, being 1.8-fold higher than that of free P18 under a 9-min PDT treatment. These results identify that the PDT efficacy can be significantly enhanced through the formation of amphipathic nanostructures in SNCPS, attributed to their improved solubility. Moreover, the DPBF absorption spectra of SNCPS and free P18 under laser condition again corroborated a better photodynamic effect of SNCPS in an irradiation time-dependent manner (Fig. 2H and Fig. S4). Overall, the high yield of 1O2 by SNCPS strongly underscores its potential as a promising photodynamic therapeutic agent [41].

Intracellular antitumor ability and oxidative damage of SNCPS

In consideration of the dual damage mechanism involving PDT-mediated oxidative damage and SAS-induced ferroptosis, we explored the intracellular antitumor ability of SNCPS along with their associated biomedical safety. Theretofore, a time-dependent internalization of SNCPS in murine breast cancer cells (4T1 cells) was observed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) and flow cytometry (FCM) analysis, with uptake efficiency of up to 98.6% after a 6 h incubation (Fig. 3A and Fig. S5). Afterward, the MTT assay was employed to benchmark the viability of 4T1 cells and normal fibroblast cells (L929 cells) treated with varying concentrations of the formulas. As shown in Fig. 3B and C, no obvious cytotoxicity appeared in L929 cells, with a cell viability remained above 90%, even at the highest SAS concentration of 24.0 μg/mL under dark conditions, identifying the preliminary biocompatibility of SNCPS. Conversely, SNCPS exhibited stronger cytotoxicity against 4T1 cells under laser irradiation, which can be attributed to nanoparticle-enhanced drug penetration and synergistic effects between the drugs. When combined with laser irradiation, SNCPS induced a cell mortality rate as high as 86.8% in 4T1 cells, significantly higher than that of the non-irradiated SNCPS group. This indicates that SNCPS possesses excellent PDT-mediated oxidative damage effects, and the significant difference between the SNCP (+L) and SNCPS (+L) groups further suggests that the introduction of SAS may enhance the photodynamic effect. These results demonstrate that SNCPS can achieve PDT-mediated oxidative damage and may further potentiate SAS-induced ferroptosis through drug synergy. Moreover, the cell lethal effects of SNCPS were further visually confirmed through fluorescein diacetate (FDA)/propidium iodide (PI) live/dead cell double-staining assays (Fig. 3D and E) [42].

Encouraged by the antitumor outcome, we subsequently studied the possible mechanism of ROS generation by intracellular fluorescence probes. As displayed in Fig. 3F and G, negligible green fluorescence was observed in the PBS, SAS, and SNCPS groups, whereas detectable green fluorescence was present in all L-treated groups. Notably, the SNCPS (+L) group exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence intensity compared to the SNCP (+L) group, suggesting the surge of intracellular ROS levels. More specifically, the SOSG fluorescence probe was employed to quantify the intracellular 1O2 production induced by the type II photosensitizer properties of P18. Treatment with SNCPS and laser significantly enhanced intracellular 1O2 levels, as evidenced by the observable fluorescence of SOSG under laser exposure (Fig. 3H and I).

It is generally recognized that ROS generation can lead to mitochondrial oxidative damage. Apparently, effective mitochondrial colocalization of SNCPS serves as a critical foundation for inducing mitochondrial damage. As expected, when the incubation time was extended to 5 h, a significant overlap (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.76) was observed between the red fluorescence of SNCPS and the green fluorescence of mitochondria (Fig. 3J and Fig. S6), which SNCPS exhibit a strong association with mitochondria-rich regions. The fluorescent probe JC-1 was employed to monitor alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential. Under normal conditions, JC-1 forms aggregates within the mitochondrial matrix, emitting red fluorescence [43]. However, when the permeability of the mitochondrial membrane increases, JC-1 exists in its monomeric form, resulting in green fluorescence emission. Cells treated with SNCPS in combination with laser irradiation exhibited the lowest ratio of aggregates to monomers compared to all other groups (Fig. 3K and L), as evidenced by the highest intensity of green fluorescence and the weakest intensity of red fluorescence, which collectively indicate the most severe mitochondrial damage. These results demonstrate that SNCPS can generate substantial ROS under laser irradiation, thereby intensifying oxidative stress and leading to mitochondrial damage.

SNCPS-induced ferroptosis and ICD effects

Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of programmed cell death mediated by excessive lipid peroxidation [44]. It is widely accepted that the inactivation of GPX4 induced by GSH depletion represents a defining characteristic of ferroptosis [28]. Therefore, the GSH metabolic capacity of 4T1 cells under different treatment conditions was firstly tested using the thiol-specific probe ThiolTracker Violet. Due to the presence of SAS, a well-documented ferroptosis inducer, the SNCPS group exhibited a marked reduction in GSH levels (Fig. 4A and B). Notably, an even more drastic depletion of GSH was noticed in the SNCPS + L group, revealing that GSH was further oxidized to GSSG. This phenomenon may be associated with intracellular redox imbalance induced by elevated levels of ROS. Furthermore, the detection results of GPX4 expression levels showed that the fluorescence signal of GPX4 in the SNCPS (+L) group was weakened compared to the SNCPS group (Fig. 4C and D). This phenomenon may be associated with changes in GPX4 enzymatic activity and function caused by GSH depletion. These results suggest that laser irradiation may enhance the induction effect of SNCPS on ferroptosis-related active factors in 4T1 cells (Fig. 4E). To evaluate the LPO levels in 4T1 cells under different treatment conditions, fluorescence images of 4T1 cells stained with C11-BODIPY581/591 were captured [45]. Fig. 4F illustrates a transition from red emission (non-oxidizing state) to green emission (oxidizing state) in the SNCPS and SNCPS (+L) groups. Quantitatively, the SNCPS + L group exhibited a decrease of 69.2% in the ratio of red fluorescence intensity to green fluorescence compared with the SNCPS group (Fig. S7), indicating the enhanced ferroptotic cell death of SNCPS under laser exposure. Considerable evidence indicates that malondialdehyde (MDA), a prominent product of LPO, can abnormally accumulate on the membrane surface, thereby further exacerbating membrane damage. Indeed, we found that the SNCPS + L treatment significantly increased the MDA concentration by 12.3-fold and 1.3-fold compared to the PBS group and the SNCP (+L) group (Fig. 4G), respectively. The aforementioned results suggest that released SAS may disrupt intracellular redox homeostasis by depleting GSH, thereby reducing tumor-cell resistance to PDT, and allowing 1O2 to have greater opportunities to induce severe damage to tumor cells, ultimately enhancing the efficacy of PDT.

Exposing tumor cells to an immune activation system is anticipated to enhance the efficacy of tumor therapy. Amplified oxidative stress can trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby bridging PDT and immunotherapy through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which enhance antigen presentation to immune cells [46,47]. To evaluate whether SNCPS boost ROS-induced photo-immunotherapy, the characteristics of ICD activation were inspected in vitro, including the translocation of calreticulin (CRT) to the cell surface, the release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) secretion. Investigated by immunofluorescence staining, the HMGB1 levels in the SNCPS (+L)-treated cells were found to be the lowest, being 86.8% and 64.5% lower than those in the SNCPS group and SNCP (+L) group (Fig. 4I, and Fig. S8), respectively. Conversely, this treatment resulted in the highest levels of CRT expression and ATP secretion (Fig. 4H and J, and Fig. S8). These findings unequivocally confirm the successful activation of ICD by SNCPS.

Transcriptomics-based therapeutic mechanism of SNCPS

Transcriptomics offers a robust approach to elucidate the cellular-level mechanisms underlying the action of SNCPS. 4T1 cells were collected from both the untreated control group and the SNCPS (+L) -treated group, and transcriptomics data were generated by an RNA sequencing platform. The differences in gene expression profiles were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA), where a distinct separation between two groups indicates an obvious difference in the gene expression patterns (Fig. 5A). Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 5B), performed under the screening criteria of P < 0.05 and |log2 FoldChange| ≥ 1, identified 417 unique differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the SNCPS (+L)-treated group and the control group. Among these, 305 DEGs were specific to the SNCPS (+L)-treated group, and 112 DEGs specific to the control group. Volcano plot analysis (Fig. 5C), conducted under the same screening criterion, further revealed that 1304 genes were upregulated and 1317 genes were downregulated in the SNCPS (+L)-treated group relative to the control group.

As shown in Fig. 5D, the expression of Slc3a2, a subunit of the System Xc-, was downregulated upon SNCPS (+L) treatment, indicating partial inhibition of System Xc-. Furthermore, the SNCPS (+L) treatment also downregulated the expression of Bcl2 and Ccnb1, indicating the inhibition of 4T1 cell proliferation, as its mechanism of action also involves apoptosis and a range of immune response signaling pathways. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed on these differentially expressed genes. As illustrated in Fig. 5E, the comparison between SNCPS (+L) -treated and untreated samples revealed that the significantly enriched pathways were predominantly linked to the “p53 signaling pathway”, “ferroptosis pathway”, and “pathways involved in inflammatory and immune responses” [48,49]. Although these transcriptomic data have revealed the photo-ferroptosis therapeutic mechanism of SNCPS at the transcriptional level, standard protein-level validation (e.g., Western blot analysis of GPX4/SLC7A11) or functional rescue using specific inhibitors remains important aspects to be further addressed in future studies.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of SNCPS

Harnessing the advantageous properties of SNCPS, we further explored their antitumor effects in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. It is essential to recognize that the efficient tumor accumulation and penetration of SNCPS play a pivotal role in achieving tumor regression. To estimate the tumor penetration dynamics of SNCPS, multicellular spheroids (MCSs) were cultivated using 4T1 cells. As the incubation time with MCSs increased, the penetration depth of SNCPS reached 100 μm, preliminarily demonstrating their penetration capability (Fig. 5F and G). Before commencing oncotherapy, we examined the tumor accumulation of SNCPS using the IVIS imaging system to determine the optimal treatment window. We observed that, in contrast to the sparse P18 signal in tumors, SNCPS greatly accumulated at the tumor sites and reached maximum accumulation approximately 4 h post-intravenous injection (Fig. 5H and I). Thereupon, irradiation treatment was administered 4 h post-injection. Furthermore, frozen section staining revealed a remarkable difference in drug permeation within tumor tissues between the P18 and SNCPS groups (Fig. 5J and Fig. S9), with strong drug signals were observed in the deep-seated tumors from mice treated with SNCPS. These outcomes confirm the exceptional tumor accumulation and penetration performance of SNCPS. Therefore, the SNCPS near-infrared fluorescent nanoprobe, constructed through the loading of P18, demonstrates exceptional spatial resolution that can provide crucial information for the characterization of tumor microstructure.

We successfully established a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model by subcutaneously inoculating a 4T1 cell suspension into the right thigh of Balb/C mice. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5): (G1) Saline, (G2) SAS, (G3) SNCPS, (G4) P18 (+L), (G5) SNCP (+L), and (G6) SNCPS (+L), according to the treatment schedule depicted in Fig. 6A. From the tumor growth curves, it was evident that all treatment groups, except for the saline group, demonstrated significant tumor suppression effects (Fig. 6B, C and Fig. S10A). Notably, the mice treated with SNCPS (+L) had the most pronounced therapeutic effect in terms of tumor suppression, achieving an inhibition rate of 86.5%, which can be attributed to the synergistic action of SAS-induced ferroptosis and ROS burst from PDT. Additionally, the excised tumors from the SNCPS (+L) group were photographed and weighed, and were found to be the smallest and lightest, measuring only 0.2 g—approximately 80.3% and 51.8% less than those in the PBS and SNCP (+L) groups (Fig. 6D and Fig. S10B), respectively. More importantly, the inter-group variations in organ and body weight changes were counted to be within the margin of error, indicating that the adverse effects of all treatments are likely to be minimal (Fig. 6E and Fig. S11).

Subsequently, the therapeutic effects of mice treated with various formulations were further evaluated using hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E) and TdT-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) staining (Zeng et al., 2022). More severe nuclear damage and pronounced apoptotic signals were observed in the SNCPS (+L) treatment group (Fig. 6F and Fig. S12), underscoring its compelling superior tumor-regression effect. Thereafter, immunofluorescence staining for CD31, GPX4, and SLC7A11 was used to assess the treatment mechanism of SNCPS [[50], [51], [52]]. The marked downregulation of CD31 was noted in tumor tissue from SNCPS (+L)-treated mice (Fig. 6F and G), illustrating that this treatment regimen could reduce abnormal vascularization. Additionally, we discovered significant inhibitory activity against GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression following SNCPS (+L) treatment, substantiating the induction of ferroptosis within tumor tissues (Fig. 6F–H and I). The aforementioned findings confirm the superior antitumor effects of SNCPS by enhancing ferroptosis and oxidative damage induced by PDT.

Histological analysis of major organs revealed no detectable pathological abnormalities following various treatments (Fig. S13), signifying the minimal short-term toxicity of SNCPS in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice during the photodynamic and ferroptosis therapy. Furthermore, hematology analysis was performed, and no obvious parameter variations occurred in the SNCPS group (Fig. S14), implying the desirable good biosafety of SNCPS. Collectively, self-assembled sericin prodrug nanoformulations effectively prevent premature drug release, thereby enhancing drug utilization and reducing systemic toxicity.

Conclusion

In summary, we meticulously built an acid-responsive sericin prodrug nanoplatform (SNCPS) for the loading of SAS via amphiphilic self-assembly, enabling a ferroptosis-boosted PDT effect. Upon stimulation by the acidic tumor intracellular environment, SNCPS rapidly decomposed and released substantial amounts of SAS to suppress GPX4 expression and induce lipid peroxidation through GSH depletion, thereby activating ferroptosis. The GSH exhaustion effectively enhanced ROS yield and amplified oxidative damage during SNCPS-mediated PDT, further eliciting the ICD effect. It was noteworthy that the synergistic effect of PDT and ferroptosis significantly suppressed the viability of 4T1 tumor cells without harming normal cells. A subcutaneous 4T1 tumor-bearing model further verified its highly efficient anti-tumor performance through the induction of PDT and ferroptosis, as well as therapeutic safety. Thus, SNCPS acts as a ferroptosis-enhanced phototherapeutic nanoprobe, demonstrating reliable monitoring capability and therapeutic effectiveness, and holds great potential for biomedical applications.
