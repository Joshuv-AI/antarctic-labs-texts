Interactions between cytochrome P450 enzymes and traditional Chinese herbs: A comprehensive review
Authors: Ying-Hsueh Hsieh, Sheng-Teng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes, predominantly expressed in the liver and intestine, are central to phase I drug metabolism. The CYP1, CYP2, and particularly CYP3 families account for most oxidative biotransformation and nearly half of all drug elimination. Their activities are modulated by genetic polymorphisms, environmental factors, and drug interactions, contributing to interindividual variability in drug efficacy, safety, and susceptibility to drug-induced liver injury. This study systematically evaluated the regulatory effects of commonly used Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and Western herbal medicines on major CYP isoforms. PubMed and Google Scholar were searched for studies published between January 2000 and March 2025, with UpToDate used as a supplementary resource. Eligible evidence included in vitro assays, in vivo animal studies, human pharmacokinetic investigations, and well-documented case reports assessing herbal effects on CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP1A2. Evidence was graded by strength and integrated with mechanistic and pharmacokinetic data for interaction risk assessment. Herbal candidates included Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi, 黃耆), Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen, 丹參), Ginkgo biloba (Ginkgo, 銀杏), Bupleurum chinense (Chaihu, 柴胡), and Hypericum perforatum (St. John’s wort, 聖約翰草). Many herbs demonstrated clinically relevant induction or inhibition of CYP activity, particularly CYP3A4, with the potential to alter drug exposure, reduce therapeutic efficacy, or increase toxicity. Collectively, these findings underscore the imperative for systematic assessment of CYP-mediated herb–drug interactions in integrative medicine and support the development of safer, personalized therapeutic strategies grounded in a refined understanding of metabolic variability.

1. INTRODUCTION

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), with a history spanning millennia, is currently integrated into the healthcare systems of over 140 countries. Supported by more than 300 000 practitioners globally, TCM is extensively utilized in East Asia; in regions such as Taiwan, Hong Kong, Japan, and South Korea, it serves an estimated 60% to 75% of the population.1 Its global relevance is further underscored by its inclusion in the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-11. Numerous widely used herbs, such as Angelica sinensis (Dong quai, 當歸), Ligusticum chuanxiong (Chuanxiong, 川芎), and Rheum palmatum (Dahuang, 大黃), have been shown to modulate cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme activity, potentially leading to significant herb–drug interactions (HDIs). Preclinical investigations, particularly in animal models, indicate that extracts from Dong quai and Dahuang can modulate key CYP isoforms, such as CYP2D6 and CYP3A. These findings suggest a substantial risk of HDIs involving substrates of these enzymes.2 Collectively, these findings underscore the necessity of elucidating CYP-mediated mechanisms in both conventional and traditional therapies to ensure clinical safety and therapeutic efficacy.

1.1. Overview of CYP enzymes

The safety and efficacy of pharmacotherapy are largely determined by the metabolic activity of CYP enzymes, which mediate the biotransformation of the majority of clinically used drugs.3,4 Importantly, five major isoforms (CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP1A2) collectively account for approximately 90% of drug metabolism.5 CYP activity is highly susceptible to modulation by exogenous factors, particularly herbal constituents, which may lead to clinically significant HDIs.4 Such interactions can alter pharmacokinetic profiles, resulting in subtherapeutic drug exposure or, conversely, increased risk of toxicity.6 Therefore, systematic evaluation of CYP-mediated HDIs is essential to ensure patient safety and optimize therapeutic outcomes, particularly in patients receiving complex pharmacotherapeutic regimens.4,6

1.2. Overview of TCM

1.2.1. Global and regional prevalence

Herbal medicine is widely regarded as one of the most commonly utilized forms of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), with a significant portion of these herbal remedies derived from TCM. The adoption of and interest in CAM therapies have increased substantially, particularly in Western societies. Various socioeconomic, demographic, and health-related factors appear to influence the prevalence of CAM usage across different countries. Data from 2002, 2007, and 2012 revealed that the percentages of American adults who reported using any form of CAM in the previous year were 32.3%, 35.5%, and 33.2%, respectively.7 Moreover, the prevalence of CAM use varies significantly across nations. In European countries such as France and Germany, which exhibit relatively higher rates, the percentages reached 49% and 46%,8 respectively, while in Australia, the figure was approximately 52%,9 and in Singapore, nearly 76%.10 Although these data were collected at different time points, they collectively suggest substantial regional variation in CAM utilization worldwide.

1.2.2. Increasing popularity and integration with Western medicine

Several herbs used in TCM have also been applied in Western medicine. For example, Panax ginseng (Ginseng, 人參) decoction, commonly used in TCM to invigorate “Qi” and prevent exhaustion, has been prescribed for hemorrhagic or cardiogenic shock.11 Artemisia annua L. (Qing Hao,青蒿) gained prominence in the West after Tu Youyou’s discovery of artemisinin, for which she won the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The research culminated in the isolation of artemisinin and its more potent derivative, dihydroartemisinin, both of which demonstrate high antimalarial efficacy, rapid onset of action, and favorable safety profiles. In recent decades, the number of scientific articles on Chinese herbs published in international journals has increased significantly, reflecting growing global academic interest in TCM.12 An analysis of PubMed reveals a rising trend in publications on these herbs, supporting the integration of TCM into Western healthcare systems.13

1.2.3. Clinical relevance of CYP-mediated interactions involving Chinese herbs

CYP-mediated interactions involving Chinese herbs have demonstrated significant clinical relevance, particularly in vulnerable in older adults and patients with cancer. Certain herbs, including Hypericum perforatum (St. John’s wort, 聖約翰草), have been shown to induce CYP3A4 and CYP2E1 activity, potentially reducing the plasma concentrations of co-administered drugs and diminishing therapeutic efficacy, whereas others, such as garlic oil, may inhibit CYP2E1, thereby increasing the risk of toxicity through reduced drug clearance.14 Case reports have documented serious adverse outcomes associated with herb–drug combinations, including intracranial hemorrhage associated with the concomitant use of Ginseng and warfarin, hepatotoxicity associated with Ginseng and imatinib, and therapeutic failure associated with the co-administration of St. John’s wort and immunosuppressants.15 Despite these reports, widely used drug interaction databases (eg, Lexicomp and Medscape) demonstrate poor concordance with published case reports, with Cohen’s kappa values approaching zero, underscoring the need for improved clinical tools and education to identify and manage HDIs.16 These findings highlight the importance of integrating phytovigilance into routine clinical practice, particularly for patients receiving medications with narrow therapeutic indices.

2. CYP ENZYMES: GENERAL OVERVIEW

2.1. Structural and functional characteristics

Approximately four decades ago, Nebert17 proposed the classification system for CYP enzymes, a superfamily of heme-containing monooxygenases essential for phase I biotransformation.3,4,18 In humans, 57 functional CYP genes have been identified and are organized into families (eg, CYP2) and subfamilies (eg, CYP2D) based on sequence homology; proteins within the same family typically share >40% amino acid identity.19–21 These enzymes are primarily localized to the cytoplasmic side of the endoplasmic reticulum (microsomal type) or, to a lesser extent, within the mitochondria.4

Structurally, CYP enzymes share a conserved heme–iron catalytic center and a common tertiary architecture composed of 12 α-helices and four β-sheets. Flexible regions, including the F, G, and B′ helices, confer conformational adaptability, allowing the active site to accommodate structurally diverse substrates.3,4

2.2. Major CYP enzymes involved in drug metabolism

The liver and small intestine serve as the primary anatomical sites for CYP-mediated biotransformation, a process that substantially influences the efficacy, safety, and bioavailability of xenobiotics.4 Although the human genome encodes 57 functional isoforms, the CYP1, CYP2, and CYP3 families dominate clinical pharmacology, collectively accounting for the metabolism of 80% to 90% of all pharmaceuticals.3,4,22 As illustrated in Fig. 1, nearly 90% of CYP-related drug clearance is mediated by five key isoenzymes: CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, and CYP1A2.5

2.3. Factors influencing CYP enzyme activity

2.3.1. Genetics (polymorphisms)

Genetic variations within the CYP1, CYP2, and CYP3 families critically influence enzyme expression and catalytic activity. Clinically significant polymorphisms in CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4/5 produce poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultrarapid metabolizer phenotypes, thereby contributing to interindividual variability in drug response.29 For instance, loss-of-function CYP2C19 alleles (*2, *3), prevalent in Asian populations, reduce the efficacy of antiplatelet agents such as clopidogrel, while CYP2D6 variants alter the metabolism of many antidepressants and analgesics. 2.3.2. Environmental and nuclear receptor regulation

Environmental pollutants, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) from cigarette smoke, can induce enzymes like CYP1A1 and CYP1A2 through the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR).30 Other nuclear receptors, including the pregnane X receptor (PXR) and the constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), mediate the transcriptional regulation of CYP2C9, CYP2B6, and CYP3A4 in response to xenobiotic stimuli, highlighting the high inducibility of the CYP system.30

2.3.3. Dietary and herbal influences

Dietary constituents and herbal products can markedly modulate CYP-mediated metabolism, thereby increasing the risk of clinically significant HDIs.6 For example, St. John’s wort induces CYP3A4, reducing drug efficacy, whereas cranberry (蔓越莓), pomegranate (石榴), and goji berry (枸杞) juices can inhibit CYP3A4 and CYP2C9, increasing the risk of toxicity associated with substrates like warfarin or statins.30 In oncology, where many agents have narrow therapeutic windows, such interactions are particularly hazardous.6 Monitoring these influences is essential to prevent therapeutic failure or severe toxicity (summarized in Table 3).6,30

3. TRADITIONAL CHINESE HERBS AND THEIR PHARMACOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE

3.1. Brief overview of Chinese herbal medicine

Chinese herbal medicine, a key component of TCM, has been practiced for thousands of years. It emphasizes holistic and individualized treatment, using natural substances derived primarily from plants—such as roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits—as well as animal and mineral products. Unlike Western medicine, which typically focuses on standardized dosing and symptom-specific treatment, Chinese herbal therapy is based on syndrome differentiation and aims to restore internal balance.

3.2. Commonly used herbs with pharmacological significance

Several Chinese herbs are now used globally in both traditional and integrative healthcare settings. Selected illustrative examples are provided in the following paragraphs.

3.2.1. Ginseng

Ginseng, a member of the Araliaceae family, is a widely used medicinal herb with significant pharmacological value. The dried roots and rhizomes of Ginseng have been utilized in TCM for centuries and are classified as a “superior” herb in the ancient Chinese medical text Shen Nong’s Herbal Classic, where it was traditionally credited with functions such as “calming the spirit, replenishing Qi, and promoting longevity.” Modern pharmacological studies have demonstrated that Ginseng possesses various bioactive properties, including anti-fatigue, antioxidant, anti-aging, and immunomodulatory effects, making it a valuable therapeutic agent in both traditional and contemporary medical practices.31

3.2.2. St. John’s wort

St. John’s wort is one of the top 10 best-selling herbs in the United States. It is commonly used for a wide range of conditions, such as depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, the common cold, herpes, and HIV. In addition to oral use, it is also applied topically as an analgesic and even used as an enema for ulcerative colitis. However, despite its diverse therapeutic applications, St. John’s wort is a potent inducer of the CYP enzyme system, particularly CYP3A4, which plays a major role in the metabolism of many prescription drugs.33,34

3.2.3. Danshen

Danshen exhibits a wide range of pharmacological activities, with particularly prominent effects in cardiovascular protection. Its major bioactive constituents include lipophilic tanshinones (such as tanshinone IIA, cryptotanshinone, and dihydrotanshinone I) and hydrophilic phenolic acids (such as salvianolic acid A, salvianolic acid B, and danshensu), which work synergistically to exert antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, inhibition of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) oxidation, vasodilatory, endothelial-protective, and antiplatelet aggregation effects.35 These mechanisms help mitigate pathological processes such as atherosclerosis and myocardial ischemia. In addition, Danshen also demonstrates anti-arrhythmic, anti-cardiac hypertrophic, and anti-fibrotic effects, indicating its broad therapeutic potential in treating coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, and cerebrovascular disorders.

3.2.4. Glycyrrhiza uralensis (Gan Cao, 甘草)

Gan Cao (along with G. glabra and G. inflata) is a widely used herb in TCM for tonifying the spleen and Qi, clearing heat, detoxifying, relieving pain and muscle spasms, resolving phlegm, and harmonizing other herbs in formulas.36 Its roots contain bioactive compounds including triterpenoid saponins (mainly glycyrrhizic acid), flavonoids, polysaccharides, and coumarins, which underlie its diverse pharmacological effects.37 Glycyrrhizic acid exhibits anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antitumor, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective activities, as well as corticosteroid-like effects via inhibition of 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2.38 Flavonoids such as liquiritin and isoliquiritigenin, together with polysaccharides, demonstrate antispasmodic, antitussive, anti-allergic, and immunomodulatory effects.39 Evidence supports its therapeutic potential in chronic hepatitis, peptic ulcers, upper respiratory tract infections, asthma, and certain cardiovascular conditions.40

3.2.5. Astragalus membranaceus (Huangqi, 黃耆)

Huangqi is a well-established Qi-tonifying herb in TCM, first recorded in the Shennong’s Classic of Materia Medica (Shennong Bencao Jing,《神農本草經》) as a “superior” medicinal substance. It is widely used in TCM formulations to enhance immune function, regulate metabolic and respiratory systems, and support general health. Modern pharmacological studies have identified key active constituents, including polysaccharides, flavonoids, and triterpenoid saponins such as Astragaloside IV. These components have demonstrated multiple biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and anti-aging effects. Huangqi has been shown to promote telomerase activity, reduce oxidative stress, improve mitochondrial function, and regulate both innate and adaptive immune responses, contributing to its efficacy in aging-related and chronic disease conditions.41

3.3. Importance of understanding HDIs to ensure patient safety

HDIs pose significant patient safety risks by modulating CYP-mediated phase I drug metabolism. For example, St. John’s wort induces CYP3A4 and can substantially reduce the efficacy of co-administered medications, whereas other herbs may inhibit CYP activity and increase the risk of drug toxicity. Clinically, HDIs have caused bleeding with warfarin, reduced tacrolimus effects, and elevated statin levels. These interactions can occur at low doses and are influenced by CYP-related genetic polymorphisms. Patients on multiple or narrow therapeutic-index drugs are particularly vulnerable. Increased clinical vigilance and patient education are critical to prevent avoidable adverse events.

4. MECHANISMS OF CYP–HERB INTERACTIONS

4.1. Induction of CYP enzymes by Chinese herbs

Several Chinese herbal medicines have been reported to induce CYP enzymes through diverse mechanisms, although the strength of supporting evidence varies. Huangqi has shown potential to induce CYP3A4 based primarily on in vitro and animal studies, possibly via activation of the PXR.42 Danshen, which contains tanshinones, has demonstrated moderate inductive effects on CYP1A2, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4 through possible activation of PXR and the AhR.43 G. uralensis (Gan Cao, 甘草) may mildly induce CYP3A4 via flavonoid-mediated modulation of PXR, although evidence remains limited to in vitro data.44 In contrast, Ginkgo biloba (Gingko, 銀杏) demonstrates strong clinical evidence via the induction of CYP2C19 and CYP3A4, presumably through nuclear receptor activation.45,46 L. chuanxiong (Chuanxiong, 川芎) has been observed in animal models to induce CYP1A2 and CYP3A4, likely via enhanced gene transcription.47 Finally, B. chinense (Chaihu, 柴胡) shows the potential to induce CYP3A4 in vitro via saikosaponin-mediated activation of PXR, resulting in increased gene expression.48

4.2. Inhibition of CYP enzymes by Chinese herbs

A growing body of evidence indicates that many commonly used Chinese herbal medicines can inhibit CYP enzymes, potentially leading to clinically significant HDIs. Liu et al49 systematically evaluated the inhibitory effects of several traditional Chinese herbs on six major CYP isoforms (CYP1A2, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, 2E1, and 3A4) in vitro. Epimedii Folium (Yinyanghuo, 淫羊藿) showed strong, broad-spectrum inhibition across all six isoforms, with half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values ranging from 67.5 to 185.9 µg/mL, likely due to its flavonoid constituents such as quercetin.49 Other herbs, including Gan Cao, Leonuri herba (Yimucao, 益母草), and Danshen, exhibited mild to moderate inhibition, particularly against CYP1A2 and CYP2C subtypes.49 Additionally, Ashour et al reported potent inhibition of CYP3A4 by aqueous extracts of several traditional herbs, such as Andrographis paniculata (Chuanxinlian, 穿心蓮),50 Acacia catechu (Ercha, 烏樹皮), Areca catechu (Binglang, 檳榔), and Dysosma versipellis (Bā Jiǎo Lián, 八角蓮), with inhibition exceeding 85% at 100 μg/mL. The strong CYP3A4 inhibition observed was attributed to the presence of polyphenolic compounds, diterpenoids, lignans, and alkaloids within these herbs. Additionally, aqueous extracts of Chinese herbs generally exhibited stronger inhibitory activity than methanolic extracts, especially in those rich in polyphenols.51 These findings indicate the potential for TCM-derived preparations to alter the metabolism of coadministered drugs through CYP inhibition, particularly for drugs with narrow therapeutic windows or those metabolized predominantly by CYP3A4. 4.3. Overview of competitive and noncompetitive enzyme inhibition and associated metabolic interactions

CYP enzymes are key catalysts in phase I drug metabolism, and their inhibition is a major cause of HDIs (Fig. 2). Inhibition can be competitive, where herbal constituents compete with drugs for the active site, or noncompetitive, where they bind to allosteric sites, altering enzyme conformation and reducing activity. Competitive inhibition depends on relative affinity and concentration, while noncompetitive effects are not overcome by higher substrate levels and may be long-lasting. Certain flavonoids and alkaloids have been identified as potent noncompetitive inhibitors. Recognizing these mechanisms is essential for assessing interaction risks and guiding clinical management.52

A, Normal substrate binding to the enzyme’s active site; (B) competitive inhibition, where an inhibitor competes with the substrate for the active site; and (C) noncompetitive inhibition, where the inhibitor binds to an allosteric site, altering enzyme activity without blocking substrate binding.

4.4. Examples of bioactive constituents and their metabolic interactions (eg, ginsenosides, flavonoids, alkaloids)

Numerous herbal medicines contain bioactive constituents that may significantly interact with conventional drugs, altering pharmacokinetics and clinical outcomes. For example, Ginseng may reduce warfarin efficacy, enhance central nervous system (CNS) drug effects, and increase digoxin toxicity. Scutellaria baicalensis (Huangqin, 黃芩) inhibits CYP-mediated metabolism, increasing toxicity risks for drugs such as cyclosporine and midazolam. Grapefruit and related Citrus species inhibit CYP3A4, elevating simvastatin and nifedipine exposure, potentially causing myopathy or hypotension. Ginkgo may potentiate bleeding and reduce antiepileptic drug efficacy. Chuanxiong enhances anticoagulant effects of warfarin and aspirin. Coptidis Rhizoma (Huanglian, 黃連) impairs metoprolol and diazepam metabolism, risking bradycardia and sedation. Ephedra sinica (Mahuang, 麻黃) induces hepatic enzymes, reducing efficacy of drugs like theophylline and phenytoin while promoting CNS overstimulation. Tripterygium wilfordii (Leigongteng, 雷公藤) may exacerbate immunosuppressant toxicity. Danshen alters statin metabolism and increases bleeding risk with warfarin. St. John’s wort, a strong inducer of CYP enzymes and P-glycoprotein (P-gp), reduces plasma concentrations of cyclosporine, oral contraceptives, imatinib, and sertraline, risking treatment failure. These interactions underscore the importance of clinical vigilance and therapeutic monitoring in patients concurrently using herbal and conventional medications.

5. HERB-SPECIFIC ANALYSIS OF CYP-MEDIATED INTERACTIONS

5.1. Ginseng

5.1.1. Influence on CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 activities

Ginseng may modulate CYP3A4 activity in both inductive and inhibitory ways. In a clinical study, 28-day administration of Ginseng (500 mg twice daily) significantly reduced midazolam exposure, suggesting CYP3A4 induction, while P-gp activity remained unaffected.53 In contrast, intact ginsenosides have minimal effects on CYP3A4 and CYP2D6, but their gut-derived metabolites—such as compound K, protopanaxadiol (PPD), and protopanaxatriol (PPT)—exhibit stronger inhibition of CYP3A4.54 Thus, Ginseng’s impact on drug metabolism may differ depending on its preparation and biotransformation, indicating the need for caution with CYP3A4-metabolized drugs.

5.1.2. Clinical studies demonstrating interactions

Ginseng may inhibit CYP3A4, the primary enzyme metabolizing imatinib and other clinically important drugs. A clinical case reported acute hepatotoxicity in a chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML) patient consuming a Ginseng-containing energy drink during stable imatinib therapy, with both the adverse reaction and HDI deemed “probable” by Naranjo and Horn scales.55 In vitro, ginsenosides and metabolites (compound K, PPD, PPT) inhibit CYP3A4, and Ginseng has been shown to alter the pharmacokinetics of midazolam, nifedipine, and warfarin.56,57 Clinically reported interactions are limited and sometimes contradictory, though coadministration with warfarin or statins has been associated with bleeding or potential hepatotoxicity, possibly via CYP3A4 or OATP1B1 inhibition.57 Given its effects on narrow therapeutic index drugs such as cyclosporine, tacrolimus, and amlodipine, careful monitoring is warranted during coadministration.

5.2. St. John’s wort

5.2.1. Potent induction of CYP3A4 and influence on therapeutic drugs

St. John’s wort contains hyperforin, a major active constituent that significantly modulates CYP enzymes, particularly CYP3A4. Hyperforin activates the PXR, leading to upregulation of CYP3A4 and P-gp, thereby enhancing drug metabolism and reducing plasma drug levels.58,59 High-hyperforin extracts have been linked to substantial reductions in concentrations of drugs such as cyclosporine, sometimes resulting in transplant rejection.60 In contrast, low-hyperforin products (<1%) exhibit minimal enzyme induction and a lower risk of interaction.61 Hyperforin may also inhibit other CYP isoforms like CYP2C9 and CYP2D6, though such effects are weaker and less predictable.62,63 Thus, hyperforin content is a key determinant of St. John’s wort’s potential for clinically significant HDIs.

5.2.2. Impact on therapeutic drugs (eg, oral contraceptives, anticoagulants)

St. John’s wort is a potent inducer of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and P-gp, leading to enhanced metabolism and reduced plasma concentrations of numerous drugs. This can compromise therapeutic efficacy across multiple drug classes. For example, it reduces exposure to immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine and tacrolimus, increasing the risk of transplant rejection. It also diminishes the effects of warfarin and rivaroxaban, raising thromboembolic risk, and lowers systemic hormone levels from oral contraceptives, potentially causing contraceptive failure.64,65 Antiretrovirals like indinavir and nevirapine may lose efficacy, risking viral breakthrough,66 while tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as imatinib may be rendered less effective, contributing to disease progression.67 Psychotropics, including benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), and antipsychotics, can be unpredictably affected, with risks such as serotonin syndrome.68 Digoxin pharmacokinetics may also be altered, compromising cardiac control69 and the effectiveness of CYP3A4 substrates like omeprazole and oxycodone may be impaired.70 Given these broad and clinically significant interactions, concomitant use of St. John’s wort with conventional medications should be avoided or closely supervised.

5.3. Danshen

5.3.1. Interaction with CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 related to clinical risk with anticoagulant therapy

Danshen modulates CYP enzymes, particularly CYP2C9 and CYP3A4, both of which are important in the metabolism of anticoagulant drugs. Tanshinones, its lipophilic constituents, inhibit these enzymes, potentially reducing drug clearance, elevating plasma concentrations, and increasing bleeding risk. In contrast, salvianolic acid B, a hydrophilic component, may induce the same enzymes, potentially diminishing anticoagulant efficacy, contributing to interindividual variability in clinical outcomes.71 Danshen also affects drug transporters such as P-gp, enhancing dabigatran absorption and systemic exposure, thereby increasing hemorrhagic risk.72 For factor Xa inhibitors like rivaroxaban and apixaban, combined CYP3A4 and/or P-gp inhibition may further impair drug clearance.72 Additionally, CYP2C19 inhibition by tanshinones could reduce clopidogrel activation, compromising its antiplatelet effect and heightening thrombosis risk.72 These interactions, largely derived from preclinical CYP data, highlight the need for close monitoring and potential dosage adjustments when Danshen is coadministered with anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents.

5.4. Gan Cao

5.4.1. Effects on CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 activities and the risk associated with chronic administration73747576

Gan Cao and its bioactive constituents exhibit both pharmacokinetic and endocrine-modulating effects with potential clinical implications. In vitro studies using human liver microsomes demonstrate that glycyrrhizin inhibits CYP enzymes in a dose-dependent manner, most prominently CYP2C9, followed by moderate inhibition of CYP3A4 and CYP2C19.73 Additional in vitro data confirm significant CYP3A4 inhibition by Gan Cao extract and glycyrrhizin, indicating a potential for clinically relevant HDIs.74 Beyond metabolism, Gan Cao components such as glabrene display phytoestrogenic activity in MCF-7 cells, while chronic intake in healthy females reduces serum testosterone, highlighting endocrine-modulating effects.75,76 Collectively, these findings underscore both pharmacokinetic and endocrine-related risks associated with long-term Gan Cao use, particularly in patients on CYP-metabolized drugs or with susceptibility to hormonal imbalances.

5.5. Huangqi

5.5.1. Limited data on CYP interactions and safety concerns

Huangqi, a widely used TCM herb, exhibits limited potential for CYP-mediated interactions. Clinical resources, including UpToDate, report insufficient in vivo evidence for significant modulation of major CYP isoforms such as CYP3A4 and CYP2C9.77 In vitro studies using pooled human liver microsomes demonstrated only weak inhibition of CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, and CYP3A4, with IC20 values of 0.45 to 0.66 mg/mL; effects were most notable for CYP2C9 and CYP3A4. These data suggest a low risk of clinically relevant HDIs, though caution is warranted under polypharmacy or high-dose administration.

5.6. Other commonly used herbs

5.6.1. Huangqin

Emerging data indicate that Huangqin can modulate CYP enzyme activity, posing potential pharmacokinetic HDIs. A human study by Yi et al reported that repeated oral administration of Huangqin extract reduced CYP2C9 activity to 71% of baseline and increased CYP2E1 activity by 1.42-fold, whereas CYP3A4, CYP1A2, CYP2C19, and CYP2D6 were largely unaffected.75 Preclinical evidence summarized by Zhou et al demonstrates that major flavonoids—baicalin, baicalein, and wogonin—can inhibit CYP3A4 and alter P-gp–mediated drug transport.76 These findings suggest that Huangqin may influence the metabolism of CYP2C9 or CYP2E1 substrates, particularly narrow therapeutic-index drugs, although clinical validation remains limited, warranting cautious coadministration.

5.6.2. Yinyanghuo

Yinyanghuo exhibits anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities and inhibits CYP3A4 in a dose-dependent manner (33.5%-98% inhibition at 31.25-500 μg/mL). Its flavonoid constituent kaempferol shows potent CYP3A4 inhibition (IC50 = 9.8 μg/mL) with direct active-site binding.78 In vitro, co-administration of Epimedium or kaempferol with dexamethasone enhanced suppression of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-8 (IL-8), suggesting a synergistic effect likely mediated by reduced CYP3A4-dependent metabolism and prolonged steroid exposure.78 This interaction may allow improved efficacy at lower corticosteroid doses in short-term or localized therapy; however, concomitant use with other CYP3A4 substrates or prolonged administration may increase the risk of drug accumulation and toxicity. Further clinical studies are warranted to define its safety and therapeutic relevance.

5.6.3. Polygonum multiflorum (Heshouwu, 何首烏)

Heshouwu is a traditional Chinese medicinal herb used for anti-aging and liver-nourishing purposes, but growing evidence highlights safety concerns, particularly hepatotoxicity and potential interactions with conventional drugs. Components of Heshouwu have been reported to inhibit CYP enzymes, markedly CYP1A2, CYP2E1, and CYP3A4.79 Given the central role of CYP3A4 in the metabolism of many clinically used agents, such inhibition may increase systemic drug exposure and the risk of adverse effects, especially in polypharmacy settings. Although these effects are mainly derived from in vitro studies and lack definitive in vivo confirmation, they warrant clinical caution, particularly in patients receiving hepatically metabolized drugs or with underlying liver dysfunction.

5.6.4. Schisandra chinensis (Wuweizi, 五味子)

Wuweizi, a traditional herbal medicine known for its liver-protective, antioxidant, and immune-enhancing effects, significantly increases tacrolimus blood levels by inhibiting CYP3A enzymes and P-gp, as shown in animal studies and summarized in reviews.80,81 Rats treated with Wuweizi extract showed a 6- to 13-fold rise in tacrolimus area under the curve (AUC) and about a 10-fold increase in peak concentration, indicating reduced clearance. Although human trials are limited, consistent pharmacokinetic data provide moderate-to-high evidence for this interaction.82,83 Clinically, Wuweizi may improve tacrolimus bioavailability and permit dose reduction; however, it may also increase the risk of toxicity, making therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) essential.

5.7. Impact of common Western herbal medicines on CYP-mediated drug metabolism

5.7.1. Commiphora myrrha resin extract (沒藥樹脂萃取物)

Myrrh, an oleo-gum resin from Commiphora myrrha, traditionally used in Unani, Ayurvedic, and Chinese medicine, is employed for infections, asthma, arthritis, and metabolic disorders. Myrrh extract may induce CYP2C9 via PXR activation, increasing clearance of CYP2C9 substrates such as warfarin and phenytoin, potentially reducing drug efficacy and requiring careful TDM.84

5.7.2. Vernonia amygdalina aqueous leaf extract (苦參葉水萃取物)

Vernonia amygdalina (苦參), a perennial African herb, is traditionally used for nutrition and medicine. In diabetic rats, coadministration with metformin increased systemic exposure (AUC +40%, maximum plasma concentration [Cmax ] +27%) and reduced clearance (−36%). In vitro, it dose-dependently inhibited CYP3A4 and CYP2C9, suggesting a potential risk for HDIs via impaired metformin metabolism.85

5.7.3. Matricaria chamomilla (German chamomile, 德國洋甘菊) extract

German chamomile, commonly used for gastrointestinal and sleep disorders, contains flower-derived bioactives. In mice, chamomile consumption transcriptionally induced hepatic CYP3A11 and CYP1A2 via increased nuclear BMAL1, disrupting CYP3A11 circadian rhythm. These tissue-specific effects highlight potential chronopharmacological effects on CYP-mediated drug metabolism.86

5.7.4. Berberis vulgaris (刺檗) and its active constituent berberine

Berberine, present in plants like goldenseal and barberry, exhibits anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects. It consistently inhibits CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 in human liver microsomes and clinical studies, representing the main clinically relevant risk for HDIs with berberine-containing products.87

5.7.5. Withania somnifera (Ashwagandha, 睡茄) root and leaf extracts

Ashwagandha, used in Ayurveda and Unani medicine, contains withanolides, flavonoids, and alkaloids. High-throughput liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) testing showed no significant inhibition of seven major CYP enzymes (IC50 >100 µg/mL), suggesting a low potential for CYP-mediated HDIs.

5.8. Comparative characteristics of TCM and Western herbs in CYP modulation

TCM emphasizes a holistic, systems-based approach and is characterized by the use of multicomponent formulations with synergistic effects. The diverse phytochemicals present in TCM formulas may modulate multiple CYP enzymes simultaneously, resulting in broad but often relatively modest effects on drug metabolism. In contrast, Western herbal products are typically marketed as single-herb standardized extracts containing well-characterized active constituents, which may produce more specific and predictable effects on CYP activity and are often supported by stronger clinical evidence. Understanding the differences in CYP modulation between TCM and Western herbal medicines is essential for accurate risk assessment, the prevention of HDIs, and the safe integration of herbal and conventional therapies.

6. CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND CASE STUDIES

6.1. Documented clinical cases of CYP-mediated HDIs

St. John’s wort strongly induces CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and P-gp, reducing drug levels of cyclosporine, antiretrovirals, oral contraceptives, midazolam, and irinotecan—leading to transplant rejection, treatment failure, and decreased efficacy.65–70 Ginkgo, with antiplatelet effects associated with possible CYP interaction, has been linked to intracranial bleeding when combined with aspirin, warfarin, or caffeine.46,47 Ginseng may enhance warfarin’s bleeding risk, though its CYP mechanism is unclear.56,57 Silybum marianum (milk thistle, 水飛薊) mildly inhibits CYP3A4, reducing indinavir exposure. Gan Cao induces CYP3A4 and P-gp, decreasing cyclosporine bioavailability in animal studies.73,74 In contrast, Wuweizi inhibits CYP3A and P-gp, increasing tacrolimus levels and toxicity risk.80,81 These examples illustrate how herbal medicines can either induce or inhibit drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, leading to serious clinical consequences. Understanding HDIs is essential to prevent therapeutic failure and minimize drug-related toxicity, particularly in patients receiving immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, antivirals, or anticancer therapies.

6.2. Recommendations for clinicians in clinical practice

Clinicians should conduct comprehensive medication reconciliation encompassing prescription, over-the-counter, and herbal products, with particular attention to patients receiving polypharmacy or medications with narrow therapeutic indices. Proficiency in recognizing herbs known to modulate CYP enzymes and drug transporters—such as St. John’s wort, Ginkgo, Ginseng, Gan Cao, and Wuweizi—is imperative. Upon suspicion of clinically significant HDIs involving critical agents such as cyclosporine, tacrolimus, anticoagulants, or antiretrovirals, implementation of TDM is recommended to reduce the risk of subtherapeutic exposure or toxicity. Patient counseling regarding the potential risks associated with unsupervised herbal medicine use is essential, and the use of traditional medicines should be managed by qualified healthcare professionals. Integration of evidence-based HDI data into clinical decision-making optimizes patient safety and therapeutic efficacy.

7. ANALYTICAL TECHNIQUES AND METHODOLOGICAL APPROACHES

7.1. Overview of methods for evaluating CYP–herb interactions

7.1.1. In vitro assays (microsomal incubation, hepatocyte assays)

In vitro methods are fundamental for evaluating herb–CYP interactions. For example, Wang et al demonstrated that ethanol extracts of Wuweizi significantly inhibited CYP3A-mediated metabolism in rats, likely due to lignan compounds such as schisandrin.82 Common in vitro approaches include liver microsomal incubations and primary hepatocyte assays. Microsomal assays typically involve the co-incubation of herbal extracts with probe substrates (eg, midazolam for CYP3A4) and measuring metabolite formation to assess inhibitory or inductive effects.84 Primary human hepatocytes are also used to evaluate CYP enzyme induction at the transcriptional level (quantified using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction [RT-PCR]) and at the protein or functional level (quantified using LC-MS/MS or specific activity assays).

7.1.2. In vivo studies (animal models, clinical trials)

In vivo approaches include both animal models and human clinical trials. Animal studies often use rats pretreated with herbal extracts (eg, Wuweizi) for 7 days, followed by administration of a CYP-metabolized drug such as tacrolimus. In a pharmacokinetic study, pretreatment with Wuweizi led to a 6- to 13-fold increase in tacrolimus AUC and nearly a 10-fold rise in Cmax, indicating significant CYP3A and P-gp inhibition.81 Clinical trials often adopt a crossover design in healthy volunteers, comparing pharmacokinetic parameters such as AUC, Cmax, and clearance before and after herbal intervention. These studies are essential for assessing clinical relevance, particularly for drugs with narrow therapeutic indices.

7.2. Challenges and limitations in current methodologies

Despite their utility, both in vitro and in vivo approaches have limitations. In vitro findings do not always predict in vivo outcomes due to differences in the absorption, metabolism, and bioavailability of herbal constituents. Variability in herbal composition, dosage, and formulation further complicates reproducibility, and most studies examine isolated herb–drug pairs rather than complex polypharmacy. Regulatory agencies such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) recommend standardized methodologies and the integration of physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models to improve translational predictability. PBPK models use mathematical simulations to predict drug pharmacokinetics, translating in vitro CYP inhibition and inactivation data into quantitative in vivo predictions of HDIs, and estimating absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) processes across populations defined by genetics, life stage, or disease states.89 However, current regulatory submissions primarily focus on drug–drug interactions, with limited attention to susceptible subpopulations. Emerging approaches integrate artificial intelligence (AI) with PBPK modeling to improve the prediction of HDIs and pharmacokinetic variability during drug development.90,91

8. REGULATORY CONSIDERATIONS AND GUIDELINES

8.1. Current guidelines from global regulatory agencies

In recent years, key regulatory bodies have updated their guidelines to address the complexities of HDI assessment. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) adopted the ICH M12 “Guideline on Drug–Drug Interaction Studies” in November 2024, harmonizing in vitro and clinical study design principles and emphasizing integrated evaluation of CYP enzymes and transporters such as P-gp and OATP1B1/1B3 to improve translational predictability from nonclinical to clinical settings.91 The guideline provides a unified global framework for evaluating CYP-mediated pharmacokinetic DDIs, standardizing assessment across key isoforms (CYP1A2, 2B6, 2C8/9/19, 2D6, 3A).92 It refines vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) criteria by allowing the use of reliably measured unbound plasma fractions (fu,p) for highly protein-bound drugs (>99%), mitigating false-positive predictions.92 The guideline further refines mechanistic modeling through adjusted scaling factors: the time-dependent inhibition factor is reduced from 50× to 5× maximum unbound plasma concentration (Cmax,u) in static models, while the CYP induction factor is increased to 50× to address false negatives associated with low fu,p.92 This framework explicitly endorses Mechanistic Static (MSM) and PBPK models for the quantitative prediction of complex interactions.92 The EMA issued Revision 1 of its “Guideline on the Investigation of Drug Interactions” in 2019, mandating that new drug applications and product labeling include systematic, mechanism-based assessments of herbal preparations’ effects on CYP isoforms and transport proteins, along with corresponding risk-management recommendations.93 In China, the National Medical Products Administration’s Center for Drug Evaluation (NMPA-CDE) released its “Technical Guidelines for Drug Interaction Studies (Trial)” in January 2021, prescribing combined in vitro–in vivo strategies for key enzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP3A4) and calling for rigorous standardization of herbal formulations to ensure data reliability and comparability.94

8.2. Recommendations for herbal medicine labeling and drug interaction warning

Herbal product labels should clearly identify the scientific and common names of the herb, the plant part used, the extraction method, and the content of key active constituents or marker compounds, and include CYP-related drug interaction warnings and high-risk combinations. For drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (eg, tacrolimus, cyclosporine), contraindications and dose adjustment guidance should be provided, with a reminder to consult a healthcare professional when coadministering critical medications. Where feasible, labels may include a QR code or URL linking to detailed interaction information and safety updates, and display the batch number, expiration date, and evidence of compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards to ensure traceability and quality.

9. FUTURE PERSPECTIVES AND RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

9.1. Identification of research gaps and unanswered questions

To date, most investigations of HDIs have been limited to single-herb-single-drug paradigms, leaving the combined effects of multicomponent botanical formulations and polypharmacy largely unexplored. Moreover, the identities, biotransformation pathways, and transporter interactions of bioactive herbal metabolites generated in vivo remain poorly characterized. Although several CYP polymorphisms have been shown to influence enzyme responses to herbal constituents, gene–environment interactions across populations stratified by age, sex, and disease state have not been systematically investigated. Addressing these gaps will require comprehensive evaluation of complex herbal formulations in polypharmacy settings, detailed characterization of active metabolites and transporter involvement, and robust phenotyping of CYP-mediated variability in diverse patient populations.

9.2. Potential for personalized medicine approaches (genetic polymorphisms)

Genetic polymorphisms, particularly CYP3A5, critically influence HDIs and drug pharmacokinetics. Genotype-guided dosing can reduce variability and improve outcomes, as shown for tacrolimus.95 Applying these principles to herbal medicine, the integration of pharmacogenetic testing with TCM constitution-based diagnostics may facilitate patient stratification, individualized herb selection, and dosing, mitigating interaction risks while preserving efficacy, and thereby bridging precision medicine and traditional medical practice.

9.3. Integrative strategies for the management of HDIs

Effective management of HDIs requires an evidence-based, multidisciplinary approach incorporating pharmacogenomics, CYP modulation mechanisms, and clinical monitoring. CYP3A5 polymorphisms affect drug metabolism; for example, CYP3A5 expressers generally require higher tacrolimus doses, with CYP3A inhibitors like Wuweizi potentially increasing toxicity. Herbs such as St. John’s wort induce CYP3A4 and P-gp, lowering drug levels, while Gan Cao, Ginseng, and Danshen exhibit variable CYP effects. Interprofessional collaboration, TDM, and patient education are essential for the personalized and safe management of HDIs.

10. DISCUSSION

CYP-mediated HDIs remain an under-recognized yet clinically significant concern. Although in vitro studies suggest that certain herbs may modulate CYP activity, the clinical relevance of these findings has yet to be fully established because of variability in herbal composition, extraction methods, dosage, and pharmacokinetic characteristics. Furthermore, standardized reporting frameworks, phytovigilance systems, and integrated clinical decision-support tools remain insufficiently developed, limiting the safe and effective clinical implementation of integrative therapies.

To support clinical practice, the Chinese medicinal herbs evaluated in this review are summarized and stratified according to their potential interaction risk in Table 7. High-risk herbs are characterized by clinically relevant CYP inhibition or induction demonstrated in clinical studies or supported by robust preclinical evidence, particularly when interacting with drugs that possess narrow therapeutic indices. Conversely, moderate- and low-risk herbs are supported by limited, inconsistent, or predominantly preclinical evidence.

Emerging approaches, including physiologically based PBPK modeling, pharmacogenomics-guided prediction, AI-assisted risk assessment, and high-throughput metabolite screening, may improve the prediction and management of HDIs and support safer integrative therapeutic strategies. The risk stratification framework presented in Table 7 may serve as a practical reference for healthcare professionals to identify potential interaction hazards and optimize therapeutic outcomes in patients receiving integrated Chinese and Western medical therapies.

11. CONCLUSION

11.1. Summary of key findings and clinical relevance

This comprehensive review highlights that traditional Chinese herbs can exert diverse modulatory effects on CYP enzymes, particularly CYP3A4, CYP2C19, and CYP1A2. These interactions may result in clinically significant alterations in the pharmacokinetics of concomitantly administered medications. Herb-induced inhibition may increase systemic drug exposure and the risk of adverse drug reactions, whereas enzyme induction may accelerate drug metabolism and reduce therapeutic efficacy. Notably, herbs such as Huangqin, Danshen, and Huangqi have demonstrated these interactions in both in vitro and in vivo studies. Additionally, pharmacodynamic interactions, including synergistic and antagonistic effects, have been reported. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of carefully evaluating potential HDIs in clinical practice, particularly among patients exposed to polypharmacy or long-term herbal medicine use.

11.2. Importance of ongoing research for public safety and clinical efficacy

Herb–CYP interactions are increasingly recognized, yet much of the current evidence is derived from in vitro and animal studies, with relatively limited clinical validation. Complex herbal formulations and substantial interindividual variability in genetic, physiological, and environmental factors continue to complicate the prediction and monitoring of HDIs. Furthermore, the lack of standardized databases and validated assessment tools remains a barrier to the safe integration of herbal medicines into routine clinical practice. Continued high-quality research, including human pharmacokinetic studies, PBPK modeling, pharmacogenomics, and real-world clinical investigations, is essential to improve the safety and efficacy of integrative medicine. The proposed clinical algorithm for assessing CYP-mediated HDIs (Fig. 3) may provide a practical framework for risk stratification and the safe co-administration of herbal and conventional therapies through evidence-based evaluation and clinical monitoring.
