Phase II study of olaparib and durvalumab in patients with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPoly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibition (PARPi) is a precision medicine strategy in advanced prostate cancer, with the greatest benefit seen in a subset of patients with homologous recombination repair (HRR) gene alterations. Combination approaches may expand activity beyond HRR-altered disease. We conducted a phase 2 study of the PARP inhibitor olaparib in combination with the anti-PD-L1 antibody durvalumab in an HRR-unselected population of men with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC).MethodsThis is a single-arm, open-label phase 2 trial of olaparib plus durvalumab in men with mCRPC previously treated with abiraterone and/or enzalutamide. Pretreatment biopsies of metastatic lesions were attempted for tumor sequencing. Peripheral blood was collected longitudinally for immune profiling of cell subsets and soluble factors, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analyses, and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) gene expression profiling.Results61 patients were enrolled; 60 were evaluable. Median age was 65 years (45–88). Median radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) was 5.0 months (95% CI 4.6 to 7.6) and median overall survival (OS) was 19.1 months (95% CI 15.0 to 29.3 months). 10 patients achieved partial responses, and 17 (28%) had ≥50% prostate-specific antigen declines. Patients with BRCA2 variants experienced longer rPFS (13.2 months; 95% CI 7.7 to 20.2 months) than patients without BRCA2 variants (4.8 months; 95% CI 4.5 to 6.4 months, p=0.0026). Baseline ctDNA fraction was higher in patients with radiographic progression than in those with partial response (p=0.022) and inversely correlated with time-to-progression (ρ=−0.51). Treatment- induced early peripheral immune perturbations included transient inflammatory cytokine increases and expansion of activated/proliferating T cells with concurrent regulatory features. Exploratory PBMC profiling identified IL1R2 as a response-associated transcript, and lower IL1R2 expression was associated with longer OS.ConclusionsOlaparib plus durvalumab demonstrated modest activity in HRR-unselected mCRPC, with clinical benefit enriched in BRCA2-variant disease. Integrated ctDNA and peripheral immune analyses support ongoing efforts to refine molecular and immune selection strategies and to better define mechanisms of benefit and resistance for combination approaches in mCRPC.Trial registration numberNCT02484404.Keywords: Prostate Cancer, GENETICS, Immunotherapy, T regulatory cell - Treg

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in men in the USA, with an estimated 313 780 new cases and 35 770 estimated deaths in 2025.1 The treatment landscape for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) includes multiple next-generation hormonal agents including androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs),2,5 taxane chemotherapy,6 7 radioligand therapy,8 and radiopharmaceuticals.8 9 Despite these options, most deaths occur due to advanced disease, and median survival remains limited.10 In this setting, inherited and acquired defects in homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathways have emerged as an actionable precision medicine strategy in mCRPC.11 12 Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors (PARPi) demonstrate meaningful clinical benefit in patients with HRR alterations, particularly to BRCA1 or BRCA2, and olaparib and rucaparib are Food and Drug Administration approved for HRR-altered mCRPC.13,16 Somatic and/or germline variants in HRR genes are present in a substantial minority of patients with advanced prostate cancer,17 and PARPi combinations with ARPIs have improved radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS) in selected settings.18,20 However, clinical benefit from PARPi is most consistently enriched in limited pathogenic HRR subsets, and resistance to PARPi can arise through multiple mechanisms.21

For these reasons, PARP inhibitors are being studied in combination with immunotherapy to expand benefit beyond HRR-altered disease. Preclinical data suggest that PARP can activate DNA damage-associated innate immune sensing (including cGAS–STING) and type I interferon programs, with adaptive upregulation of PD-L1.22 23 In parallel, immune checkpoint blockade has been transformative in several solid tumors and has tumor-agnostic approvals in microsatellite instability-high or mismatch repair deficient disease,24 but has yet shown limited activity as monotherapy in unselected mCRPC.25 26 Although our current study was initiated before phase III results were available, subsequent randomized data in biomarker-unselected mCRPC have not demonstrated an overall survival (OS) improvement for pembrolizumab plus olaparib, underscoring the need for improved biomarker and mechanistic understanding.27

We therefore evaluated olaparib combined with durvalumab, a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting PD-L1, in a phase 2 study enrolling patients with mCRPC following progression on abiraterone and/or enzalutamide. We previously reported outcomes for the initial 17 patients28; here we report the clinical activity and associated molecular and peripheral immune correlates in the full evaluable cohort. The regimen was selected based on the phase 1 results of an initial study in ovarian cancer.29 This study aimed to describe clinical outcomes and explore genomic and peripheral immune correlates of response and resistance to combined PARP inhibition and PD-L1 blockade in mCRPC.

Patients and methods

Study design

Between May 2016 and June 2021, 60 patients previously treated with enzalutamide and/or abiraterone were enrolled at the National Cancer Institute, Center for Cancer Research, National Institutes of Health and treated with olaparib plus durvalumab. This was an investigator-initiated, single-center, open-label phase 2 study conducted at the National Cancer Institute under a Cooperative Research and Development Agreement with AstraZeneca. The primary objective was to determine clinical efficacy as determined by PFS. The secondary objectives were to determine overall response rate, safety, duration of response, and PSA response. Prespecified translational and exploratory objectives were to correlate changes in plasma cytokines and immune-related response criteria with clinical outcomes.

Durvalumab was administered at 1500 mg intravenously every 28 days in combination with olaparib tablets administered at a dose of 300 mg every 12 hours by mouth. Eligible patients had mCRPC and were post-treatment with abiraterone plus prednisone, enzalutamide, or both, with the exception of patients who were treated with docetaxel and androgen deprivation therapy for metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer and progressed on docetaxel treatment, or who progressed within one month of the last dose of docetaxel, with adequate end-organ function as defined by absolute neutrophil count ≥1500 /µL; white blood cell count (WBC) ≥3.0 x ×10⁹/L; platelets ≥100 x 10⁹/L; hemoglobin ≥90 g/L; aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ≤2.5×; bilirubin ≤1.5× upper limit of normal; creatinine ≤1.5× upper limit of normal. Exclusion criteria included prior PARPi therapy and radionuclide treatment within 6 weeks before the start of therapy. Patients were assessed for toxicity per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.4.03. Clinical response was assessed initially at 8 weeks and then every 12 weeks with CT imaging and Tc99 bone scan imaging via Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1 criteria. If one drug was discontinued due to toxicity, the other drug could be continued at the investigator’s discretion.

A baseline metastatic tumor biopsy was mandatory when a lesion was safely accessible, with an optional on-treatment biopsy (cycle 1 day 15) and an optional biopsy at progression. All patients underwent attempts for pretreatment biopsy of metastatic sites of disease except for 2 patients who were enrolled during the COVID-19 pandemic and non-essential services were disrupted.

Blood samples analyzed were also collected under Biospecimen Acquisition from Human Subjects (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00034216).

Statistical considerations for the clinical trial

Patients were enrolled in an expansion phase 2 evaluation using the recommended phase 2 dose to determine the clinical response rate to the drug combination in patients with prostate cancer who did not have a HRR variant. The primary endpoint was the 4-month rPFS rate. Initially, the study was designed to enroll 25 patients in order to have 80% power to determine whether there is a difference between a 50% 4-month PFS and an improved 70% 4-month PFS, with a one-sided 0.10 alpha level test, using the Brookmeyer and Crowley method in a preliminary cohort of patients.30 In the expansion cohort, if there were 45 patients without the HRR variants who received the combination treatment, 14 responses in 45 patients (31.1%) would be associated with a two-sided 90% exact CI of 19.9% to 44.3%, and 13 responses in 45 (28.9%) would be associated with a two-sided 90% exact CI of 18.0% to 42.0%. Patients who were included in the initial 25 patients with HRR variants could be included as part of this cohort.

Sample acquisition and processing

Germline and somatic tumor variant data available from prior genetic studies were acquired with the patient’s consent and entered into the patient’s medical record. All other patients provided a germline sample (saliva) and somatic sample (tissue biopsy). Germline material was acquired from all patients. Baseline metastatic biopsies were attempted from 58 out of 60 patients, and somatic genomic profiling was performed successfully in 45 out of 58 biopsies.

For in-house germline analyses, saliva was acquired and preserved using the Oragene-Discover (DNA Genotek, Cat# OGR-600) collection tubes. Saliva was preserved in storage buffer at −20°C until DNA was extracted. For in-house somatic analyses, CT-guided biopsies were acquired by an interventional radiologist to metastases of soft tissue. Bone biopsies were acquired from patients without visceral metastases. Biopsies were preserved in formalin and processed into paraffin.

Blood for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analyses was acquired in Cell-Free DNA BCT blood collection tubes (Streck, Cat# 218962). Blood samples were processed within 72 hours of acquisition. The sample was first centrifuged for phase separation at 300×g for 20 min at room temperature using an Eppendorf 5430 R centrifuge with the ramping speed set to low. After the first round of separation, the upper plasma fraction was transferred into a separate tube. The buffy coat was collected and transferred into a cryovial and stored at −80°C. The separated plasma fraction was then centrifuged a second time to pellet residual cells at 5000×g for 10 min at room temperature with the ramping speed set to high. Plasma was transferred into cryovials and stored at −80°C.

DNA purification

Tumor DNA was purified from tissue biopsies from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections mounted on slides, after microdissection to enrich for viable tumor content. DNA was extracted using the QIAmp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 56404) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Germline DNA (gDNA) from 500 µL of preserved saliva was extracted using the prepIT L2P Kit (DNA Genotek, Cat# PT-L2P-5) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell-free DNA was extracted from 1 mL of plasma using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 55114) following the manufacturer’s instructions with two modifications. The first modification is that the incubation of lysates with buffer ACB was extended from 5 min to 10 min. The second modification is that elution was performed with 50 µl of Buffer AVE and repeated twice. gDNA for matched cfDNA analyses was extracted from 100 µL of buffy coat using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 69504) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

DNA sequencing

Tumor DNA with matched saliva gDNA was prepared using the TruSight Oncology 500 hybrid capture system (Illumina) and sequenced on a NextSeq 550Dx system (Illumina). Germline and somatic sequence reads were processed using the TruSight Oncology 500 system (Illumina) for alignment and variant calling. Variant classification used multiple clinical databases including COSMIC, ClinVar, gnomAD, and HGMD.

Circulating cell-free DNA was prepared into sequencing libraries using the Kapa HyperPrep Kit (Roche, Cat# KK8500) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with several modifications: the DNA shearing step was eliminated, the maximum range was used for determining adaptor/insert ratios, adaptor ligation was performed at 4°C overnight, and the final PCR was performed with sufficient steps to generate 1 µg of library. Buffy coat DNA was prepared into sequencing libraries using the Kapa HyperPlus Kit (Roche, Cat# KK8510) according to the manufacturer’s protocol with several modifications: 250 ng of DNA was used as input into EDTA conditioning, fragmentation was performed at 37°C without pre-cooling to target a 350 bp fragment size, end repair was performed using the HyperPlus enzyme mix, adaptor ligation was performed at 4°C overnight, and the final PCR was performed for three cycles to generate 1 µg of library.

Low-pass whole-genome sequencing of ctDNA and buffy coat libraries was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 S4 flow cell with 150 cycles paired-end. Sequencing adaptors were bioinformatically removed from sequence reads using Trimmomatic (version 0.39), and reads were aligned to the human genome hg38 assembly using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (version 0.7.17). Mapped reads were then coordinate-sorted and duplicates identified using MarkDuplicatesSpark (GATK, V.4.2.6.1), NM, MD, UQ tags fixed using SetNmMdAndUqTags (Picard) and recalibrated for base quality using BQSRPipelineSpark (GATK). Reads from multiple flow cells and lanes were merged and sorted for coordinates and marked for PCR duplicates again using MarkDuplicates. Averaged genome coverage and insert size were estimated using CollectWgsMetrics and CollectInsertSizeMetrics (Picard). To estimate the fraction of ctDNA, a computational algorithm that determined somatic copy number profile, TitanCNA (V.1.17.1), was used for each ctDNA sample with its matched buffy coat sample. ctDNA fraction was directly exported from the tumor purity estimated by TitanCNA. For dichotomous assessments of ctDNA presence, a ctDNA fraction of at least 20% was necessary for defining a tumor-positive sample. For samples that had a ctDNA fraction less than 20%, a sample was determined to be positive if any signs of significant copy number alterations were detected by manual inspection of somatic copy number traces using the Integrative Genomics Viewer, or if sufficient depth permitted the identification of somatic single nucleotide variants (with matched germline coverage) for a recurrent variant previously documented in prostate cancer.

Peripheral blood immune subset analysis

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood collected in cell preparation tubes with sodium citrate and viably frozen until analysis. After thawing, PBMCs were washed with PBS, incubated with Fc receptor blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec), and stained with monoclonal antibodies for 20–30 min at 4°C. Dead cells were excluded from the analysis using the viability dye, LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua (Life Technologies). All assays were performed using multiparametric flow cytometry (MACSQuant; Miltenyi Biotec). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software V.10.6.1 (FlowJo, LLC). Flow cytometric data were quantified either as the median fluorescence intensity or as a percentage of cells, as indicated.

For analysis of soluble analytes, research blood was collected in serum separator tubes; serum was collected following centrifugation and stored at −80°C prior to analysis. Serum levels of IFNγ, IL-10, and TNFα were evaluated using a custom Meso Scale Discovery V-PLEX Panel (Meso Scale Diagnostics LLC, Rockville, Maryland, USA), and soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) was measured with an ELISA kit from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Complete blood counts (CBCs) with differentials were performed at the National Cancer Institute’s Center for Cancer Research.

PBMC gene expression profiling

Whole blood was collected into lithium–heparin tubes and processed with Histopaque-1077 to isolate PBMCs according to manufacturer’s instructions. The NanoString PanCancer Immune Profiling nCounter panel was used to quantify the expression of 770 immune-related genes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism V.8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), Microsoft Excel for Mac V.16 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA), SAS/STAT software V.14.1 (SAS Institute), and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; V.4.5.2). Survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan-Meier analyses with median survival and 95% CIs, and curves were compared using log-rank tests. For subgroup analyses involving three genomic groups, pairwise log-rank comparisons were adjusted within each endpoint using the Holm method. As a sensitivity analysis for small-sample assumptions in BRCA2 vs non-BRCA2 comparisons, permutation-based (Monte Carlo) log-rank tests were performed with 50 000 resamples.

For longitudinal immune and laboratory measures, paired comparisons vs baseline were performed using Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests. P values of laboratory tests and cytokines were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, while pre-specified flow cytometry comparisons were presented without multiplicity adjustment. Comparisons across RECIST response groups were performed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. ctDNA comparisons across response groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s multiple-comparisons correction, and associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman rank correlation. Exploratory differential gene expression analyses in PBMCs were performed using t tests without multiple-comparisons adjustment.

All tests were two-sided, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and demographics

Baseline characteristics and demographics are described in table 1. 61 patients were enrolled in the study; one patient was not evaluable secondary to the development of a pulmonary embolism on the day of informed consent prior to treatment initiation. The median age at enrollment was 65 years old (45–88 years old). Median on-study PSA was 76.7 ng/mL (<0.02–5000 ng/mL). Median Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status was 1. Most patients (80%) self-identified as White. Nearly all patients (93%) had prior next-generation ARPI, while nearly half of patients (45%) had prior treatment with both enzalutamide and abiraterone. 35% of patients had prior use of docetaxel or cabazitaxel. 48% of patients had prior treatment with a vaccine including sipuleucel-T31 or PROSTVAC.32 No patient had received prior immune checkpoint blocking antibodies.

Safety

Adverse events (AEs) at grade 2 or higher are reported in online supplemental table 1. AEs of interest related to durvalumab and/or olaparib that occurred included anemia (32%), fatigue (32%), decreased lymphocyte count (28%), decreased WBC count (10%), decreased neutrophil count (8%), and digestive disorders: constipation (12%), diarrhea (10%), nausea (8%), anorexia (8%), vomiting (8%), dyspepsia (3%). Immune-related AEs included hearing loss (3%), optic neuritis (3%), colitis (3%), arthritis (2%), pneumonitis (2%), and proctitis (2%).

17 patients (28%) had ≥50% PSA declines from baseline (figure 1B), with the greatest declines being those patients with partial responses (PRs) in solid tumor size. 34 patients (57%) with soft tissue/visceral disease had at least one restaging CT imaging scan 60 days after study entry to measure tumor regression using RECIST V.1.1 criteria (figure 1C). In patients without HRR variants, the Kaplan-Meier estimate of 4 month rPFS was 74.2% (90% CI 58.6 to 84.7) (n=31), exceeding the prespecified 50% benchmark. The median rPFS was 5 months (figure 1D, 95% CI 4.6 to 7.6 months), and the median OS in this cohort was 19.1 months (figure 1E, 95% CI 15.0 to 29.3 months).

Genomic correlates of treatment efficacy

On study entry, germline and somatic variant status was attempted and profiled on saliva or buffy coat DNA (germline) and a solid tissue biopsy DNA (somatic) (figure 2A), using a sequencing panel targeting 500 cancer-associated genes. We also reviewed and considered outside genomic analyses from commercial germline and somatic panels. Figure 2A depicts pathogenic somatic and germline small nucleotide variants in HRR genes, oncogenes, and tumor suppressor genes that were observed in at least two patients or have been previously shown to have a clinical impact on prostate cancer. Of the ten patients who exhibited PRs on study, eight patients harbored germline and/or somatic variants in BRCA2. Of these, five patients had tumors that harbored a somatic BRCA2 variant, five patients had germline BRCA2 variants, and one patient had both somatic and germline BRCA2 variants (figure 2A). A total of 14 patients (23%) harbored at least one variant allele of BRCA2. Additional germline and somatic variants in other HRR genes included ATM (n=4), NBN (n=3), CHEK2 (n=4), PALB2 (n=2), CDK12 (n=2), and RAD50 (n=1). The remaining two patients exhibiting PRs had no identifiable HRR variants detected on biopsies of metastatic sites of disease (figure 2A).

Stratified by BRCA2 status, patients with BRCA2 variants exhibited significantly longer median rPFS (13.2 months; 95% CI 7.7 to 20.2 months) than patients without a BRCA2 variant (4.8 months; 95% CI 4.5 to 6.4 months, log-rank p=0.006) (figure 2B). Patients with BRCA2 variants also demonstrated longer median OS (29.3 months; 95% CI 16.1 to 39.7 months) than patients without a BRCA2 variant (17.7 months, 95% CI 12.5 to 22.5 months), although this difference was not statistically significant given wide CIs (p=0.14). As a sensitivity analysis to assess robustness to small-sample assumptions, permutation-based (Monte Carlo) log-rank testing (50 000 resamples) yielded p=0.0014 for rPFS and p=0.098 for OS.

Further stratifying non-BRCA2 variant patients, median rPFS was 4.8 months (95% CI 1.8 to 7.6) in patients with other HRR variants and 4.83 months (95% CI 4.4 to 6.4) in patients with no HRR variants (online supplemental figure 1A). Pairwise testing with Holm adjustment across the three comparisons maintained a significant difference between BRCA2 variants versus no HRR variants (Holm-adjusted p=0.0055), while the differences between BRCA2 variants versus other HRR variants (Holm-adjusted p=0.10) and other HRR variants versus no HRR variants (Holm-adjusted p=0.9) were not significant. Median OS was 38.7 months (95% CI 7.9 to not estimable) in patients with other HRR variants and 17.1 months (95% CI 11.3 to 20.7) in patients with no HRR variants (online supplemental figure 1B). Holm-adjusted pairwise log-rank comparisons indicated significant differences for both subgroups vs no HRR variants (BRCA2 variants vs no HRR variants p=0.047; other HRR variants vs no HRR variants p=0.047), while BRCA2 variants vs other HRR variants was not significant (p=0.7). Overall, BRCA2 variant status was associated with longer rPFS relative to patients without HRR variants, whereas OS differences across genomic subgroups should be interpreted cautiously given wide CIs and limited events.

ctDNA levels have demonstrated prognostic utility in patients with prostate cancer prior to receiving androgen receptor-targeted therapies, with greater ctDNA fractions at baseline associated with shorter times to progression.33 Therefore, we assessed ctDNA levels in a subset of patients at baseline and on radiographic progression using low pass whole-genome sequencing of plasma cell-free DNA. Prior to the initiation of therapy, ctDNA fraction was significantly greater in those patients who went on to exhibit progressive disease (PD) vs PR (Padj=0.022, figure 2D). As a continuous variable, ctDNA fraction at baseline was inversely correlated with time-to-progression (ρ=−0.51; figure 2E). Per RECIST V.1.1 criteria, radiographic progression on therapy considers the volume of all lesions. Despite these increases in tumor volume, however, the overall relationship between ctDNA levels at the time of progression and the time to progression was not statistically significant (figure 2F). Together, BRCA2/HRR status and baseline ctDNA fraction identify subgroups with differential clinical outcomes, but these genomic and tumor burden-associated measures do not fully account for the heterogeneity of response observed on study.

Early modulation of peripheral and regulatory immune features on therapy

To better characterize immunologic effects of durvalumab plus olaparib beyond tumor genomics, we profiled circulating cytokines/soluble factors, CBCs, and immune cell subsets in peripheral blood collected pretreatment and at 2 and 8 weeks after therapy initiation. Differences between baseline and post-treatment time points were assessed using paired Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment (online supplemental table 2).

At 2 weeks, we detected early increases in inflammatory cytokines, including IFNγ (Padj=0.022) and TNFα (Padj=0.046), but these increases were not sustained at 8 weeks. Hematologic indices showed early on-treatment decreases, including RBC, hemoglobin, and hematocrit (all significant at 2 weeks), with RBC and hematocrit remaining significantly decreased at 8 weeks. In addition, absolute monocyte count decreased at 2 weeks (Padj=0.042), with similar downward trends in monocyte subsets and absolute lymphocyte count that did not remain statistically significant at 8 weeks. Among immune cell subsets, early increases in naïve CD4+ (Padj=0.014) and naïve CD8+ (Padj=0.046) T cells were observed at 2 weeks, but these differences were not significant at 8 weeks, likely reflecting early on-treatment redistribution of peripheral T-cell subsets.

Consistent with this, a transient increase in activated/proliferating T cells was observed at 2 weeks, reflected by increased frequencies of Ki67+PD1+ CD4+ and Ki67+PD1+ CD8+ T-cells (figure 3A,B). Regulatory features also increased at 2 weeks, including higher CTLA-4 expression on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and on Tregs, as well as an increased proportion of Tregs among CD4+ T-cells (figure 3C–F). Collectively, these data indicate that durvalumab plus olaparib induces an early systemic immune perturbation characterized by transient inflammatory cytokine elevation and increased circulating T cell activation, accompanied by concurrent upregulation of regulatory markers.

Peripheral immune features associated with clinical responses

We next asked whether baseline immune features and early on-treatment immune changes were associated with clinical response by RECIST criteria (PR, SD, or PD). Immune correlates were compared across response groups at baseline and as percent change from baseline at 2 and 8 weeks after treatment initiation (figure 4). At baseline, patients who subsequently achieved PR or SD showed immune features more consistent with a primed adaptive response compared with patients who developed PD. Specifically, baseline IL-8 (Tukey-adjusted p=0.042) and sPD-L1 (p=0.006) were lower in PR/SD relative to PD, with similar trends for sPD-1 and TNFα (figure 4A). By contrast, patients who developed PD exhibited higher baseline NK cell frequencies (p=0.009) and lower baseline CD4+ T cells (p=0.050), along with trends toward lower naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell frequencies (figure 4A). Together, these baseline differences suggest that patients destined for PD had comparatively greater innate immune features and reduced circulating CD4+ T-cell representation at study entry.

Early on treatment, however, immune changes differed by response group. At 2 weeks, patients with PR demonstrated greater increases from baseline in sPD-1 (p=0.024) and CD4 effector-memory (EM) T cells (p=0.030) compared with SD or PD, with a similar directional trend for CD8 EM (figure 4B). By 8 weeks, patients with PR continued to show trends toward greater increases in sPD-1 and Ki67+ CD8+ T cells compared with PD (figure 4C), consistent with sustained immune activation in responding patients. Conversely, patients with PD exhibited greater increases in granulocytic myeloid- derived suppressor cells (gMDSCs) at 8 weeks (p=0.022) compared with PR or SD (figure 4C), suggesting emergence of an immunosuppressive myeloid phenotype in non-responders. Collectively, these findings support a model in which favorable RECIST responses are associated with a baseline immune milieu less dominated by innate inflammatory markers and with early evidence of peripheral T-cell activation, whereas PD is accompanied by higher baseline innate features and on-treatment expansion of suppressive myeloid populations.

Transcriptomic analysis identifies IL1R2 as a key correlate of response

Given our observations of response-associated differences in circulating immune phenotypes, we next asked whether PBMC transcriptional programs captured early on-treatment immune states linked to clinical response. We therefore performed NanoString PanCancer Immune nCounter profiling on PBMCs collected at baseline and during therapy to explore potential biology underlying response-associated peripheral immune patterns. Differential expression analysis across all samples identified 13 genes at 2 weeks and 18 genes at 8 weeks that differed from baseline (figure 5A,B). Among the treatment-associated changes, canonical interferon/PD-1 pathway–responsive genes including STAT1, CD274 (PD-L1), and IDO1 were upregulated early on therapy (figure 5A,B), consistent with on-treatment immune activation.

We next asked whether PBMC transcriptional changes differed by clinical response (PR vs PD). IL1R2 emerged as the most prominently decreased transcript in PBMCs from patients with PR compared with PD at 2 weeks and 8 weeks, whereas baseline IL1R2 did not differ to the same extent (figure 5C–E). These findings suggest that IL1R2 downregulation is most evident on treatment in patients who respond clinically. IL1R2 encodes a decoy receptor for IL-1, is expressed in myeloid and regulatory immune subsets, and is often interpreted as a marker of immunoregulatory/myeloid programs, consistent with its association with non-response.

Finally, to assess the clinical relevance of IL1R2 expression in PBMCs, we stratified patients by median IL1R2 expression at each time point. Lower-than-median IL1R2 expression was associated with improved OS at baseline and at 8 weeks (log-rank p=0.0083 and p=0.024, respectively; figure 5F–G). Collectively, these data support a model in which early on-treatment PBMC transcriptional programs driven in part by IL1R2 track with clinical response and longer-term outcomes, complementing response-associated immune phenotypes in subsets of patients.

Discussion

In the ever-evolving realm of precision medicine, it is imperative to expand the utility of targeted treatments through rational combination strategies, including with immunologic agents or ARPIs. In mCRPC, immune checkpoint blockade has shown limited and heterogeneous activity, including low objective response rates to pembrolizumab monotherapy,34 and no improvement in OS or rPFS when pembrolizumab was added to enzalutamide in a phase III trial.35 Subsequent clinical experience with PARP inhibition plus PD-1/PD-L1 blockade has not demonstrated an OS improvement in biomarker-unselected populations.27 36 In our cohort, clinical responses were enriched among patients with BRCA2 variants, consistent with established sensitivity to PARP inhibition in this subgroup.13,15 As in other settings, combination approaches may increase immune-related toxicity without clearly increasing response in all patients, underscoring the need for better biologic selection.37

A distinctive component of the present study is the in-depth analysis of peripheral immune subsets. Peripheral blood was used because it enabled longitudinal, minimally invasive tracking of systemic immune kinetics that reflected treatment-induced immune priming and differentiation over time, whereas metastatic biopsies in mCRPC are sparse and impractical for serial sampling. At the same time, studies that directly link peripheral immune kinetics to the tumor microenvironment will be important to clarify how blood-based changes on treatment correspond to intratumoral immune states and therapeutic activity.

These early kinetics are consistent with our prior report from the initial 17 patients enrolled on this trial.28 Across patients, we observed an early systemic immune perturbation on therapy, characterized by transient increases in inflammatory cytokines and expansions of activated/proliferating T cells at 2 weeks, with concurrent upregulation of regulatory markers. This pattern of early immune activation accompanied by compensatory regulatory features fits a model in which treatment induces systemic immune engagements while simultaneously triggering feedback pathways that constrain durable antitumor immunity. Although our single-arm design does not allow attribution of these kinetics to one agent vs the other, the pattern is consistent with the proposed biology of PARP inhibition. PARP inhibition can activate cGAS–STING/type I interferon innate immune sensing programs and promote adaptive immune engagement.22 23 Recent prostate cancer mechanistic work further supports a link between DNA-damage signaling and innate immune sensing, showing that inhibition of the DNA damage signaling kinase ATR can modulate cGAS-STING-dependent antitumor immunity and has effects even beyond PARP inhibition.38 Related peripheral T-cell activation/memory shifts and dynamic regulatory features have also been reported in other checkpoint-treated settings.39,43 Compensatory checkpoint upregulation may follow, providing a potential mechanistic basis for pairing PARP inhibition with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.

When stratified by clinical response, baseline and on-treatment immune patterns further suggested that non-response may track with higher inflammatory/innate features and on-treatment emergence of myeloid suppression. In particular, lower baseline IL-8 in patients achieving PR/SD may reflect lower systemic inflammatory tone and/or lower disease burden, rather than a treatment-specific immune mechanism. This interpretation is supported by our finding that baseline ctDNA fraction (a tumor burden-associated measurement33) was higher in patients who experienced radiographic progression. Conversely, the on-treatment increase in granulocytic MDSCs among patients with PD is consistent with an evolving immunosuppressive myeloid phenotype that may counteract productive T-cell responses and contribute to resistance.

This study has important limitations. It is a single-center, single-arm phase 2 trial without a comparator, which constrains conclusions about additive efficacy of PD-L1 blockade beyond PARP inhibition. Given negative randomized data in biomarker-unselected mCRPC27 and modest activity in KEYNOTE-365 Cohort A,36 our results must be interpreted cautiously. In addition, we lacked serial on-treatment metastatic tissue biopsies for immune profiling, limiting our ability to determine how peripheral immune changes map onto the tumor microenvironment. Finally, transcriptomic analyses of PBMCs should be interpreted as exploratory and warrant independent validation. Nonetheless, these clinical and translational data support continued efforts to refine molecular and immune selection strategies for combination approaches and to better define mechanisms of benefit vs resistance in mCRPC.
