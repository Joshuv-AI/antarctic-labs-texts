Central Message

Swine cardiac autotransplantation provides a reproducible model of heart procurement and perfusion strategies, isolating myocardial effects of interventions from immunologic variables.

Large animal orthotopic cardiac allotransplantation models introduce immunologic and logistical confounders, including dual cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) circuits, immunosuppression requirements, subtle immunologic differences that arise even between properly matched donor-recipient animal pairs, and increased animal use, that limit the mechanistic study of the impact of various procurement and perfusion strategies.1 Application of an autotransplantation model eliminates these limitations, although it presents unique technical challenges. Our objective is to demonstrate a reproducible swine autotransplantation model incorporating normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) before reimplantation.

Case Video

The study protocol was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number: 806365) and adhered to the position of the American Heart Association on Research Animal Use. A 90-kg adult Yorkshire swine underwent midline sternotomy and pericardial exposure. After ligation of the azygos and left-sided azygous veins (the latter unique to swine, draining to the coronary sinus), heparinization, and CPB initiation via distal aortic and bicaval cannulation, cardiac arrest was achieved with custodial cardioplegia.

Cardiectomy was performed, with care taken to preserve adequate residual cuff length at the aorta, pulmonary artery (PA), superior and inferior vena cavae (SVC/IVC), and left atrium. After cardiectomy, the explanted heart was cannulated for NMP via the aorta and PA. The SVC and IVC were oversewn to ensure complete right-heart drainage and quantification of flow via the PA. The NMP pump, in this case, was primed with 750 mL of autologous heparinized blood. The heart was perfused at 35 °C, with a flow rate of 550 to 650 mL/min, targeting an aortic root pressure of 75 to 80 mm Hg for 60 minutes. Autotransplantation proceeded with sequential left atrial, aortic, IVC, PA, and SVC anastomoses; an SVC interposition graft placed during NMP provided key additional length for reimplantation.

Total ischemic time was 49 minutes and reimplantation time 56 minutes. The animal was successfully weaned from CPB with modest right ventricular dysfunction; intracardiac echocardiography confirmed hyperdynamic left ventricular function with moderate right ventricular dysfunction (Video 1).

This model offers a reproducible platform for isolating the effects of procurement and perfusion strategies on myocardial cellular and subcellular function while reducing the necessity of a dedicated donor animal. Critical technical considerations, including meticulous left atrial division, distal aortic cannulation, and standardized NMP circuit setup are highlighted in the accompanying video.

Discussion

Large animal models are a critical component of translational cardiovascular research. Porcine models have been extensively applied across organ transplant experiments, with the swine demonstrating anatomical and physiologic similarity to the human heart.2, 3, 4 Ex vivo porcine perfusion and transplantation has been validated as a suitable, reliable, and reproducible platform for investigating organ preservation techniques.2,3 Such models are critical for elucidating the molecular mechanisms contributing to primary graft dysfunction and for evaluating novel therapeutic interventions designed to optimize allograft preservation and performance. The model described advances this framework by demonstrating the efficacy of a swine autotransplantation platform. This model will further enable the systematic study of cardiac procurement and perfusion strategies while eliminating the immunologic variables and logistical burdens inherent to allotransplant designs.

Porcine cardiac autotransplantation has been previously described as a technically feasible approach for studying ischemia-reperfusion injury and donor preservation.5 For example, Rogers and colleagues5 demonstrated that orthotopic cardiac autotransplantation in the porcine model is a plausible method for reliable study of ischemia-reperfusion injury; in their description of 5 cases, they note that the principal technical barrier is inadequate vessel length for tension-free anastomoses. However, their described model did not incorporate ex vivo machine perfusion; this study is, thus, the first to our knowledge to introduce normothermic machine perfusion to a heart autotransplant model. In addition, an application of autotransplantation techniques to the comparative study of cardiac procurement and perfusion strategies has not been previously described, to our knowledge.

Two notable technical limitations warrant discussion. First, vessel length insufficiency, particularly at the SVC, was encountered and mitigated by placing an interposition graft during the storage period. Graft anastomosis to the residual pig SVC during the storage period prevents any prolongation of the ex vivo period, as well as any necessary manipulation of the heart while on the NMP pump. Second, the volume of autologous blood required for NMP priming presents a challenge, because drawing the perfusate volume acutely risks compromising the circulating hematocrit to support weaning from bypass. In subsequent cases, this was addressed by harvesting blood 7 to 10 days before the transplant procedure, allowing physiologic repletion before the operative day. Despite this strategy, crystalloid pump priming and prolonged bypass runs for extended duration heart storage assessment may be associated with critical anemia; this represents a key limitation of the autotransplant model that must be considered.

Nevertheless, we anticipate that this platform will serve as a foundation for future comparative studies of procurement and perfusion strategies and can be further modified to support diverse research objectives in cardiac transplantation. Further work in our laboratory has successfully applied this approach to 27 additional autotransplant procedures in a donation after circulatory death procurement setting, facilitating the assessment of the consequences of ischemia-reperfusion injury during the donation after circulatory death transplant procurement, preservation, and reimplantation process.
