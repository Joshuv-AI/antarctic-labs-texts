Introduction

Cerebral salt‐wasting syndrome (CSWS) is a potential cause of hyponatremia in the setting of disease of the central nervous system (CNS). CSWS is characterized by hyponatremia with elevated urine sodium and hypovolemia [1]. The clinical manifestations of hyponatremia are listlessness, lethargy, nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and fatigue. Because it is associated with cerebral edema and cerebral ischemia, severe hyponatremia may aggravate neurological symptoms, such as seizures and even disturbance of consciousness [2, 3]. CSWS is still a debated condition, which is most commonly seen after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage and can be seen after other insults to the central nervous system [4]. Besides, CSWS is also associated with infections including tuberculous meningitis and viral meningitis [5].

The blood sodium level of most patients can return to normal after sodium supplementation under central venous pressure control, and the condition can be alleviated. However, a few patients with hyponatremia and hypovolemia cannot be improved. The addition of corticosteroids such as fludrocortisone to these patients can quickly and effectively reduce urinary sodium levels and urine volume, correct hyponatremia and hypovolemia, and thus improve the prognosis [6].

To date, approximately 110 cases of CSWS aged ≤ 18 years have been reported, with a gender distribution of 63% male and 37% female. However, most cases lack clear diagnostic criteria. To the best of our knowledge, there are few documented cases of children experiencing CSWS due to intractable epilepsy, specifically associated with a KCNT1 gene mutation. In this report, we present the case of a 5‐year‐old child with a KCNT1 gene mutation (heterozygous nucleotide mutation of c.1283G>A) who had been suffering from recurrent convulsions for 5 years, resulting in CSWS secondary to refractory epilepsy. Despite undergoing intensive fluid and salt therapy, the patient's symptoms remained unrelieved. Following the administration of a combination treatment involving fludrocortisone and hydrocortisone, there was a notable reduction in urine output and sodium excretion, ultimately leading to the normalization of serum sodium levels.

Case History/Examination

A 5‐year‐old boy, diagnosed with epileptic encephalopathy due to a KCNT1 gene mutation at 22 days postnatal, was admitted to the hospital with a history of “repeated convulsions for 5 years, diarrhea, and vomiting for 1 month.”

Upon admission, his vital signs were recorded as follows: temperature 36.6°C, blood pressure 97/55 mmHg, pulse rate 102 beats per minute, and respiratory rate 28 breaths per minute. Laboratory investigations revealed a C‐reactive protein (CRP) level of 1.21 mg/L, a white blood cell (WBC) count of 5.68 × 109/L, serum potassium level of 2.85 mmol/L, and serum sodium level of 133.8 mmol/L.

The patient exhibited significant developmental delay, poor cognitive function, a lack of response to visual stimuli, recurrent seizures, and difficulties in managing various medications.

Based on these findings, the admission diagnoses included intractable epilepsy, epileptic encephalopathy associated with the KCNT1 gene mutation, gastroenteritis, and electrolyte disturbances.

Methods

Upon admission, the child presented with a serum sodium level of 118 mmol/L, a urine sodium level of 196 mmol/L, a brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) of 2139 pg/mL, and a 24‐h urine volume of 3000 mL, confirming the diagnosis of CSWS. The patient's CNS imaging shows structural abnormalities. The treatment plan focused on rehydration, sodium supplementation, administration of posterior pituitary hormones to regulate urine output, and hydrocortisone therapy.

Electrolyte levels were closely monitored, and the amounts of rehydration and sodium supplementation were adjusted promptly based on the monitoring results. Within 3 days, the child's serum sodium returned to normal; however, BNP levels increased to 4909 pg/mL. In response, fludrocortisone was added to the treatment regimen, resulting in a serum sodium level of 137.6 mmol/L and a decrease in urine volume to 1770 mL. Consequently, hydrocortisone was discontinued.

Following 3 days without hydrocortisone, electrolyte re‐evaluation revealed a drop in serum sodium to 124.3 mmol/L and an increase in urine volume to 4984 mL, prompting the reintroduction of hydrocortisone. With the combined administration of hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone, the child's serum sodium levels normalized, and urine volume gradually decreased (see Table 1). However, hypokalemia was noted as an adverse reaction, leading to the initiation of potassium supplementation based on regular potassium monitoring.

After 5 days of combined treatment, the child's polyuria symptoms improved significantly, allowing for the discontinuation of posterior pituitary hormone therapy. Sodium supplementation was halted once BNP levels reached 92 pg/mL. To prevent corticosteroid withdrawal reactions, the doses of fludrocortisone and hydrocortisone were gradually reduced. Throughout this tapering process, no adverse reactions were observed until the medications were completely discontinued.

Conclusion and Results

After 5 days of combined treatment, his polyuria symptoms improved and posterior pituitary hormone was discontinued. Sodium supplementation was stopped when BNP reached 92 pg/mL. To prevent corticosteroid withdrawal reactions, the doses of fludrocortisone and hydrocortisone were gradually reduced. Throughout the gradual reduction of the medication, no adverse reactions were observed until the medications were completely discontinued medications.

Following discharge, the child continued on an anti‐epileptic regimen that included sodium valproate oral solution, topiramate capsules, and clobazam. Regular monitoring of valproate blood levels was recommended to ensure therapeutic effectiveness and prevent toxicity. Ongoing follow‐up appointments were also advised to closely monitor the child's neurological status and adjust treatment as necessary.

Discussion

CSWS can result from various forms of CNS injuries, including epilepsy. We present a case of a child with epileptic encephalopathy who developed CSWS and was successfully treated with a combination of fludrocortisone and hydrocortisone, with a favorable prognosis. No serious adverse reactions were observed during the hospitalization. The use of fludrocortisone appears to be a safe and effective option for children with refractory CSWS.

Natriuretic factors, including atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), C‐type natriuretic peptide, and dendroaspis natriuretic peptide, may play a role in CSWS, with BNP being considered the primary contributor to CSWS [7]. Natriuretic peptides released from the hypothalamus following traumatic brain injuries can directly affect the renal medullary collecting duct, inhibiting the secretion of renin and aldosterone, thereby increasing urinary sodium excretion. They can also directly impede nerve impulse transmission from the brainstem, participating in the regulation of the nervous system on the kidney to counteract the rise in intracranial pressure and protect the brain tissue [8]. Consequently, elevated intracranial pressure may trigger the release of brain BNP, and previous studies have identified a linear correlation between intracranial pressure and natriuretic peptide concentration in cerebrospinal fluid among patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage. In this case, the initial BNP level upon the child's admission was 2139 pg/mL, showing a progressive increase to 4909 pg/mL.

The most common manifestation of CSWS is hyponatremia, characterized by a decrease in serum sodium levels and an increase in urinary sodium, accompanied by clinical symptoms of hypovolemia such as hypotension, decreased central venous pressure, lack of skin turgor, or elevated hematocrit. However, the syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) with hyponatremia and increased urine sodium has a similar laboratory picture to CSWS [9], but opposite treatment strategies, so early and accurate diagnosis and treatment of CSWS can greatly promote recovery and improve the prognosis of patients. The child exhibited a rapid decline in serum sodium, an increase in urinary sodium, and symptoms of hypotension. Coupled with the progressive elevation of BNP, the diagnosis of CSWS was established.

The primary treatment for CSWS involves correcting hyponatremia and addressing hypovolemia. A rapid decline in serum sodium levels (120–125 mmol/L) can lead to serious complications such as cerebral edema, cerebral herniation, and even death. Without timely intervention, these conditions may result in lasting sequelae to the central nervous system [10]. As ANP may suppress mineralocorticoid secretion in CSWS patients, the administration of agents with mineralocorticoid activities has proven effective in normalizing serum sodium levels [3]. Therefore, hydrocortisone as a natural glucocorticoid exhibits some sodium and water retention effects akin to mineralocorticoid. It significantly reduces urinary sodium excretion and urine output in the treatment of CSWS. Fludrocortisone, a 9 alpha‐fluorinated derivative of hydrocortisone, demonstrates robust mineralocorticoid activity, along with some glucocorticoid activity. The mechanism of fludrocortisone in treating CSWS involves reducing renal sodium excretion and increasing renal sodium reabsorption, resulting in decreased sodium excretion and urine volume. A single‐center randomized controlled trial investigated the efficacy and safety of fludrocortisone in the treatment of CSWS combined with tuberculous encephalopathy. The results indicated that fludrocortisone could rapidly normalize serum sodium levels [11]. However, the current use of fludrocortisone in the treatment of CSWS in children has not been thoroughly explored, with only a few case reports available. Fludrocortisone may emerge as a safe and effective treatment for children with refractory hyponatremia when routine sodium supplementation and rehydration are ineffective. When the efficacy of hydrocortisone alone is insufficient, combination therapy with fludrocortisone can be considered for treating CSWS [12].

After diagnosing the patient with CSWS, a single‐drug treatment with hydrocortisone was initiated, leading to the normalization of serum sodium levels. However, despite this improvement, BNP levels continued to progressively increase. When treated solely with fludrocortisone, there was a decrease in serum sodium levels accompanied by an increase in urine output. Therefore, a combination therapy of both hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone was administered. As a result, the patient's serum sodium levels, urine output, and BNP all returned to normal.

For older children, fludrocortisone doses in the range of 0.05–0.1 mg daily are recommended, with adjustments as necessary [6]. The 5‐year‐old patient initially received a dose of 0.05 mg of fludrocortisone twice daily, along with a combination of hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone at doses of 100 mg three times daily and 0.075 mg twice daily, respectively. As clinical symptoms improved, and to prevent corticosteroid withdrawal syndrome, the fludrocortisone dose was reduced to 0.05 mg twice daily, and the hydrocortisone dose was decreased to 100 mg every 12 h. Once BNP levels returned to normal, a further reduction in the dosage of both medications was implemented. Fludrocortisone was decreased to 0.025 mg twice daily, and hydrocortisone was reduced to 100 mg once daily until discontinuation. The gradual reduction and discontinuation process for both medications remained stable, and the patient tolerated it well without relapse. Furthermore, research indicates that BNP levels in children with epilepsy are elevated compared to the control group, particularly 12 h after seizures. This finding suggests that the secretion of ANP and BNP may be triggered by epileptic stimulation [13]. Consequently, clinicians should exercise heightened vigilance for CSWS in children with epilepsy and promptly initiate safe and effective treatment measures.

Take‐Away Lessons

This case highlights the integral role of clinical pharmacists in the treatment process and suggests that fludrocortisone can serve as a safe and effective treatment option for CSWS, especially when standard sodium supplementation and rehydration are insufficient. The combination of hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone demonstrated a positive clinical response, indicating the importance of tailored therapeutic strategies in managing this condition. CSWS can result from various forms of central nervous injury, including epilepsy. Timely diagnosis and targeted treatment strategies are essential for improving the prognosis in pediatric patients with CSWS, underscoring the need for increased awareness among clinicians.
