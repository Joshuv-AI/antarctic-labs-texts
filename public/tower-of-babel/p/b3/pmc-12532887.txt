Clinical evaluation of a novel-developed clone 3E2 for the detection of PD-L1 expression status in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Fei Qu, Jingnan Wang, Qingqing Zhao, Hui Jiang, Xiaoyi Zhong, Qingqing Xu, Xinyu Yang, Liqiao Hu, Zhidong Zhang, Yan Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDetecting programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression helps identify patients likely to respond to PD-1/PD-L1 therapies. However, the high costs of PD-L1 assays, such as SP263 pharmDx, limit their widespread use, creating a need for cost-effective alternatives. This study evaluates the analytical performance and concordance of the newly developed 3E2 PD-L1 antibody with established clones in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).MethodsThe 3E2 monoclonal antibody was developed using the hybridoma technique. Its performance was compared with that of the SP263, Cell Signaling Technology (CST) E1L3N, and Abcam 28–8 clones using immunohistochemistry on 101 LUAD samples. Concordance was assessed using Bland–Altman plots, confusion matrices, and quantitative analyses. Survival analysis was performed to investigate the correlation between PD-L1 expression detected by the 3E2 clone and treatment outcomes in patients undergoing immunotherapy.ResultsAmong 30 PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies generated via hybridoma, the 3E2 clone showed high sensitivity and specificity for PD-L1 detection. It exhibited strong concordance with Abcam 28–8 in positive (placenta) and negative (normal stomach mucosa) control tissues, as well as non-small cell lung cancer and melanoma samples (accuracy: 90.1%, κ: 0.797). Moderate agreement was observed with CST E1L3N (accuracy: 69.8%, κ: 0.401) and SP263 (accuracy: 55.4%, κ: 0.262), with higher PD-L1 expression detected by E1L3N and SP263. Bland–Altman analysis showed minimal bias between 3E2 and 28–8. Survival analysis revealed that patients with PD-L1 expression ≥ 5% (detected by 3E2) had significantly better outcomes (p = 0.021).ConclusionsThe 3E2 antibody demonstrates high concordance with Abcam 28–8, offering a potential cost-effective alternative for PD-L1 detection with preliminary prognostic value in immunotherapy-treated LUAD patients. Further validation is required to confirm its clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14941-z.Keywords: 3E2 antibody, 28–8 antibody, PD-L1, Immunohistochemistry, Lung adenocarcinoma

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for approximately 40–50% of all NSCLC cases [1]. Over recent decades, LUAD has seen remarkable advances in treatment, transitioning from conventional therapies to highly personalized approaches. These advancements include targeted therapies for patients with actionable genetic mutations, such as EGFR, ALK, ROS1, RET, MET, BRAF, and NTRK mutations. Immunotherapy has also emerged as a revolutionary approach for treating genetic wild-type LUAD. Recent clinical trials targeting LUAD without actionable oncogenic mutations have shown significant and durable responses to programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) checkpoint blockade immunotherapies. However, only a small subset of patients undergoing immunotherapy achieve a positive response. Consequently, it is critically important to develop reliable methods for accurately predicting which patients are most likely to benefit from these treatments.

Recent approaches to biomarker analysis include measuring tumor mutational burden (TMB), conducting gene expression profiling (GEP), and evaluating multiple proteins using multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) or immunofluorescence (mIHC/IF) [2]. Among various strategies, the expression of the PD-L1 protein was the first candidate biomarker associated with responses to anti-PD-1 therapy [3]. When PD-L1 binds to PD-1, it suppresses T cell growth, cytokine release, and cytotoxic functions, effectively deactivating these cells. PD-L1 may be expressed on both tumor cells (TCs) and tumor-associated immune cells (ICs), with increased levels on TCs helping tumors evade immune detection [4]. Consequently, interrupting PD-L1/PD-1 interactions has been adopted as a strategy to rejuvenate tumor-specific T cell responses. The presence of PD-L1 on TCs or ICs has been linked to positive outcomes in cancer therapy, specifically when using PD-L1/PD-1 blocking agents across various cancer types [3, 5]. The clinical significance of PD-L1 expression in LUAD continues to be elucidated. High PD-L1 expression has been associated with improved responses to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy and better overall survival in certain patient subgroups [6].

Alongside the development of new PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, several IHC tests designed to assess PD-L1 levels have been approved by the FDA as companion or complementary diagnostic tools. Companion diagnostic (CDx) tests are crucial for the safe and effective use of specific drugs. Currently, the FDA has approved four PD-L1 IHC assays as CDx for specific NSCLC treatments: Dako 22C3 (22C3) for pembrolizumab in various solid tumors, Ventana SP142 (SP142) for atezolizumab in urothelial carcinoma or NSCLC, Dako 28–8 (28–8) for the ipilimumab-nivolumab combination in NSCLC, and Ventana SP263 (SP263) for atezolizumab in NSCLC. Among these, the 28–8 antibody clone, produced by Dako (Agilent), has been approved for PD-L1 detection in multiple cancer types, including NSCLC [7–9]. head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [10], melanoma [11, 12], and urothelial carcinoma [13]. However, the current PD-L1 IHC landscape faces challenges. The development of new PD-L1 detection clone addresses several key limitations in current diagnostic practices, including clone variability, platform dependencies, and high costs that restrict accessibility [14, 15]. Multiple proprietary clones (e.g., 28–8, SP263 and SP142) are tied to specific immunotherapies and vendor staining platforms [16]. These usually result in higher costs that hinder widespread use, especially in resource-limited labs [17]. A standalone clone, potentially produced by regional manufacturers, could reduce expenses and enable broader screening without proprietary burdens [18, 19]. Furthermore, guidelines from the IASLC Pathology Committee support validating PD-L1 protocols on various platforms, encouraging the creation of flexible, cost-effective clones for equitable immunotherapy access [20]. Affordable testing would democratize diagnostics, ensuring more patients benefit regardless of finances, leading to earlier diagnoses, better planning, and improved outcomes.

By focusing on these aspects, the development of new clones aims to provide a reliable, cross-platform compatible option that enhances diagnostic consistency, reduces costs, and improves patient outcomes in diverse clinical environments. Based on these motivations, we developed the 3E2 monoclonal antibody (mAb) specifically targeting human PD-L1. We prioritized consistency between the 3E2 and 28–8 mAb, as the 28–8 mAb serves as a benchmark approved for a wide array of tumor types. Rigorous comparative experiments with the Abcam 28–8 mAb, CST E1L3N mAb, and the FDA-approved Ventana SP263 CDx assays revealed that the 3E2 antibody exhibits sensitivity and specificity comparable to those of the 28–8 mAb.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The athymic BALB/C nude mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All animals were kept in laminar flow cabinets under specific, pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. The animal experiment protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Guangzhou National Laboratory (GZLAB-AUCP-2023–01-A07). The BALB/C nude mice were housed in controlled conditions, and all efforts were made to minimize pain and discomfort. Animals were euthanized using a carbon dioxide (CO₂) anesthesia chamber to minimize pain and distress. The method was chosen for its effectiveness and compliance with ethical guidelines.

Monoclonal antibody production

The generation of monoclonal antibodies adhered to the established hybridoma technique [21]. Initially, eight female BALB/c mice received subcutaneous immunizations with 50 μg of the antigen (Med Trace, Guangzhou, China) mixed in 0.25 mL of 0.9% saline and 0.25 mL of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA). Subsequent boosts with 25 μg of the antigen in incomplete Freund's adjuvant (IFA) were administered at two, four, and six weeks. One week post the third and fourth injections, blood samples were extracted from the orbital sinus for antibody titer and sensitivity assessment via ELISA. The mouse with the optimal antibody response was selected, euthanized, and its splenocytes were fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol (PEG 2000). The resultant hybridomas were cultivated in hypoxanthine-aminopterin-thymidine (HAT) medium and dispersed across six 96-well microculture plates. Antibody screening was performed on the culture supernatants 8–10 days following the fusion, specifically for antibodies recognizing erythromycin. Positive hybridomas were subsequently isolated using the limiting dilution method and expanded from 96-well to 24-well plates, each containing 0.5 mL of culture medium. Upon reaching confluency, the cultures were scaled up to 20 mL flasks. Finally, hybridoma cells were harvested, centrifuged, and the antibody-rich supernatants were preserved at − 20 °C for future use.

Patient cohort

With approval from the review board and the ethics committee, placental, gastric mucosal, non-small cell Lung cancer, and malignant melanoma tissue samples were collected from Jinan Central Hospital during the initial screening phase. Additionally, 101 pathologically confirmed LuAD tissue specimens were obtained from patients admitted to the Central Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University between May 2018 and November 2021. These patients had undergone primary surgical resection or biopsy prior to receiving any chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Immunohistochemistry

We analyzed 101 samples from patients with LuAD using IHC, including a mix of real-world histological samples: 89 resection specimens (88.1%), 11 biopsy samples (10.9%), and 1 cytology specimen (1.0%) from pleural effusion enrichment. All tissues were fixed in 10% buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides were reviewed for all cases to confirm diagnoses. IHC testing was performed using PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies, including clones 28–8 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), E1L3N (Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA), SP263 (Ventana Medical Systems Inc, Tucson, USA), and our self-developed monoclonal antibody 2E3 mAB. SP263 staining was executed on the Ventana Benchmark ULTRA platform, while manual IHC staining for CST E1L3N and Abcam 28–8 was performed following their respective protocols. The candidate 2E3 mAB also underwent manual IHC staining, with the Abcam 28–8 protocol serving as a reference for optimizing antibody concentration and staining conditions.

PD-L1-positive tumor cells scoring

PD-L1 expression was assessed based on the proportion of membranous PD-L1-positive tumor cells (TPS), excluding staining intensity. Two independent, blinded expert pathologists scored each case, using the highest score across all cores. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. A three-tier scoring system was employed: < 1%, ≥ 1–50%, and ≥ 50%. TPS ≥ 1% was defined as PD-L1 positive. We further categorized PD-L1 positivity into moderate (TPS ≥ 1–50%) and high (TPS ≥ 50%) expression levels. This scoring method was adapted from the KEYNOTE-042 trial [22]. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion. Cores with a minimum neoplastic component of ≥ 30% were considered adequate for analysis, and others were excluded. Placenta and macrophages served as external and internal controls, respectively, to verify the adequacy of PD-L1 staining. The 2E3 and 28–8 mAb assays demonstrated comparable results in all 101 cases. Comparisons between E1L3N and SP263 were feasible in 86 and 65 cases, respectively.

Data analyses

Concordance was assessed using Bland–Altman plots, confusion matrices, and quantitative analyses. LOESS regression analysis was employed to generate the best-fit curves depicting the expression profiles observed in the staining patterns of the four antibodies. Agreement for the three-tier scoring system across different assays was evaluated using Cohen's non-weighted Kappa coefficient. Cohen's Kappa ranges from 0 to 1, with interpretation as follows: 0.10–0.20: slight agreement; 0.21–0.40: fair agreement; 0.41–0.60: moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80: substantial agreement; and 0.81–0.99: near perfect agreement [23, 24] Survival analysis was performed to assess the prognostic impact of PD-L1 expression on patient outcomes using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.3.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [25].

Results

Preliminary IHC screening of monoclonal antibody of PD-L1

Employing the hybridoma technique, we generated 30 PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). These proprietary mAbs underwent initial screening on an IHC platform, comparing their performance to the commercially available positive control antibody, Abcam 28–8. To validate antibody performance, we used positive control tissues (placenta), negative control tissues (normal stomach mucosa), and patient samples from NSCLC and melanoma. This screening identified two promising candidates for further development into IHC kits: clones 3E2 and 10E2. However, 10E2 exhibited a significantly lower binding affinity compared to 3E2. Consequently, 3E2 was chosen for subsequent LUAD consistency validation experiments (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Clinicopathological characteristics of the LUAD cohort

A total of 101 LUAD specimens meeting the inclusion criteria were analyzed using four PD-L1 IHC assays: SP263, CST E1L3N, Abcam 28–8, and the candidate 3E2 mAb. Detailed demographic and clinicopathological data for these individuals are presented in Table 2 and Supplemental Material 1. The median patient age was 64 years (range: 34–86 years). Of these 101 specimens, 10.9% (11/101) were biopsies, 88.1% (89/101) were resection specimens, and 1.0% (1/101) were cytology specimens. Females comprised a higher proportion 61.4% (62/101) of the patient population. Staging distribution revealed a predominantly early-stage disease pattern, with 84.2% (85/101) of cases classified as stage I-II and 15.8% (16/101) classified as stage III-IV. Tumor grading analysis indicated 19.8% (20/101) of specimens were grade I, 63.4% (64/101) were grade II, and 16.8% (17/101) were grade III. PD-L1 expression on TC was assessed using SP263, CST E1L3N, Abcam 28–8, and the candidate 3E2 mAb (representative staining patterns are shown in Fig. 2). The proportion of TC with a TPS ≥ 1 was 47.7% (31/65) for SP263, 74.4% (64/86) for CST E1L3N, 65.3% (66/101) for Abcam 28–8, and 64.4% (65/101) for the candidate 3E2 mAb. The 3E2 clone demonstrated consistent performance across resection, biopsy, and cytology samples, with no notable discrepancies in staining patterns or concordance metrics.

Consistency analysis of PD-L1 antibody Clone 2E3 with other clones by bland–altman plots

The Bland–Altman plots were used to assess the consistency between PD-L1 antibody clone 2E3 and three other clones: Abcam 28–8, CST E1L3N, and SP263 (Fig. 4). The comparison with 28–8 showed a mean difference of −0.65, indicating minimal bias and high agreement, with most data points falling within the limits of ± 1.96 standard deviations (+ 12.64 and −13.94). In comparison with E1L3N, a mean difference of −4.13 was observed, suggesting a slight bias but still reasonable agreement, as most data points were within the limits of + 19.34 and −27.59. Lastly, the comparison with SP263 displayed a mean difference of −8.08, indicating a more pronounced negative bias. While the majority of data points fell within the limits of + 26.06 and −42.21, the significant mean difference points to a systematic bias between the two methods. Overall, method 3E2 demonstrated varying levels of consistency with the other methods, with the highest agreement observed with 28–8 and the lowest with SP263.

Quantitative consistency analysis of PD-L1 Antibody Clone 2E3 with other clones

To quantify potential differences between measurement methods, a pairwise comparison between method 3E2 and three other methods (28–8, E1L3N, and SP263) was assessed using confusion matrices and statistical measures. The comparison between methods 3E2 and 28–8 showed a high level of agreement, with an overall accuracy of 90.1% and a Cohen's kappa of 0.797, indicating substantial consistency. The confidence interval for accuracy ranged from 82.5% to 95.1%, and the p-value was statistically significant, demonstrating robust agreement between these two methods (Fig. 5).

Kappa consistency tests between 3E2 clone and the other three clones 28–8, E1L3N and SP26 clone. A The comparison between the 3E2 and 28–8 methods displayed a high overall accuracy of 0.901 and a Kappa value of 0.797, indicating high consistency, with most samples being classified similarly by both methods. B The comparison between the 3E2 and E1L3N methods showed moderate consistency, with an overall accuracy of 0.698 and a Kappa value of 0.401, suggesting that while there is some agreement, there are also notable discrepancies. C The comparison between the 3E2 and SP263 methods indicated low consistency, with an overall accuracy of 0.554 and a Kappa value of 0.262, highlighting significant differences in classification between these two methods

In contrast, the comparison between methods 3E2 and E1L3N exhibited moderate consistency, with an overall accuracy of 69.8% and a Cohen's kappa of 0.401. The confidence interval for accuracy was 58.9% to 79.2%, but the p-value was not statistically significant, indicating that the agreement between these two methods was less pronounced and more variable. Lastly, the comparison between methods 3E2 and SP263 showed moderate-to-low consistency, with an overall accuracy of 55.4% and a Cohen's kappa of 0.262. The confidence interval for accuracy ranged from 42.5% to 67.7%, and the p-value was not statistically significant, suggesting a lower level of agreement between these methods.

Overall, method 3E2 demonstrated the highest consistency with 28–8, followed by moderate consistency with E1L3N, and the lowest consistency with SP263.

Survival analysis in LUAD patients receiving immunotherapy

We further analyzed the prognostic impact of PD-L1 expression detected by the 3E2 and 28–8 antibody clones on patients with Stage III-IV cancers who received immunotherapy. The immunotherapy subgroup consisted of 16 patients with stage III-IV LUAD who received PD-1 inhibitors (tislelizumab, sintilimab, or camrelizumab, often in combination with chemotherapy) following PD-L1 assessment on pre-treatment tissues. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplemental Material 2. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that for the 3E2 clone, PD-L1 expression ≥ 5% was associated with significantly improved survival compared to < 5% (p = 0.021, Fig. 6 B). However, no significant survival difference was observed when the threshold was set at 1% (p = 0.087, Fig. 6 A). Similarly, for the 28–8 clone, PD-L1 expression ≥ 5% was significantly associated with improved survival compared to < 5% (p = 0.019, Fig. 6 D), while the 1% threshold did not show statistical significance (p = 0.270, Fig. 6 C). These findings suggest that the prognostic value of PD-L1 may vary depending on the antibody clone and the expression threshold used, particularly in advanced-stage patients undergoing immunotherapy. These findings support the utility of the 3E2 clone as a promising biomarker for predicting overall survival in advanced lung adenocarcinoma patients undergoing immunotherapy.

Discussion

The ongoing elucidation of the clinical implications of PD-L1 expression in immunotherapy is a critical endeavor in the field of oncology. High PD-L1 levels are increasingly recognized as a key prognostic factor for the success of immunotherapy. Patients with high PD-L1 expression often exhibit a better response to immune checkpoint inhibitors and improved overall survival, particularly within defined patient subgroups. The pursuit of cost-effective PD-L1 testing methodologies is a significant focus within the current research landscape. Our team has developed a novel PD-L1-recognizing antibody, the 3E2 mAb, displaying promising diagnostic potential in initial screenings. Comparisons with commercially available clones (SP263, CST E1L3N, Abcam 28–8) revealed high concordance in staining patterns with Abcam 28–8. Furthermore, the 3E2 clone offers greater cost-effectiveness. For instance, commercial in vitro diagnostic assays like SP263 or 28–8 typically cost approximately 150–200 € per test, including reagents and platform requirements, whereas laboratory-developed tests such as the 3E2 clone are estimated at 50–100 € per test, providing a 30–60% reduction based on expert analyses and cost-effectiveness studies [14, 26]. This high diagnostic agreement combined with enhanced cost-effectiveness underscores the 3E2 mAb's potential as a reliable alternative for PD-L1 evaluation.

In 2019, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) expanded the approval of pembrolizumab for first-line treatment of metastatic NSCLC in patients whose tumors express PD-L1 with a TPS of ≥ 1% [27–29]. The KEYNOTE clinical investigations established a link between PD-L1 expression, as determined by IHC in tumor tissues, and improved objective response rates (ORR) as well as more favorable survival outcomes with pembrolizumab treatment [22, 29–31]. The patient selection for the KEYNOTE-001, −010, −024, and −042 studies was predicated on the IHC-based PD-L1 22C3 assay, facilitated by the Dako Autostainer system [22, 29–32]. Driven by the continuous advancement of immunotherapy drugs, researchers have embarked on a concerted effort to explore alternative platforms or antibodies for PD-L1 assessment, such as the SP263 and 28–8 antibodies [28, 33, 34]. However, despite their effectiveness in predicting benefits of anti-PD1 therapy, the high cost of current PD-L1 diagnostic tools, including both the antibody price and the requirement for specific automated platforms from commercial vendors (e.g., Ventana BenchMark ULTRA for SP263 and Dako Link AutoStainer for 28–8), has hampered their widespread adoption [28, 33]. In our research, we generation 3E2 monoclonal antibody adhered to the established hybridoma technique, ensuring a rigorous and mature development process. This technique involved multiple stages of immunization, cell fusion, and antibody screening, ultimately leading to the selection of the 3E2 clone due to its high binding affinity and performance in IHC screening. The development process was not only rigorous but also cost-effective, offering significant economic benefits. By reducing dependency on expensive commercial antibodies and producing a high-quality, proprietary antibody clone domestically, we can lower costs and enhance accessibility for PD-L1 testing in clinical settings.

Our study comprehensively evaluated the efficacy of the 3E2 antibody in detecting PD-L1 expression levels and the clinical significance of PD-L1 positivity using the 3E2 assay. We observed a strong correlation between the 3E2-derived TPS and treatment outcomes with those obtained using the commercially available 28–8 assay. The ‘best fit’ curves using LOESS regression analyses depicted that the candidate 3E2 clone showed similar expression profiles, demonstrating a consistent pattern with Abcam 28–8. Furthermore, the Bland–Altman plots provided insights into the agreement between the candidate 3E2 clone and the other three clones. For the comparison with Abcam 28–8, the mean difference was −0.65 with limits of agreement ranging from −13.94 to 12.64, indicating minimal bias and narrow limits of agreement. The confusion matrices further quantified the agreement between 3E2 and the other clones. The overall accuracy of 3E2 compared to Abcam 28–8 was 90.1%, with a kappa value of 0.797, indicating strong consistency. Previous studies have shown varying diagnostic sensitivity and specificity among PD-L1 staining antibodies. In NSCLC, Ventana PD-L1 (SP142) had the lowest sensitivity and specificity, followed by PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx, PD-L1 IHC 28–8 pharmDx, and PD-L1 SP263 Ventana assay with the highest scores [35, 36]. Some studies found no significant differences in PD-L1 staining and quantification between IHC antibodies 28–8 and SP263 [37]. Our results showed high concordance between the candidate clone 2E3 and 28–8 expression. As clone 28–8 is widely used in lung cancer and has been validated by numerous high-quality RCTs for its reliability in assessing immunotherapy efficacy [7–13], indicating the potential of clone 2E3 as a reliable strategy to assist in detecting PD-L1 expression in a manner comparable to existing commercial antibodies. Further prospective studies are needed to validate its diagnostic performance as a companion diagnostic tool.

On the other hand, we also found the clone 2E3 exhibited relatively low consistency when compared to CST E1L3N and SP263. Previous studies have found that the E1L3N clone is highly consistent with the SP263 assays at both cutoffs using the Ventana platform [38]. Our findings also showed high concordance between E1L3N and SP263. However, PD-L1 expression detected by E1L3N and SP263 was higher than that detected by the candidate 3E2 and 28–8 clones. These discrepancies could be due to tumor expression heterogeneity, inter-clone differences, different platforms, and inter/intra-observer variability [18].

The development of the 3E2 clone represents a significant step toward providing a novel antibody for PD-L1 detection with significant clinical implications. This not only reduces dependency on foreign antibodies but also ensures tailored and potentially more cost-effective diagnostic options for patients in our country. The high concordance with established commercial clones supports its use in clinical settings, offering reliable guidance for immunotherapy decisions. Given the demonstrated strong consistency between 3E2 and Abcam 28–8, particularly in LuAD, the 3E2 clone is poised to be an effective tool for predicting immunotherapy efficacy. Given the strong concordance with the 28–8 clone, which is validated for PD-L1 detection in various tumor types including HNSCC, melanoma, and urothelial carcinoma [10–13], the 3E2 clone may perform similarly in these contexts. Preliminary screening in melanoma and NSCLC samples (as shown in Fig. 1) supports this, with comparable staining patterns. Future studies are needed to validate 3E2 in diagnostic practice for other tumors to expand its utility.

Moreover, survival analysis using the 3E2 and 28–8 clones revealed that a higher PD-L1 expression threshold (≥ 5%) was significantly associated with improved overall survival, while the lower threshold (≥ 1%) did not reach statistical significance. In LUAD patients undergoing immunotherapy, PD-L1 expression detected by the 3E2 and 28–8 clones demonstrated potential as a predictive marker for overall survival. These findings suggest that the prognostic value of PD-L1 may be influenced by both the antibody clone used and the cutoff threshold selected for stratification. Nevertheless, the small sample size limits the strength of survival findings, which should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and require further validation in larger, well-designed patient cohorts to confirm the reliability.

While our study provides promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample size used in our analysis was relatively small, which may affect the generalizability of our findings. Second, our study focused solely on LuAD, and the performance of the 3E2 clone in other tumor types remains to be validated. Third, the 22C3 clone was not included due to our focus on 28–8 as a benchmark for multiple tumor types and resource limitations, although prior studies report high concordance between 28–8 and 22C3 (e.g., weighted kappa 0.75–0.91) [36, 39, 40], this limitation may constrain direct generalizability to pembrolizumab-associated contexts. In addition, as a real-life study conducted in a developing country[37], PD-L1 assay selection is primarily influenced by the availability of platforms and trained pathologists. Therefore, approaches to harmonizing testing methods are crucial for ensuring appropriate treatment selection for our patients [41]. Future studies with larger sample sizes and a broader range of cancer types are necessary to confirm the robustness and applicability of the 3E2 antibody.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our newly developed PD-L1 antibody clone 3E2 demonstrates substantial concordance with the commercial clone Abcam 28–8 in the assessment of PD-L1 expression in Lung adenocarcinoma. These preliminary findings suggest that the 3E2 monoclonal antibody may offer a potential and cost-effective alternative for evaluating PD-L1 status, which could support more strategic use of immunotherapies. However, further validation in diverse tumor types, larger cohorts, and prospective clinical trials is essential to confirm its broader applicability and predictive value for immunotherapy outcomes.
