Spontaneous Combustion Hazard Zone in the Goaf and Collaborative Prevention and Control Technique under Dynamic Evolution of Coal Pillar Air Leakage
Authors: Yang Liu, Xuyao Qi, Yongqin Zhang, Dayong Luo, Jiangtao Qin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous combustion of residual coal in goafs is one of the most severe disasters in coal mines. In China, coal spontaneous combustion (CSC) results in an annual loss of about 20 million tons of coal and the release of pollutants, such as carbon dioxide and suspended particles, severely degrading the environment and triggering frequent secondary disasters. The air leakage behavior of coal pillars, which functions as the supporting structure at the boundary of goaf areas, enhances the risk of a CSC through seepage channels formed by fracture networks. This problem is particularly prominent in the high-ground-stress environment of deep mining. Current research primarily focuses on the mechanical properties of coal pillars, such as stress–strain and reasonable dimensions, as well as sealing materials for coal pillar air leakage, yet there is still a lack of systematic study on the collaborative prevention and control of the coal pillar-goaf system. In view of the above fact, this study investigated the mechanism of coal pillar fragmentation and air leakage and further delineated the CSC hazard zone in the goaf under coal pillar air leakage conditions. The results disclosed that coal pillar air leakage is adjusted dynamically in response to mining activities. Simultaneously, coal pillar air leakage can lead to an expansion of the CSC hazard zone in the goaf and intensify the risk of residual coal ignition. Therefore, based on multifield coupled numerical simulations, a collaborative prevention and control technique combining wind pressure matching and zonal blocking was proposed here. By regulating the dynamic equilibrium between the seepage field in the coal pillar and the oxygen distribution field in the goaf, the area of the CSC hazard zone affected by coal pillar air leakage shrinks by 52.82%. This research can provide new insights into effectively preventing mine fire accidents caused by coal pillar air leakage.

Introduction

Coal spontaneous combustion (CSC) is one of the major disasters in coal mines. In China, the CSC-induced direct loss of coal resources amounts to 20 million tons annually. CSC releases massive harmful gases and particulate matter, such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and suspended particles, seriously polluting the air and water sources. , CSC, which can easily trigger major accidents such as gas combustion and explosion, causes enormous economic losses and significant casualties. Air leakage is one of the key factors inducing CSC. It not only promotes the contact between coal and oxygen but also accelerates the oxidation reaction of coal, thereby promoting the risk of CSC. Especially in some special mines, such as those with shallow burial depth, isolated working faces, and multiseam mining operations, air leakage undoubtedly becomes the primary challenge in mine fire prevention and control. Studies have shown that when the air leakage rate of the working face grows from 0% to 12%, the maximum oxygen concentration in the overlying coal seam of the goaf rises markedly. As air leakage intensifies, the contact area between coal and oxygen expands and the oxidation reaction accelerates. Eventually, the risk of CSC is aggravated.

The air leakage behavior of coal pillars, which function as the supporting structure at the boundary of goaf areas, is a key factor contributing to CSC. Research indicates that coal pillars develop tensile-shear composite fractures under mining-induced stress and overlying strata movement, which give rise to a chain response of “fracture networkseepage channeloxygen transport”. This response may trigger CSC both within the coal pillars themselves and in residual coal in the adjacent goaf. Three-dimensional similarity simulation experiments have confirmed that the air leakage rate of coal pillars rises exponentially with the fracture aperture and the leakage channels tend to migrate dynamically over time. This process leads to nonuniform oxygen concentration distribution in the goaf, thus affecting the determination of CSC hazard zones. Research has demonstrated that air leakage through coal pillars can not only alter the migration patterns of gas and intensify the risk of gas accumulation , but also lead to the release of toxic and harmful gases and dust into underground and pose environmental hazards. , These findings provide an important theoretical basis for the comprehensive treatment of coal pillar air leakage and highlight its dual importance for mine safety and environmental protection. −

Air leakage control techniques can be classified into passive sealing and active regulation. Passive sealing primarily employs grouting and sealing, which evolves from traditional cement-based foams to cement-based foams, antioxidant gel foams, time-varying viscosity solidified foams, and inorganic thixotropic gels. While these materials demonstrate noticeably improved permeability and compressive strength, they face challenges such as high costs and complex processes. Active regulation inhibits oxygen accumulation by altering the airflow distribution. Examples include monitoring and controlling air leakage, optimizing ventilation systems, installing windbreak barriers and isolation walls to reduce leakage, ,− as well as injecting inert gases like nitrogen. − In recent years, composite prevention and control systems have emerged as a research focus. For instance, a collaborative technique combining “gel injection for sealing + directional nitrogen injection for inertization” reduced the CSC probability in a mine goaf by 72%. Additionally, scholars have conducted research on air leakage sealing techniques under specific conditions, such as roof-cutting and gob-side entry retention, steeply inclined regenerated roof conditions, and ultralong working faces, and have developed a multi-index graded early warning method for CSC under air leakage sealing conditions.

In summary, current research was primarily focused on the fragmentation of coal pillars and the resulting coal pillar spontaneous combustion, yet there is still a lack of systematic investigation into the collaborative prevention and control of the coal pillar-goaf system. In terms of air leakage prevention and control techniques, most efforts have been directed toward developing sealing materials or addressing air leakage under special singular conditions, with insufficient consideration given to the integrity of the coal pillar-goaf system. Given the abovementioned research status, it is urgent to carry out research on the formation of air leakage channels due to compression and deformation of coal pillars, analyze the impact of these air leakage channels on the CSC hazard zones in the goaf, and propose cost-effective control measures accordingly.

Theoretical Analysis on Air Leakage through a Compressed and Fractured Coal Pillar

Overview of the Experimental Working Face

The designed length of the 31,305 working face is 4228 m, with an open-off cut length of 300 m. The 31,305 working face is located in the 3-1# coal seam, which has a high propensity for CSC and the shortest CSC period of 39 d. The air supply volume to the 31,305 working face is 1500 m3/min, and it adopts a downward ventilation method. The 31,307 working face is a prepared mining face, separated from the 31,305 working face by a 19.51 m wide coal pillar. The air intake volume of the 31,307 working face is 1300 m3/min, and it adopts an upward ventilation method. Affected by mine pressure, the coal ribs along the intake airway of the 31,305 working face were seriously fractured during mining, posing a potential risk of air leakage. Theoretical Analysis on Coal Pillar Air Leakage

The large system of mine goaf air leakage is simplified, as shown in Figure . It is assumed that an ideally sealed goaf system approximates an isolated system, where the exchange of heat, mass, and work with the external environment approaches zero. During mining activities in surrounding coal masses, the original sealed system will exchange heat and mass and work with currently active production areas through its boundaries (coal pillars). The imbalance potential (pressure, temperature, and air density field) between the original system and the external environment causes air leakage in adjacent goafs, with the energy transfer channel mainly affected by the development of coal pillar fractures under the influence of in situ stress.

Theoretical Analysis on Coal Pillar Fragmentation

The comprehensively mechanized full-seam mining of the working face results in high mining intensity and consequently exacerbates the manifestation of the mine pressure. Under the influence of mine pressure, the original structure of the coal pillar on the goaf side is damaged, resulting in instability and fracture formation. According to the relevant literature, the strength of coal pillars is determined by coal strength, width, height, and overall structural characteristics. The stability of the coal pillar was verified by using empirical formulas: Obert-Duvall/Wang and Bieniawski formulas. , 2 R=RC(0.778+0.222B/H) where R is the strength of the coal pillar, MPa; R C is the uniaxial compressive strength of the in situ critical cube of the coal pillar, MPa; B is the width of the coal pillar, m; and H is the height of the coal pillar, m. 3 R=RC(0.64+0.36B/H) where R C is the uniaxial compressive strength of the in situ critical cube of the coal pillar. Experimental results reveal that the coal pillar becomes structurally weaker and more likely to lose stability when its width-to-height ratio B/H is less than 5. For the 31,305 working face, the coal pillar is 19.51 m wide and 6.14 m high on average. That is, its width-to-height ratio is 3.178, which is less than 5. Theoretically, it is verified that the coal pillar may undergo instability and fracturing and witness the generation of air leakage channels.

SF6 Tracer Gas-Based Air Leakage Detection in the Coal Pillar

According to the predicted results from theoretical calculation and numerical simulation, the coal pillar has deformed and generated fractures. To detect air leakage channels in the coal pillar, hexafluoride sulfur (SF6) tracer gas analysis was employed in this study. SF6, a colorless, odorless, and nonflammable inert gas, is physically active, which enables it to be sensitively detected in complex underground airflow environments. Meanwhile, it is insoluble in water and does not adsorb onto the surfaces of underground materials. Most importantly, its background concentration in the mine environment is extremely low, which ensures the accuracy of the detection results. To detect air leakage, a mine air leakage detection device was designed. This device, comprised a cylinder, a pressure reducing valve, a flow meter, and a flow valve, is capable of achieving stable and continuous release of the tracer gas at a specific concentration. Quantitative analysis was conducted using an SP-6800 gas chromatograph to precisely measure SF6 concentrations, so as to realize quantitative calculation of air leakage volumes. The measurement protocol focused on two key parameters: (1) the total air leakage volume of the working face and (2) the air leakage volume through the coal pillar, which can serve as a basis for identifying the air leakage sections.(1)Detection of air leakage in the working face

Discussion

The fragmentation of the coal pillar causes dynamic air leakage. Field measurements and numerical simulations confirm that due to dynamic air leakage, the oxygen concentration in the coal pillar side remains high, which is a mark of a potential risk of CSC. Simultaneously, under this influence, the oxygen concentration on the intake airway side of the goaf declines slowly, accompanied by backward movement of the “oxidation zone” and expansion of the hazardous area. Given these findings, can the impact of coal pillar air leakage be mitigated by regulating the pressure difference between its two ends?

Using the previously established geometric model parameters, the intake airflow at the 31,307 working face was adjusted to 1500 m3/min to match the 31,305 intake airway for numerical simulation. After calculation convergence, the contour plots of the oxygen concentration distribution in the goaf of the working face were exported (Figure ). Figure a–d represents the contour plots of oxygen concentration distribution at vertical heights of 0, 2, 25, and 35 m in the goaf, respectively. Analysis demonstrates that, affected by pressure difference on its two sides, the air leakage volume of the coal pillar shrinks and the air leakage influence radius shortens. The range of “three zones” with a CSC hazard in the goaf is considerably reduced, and the hazardous area of the CSC shifts forward, implying a reduced leakage-induced risk of CSC in the goaf. From the above observation, it can be concluded that the CSC hazard zone in the goaf can be markedly reduced by matching the air volume and reducing the pressure difference on both sides of the coal pillar. Meanwhile, the subsequent application of shotcrete to the coal pillar for zonal blocking can largely curb the impact of air leakage through the coal pillar on the CSC hazard zone in the goaf. The coal pillar shotcrete process was adopted by the conventional wet spraying process. The spraying material, which mainly consists of a mixture of cement, spraying additives, clay, glass fibers, and other inorganic materials, forms a membrane with strong sealing properties after hydration. The spraying material has a water-cement ratio of 1:1.2, a thickness of not less than 100 mm, and a spraying length of 60 m.

Conclusions

This paper takes the 31,305 working face of a coal mine in Inner Mongolia as the research object. Through theoretical analysis, field measurement, and numerical simulation verification, this study investigated CSC hazard zones in the goaf under dynamic coal pillar air leakage conditions. On this basis, a collaborative prevention and control technique combining wind pressure matching and zonal blocking was developed and applied on site. The application achieved a pronounced effect. The main conclusions are as follows.(1)Driven by mine pressure, the coal pillar deforms and develops air leakage channels. Tracer gas detection reveals that coal pillar air leakage adjusts dynamically in response to mining activities. The most significant air leakage channels are identified at a distance of 0–20 m from the working face, the air leakage volume reaching up to 79.44 m3/min.(2)The dynamic air leakage causes backward movement of the “three zones” with CSC hazard in the goaf and expansion of the CSC hazard zone. By employing the collaborative prevention and control technique combining wind pressure matching and zonal blocking to regulate the dynamic equilibrium between the seepage field in the coal pillar and the oxygen distribution field in the goaf, the air leakage volume through the coal pillar can be effectively reduced, thereby mitigating the leakage-induced risk of CSC in the goaf. The area of the CSC hazard zone in the goaf shrinks by 52.82% after the application of this technique.(3)With the continuous increase in mining depth, the safety hazards arising from coal pillar air leakage under deep mining conditions are becoming increasingly evident. The pressure-matching and zonal-sealing synergistic prevention and control system proposed in this study offers valuable theoretical guidance and practical significance for effectively preventing mine fire accidents induced by coal pillar air leakage.

By employing the collaborative prevention and control technique combining wind pressure matching and zonal blocking to regulate the dynamic equilibrium between the seepage field in the coal pillar and the oxygen distribution field in the goaf, the air leakage volume through the coal pillar can be effectively reduced, thereby mitigating the leakage-induced risk of CSC in the goaf.
