“Sisters Influencing Sisters:” Findings from a Community-Engaged Process to Develop a Social Media-Based, Influencer-Delivered Intervention to Increase PrEP Awareness, Knowledge, and Uptake Among Transgender Women
Authors: Sophia Zamudio-Haas, Jack Johnson, Katie Singley, Emily A. Freedman, Catherine Lane, Elizabeth Knight, Stephen Scott, Viraj V. Patel, Jae M. Sevelius, Sarah Bauerle Bass
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transgender women (TW) shoulder a disproportionate burden of HIV diagnoses, yet are less likely to use PrEP, due to intersectional social and structural barriers to accessing services. TW utilize social media to share health information and foster community, yet to date, most studies leveraging the power of these platforms for PrEP interventions have focused on gay men. To address this gap, we partnered with TW social media influencers and engaged a Community Advisory Board (CAB) to develop an Instagram-based intervention to increase PrEP awareness, knowledge, and use among TW. Over quarterly meetings, surveys, and interviews, CAB members provided systematic input on intervention design. CAB guidance centered on three themes- leveraging platform functionality; maximizing representation and resonance, and fostering trust and credibility. In collaboration with the CAB, we shortlisted potential influencers and reviewed their accounts for reach, follower demographics, and content. We established paid partnerships with TW influencers (8) and held a 10-h virtual workshop to prepare them to be PrEP educators and navigators for their online followers. Before the funding for this research was interrupted by a NIMH termination letter, we developed the “Sisters” intervention to increase access to PrEP information delivered by TW influencers, foster dialogue around PrEP within online spaces, and provide Instagram-based linkage to vetted, gender affirming, HIV services. Partnering with community was essential to designing a social-media intervention tailored to considerations TW have around PrEP. This community-engaged, influencer-delivered model contributes new insights into digital public health design for marginalized populations.

Introduction

Transgender women (TW) shoulder a disproportionate burden of new HIV diagnoses, yet Pre Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) use remains low, due in large part to intersectional social and structural barriers to accessing prevention services.1–4 Despite an estimated decrease of about 12% in new HIV diagnoses among all adults in the US in 2022 as compared to 2018, new HIV diagnoses rose by 25% among TW.5 A recent multi-site national cohort study with TW found an HIV incidence of 5.5 (95% CI 2·7–8·3) per 1,000 person-years - over three times higher than the estimated incidence for adults in North America.4,6 Authors emphasized that similar factors, rooted in social exclusion- such as low employment, high homelessness, low insurance coverage, and poverty- drove both HIV incidence and mortality for participants.4

PrEP is effective at preventing HIV, yet TW are less likely to be aware of, use, or adhere to PrEP than other population groups who are highly susceptible to HIV.2,7–9 The first national probability study of sexually active transgender people in the US found that only 35% of TW reported being familiar with PrEP. Among those who were aware, almost 80% favored using PrEP, but less than 3% currently reported doing so, and only about a third was estimated to have been prescribed PrEP.2,10,11 Lack of PrEP knowledge and uptake can stem from numerous social and structural barriers. Often, information about PrEP is not framed in ways that are relevant to the health priorities of TW, making it less salient.12,13 TW report pervasive transphobic and discriminatory experiences in health care settings, contributing to avoidance of health care and reducing access to HIV prevention services, including PrEP.14,15 Discrimination in hiring and in the workplace limit job opportunities for TW, contributing to poverty, low insurance coverage, housing instability, and reducing access to healthcare and PrEP.7,14,16,17 TW may also experience family and peer group stigma and worries about what others may think if they take PrEP.11,18 Perceptions that PrEP might interfere with feminizing hormone therapy and prioritization of gender affirming care over other types of healthcare, in the context of limited resources, are other factors that may reduce PrEP use.19

Social media platforms have become important spaces for health communication for transgender people, and have enormous potential to increase PrEP awareness, knowledge, and uptake among TW. Applications like TikTok, Instagram, and Facebook provide a democratic environment where users can curate their own narratives and control the flow of information that they consume, reducing the potential for discriminatory experiences.20,21 Transgender youth and adults are already using social media to conduct background research on gender affirming care and other health topics, using platforms to connect with other transgender people to ask questions about procedures and medications, share recommendations on providers, and seek social support around fears, hopes and concerns from a virtual community.22–26 At the same time, seeking health information or vetting providers via social media can incur potential medical misinformation.27 Dialogue on social media platforms can propagate stereotypes and contribute to stigma.28–31 Community-engaged research on how best to leverage social media to increase access to health information and services, while maximizing the strengths and minimizing the risks of using these platforms, is urgently needed.

To date, the potential for social media platforms to improve access to HIV services for transgender people remains largely understudied and unrealized, despite burgeoning research for other population groups.32–36 Studies have shown how social media-based interventions can reduce internalized HIV stigma, increase HIV testing, provide linkage to services, increase engagement in HIV care, and offer peer support to promote mental health.37–39 Groups that are particularly susceptible to HIV or have higher HIV prevalence, such as youth and immigrants, might be more interested and engaged via social media-based interventions than traditional approaches.35,40 Influencer delivered interventions have been shown to reach participants who have higher levels of medical mistrust and lower engagement in health services, as compared to participants who were reached via paid social media adverstisements.41 However, most of the social media based HIV research has focused on gay men or enrolled small numbers of TW.42,43 Only a few social media-based studies have focused specially on the HIV-related needs and priorities of TW, which have been insufficient to keep advancing the field.32,44,45

To address this important gap, we developed Sisters Influencing Sisters (“Sisters”), an-Instagram based, PrEP promotion intervention led by TW influencers, informed by systematic community input, and grounded in social-marketing.46 We designed the Sisters intervention to reduce barriers to PrEP uptake by offering education from a trusted source, on a platform frequently used for health communication, and providing referrals to vetted PrEP services in Philadelphia and the San Francisco Bay Area.11,15,47 Using the 2013 International Social Marketing Association’s global consensus on social marketing principals, concepts, and techniques,48 the Sisters intervention sought to facilitate social change by using marketing principles through a customer oriented approach, to deliver value that could translate into the adoption of a positive behavior (e.g. PrEP use).49 As noted by Domegan, social marketing should include system settings, stakeholder engagement, and the use of digital technologies, beyond communication and promotion.50 Our community-engaged study integrated these tenets throughout the intervention development process.

This paper describes the development and design activities for the Sisters social media-based intervention that were completed before receiving a notice of study termination from the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). We delineate how we established a community advisory board (CAB), gathered systematic qualitative and quantitative information from the CAB, conducted co-analysis with the CAB to elaborate findings and apply them to shape intervention content, and prepared influencers to be peer health educators for their social media followers. Findings on our community-engaged intervention development process can benefit other groups conducting social-media based research with TW, a population too often left out of scientific advances, despite experiencing persistent HIV related health disparities.51

Methods

Community-engaged study design

The Sisters study team dedicated about 18-months to engage community in intervention design. We conceptualized “community” as TW advocates, thought leaders, HIV clinical and social service providers, PrEP consumers, and social media influencers. We convened a CAB that shaped all aspects of intervention design- including selecting a social media platform, identifying influencers for partnerships, finalizing intervention content, and designing the curriculum for a workshop for the influencer partners. We then recruited TW influencers and held a one-week workshop over synchronous video, to provide education on HIV, PrEP, social drivers of health, and the epidemiology of HIV among transgender communities. We reviewed preliminary intervention content and delivery schedule and finalized these together.

Community advisory board (CAB)

We recruited potential CAB members who were TW community activists (7) and healthcare providers (3) who serve TW in Philadelphia and the San Francisco Bay Area. We began by talking with local community advocates and providers working with transgender individuals, clinical partners, and transgender activists to identify people who might be interested. Once we generated a group of potential CAB members, we reached out to individuals over email and phone, to describe the study, discuss board member responsibilities, and compensation. We sought a balance of CAB members from the two geographical regions of focus.

CAB members were compensated for their time via $75 electronic gift cards for every meeting and study activity, for a total maximum compensation of $600 per CAB member. The CAB met four times as a group over video conference, at a day and time that worked best for CAB members. Meetings lasted about 90 min and agendas were circulated in advance, with requests for items or edits. Authors SZH and SB co-facilitated CAB sessions. The CAB also provided systematic input on intervention content via online surveys (3) and semi-structured, one-time interviews.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

We developed three brief, self-administered online surveys in Qualtrics and circulated these to CAB members via email. Based on a review of public health literature on social media interventions,37,52–55, our team established initial criteria for selecting TW influencers who had between 1,000– 5,000 followers and public accounts. The first survey, sent in January 2023, provided a list of potential influencers selected based on our initial criteria, including 8 based in Philadelphia, 4 based in the San Francisco Bay area, 5 based in Los Angeles, and 8 based in New York City. For each influencer, the survey asked if the respondent was familiar with the influencer, if they followed the influencer, and if they recommended the influencer as a partner for the Sisters intervention. This survey also included two open-ended questions asking for (1) recommendations on additional influencers not included in the short list, who respondents thought would make good partners for Sisters and (2) what criteria CAB members thought were important to consider when choosing influencers.

In July 2023, we developed and circulated a second survey that used Likert-type questions to gather preferences on content and messages produced in prior work.18 This survey included 14 examples of intervention content – 4 palm cards, 4 posters, 3 booklets, and three comic book panels. Respondents were asked to rank each example according to order of preference and provide qualitative comments on the messaging and visuals included in each example, such as what they did or did not like about the materials.

Finally, a third online survey, sent May 2024, asked CAB members, study staff, and study investigators to provide feedback on a study logo. This survey included four logo options and requested participants to rank them in order of preference and to provide comments about what they did and did not like about each option. Logos varied in terms of color, graphics, and language.

All survey data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to summarize participant responses. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables to characterize the distribution of responses across items. For scaled questions, means and medians and standard deviations were computed to assess overall trends and variability within the sample. In addition, items that were rank ordered used mean scores and percentages to identify priorities among respondents. This approach provided a clear overview of response patterns and facilitated comparison across survey domains.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

The study team developed a semi-structured guide for interviews with CAB members that included questions on social media use patterns, considerations that shape PrEP use for TW, and overall guidance on intervention implementation. Questions around intervention implementation sought recommendations and considerations for the length of intervention, frequency of sharing and posting content, and modalities for content (e.g. reels, stories, posts). CAB members were invited to participate in individual interviews, facilitated by author EF, over Zoom, at a time that was convenient for them and lasted for about 30–45 min. With participants’ permission, interviews were audio-recorded.

We conducted a multi-step approach for participatory, thematic analysis.56,57 As a first step, we conducted data immersion, reading and discussing transcripts to identify important topics. Rather than code data, given the small number of transcripts, for our second step, we grouped and organized data excerpts in tables, by main topics that corresponded to interview domains. We grouped excerpts related to social media use patterns, intervention recommendations, and key topics about PrEP for TW, to facilitate comparative analysis. We next consolidated our findings into initial themes, which we further elaborated through collaborative analysis with CAB members. During a dedicated CAB session, we discussed interview analysis and preliminary themes, to validate interpretation, finalize theme definitions, and connect findings to intervention design and content. Author SZH facilitated this discussion, using open ended questions and prompts to flesh out the overarching narrative. If participants disagreed on interpretation, SZH moderated the discussion until we reached consensus, by modifying as needed until everyone in the group approved of interpretation.

As a final step, we defined themes and delineated implications for the Sisters intervention design.56,57 Theme 1, “Leveraging Platform Functionality” captured the importance of using multiple features of Instagram to develop a dynamic intervention, with options to interact with content and dialogue with Influencers and other followers about PrEP. Theme 2, “Maximizing Representation and Resonance” described findings around including narratives and images of TW with diverse characteristics around age, race, ethnicity, and gender presentation. Theme 3, “Fostering Trust and Credibility” encompassed recommendations around the importance of messaging that is strengths based and comprehensive, in contrast to some of the more fear-based messaging that participants had seen in PrEP health education materials previously.

Establishing paid partnerships with influencers

Influencers were hired as consultants with Temple University. Intervention influencers were paid a total of $4500 to complete human subjects training, participate in the 10-h online training workshop, post recruitment materials on their accounts to enroll study participants, and create and share PrEP-related content over 12 wk. The two comparison influencers did not participate in the training or post PrEP information. They were compensated with $500 to post about the study and recruit their followers to complete surveys, as participants in a comparison group for the pilot test.

Results

Quantitative results and integration into intervention design

Results from the surveys conducted with CAB members were instrumental to selecting influencers for partnership, developing intervention content, and finalizing the intervention content delivery schedule. In the first survey, CAB members (n = 8) provided valuable information on the preliminary list of influencers and suggested several others to review. Comments allowed the study team to gather potential concerns CAB members had about specific influencers, such as having too large a following or feeling they would not be a good ambassador for sharing PrEP information.

In the second survey, overall reactions to the PrEP messaging and visuals were positive. Most respondents rated the messages as excellent or good (n = 4/6; 67%). All participants thought the messages would be very or somewhat helpful when deciding about PrEP and rated the messages and wording as very easy or somewhat easy to understand. Most participants (n = 5) believed there was enough information to help people decide about using PrEP. This feedback was incorporated into visuals and content as material was created. Finally, feedback on the study logo survey (n = 20) assisted in identifying the best logo for the study and allowed the study team to incorporate final changes based on feedback, including changing fonts for readability. This logo was then used to brand all study materials and posts.

Qualitative findings and integration into intervention design

Eight CAB members participated in the interviews. Through our participatory, analytic process to systematically interpret interviews, we identified three central themes that shaped intervention content and approach. Table 1 presents these themes with emblematic quotes.

Theme 1: Leveraging Platform Functionality.

Findings from our interviews with CAB members emphasized the importance of using infographics, stories, and reels to convey dynamic narratives around PrEP. This led to the selection of Instagram as the social media platform of choice, given that CAB members described Instagram as having features that facilitated more interactive communication. While Facebook has similar features, CAB members suggested that Instagram was more popular with the target audience. In interviews, CAB members recommended that the intervention leverage multiple Instagram functions, such as interactive Stories, live streams, reels, and carousel posts, to provide followers with a variety of ways of receiving and responding to PrEP information. It would also expand opportunities for followers to see content, as interviews revealed different patterns in the ways that followers interact with Instagram content. Some followers might rely heavily on stories, while others scroll through posts, and others return to known accounts to see saved stories and reels.

Based on findings related to this first theme, previous content from our formative work developed by a transgender graphic artist that consisted of an 8-page comic book, was re-designed into a series of smaller images that could be shared in a carousel sequence as both a post and a reel (Figure 1). The comic book featured conversation among a group of friends sitting around a table at a diner, talking about relationships, dating, and PrEP. By tailoring this content to be both shared via stories, with the conversation unfolding over a series of 24-stories over a weeklong period, posting the images as a carousel, and animating it as a reel; followers could have various ways to access the intervention content that leveraged different platform features for sharing information.

We further integrated findings from the theme around ‘leveraging platform functionality’ to create a dynamic content delivery schedule, that used multiple features to share information. Like paid partnership models, Sisters included a planned delivery schedule, as shown in Table 2, which incorporated various modalities of information delivery. While posts remained a central part of the planned intervention, we identified Stories and Live Streams as other essential strategies to engage directly with followers around PrEP. Stories, with popular trends such as text prompting viewers to “Ask me Anything” and an open text box for comments, would allow for dual communication and amplification of community voice. Influencers could then end the 24-h story cycle by re-positing questions from the text box, with their responses. Live stream could enable a more intimate feel of communication with influencers, who planned to narrate live videos of themselves while going about their daily lives- like getting dressed, putting on make-up (the “get ready with me” trend), playing video games, cooking, or simply facing the camera- while encouraging viewers to post comments, questions, and emoji reactions that are shown in real time. Our final intervention delivery plan included live streams to feature influencers and invite providers to talk about PrEP services and answer questions from viewers. We designed the intervention delivery plan to leverage platform functionality, encourage bi-directional communication, and provide opportunities for real-time engagement.

Theme 2: Maximizing Representation and Resonance.

Interviews with CAB members highlighted the importance of “representation and resonance” as key to an effective PrEP intervention. Most CAB members shared previously seeing PrEP marketed on social media to gay men, an approach that was off-putting. One member shared that she never sees products marketed to TW specifically, making her feel like she was part of an overlooked audience. These experiences informed CAB members’ emphasis on the need for nuanced representation of TW of different ages, racial and ethnic backgrounds, and styles, so that viewers could “see themselves” in the intervention content. CAB members further described how inclusivity and sex positivity in messages and graphics could help to combat PrEP related stigma. Members highlighted the need for balance, to both talk comfortably and openly about sex, without overly sexualizing TW. Ultimately, this guidance informed messaging that spoke to women and emphasized self-care and empowered action, summarized in the tagline that rifts on the reproductive choice tagline, “My body, My choice, My PrEP,” shown in Figure 2.

In response to this thematic finding around the need to maximize resonsance and representation, intervention content centered PrEP considerations that the literature and interviews with CAB members noted were important to TW when using PrEP, like interaction with hormone therapy, cost, and PrEP-stigma. Study content addressed drug and gender affirming hormone therapy interaction, clarifying that both therapies can safely be taken together, without diminishing the strength of either one. Infographics addressed key concerns around cost and insurance access, as well as providing links to resources for telehealth and other options to access PrEP.

This emphasis on nuanced representation was also integrated into the comic book series, which included a narrative around using PrEP in a new love relationship, in contrast to stigmatizing associations with casual sex or multiple partners. The comic book featured dialogue between friends that connected PrEP use to both sex and a sense of ease, rather than a lack of trust in your partner. For example, one comic book character, who had just met “the one,” who she described as “so sweet and gentle,” was advised by her friend that it can still be “such a relief” to not “worry” about HIV status when you are on PrEP. In this way, the comic book narrative counteracted the association of PrEP with casual sex or multiple partners, by talking about it in the context of romance and partnership.

Theme 3. Fostering Trust and Credibility.

The third theme emphasized the importance of strategies to foster trust and credibility with the priority intervention audience. CAB members described the importance of strengths-based communication, rather than promoting PrEP for “people at high risk of HIV,” which could increase stigma and deter use. One CAB member cautioned against constantly focusing on condoms for prevention, stating that the intervention content should present multiple HIV prevention strategies. Rather than highlighting risk or alarming statistics, CAB members recommended intervention messages and graphics that centered PrEP use as a form of self-care, as shown in example content in Figure 3. CAB interviews noted the importance of content that reflected positive images of transgender community, with uplifting messages and color pallets, to build trust with the audience. This approach contrasted with strategies that CAB members shared as negative examples of PrEP promotion, such as marketing that grouped TW with gay men, emphasized casual sex, or used fear tactics.

To further ‘foster trust and credibility’ our team partnered with TW influencers who were well known and respected within their communities, to deliver accurate and accessible information around PREP. Given the intervention’s focus on HIV prevention and the smaller amount that our team was able to pay compared to private sector brand partnerships, which can pay $5,000 or more for a post, the CAB suggested that we partner with influencers who had a demonstrated commitment to public health and interest in being advocates. Establishing partnerships with community leaders, who were also influencers, was an important strategy to build credibility with the audience and lay a foundation of trust for the intervention content.

Process for identifying influencers for partnership

We created an initial list of about 40 influencers by reviewing self-described TW’s public accounts on Instagram, with followings specific to the Philadelphia, PA and the San Francisco Bay, CA areas (intervention locations) and Los Angeles, CA and New York City, NY (control locations). We next reviewed accounts for size of following and current posting content to narrow the list to about 25 influencers. We shared this list with our CAB in the first online survey. Based on their recommendations and additions, we created a final list of about 45 influencers. We conducted a more thorough review of each account, to ensure that content was gender affirming and did not further myths, misconceptions, or stereotypes about HIV/STIs. We also sought a racially and ethnically diverse group of influencers.

We made initial contact with potential influencer partners over social media. Author JJ reached out to influencers over direct message on Instagram from the Sisters study page and via email – 12 in Philadelphia, 11 in San Francisco Bay, 17 in New York City, and 5 in Los Angeles. The messages briefly described the study and the partnership opportunity. Of the initial 45, 11 responded and 8 scheduled a longer phone or Zoom meeting to discuss the opportunity. Of these, 7 influencers (3 in Philadelphia, 3 in San Francisco Bay Area, and 1 in Los Angeles) agreed to participate. No influencers in New York City indicated interest. As a result, we changed locations to Baltimore, MD and recruited 1 influencer from that location, for a total of 8 influencers.

Workshop curriculum: preparing influencers to be PrEP champions and navigators

In partnership with the CAB, we designed a workshop curriculum to comprehensively and succinctly prepare influencers to be PrEP champions and navigators for their Instagram community. Curriculum offered a foundation in public health research and intervention delivery, with in depth education on HIV and PrEP access in transgender communities. Led by two experienced queer and transgender health educators, the workshop was held for two hours over five consecutive days, with curriculum building toward more complicated topics and skills. The final session focused on brainstorming anticipated challenges in implementation and discussing solutions, such as low engagement with posts, and changing images or messages to find what resonated more with followers. All six intervention influencers participated.

Each day included an ice breaker, educational content, and skills building through role play. Sessions were designed to be conversational, drawing on influencers’ expertise in content development and experiences interacting with followers, while addressing how these dynamics differ in a research context versus typical Instagram activities. The curriculum included real world examples and discussion prompts on how to respond to questions around where to access PrEP, how to access health insurance, and how to address PrEP misinformation in direct messages or comments. The workshop provided time to review and revise content, like changing color contrasts for better readability or editing text on graphics.

Building knowledge about PrEP services available in their area and providing foundational training in PrEP navigation were critical parts of the Workshop. The curriculum included one session focused on learning about the roles and responsibilities of PrEP navigators, to prepare influencers to offer similar, abbreviated support to their followers. Each influencer received a resource list for their city-including HIV prevention services, housing, emergency resources, and clinics that offer gender affirming care, and a general list of national PrEP resources for followers based in different geographical locations. Role plays were used to practice providing referrals through direct messages to partner clinics that had established PrEP programs, designed to serve transgender and gender expansive clients.

Before holding the workshop, our team developed partnerships and linkage protocols with five community public health clinics in the SF Bay Area and Philadelphia. At each clinical site, we established relationships with PrEP providers and navigators, so that influencers could refer their followers to specific contacts. The intervention delivery schedule (Table 2) included Live Streams with local PrEP providers to ask/answer questions, posts that profiled partner clinics, and stories that featured pictures and brief biographies of referral contacts. The Live Streams were intended to be saved as reels, so that followers could reference back to the videos if they missed the stream.

Discussion

In collaboration with a CAB and in partnership with social media influencers, we developed the “Sisters” intervention for TW, designed to increase access to PrEP information, foster dialogue around PrEP within online spaces, and provide linkage to vetted, gender affirming, HIV services. Findings from our study suggested that a paid partnership model with TW influencers is a feasible and acceptable approach for an Instagram-based, PrEP intervention for TW. While this model has been previously successful with social media-based HIV interventions designed for gay men,54 to our knowledge, we are one of the few examples that specifically prioritized partnerships with TW,39,45 to create and share content designed for other TW. Further, we uniquely detailed our approach to select, hire, and prepare influencers to be PrEP champions for their online communities, contributing knowledge around practical considerations for implementation research in social media spaces.

The PrEP intervention content and delivery strategies that resulted from our community-engaged development and design process looked very different from traditional approaches. While we maintained evidence informed practices, such as peer-delivered education and active referrals to vetted providers,58 the Sisters intervention included innovative content to provide information about PrEP that would resonate with community and amplify diverse representation of TW. By framing PrEP use around self-care and in the context of loving relationships, Sisters content counteracted messaging that CAB members described as stigmatizing and off-putting, which centered the desires of cisgender gay men or emphasized risk and statistics. While these concerns have been well documented in the literature as barriers to use among TW,13,16 our community-engaged development process yielded educational content designed to resonate differently, so that followers could see themselves reflected in content. Sisiters included multi-featured delivery strategies for social media, moving away from static health communication, to engage followers with livestreams, videos, and reels. Given the rapidly changing functionality of social media platforms, findings from the Sisters development process contributed new ideas for content creation and delivery that expanded on examples from previous research.33,44

At the time of grant termination in March 2025, influencers had just completed training and launched recruitment of participants to test the intervention. The termination disrupted our ability to retain research staff and maintain engagement with the influencers. As TW, some of whom were also immigrants, the influencers we partnered with faced multi-faced attacks on their rights, social inclusion, and economic opportunities that the study termination might have amplified.59 Between the three and a half months that passed between termination and reinstatement, most influencers disengaged with the study (5/8). The termination harmed team morale and eroded relationships with community collaborators, just as we were building momentum to pilot Sisters. Because of staff loss and influencer disengagement, we could not complete the pilot as designed, leaving key implementation questions related to reach, engagement, and delivery unanswered. Future research with influencer led, Instagram-based interventions should consider who might be missed, such as TW without smart phones, and how to make content accessible for people with different abilities. Social media carries a documented risk of misinformation. Future implementation research should test strategies to mitigate this risk and explore how to navigate the unique ethical considerations inherent in Influencer-led interventions, delivered on public accounts.

Despite termination, during the design phase of our study, we developed unique content and delivery strategies for an Instagram-based PrEP intervention, that centered the voices and expertise of TW influencers and amplified their leadership as agents of change. This community engaged process resulted in intervention storylines that promoted strengths-based messages and contextualized PrEP as a choice in the vibrant lives of TW. The images and narratives that evolved from our design and development approach, systematically using our CAB input, contrasted sharply with previous research and programs that used risk-based messages, grouped TW with gay men, or emphasized aspects of PrEP like sexual freedom, that can be a turn off to audiences already stereotyped as hypersexual.13,60 Without talking about stigma, the final Sisters intervention content and delivery plan thoughtfully counteracted ways that PrEP promotion has unintentionally deterred uptake among TW, while centering gender affirming messages and images.13,61

Conclusions

Using a community-engaged design, we developed “Sisters Influencing Sisters,” an influencer-delivered, Instagram-based HIV prevention intervention tailored for TW. Sisters sought to increase access to PrEP information, foster dialogue around PrEP within community online spaces, and provide linkage to gender affirming HIV prevention services. Partnering with influencers and engaging community advisors was essential to developing a social media-based intervention that could reach an important and often neglected audience, using narratives created to amplify representation, resonance, credibility, and trust. This model demonstrated how participatory influencer partnerships can integrate social-marketing strategies with transgender-led advocacy to bridge research and community practice.
