Smoking After a Cancer Diagnosis in Brazil: Divergent Perceptions and Awareness of Tobacco‐Related Harms Among Patients and Caregivers
Authors: V. S. Lemos, T. E. Colombo, M. Saleh, N. T. Guilherme, L. A. Bauleo, P. R. Silva, P. H. C. Farina, A. M. T. Nascimento, L. H. Manoel, A. F. Fares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients' awareness of tobacco's harms is a key driver for smoking cessation. In Brazil, unique smoking practices—such as straw cigarettes—may influence these views. We hypothesized that cancer patients who continue smoking exhibit an attenuated perception and awareness of tobacco‐associated impact on treatment efficacy and survival compared to their primary caregivers.MethodsThiscross‐sectional study recruited144 active smoker patients with solid cancers and their primary caregivers at a Brazilian public academic hospital. Participants completed a structured questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, smoking history, perceptions of smoking's impact on quality of life, fatigue and treatment outcomes, and objective awareness of tobacco‐related harms. Tumor information was abstracted from medical charts. Groups were compared using chi‐square, t‐tests, and logistic regression analyses.ResultsFrom 1342 patients approached; 144 (10.9%) active smokers and their caregivers were enrolled. Patients were older (median age 63 vs 51 years), and predominantly male (69%). Among patients, 46% smoked non‐manufactured cigarettes (straw, rope, or mixed types). Compared to caregivers, patients exhibited lower scores for both perception (19.8 vs. 23.6; p < 0.001) and awareness (23.0 vs. 25.1; p = 0.02) regarding the harms of continued smoking on both cancer and non‐cancer outcomes. Notably, 50% of patients disagreed that smoking adversely affects treatment efficacy, vs. 23% of caregivers (p < 0.001). Only half of both groups reported feeling adequately supported by their medical team to quit smoking. Higher nicotine dependence (Fagerström score; aOR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.10–5.20) and use of manufactured cigarettes (aOR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.10–5.10) were independently associated with an improved perception of tobacco harms.ConclusionsCancer patients in the Brazilian public health system who continue smoking tend to underestimate tobacco's harms relative to their primary caregivers. Targeted educational interventions and enhanced cessation support are needed to improve cancer care outcomes.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is the leading preventable risk factor for cancer, with decades of research demonstrating its strong association with cancer incidence and mortality [1, 2, 3]. Despite widespread public awareness of smoking's role in cancer development, the detrimental impact of continued smoking after a cancer diagnosis remains underrecognized [3, 4, 5, 6, 7].

Continuing to smoke after a cancer diagnosis is associated with numerous adverse outcomes. In the short term, persistent tobacco use increases postoperative complications, diminishes the effectiveness of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and exacerbates treatment‐related toxicities [5, 6, 8, 9, 10]. Over the long term, it contributes to a higher risk of cancer recurrence, development of second primary malignancies, and overall poorer survival [3, 6, 11, 12].

In Latin America—and particularly in Brazil—there is a notable lack of data on how patients and their families perceive the harms of smoking after a cancer diagnosis [7, 13, 14, 15]. This gap is further compounded by unique local smoking practices. For example, straw cigarettes—a hand‐rolled, unfiltered cigarette made with corn husks—are commonly used in Brazil [16]. Although these products are often marketed as more “natural” or healthier alternatives, current evidence does not support the notion that they are less harmful than manufactured cigarettes [16].

Awareness of the harms associated with continued tobacco use is a critical determinant of successful smoking cessation, influencing motivation to quit and adherence to evidence‐based cessation strategies [4, 17]. Yet, many patients with cancer who continue smoking appear to underestimate the negative impact of tobacco on treatment outcomes and overall health [17, 18, 19, 20]. Although clinicians can play an important role in providing cessation counseling, practical constraints such as limited time and resources often preclude in‐depth discussions [21, 22, 23]. Brief counseling interventions and appropriate referrals have shown promise [19, 23, 24, 25]; however, the influence of family support—especially from primary caregivers—on smoking cessation is less well understood [26, 27].

Given that caregivers frequently provide critical encouragement during the cessation process [26, 27], it is important to investigate whether differences exist between the perceptions of cancer patients who continue smoking and those of their caregivers. Moreover, in the Brazilian context—where alternative tobacco products such as straw and rope cigarettes are common [16]—we hypothesize that patients who continue to smoke after a cancer diagnosis have an attenuated awareness of tobacco's harms compared with their caregivers and that users of straw or rope cigarettes perceive tobacco as even less harmful than users of manufactured cigarettes [16, 27].

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional, questionnaire‐based study was conducted at a single center in São José do Rio Preto, São Paulo, Brazil, from October 2021 to May 2022. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Pilot Study and Protocol Amendment

Before the main study, a pilot was conducted exclusively among lung cancer patients and their caregivers to evaluate the quality and feasibility of the questionnaire. However, several issues were identified, including content gaps and slower‐than‐expected recruitment (25 patients over 3 months), which prompted a protocol amendment. The inclusion criteria were subsequently broadened to enroll adults (≥ 18 years) with any solid tumor who were actively smoking at the time of the survey, regardless of cancer stage or treatment status. Patients with cognitive impairment or significant social barriers that could interfere with questionnaire comprehension were excluded.

Only current smokers and their primary caregivers—defined as individuals providing close support throughout the cancer journey, irrespective of familial relationship—were included in the final survey analyses. Former smokers and never‐smokers were excluded from the questionnaire component; however, their clinicopathological and treatment data were still collected for comparative analyses.

Questionnaire Development and Structure

The questionnaire was developed following an extensive literature review and was based on the Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) [28] and a previously published instrument assessing tobacco harm perceptions. For sections 3–5, responses were recorded on a 4‐point Likert scale (ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”), with each item scored from 1 to 4 (maximum possible total of 32 per section). For analysis, participants were categorized as “aware” only if they selected the highest score (4); scores of 1–3 were classified as “unaware.” This dichotomization was conceptually driven to distinguish participants demonstrating clear and confident understanding of tobacco‐related harms from those with partial or uncertain awareness. Intermediate Likert responses (scores 1–3) primarily reflected uncertainty rather than gradations of knowledge, making binary classification more interpretable for clinical and public health applications. This approach is consistent with previous studies assessing tobacco‐related health risk awareness [17, 18, 19].

To evaluate the robustness of this operational definition, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative cutoff in which responses ≥ 3 (“agree” or “strongly agree”) were classified as aware. The pattern and strength of associations remained similar to the primary analysis, indicating that results were not dependent on the specific threshold applied.

Responses marked as “I don't know” were treated as missing values in the perception and awareness sections because they did not represent a quantifiable position on the Likert scale. These responses were excluded from section score calculations. Given their low frequency (approximately 7%) and random distribution across participants, no data imputation was performed.

Perception was defined as subjective interpretation of smoking‐related information, encompassing individual beliefs, attitudes, and personal experiences regarding tobacco's impact on health and cancer treatment. In contrast, awareness was defined as factual understanding or recognition of scientifically established harms associated with continued smoking after cancer diagnosis. This distinction emphasizes that perception reflects personal meaning‐making and evaluative judgment, whereas awareness represents objective, knowledge‐based understanding of tobacco‐related risks.

Additional clinical and treatment data—including comorbidities, primary cancer site, date of diagnosis, disease stage, treatment modalities, and ECOG performance status—were abstracted from electronic medical records.

Immediately following the survey, participating patients and caregivers received an educational pamphlet outlining the deleterious effects of smoking and the benefits of smoking cessation after cancer diagnosis.

Patients who had quit smoking more than 3 months prior were classified as former smokers, and those with fewer than 100 lifetime cigarettes were considered never smokers. Although cumulative smoking history was initially measured in pack‐years (packs per day × years smoked), the prevalent use of straw cigarettes—hand‐rolled, unfiltered cigarettes made with corn straw—in Brazil complicates direct comparisons. Therefore, pack‐years were not used for inferential analyses; for descriptive purposes, we normalized straw cigarettes by estimating that one straw cigarette equals five manufactured cigarettes.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (means, medians, and percentages) were used to summarize socioeconomic, educational, clinicopathological, and smoking‐related variables. Group differences between patients and caregivers were evaluated using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and the independent samples t‐test for continuous variables.

Study variables were selected based on a priori hypotheses, considering their clinical and epidemiological significance. All covariates were included in the multivariable model regardless of univariable analysis results. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were employed to examine associations between various factors and patients' perceptions of and awareness regarding tobacco‐related harms after cancer diagnosis.

Initially, each survey item (representing a specific perception or awareness outcome) was analyzed separately, with adjustment for age, sex, educational level, Fagerström score, cigarette type, and cancer stage. Next, composite outcomes for “high perception” (including all perception questions) and “high awareness” (including all awareness questions) were dichotomized at the median and modeled using the same set of covariates. All tests were two‐sided, with p‐values ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated to assess multicollinearity among predictors in logistic regression models. VIF values > 5 were considered indicative of moderate multicollinearity, and values > 10 indicated high multicollinearity.

Supplementary Linear Regression Analysis was performed to assess the robustness of our findings across different analytical approaches; we performed supplementary linear regression analyses treating perception and awareness scores as continuous outcomes. These models included identical covariates as the logistic regression analyses (age, sex, educational level, Fagerström score, cigarette type, and cancer stage). Model assumptions were evaluated through examination of residual plots, Durbin‐Watson statistics for independence of residuals, Shapiro–Wilk tests for normality of residuals, and variance inflation factors for multicollinearity. We tested for potential effect modification by examining interaction terms between clinically relevant variable pairs, including education × cigarette type, education × Fagerström score, Fagerström score × cigarette type, and age × Fagerström score. Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare models with and without interaction terms.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Between October 2021 and May 2022, we approached 1038 patients with solid cancers. Among these, 514 (49.5%) were never smokers, 324 (31.2%) were former smokers, and 200 (19.3%) were current smokers. Of the current smokers, 144 consented to participate and provided data alongside their respective primary caregivers, yielding a total of 288 participants. All participants completed a 20‐min interview with a research assistant.

Sociodemographic Characteristics

As summarized in Table 1, patients were predominantly male (69.4%) with a median age of 63 years, whereas caregivers were mostly female (81.3%; p < 0.001) and younger (median age: 51 years; p < 0.001). Educational attainment also differed significantly; 70.8% of patients had primary education or less compared with 34.1% of caregivers (p < 0.001).

Clinical and Smoking Characteristics

Table S1 details the clinicopathological profiles and smoking habits of the patient cohort. Cancer sites included gastrointestinal (29%), genitourinary (24%), lung (16%), head and neck (13%), breast (7%), cutaneous (6%), and other cancers (3.5%). Among patients, 79 (54.9%) had been diagnosed less than 1 year prior to the interview, 30 (20.8%) between 1 and 3 years, and 35 (24.3%) three or more years earlier. Approximately 32% of patients had metastatic disease, and 43.8% were receiving active treatment at enrollment.

Regarding smoking habits, 54% of patients smoked manufactured cigarettes, whereas 46% used alternative forms: 32% smoked straw cigarettes, 9.8% used a combination of manufactured and straw cigarettes, and 3.4% smoked rope cigarettes. Although pack‐years were calculated for descriptive purposes among manufactured cigarette smokers, this measure was not used for comparative analysis given the variability in tobacco content among straw and rope cigarettes. According to the Fagerström test, 57% of patients exhibited low or very low nicotine dependence, 16.7% had moderate dependence, and 26.4% had high or very high dependence. In addition, approximately 24% of caregivers reported being current smokers.

Perceptions of the Harms of Continued Smoking

Table 2 summarizes the findings regarding patients' and caregivers' perceptions of the impact of continued smoking after cancer diagnosis on general health and well‐being. Overall, patients exhibited attenuated perception of tobacco‐related harms compared with caregivers (mean score 19.8 vs. 23.6; p < 0.001). This disparity was evident for individual items as well. For example: (1) Quality of Life: Approximately 44% of patients disagreed that smoking negatively affects quality of life, versus 16% of caregivers (p < 0.001); (2) Treatment Efficacy: Fifty percent of patients disagreed that smoking adversely affects treatment efficacy compared with 23% of caregivers (p < 0.001); (3) Treatment Impact of Cessation: When asked whether smoking cessation would positively impact cancer treatment, 44.5% of patients disagreed, in contrast to 31% of caregivers (p = 0.01).

Awareness of the Harms of Continued Smoking

Table 3 details the awareness outcomes regarding the negative effects of continued smoking on cancer treatment. Overall, patients were less aware of these harms compared with caregivers, as reflected by lower mean awareness scores (23 vs. 25.1; p = 0.02). Differences were also observed for specific outcomes: (1) Surgical Outcomes: A higher proportion of patients (32.6%) were unaware that continued smoking adversely affects surgical outcomes compared with caregivers (22.8%; p = 0.09); (2) Radiation Side Effects: Unawareness of increased radiation side effects was reported by 43.8% of patients versus 31.5% of caregivers (p = 0.07); (3) Chemotherapy/Radiation Efficacy: Forty‐four percent of patients were unaware of the negative impact on chemotherapy and radiation efficacy, compared with 30% of caregivers (p = 0.02); (4) Quality of Life During Chemotherapy: Unawareness that smoking reduces quality of life during chemotherapy was higher in patients (41.4%) than in caregivers (27.2%; p = 0.02); (5) Secondhand Smoke Risks: When queried about the risks associated with secondhand smoke (e.g., cancer, heart attack, stroke), 31.6% of patients reported being unaware, compared with 23.1% of caregivers.

Univariable and Multivariable Analyses of Overall Perception and Awareness

To identify factors associated with overall perception and awareness of tobacco harms, we conducted separate univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. For these analyses, the composite perception and awareness scores—originally continuous variables—were dichotomized at the median. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, educational level, Fagerström score, cigarette type, and cancer stage.

Overall Perception

As shown in Table 4, in the multivariable model, older patients had lower odds of perceiving the harms of smoking compared to younger patients (aOR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98). In contrast, patients with higher nicotine dependence had 2.48 times the odds of perceiving smoking as harmful compared to those with lower dependence (aOR = 2.48, 95% CI: 1.10–5.20), and patients who smoked manufactured cigarettes were 2.36 times more likely to perceive smoking harms compared to those who used alternative forms (aOR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.10–5.10).

Sensitivity and Supplementary Analyses

Assessment of Multicollinearity and Confounding

VIF analysis revealed no problematic multicollinearity among predictors (Table S4), with all VIF values below 5 (Age: VIF = 3.07; Sex: VIF = 1.53; Education: VIF = 1.42; Fagerström score: VIF = 1.77; Cigarette type: VIF = 2.46; Stage: VIF = 1.57). These low VIF values indicate that multicollinearity does not substantially affect the reliability of the coefficient estimates. Exploratory analysis revealed that patients with higher education were somewhat more likely to use manufactured cigarettes (64.3%) compared to those with lower education (51.5%), though this association was not statistically significant (χ2 = 1.46, p = 0.226).

The observed reversal in the direction of the education effect between univariable (OR = 2.33, p = 0.044) and multivariable models was explained by confounding by cigarette type. Stratified analyses revealed that patients with higher education were more likely to use manufactured cigarettes (66% vs. 50% among those with lower education), and the education effect differed by cigarette type (OR = 3.30 among manufactured cigarette smokers vs. OR = 1.53 among straw/rope smokers).

Supplementary Linear Regression Analysis

Supplementary linear regression analyses (Table S3) yielded findings consistent with our primary dichotomized analyses. In the perception model (R 2 = 0.178, p < 0.001), older age was associated with lower perception scores (β = −0.13 points per year, 95% CI: −0.21 to −0.05, p = 0.0015), while higher nicotine dependence (Fagerström score) was associated with higher perception scores (β = 2.80 points, 95% CI: 1.12–4.49, p = 0.0013). In the awareness model (R 2 = 0.088, p = 0.053), only age showed a significant association (β = −0.23 points per year, 95% CI: −0.36 to −0.09, p = 0.0013), with no other predictors reaching statistical significance. These supplementary analyses confirm that our main conclusions are robust across different analytical approaches.

Interaction Testing

Among the interaction terms tested (Table S5), only the education × Fagerström interaction was statistically significant (OR = 16.42, 95% CI: 1.49–181.50, p = 0.021; likelihood ratio χ2 = 7.46, p = 0.006). This finding suggests that the effect of nicotine dependence on harm perception is substantially amplified in patients with higher education. However, given our sample size limitations, we present the main effects model in the primary analyses, with this interaction result considered exploratory.

Discussion

Quitting smoking at the time of cancer diagnosis plays a crucial role in the effectiveness of cancer treatment. Despite this, many patients continue to smoke after their diagnosis [1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. In our study, we examined how cancer patients who continue smoking perceive the harms of tobacco compared with their primary caregivers, as well as their awareness of the negative impacts of smoking on cancer treatment outcomes. We found that patients who persist in smoking tend to underestimate the harmful effects of tobacco and are less aware of its adverse impact on treatment compared with their caregivers.

The finding that caregivers demonstrated higher awareness of tobacco‐related harms than patients suggests an untapped opportunity for family‐based smoking cessation strategies within oncology care. Caregivers could be engaged as active partners in cessation interventions by receiving structured counseling on communication strategies, relapse prevention, and emotional support tailored to the patient's stage of treatment. Integrating caregivers into multidisciplinary cessation programs—through joint counseling sessions, brief motivational training, or digital follow‐up tools—may enhance patients' motivation and adherence to quitting. Evidence indicates that involving family members in cessation planning can improve quit attempts and long‐term abstinence rates [26]. In the Brazilian context, where formal cessation infrastructure is limited, empowering caregivers with accurate, accessible information could serve as a low‐cost, scalable strategy to strengthen cessation support across oncology settings.

Perceiving smoking as harmful after cancer diagnosis has been linked to higher cessation success rates [5, 9, 12, 18]. Our item‐by‐item analysis revealed that caregivers are significantly more likely than patients to believe that continued smoking detrimentally affects quality of life, overall well‐being, and the family environment. Moreover, caregivers overwhelmingly perceive that smoking exacerbates patient fatigue and diminishes treatment efficacy—indicating strong opposition to continued smoking—whereas patients often underestimate these risks.

Notably, only approximately half of both patients and caregivers felt that the medical team provided adequate support for smoking cessation [10, 15, 23]. It is important to note that all participants were interviewed in the oncologic outpatient clinic of a public, academic hospital in Brazil. Brazil's Strategic Action Plan to Tackle Non‐communicable Diseases (2011–2022) and the National Policy on Tobacco Control have led to significant advances in tobacco regulation, such as the promotion of smoke‐free environments and the inclusion of warnings on cigarette packaging. As a result, smoking prevalence among healthy adults decreased from 18.2% in 2008 to 14.7% in 2013, and continues to decline, reaching approximately 9.3% in 2023 [14, 16]. However, for cancer patients and other individuals who are currently smoking within the public health system, the infrastructure for smoking cessation remains limited [7, 14, 18, 20]. Unlike many developed countries, Brazil lacks a nationwide, personalized smoking cessation program; existing initiatives are typically tied to academic centers and focus more on education rather than comprehensive counseling, pharmacotherapy, and nicotine replacement [15, 18, 20]. Consequently, organizational and individual barriers contribute to low uptake of smoking cessation services among cancer patients.

Clinicians treating cancer patients often lack the time and expertise to address smoking cessation effectively, as they are overwhelmed with the complexities of cancer care and other pressing patient and family issues [10, 20, 22, 25]. Furthermore, emphasizing smoking as “the cause of cancer” may inadvertently intensify patients' feelings of guilt, thereby inhibiting open discussions about cessation [23, 26]. The absence of easily accessible, referral‐free quit‐smoking clinics in the public system further reinforces the perception among patients and caregivers that adequate support is lacking [14, 15, 18]. In fact, nearly half of our patient cohort believed that quitting smoking after cancer diagnosis would not positively influence their prognosis—citing beliefs that it was “too late to quit” or that “the damage has already been done”—while caregivers maintained a more optimistic outlook.

Age also emerged as a significant factor in both models, with older patients showing lower perception and awareness of tobacco‐related harms compared with younger individuals. This finding contrasts with previous studies, which have generally reported higher health risk awareness and greater readiness to quit among older adults [17, 18, 20, 26]. One possible explanation is that older patients may experience cognitive fatigue, treatment‐related stress, or fatalistic beliefs that reduce engagement with cessation information and perceived benefits of quitting. In contrast, younger patients might be more exposed to digital and public health campaigns and therefore more responsive to cessation messaging. These results highlight the need for age‐sensitive interventions that address motivational, cognitive, and informational barriers among older cancer patients within oncology care.

Regarding awareness‐specific outcomes, although patients and caregivers showed generally similar levels of objective knowledge, caregivers were notably more aware of the negative effects of smoking on chemotherapy and radiotherapy efficacy and on quality of life during treatment. Interestingly, 46% of the patients reported smoking unconventional cigarettes (including straw and rope cigarettes). Traditionally used in rural areas, these unfiltered products have gained popularity in urban settings in recent years, partly due to branding that promotes them as “natural” or even “organic.” However, the health risks associated with these products remain uncertain, given the variability in tobacco and nicotine content [16]. Notably, our analysis indicated that patients who smoked manufactured cigarettes were three times more likely to feel supported in their cessation efforts compared with straw/rope cigarette smokers. An exploratory analysis suggested that this difference might be partially related to higher educational attainment among manufactured cigarette smokers, although this trend did not reach statistical significance. Clearly, there is a substantial knowledge gap regarding the risks of straw and rope cigarettes—a gap that should be addressed by both the medical community and policymakers in Brazil.

Our multivariable analysis further demonstrated that, after adjustment for key covariates, patients with higher nicotine dependence (Fagerström score) and those who smoked exclusively manufactured cigarettes exhibited heightened perception of tobacco's harms after cancer diagnosis. While no statistically significant associations were found in the awareness model, trends in certain variables, such as cigarette type, suggest potential areas for further research.

Time since diagnosis also appears to be an important contextual factor influencing patients' perceptions and awareness of tobacco‐related harms. In our study, more than half of the participants had been diagnosed within the previous year, while nearly one‐quarter had lived with cancer for 3 years or longer. This temporal variability may partly explain differences in attitudes toward smoking, as patients recently diagnosed may experience greater emotional distress and be more receptive to cessation advice, whereas those living with cancer for longer periods may develop adaptive beliefs or desensitization toward tobacco risks [9, 11, 17, 18, 26]. These observations reinforce the need for longitudinal approaches and tailored cessation strategies that consider the stage of the patient's cancer journey and the evolving psychological and behavioral context over time.

Our analyses included comprehensive assessment of multicollinearity, supplementary linear regression modeling, and interaction testing to ensure the robustness of our findings. The reversal of the education effect between univariable and multivariable models was explained by confounding: patients with higher education were more likely to use manufactured cigarettes, which are associated with higher harm perception. Supplementary linear regression analyses confirmed our main findings, with consistent direction and magnitude of associations across dichotomized and continuous analytical approaches, validating our methodological choices. An exploratory finding of particular interest was the significant education × Fagerström interaction (OR = 16.42, p = 0.006), suggesting that nicotine dependence effects on harm perception are amplified in patients with higher education. This may reflect synergy between cognitive advantages of education and physiological effects of dependence or increased cognitive dissonance in educated smokers. However, given sample size limitations, this interaction requires replication in larger samples before informing interventions.

This study has several limitations. First, although our sample of patients and caregivers was substantial, the analysis was conducted at a single center within Brazil's public health system. This may limit the generalizability of our findings in a country characterized by highly heterogeneous public and private healthcare sectors. Second, recruitment was restricted to patients whose primary caregivers were present and willing to participate, which may introduce selection bias. Demographic or clinical information was not collected for ineligible or non‐consenting patients, precluding direct comparison between participants and those not enrolled. Nevertheless, the high response rate (72%) suggests that the risk of substantial selection bias is likely limited. Third, in dichotomizing awareness outcomes, responses of “I don't know” were treated as missing data, which may have affected results in approximately 7% of cases. It is also important to note that an “I don't know” option was not available for perception outcomes. Finally, we did not collect detailed information on caregivers' smoking history—such as duration, intensity, or cessation attempts—but only whether they were current smokers, which may have influenced their perceptions of smoking‐related harms and their comparative awareness relative to patients. Although all patients included in the study were current smokers, nearly one in four caregivers (23.6%) also reported smoking, highlighting shared behavioral risks within patient–caregiver pairs.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that cancer patients who continue smoking tend to underestimate the harmful effects of tobacco relative to their caregivers, despite having similar levels of objective awareness regarding its impact on treatment and overall health. Moreover, nearly half of both patients and caregivers reported insufficient support from the medical team for smoking cessation—especially among those using straw/rope cigarettes.

In light of the current findings and global literature, we propose that smoking cessation be reframed not merely as secondary prevention, but as an integral component of cancer treatment. This paradigm shift is especially critical in healthcare systems like Brazil's, where institutional support remains limited and alternative tobacco products are increasingly normalized. By tailoring cessation interventions to cultural practices, incorporating family dynamics, and addressing the timing of diagnosis, clinicians and policymakers can optimize cancer care outcomes and bridge a crucial gap in public health.
