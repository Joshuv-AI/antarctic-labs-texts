Primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraception counselling and care: a rapid review
Authors: Jenna Perkins, Sian Stuckey, Danielle Mazza, Jessica R Botfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary healthcare practitioners play a pivotal role in postpartum care. Many countries encourage women to attend a postnatal check after birth, providing an ideal opportunity for contraceptive care. However, contraception is often not discussed, increasing the risk of unintended pregnancy and short interpregnancy intervals.ObjectiveTo explore primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraceptive counselling and care to better understand the gaps, needs, and opportunities.MethodsA rapid review was conducted of English language peer-reviewed primary research studies from high-income countries focussing on primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraceptive counselling and care. Studies were retrieved from five databases, supplemented by Google Scholar and citation searching. Key characteristics were extracted, and thematic analysis identified key themes.ResultsAltogether, 2255 references were identified, with eight studies included. Four themes were constructed: (1) varied knowledge and attitudes regarding postpartum contraception, including views on of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), safety concerns, and optimal counselling timing; (2) structural and logistical barriers to contraception provision in primary healthcare, including lack of LARC availability, time constraints, and funding issues; (3) limited contraception education and training for practitioners in LARC provision, with practitioners lacking skills or motivation to upskill; and (4) gaps in postpartum contraception resources and guidelines needed to promote postpartum contraceptive care.ConclusionImproving postpartum contraceptive counselling requires embedding comprehensive contraceptive training in healthcare education, developing standardized guidelines for postpartum appointments, and addressing barriers such as time constraints, lack of resources and funding to support effective contraception care for postpartum women.Keywords: primary care, reproductive health/family planning/contraception, public health, sexual health, psychotherapy/counseling

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends “comprehensive contraception information and services be provided during antenatal and postpartum care” [1] to support decision-making and facilitate access to contraception in the postpartum period [2]. Primary healthcare practitioners, including general practitioners or family physicians, practice nurses, midwives, and allied health professionals, play a major role in providing postpartum care including contraception services [3].

In many countries, women are encouraged to attend a postnatal check with a primary healthcare practitioner 6-to-8 weeks after giving birth [4]. Comprising physical, emotional, and social assessments, the postnatal check aims to support and improve the health and well-being of both the mother and infant. It also provides an ideal opportunity for contraceptive counselling and care [5]. Educating women about fertility return and the range of contraception options available is important. These include short and long-acting reversible contraception (LARC), the lactational amenorrhea and barrier methods. Providing information and facilitating access to provision of contraception, if desired, can help reduce the risk of an unintended pregnancy and/or short interpregnancy intervals, both of which can lead to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [6].

However, contraception is often not discussed during postpartum appointments, as it is not prioritized by practitioners or mothers due to other presenting concerns and competing priorities, particularly those related to their infant [6, 7]. This lack of prioritization of contraception may be influenced by a range of factors such as limited awareness of safe and effective contraception options, time during appointments, and challenges relating to costs [8, 9].

We were interested in reviewing and identifying what is known about how primary healthcare practitioners themselves view and experience the provision of contraceptive information and care during the postpartum period. To better understand the gaps, needs, and opportunities for improved care in this area, we conducted a rapid review to explore primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences in providing postpartum contraceptive counselling and care.

Methods

Search strategy

Our rapid review was informed by Cochrane Rapid Review methodology [10]. This review methodology aims to synthesize knowledge using a systematic review approach with simplification and omittance of some processes to allow researchers to gain an overview of a particular area of research within a timely manner [11]. Following consultation with a university librarian and search term finalization, five databases were searched on 7 April 2025: Ovid Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Scopus (Supplementary Appendix a). Google Scholar and reference list searching were also used to search for studies. The search keywords were “general practitioners”, “family physicians”, “primary healthcare”, “postpartum period,” and “post-natal care”.

All references obtained from the database search were imported into Covidence systematic review software [12] for screening and data extraction. Duplicates were removed prior to title and abstract screening. Relevant studies found through Google Scholar and reference list searches were also added to Covidence. Two reviewers (JP and SS) independently conducted title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening to assess eligibility. Reasons for exclusion were listed and recorded for all ineligible studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers, and there was no need for adjudication from a third reviewer.

Inclusion criteria

Peer-reviewed, English-language primary research studies were eligible for inclusion if they focused on primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraceptive counselling and care within primary healthcare settings in high-income countries as defined by The World Bank Group [13]. Primary healthcare practitioners eligible for inclusion in this review included general practitioners or family physicians. Studies addressing any form of postpartum contraception were eligible for inclusion. Grey literature, conference abstracts, theses, and reviews were excluded, as well as studies focusing on hospital or specialist care settings/providers.

Data extraction and analysis

JP extracted relevant data from the eligible studies and documented them in an Excel spreadsheet, including characteristics of included studies and key findings from each study (Table 1). Thematic analysis, informed by Braun and Clarke [22], was conducted to identify and synthesize key themes following the six phases of analysis: data familiarization (phase 1), initial code generation (phase 2), key theme searching (phase 3), review of identified themes (phase 4), theme defining and naming (phase 5), and production of the final report (phase 6) [22]. A quality assessment of included studies was also undertaken using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool to appraise methodological quality [23].

Results

We identified 2251 references through database searching and an additional four through citation searching, totalling 2255 potential studies. Figure 1 shows the flow chart of the screening process following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Appendix b) [24]. Sixty full-text studies were assessed for eligibility, and eight met the inclusion criteria. All included studies were deemed to be of moderate or high quality in the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool quality assessment undertaken, which appraises the methodological quality of included studies (Supplementary Appendix c) [23].

PRISMA diagram depicting the article screening process.

Study characteristics

Overall, eight studies were included in the review. Table 1 outlines the key characteristics and findings from each study. Of the eight included studies, four were from the USA [6, 16, 18, 20], two from the UK [15, 17], one from Finland [21], and one Australia [19]. The majority of studies (n = 6, 75%) [6, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21] were quantitative using cross-sectional surveys, and two were qualitative using semi-structured interviews (n = 2, 25%) [17, 19].

Although all studies addressed primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of undertaking postpartum contraception counselling and care, only two studies focussed solely on primary healthcare practitioners [17, 19]. The other six studies also included other health professionals such as obstetricians, gynaecologists, and nurses [6, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21].

All but one study specifically focused on the provision of contraception to postpartum women [6, 15, 17, 18, 20, 21]. The remaining study also examined contraception provision among other population groups, including adolescents and post-abortion women [16]. The majority of studies focused predominantly on LARC provision in the postpartum period exclusively or as their primary outcome [14, 15, 17-20], while two included other methods such as oral contraception, condoms, or sterilization [16, 20].

Thematic analysis

We constructed four themes relating to primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraception counselling and care from the eight included studies: (1) varied knowledge and attitudes regarding postpartum contraception; (2) structural and logistical barriers to contraception provision in primary healthcare; (3) limited contraception education and training for practitioners in LARC provision; and (4) gaps in postpartum contraception resources and guidelines.

Theme 1. Varied knowledge and attitudes regarding postpartum contraception

Theme 1.1. Contraception types prescribed and influencing factors

Primary healthcare practitioners demonstrated varied attitudes towards contraception care in the postpartum period and their prescribing practices. While many acknowledged the importance of contraception use postnatally and recognized the effectiveness and convenience of LARC methods in particular, many also had concerns about the appropriateness and safety of contraception in the postpartum period [14, 15, 17, 19, 21].

Practitioners expressed a range of views regarding postpartum contraception, particularly in relation to LARC. Many practitioners supported expanding access to LARC, viewing it as an important option for postpartum contraception care [15]. Others emphasized the need to tailor contraception discussions to individual circumstances, often suggesting methods such as implants or intrauterine devices (IUDs) for women not planning pregnancy in the near future [19]. In contrast, a few practitioners expressed more negative views towards LARC, perceiving it as burdensome or inconvenient to provide [17]. This variation in attitudes was reflected in reported prescribing practices; for example, a survey study in Finland reported at the postnatal check that 28% of participants said they would initiate progestin only pill, while fewer offered copper IUDs (12%), levonorgestrel IUDs (7%), or implants (1%) [21].

Concerns about the safety of postpartum LARC provision were reported in most studies [14, 15, 18, 20]. In a US study of primary healthcare practitioners’ views on LARC, although most (91%) of the surveyed participants believed that IUDs are safe, less than half (43%) believed IUDs could be safely inserted postpartum [14]. Similarly, less than half (41%) of practitioners in a UK study reported being comfortable promoting postpartum IUD provision [15], and approximately one-third of practitioners in a US study considered immediate postpartum LARC insertion to be a contraindication [18]. Commonly mentioned safety concerns included method contraindications, risk of expulsion or uterine perforation, impacts on breastfeeding, and potential effects on future fertility [14, 18, 20].

Theme 1.2. Approaches to contraceptive counselling and responsibility for postpartum contraception

Practitioners also expressed varied views on the optimal timing and approach for initiating contraception discussions. While many practitioners considered the traditional 6–8 week postnatal check to be a good time to initiate contraception discussions, particularly as women may resume intercourse around this time [17], others felt this was too late, particularly for women who did not attend the appointment [17, 19]. Some practitioners from a UK study investigating practitioners views on their role in delivering contraception postpartum and providing LARC highlighted that women can become pregnant again soon after birth, suggesting that waiting until the postnatal check risks missing opportunities for timely contraception provision [17]. Other practitioners described some women not being receptive to contraception counselling during the postnatal check, suggesting that earlier counselling during antenatal visits or in hospital after birth could improve receptiveness and overall engagement [19]. However, they recognized that opportunities for earlier conversations in primary care settings may be limited when midwives or obstetricians manage antenatal care, and questioned who should lead postpartum contraception counselling [17, 19]. In a UK study, some participants believed they should take primary responsibility due to their ability to prescribe and their access to the mother's medical history [17], while others suggested a more multidisciplinary approach, suggesting that midwives or health visitors might be better placed to initiate contraception conversations earlier [17, 19].

These reported views and experiences highlight that both practitioner attitudes and knowledge and patient receptiveness likely influence the implementation and provision of postpartum contraception counselling and care.

Theme 2. Structural and logistical barriers to contraception provision in primary healthcare

Structural and logistical challenges were reported as major barriers to the provision of postpartum contraception care in primary healthcare settings. Many practitioners highlighted issues such as lack of onsite LARC availability, limited appointment time, trained staffing shortages, and funding constraints [14-17, 19].

Onsite availability of LARC devices varied considerably across primary healthcare settings [14, 17-19]. In one US survey of primary healthcare practitioners within family planning access care and treatment (PACT) sites, two-thirds (65%) of practitioners had IUDs available onsite and one-third (34%) had implants. Additionally, less than one-third (29%) of practitioners were able to offer same-day LARC insertion [14]. Across different countries and settings, practices where LARC devices were not stocked onsite but insertions were offered, women had to attend multiple appointments, for counselling and obtaining a prescription, before collecting the device from a pharmacy, and returning for insertion [17, 19].

Time constraints during the postnatal check were also identified as a consistent barrier that limited provision of comprehensive contraception counselling [15, 17, 19]. Competing demands, including assessment of the newborn and addressing maternal physical and mental health concerns, often reduced the time available to discuss contraception [19]. Participants reported that consultations usually tended to prioritize infant care or more immediate maternal concerns, with less attention given to postpartum contraceptive needs [17]. Time pressure was particularly problematic when caring for women perceived to require more support or nuanced counselling [17, 19]. Practitioners described challenges in delivering effective contraception discussions with certain groups, including young mothers at higher risk of early repeat pregnancy, women with complex social or medical needs, those with limited English proficiency or living in deprived circumstances such as rural/remote locations. These consultations often demanded more time and sensitivity, which was not always available within standard appointment durations [17].

Logistical issues related to staffing were reported as barriers impacting the provision of postpartum contraceptive care [16, 18, 19]. This included a lack of primary health practitioners such as general practitioners and practice nurses able to insert or even assist with LARC methods, which limited availability [17, 19]. In a UK-based study, some women faced wait times of up to 2 to 3 months for IUD insertion due to insufficient availability of trained practitioners [17].

Issues relating to funding and costs to practitioners and practices were also identified as a barrier to providing postpartum contraception. Practitioners reported inadequate reimbursement, high costs associated with training, upfront investment in purchasing contraceptive devices, and a lack of financial incentives to offer postpartum contraception services [18, 19]. Participants in the Australian study suggested that financial incentives, such as increased rebates from the government for postnatal checks and contraception care, could motivate primary healthcare practitioners to pursue training and expand contraception service provision.

Theme 3. Limited contraception education and training for practitioners in LARC provision

A lack of practitioner education and training was commonly identified as a barrier to providing postpartum contraceptive care, particularly in relation to LARC methods [14, 15, 18-20]. A US study reported that 31% of primary healthcare practitioners identified a lack of skill as a barrier to contraception provision, particularly LARC [18]. Across multiple studies, many participants reported lacking the necessary skills or confidence to insert IUDs or implants, with some relying on referrals to other practitioners due to their own lack of training [14, 18, 19]. In one US study surveying resident primary healthcare practitioners, only 5% of participants were trained in IUD insertion, 42% in implant insertion, and only 4% in both IUD and implant insertion [20]. Similarly, another US survey study found that less than half (43%) of participants were skilled in inserting at least one LARC method [18]. In the Australian study, while all participants were trained in implant insertion, only about half (52%) had received training in IUD insertion [19].

Barriers to contraception training and education were reported, including practitioners having limited time to attend training, long course waitlists, high course fees, and travel-related costs [15, 18, 19]. Australian practitioners highlighted the need for training formats that are better suited to primary healthcare practitioners, such as shorter, bite-sized modules with flexible delivery modes [19]. This sentiment was echoed in a UK study, where a participant described concerns about the high workload associated with current training models and the need for more sustainable approaches [15]. Across studies, participants emphasized the importance of increasing access to practical, flexible, and time-efficient training opportunities to build practitioner confidence and enhance the delivery of postpartum contraceptive services in primary healthcare [18, 19]. Others also highlighted the importance of having opportunities to perform LARC insertions in their practice after being trained, in order to maintain their skills [14, 19].

Some practitioners also expressed having limited motivation to pursue education and training opportunities, particularly for LARC methods [14, 15, 19]. In a UK study, few (11%) practitioners expressed interest in receiving training for postpartum IUD provision [15]. This lack of motivation was attributed to several factors including perceived low patient demand, with some practitioners believing that few postpartum women are interested in LARC [14, 19]. Additionally, some practitioners felt that LARC insertion was outside their scope of practice and saw no need to upskill when they could easily refer patients to other trained practitioners [15, 19]. Religious affiliation may also impact practitioners interest in upskilling [16, 18, 19]. A US study found that 89% of practitioners with no religious affiliation believed contraception should be considered “standard medical practice,” compared to 69% of those with a religious affiliation [16].

Theme 4: Gaps in postpartum contraception resources and guidelines

Across studies, primary healthcare practitioners reported inconsistencies in the provision of postpartum contraceptive care, which they commonly linked to the lack of clear guidelines on postnatal appointments and limited practical resources such as suitable contraceptive options postpartum [14, 16, 18, 19]. Without a standardized guideline for postnatal checks, primary healthcare practitioners relied heavily on personal experience, informal protocols, or hospital-derived guides to support contraceptive counselling during the postnatal check, which they felt contributed to missed opportunities for contraception counselling and provision [19]. A US survey study found that while most participants (71%) believed contraception counselling and family planning should be a standard service, nearly one-third (29%) viewed it as a supplementary service provided on request. The same study also found that participants were more likely to offer contraception counselling based on the number of children a woman had, with 71% doing so after one child, 82% after three children, and 84% after five [16].

In situations where contraceptive counselling was provided, studies reported variation in the contraceptive methods that practitioners typically recommended and provided postpartum [17, 21]. In a UK survey study, almost all practitioners identified oral contraceptives as the method of postpartum contraception they were providing most commonly to women. Participants in all studies addressing guidelines suggested that standardized guidelines for the postnatal check and resources outlining appropriate postpartum contraception options could help ensure consistency in postpartum care, reduce variation in counselling content, and strengthen practitioner confidence [14, 18, 19].

Discussion

This review provides insights into the views and experiences of primary healthcare practitioners on the provision of postpartum contraceptive counselling and care in high-income countries. Despite the postpartum period being a critical window to promote contraceptive awareness and uptake, it remains underutilized by primary healthcare practitioners, leaving women potentially at risk of an unintended pregnancy and short interpregnancy interval. Across the eight included studies, practitioners showed varied knowledge and attitudes towards postpartum contraception care, particularly regarding LARC methods, safety concerns of contraception use postpartum, and the optimal timing for contraceptive discussions. Structural and logistical barriers to contraception provision in primary healthcare were also identified, including limited availability of LARC devices, time constraints during appointments, shortage of trained staff, and funding issues. Additionally, practitioners reported contraception is not currently included in training, and many practitioners are unable or unmotivated to pursue additional training to provide comprehensive postpartum contraception care. Finally, there is a need for resources for postpartum contraception options and guidelines for postnatal checks to support comprehensive contraceptive care during the postpartum period.

Practitioners demonstrated diverse knowledge and attitudes towards postpartum contraception with many recognizing the value of early contraceptive intervention in the postpartum period [14, 15, 17, 19]. However, reservations were also common, often rooted in concerns about the appropriateness or safety of LARC methods in the postpartum period [14, 15, 18, 20]. Another study in the UK found that 40% of surveyed practitioners considered postpartum IUD insertion to be unsafe, reflecting substantial clinical hesitancy [25]. Such concerns, whether grounded in current evidence or reflecting outdated knowledge and limited training, can significantly influence whether practitioners provide contraception counselling, LARC insertion, or refer women to specialist services [15, 17, 19]. Familiarising practitioners with the benefits, risks, and contraindications associated with contraception use, particularly in the context of breastfeeding, may ease concerns around safety of contraception use postpartum [25, 26]. When appropriately trained and supported, practitioners are more likely to initiate contraception discussions and offer a broader range of methods, including LARC [6, 27, 28]. Integrating comprehensive educational content and advanced training into medical and nursing degrees and continuing professional development programmes is essential to strengthen practitioners’ confidence and competence in delivering contraception care during the postpartum period and beyond [19, 29].

Practitioners in our included studies reported an absence of clear, standardized guidelines for postnatal checks and resources on postpartum contraception options, which they felt led to inconsistent practices or minimal to no discussion of contraception. This lack of guidance was perceived to delay contraception initiation, increasing the risk of unintended pregnancy and short interpregnancy intervals [30]. Some international guidelines, such as the UK Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use, provide guidance on postpartum contraception, including recommendations for use during breastfeeding, and may serve as useful references for practitioners without a national guideline [31]. Structural and logistical barriers further compound these challenges. Lack of on-site contraceptive devices, appointment time constraints, staff shortages, and limited funding were consistently identified across studies as barriers restricting effective contraception care [14-19]. When LARC methods are not available on-site, women must navigate multiple appointments for counselling, device collection, and insertion, leading to delays and an increased risk of disengagement from care [9, 32]. Time constraints within consultations further reduce opportunities for contraceptive counselling, as postpartum appointments are often brief and focused on infant health and broader maternal concerns [15, 17, 19, 29]. Additionally, many primary healthcare settings lacked adequately trained practitioners and support staff, such as practice nurses, who feel confident or competent in providing LARC [16, 18, 19]. A national survey from the USA found that less than half (42%) of family physicians reported feeling comfortable inserting IUDs, with even fewer (11%) confident in implant insertion [33].

Limited financial support for education and training further restricts capacity, as upskilling in LARC provision, as with other areas of healthcare, often requires practitioners to pay for courses, cover travel expenses, and forgo income while attending training [34, 35]. Developing clear postnatal check guidelines that outline when and how to offer contraceptive options, alongside funding models that prioritize women's sexual and reproductive health and reduce financial barriers for practitioners could help ensure that all women receive timely, consistent contraception counselling and access after birth.

Limitations

The limitations of this review should be considered when interpreting the findings. Compared to a systematic review, a rapid review typically involves a less comprehensive search, and findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously, acknowledging the potential for limitations and biases [10, 11]. Our search strategy may have excluded papers with valuable insights that were framed from perspectives outside of our inclusion criteria, including postpartum women themselves. Although we intentionally focused our review on high-income countries to promote comparability of healthcare systems, there is still considerable variation in service delivery models, funding structures, and workforce capacity both within and across high-income countries. These differences may limit the generalisability of our findings, even within high-income settings. Additionally, our literature search was restricted to peer-reviewed articles published in English language, which may have further excluded relevant research published in other languages or in other formats such as grey literature or preprints, introducing potential publication bias.

While this review set out to explore postpartum contraceptive counselling and care broadly, the available evidence was predominantly LARC-focused. This reflects a gap in the existing literature rather than the scope of the review itself. Consequently, the findings and recommendations reported here are more strongly informed by evidence relating to LARC provision, with limited insight into practitioners’ views and experiences of providing non-LARC postpartum contraceptive options.

Future research

Future research should focus on the development of targeted educational resources for primary healthcare practitioners to enhance knowledge and confidence in discussing and providing contraception during the postpartum period, as well as on patient-facing resources communicating the importance of postpartum contraception, available method options, and how to access them. In addition, future studies should explore primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing non-LARC postpartum contraception, as the absence of such studies in this review highlights an important gap. The development of a standardized, evidence-based guideline for the 6-to-8-week postnatal check that includes contraception counselling is also warranted. Such tools and resources should be evaluated for their effectiveness in improving practitioner's and women's knowledge, supporting women's decision-making, and facilitating timely access to women's preferred contraceptive method.

Conclusion

Our rapid review explored primary healthcare practitioners’ views and experiences of providing postpartum contraception counselling and care in high-income countries. Findings highlight significant variability in practitioner knowledge and attitudes towards postpartum contraception, particularly LARC, persistent structural and logistical barriers such as consultation time constraints, funding issues, and limited access to contraceptive devices onsite, gaps in contraception education and training, and guideline support for postnatal checks and postpartum contraception options. Although the early postpartum period presents a key opportunity to address women's contraceptive needs, it is clear that it remains underutilized in primary healthcare settings. Addressing the barriers identified in this review by improving structural and logistical supports, such as funding and device availability, integrating comprehensive contraception training into medical and nursing education, and developing standardized guidelines for postnatal checks that include contraception counselling and resources for postpartum contraception options, is essential to better enable and support primary healthcare practitioners to provide postpartum contraception care to women.
