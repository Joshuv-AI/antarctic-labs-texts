Development and internal validation of a prognostic nomogram for poor outcomes in neonatal bacterial meningitis
Authors: Min Yu, Yiping Fang, Xiaofeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal bacterial meningitis (NBM) remains a life-threatening condition with considerable mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment. Early prognostic assessment is challenging, and prediction models tailored to neonates are limited. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic nomogram for predicting poor outcomes in neonates with bacterial meningitis.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted including 214 neonates diagnosed with bacterial meningitis between 2011 and 2024. Independent predictors of poor outcome were identified using Firth penalized logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias. A prognostic nomogram was developed and internally validated using bootstrap resampling to assess model performance.ResultsAmong the 214 neonates, 83 (38.8%) experienced poor outcomes. Lower gestational age, seizures, elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, and reduced CSF glucose were independently associated with poor prognosis. The model demonstrated good discrimination, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.79 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.72–0.85] and an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.77 after bootstrap validation. Calibration analysis showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities. Decision curve analysis suggested net clinical benefit across threshold probabilities ranging from 0.05 to 0.65.ConclusionsThis model integrates inflammatory biomarkers reflecting central nervous system injury with clinical parameters, providing a potential bridge between pathophysiological mechanisms and bedside risk stratification. External validation in independent cohorts is warranted prior to routine clinical application.Keywords: Neonatal bacterial meningitis (NBM), prognosis, nomogram, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), Firth logistic regression

Highlight box

Key findings

• A prognostic model incorporating gestational age, seizures, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, white blood cell count, and CSF glucose was developed to predict poor outcomes in neonates with bacterial meningitis. The model demonstrated good discrimination (C-index 0.79; optimism-corrected 0.77) and acceptable calibration after bootstrap validation. Elevated CSF protein and seizures were strong independent predictors of poor prognosis.

What is known and what is new?

• Neonatal bacterial meningitis is associated with substantial mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae, and early risk stratification remains challenging. Previous studies have identified potential risk factors; however, robust prognostic models specifically for neonates are limited.

• This study provides a Firth penalized logistic regression-based prediction model with internal validation and a clinically applicable nomogram for individualized risk estimation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed model may assist clinicians in early identification of high-risk neonates and guide timely intervention and monitoring strategies. Implementation of risk-based stratification could improve clinical decision-making and optimize resource allocation in neonatal intensive care settings. External validation in multicenter cohorts is warranted before routine clinical adoption.

Introduction

Neonatal bacterial meningitis (NBM) is one of the most severe central nervous system infections in the neonatal period, characterized by insidious onset, rapid progression, and high mortality and morbidity rates (1,2). Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and neonatal intensive care, a considerable proportion of affected neonates experiences adverse outcomes, including death, seizures, hydrocephalus, and persistent neurological deficits (1,3,4). Early identification of high-risk patients remains critical for optimizing clinical management and improving outcomes.

However, the clinical manifestations of NBM are often nonspecific, making early diagnosis and prognosis assessment particularly challenging (5,6). Several clinical and laboratory indicators—such as seizures, elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein, and decreased CSF glucose—have been individually associated with adverse outcomes in NBM. However, these predictors are often interpreted in isolation in routine practice. Clinicians currently lack an integrated and quantitatively validated tool that combines routinely available indicators to estimate individualized risk.

Multivariable prediction modeling provides an opportunity to translate combined clinical information into individualized probability estimates. A well-constructed and validated prognostic model may improve bedside decision-making by enabling risk-stratified management strategies rather than relying solely on single risk markers.

Existing prognostic scoring systems, mainly derived from adult or pediatric populations, cannot be directly applied to neonates due to their distinct physiological characteristics, including immune immaturity, nonspecific clinical presentation, and gestational age-dependent variation in physiological parameters such as CSF composition (7-10). Moreover, there is a lack of data-driven, internally validated prognostic models specifically tailored to neonates, particularly within the Chinese population.

Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and internally validate a multivariable prognostic model for predicting poor outcomes in neonates with bacterial meningitis using routinely available clinical parameters. Furthermore, we sought to construct a clinically applicable nomogram and evaluate its discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility to facilitate individualized risk assessment in neonatal intensive care settings. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0206/rc).

Methods

Study population

This study was a retrospective review of medical records of neonates with NBM. A total of 256 neonates treated for bacterial meningitis in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University between January 2011 and January 2024 were initially identified. After applying the exclusion criteria, 42 cases were excluded, and 214 neonates were ultimately included in the analysis (Figure 1). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (approval No. 2023CS193). Given the retrospective design and use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

The flow chart of study design. Flow diagram illustrating the inclusion and exclusion of neonates with bacterial meningitis in the study cohort. NBM, neonatal bacterial meningitis.

NBM was diagnosed based on compatible clinical manifestations in conjunction with CSF examination and/or microbiological evidence. Confirmed NBM was defined as a positive CSF culture for a bacterial pathogen; or a positive blood culture for a recognized bacterial pathogen in the presence of abnormal CSF findings consistent with meningitis. Probable NBM was defined as neonates with clinical features suggestive of meningitis and abnormal CSF parameters but without microbiological confirmation.

Abnormal CSF parameters were defined according to gestational age-specific thresholds. In term neonates: CSF leukocyte count >32×106/L, protein >1,700 mg/L, or glucose <1.9 mmol/L (34 mg/dL); in preterm neonates: CSF leukocyte count >29×106/L, protein >1,500 mg/L, or glucose <1.3 mmol/L (24 mg/dL) (11).

Exclusion criteria: neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, bilirubin encephalopathy, congenital metabolic disorders, genetic syndromes, chromosomal abnormalities, or those who discontinued treatment with unknown outcomes.

Because CSF parameters were used for both diagnosis and prognosis, sensitivity analysis was restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases to minimize incorporation bias. CSF protein was dichotomized at 2.0 g/L based on clinical distribution and prior literature to enhance prognostic discrimination rather than diagnostic classification (12,13). To assess the robustness of this categorization, CSF protein was additionally modeled as a continuous variable in sensitivity analysis. Gestational age was categorized as term (≥37 weeks) and preterm (<37 weeks) for regression modeling. To assess potential temporal bias, cases were categorized into two treatment eras.

Outcome data were determined on the day of discharge from hospital. The clinical status was evaluated with the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) which ranges from 1 to 5: 1= death; 2= persistent vegetative state; 3= severe disability; 4= moderate disability; 5= good recovery. Moderate or severe disability was defined as any of the following conditions: spasticity, muscle weakness and immobility in one or more limbs; microcephaly; hydrocephalus; seizure disorder; hearing loss. For the purpose of this analysis, patients with score 5 were defined as having favorable outcome while patients with score 1 to 4 were defined as having poor outcome (13). This dichotomization was chosen to reflect clinically meaningful neurological impairment at discharge. To assess the robustness of the outcome definition, sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative categorizations of GOS, including GOS 1–2 vs. 3–5 and GOS 1–3 vs. 4–5.

Data collection

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic medical records, including demographic information (sex, age at onset, gestational age, birth and admission weight), perinatal risk factors (maternal fever), clinical manifestations (fever, hypothermia, poor feeding, seizures, jaundice, diarrhea, lethargy, abdominal distension), and laboratory results [complete blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP), CSF analysis and culture, blood culture]. Neuroimaging findings [magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)], brainstem auditory evoked potentials, treatment regimens, and outcomes were also collected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons used t-test, Mann-Whitney U, χ2, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Univariable associations were assessed using Firth logistic regression. Variables with P<0.20 entered least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation. Predictors with non-zero coefficients at λ.1se were included in multivariable Firth modeling.

Discrimination was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC); calibration with calibration plots. Internal validation used 1,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate optimism-corrected C-index and calibration slope. Clinical utility was assessed with decision curve analysis. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2) following TRIPOD guidelines. This multi-step approach was adopted to balance variable selection and model stability. Univariable Firth regression was first used to reduce small-sample bias. LASSO regression was then applied to select the most relevant predictors and prevent overfitting. Finally, multivariable Firth regression was used to obtain stable and unbiased effect estimates.

Two sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary multivariable model. (I) The primary model was rerun using a more stringent case definition for confirmed NBM; (II) To evaluate whether the use of a binary cut-off (>2.0 g/L) affected the results, CSF protein was alternatively modeled as a continuous variable in the same Firth logistic regression framework.

The events-per-variable (EPV) was calculated to assess the adequacy of the sample size for model development. In this study, the EPV was approximately 16.6 (83 events and 5 predictors), indicating an acceptable level to reduce the risk of overfitting. Additional methodological details are provided in Appendix 1. The R code used for model development and validation is available in Appendix 2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 214 neonates were included, of whom 83 (38.8%) experienced poor outcomes. Among the cohort, 103 (48.1%) were classified as confirmed cases and 111 (51.9%) as probable cases. Preterm infants accounted for 24.3% of the population. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort comprised 121 males (56.5%) and 93 females (43.5%). The median age at onset was 10 days (IQR 5–19), and the median birth weight was 3.2 kg (IQR 2.7–3.6). Maternal fever was reported in 10 cases (4.7%), while neonatal fever and hypothermia occurred in 159 (74.3%) and 17 (7.9%) cases, respectively. Common clinical manifestations included poor feeding (52.3%), lethargy (36.0%), jaundice (34.6%), seizures (11.2%), abdominal distension (8.9%), and diarrhea (1.9%).

Laboratory findings showed a median peripheral white blood cell (WBC) count of 11.2×109/L (IQR 7.3–16.5×109/L), platelet count 255.0×109/L (IQR 151.0–353.0×109/L), and CRP level of 25.3 mg/L (IQR 3.3–80.9 mg/L). CSF analysis revealed a median WBC count of 132.0×106/L (IQR 36.0–1,060.0×106/L), glucose 2.2 mmol/L (IQR 1.5–2.7 mmol/L), and protein 1474.0 mg/L (IQR 902.0–2,354.0 mg/L). Blood cultures were positive in 89 neonates (41.6%), and CSF cultures were positive in 47 (22.0%). The most frequently isolated pathogen was Escherichia coli (35 cases, 16.4%), followed by group B Streptococcus (29 cases, 13.6%), Staphylococcus hominis (14 cases, 6.5%), Klebsiella species (9 cases, 4.2%), Listeria monocytogenes (8 cases, 3.7%), and other organisms (8 cases, 3.7%) (Table 1).

Comparison between prognostic groups

Of the 214 neonates, 131 had favorable outcomes and 83 had poor outcomes. Compared with the favorable outcomes, those with poor outcomes had a younger age at onset (P=0.02), lower gestational age (term vs. preterm; P=0.04), and lower birth weight (P=0.01). The incidences of fever (P=0.048), poor feeding (P=0.005), seizures (P=0.001), and lethargy (P=0.005) were significantly higher in the poor outcome group. No significant difference in calendar period distribution was observed between the two groups (P=0.41).

Regarding laboratory parameters, peripheral blood WBC count (P=0.046) and CSF protein level (P<0.001) were significantly higher in the poor outcome group, whereas platelet count (P=0.03) and CSF glucose level (P<0.001) were significantly lower. CSF WBC count did not differ significantly between groups (P=0.16). Positive blood culture (P=0.047) and positive CSF culture (P=0.005) were also more frequent among neonates with poor outcomes (Table 1).

Logistic regression analysis

Univariable Firth penalized logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of poor outcome. Several variables were significantly associated with increased risk, including lower gestational age [odds ratio (OR) 0.40, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.21–0.75, P=0.004], younger age at onset (OR 0.97, 95% CI: 0.93–0.997, P=0.03), lower birth weight (OR 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99, P=0.007), presence of seizures (OR 4.37, 95% CI: 1.82–11.44, P<0.001), poor feeding (OR 2.32, 95% CI: 1.33–4.12, P=0.003), lethargy (OR 2.35, 95% CI: 1.33–4.17, P=0.003), need for mechanical ventilation (OR 2.56, 95% CI: 1.27–5.26, P=0.009), lower CSF glucose level (OR 0.51, 95% CI: 0.37–0.69, P<0.001), and CSF protein >2.0 g/L (OR 4.94, 95% CI: 2.71–9.19, P<0.001).

Positive blood culture (OR 1.82, 95% CI: 1.05–3.19, P=0.03), positive CSF culture (OR 2.65, 95% CI: 1.38–5.15, P=0.003), infection with group B Streptococcus (OR 2.40, 95% CI: 1.06–5.51, P=0.04), and E. coli (OR 2.37, 95% CI: 1.10–5.15, P=0.03) were also associated with poor outcomes (Table 2). Following LASSO-based variable selection from 13 candidate predictors, five variables were included in the multivariable Firth logistic regression model (Figure S1).

Construction and validation of the nomogram model

The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination, with an apparent AUC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.72–0.85) (Figure 2A). The apparent calibration slope was 0.99 with an intercept of 0.00 (Figure 2B). Decision curve analysis demonstrated greater net benefit compared with both treat-all and treat-none strategies across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Figure 2C). Internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples yielded an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.77, indicating limited overfitting (Figures S2,S3).

The optimal cutoff probability determined by the Youden index was 0.447, corresponding to a sensitivity of 69.9% and a specificity of 77.9%. To prioritize the identification of high-risk neonates, an alternative high-sensitivity threshold of approximately 0.218 was identified, yielding a sensitivity of 85.5% and a specificity of 52.7%. Conversely, a high-specificity threshold of approximately 0.524 provided a specificity of 86.2% and a sensitivity of 54.2%. Based on these thresholds, a three-tier risk stratification system was proposed: low risk (<0.218), intermediate risk (0.218–0.524), and high risk (>0.524), thereby enhancing clinical interpretability. The distribution of patients across risk groups was: low risk: 81 patients (14.8% observed poor outcome), intermediate risk: 68 patients (38.2% observed poor outcome), high risk: 65 patients (69.2% observed poor outcome) (Figure 4). A significant association was observed between risk group and actual outcome (χ2=44.99, df =2, P<0.001), indicating strong discriminatory performance of the stratification system.

The apparent calibration slope was 0.99 with an intercept of 0.00 (Figure 2B). After bootstrap correction, the optimism-corrected slope was 0.90. The likelihood ratio test confirmed overall model significance (χ2=56.09, df =5, P<0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analysis restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases, the direction and magnitude of associations remained generally consistent with the primary analysis, although some variables did not reach statistical significance, likely due to reduced sample size and statistical power (Table S5).

When CSF protein was modeled as a continuous variable, higher levels remained independently associated with poor outcome, supporting the robustness of the primary findings (Table S6). And the direction remained generally consistent across two sensitivity analyses, using alternative definitions of poor outcome (Table S7).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we developed and internally validated a nomogram for predicting poor outcomes in NBM. The model, incorporating gestational age, seizures, peripheral WBC count, CSF protein, and CSF glucose, demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.79), satisfactory calibration, and favorable clinical utility. A nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk estimation at bedside. Although the predictors identified are clinically recognized, our study integrates them into a quantitative risk prediction framework to improve individualized risk estimation.

Several predictors identified in our model have been previously associated with adverse outcomes. Prematurity and low birth weight have consistently been associated with increased mortality and long-term neurological sequelae in NBM. Prior studies have demonstrated that preterm infants with invasive bacterial infections experience higher rates of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes compared with term infants (14-17). These findings are consistent with our results and reflect the biological vulnerability of immature immune systems and incomplete blood-brain barrier development.

Seizures emerged as one of the strongest predictors in our model. In NBM, seizures typically indicate severe central nervous system involvement and may reflect acute cortical irritation, increased intracranial pressure, or established neuronal injury secondary to inflammation (15,18). The strong association observed in our cohort underscores the prognostic importance of early neurological manifestations in risk stratification.

CSF biomarkers also contributed significantly to prognostic discrimination. Increased CSF protein reflects enhanced vascular permeability and intensified inflammatory responses within the subarachnoid space, which are closely linked to neuronal damage (13,19). Whereas decreased CSF glucose reflects increased metabolic consumption by pathogens and inflammatory cells, Previous studies have shown that reduced CSF glucose levels are associated with higher bacterial burden, more severe inflammation, and worse neurological outcomes (12,20). Both parameters are routinely available in clinical practice and provide objective indicators of disease severity. Although these biomarkers are individually recognized as markers of meningitis severity, their integration into a multivariable predictive framework allows quantitative risk estimation rather than reliance on isolated laboratory thresholds.

Peripheral blood WBC count showed an association with poor outcome in univariable analysis but did not retain independent significance in the multivariable model. This likely reflects the nonspecific nature of systemic inflammatory markers in neonates and potential overlap with more disease-specific indicators such as CSF abnormalities and neurological manifestations. Notably, WBC was retained during the variable selection process, suggesting a potential contributory role in overall model stability despite its lack of independent statistical significance.

Importantly, we translated these predictors into a clinically interpretable three-tier risk stratification system. From a translational perspective, threshold-based stratification aligns with bedside decision-making and resource allocation. The progressive increase in observed poor outcome rates from low (14.8%) to intermediate (38.2%) and high risk groups (69.2%) demonstrates meaningful clinical separation. Importantly, the statistically significant gradient across groups (P<0.001) supports the robustness of the classification framework. Neonates classified as high risk (>0.524) may benefit from intensified monitoring, early neuroimaging, aggressive antimicrobial optimization, and consideration of neuroprotective strategies. Intermediate-risk patients require close surveillance, whereas low-risk neonates may avoid unnecessary escalation of interventions. Such stratification transforms a statistical model into a pragmatic clinical tool. Unlike purely diagnostic biomarker studies, our model provides a risk probability output that can directly inform bedside risk stratification and clinical monitoring intensity.

To ensure robustness of our findings, three sensitivity analyses were performed. First, the model was restricted to microbiologically confirmed cases, and overall effect estimates remained directionally consistent with the main analysis. Second, CSF protein was analyzed as a continuous variable rather than dichotomized, and the association with poor outcome persisted. Third, sensitivity analysis based on different categorizations of the GOS showed consistent results, suggesting that the model performance was not dependent on outcome definition. These results suggest that the observed relationships were not dependent on case definition or variable categorization. These analyses strengthen the internal validity of the proposed model. To improve model stability and reduce the risk of overfitting, a multi-step variable selection strategy combined with penalized regression was adopted. The EPV was 16.6, exceeding commonly recommended thresholds for clinical prediction models. In addition, bootstrap internal validation demonstrated consistent optimism-corrected performance, suggesting acceptable model stability and reproducibility despite the relatively modest sample size. However, this nomogram should not be interpreted as a standalone decision-making tool, particularly given its development based on a single-center retrospective dataset. It is intended to complement, rather than replace, clinical judgment and comprehensive evaluation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may limit generalizability. Second, although bootstrap internal validation was performed, external validation in independent cohorts is necessary before clinical implementation. Third, the inclusion of probable cases may introduce potential misclassification bias, although sensitivity analyses yielded consistent results. Fourth, outcomes were assessed at hospital discharge, and long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up was not available. In addition, dichotomization of GOS may reduce clinical granularity, and alternative approaches such as ordinal regression could provide more detailed prognostic information. Finally, causative pathogens were not included in the final multivariable model; although some pathogens were associated with outcomes in univariable analysis, their potential confounding effects cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

We developed and internally validated a clinically applicable prognostic nomogram for neonates with bacterial meningitis. The model enables individualized risk estimation and may support risk-stratified management in neonatal intensive care settings. External validation will be essential to confirm its generalizability and clinical impact.
