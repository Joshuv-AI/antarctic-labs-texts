Identification of key biomarkers and immune microenvironment features in gliomas based on single-cell analysis combined with bioinformatics
Authors: Yang Zhang, Lisha Liu, Chao Peng, Lu Wang, Jun Yang, Yingjiang Gu, Yu Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gliomas are highly invasive and heterogeneous tumors in the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by poor prognosis and significant therapeutic challenges. The comprehensive understanding of their molecular mechanisms remains a critical focus and challenge in current research. This study aims to integrate bioinformatics and single-cell analysis technologies to explore glioma-related cell types and immune cell infiltration features, providing new insights into the molecular pathogenesis of gliomas and identifying potential therapeutic targets. Gene expression profiles were selected from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and a glioma-related gene dataset was obtained from GeneCards. Single-cell analysis was employed to identify cell types, and bioinformatics techniques were applied to identify potential pathogenic targets in gliomas. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed, followed by functional enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Ultimately, drug target prediction and molecular docking analysis revealed the mechanisms of potential drugs. Single-cell analysis identified 10 cell types, with microglial cells and oligodendrocytes playing crucial roles in gliomas. Molecular biological analysis identified 20 key genes. GO and KEGG analyses indicated that these hub genes were primarily enriched in processes such as cellular component organization or biogenesis, cellular processes, cell junctions, and catalytic activity. The main signaling pathways involved include the p53 signaling pathway, cell cycle, and cellular senescence. Furthermore, molecular docking results showed that quercetin effectively binds to four hub targets (DLGAP5, TOP2A, CHEK1, MKI67), suggesting that quercetin may improve glioma-related biological features by acting on these targets. In conclusion, this study not only reveals the significant roles of specific cell types and key genes in gliomas but also preliminarily elucidates the molecular mechanisms of quercetin as a potential therapeutic agent, providing a solid theoretical foundation and new research directions for future glioma intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03450-x.

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common and aggressive primary tumors of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by high cellular heterogeneity and strong invasiveness, leading to poor clinical prognosis [1]. Based on their morphological and molecular characteristics, gliomas can be classified into various subtypes, with malignant gliomas such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) posing significant clinical challenges due to their extremely high recurrence rates and resistance to treatment [2]. Despite advances in molecular biology, genomics, and imaging techniques that have improved early diagnosis and treatment strategies for gliomas, their treatment remains highly challenging, and the pathological mechanisms are still not fully understood [3]. Therefore, an in-depth exploration of the molecular pathogenesis, cellular microenvironment, immune responses, and new potential therapeutic targets of gliomas has become a key focus in current oncological research [4].

The development and progression of gliomas involve complex molecular networks that regulate not only tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and drug resistance but also the tumor’s immune evasion [5]. The alteration of the tumor immune microenvironment is a hallmark of malignant tumors such as gliomas [6]. Immune cells, particularly microglial cells and astrocytes, play critical roles in tumor initiation and progression. Microglia, as the innate immune cells of the CNS, can influence tumor immune responses by secreting cytokines and chemokines, while glioma cells modulate immune cell functions or suppress immune responses to evade host immune surveillance. Furthermore, the immune microenvironment in gliomas is highly complex, consisting of various immune cell types, cytokines, and metabolic products, all of which together affect the tumor’s immune evasion mechanisms and disease progression [7–9].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has provided a revolutionary tool for studying cellular heterogeneity and the tumor immune microenvironment [10]. Through single-cell analysis, researchers can explore the gene expression profiles of different cell types within tumors and elucidate the roles of various cell populations in tumor initiation and progression. This approach offers unprecedented opportunities to uncover immune evasion mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment and identify potential therapeutic targets. In glioma research, single-cell analysis has not only helped identify different types of tumor and immune cells but also revealed the complex interactions between tumor and immune cells, providing new perspectives for precision therapy [11, 12]. For instance, Xiao et al. applied single‑cell transcriptomics to human glioblastomas and revealed that distinct molecular subtypes harbor unique immune microenvironments, with variations in T cell, macrophage, and dendritic cell infiltration patterns across subtypes [13].

Bioinformatics methods, particularly the integration and analysis of gene expression data, can reveal key genes, molecular pathways, and potential therapeutic targets associated with diseases. Through functional enrichment analysis using databases like Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), the biological functions of these key genes and the signaling pathways they are involved in can be further elucidated, thereby identifying potential pathogenic mechanisms in gliomas. Additionally, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis can uncover the relationships between different genes, helping researchers understand the molecular structure and functional networks of tumors [14, 15].

To further explore the therapeutic possibilities for gliomas, this study also combines drug target prediction and molecular docking techniques to screen compounds with potential therapeutic effects. Quercetin, a natural polyphenolic compound, has been shown to exhibit anti-tumor, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, and its potential applications in various cancers have garnered increasing attention. Through molecular docking analysis, we found that quercetin has a strong binding affinity with key glioma targets (such as DLGAP5, TOP2A, CHEK1, and MKI67), suggesting that quercetin may exert anti-glioma biological effects by modulating these targets.

This study aims to comprehensively reveal the cellular heterogeneity and immune microenvironment features of gliomas through the integration of scRNA-seq and bioinformatics analyses. By performing systematic gene functional enrichment analysis, constructing PPI networks, and predicting drug targets, we identified multiple potential pathogenic targets in gliomas and explored the molecular mechanisms of quercetin. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis and new research directions for the future precision treatment of gliomas, particularly for the development of targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Furthermore, the discoveries offer valuable insights for optimizing early diagnosis and therapeutic strategies for gliomas.

Microarray data source and differential analysis using Limma

The dataset used in this study was obtained from the GEO database, specifically from the GSE50161 collection. This dataset was selected based on the following criteria: (1) inclusion of both glioma and normal brain tissue samples to facilitate differential expression analysis; (2) gene expression profiling performed using the Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array (GPL570), a widely adopted and reliable platform; and (3) relatively large sample size among publicly available datasets, comprising 34 glioma and 13 normal brain samples [16]. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the data, preprocessing steps were carried out using Sangerbox 3.0, which included background correction, data normalization, and removal of batch effects. Additionally, a Log2 transformation was applied to the dataset to standardize the expression values. To identify genes with significant expression differences between glioma samples and normal controls, we performed differential expression analysis using the limma package in R [17]. Initially, we applied the lmFit function to model the gene expression data, followed by the eBayes function to calculate adjusted p-values and log-fold changes for each gene.We considered genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 (using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate) and a |log2FoldChange| >1 as differentially expressed. To visualize the results, we generated volcano plots and heatmaps to display the significant DEGs.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To explore gene relationships, we employed Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [18]. Initially, we calculated the median absolute deviation (MAD) for each gene and excluded the bottom 50% of genes with the lowest MAD values. We then filtered the DEG expression matrix using the samplegenes function, removing genes and samples that did not meet the criteria, thereby constructing a scale-free co-expression network. Using the co-expression similarities, we determined the optimal “soft” threshold power (β) to calculate the adjacency matrix, which was then converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to assess gene connectivity. Next, we applied hierarchical clustering and the dynamic tree cut method to identify gene modules, grouping genes with similar expression patterns into distinct clusters. We calculated the module eigengenes to determine the cut-off point for the module tree and combined related modules for further analysis. We constructed a signed weighted co-expression network using the WGCNA package. The signed network preserves the sign of the correlation between gene expression profiles, thereby emphasizing positively correlated gene modules and minimizing noise from inverse relationships. This approach is particularly suited to identifying co-regulated genes with potential biological significance in glioma. Finally, the VennDiagram package was used to identify the overlap of core module genes, DEGs, and data from GeneCards.

Acquisition of glioma-associated gene data

We retrieved the glioma-related gene dataset from the GeneCards database (https://www.lifemapsc.com/gcsuite/gc_trial/), a comprehensive resource that compiles gene data from various sources, including gene function, chromosomal positions, expression data, and disease correlations [19]. By searching for the term “glioma,” we compiled a list of genes associated with this disease and cross-referenced them with our identified DEGs to pinpoint genes that show significant associations with glioma.

Analysis of protein–protein interaction networks

To investigate the relationships between genes, we utilized the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to build a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network [20, 21]. Initially, we submitted the DEGs and glioma-related genes identified through WGCNA and GeneCards into the STRING platform. Genes that showed a combined interaction score greater than 0.4, based on experimental data, were considered to have significant interactions. The resulting PPI network was then visualized using Cytoscape software [22].These key interacting genes may play essential roles in glioma progression.

Enrichment analysis of gene functions

To understand the biological roles of the identified genes, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The GO analysis covered three categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) [23]. KEGG pathway analysis was employed to identify key pathways involved in glioma [24]. The R package clusterProfiler was used for the enrichment analysis, and terms or pathways with adjusted p-values (FDR < 0.05) were considered significant. The results of the enrichment analyses were visualized through bar plots to highlight the involvement of DEGs in critical biological processes and signaling pathways.

Immune cell infiltration analysis in disease

To investigate the immune landscape of glioma and elucidate how immune cell populations may correlate with identified hub genes, we conducted immune infiltration analysis using bulk RNA-seq data.The immune microenvironment consists of a variety of components, including immune and inflammatory cells, fibroblasts, cytokines, and chemokines. Understanding immune cell infiltration is essential for predicting disease outcomes and evaluating patient responses to therapies. In this study, we utilized the CIBERSORT method to deconvolve gene expression data and assess the immune cell composition. Using RNA sequencing data from the GSE50161 dataset, immune cell infiltration was quantified by applying the CIBERSORT algorithm (version 0.1.0) to estimate the proportions of different immune cell types present in the samples [25, 26]. Prior to CIBERSORT deconvolution, the gene expression matrix underwent background correction, quantile normalization, and log2 transformation using the Sangerbox 3.0 platform to ensure compatibility with the LM22 signature matrix. Although tumor purity information was unavailable for direct adjustment, comparative analysis between glioma and normal groups was used to mitigate the impact of tumor heterogeneity on infiltration estimation.

Single-cell analysis based on scRNA-seq

Preprocessing of single-cell data

The single-cell dataset GSE276177 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). After importing the data in standard format, mitochondrial content and rRNA levels were assessed using the PercentageFeatureSet function in the Seurat package in R. We further analyzed the correlation between mitochondrial content, UMI counts (nCount), and the number of detected genes (nFeature). Following the quality control steps, cells that contained excessive noise and debris were excluded. The filtering criteria were set as follows: UMI count between 300 and 30,000, and mitochondrial content below 10%. After filtering, UMAP plots were used to visualize the distribution of cells. If cells appeared to cluster non-randomly within the samples, batch effects were addressed by applying the Harmony method, which effectively removed any batch-related variations until no such effects were detected in the data.

Cell subpopulation clustering and annotation

Cells of different types typically exhibit distinct transcriptional profiles, allowing us to group similar cells and identify subpopulations with shared characteristics. For dimensionality reduction, we selected 20,838 highly variable genes and performed principal component analysis (PCA). Clustering was then performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions in the Seurat package (resolution = 0.1), and visualized using the t-SNE algorithm.

To annotate the resulting clusters, we used the FindAllMarkers function to identify the top five highly expressed and differentially expressed genes for each cluster, based on bimodal statistical testing. Marker genes were defined as those with high expression within the cluster and low expression in other clusters. These candidate markers were then cross-referenced with established cell-type marker databases such as CellMarker and PanglaoDB, as well as relevant published literature, to ensure biologically accurate classification of subpopulations. The final annotations included canonical cell types such as oligodendrocytes (OLIG1, OLIG2), astrocytes (GFAP, AQP4), microglial cells (P2RY12, CX3CR1), T cells (CD3D, CD3G), and endothelial cells (PECAM1, VWF).

Importantly, one cluster was annotated as “proliferating cancer cells” based on its distinct expression of cell cycle–related genes including MKI67, TOP2A, DLGAP5, and CHEK1, which are widely recognized as markers of active proliferation in malignant glioma cells. This designation reflects the proliferative phenotype of tumor cells, rather than a distinct lineage, and is consistent with nomenclature used in other glioma single-cell transcriptomic studies.

Cytotrace-based cell trajectory analysis by cell type

To explore the developmental paths of various cell types, we conducted a cell trajectory analysis utilizing the CytoTRACE algorithm. This method enables us to assess the differentiation potential and developmental trajectory of each cell type within our dataset. Initially, after clustering the cells into distinct subpopulations, we applied CytoTRACE to estimate the differentiation potential for each cell. The algorithm generates a differentiation score for each cell, where higher scores correspond to more differentiated states. We visualized the cell trajectories through UMAP plots, positioning the cells along their predicted developmental paths to observe transitions between different cellular states. This analysis provides insights into the dynamic processes of cell populations and their potential progression through various stages of development or disease.

Abundance proportions of cell subpopulations

To assess the relative abundance of different cell subpopulations within our samples, we analyzed the annotated single-cell RNA sequencing data. Initially, we used the Idents function from the Seurat package to assign each cell to a specific subpopulation, ensuring that every cell was accurately categorized. Then, we determined the proportion of each cell subpopulation by calculating its abundance relative to the total number of cells in the sample. This was done by counting the cells in each subpopulation using the table function and dividing by the total cell count, resulting in the abundance ratio for each subpopulation. To visualize these proportions more clearly, we generated bar plots using the ggplot2 package. This approach allowed us to effectively compare the distribution and relative abundance of various cell subpopulations in the samples, providing valuable insights for further biological interpretation and disease mechanism studies.

Expression of specific genes across cell populations

To investigate the expression patterns of specific genes across various cell subpopulations, we conducted a detailed analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing data. Initially, we selected the genes of interest from the processed dataset. We then employed Seurat’s FeaturePlot and VlnPlot functions to visualize the expression levels of these genes in different cell clusters. The FeaturePlot function was used to display the spatial distribution of gene expression on UMAP or t-SNE dimensionality reduction plots, showing how gene expression varies across cell groups. This visualization allows for a clear comparison of gene expression across distinct cell populations, providing insights into their functional roles and potential involvement in biological processes.

Drug prediction

In this study, we utilized bioinformatics approaches to predict potential therapeutic drugs targeting 20 key genes associated with glioma. Initially, we explored existing drugs by querying public databases such as DrugBank, PubChem, and the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD), focusing on those linked to glioma. Through this screening process, we identified quercetin as a promising candidate. To further evaluate its therapeutic potential, we performed molecular docking simulations to assess the binding affinity of quercetin with the target genes. This approach enabled us to pinpoint a drug with significant binding capacity to the selected genes, offering a basis for further experimental validation.

Screening of small molecule agents and molecular Docking analysis

In this study, we utilized the Connectivity Map (CMap, https://clue.io/) database to investigate the relationships between gene expression profiles, small molecules, and diseases, aiding in the identification of potential drugs. For molecular docking, the three-dimensional structure of the target molecule was retrieved in mol2 format from PubChem. The ligand was then prepared using AutodockTools 1.5.6 by adding hydrogen atoms, assigning charges, defining rotatable bonds, and saving the structure in pdbqt format. The target protein structure was sourced from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org/) and processed by calculating Gasteiger charges, adding nonpolar hydrogens, and saving it as a pdbqt file to be used as the receptor. For docking simulations with AutoDock Vina, we set parameters such as exhaustiveness to 15, adjusted the box size and coordinates, while leaving other settings at default. We conducted semiflexible docking using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 and selected the conformation with the highest binding affinity as the final result [27].

CCK8 assay

Well-grown U87MG cells were collected and seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 10³ to 4 × 10³ cells/mL. To prevent medium evaporation near the edges, 200 µL of PBS was added to the peripheral wells. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing QE, with three replicate wells per group. After 24 h, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated using the formula:

Western blot experiment

Protein extraction was performed from three experimental groups: the Control group (HEB cells), the Model group (U87MG cells), and the QU group (U87MG cells treated with 100 µM quercetin for 24 h). Cells at 80–90% confluence were trypsinized, washed with PBS, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, repeated twice. The resulting cell pellets were resuspended in protein lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors and then sonicated on ice. After centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and quantified using the BCA protein assay. Protein samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer, vortexed, and denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. SDS-PAGE gels were prepared and poured into gel plates. After polymerization of the separating gel and overlaying with ethanol, the stacking gel was added and a comb inserted. Electrophoresis was carried out at 80 V for 30 min in the stacking gel and continued at 120 V until the bands reached the bottom of the separating gel. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes at 250 mA. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1500 dilution). After washing, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution) for 1–2 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL and ECL + 1:1) detection system and imaged using a chemiluminescent imaging system.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 software was used for data analysis, and the results were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation.” For comparisons between two groups, a t-test was used, while for comparisons between multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was performed. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Using the Limma method for differential analysis, we identified 394 genes with significant expression differences. Among which 104 genes were significantly upregulated and 290 genes were downregulated. To visualize these results more effectively, we generated volcano plots and heatmaps. Supplementary Table S1 provides detailed statistical metrics for each DEG, including gene symbol, log₂ fold change, p-value, and adjusted p-value, to ensure reproducibility and facilitate further research. These findings establish a solid basis for further investigation and analysis in our study (Fig. 1).

In conducting the weighted gene co-expression network analysis, we determined the optimal parameters by selecting a β-value of 8, which resulted in a scale-free R² value of 0.87, based on the metrics of scale-independence and average connectivity (Fig. 2A, B). The gene clustering dendrograms for both normal and glioma samples were visualized in Fig. 2C, from which we identified 13 distinct co-expression modules, each represented by a different color (Fig. 2D).

To identify the module most strongly correlated with glioma, we examined the relationship between each gene co-expression module and glioma (Fig. 2E). The blue module demonstrated the highest correlation with glioma (correlation coefficient = 0.83, p < 0.01), and thus, this module, which contained 798 key genes, was selected for further investigation (Supplementary Table S2).

This finding confirms that the blue module plays a significant role in glioma pathology.

Screening of glioma hub genes

To identify glioma hub genes, we first combined the key genes from the WGCNA modules, the DEGs, and data from the GeneCards disease database (Supplementary Table S3). This resulted in the identification of 37 common genes, as shown in Fig. 3A. These genes were then used to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, which was visualized using the STRING (v11.5) databaseand Cytoscape software. The interaction map, displayed in Fig. 3B, highlights the relationships among these 37 genes, which were subsequently recognized as important glioma-related genes. Next, we utilized CytoHubba to assess the centrality and topological significance of the nodes in the network. The centrality measures helped to highlight the most influential genes, with shading indicating their importance within the PPI network (Fig. 3C). From this analysis, we identified the top 20 key genes, and ultimately, the top 4 hub genes. The expression levels of these four genes across different groups are shown in Fig. 3D.

Analysis of immune infiltration results

We conducted immune infiltration analysis of the glioma hub genes using the CIBERSORT algorithm. The correlation between various immune cell types is presented in Fig. 4B, while the immune infiltration scores are visualized in a stacked bar plot (Fig. 4A). The analysis revealed that key immune cells associated with the glioma hub genes include macrophages, B cells, T follicular helper cells (Tfh), Th2 cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. The differences in immune cell infiltration across different subgroups are further illustrated in Fig. 4C. Additionally, a landscape plot was generated to compare the distribution of immune cells between the glioma and normal groups. The results indicated significant differences in the trends of immune cell distribution, with CD8^+ naïve T cells, Tr1 cells, Th1 cells, induced regulatory T cells (iTreg), and central memory T cells showing distinct patterns between the two groups. These findings suggest that these immune cells are significantly involved in glioma development and contribute to the progression of the disease (Fig. 4D, Supplement Table S4).

Enrichment analysis

We conducted functional enrichment analysis of the 20 key genes to explore their biological functions and involvement in glioma. The Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that these genes were associated with 296 GO terms (Supplement Table S5), spanning 182 biological processes (BP), 63 cellular components (CC), and 51 molecular functions (MF). Among the biological processes, the top 10 enriched terms included regulation of mitotic centrosome separation, regulation of p53-mediated signaling, chromosome segregation, microtubule-based movement, positive regulation of mitotic metaphase/anaphase transition, G2/M transition in the mitotic cell cycle, cell division, and mitotic spindle organization. In terms of molecular functions, key terms included kinetochore binding, histone kinase activity, protein binding, and protein kinase activity (Fig. 5A, B).

Analysis of cell subpopulation abundance proportions

Figure 7C presents the abundance proportions of different cell subpopulations in the control and glioma groups. Notably, in the glioma group, there was a significant increase in the proportions of T cells, proliferating cancer cells, and inhibitory neurons, while the proportions of oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) were notably reduced. These alterations suggest a substantial remodeling of the cellular composition during glioma progression. The increased presence of T cells and proliferating cancer cells could be linked to immune responses and tumor growth, while the decreased proportions of oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and TAM may indicate alterations in the supportive and regulatory roles of these cells within the glioma microenvironment. These findings provide a deeper understanding of the cellular dynamics and immune landscape associated with glioma and suggest potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

Expression of specific genes in cell subpopulations

Figure 7D illustrates the expression patterns of specific genes across different cell subpopulations. Our analysis revealed that CHEK1, DLGAP5, MKI67, and TOP2A were predominantly expressed in proliferating cancer cells. This observation is highly relevant to glioma pathogenesis, as these genes are closely associated with cell cycle regulation, cell proliferation, and genomic stability. CHEK1 (Checkpoint kinase 1) plays a crucial role in the DNA damage response and checkpoint regulation during the cell cycle. Its elevated expression in proliferating cancer cells suggests an adaptive mechanism employed by these cells to survive under the increased DNA damage typically present in tumors. By maintaining cell cycle progression despite genetic instability, CHEK1 contributes to glioma cell proliferation and resistance to therapies that induce DNA damage [28]. DLGAP5 (Discs large-associated protein 5) is involved in regulating the mitotic spindle, which is essential for proper chromosome segregation during cell division. The high expression of DLGAP5 in proliferating cancer cells reflects its role in promoting mitotic stability, enabling rapid tumor growth and facilitating glioma cell proliferation [29]. MKI67 (Marker of proliferation Ki-67) is a well-known cell proliferation marker. Its abundant expression in proliferating cancer cells highlights the high mitotic activity within glioma tumors. As MKI67 is absent during quiescence and G0 phase, its upregulation signifies active cell division and can be used as a marker to assess the proliferative potential of glioma cells [30]. TOP2A (Topoisomerase II alpha) is an enzyme critical for DNA replication, transcription, and chromosome segregation. Elevated levels of TOP2A in proliferating cancer cells suggest an increase in DNA replication and the unwinding of supercoiled DNA, which is necessary to accommodate the rapid cell division seen in gliomas [31].

The strong expression of these genes in proliferating cancer cells underscores the high proliferative capacity of glioma cells. It also indicates that these cells rely on mechanisms that enhance DNA repair, mitotic progression, and genomic stability, all of which contribute to the aggressive growth and therapeutic resistance of gliomas. These genes, particularly in combination, represent potential therapeutic targets, as their inhibition could hinder cell cycle progression, induce DNA damage, and reduce glioma cell proliferation. Additionally, targeting the specific pathways regulated by these genes could enhance the effectiveness of existing treatments, such as chemotherapy or targeted therapy, in glioma patients.

Results of molecular docking

In this study, molecular docking was used to predict the potential of quercetin (QU) as a drug. First, four key target genes—CHEK1, DLGAP5, MKI67, and TOP2A—were identified through bioinformatics screening. Subsequently, molecular docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina software to assess the binding ability of QU with these target proteins. Figure 8A shows the chemical structure of quercetin. Based on the screening results, molecular docking was conducted to evaluate the interactions between quercetin and the four hub genes. Binding energy values lower than 0 kJ/mol generally suggest the possibility of spontaneous binding between the small molecule ligand and the protein receptor, with values less than − 5.0 kJ/mol indicating a stronger binding affinity. The docking simulations revealed favorable results, with all four interactions showing binding energies below − 5 kJ/mol, indicating strong binding affinities. The docking conformations were visualized using PyMOL software, as shown in Fig. 8B and E, further supporting the potential of quercetin as an inhibitor of these target genes.

To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying quercetin’s anti-glioma effects, we examined the expression levels of four key target proteins—CHEK1, DLGAP5, MKI67, and TOP2A—by Western blot analysis across three experimental groups: the control group (HEB cells), the model group (U87MG cells), and the QU group (U87MG cells treated with 100 µM quercetin). Discussion

Glioma is the most common primary brain tumor in adults, particularly prevalent in middle-aged and elderly populations [32]. According to data from the World Health Organization (WHO), the annual incidence of glioma is approximately 3.2 to 5.0 cases per 100,000 people, with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) being the most common type, accounting for about 50% of all glioma cases [33]. Despite significant advances in surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for glioma remains poor, especially for GBM, where the median survival is typically less than two years [34]. This gap is mainly due to the tumor’s high heterogeneity, immune evasion during treatment, and its resistance to traditional therapies. Therefore, further exploration of the molecular mechanisms underlying glioma, particularly the roles of key genes and the immune microenvironment, is crucial for identifying new therapeutic targets and developing more effective treatment strategies [35].

This study used multidimensional data analysis to explore the molecular mechanisms of glioma and identified DEGs and hub genes closely related to glioma progression using various bioinformatics tools. Through differential analysis, we identified 394 genes with significant expression differences between glioma and normal samples. In the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we identified the blue module, which was strongly associated with glioma, containing 798 key genes. The high correlation of these genes with glioma suggests their important role in the pathological process of the disease. Further analysis revealed that these genes are primarily involved in key biological processes such as cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, and immune response, providing potential candidate genes for subsequent therapeutic target screening. In the search for glioma-related hub genes, we combined data from the WGCNA modules, DEGs, and the GeneCards database, successfully identifying CHEK1, DLGAP5, MKI67, and TOP2A as key genes in glioma. By constructing a PPI network, we further validated the critical role of these four genes in glioma and used CytoHubba analysis to assess their centrality within the network, confirming them as core genes of glioma. In immune infiltration analysis, we employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to evaluate the relationship between glioma and immune cells. Although the CIBERSORT-based immune infiltration analysis provided valuable insights into the immune landscape of glioma, we acknowledge that these findings were derived from computational estimation and have not yet been validated in an independent cohort or through experimental methods such as flow cytometry. Future work involving patient tissue samples and immunophenotyping by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry is planned to empirically validate the immune cell distributions identified in this study. The analysis revealed that macrophages, B cells, Tfh cells, Th2 cells, and NK cells are closely associated with glioma. The distribution patterns of immune cells showed significant differences between groups, especially the immune responses of CD8^+ naïve T cells, Tr1 cells, Th1 cells, iTreg, and central memory T cells, which play an important role in glioma progression. Functional enrichment analysis of the 20 key genes revealed that they are mainly involved in the cell cycle, DNA repair, and immune response. GO analysis indicated that the identified hub genes are mainly involved in biological processes such as mitosis, the p53 signaling pathway, and microtubule-based movement. KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed their enrichment in the p53 signaling pathway and revealed significant involvement in critical regulatory sub-pathways within the cell cycle. The p53 signaling pathway, identified as one of the top enriched KEGG pathways, plays a central role in glioma pathogenesis. TP53 mutations are present in ~ 30–40% of GBM cases and contribute to loss of DNA damage response, evasion of apoptosis, and uncontrolled proliferation. Several of our hub genes, such as CHEK1 and TOP2A, are directly involved in the p53-mediated checkpoint response. CHEK1 phosphorylates and stabilizes p53 in response to DNA replication stress, while TOP2A is essential for chromosomal segregation and is regulated by p53 under genotoxic stress. The aberrant overexpression of these genes in glioma may reflect a dysfunctional p53 axis, enabling tumor cells to bypass cell cycle arrest and accumulate mutations. Notably, beyond the general term “cell cycle,” these genes were specifically enriched in pathways such as the G2/M phase transition, regulation of mitotic metaphase/anaphase transition, and chromosome segregation. These processes are essential for accurate cell division, and their disruption is closely linked to uncontrolled proliferation and genomic instability in glioma. Additionally, the enrichment in the p53 signaling pathway underscores the involvement of these genes in DNA damage response and cell cycle checkpoint control—tumor-suppressive mechanisms that are often impaired in glioma. Together, these findings suggest that the hub genes may contribute to glioma pathogenesis by facilitating aberrant cell cycle progression and weakening p53-mediated tumor suppression.

Molecular docking results further supported the feasibility of CHEK1, DLGAP5, MKI67, and TOP2A as potential therapeutic targets. In this study, quercetin (Qu) was selected for molecular docking analysis based on its well-documented anticancer properties and previous evidence of relevance to glioma. The results demonstrated that quercetin exhibited strong binding affinities to all four hub proteins, with binding energies consistently below − 5.0 kJ/mol, suggesting stable and favorable interactions. However, we acknowledge that the current analysis did not include docking with non-target or structurally unrelated proteins as negative controls, which limits the ability to fully assess the specificity of quercetin’s interactions. This limitation will be addressed in future studies by incorporating appropriate negative controls to more rigorously validate target selectivity. Despite this, our findings offer preliminary theoretical evidence supporting quercetin as a promising targeted therapeutic agent for glioma, meriting further experimental exploration.

Although genes such as CHEK1, MKI67, DLGAP5, and TOP2A have previously been associated with glioma-related processes, our study provides new insights by integrating multiple layers of analysis to reevaluate their roles in a broader glioma context. These genes were not only found to be upregulated in glioma tissues but were also identified as hub genes through WGCNA and PPI network analyses, underscoring their central regulatory positions within glioma-specific co-expression modules. Furthermore, through single-cell analysis and immune deconvolution, we explored their cell-type-specific expression patterns and potential involvement in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Importantly, our molecular docking and expression validation experiments suggest that these genes may serve as candidate targets for quercetin, offering a novel therapeutic connection. This systems-level integration of transcriptomics, network biology, and drug prediction provides a more comprehensive understanding of glioma pathogenesis and lays a foundation for future translational applications.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study lacks in vivo experimental validation, which limits the direct applicability of the therapeutic predictions to clinical settings. Second, although quercetin showed favorable binding affinities and anti-proliferative effects in vitro, its potential off-target interactions and relatively low bioavailability remain unresolved issues. Third, the bioinformatics-based screening and pathway enrichment analyses are exploratory in nature and rely heavily on existing databases and assumptions, which may introduce analytical biases. These limitations warrant further experimental and clinical investigations to consolidate the therapeutic implications proposed.

To strengthen the translational relevance of our findings, we further explored the clinical implications of the identified hub genes (CHEK1, MKI67, DLGAP5, and TOP2A) and their interaction with quercetin in the context of current therapeutic challenges in glioma. Notably, glioblastoma patients often exhibit or develop resistance to temozolomide (TMZ), the first-line chemotherapeutic agent, which is largely attributed to enhanced DNA damage repair mechanisms, deregulated cell cycle progression, and increased genomic instability.

Several of the hub genes identified in our study are directly implicated in these resistance mechanisms: CHEK1 promotes G2/M checkpoint control and DNA repair, and its overactivation enables glioma cells to evade TMZ-induced cytotoxicity. MKI67 and DLGAP5 are proliferation markers that drive mitotic activity and have been linked to aggressive tumor behavior and chemoresistance. TOP2A, a key enzyme in DNA replication and chromatin remodeling, is frequently overexpressed in gliomas and associated with poor prognosis and resistance to alkylating agents. Inhibition of CHEK1 or TOP2A has been shown to sensitize glioma cells to TMZ by impairing checkpoint integrity and promoting apoptosis.

Importantly, these genes also hold promise as minimally invasive biomarkers: their transcripts or protein products have been detected in extracellular vesicles (EVs) or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), supporting their potential use in liquid biopsy for glioma diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment response monitoring.

Our experimental results demonstrated that quercetin downregulates the expression of these hub genes in glioma cells, providing a mechanistic rationale for its anti-proliferative effect. Previous studies have also suggested that quercetin enhances tumor cell sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents. Thus, quercetin may serve as a chemosensitizer in combination with TMZ, or as a standalone therapeutic option, particularly in TMZ-resistant gliomas.

While our in vitro experiments demonstrated that quercetin effectively suppressed glioma cell viability and downregulated key oncogenic proteins, we acknowledge the current lack of in vivo validation, which limits the assessment of therapeutic efficacy under physiological conditions. Therefore, further studies using glioma-bearing animal models are warranted to evaluate quercetin’s pharmacodynamics, safety, and anti-tumor activity in vivo.Additionally, quercetin’s clinical application is hindered by its poor water solubility, low oral bioavailability, and limited permeability across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). These pharmacokinetic limitations significantly compromise its therapeutic potential for brain tumors.To address these challenges, several advanced drug delivery strategies have been proposed. Among them, nanoparticle-based delivery systems—including quercetin-loaded liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and brain-targeted micelles—have shown promise in enhancing drug solubility, stability, and BBB penetration. Alternatively, chemical modification of quercetin to generate more bioavailable derivatives may offer another feasible approach. Recent advances have highlighted the role of polyphenolic compounds, such as quercetin, in regulating glioma pathogenesis at both transcriptomic and epigenetic levels. A recent study by Rezaie et al. (2024, PMID: 38639278IF: 3.3 Q3 B4) demonstrated that flavonoids can modulate the expression of oncogenic and tumor-suppressive microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby influencing downstream targets such as CHEK1 and TOP2A, and reshaping the tumor microenvironment. These miRNA-mediated mechanisms add another layer of regulatory control that supports the anti-proliferative and immunomodulatory potential of quercetin. Collectively, this evidence strengthens the therapeutic rationale for using flavonoid-based strategies in glioma therapy [].

In future work, we aim to investigate quercetin-loaded nanocarriers in orthotopic glioma models to validate both its anti-tumor efficacy and delivery efficiency, thereby bridging the gap between bench and bedside applications.

Conclusions

In this study, we utilized comprehensive bioinformatics analysis and single-cell data to identify key genes associated with glioma and explore its immune microenvironment characteristics. We identified 394 differentially expressed genes and used WGCNA to select the blue module closely related to glioma. Further analysis revealed the crucial roles of these genes in glioma progression, and four core genes were identified through PPI network analysis. Immune infiltration analysis showed significant changes in the distribution of immune cells such as macrophages, B cells, Tfh cells, Th2 cells, and NK cells, suggesting their key role in the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, single-cell trajectory analysis revealed the differentiation dynamics of different cell subpopulations, with particular emphasis on the significant role of oligodendrocytes in glioma. Molecular docking simulations demonstrated that quercetin has strong binding affinity with multiple key genes, suggesting its potential as a candidate drug for glioma treatment. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of glioma and offer a theoretical basis for future targeted therapy strategies.
