Continuous glucose monitoring-derived glycemic variability and low skeletal muscle mass in middle-aged and older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus: an analysis using AWGS 2025 criteria
Authors: Yanling Yang, Hongrong Deng, Beisi Lin, Yintong Huang, Jinhua Yan, Xubin Yang, Cailian Cheng, Wen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of glycemic variability (GV) on muscle mass in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) remains unclear under the updated Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2025 criteria. We investigated the association between continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)-derived GV and low skeletal muscle mass (LSMM).MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 614 hospitalized T2DM patients aged ≥ 50 years. GV metrics (SD, MODD, LAGE, and CV) were derived from 7-day CGM data. LSMM was identified using bioelectrical impedance analysis based on age-specific AWGS 2025 thresholds. Multivariate logistic regression, restricted cubic splines (RCS), subgroup, and mediation analyses were performed.ResultsPatients with LSMM exhibited significantly higher GV. In fully adjusted models, higher SD (OR = 1.36), MODD (OR = 1.38), and LAGE (OR = 1.08) were independently associated with increased LSMM risk (all p < 0.05). RCS analysis revealed a linear dose-response relationship between GV and LSMM. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent associations across BMI, eGFR, sex, age and HbA1c categories (all p for interaction > 0.05). Mediation analysis indicated that insulin sensitivity-related indices (HOMA2-IR) partially mediated the GV-LSMM relationship.ConclusionsElevated GV is independently associated with LSMM in middle-aged and older adults with T2DM, showing a linear dose-response pattern consistent across diverse clinical profiles. Strategies targeting glucose fluctuations may be crucial for preserving muscle mass in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02112-4.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is characterized by chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, contributing significantly to the global burden of microvascular and macrovascular complications [1]. Beyond these classical outcomes, T2DM is increasingly recognized as a key driver of skeletal muscle impairment [2, 3]. Since skeletal muscle accounts for the majority of insulin-mediated glucose disposal, loss of muscle mass and function can worsen metabolic control. Conversely, diabetes-related metabolic disturbances accelerate muscle loss, creating a bidirectional cycle involving sarcopenia-related phenotypes [4].

Glycemic variability (GV), which refers to the fluctuations in blood glucose levels over time, has emerged as an independent risk factor for the development of diabetic complications [5]. Although hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) remains the gold standard for assessing long-term glycemic control [6], it is unable to adequately capture acute glucose excursions [7]. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) offers a comprehensive assessment of both intra-day and inter-day GV [8], providing key metrics including the glucose coefficient of variation (CV), the mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE), the largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE), standard deviation (SD) reflecting overall dispersion, and the mean of daily differences (MODD) capturing inter-day variations [9]. Elevated GV is linked to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [10], all of which contribute to the acceleration of muscle protein degradation and the impairment of muscle regeneration [11].

Despite growing interest in the interplay between metabolic dysregulation and sarcopenia, the association between GV and low skeletal muscle mass (LSMM) in patients with T2DM has been insufficiently studied. Previous studies have primarily focused on hyperglycemia, HbA1c, or insulin resistance as key contributors to muscle loss, with insufficient consideration of short-term dynamic glucose fluctuations [12, 13]. In addition, the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2025 consensus update introduced age-specific cutoffs for low muscle mass [14], which may improve identification of muscle loss beginning in midlife; however, evidence linking CGM-derived GV to LSMM defined by these updated criteria in T2DM remains limited.

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association between CGM-derived GV metrics and LSMM, assessed by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), in patients with T2DM. We adopted the diagnostic thresholds from the AWGS 2025 consensus update, incorporating age-specific cutoffs to identify muscle loss in both middle-aged and older adults. We hypothesized that higher GV is independently associated with LSMM after adjusting for confounders and explored whether insulin sensitivity-related indices mediate this relationship.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 614 patients with T2DM were enrolled from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between October 2023 and June 2024 (Fig. S1). The primary indication for hospital admission for all participants was poor glycemic control requiring intensive insulin pump therapy and glucose monitoring. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosed with T2DM according to the 1999 World Health Organization criteria; (2) Aged ≥ 50 years (aligning with the AWGS 2025 consensus which emphasizes early detection of muscle loss starting from middle age); (3) Antihyperglycemic treatment was stable for at least 3 months prior to admission; (4) received both CGM for at least 8 consecutive days via the CGM system (Medtronic Guardian 4 Sensor, Medtronic MiniMed, Northridge, CA, USA) and BIA device (InBody 570, InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) during hospitalization; (5) obtained at least 7 days of valid analytical CGM data (excluding the first 24 h). Those who recently had diabetic ketoacidosis or hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state events, severe cardiovascular diseases, pregnancy, malignancy, severe chronic kidney dysfunction, or anemia were excluded. All patients were informed that their medical records from their hospital stay could be used for scientific research, and they provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (II2024-241-01).

Glucose control

Upon admission to the hospital, all patients started insulin pump therapy (MiniMed Paradigm 722, Medtronic, Northridge, CA, USA) with Humalog rapid-acting insulin (insulin lispro; Eli Lilly, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The initial insulin dose was 0.5 units per kilogram of body weight, with 40% for basal and 60% for bolus administration. Basal insulin rates were adjusted based on time intervals as follows: 22:00–03:00 (basal dose/24 × 0.8), 03:00–07:00 (basal dose/24 × 1.2), 07:00–22:00 (basal dose/24 × 1.0). Every 2 days, physicians adjusted basal rates (± 0.2 units/h) and bolus doses (± 2 units) based on capillary blood glucose measurements, aiming for fasting glucose levels < 7.0 mmol/L and postprandial averages < 10.0 mmol/L.

Throughout the hospitalization period, patients received standardized lifestyle education and diet. Nurses utilized a calibrated glucometer to monitor glucose seven times daily (pre-meal, 2-hour postprandial, bedtime).

Data collection

We collected data on pre-admission antidiabetic treatments, diabetes duration and complications from clinical records and patient interviews. Physical examinations included measurements of height, weight, and blood pressure. Blood samples were collected on the morning of Day 2 (the day following admission) following an overnight fast for the assessment of HbA1c levels, lipid profiles, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-creatinine ratio (UACR).

The CGM system was employed to assess GV. CGM sensors were applied upon admission. To ensure data accuracy, the first 24 h of recording were excluded to account for the sensor warm-up period. Metrics were calculated based on data from Day 2 to Day 8. Intraday GV metrics included CV, MAGE, LAGE, and SD [15]. CV was calculated by dividing the standard deviation of sensor glucose values by the mean glucose level [16]. MAGE represents the arithmetic mean of differences between consecutive peaks and valleys, taking into account deviations greater than one standard deviation of the mean sensor glucose [17]. LAGE is defined as maximum daily sensor glucose levels minus the minimum daily sensor glucose levels [18]. It can reflect the characteristics of within-day sensor glucose variations. Inter-day GV was evaluated using MODD, which was calculated as the average absolute difference between glucose levels measured simultaneously on two consecutive days [19]. Additional metrics included time in range (TIR; 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time above range (TAR; > 10.0 mmol/L), time below range (TBR; < 3.9 mmol/L), estimated hemoglobin A1c (eHbA1c), mean sensor glucose (Mean SG), minimum sensor glucose (Min SG), maximum sensor glucose (Max SG), J-index, low blood glucose index (LBGI) and high blood glucose index (HBGI) [20].

BIA was used to assess skeletal muscle mass [21, 22]. Participants were required to wear light indoor clothing, stand upright with arms extended, and legs slightly spread. Measurements were obtained after an overnight fast to minimize hydration fluctuations. The skeletal muscle index (SMI) provided by the InBody system was calculated as appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) divided by height squared (SMI = ASM/height², kg/m²) [23]. LSMM was defined according to the AWGS 2025 consensus using age-specific SMI cutoffs: for older adults (≥ 65 years), SMI < 7.0 kg/m² for men and < 5.7 kg/m² for women; for middle-aged adults (50–64 years), SMI < 7.6 kg/m² for men and < 5.7 kg/m² for women [14]. Insulin resistance was assessed using the following surrogates calculated based on fasting C-peptide with the HOMA2 calculator (https://www.dtu.ox.ac.uk/homacalculator/, version 2.2.3): homeostasis model assessment 2 of β-cell function (HOMA2-%B, a marker of insulin secretion), homeostasis model assessment 2 of insulin sensitivity (HOMA2-%S), and homeostasis model assessment 2 of insulin resistance (HOMA2-IR). Triglyceride glucose index (TyG), and triglyceride glucose combined with body mass index (TyG-BMI) were derived from previous studies: TyG = ln[fasting triglycerides (TG) (mg/dL)×fasting blood glucose (FBG) (mg/dL)/2] [24]; TyG-BMI = TyG×BMI [25].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as medians (25th-75th percentile) for continuous variables and as frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. The characteristics of the study cohort were compared according to LSMM categories. For continuous variables, statistical comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data or Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed via the chi-square test. Categorization of GV metrics was performed by dividing the values into tertiles (T1, T2, T3) based on the 33rd and 66th percentiles of the cohort distribution. This approach was adopted to evaluate potential non-linear associations and explore dose-response relationships between GV and LSMM. To assess the independent associations between GV (as both continuous and categorical variables) and LSMM, a binary logistic regression model was employed to derive unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) for the likelihood of LSMM, while adjusting for multiple confounding variables. Confounding factors included in the multivariate models were selected based on clinical relevance and significant differences (p < 0.05) observed in baseline characteristics. Consequently, HbA1c was excluded from the final adjustment models due to the lack of significant intergroup differences and to avoid potential multicollinearity with GV metrics. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots were utilized to investigate the dose-response relationship between GV and LSMM. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on predefined groups, including body mass index (BMI) (< 24 or ≥ 24 kg/m2), eGFR (≥ 90 or < 90 mL/min/1.73m2), sex, age (≥ 65 or < 65 years) and HbA1c (≥ 7 or < 7%). Mediation analysis was performed using the R package ‘mediation’ to explore the role of insulin sensitivity-related indices in the association between GV and LSMM. The bootstrap method was utilized to estimate robust standard errors for the mediated effects, thereby ensuring statistical reliability in quantifying indirect pathways. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (https://www.r-project.org), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of the 614 participants. Compared to the non-LSMM group (n = 440), the LSMM group (n = 174) had a higher proportion of males (70.1% vs. 50.5%) and significantly lower BMI (21.00 vs. 24.40 kg/m2) (both p < 0.001). Notably, patients with LSMM demonstrated higher glucose variability, with significantly higher SD (2.63 vs. 2.46 mmol/L), MODD (2.30 vs. 2.10 mmol/L), LAGE (13.99 vs. 13.10 mmol/L), and CV (30.93% vs. 29.12%) (all p < 0.05). Conversely, the LSMM group exhibited better insulin sensitivity, indicated by lower HOMA2-IR, TyG, and TyG-BMI indices. The prevalence of diabetic peripheral neuropathy was lower in the LSMM group (p = 0.011). Associations between other CGM-derived metrics and low skeletal muscle mass

To distinguish the impact of glycemic exposure from variability, we further examined a comprehensive panel of CGM metrics (Table S1), including TIR, TAR, TBR, eHbA1c, Mean SG, Min SG, Max SG, and composite indices such as J-index, LBGI and HBGI. Notably, metrics reflecting average glycemic exposure (Mean SG, eHbA1c) or time-in-ranges (TIR, TAR, TBR) failed to show significant associations with LSMM in multivariate adjusted models (all p > 0.05). Conversely, Max SG, which captures peak hyperglycemic excursions, remained independently associated with LSMM (adjusted OR = 1.07, p = 0.035).

Subgroup analyses

To verify the consistency of our findings, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by BMI, eGFR, sex, age, and HbA1c (Fig. 2). Crucially, no evidence of interaction was observed between these baseline characteristics and any of the GV indices (SD, MODD, LAGE, or CV) regarding the risk of LSMM (all p for interaction > 0.05). While nominal associations appeared numerically stronger in participants aged < 65 years or those with HbA1c ≥ 7%, the absence of statistical heterogeneity suggests that the detrimental impact of elevated glucose variability on skeletal muscle mass is consistent and generalized across varying clinical profiles, regardless of age, renal function, or glycemic control status.

Mediation analysis of insulin Sensitivity-related indices

To investigate potential pathophysiological pathways, mediation analyses were conducted to quantify the role of insulin sensitivity-related indices in the GV-LSMM relationship (Table S2; Fig. 3). After adjusting for covariates, HOMA2-IR emerged as a consistent and significant mediator for all four GV metrics (SD, MODD, LAGE, and CV). Specifically, the Average Causal Mediation Effects (ACME) were positive (Table S2), indicating that elevated GV contributes to LSMM risk partially through pathways involving insulin sensitivity-related indices (p < 0.05). In contrast, the lipid-related surrogate markers showed limited involvement: TyG-BMI mediated the association solely for CV (p < 0.05), while the TyG index did not exhibit significant mediation for any GV metric.

Discussion

Association between glycemic variability and low skeletal muscle mass

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between CGM-derived glycemic variability and LSMM using the updated AWGS 2025 criteria in middle-aged and older adults with T2DM. Our principal findings demonstrate that elevated glycemic variability, specifically SD, MODD, and LAGE, is independently associated with an increased risk of LSMM. Notably, this association follows a linear dose-response pattern without an evident saturation threshold. These results align with emerging evidence suggesting that glucose fluctuations act as a distinct physiological stressor contributing to muscle depletion, providing clinical value independent of mean glucose levels [12, 26, 27].

Distinct impact of acute fluctuations vs. average glycemia

Dynamic GV metrics emerged as significant predictors of LSMM in our study. This highlights that acute glucose excursions convey risk information that is not fully captured by integrated markers like HbA1c [28]. While HbA1c reflects average glycemia over the past 2–3 months, it masks the magnitude of acute glycemic excursions [29]. The deleterious impact of GV likely stems from oxidative stress [30]. As established by Monnier et al., oscillating glucose levels trigger a more potent activation of oxidative pathways than chronic sustained hyperglycemia [10]. These acute spikes lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [31]. Evidence suggests that such oxidative stress activates proteolytic pathways, such as the ubiquitin-proteasome system, and concurrently inhibits protein synthesis, ultimately accelerating muscle wasting [32].

The “Insulin resistance Paradox” and metabolic context

Notably, our data revealed a paradoxical profile in the LSMM group. Despite comparable diabetes duration yet worse muscle outcomes, these patients exhibited lower HOMA2-IR levels. This apparent metabolic improvement should be interpreted with caution [33]. The clinical profile, characterized by significantly lower fasting C-peptide and BMI, suggests a state of β-cell exhaustion and malnutrition rather than true insulin sensitivity. This observation aligns with findings from novel diabetes sub-classification studies, where reduced insulin secretion can mask insulin resistance estimates [34]. In this catabolic context, the loss of adipose tissue reduces systemic lipotoxicity, leading to a “pseudo-normalization” of insulin resistance markers. Thus, the lower HOMA2-IR may reflect absolute insulin deficiency and a shrinking metabolic sink [35]. Crucially, despite this apparent pseudo-normalization of basal IR levels due to low BMI in the LSMM group, our mediation analysis revealed a significant positive indirect effect (ACME). This indicates that the specific component of insulin sensitivity-related indices induced by glycemic variability continues to exert a deleterious effect on muscle mass. This finding supports the ‘anabolic resistance’ hypothesis, where GV-driven metabolic stress impairs muscle protein synthesis pathways, even in patients who appear clinically insulin-sensitive due to weight loss [32, 36]. However, as direct measurements of insulin sensitivity (e.g., hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps) were not performed [37], these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative. This underscores the complexity of interpreting conventional metabolic markers in advanced diabetes and highlights the critical value of GV assessment in unraveling underlying dysregulation.

Linear dose-response relationship and clinical implications

Our use of RCS analysis provided novel insights into the nature of this association. We observed a linear dose-response relationship between GV metrics and LSMM risk, suggesting the absence of a “safe threshold” below which fluctuations are harmless. Even incremental increases in glucose variability were associated with a corresponding rise in the odds of LSMM. This finding suggests that interventions aimed at minimizing glucose excursions, often described as “flattening the glucose curve” should be considered a fundamental aspect of diabetes management to prevent sarcopenia from an early stage, rather than being reserved only for patients with erratic control [8].

Relevance of the AWGS 2025 criteria

A key strength of the present study is the application of the updated AWGS 2025 criteria. Unlike the 2019 guidelines, the 2025 update introduces age-specific SMI thresholds and explicitly recognizes the physiological decline in muscle mass associated with aging [14]. By utilizing these more sensitive and stratified criteria, our study provides a more precise risk assessment. Our subgroup analyses further confirmed the robustness of our findings across diverse subgroups defined by BMI, eGFR, sex, age and HbA1c. Notably, the association between elevated GV and LSMM persisted consistently regardless of age, kidney function or glycemic control status, supporting the generalizability of GV as an independent risk factor.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and prospective cohort studies are needed to confirm the temporal relationship. Second, our study population consisted of hospitalized patients who generally have poorer glycemic control, which may introduce selection bias. Third, although BIA is a validated tool recommended by AWGS, it has the potential to underestimate muscle mass compared to dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [22]. Fourth, insulin resistance and β-cell function were evaluated using surrogate indices (HOMA2, TyG, TyG-BMI) rather than the gold-standard hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, which may limit the precision of metabolic assessments [37]. Finally, while we adjusted for key confounders, unmeasured factors such as detailed dietary protein intake [38] and physical activity logs were not available [39].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that elevated CGM-derived glycemic variability, specifically SD, MODD, and LAGE, is independently associated with increased odds of low skeletal muscle mass in middle-aged and older adults with T2DM, as defined by the AWGS 2025 criteria. Unlike traditional markers of average glycemic control such as HbA1c, dynamic glucose fluctuations exhibited a robust, linear dose-response relationship with muscle loss, suggesting that glycemic instability may exert a direct detrimental effect on muscle health. Furthermore, our findings indicate that this association is partially mediated by insulin sensitivity-related indices. Clinically, these results highlight the importance of looking beyond mean glucose levels and prioritizing the minimization of glucose excursions as a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent muscle wasting in the aging T2DM population.
