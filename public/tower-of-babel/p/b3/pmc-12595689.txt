Prevention of alcohol use and promotion of well-being among middle school students: a study protocol for a randomized controlled trial of ADAPT
Authors: Kristine Rømer Thomsen, Natascha Hareskov, Camilla Krogner Caspersen, Anne Maria Lauenborg, Mette Gammelmark Bergmann, Eva Ørnbøl, Kaare Bro Wellnitz, Sarah W Feldstein Ewing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlcohol use during adolescence has both immediate as well as long-term negative effects; in turn, delaying onset and preventing hazardous use in early adolescence is one essential avenue to protect and bolster adolescents’ socioemotional growth and neurodevelopment. This trial examines the effectiveness, feasibility and acceptability of a novel neurodevelopmentally informed intervention – Adolescent Developmentally-Appropriate health Promotion Therapy (ADAPT) among students in middle school. We hypothesize that, compared to the delayed treatment condition (DT), ADAPT students will show statistically significant behavior change across measures, including: intentions to drink (primary outcome), alcohol use and alcohol-related consequences, well-being and life satisfaction (secondary outcomes) from baseline to 3 months.MethodThe study employs a 2-condition cluster randomized controlled trial comparing: (1) ADAPT with a (2) DT condition. Approximately N = 1000 8th grade students (ages 13–15) will be recruited from public middle schools in a global region with elevated rates of adolescent drinking (Denmark; during the school year 2025/2026). Data evaluating primary and secondary outcome measures (e.g., intentions to drink, alcohol use, alcohol-related consequences, well-being, life satisfaction) will be collected via online questionnaires at baseline, and 1- and 3-months post intervention. Feasibility and acceptability will be assessed via attendance in ADAPT group sessions and parent meetings, student surveys, and interviews with middle school staff.DiscussionThis is the first trial to examine effectiveness of the novel neurodevelopmentally informed intervention ADAPT on adolescent drinking and well-being. Novel interventions are needed to address the high prevalence of early drinking and the past decade decline in adolescent well-being, particularly in regions of the globe with elevated adolescent drinking rates. The findings of the study hold substantial significance for the health and well-being of adolescents and can inform evidence-based decisions on alcohol prevention policy, and professional health care practices in Scandinavia and throughout the globe.The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on August 11th , 2025. Background

Alcohol is the top substance used by adolescents globally [1, 2]. One of the biggest issues for young people who engage in hazardous alcohol use is that while numerous interventions exist, they have been largely developed for adults [3, 4]. And not only are very few adolescents treatment seeking in the same way as adults, adolescent alcohol use has quite different patterns of onset and trajectory [4]. Briefly, due to their different and very specific age of socioemotional as well as neural development, nature of social benefits vs. consequences (e.g., roles of peers; roles of social media) [5], and nature of engagement with treatment providers, interventions for adolescents engaged in alcohol use continue to show quite a bit of room for improvement [4].

One benefit of conducting empirical trials of alcohol interventions with adolescents in Denmark is that they show some of the overall highest rates of global alcohol use. Specifically, models of prevention and intervention programming can benefit from development, testing, and refinement in countries with high levels of adolescent drinking, before being brought to the larger global prevention and treatment stage. In turn, in this study, we selected Denmark as an optimal region to evaluate our novel intervention.

Danish adolescents continue to have some of the highest rates of alcohol consumption globally: 55% of 15–16-year-olds report past month heavy episodic drinking; the highest rate of all European countries [6]. Despite these heavy drinking behaviors, 56% of 15 to 25-year-old Danes acknowledge that the level of alcohol consumption among their peers and at their schools is too high [7]. Among Danish adolescents who drink, many report associated negative consequences. In a survey of 16–17-year-olds from 2023 [8], 46.7% reported past year experiences of blackout and a range of other past year negative consequences because of drinking, including accidents and injuries such as bike crashes (26.3%); intimate and sexual experiences, which they later regret (kissing 31.1%; sex 9%); sending texts or sharing something on social media, which they later regret (25.7%); taking illegal drugs, which they later regret (3.7%); and missing out on school or work (12.9%). While some of these issues may seem benign, each has been correlated with other serious health sequelae including, but not limited to, head injury (which can have protracted and long-term disruptions on learning and academic success) and contraction of sexually transmitted infections. Furthermore, they most likely disrupt and negatively impact adolescent well-being, at a moment in time when adolescents are experiencing elevated negative affect and feelings of isolation and disconnection [9–11].

Existing prevention interventions for adolescent substance use have yielded mixed results; in turn, there is a need for advancing novel approaches in substance use prevention in this age group [4, 12, 13]. In effect, adolescent researchers have been calling for the creation of novel behavioral approaches to substance use prevention and health promotion that draw on current neurodevelopmental research and are thus specifically articulated to adolescents [4].

Although a rise in mental health problems [14] and a decline in youth subjective well-being [15] was evident well before COVID-19 [16, 17], the pandemic and the subsequent lockdowns exacerbated these tendencies globally [10, 18–21]. As a consequence, the high prevalence of mental health problems among adolescents indicate that well-being and life satisfaction are now so low [11, 22] that public and health agencies have declared a “Global Youth Mental Health Crisis” in our time [23]. This is also true for Danish adolescents. A recent study found that indicators of well-being, including life satisfaction, self-rated health, and psychosocial well-being among adolescents have been deteriorating over 12 years [9]. In 2021, 8% of boys and 19% of girls exhibited low life satisfaction [9]. The same pattern is evident in the new HBSC survey which shows deterioration the past decade on all measures of mental health among Danish school children, particularly the girls [24].

Combined with limited evidence for the efficacy of existing school-based mental health and well-being interventions [25], these numbers point to the public health importance and time-sensitive need for developing innovative ways of enhancing well-being and supporting mental health among adolescents. This is especially pertinent as adolescence is a crucial window of opportunity [4, 26]; adolescents who thrive become adults who thrive and thus this developmental period is a pressing time to address concerns for adolescent mental health.

Adolescent Developmentally-Appropriate health Promotion Therapy (ADAPT)

ADAPT is a novel, neurodevelopmentally informed intervention aimed at preventing and reducing substance use and promoting well-being (developed by our team [27]), targeted to middle school through high school. By harnessing and bolstering the developmentally specific gifts of the adolescent brain, the intervention aims to reduce risk and enhance resilience.

The intervention is based on recent theoretical and empirical work in the field of developmental neuroscience. We have translated this research into tangible intervention principles and techniques to ensure that the intervention is specifically articulated to this particular stage of adolescent development. Thus, ADAPT aims to help mental health and school practitioners working with adolescents approach substance prevention and the promotion of well-being for this age group via a neurodevelopmental lens. We propose four key neurodevelopmental features of adolescence, including; pubertal changes, a surge of cognitive skills, a developing sense of self, and changing social landscape [4, 27]. These features, along with their interplay and impact on the behavior and dispositions of adolescents, highlight important new directions for development of health promotion programs for this age group, and form the basis of ADAPT. ADAPT is, to our knowledge, the first truly translational (integrated brain + behavior) health promotion intervention developed specifically for adolescents. ADAPT consists of three group sessions with 6–8 participants in each group and an interactive parent component.

Aim and hypotheses

The main aim of this study is to examine the effectiveness of the novel, neurodevelopmentally informed intervention - ADAPT – on preventing alcohol use and promoting well-being among students in middle school in a heavy adolescent alcohol use region (Denmark).

Using a 2-condition cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) of students in 8th grade (age 13–15), we will examine the following hypotheses:

Compared to DT, ADAPT adolescents will show significant reductions in intentions to drink (primary outcome) from baseline to 3 months post intervention.

Compared to DT, ADAPT adolescents will show significant reductions in intentions to drink (primary outcome) from baseline to 3 months post intervention.

Compared to DT, ADAPT adolescents will show significant reductions in alcohol use and alcohol-related consequences, and significant increases in well-being and life satisfaction (secondary outcomes) from baseline to 3 months.

Compared to DT, ADAPT adolescents will show significant reductions in alcohol use and alcohol-related consequences, and significant increases in well-being and life satisfaction (secondary outcomes) from baseline to 3 months.

We will also examine ADAPT’s feasibility and acceptability with students in middle school, their parents, and with staff (e.g., teachers and principals), to inform and guide next step, larger scale randomized controlled trials.

Study setting and recruitment

The last years of middle school are a sensitive window because of the high rates of drinking in this age group. Among 13-year-old Danes, 39% of girls and 40% of boys have tried drinking, which increases to 85% for both groups at age 15 [24]. Among 15-year-old Danes, 22% of girls and 27% of boys drink alcohol weekly, and 44% of girls and 48% of boys report heavy episodic drinking more than once [24]. Yet, despite these very high rates, there is a paucity of evidence-based and impactful substance use prevention interventions integrated into middle schools in Demark, and internationally.

Public middle schools (attended by 82% of children in Denmark) were contacted by e-mail and invited to hear more about participation, with those interested scheduling an in-person meeting with the research team. Informational sessions were conducted during Spring-Summer 2025 until a sufficient N of 1000 8th grade students was reached. A total of 15 middle schools in 14 Danish cities or smaller towns opted to participate, situated in 6 Danish municipalities. Students starting 8th grade at the involved schools in August 2025 will be invited to participate. Students and their parents/guardians will receive participant information minimum two weeks before enrollment (distributed directly by schools).

Eligibility criteria

Publicly funded middle schools in Denmark were eligible if they: (1) have ≥ 2 8th grade classes in the school year 2025/2026; and (2) are within 1.5-hour drive from Aarhus University. In Denmark, children with special needs (e.g., autism or other developmental disorders) attend special classes that cater for their needs. These special classes were not included.

Inclusion criteria

Students: starting 8th grade in a participating public middle school in August 2025, able to speak and understand Danish, and giving informed active consent with informed passive consent from parents/guardians.

Conditions

ADAPT

ADAPT consists of three group sessions (of 75–90 min), with 6–8 8th grade students in each group, administered over three consecutive weeks, plus one interactive parent component (of 45 min) administered during the three weeks (separate from the student sessions). The first ADAPT session will be delivered in the week following the baseline survey.

The three group sessions are manualized, but the content may be adjusted to align with the needs and developmental level of the included participants. Session 1’s theme is “Working together to build self and community”. The constructs targeted in Session 1 include ADAPT group cohesion, sense of self-worth, sense of community, prosocial behavior, contribution, and making a difference. Session 2’s theme is “Working together to engage as critical consumers”. The constructs targeted in Session 2 include critical perceptions of and attitudes towards alcohol use, sense of social justice, well-being and community. Session 3’s theme is “Working together to make positive contributions in your communities”. The constructs targeted in Session 3 include sense of community, belief in ability to contribute, drive for social justice, prosocial behavior, and contribution (See Fig. 2). Group sessions will be delivered by study staff, trained in ADAPT.

Overview of the standard content and foci of the ADAPT group sessions and parent meeting

Participants will be divided into groups of 6–8 participants (depending on class size). Groups will be mixed gender and similar in size to the ADAPT pilot [27] and previous clinical trials of group-based interventions performed by this team [28, 29]. ADAPT is designed to be delivered to students regardless of individual alcohol use – this is important to ensure maximal reach and generalizability across the range of adolescent drinking, and to avoid inadvertent stigmatization e.g. of adolescents already drinking/not drinking. Following our recent trials [27–29], the ADAPT groups will take place in a classroom at the target schools during the school day. All sessions will be audio-recorded via a digital recorder for intervention fidelity and supervision.

The interactive parent meeting is 45 min (in person, held at the school) and will take place within the three weeks of ADAPT group sessions. Parents from each school class will be invited to attend together. The parent session begins with a 15-minute presentation emphasizing the importance of parents in promoting adolescent well-being and health, including alcohol use prevention. Parents will be afforded concrete, empirically-based advice about how parents can serve in a protective role, support their adolescents, and help them navigate the challenges related to this specific developmental window. As examples, parents/caregivers are informed that spending time with their adolescents and showing an active interest in knowing where they are and who they are with, serve as protective factors. Following the presentation, the group leader facilitates a discussion among parents in smaller groups (approximately 30 min). After the meeting, parents will be given leaflets to take home, summarizing the information, which can serve as a basis for conversations at home with their adolescents, who are participating in ADAPT group sessions simultaneously at the school.

Delayed treatment (DT)

The control condition is a DT condition. All adolescents in the DT condition will receive assessments on the same timeline as ADAPT adolescents, but will not receive the intervention. Following the final 3-months follow-up survey, ADAPT will be offered to students and their parents in DT school classes.

Power analysis and sample size

Based on the primary outcome of intentions to drink (dichotomized), sample size planning was done using simulation in Stata version 18 [30]. Specifically, realizations of the dichotomized intentions to drink outcome was sampled from a logistic model allowing for different proportions of gender at baseline and different proportions for gender, condition, and gender-by-condition-interaction at 3-months follow up. Simulated data were then analyzed using a simpler parameterized logistic regression model, only including time and time*condition interaction (i.e., model assuming common baseline levels of intentions to drink due to randomization, allowing for different developments for the two conditions). Power was then estimated as the proportion of 1000 resamples where the interaction between time and condition (i.e., the comparison of ADAPT versus DT at 3-month follow-up) was significant at p <.05.

Assuming stability of intentions to drink for ADAPT students (baseline: boys, 50%; girls: 30%; average 40%; follow-up: boys, 50%; girls, 30%; average, 40%), but poorer drinking outcomes measured as greater intentions to drink for DT (baseline: boys, 50%; girls: 30%; average, 40%; follow-up: boys, 60%; girls, 40%; average, 50%) – which corresponds to an odds ratio at follow-up of 0.66 – power of 0.80 was reached for a sample of 750 students.

To account for anticipated attrition, an N of 1000 students is required to fully power the planned analyses for the primary outcome. Assuming an average of 24 students in each 8th grade classroom, we aim to include 42 school classes, with randomization at the class level.

Randomization

KBW generated the allocation sequences via computer-generated random numbers in Stata version 17 [31]. Allocation sequence was made in a way where randomized number of classes in each of the two conditions was as equal as possible at each school and ultimately across all randomized school classes. Specifically, schools with equal number of classes were randomized based on a randomization sequence with a block-size of 2 – for each draw of 2 consecutive allocations, one would be intervention, and one would be control. For schools with 3 school classes, randomization was done based on nested block structure, with 2 blocks of 3 classes nested within one block of 6 classes. Thus, for each consecutive pair of schools with 3 classes, 3 classes would be allocated to intervention, and 3 classes would be allocated to control, while each school separately would either have 1 or 2 classes in intervention and conversely 2 or 1 classes in control.

KBW kept the allocation sequence, and upon request from the lead investigator on the ground (KRT), disclosed which school classes on a given school were allocated to which condition. Because the result of randomization was provided in two chunks of schools (with 8 and 7 schools, respectively), KRT could not predict if a school with uneven number of classes was going to have 1 or 2 classes in the ADAPT condition. CKC, AML and MGB disclosed the list to each individual school principal. The random school class allocation to either the ADAPT condition or the DT condition was therefore disclosed simultaneously for all school classes at a particular school. After assignment to interventions, blinding is not possible.

Assessment of outcomes

Survey data from students will be collected at baseline, and 1- and 3-months post intervention. The surveys are online and will be completed during school hours. Members of the research team will introduce the surveys in person and will be present to answer any questions and assist with troubleshooting. This may facilitate retention in the trial.

Assessment of feasibility and acceptability

Feasibility of ADAPT will be measured quantitatively via enrollment and attendance in the three groups sessions. Acceptability of ADAPT will be measured quantitatively via student satisfaction with the sessions, including whether they liked taking part and would recommend it to other students in 8th grade. Furthermore, we will use open-ended questions in the student survey to examine what they liked, what could be improved, and what made an impression on them.

To ensure intervention integrity, in line with our recent studies in Denmark [27–29], researchers delivering ADAPT will be trained in the ADAPT manual and practice by KRT and NHJ. All sessions will be audio recorded for fidelity and integrity evaluation.

To evaluate feasibility of the interactive parent meeting, we will evaluate attendance rates. To evaluate acceptability of the parent meeting, we will examine parent satisfaction with the meeting, including whether they found the information relevant, and whether they would recommend the school to offer this meeting to next years’ parents of 8th grade students. These measures will be administered via an anonymous link at the end of the parent meeting.

To evaluate feasibility and acceptability at the institutional (school) level, we will conduct qualitative, semi-structured interviews with staff involved in the project (principal and relevant teachers/other staff). Interviews will be done after the last follow-up survey in the delayed control comparison condition (i.e. at the end of the school year, or during the next) and will be focused on the perception of ADAPT (group sessions and parent meeting), how participation in ADAPT was perceived and suggestions for improvement of ADAPT and for potential future implementation.

Data management

Quantitative data from the surveys will be entered into a SurveyXact server hosted on a secured server in Denmark. Quantitative data from the randomization, enrollment, and surveys; audio recordings of the group sessions; and qualitative data from the interviews will be kept on a secure server hosted by Aarhus University. Audio recordings of group sessions will be deleted after fidelity check. Audio recordings of interviews with school representatives will be transcribed and pseudonymized after which audio files will be deleted. Only current and future members of the research team, who have a research affiliation to Aarhus University, will have access to the data.

Statistical analyses

All descriptive analyses will be reported and summarized after the quantitative data collection has ended. Summaries will be presented as means and standard deviation of variables that are approximately normally distributed, or as medians and IQRs for skewed variables. Categorical variables will be summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Enrollment, study flow, and retention details will be provided in a CONSORT chart.

The primary and secondary hypotheses will be analyzed using generalized estimation equations (GEE) to account for the nested data structure: repeated measures, nested within students, nested within groups, nested within school classes, nested within schools. GEE analysis will employ an unstructured within student correlation matrix to account for within-subject correlations across repeated measurements, whereas robust standard errors will be employed to adjust for left-over clustering from class and school. The specific family and link will depend on the level of measurement of the particular outcome: The primary binarized outcome of intentions to drink will be analyzed using binomial family and logit link. Secondary count outcomes may have excess zeros due to age and will ideally be analyzed using zero-inflated GEE with Poisson family and log link. Lastly, continuous secondary outcomes will be analyzed using Gaussian family and identity link. For all primary and secondary outcomes, hypotheses are evaluated based on the interaction between time and intervention at 3-months follow-up, which translates directly into the difference in development (i.e., intervention effect) assuming equal baseline values due to randomization. Hypotheses are evaluated based on alpha = 0.05.

Analyses of effectiveness will be based on the intention-to-treat sample, utilizing all available follow-up data from all randomized participants. All randomized participants will be analyzed within the condition to which they were allocated, regardless of whether they receive or complete the intervention. Participants who withdraw their consent for use of their data during the trial period will not be included in analyses.

No interim analyses and stopping guidelines will be applied, because the prevention intervention of the trial does not entail risk of harm to the participants.

Qualitative analyses

To investigate the feasibility and acceptability of ADAPT at the institutional (school) level, qualitative interviews with school staff will be coded and analyzed in NVivo using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework for thematic analysis [40]. This approach provides a flexible yet rigorous method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns within qualitative data. Thematic development will be an active and recursive process, involving interpretation of both semantic and latent content. The resulting themes will reflect both data-driven insights and theoretical constructs relevant to intervention feasibility and acceptability in school settings.

Dissemination

Results from the trial will be published in international peer-reviewed journals, preferably with open access, and in national outlets with open access. Furthermore, presentations of main group-level findings are offered to participating schools and other interested stakeholders.

Discussion

The overall aim of this RCT is to test the effectiveness of a novel neurodevelopmentally informed intervention, Adolescent Developmentally-Appropriate health Promotion Therapy (ADAPT), on adolescent drinking and well-being in 13 to 15-year-old Danish middle schoolers. By conducting a fully-powered 2-condition cluster RCT with baseline and follow-up at 1- and 3-months, we will examine ADAPT compared to a delayed treatment (DT) condition on intentions to drink (primary outcome), alcohol use and related consequences, and well-being and life satisfaction (secondary outcomes).

The potential effectiveness of ADAPT holds significant implications for adolescent health and well-being globally, influencing multiple facets of adolescents’ lives. With growing evidence showing that early (before age 18) heavy episodic drinking negatively impacts cognition, brain structure, and function in otherwise healthy adolescents [41], delaying alcohol onset and preventing heavy episodic drinking prior to alcohol becoming inculcated into young adults’ day-to-day social culture is a critical avenue to protect and facilitate adolescent’s growth and neurodevelopment.

Existing interventions in this area have been largely developed for adults and informed by adult frameworks [3, 4]. However, due to their different and specific age of socioemotional, cognitive, and neural development [4, 42, 43], as well as different nature of social benefits vs. consequences [5], adolescents engage with prevention and treatment providers in a diffent way than adults. Behavioral approaches that draw on current neurodevelopmental research and are specifically articulated to adolescents hence represent a promising new approach [4].

ADAPT targets substance use prevention and well-being promotion via a neurodevelopmental lens and is, to our knowledge, the first truly translational (integrated brain + behavior) health promotion intervention developed for adolescents [27]. This study is the first trial to examine the effectiveness of ADAPT on adolescent drinking and well-being.

Because of the increase in drinking from ages 13 to 15 in Denmark [24], and the further increase in drinking in the shift from middle school to high school [7, 44], the last years of middle school is an important time to intervene. Middle school is mandatory in Denmark and hence offers a unique opportunity to target all adolescents. Importantly, if successful, ADAPT may be implemented across middle schools countrywide. Efficacy in a region with elevated alcohol use at younger ages, like Denmark, has a high degree of global significance and promise, in terms of future generalizability and ultimately, potential implementation across myriad nations.

Implementing effective, feasible, and acceptable prevention interventions in salient settings, such as public middle schools, is of great importance and utility. If effective, ADAPT can be implemented at low cost. Personnel at schools or prevention/health professionals from the municipalities can be trained to facilitate ADAPT student groups and deliver the parent meeting at low cost and using existing resources within school infrastructure. The research team members delivering ADAPT in a pilot trial of ADAPT [27] and the current RCT receive minimal training (4-day course), an intentional choice in intervention design for ease of widespread future implementation.

In conclusion, the trial offers valuable insights on the effectiveness of the first truly neuro-developmentally informed health promotion intervention aimed at preventing alcohol use and related problems and promoting well-being in adolescents. The findings of the study hold substantial significance for the health and well-being of adolescents and can inform evidence-based decisions on alcohol prevention policy, and professional health care practices in Scandinavia and throughout the globe.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study is carried out in accordance with the study protocol, and the Helsinki Declaration. The study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency and the Research Ethics Committee at Aarhus University, Denmark (ID BSS-2025-056-S2). Participants will receive written and verbal information about the study, including that participation is voluntary and they are free to withdraw from the study. Parents will receive the same written information and can contact the research team with questions or concerns. Informed consent will be obtained from students prior to the baseline survey (this will be done online for all students; a researcher from the team will be present in the class to answer potential questions). Additionally, written informed consent will be obtained for ADAPT group sessions, before initiation of the first group session (a researcher from the team will be present to answer potential questions). Passive informed consent will be obtained from parents/guardians of students. This means that parents are informed that they are free to contact the principal investigator if they do not want to consent to their adolescent’s participation. This is in accordance with Danish law for this project type and has been approved by The Research Ethics Committee, Aarhus BSS, at Aarhus University. Several studies (including the ADAPT pilot study) show that parents’ non-response to invitations to adolescent/child participation in research is mainly due to practical issues with returning signed consent forms, rather than parents not wanting their adolescents/children to participate [45]. Further, requiring active parental consent can lead to biases in samples, underrepresenting the most at-risk adolescents, i.e., the group who would benefit most from initiatives promoting health and well-being [45–47]. Lastly, there are no known risks associated with participation, but excluding students can have potential negative consequences such as singling out individuals and excluding them from the class community. Therefore, to ensure ethical and methodological quality, we require passive parental consent, aligning with the principle that development and testing of health-promoting interventions must include all adolescents to be socially just, rather than systematically excluding those most at risk [46]. Informed consent will be obtained online from school staff prior to the qualitative interviews. Data will be stored on secure servers in accordance with the Danish Data Protection Act (Law # 502 of May 12, 2018), and the European General Data Protection Regulation (2016/679) of the European Parliament and of the Council. The Danish Data Protection Agency has approved all data collection procedures and will continue to monitor the data collection and storage throughout the project. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on August 11, 2025. ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07132866. In case of important protocol modifications, these will be reported to the Research Ethics Committee at Aarhus University, the Danish Data Protection Agency, and at ClinicalTrials.gov. The prevention intervention included in this trial does not entail risk of harm to participants. However, if individuals experience any discomfort related to their participation, they are encouraged to contact members of the team, who will help or direct them to relevant services. Potential events will be reported in publications of main findings from the trial. Participants are informed that they are free to skip questions in the surveys and in the ADAPT group sessions.
