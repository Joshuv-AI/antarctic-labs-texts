A Case Report of JAK Inhibitors Therapy for Adult-Onset Still’s Disease with Persistent Pruritic Lesions
Authors: Li Tang, Hongjian Shi, Weijun Liu, Pingxiu He, Chun Huang, Xiaobing Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAdult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a recognized autoinflammatory disorder of unknown etiology. The standard initial management for AOSD includes conventional corticosteroids and disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs. In cases that are resistant to these treatments, additional therapeutic options such as immunosuppressants, biologics, and other alternative treatments may be considered. Yet, a significant proportion of patients remain unresponsive to these therapeutic interventions. Herein, a case is reported involving a patient with AOSD who had persistent pruritic lesions that did not respond to conventional therapy, but were alleviated with Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), namely baricitinib and upadacitinib. The objective is to expand the number of refractory AOSD cases treated with JAKi in clinical practice. Another aim is to offer potentially effective therapeutic options for AOSD patients who experience persistent pruritus.MethodsA case was reported involving AOSD characterized by persistent pruritic lesions that failed to respond to conventional treatment, but showed favorable outcomes with JAKi therapy. An analysis of the PubMed literature was performed to assess the medication’s efficacy and explore possible mechanisms.ResultsThe present case study is one of the few documented instances exploring the use of JAKi for treating AOSD, aligning with previously published research. After initiating JAKi therapy, the patient exhibited significant improvement in symptoms, most notably a reduction in persistent pruritus. Additionally, there was a substantial decrease in the patient’s glucocorticoid dosage. Aside from minor renal function anomalies, no adverse reactions were observed.ConclusionsThe present case illustrates that JAKi can provide rapid and sustained clinical improvement in patients with AOSD, especially those who have not responded to conventional treatment, and they have the ability to alleviate persistent itching. Further investigation is needed to ascertain the precise mechanism.

Key Points

Adult-onset Still's disease (AOSD) is a rare, inexplicable, and difficult to treat multi-system autoinflammatory disease.Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) can play an effective role in AOSD patients who are refractory to conventional treatments.Baricitinib and upadacitinib can alleviate the symptoms of AOSD patients who are unresponsive to traditional treatments, especially persistent itching.

Introduction

Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a rare, idiopathic multisystemic autoinflammatory disorder characterized by high spiking fever, rash, polyarthralgia, sore throat, and potentially life-threatening complications such as macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) [1]. The diagnosis of AOSD is exclusive and necessitates the exclusion of infections, autoimmune disorders, and malignant tumors [2]. The treatment for AOSD typically includes the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), glucocorticoids (GCs), disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), and immunosuppressive medications. Nonetheless, a notable proportion of patients, estimated at 30–40%, exhibit inadequate responses to these therapeutic interventions [3]. Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi) are a significant category of targeted synthetic DMARDs [4], which have been effectively utilized in AOSD patients and have demonstrated favorable outcomes [1, 2]. In the present report, a case is described involving a patient with AOSD who experienced persistent pruritic lesions resistant to conventional therapy. These symptoms resolved following treatment with baricitinib and upadacitinib.

Case Report

A total of 3 weeks prior to presentation at our hospital, the patient, a 52-year-old woman, presented with fever, cough, and sore throat of unknown etiology. A week later, she developed erythema, papules, and pruritus on her head, face, neck, trunk, and limbs, along with joint and muscle pain. The local hospital diagnosed her with “allergic dermatitis.” Although anti-inflammatory and antiallergic treatments alleviated her fever and cough, the other symptoms persisted. Upon referral to our hospital, the patient was initially diagnosed with “eczema” due to intermittent fever, persistent pruritus, and sore throat. A specialist examination revealed significant pharyngeal inflammation and slight tonsillar hypertrophy. The patient exhibited erythema, papules, and patches on the head, face, neck, trunk, and proximal extremities. Joint swelling and pain were noted in the knees and wrists, along with muscular tightness (Fig. 1a–d).

Comparison of patient images before and after treatment. (a–d) Lesions before treatment; erythema, papules, and patches covered the patient’s head, face, neck, trunk, and proximal limbs. (e–h) A total of 3 months after Janus kinase inhibitor treatment; skin lesions returned to normal

Laboratory tests revealed elevated levels of white blood cells (11.6 × 109/L), neutrophils (9.4 × 109/L; 81.3%), C-reactive protein (62 mg/L), and eosinophils (0.6 × 109/L; 5.2%). Elevated liver enzyme levels were detected, including aspartate aminotransferase at 156 U/L and alanine aminotransferase at 172 U/L, indicating aberrant liver function. However, renal function was within the normal range. Furthermore, there was a significant increase in serum ferritin levels (> 2000 ng/mL). Increased levels of procalcitonin, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (104 mm/h), and antinuclear antibody titers (1:1000) were also observed. The tests for anti-streptolysin O, rheumatoid factor, human leucocyte antigen-B27, and cyclic citrullinated peptide were all negative. Additionally, throat swab culture, Mycobacterium tuberculosis screening, immunoglobulin levels, tumor markers, complement levels, and systemic lupus erythematous (SLE) panel results were all within normal limits. Ultrasound revealed splenomegaly, a thyroid nodule classified as TI-RADS 4, and Hashimoto thyroiditis. Computed tomography (CT) scans of the head, chest, and abdomen, along with ultrasound examinations of the heart and the lower limbs, revealed no abnormalities. A positron emission tomography (PET)-CT scan was not conducted. A histological examination revealed hyperplasia of the spinous layer of the epidermis, elongation of the dermal processes, and scattered necrotic keratinocytes in the epidermis. The superficial dermis showed a dispersed infiltration of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and eosinophils surrounding blood vessels and within collagen bundles (Fig. 2).

The patient received a diagnosis of AOSD based on the Yamaguchi criteria [5], after ruling out tumors, infections, and other autoimmune and pruritus diseases. After initiating treatment with methylprednisolone sodium succinate 40 mg (twice daily), celecoxib 200 mg (once daily), methotrexate 15 mg (weekly), and desloratadine 8.8 mg (once daily), the patient experienced a reduction in fever, joint discomfort, and throat pain. However, the pruritus persisted unchanged. At the patient’s request, methotrexate and celecoxib were replaced with baricitinib 1 mg (twice daily), supplemented with ebastine 10 mg (nightly), cetirizine hydrochloride 10 mg (once daily), and continued methylprednisolone sodium succinate 40 mg (twice daily). This adjustment led to a notable reduction in pruritus. After a 2-month treatment period, a slight increase in creatinine levels was noted, prompting a switch from baricitinib to upadacitinib 15 mg (once daily). Following 1 month of upadacitinib therapy, all indications and skin lesions had resolved (Fig. 1e–h). The patient has been on JAKi for 12 months with ongoing monitoring and without any adverse responses. Methylprednisolone dosage was gradually tapered from 80 mg to 2 mg (every 3 days).

Discussion

AOSD is an idiopathic systemic inflammatory disease that may be caused by pro-inflammatory cytokines released by innate immune cells, such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, IL-17, IL-23, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interferon (IFN)-γ, and macrophage inhibitory factor. These cytokines can trigger cytokine storms and play a role in the pathogenesis of AOSD [1, 3]. Janus kinase (JAK) belongs to the tyrosine-kinase family, and phosphorylated JAK can activate the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling pathway [4]. JAKi are small-molecule medications that inhibit the JAK–STAT pathway, and selectively interfere with the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) binding site of JAK. This inhibition exerts immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects [3], and JAKi have demonstrated efficacy in treating various inflammatory diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and SLE [4, 6]. The JAKi agents can be grouped by different kinase-specific effects: tofacitinib (JAK1/2/3), baricitinib and ruxolitinib (JAK1/2), and upadacitinib and filgotinib (JAK1) [4, 6]. Although JAKi are currently second-/third-line drugs for treating AOSD, studies have recently reported that JAKi can play an effective role in AOSD patients who are refractory to conventional and biologic treatments [2, 3, 7], even in AOSD patients with complicated MAS [8].

In comparison to previously reported cases, the most notable difference in the present case is that the patient experienced persistent severe pruritus, which was promptly and effectively alleviated after utilizing JAKi. Selective JAK1 inhibitors, such as upadacitinib and abrocitinib, have demonstrated in clinical trials and practice the ability to rapidly and efficiently reduce itching, making them effective for treating conditions such as atopic dermatitis characterized by severe pruritus [9]. One probable explanation is that JAK1 can efficiently inhibit inflammatory factors that produce pruritus, such as IL-31 and INF-γ [10]. JAKi can reduce GC doses from 64% to 80% in AOSD patients, although not all achieve remission [1, 6]. In the present case, the patient’s GC dosage was reduced considerably, from 80 mg methylprednisolone per day to 2 mg every 3 days. There were no significant adverse reactions except for changing the type of JAKi due to slight renal dysfunction.

In conclusion, JAKi treatment can provide rapid and sustained clinical improvement for patients with AOSD, especially those who do not respond adequately to conventional treatments. JAKi also have the potential to alleviate persistent pruritus. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these effects.
