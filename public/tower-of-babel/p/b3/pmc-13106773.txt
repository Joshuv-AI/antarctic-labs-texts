Chirality transfer from chiral perovskite to molecular dopants via charge transfer states
Authors: Guan-Lin Chen, Hsinhan Tsai, Reshna Shrestha, Aaron Forde, Matthew R Crawley, Cunyi Wei, Kai-Wei Tseng, Zhe-Yu Liu, Chi-An Dai, Xiao Tong, Mircea Cotlet, Leeyih Wang, Sergei Tretiak, Wanyi Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChiral perovskites are semiconductors with broken mirror symmetries. Their photo responses are often constrained in the UV range. In this work, we demonstrate that doping 2,3,5,6-Tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane in the chiral perovskite matrix introduces a visible light absorption feature through the emerging charge-transfer electronic states. These charge-transfer states exhibits circular dichroism inherited from the chiral host, indicating effective chirality transfer from host to guest component via electronic coupling. Quantum-chemical modeling identifies a strong wave function overlap between an electron and a hole of the guest-host in a closely packed crystal configuration promoting the charge transfer state’s optical activity. We further integrate the doped chiral perovskite film into photodetectors and demonstrate a selective detection of circularly polarized light in both UV and visible regions. Our results suggest a universal approach of introducing visible photo absorption states to the chiral matrix to broaden the optical active range while enhancing the electrical conductivity.Subject terms: Materials for devices, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

Chirality is an important symmetry property of molecular systems1: A chiral molecule cannot be superimposed on its mirror image, which breaks the molecule’s inversion symmetry2. Semiconductors crafted in chiral structures have preference to absorb photon with the same chirality. Additionally, chiral-induced spin selectivity effect3,4 is proposed in the chiral semiconductors which can discriminatorily transport spin-polarized carriers5,6. These properties are highly promising for photo-detectors and imaging that can distinguish the polarization states of photon7.

There are few types of chiral semiconductors that have drawn extensive attention, such as chiral small molecules and polymers8–10, quantum dot assembly11,12, organo-inorganic hybrids13–15 and meta surfaces16,17. The organic chiral semiconductors synthesized in helix structures can deliver strong anisotropy with a typical anisotropic factor of circular dichroism (gCD) in the range of 0.001 to 0.2 with a few exceptionally high values above 0.5 up to 1.218–21. However, the helix organics require intense synthetic approaches to obtain a highly asymmetric structure. The electrical conductivities are often low in organic materials, limiting their performances as a photo-detector.

Hybrid organo-inorganic chiral structures can accommodate a wide tunability in the local structure with tunable optoelectronic properties. Chiral perovskites are one of hybrid semiconductor systems that have shown great promise for photon detections22–25. A typical chiral perovskite structure with commonly used chiral ligands is illustrated in Fig. 1a, where chiral organic molecules incorporated into lead-halide octahedrons (PbX6) drive the rotations of the PbX6 cages following the chirality of the molecules as shown in Fig. 1a. The dipole moment of the excited states thus orients with the helix PbX6 rotation direction to impose optical selectivity to circular polarization state of photons26. With strong circular dichroism (CD) demonstrated13,14,27–30 chiral perovskites were integrated into photodetectors for the direct detection of circularly polarized light. The photo detectors were capable of differentiate the handness of the incoming circularly polarized light by its distinct change in photoconductivity, delivering anisotropic factors of 0.1~0.222,30–33. Circularly polarized light emission from the chiral perovskites was also possible31,34,35, which are promising photonic sources with polarized states. Very recently, zero-dimensional In based hybrid chiral structures have been demonstrated for exceptional polarized photoluminescence with strong intensity36.

While these pioneer demonstrations open new avenues for chiral opto-electronics, the organics in the chiral perovskites were considered insulators that add barriers to the electrical conduction of the charge carriers37–39. Chiral perovskites are typically low-dimensional structures40–42 that underpin quantum confinement. Consequently, chiral perovskites have a wide optical band gap and limited responsivity to visible light. Due to their low conductivity and lack of photo-activity in the visible range, low-dimensional chiral perovskite materials face significant limitations in their potential electronic applications. Displayed in Fig. 1b, we summarized the reported gph and their absorption cut-off wavelength of the photo-detectors made with organic, hybrids and metal nanostructured materials7,8,18–21,30,43–59. Most of the organic and hybrid chiral semiconductors show an absorption cut-off between 400 and 550 nm. The meta-surfaces of metallic nano-structures are one of the active research areas; they are not semiconductor-type but also deliver high performance, mostly in the near-IR range. Noticeably, there is a gap between 600nm and 1000 nm where only few chiral semiconductors are developed for detector. To bridge this gap, several studies have attempted to extend the absorption range via different mechanisms. For example, Peng et al.60 employed a two-photon absorption mechanism to upconvert the photon via a chiral perovskite media. With this mechanism, they have demonstrated a dual modal circularly polarized light detector with visible light (520 nm) and NIR light (800 nm). Another report by Yang et al.61 investigated a hetero-structure formed by a 2D chiral perovskite interfaced with a 3D bulk film. This structure enabled spin filtering across the interface that promoted the photocurrent produced by photon with a certain polarization state.

Here, we investigate a molecular dopant into the chiral perovskite matrix to extend the circularly polarized photon absorption into the visible range via a photoactive charge transfer state. By mixing 2,3,5,6-Tetrafluoro-7,7,8,8-tetracyanoquinodimethane (F4TCNQ) molecules into the chiral perovskite matrix, we observe the emergence of an optically active charge transfer state formed between the chiral perovskites host and the F4TCNQ guest, which adds a visible absorption band in the 550–750 nm wavelength range. Interestingly, both the intrinsic absorption band from the perovskite matrix and the charge transfer absorption feature exhibit circular dichroism, indicative of a chirality transfer from the host to the guest. Transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) confirms the presence of charge transfer states at the interface. Furthermore, atomistic time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) simulations of the chiral perovskite/F4TCNQ interface corroborate the experimental observations. Specifically, our calculations suggest that substitutional doping of F4TCNQ enhances electronic coupling, leading to the formation of optically bright charge-transfer states. The close-packed crystal structure is further observed in the gracing incident X-ray scattering pattern where a large lattice feature was observed corresponding to the interpolation of F4TCNQ between the perovskite chain. Finally, we demonstrate the direct detection of circularly polarized light by integrating our host-guest structure in a photo-detector. Due to the chirality transfer, our detector can discern the chirality of photon across both the blue and visible spectral ranges. Altogether, our findings suggest a general approach for chirality transfer from the host matrix to the guest dopant via an electronically coupled charge transfer state.

Results

Circular dichroism of chiral perovskite

We start with synthesizing chiral perovskite structure using chiral enantiomer ((S)−1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidinemethanol and (R)−1-Methyl-2-pyrrolidinemethanol) as shown in Fig. 2a. The chiral perovskites are named S-MP and R-MP thereafter for convenience. The material growth procedures are detailed in the Method Section. Following material synthesis and recrystallization, we obtain needle-like palm-yellow single crystals (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicative of a 1D structure formation. We further investigate the crystal structure by growing single crystals and conduct single crystal diffraction (Fig. 2b). After crystal structure refinement, we find that R-MP perovskite crystallized in the chiral space group P212121 (space group 19), a Sohncke space group. The detailed crystal structure information is summarized in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2 in the SI.

We next design model simulations to gain atomistic insights into the chirality property of the obtained crystals. Our approach addresses two fundamental aspects: the chirality of the PbI6 sublattice48 and chirality transfer via electrostatic interactions with the chiral molecules62,63. Specifically, to simulate the circular dichroism (CD) spectrum using TDDFT methodology, we construct our computational model from a finite fragment of the perovskite crystal structure, based on available crystallographic data. To this framework, we append either an achiral methylpyridinium (MP) cation or its chiral R-/S-enantiomers. The models are briefly outlined in the Supplementary Methods Section. As a control, we also calculate the CD spectrum for the perovskite synthesized with the achiral MP molecule using a published crystal structure file (Supplementary Fig. 2)64. Figure 2c displays the computed CD spectra, which reveals that the perovskite synthesized with chiral MP exhibits much enhanced CD intensities whereas the structure synthesized with achiral MP shows negligible signal. Note that our model is built by modeling a fragment of the crystal. The calculated absorption onset (~300 nm) is blue-shifted relative to experiment (~400 nm), primarily due to the finite size of the modeled nanowire, which introduces artificial quantum confinement. Moreover, there is a well-known optical gap dependence on choice of the hybrid density functional (Supplementary Fig. 3). Additionally, the unresolved positions and orientations of the chiral molecules in the crystallographic data limit direct spectral comparison.

Nonetheless, the calculations reproduce the key qualitative feature, the enhanced CD response observed for the chiral perovskite (Fig. 2c) compared to the achiral analog, consistent with chirality transfer from the organic to the inorganic components. This result supports the concept of chirality transfer from a chiral molecule to the 1D crystal structure of the perovskite. In our case, a structural chiral distortion in the PbI6 1D chain is identified as a dominating mechanism. We also model the CD spectrum when chiral MP are placed on the surface of the perovskite lattice without driving the PbI6 cage rotations. If the chirality transfer is mediated by electrostatic interactions, we would expect to see a mirrored CD spectrum when comparing models containing the MP enantiomers. We find that their CD spectrum are nearly identical and only slightly deviate from the bare perovskite (Supplementary Fig. 3). This comparison further confirms that the CD originates from the chirality imprinted in the PbI6 inorganic lattice driven by the chiral MP molecules.

In Fig. 2c, we observe a small but non-zero CD signal from the non-chiral perovskite structure. This response originates not from intrinsic chirality, but rather from the finite size of the perovskite fragment used in our simulations. Specifically, the finite fragment breaks translational symmetry, introducing edge effects that, in our case, lead to a breaking of inversion symmetry. As a result, a weak CD signal emerges even in an achiral system. This behavior is distinct from previous reports in the literature65–67, where weak CD responses in achiral perovskites have been attributed primarily to the Rashba effect, arising from spin–orbit coupling and structural asymmetry at the atomic scale.

We next experimentally measure the CD and absorption spectra of the perovskite thin films under illumination with left-handed and right-handed circularly polarized light as summarized in Fig. 2d–f. The achiral counterpart is also measured as a comparison. Figure 2d is the photograph of the obtained solutions. With F4TCNQ, the solution turns into a bright green color, different from either pristine solution. F4TCNQ dopant is known for its strong electron-accepting ability due to its electron-deficient nature. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, the energy level of the pristine perovskite and F4TCNQ is illustrated based on the UPS measurement (Supplementary Fig. 4). The new absorption feature is attributed to the charge-transfer (CT) state formed at the donor-acceptor interface, as documented by prior reports on organic systems doped with F4TCNQ68–70. CT states represent electronic excitations in which the photoexcited electron and the corresponding hole become localized on different fragments or regions of a molecular or hybrid system. Such states play a critical role in processes such as photoinduced charge separation, energy transport, and dopant–host interactions. The defining characteristic of CT states is the spatial separation between the electron and hole, which often leads to weak Coulomb interaction and reduced wavefunction overlap. As a result, CT transitions typically exhibit small transition dipole moments and low oscillator strengths, rendering them optically dark in many systems. However, when strong electronic coupling or orbital hybridization exists between the donor and acceptor components, the electron–hole overlap can increase, giving rise to CT excitations with noticeable optical activity.

We next spin-coat thin films with these solutions and then annealed them at 100 °C for 10 min. Like the solution, the pristine chiral perovskite thin film has a transparent yellow color, whereas the doped film exhibits a green color (see film photo shown in Supplementary Fig. S1). Figure 2e shows the CD and absorption spectra of the pristine and doped S-MP and R-MP chiral perovskite thin film. The pristine films exhibit two derivative-shaped CD features crossing zero at 360 nm and 390 nm, corresponding to the two absorption peaks of the perovskite material. Near the absorption peak, the R-MP (S-MP) sample exhibits a negative (positive) CD at higher energy and a positive (negative) feature at lower energy, manifesting a Cotton effect52,71. To eliminate the influence of the linear dichroism, linear barefringes, we test the CD spectra by rotating the samples at 180° with respect to the incident light52,72. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 5–7. The CD signature of F4TCNQ only thin film is presented in Supplementary Fig. 8, where no CD is observed. Next, F4TCNQ is added to the precursor, with the weight percentage varied from 0.1% to 5%. The achiral MP thin film has a similar absorption spectra as the chiral films. It is interesting to note that the CT state absorption is almost negligible in the 1% F4TCNQ doped A-MP perovskite thin films. After analyzing the crystal structures with different dopant levels comparing chiral and achiral samples (see Supplementary Figs. 9–11), we think the molecular incorporation into the lattice is different in A-MP sample that contributes the difference in CD. More detailed discussions are provided towards the end of this paper.

Figure 2f shows the CD spectrum of the F4TCNQ-doped chiral perovskite thin film. Here, CD signals emerge near the absorption peaks of the perovskite band edge and broaden towards 416, along with a broad visible light absorption emerging between 550 and 750 nm, which contributes to the green color observed in the film. The dopant-dependent absorption and CD are investigated and illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 12. The peak position of the CT state remains unchanged, while the magnitude of the absorption and CD increases with higher doping levels. The fact that CD signals are also detected near the broad peak in the 550 nm to 750 nm range suggests the hypothesized charge-transfer states also inherits the chiroptical properties from the perovskite. In contrast to the chiral structures, the doped achiral structure does not exhibit CD in the longer wavelength range.

Carrier dynamics

To elucidate the electronic transitions associated with the different absorption features, we perform femtosecond pump-probe broadband TAS on both pristine and doped thin-film samples. From the spectra and kinetic analysis of each peak in the TAS spectrum, we confirm the presence of charge-transfer coupling between the chiral perovskite and the F4TCNQ dopant.

The TAS spectra, probed at various delay times following the pump pulse for both samples, are plotted in Fig. 3a, b. We use a 290 nm laser pump for excitation and a broadband source to probe the transient absorption in the UV-Vis spectral region. The UV-Vis spectrum of F4TCNQ film is shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 where its most pronounced absorption is centered around 350~400 nm. Therefore, we select a 290 nm pump laser which primarily excites the chiral perovskite matrix while avoiding direct excitation of the dopant. Therefore, any signal associated with the dopant would result from electronic transitions coupled to the chiral perovskite host. In the TAS spectrum of the undoped chiral perovskite displayed in Fig. 3a, a pronounced negative feature emerges at 390 nm within sub-picosecond time range after pumping, which is attributed to the ground state bleaching73. A positive feature near 360 nm and a weaker positive peak near 490 nm are also observed in this sample, corresponding to photo-induced absorption transition in the high and low energy bands. In the doped sample, the same ground state bleaching feature at approximately 390 nm is observed, along with an additional negative peak at 416 nm, as shown in Fig. 3b. While pumping with 340 nm is possible, it yields a different ground state bleaching feature of F4TCNQ only sample at around 600 nm (see Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Therefore, the ground state bleaching at 416 nm in the doped sample originates from the formation of the charge transfer state. Moreover, a pronounced positive photo-induced absorption peak is observed in the doped sample between 500 and 600 nm. While this peak overlaps with a weak, broad absorption band in the pristine sample probed by TAS, we attribute it to an electronic transition to a different charge transfer excited state, due to its distinct vibronic shape and different recombination kinetics, which will be discussed in the next paragraph.

We further analyze the kinetics of the ground state bleaching and photo-induced absorption features for both samples, as shown in Fig. 3c, d. The 3D maps of the TAS spectra are shown in Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16. In Fig. 3c, we overlay the kinetic curves of the 390 nm peak for both the pristine and doped samples. Noticeably, the decay curve of the pristine perovskite sample can be fitted by a double exponential function, suggesting two predominant recombination pathways for the excited state relaxation to the ground state. The pristine sample has a fast component with a time constant (τ1) of 9.01 ps followed by a slow decay with a time constant (τ2) of 416.67 ps. The decay curve of the doped sample can be fitted with the same function, yielding τ1 = 16.13 ps and τ2 = 303.03 ps for the fast and slow components, respectively. The presence of the dopant in the perovskite matrix noticeably extends the short time constant while shortening the long component. The short component τ1 is typically assigned to exciton diffusion74,75. Thus, in the presence of the charge-transfer state, the exciton diffusion lifetime is extended due to charge separation. The long component τ2 is often attributed to charge recombination. The reduction in τ2 in the doped sample can be understood as accelerated electron injection back into the perovskite’s valence band, which returns the system to the ground state more quickly74.

In Fig. 3d, we compare the kinetics of the new negative peak at 416 nm and the new positive peak at 510 nm with the ground state bleaching peak at 390 nm from the perovskite. The kinetics of the F4TCNQ’s ground state bleaching peak are plotted in the same graph when pumped by a 340 nm laser. All curves can be fitted with two decay constants, as detailed in Table 1.

Both 416 nm and 510 nm peaks have τ1 of 1.32 ps and 4.38 ps, respectively, followed by τ2 of 131.58 ps and 196.08 ps. In stark contrast, the fitted τ1 and τ2 of the F4TCNQ-only sample are extremely short, at 0.19 ps and 1.53 ps, respectively. The slower decay behavior of the doped sample replicates the kinetics of the perovskite host matrix, with lifetimes significantly shortened in the presence of the dopant. These results suggest that the new features at 416 nm and 510 nm do not represent the electronic transitions within the F4TCNQ molecule itself but are associated with charge transfer and recombination processes at the interface.

From the TAS data analysis, we conclude the charge transfer kinetics as follows. The chiral perovskite matrix is first pumped, and charge rapidly transfers from the excited state of the chiral matrix to the dopant at a fast rate, with 1/kCT < 1 ps. The new ground state bleaching and photo-induced absorption features correspond to electronic transitions to the charge-transfer state. This indicates a strong interaction between the donor (chiral perovskite, host) and the acceptor (F4TCNQ, guest) components.

Crystal structure and theory modeling

To gain insight into electronic features at the host-guest interface, we next perform quantum chemical calculations (see Supplementary Methods) using two model structural arrangements of the guest in the host matrix: In model-1, F4TCNQ is intercalated between the perovskite chains, while in model-2, F4TCNQ substitutionally replaces the A-site cation (R/S-MP cations) in the perovskite structure, as illustrated in the top panels of Fig. 4a, b, respectively. We compute the electronic structure of these systems, focusing on the appearance of charge transfer excitations.

Crucially, we account for the dielectric effects of the perovskite lattice using state-specific solvation models, which are known to significantly influence the transition energies of charge-transfer states, as detailed in “Methods” section. The resulting simulated absorption spectra (black curve) along with the contributing oscillator strengths (fON) of the two model composite systems, as well as isolated perovskite and F4TCNQ molecule, are plotted in the same graphs. Notably, Fig. 4a, b displays simulations results from both state-specific non-equilibrium (CT, non-eq) and equilibrium (CT, eq) solvation. The charge transfer peak in the computed absorption spectra exhibits a significant red-shift relative to the excitonic absorption at the perovskite band edge for both models (particularly for equilibrium solvation), in-line with experimental observations. Figure 4c, d displays the natural transition orbitals (NTOs) of the lowest excited state computed for models 1 and 2. NTOs decompose a given electronic excitation into its constituent electron and hole orbitals76. We observe the distinct spatial localization of electron (on the molecule) and hole (on the perovskite) orbitals in both models. This highlights that upon doping, the electron transfers from the perovskite to F4TCNQ owing the election deficiency of the latter. In the ground state our simulations indicate some partial electron transfer (~0.1e) supported by improved electrical conductivity measurements discussed later. Upon optical excitation, more electronic density is getting transferred to F4TCNQ (~1e) depending on a particular model structure. This confirms our assignment of the red-shifted optical features to charge-transfer states introduced by the dopant molecule.

Importantly, the degree of electron transfer and the resulting modifications of the absorption spectra significantly depend on the relative alignment of the perovskite and F4TCNQ. In model-2, when the F4TCNQ dopant substitutes for A-site cations, the hole wavefunction becomes spatially delocalized between the perovskite and the molecule, resulting in a notable overlap with the electron wavefunction. Consequently, we observe a 20-fold enhancement in oscillator strength in Fig. 4b, whereas in model-1, where the dopant is intercalated, the charge-transfer state is essentially optically dark, as shown in Fig. 4a. This can be explained by the enhanced electronic coupling when the dopant is closer to the perovskite lattice leading to improved overlap of the electron and hole wavefunctions (Fig. 4c, d). Thus, our quantum-chemical modeling suggests that a closely packed host-guest crystal structure will yield a bright charge transfer state with non-vanishing oscillation strength.

Experimentally, we conduct structure characterization to probe the arrangement of the guest-host thin film structures, with the results shown in Fig. 4e, f. Figure 4e displays the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for F4TCNQ, undoped chiral perovskites, and the host-guest thin film. In the doped perovskite sample, several peaks correspond to those of the pristine chiral perovskite XRD peaks, indicating the presence of the host crystalline structure. Additionally, two new peaks appear at the lower angle range that do not match either the host or guest crystal structures. These diffraction peaks correspond to a long-range ordering of the crystalline structure. To examine the low-angle peak more closely, we collect grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) to further map the new features (Fig. 4f) in 2D space. The full images of R-MP and S-MP chiral perovskites, along with those with dopants, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 17. In the pristine film, we identify the main (011) and (020) peaks at 0.68 Å−1 and 0.73 Å−1, respectively. Upon doping, both peaks are weakened and broadened (see GIWAXS linecut in Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating that the dopant introduces disorder into the perovskite matrix. Consistent with the XRD patterns, two new peaks emerge at 0.46 Å−1 and 0.9 Å−1, corresponding to a d-spacing of 13.78 Å and 6.98 Å, respectively. The ordered structures with d-spacings of 13.78 Å and 6.98 Å likely correspond to two F4TCNQ molecules (d = 7.3 Å) stacked with intercalation77 between two perovskite chains. The F4TCNQ molecules gradually fill the space between neighboring perovskite backbones, weakening the perovskite’s crystalline packing. It worth noting that the two new peaks in the doped samples are predominantly oriented along the out-of-plane directions, with an intense scattering signal along the qz direction. From the X-ray scattering analysis, we can conclude that F4TCNQ molecules do not reside outside of the crystalline perovskite domains, but rather diffuse into the lattice, forming a closely packed supermolecular structure. This structure aligns with the scenario described in model-2, where F4TCNQ is in a close contact with the PbI6 backbone, thereby introducing optically active charge transfer states.

Photodetector demonstrations

Finally, we demonstrate a photodetector made with the guest-host chiral perovskite thin film for the direct detection of circularly polarized light. The device structure and testing conditions are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 19. Figure 5a shows the photocurrent changes with quarter-wave plate rotation angle respect to the linear polarizer’s direction. The incoming light is maintained at 45° angle with respect to normal incidence to eliminate the preferential scattering effect of linearly polarized light by the crystalline domains. The device made with R-MP-perovskite absorber shows stronger photocurrents when the incoming light is left-handed circularly polarized (quarter wave plate is at 45°, 225° to the linear polarizer’s axis), whereas the photocurrent produced by the right-handed circularly polarized light (when the quarter wave plate is at 135° and 315°) is weaker. In contrast, the perovskite made with S-MP ligands shows the opposite trend, producing lower photocurrent in response to right-handed circularly polarized light. To understand the effect of the dopant on the material’s conductivity change, we measure the temperature dependent current voltage curves of both devices in the dark. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. We observe that the room temperature dark conductivity of the doped film is improved by more than 2 orders of magnitude comparing to that of without dopant. Figure 5c displays the extracted dark current against 1/T. In both devices, the dark current grows exponentially with temperature, suggesting a thermally activated transport mechanism attributed to the charge delocalization across conducting sites. The thermal activation energy can determined by the Arrhenius equation. The device with 1% F4TCNQ dopant has a lower activation energy (EA = 350 meV) compared to the undoped device (EA = 480 meV). The F4TCNQ creates a conducting channel that facilitates electron hopping through the chiral perovskite matrix, thereby lowering the energy barrier. With a lower energy barrier for carrier conduction, there is a higher chance of extracting the charge carriers from the material.

Next, we compare the performances of the pristine and doped by changing the absorber’s thickness. Supplementary Figs. 21–26 present the photocurrent data collected from each device. We extract the device’s responsivity (R in A W−1) as a function of chiral perovskite’s thicknesses and plot them in Fig. 5d, e, and Supplementary Fig 27 shows the device’s external quantum efficiency. When illuminated by a blue light source, the undoped device shows a rapid raise in its R when the film thickness increases from about 30 nm to 380 nm, due to improved photo absorption. However, its R begins to decline with further increases in thickness over 400 nm owing to poor conductivity. In contrast, the R of the doped device continuously increases with thickness and levels off around 800 nm, aided by the improved conductivity. Figure 5e illustrates the R when illuminated by a red laser, which excites the new charge transfer absorption feature. This trend mirrors that observed with the blue laser: the R signal increases with thickness benefitted from the higher electrical conductivity from doping.

Note that the photo-detectors’ responsivity and external quantum efficiencies are relatively low in our work, which can be attributed to the unoptimized device structure and testing conditions. The interfaces are not optimized for zero bias operation, there are limited charge collection without an internal electrical field. In addition, the detectors are tested under unbiased conditions to avoid large current injection through the device. At a higher bias, the dark baseline is increased significantly that diminish the difference in the photocurrent induced by the polarization of the light.

Figure 5f–h shows the photocurrent’s g factor (gph) as a function of layer thickness. Here, we notice that the gph changes with the absorber thickness. Interestingly, it changes in accordance with the photocurrent responsivity with thickness. In the pristine sample, the gph first increases with thickness from 50 to 380 nm. After 400 nm, the gph starts to decay with higher thickness. According to the temperature-dependent charge transport study presented in our Fig. 5c, the charge conduction in the chiral perovskite polycrystalline thin film is dominated by the electron delocalization across disconnected sites. Increasing the thickness of the material will hinder the charge transfer efficiency which thus introduce decoherence of the spin-polarized electron. Therefore, at a higher thickness, gph decays because of a weakened circularly polarized photogalvanic (CPGE) effect78,79. In contrast, with F4TCNQ dopant, the conduction barrier is reduced that facilitates the charge transport to the electrodes. As a result, the gphcan be maintained when the thickness increases beyond the 400 nm range.

The thickness dependence can be understood as the balance of the selective absorption and charge scattering. The increase of gph is likely from a stronger absorption and CD that improve the photocurrent selectivity. Another important contributor of the gph is the coherent transport of the spin polarized carriers. Since the polarization induced photocurrent (or CPGE) is dependent on the sign of the spin-polarized electrons, the flipping of the spin will degrade the signal of the photocurrent.

Discussion

Through our study, we notice a few points that worth further discussion and investigation. Firstly, the charge transfer state typically typically exhibit weak oscillator strengths when electron and hole wavefunctions have minimal spatial overlap. However, we do observe stronger CD in the doped sample. Here, we attribute the stronger CD could be due to strong electronic coupling leading to orbital hybridization between the F4TCNQ dopant and the stronger oscillator strength due to the closely interacting molecules.

While absorption is related to the overlap of the wavefunction of two states described by the oscillator strength, and CD is the overlap of electrical dipole (d) and magnetic dipole (m) of the two states described by the rotational strength (R). R can be calculated by:

When the electrical dipole is weak, the oscillator strength is small, and R should thus be small. However, the coupling between the two dipoles, as well as the strength of the magnetic dipole in certain cases, could also play a role that can enhance R. In chiral systems such as chiral organic molecules and metal-organic hybrid structures, the magnetic dipole and its direction can be affected by spin orbital coupling, angular momentum mixing.

In our case, the charge transfer state appears due to the coupling between the ground state of the chiral perovskite (I-5p orbital) and the F4TCNQ’s pi orbital. Since the two orbitals are dissimilar, it results in a weak fON. However, such interfacial asymmetry can introduce spin-orbital coupling with angular momentum mixing. The spin-orbital coupling can enhance the magnetic dipole, subsequently increasing R.

Another possibility is tied to the stronger electronic coupling of the dopant to the perovskite. Computational results shown in Fig. 4 compare computed absorption spectrum when the F4TCNQ dopant is either intercalated or substitutionally replaces an A-site cation. We find that when the dopant occupies an A-site cation space, there is electronic coupling (and orbital overlap) between the dopant and perovskite, which introduces brighter than anticipated oscillator strength to the charge-transfer excitation. As shown in Fig. 4 when F4TCNQ substitutes an A-site cation, this coupling enhances delocalization and increases the oscillator strength of the charge transfer-related transitions.

We also notice that the g factor from the photocurrent selectivity (gph ~ 0.2) is significantly higher than the g factor from the CD (gCD):

Where θ is CD in mdeg, A is the absorbance of the material. The CD of our material at the band edge (λ=390nm) can be thus estimated to be ~10−4.

Significantly higher gph than gCD has been observed in chiral perovskite photo detector reports30. The significantly higher g-factor in the photocurrent measurement can come from a few origins. Firstly, the g-factor of the photocurrent can be comprised with CD (difference in the absorption) that results in a change in the charge carrier density. In this case, the g-factor of the device should be comparable to the gCD. Next, the gph can originate from the CPGE78–80. CPGE refers to a DC current driven by an inhomogeneous population of spin-polarized carriers in the momentum space, which can be significant in a non-centrosymmetric crystal structure. When circularly polarized light excites charge carriers, due to selection rule, spin-polarized charge carriers will be generated. In chiral perovskite, the broken mirror symmetry will allocate the spin-polarized energy bands at different locations in the momentum space. This causes a spin-momentum locking effect in the material. This means electron with a specific spin polarization will flow through certain directions depending on the sign of the spin. In this case, the gph will have an additional contribution from the spin-polarized carrier population at different crystal sites.

We collect the powder XRD pattern of the 1% F4TCNQ doped R-MP perovskite polycrystalline powder that is shown in Fig. S28. A crystal structure retrieve was performed by comparing the doped powder with the XRD calculated from the CIF file. However, the experimental XRD pattern did not yield useful information regarding the molecular configuration of the dopant in the chiral perovskite matrix. To further validate model-2, where F4TCNQ molecule replaces one of the organics, one needs an atomistic characterization tool. While the atomistic models of F4TCNQ interaction with the perovskite lattice are not directly derived from experimental XRD data, we sample plausible dopant configurations. Notably, the substitutional A-site replacement model (model-2) yields a 20-fold increase in oscillator strength compared to the intercalated model (model-1), aligning with experimental observations of bright charge-transfer features in absorption spectra.

Our joined experimental and theory analysis clearly demonstrates that doping chiral perovskites with F4TCNQ molecules introduces charge-transfer states, which adds visible light absorption ability. Remarkably, these new absorption features inherit chiral properties from the parent system. We attribute observed the chirality transfer to two possible origins. First, it may be caused by electron transfer between the donor and acceptor. According to our model-2, the electron and hole orbitals are localized at the perovskite chain and F4TCNQ, respectively, forming the charge transfer excited state. Since the transition dipoles of the excited states align with the chiral arrangement of the perovskite lattice due to the distribution of the hole orbital, the resulting charge-transfer states also become sensitive to circularly polarized light. Second, chirality can be transferred through crystal packing between the donor and acceptor. From our X-ray scattering analysis, F4TCNQ molecules intercalate between the perovskite chains, forming a superlattice. In this case, chiral perovskite serves as a template, allowing F4TCNQ to grow with its chiral structure. As a result, the new charge transfer absorbing states adopt the chiral arrangement, delivering noticeable circular dichroism at the visible absorption range. Altogether, our work addresses the lack of visible light response in low-dimensional chiral perovskites through molecular doping. The chirality transfer via electronic coupling between guest and host structure represents a general mechanism applicable to other chiral semiconductors. Furthermore, molecular doping in low-dimensional chiral perovskites offers practical strategies to improve electrical conductivity, creating opportunities for photodetectors sensitive to circularly polarized light and other optoelectronic applications.

Methods

Materials

Lead (II) iodide (99%), hydriodic acid (≥57.0 wt%), hypophosphorous acid (50 wt % in H₂O), dichloromethane, and (S)-(−)-1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinemethanol were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. (R)-1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinemethanol was procured from Matrix Scientific and used as received.

Synthesis of (R/S-MP)₂PbI4

Lead (II) iodide powder (0.922 g, 2 mmol) was dissolved in 4 ml of 57% aqueous hydriodic acid and 1.0 ml of 50 w.t.% hypophosphorous acid. The mixture was heated with stirring at 130 °C for 10 min until a clear solution was obtained. Subsequently, (R/S)-1-methyl-2-pyrrolidinemethanol (0.230 g, 2 mmol) was slowly added to the solution under continuous stirring. During the addition of the chiral compound, yellow crystals immediately precipitated. These crystals were then dissolved in Dimethylformamide at a concentration of 3 mg ml−1 and recrystallized using 15 ml dichloromethane at room temperature by slow diffusion.

Thin film fabrication

To fabricate thin films, we dissolve the recrystallized perovskite crystal powder in DMF solvent at a concentration of 50 mg ml−1. The dopant is added at a weight percentage of 1% relative to the weight of the (R/S-MP)₂PbI4. The obtained films are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the pristine films are pale yellow, whereas the doped films exhibit green color.

GIWAXS of thin films

GIWAXS measurements were conducted at beamline 17 A of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center (NSRRC) in Taiwan, utilizing a wavelength of 1.321184576 Å. Standard GIWAXS patterns were obtained with an incident angle of 0.14°.

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy characterization

Our ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy experiments were carried out in an ultrahigh vacuum system with base pressures <2 × 10−9 Torr equipped a hemispherical electron energy analyzer (SPECS, PHOIBOS 100) and ultraviolet source (SPECS, 10/35). He(I) at 21.2 eV radiation was used. The angle between the analyzer and UV- source is 45°, and photoelectrons were collected along the surface normal direction of the sample. The Fermi level of the spectra was calibrated vs the Fermi edge of Au(111) crystal.

Single crystal analylsis

The datum crystal (0.19 × 0.155 × 0.136 mm3) was affixed to a MiTeGen microloop with polybutene oil. Diffracted X-ray intensities were measured with a Rigaku XtaLab Synergy-S diffractometer using a PhotonJet-S microfocus X-ray source with Ag target (Ag Kα λ = 0.56087 Å) and HyPix-6000HE hybrid photon counting detector. An Oxford 1000 cryostream was used to maintain the sample at 290.0(1) K. A series of ω scans was used to cover reciprocal space. Data reduction was performed using CrysAlisPro81. Structure solution was performed via intrinsic phasing with ShelXT82 and full-matrix least-squares refinement with ShelXL83 using Olex284. R-MP perovskite crystallized in the chiral space group P212121 (space group 19), a Sohncke space group. Space group assignment was based on systematic absences, E2 statistics, and the successful refinement of the structure. Data collection, structure solution, and refinement were challenging and are outlined below.

Broadband transient absorption measurements

Broadband pump-probe transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) measurements were performed using a Newport Spectra Physics Spitfire/Topas One laser amplifier/optical parametric amplifier system (1 kHz repetition rate, 120 fs pulse width) as probe/pump sources and a Helios Ultrafast Systems transient absorption spectrometer with the supercontinuum white light generated by a sapphire crystal. TAS spectra were acquired a low pump power (50-100 µW average pump power) in order to avoid sample damage.
