Pharmacological advances in Candida auris: emerging antifungal mechanisms and next-generation therapeutic strategies
Authors: Shuaibu Abdullahi Hudu, Najlaa Saadi, Emad A Morad, Albashir Tahir, Abdulgafar Olayiwola Jimoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida auris is a major public health concern worldwide due to its efficient transmission, environmental persistence, and broad resistance to approved antifungal classes. This review consolidates recent pharmacological developments in this regard, focusing on mechanistic insights and late-stage therapeutics. Novel agents demonstrate activity against multidrug- and pan-resistant isolates via distinct mechanisms of action and enhanced specific binding to CYP51. Repositioned drugs, host-defense peptides, and quorum-sensing modulators also expand the treatable spectrum, particularly for biofilm-associated and device-related infections. Concurrently, artificial intelligence (AI)-guided discovery, nanocarrier-enabled delivery, and multi-omics profiling are greatly accelerating target identification and enhancing the drug metabolism of small-molecule fragments. The emerging combined approaches mark a transition towards mechanism-based antifungal development to combat the increasing clinical burden posed by C. auris. Ongoing integration of precision diagnostics, pharmacodynamic optimization, and novel discovery platforms will be key to translating these advances into durable, real-world therapeutic solutions.Keywords: Antifungal therapies, biofilm formation, Candida auris, emerging pathogens, multidrug resistance

Introduction

Candidiasis is an opportunistic fungal infection caused by Candida species that normally colonise human microbiota but can cause invasive disease when host immunity is compromised. Among these are Candida auris, an especially worrying pathogen driven by its fast nosocomial spread, environmental persistence, and broad antifungal resistance. C. auris poses serious challenges for infection management, with international health implications; first reported in Japan in 2009, C. auris has spread worldwide and is now considered an important healthcare-associated pathogen1. Diagnosis represents another significant impediment to the early institution of clinical interventions. Although culture is the primary tool for identifying the fungus, C. auris’s phenotypic similarity to Candida haemulonii and related species leads to frequent misidentification2. In this respect, PCR-based or MALDI-TOF MS-based confirmation methods are needed for its accurate detection3. More than 30 countries have already registered cases of the C. auris infection. As a result, the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention has identified the fungus as an urgent threat4–7. It is on the list of obligatory notifications in some states.

Various studies have indicated that global C. auris isolates can be grouped into five or more genetically distinct clades: South Asian, East Asian, South African, South American, and Iranian, each with thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms apart and uniquely susceptible to antifungal drugs8–11. Although conventional antifungal drugs have long been the cornerstone of pharmacological therapy, their efficacy has been heavily undermined by resistance-linked mutations, particularly in ERG11, FKS1, and efflux pump regulators. These limitations have been further amplified by practically untreatable, biofilm-forming capacities on indwelling devices, contributing to high morbidity in life-threateningly ill people. The COVID-19 outbreak worsened the situation by increasing ICU occupancy, overwhelming infection screening capacity, and delaying laboratory confirmation of cases, leading to record C. auris outbreaks in several locations12.

C. auris poses a novel therapeutic challenge because it exhibits multiple-drug resistance, rapid healthcare-associated transmission, and long-lived environmental reservoirs13. Thus, better diagnostics, novel antifungal drugs with unique modes of action, and new drug discovery platforms are needed to combat C. auris, which is the focus of our lab. Consequently, this review focuses solely on pharmacologic advancements in antifungal and fungicidal drug discovery targeting C. auris, the importance of novel therapeutics, mechanistic insights into activity, and next-generation discovery technologies that have the potential to alter current paradigms for antifungal infection management14.

Clinical spectrum and epidemiological trends

C. auris appears as a wide and emerging spectrum of clinical presentations, including superficial colonisation to critical invasive infection. While the most frequently described presentation remains candidemia, particularly in critically ill and immunocompromised patients with central venous catheters, mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy, or recent receipt of broad-spectrum antibiotics15, an ever-growing number of clinical syndromes continue to be reported. These are otomycosis wound infection, urinary tract infection, osteomyelitis16, otomastoiditis17, vulvovaginitis18, endocarditis19, candiduria20, spondylodiscitis21, panophthalmitis22, and central nervous system23, abdominal infections, including the soft tissue and respiratory tract infections24. That diversity is probably indicative of the organism’s capacity to adapt and successfully withstand evolutionary pressure across a variety of host niches and environments where other Candida species do not fare as well.

A hallmark feature of C. auris is its ability to spread rapidly within hospitals. In contrast to most Candida species, which are usually endogenous pathogens, C. auris can disseminate between patients and persistently contaminate healthcare environments25. The organism has a broad tolerance to disinfectants, including quaternary ammonium compounds, and this, combined with its potential for long-term persistence on plastics, bed rails, monitors, and shared medical devices26,27. High-burden skin-site colonisation, especially in the axilla, groyne, and nares, enables prolonged shedding and contributes to networks that underlie silent transmission chains of disease, making outbreak control difficult15. Asymptomatic carriage is common and often precedes invasive disease; these findings emphasise the value of active surveillance cultures in high-risk areas.

Genomic studies have revealed important insights into the epidemiology of C. auris. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) has demonstrated that strains isolated worldwide cluster into five genetically distinct clades: South Asian, East Asian, South African, South American, and a recently reported Iranian clade, each separated by thousands of single-nucleotide polymorphisms and characterised by specific antifungal susceptibility profiles28. These clades exhibit high geographic correlation, indicating endogenous, rather than pan-continental emergence. Healthcare systems with low laboratory capacity often suffer from delayed or incomplete detection, adding to the rates of multi-drug resistant (MDR) or pan-drug-resistant (PDR) isolates30. On the other hand, UK authorities also report that local transmission of imported cases within the region led to repeated introductions of infection and were not prevented in several areas31. In contrast, regions that implemented genomic surveillance early on, such as parts of Europe, have been more successful at containing outbreaks with repeated introductions by infected travellers31. The interaction among diagnostic capacity, infection control procedures, and appropriate antibiotic use shapes local epidemiology.

The COVID-19 pandemic also facilitated the rapid dissemination of C. auris and heightened susceptibility in ICUs worldwide. Crowding, widespread use of invasive devices, corticosteroid therapy, failure to maintain routine surveillance, and shortages of personal protective equipment have each contributed to prolonged outbreaks in some areas32,33. The focus on SARS-CoV-2 diagnostics and the suppression of routine fungal identification delayed recognition of C. auris, resulting in extensive silent transmission and increased outbreak complexity34. On balance, the clinical and epidemiological profile of this organism is that of a highly adaptable, multidrug-resistant pathogen with an extraordinary potential for rapid spread under healthcare pressures. Its worldwide distribution, variable resistance patterns, and preference for high-acuity settings make it a candidate for integrated surveillance, rapid diagnostics, and context-specific treatment plans.

Therapeutic and diagnostic challenges

Management of Candida auris infections remains challenging due to extensive antifungal resistance, diagnostic limitations, and the organism’s ability to persist in healthcare environments. Delayed identification and limited access to rapid susceptibility testing often hinder early recognition of C. auris infections, thereby delaying appropriate antifungal therapy and infection-control interventions. A unique feature of C. auris is its widespread resistance to all three major antifungal classes. Fluconazole resistance exceeds 90% in some areas and is mostly due to ERG11 mutations and efflux pump overexpression, severely limiting the potential of azoles as empirical therapy35. Resistance to amphotericin B, though geographically heterogeneous, is increasingly reported and results from changes in ergosterol biosynthesis that reduce drug binding35. Echinocandins, generally advocated as first-line treatment for invasive disease, are under threat from FKS1 hot-spot mutations that confer decreased susceptibility or an adverse clinical response, and some countries have reported the emergence of multidrug- or pan-resistant isolates36,37. These changing resistance patterns provide little guidance for clinicians, and poor outcomes may occur despite early/guideline-concordant therapy.

There are also pharmacologic limitations that limit therapeutic options. While Amphotericin B is an essential component for CNS or deep-seated involvement, its use is limited by nephrotoxicity and electrolyte abnormalities, especially in the setting of critical illness. Triazoles are known to have a large potential for drug–drug interactions, which can be problematic in an ICU population using multiple medications38,39. Although echinocandins are relatively safe, they achieve poor concentrations in some sanctuary sites and do not penetrate well into biofilm-embedded organisms. The naturally strong biofilm-forming ability of C. auris further complicates therapeutic failure, as biofilm-associated cells are highly tolerant of antifungal concentrations that would otherwise be effective27,40. Many pharmacologic and biological limitations could explain this, underscoring the need for novel agents with new mechanisms of action, improved tissue penetration, and antibiofilm capabilities.

Diagnostic ambiguities are also a key factor in the maintenance and transmission of C. auris. Traditional phenotypic systems, such as VITEK 2, MicroScan, and API 20 C AUX methods, frequently misidentify C. auris as C. haemulonii or related species, especially when outdated software libraries are used41. Misidentification of the organism delays the implementation of infection-control measures and results in unnecessary empiric treatment. Despite the accuracy of MALDI-TOF MS and PCR-based assays, their availability also varies worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income regions, where outbreaks of C. auris can remain undetected until late stages42. Further, the absence of clinical breakpoints for C. auris hinders susceptibility testing and interpretation, with laboratories using provisional epidemiological cut-off values that vary by geographical location43,44. This heterogeneity affects the potential to inform early, empirical treatment decisions.

Environmental detection poses additional challenges. C. auris can survive for weeks on dry hospital surfaces and is resistant to standard disinfectants, which may help contribute to the difficulties in controlling environmental reservoirs. Furthermore, the low-burden contaminating organisms may not be identified by culture-based environmental sampling, and sensitive-PCR assays continue to suffer from cost or might still not be affordable in many healthcare systems26. From a clinical standpoint, the non-specific nature of C. auris infections often mimics other Candida spp. manifestations, which may also hinder early clinical suspicion and, if confirmation at the species level is delayed, facilitate uncontrolled spread. Individually and together, the balance between antifungal resistance, pharmacologic constraints, diagnostic ambiguity, and environmental persistence poses a looming impediment to the successful management of C. auris. These comorbidities highlight the urgent need for greater accessibility to rapid molecular diagnostics, more robust susceptibility-testing algorithms, strategies to optimise therapy, and more effective infection-control measures to prevent further spiralling incidence of this extremely malleable pathogen.

Conventional and combination therapies

Therapeutic options for C. auris remain limited to the three main antifungal classes, namely echinocandins, azoles, and polyenes, despite escalating resistance levels that continue to compromise their efficacy. In this group, echinocandins remain the preferred choice for empiric treatment of invasive C. auris due to their fungicidal activity and good safety profile. Caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin are non-competitive inhibitors of β-1,3-D-glucan synthase, which disrupt the integrity of the fungal cell wall, resulting in cell lysis45. Figure 3 details the echinocandin class and the structures of these agents, thereby illustrating the most common structural scaffold and its importance in contemporary therapeutic practice. Despite the benefits of echinocandins, hot-spot mutations in FKS1 have emerged across multiple clades, reducing susceptibility and leading to documented clinical failures, highlighting the vulnerability of echinocandins as a therapeutic backbone for the management of C. auris45.

Azoles, particularly the second-generation triazoles voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazole, have traditionally played a central role in the treatment of candidemia; however, they are of limited clinical utility against C. auris. Fluconazole, however, is an unreliable therapy for empiric or definitive treatment because ERG11 mutations and efflux pump overexpression have led to resistance exceeding 90% in many areas46. Although some isolates are susceptible to extended-spectrum azoles, in practice, use is limited to combination therapy or to non-invasive infections with documented susceptibility. They have limited clinical value given extensive drug–drug interactions and critical illness-induced alterations in pharmacokinetics that render dosing optimisation in an intensive-care environment difficult.

Amphotericin B, especially in lipid complex form, is the most frequently used second-line or rescue therapy when suspected of echinocandin resistance or treatment failure. While amphotericin B remains active against many C. auris isolates, resistance via alterations in ergosterol biosynthesis has emerged in some clades47. Its nephrotoxicity, electrolyte disturbances, and infusion-related complications make its use difficult in critically ill patients as well. However, there is a need for antifungals with better tolerability and improved pharmacokinetic profiles than liposomal amphotericin B, particularly for infections of sanctuary sites (central nervous system) or when echinocandin penetration is poor.

With the lack of availability of curative monotherapy, combination antifungal strategies have become increasingly appealing as they would theoretically improve efficacy, retard the emergence of resistance, and improve outcomes in case of severe or refractory disease. Synergistic interactions between echinocandins and azoles in vitro may occur, particularly in isolates with partial resistance to either class. Flucytosine has also been tested as an additive/synergistic drug for use with amphotericin B or echinocandins, but it is not supported by extensive clinical trial data48. Among several repurposed agents demonstrated to be synergistic when combined with azoles in laboratory-based studies are lopinavir, suloctidil, co-trimoxazole, and aprepitant, raising the potential for adjunctive use against multidrug-resistant isolates49–51. Critically, some of these combinations present enhanced activity against biofilm-intercalated cells. In the context of C. auris itself, it has an intrinsic biofilm-forming potential, which correlates with persistent contamination of healthcare settings and recalcitrant infections52. Although there is evidence of laboratory efficacy, clinical implementation of combination chemotherapy is hindered by a paucity of strong, well-controlled studies, specifically assessing the safety of additional agents used in conjunction with fludarabine and response rates/long-term outcomes. For these reasons, treatment guidelines now emphasise personalised therapy based on susceptibility testing, the site of infection, the patient’s comorbidities, and clinical response to therapy. Where multidrug resistance is reported or monotherapy response is unsatisfactory, combination therapy may be an important short-term strategy until new antifungal agents become available.

Emerging antifungal therapies

The growing onslaught of multidrug- and pan-resistant C. auris has sparked aggressive worldwide efforts to broaden the antifungal arsenal beyond the limits of azoles, echinocandins, and polyenes. Novel by-developing pharmacotherapies focus on previously untargeted fungal pathways, resistance-defying strategies, and modern drug discovery platforms to speed their development. Fosmanogepix (APX001A) is one of the first-in-class antifungals with known activity against Gwt1, an essential enzyme in the GPI-anchor biosynthesis pathway. This disruption likely prevents cell-wall mannoproteins from being targeted appropriately, resulting in potent activity against C. auris, including isolates resistant to echinocandins and azoles54. Arylamidine T-2307 also displays selective mitochondrial inhibition, including loss of membrane potential and blockade of the respiratory chain, with activity against resistant isolates and mycobacterial biofilms across a wide range of TB strains71,72. Second-generation glucan synthase inhibitors are another important leap forward in therapy. Rezafungin, designed for improved stability and once-weekly administration, retains high fungicidal activity against C. auris, including isolates with decreased susceptibility to older echinocandins55. Ibrexafungerp (SCY-078), the first oral, bioavailable agent targeting the glucan synthase pathway, maintains stable activity at low pH and covers strains resistant to other agents56,57. In addition, the therapeutic options have been further expanded by new scaffold antifungals. The second-generation tetrazole VT-1598 exhibits increased selectivity for fungal CYP51 enzymes, reducing off-target toxicity and drug–drug interactions58,59. Developed for the inhalational route, PC945 (opelconazole) has a long pulmonary retention and potent Candida activity, including against C. auris, rendering it an interesting option in cases of airway colonisation or respiratory biofilm infection60–62. Repositioned agents, such as nitroxoline, also exhibit potent in vitro activity via metal-ion chelation and have good urine pharmacokinetic properties63,64.

The peptide and repurposed agents provided are still being added to the pipeline. The venom-derived peptide crotamine exhibits strong fungicidal and antibiofilm activity by disrupting membranes65,66. Ebselen is a sulphur metabolism blocker and has potent antibiofilm activity67. The quorum-sensing molecule farnesol downregulates efflux pumps and reverses azole resistance in resistant strains68,69. Histatin 5, despite stability issues, has been shown to have candidacidal activity against fluconazole-resistant C. auris70. Discovery platforms are engaging at a formidable pace. AI-driven molecular screening, nanocarriers for drug delivery, and multi-omics analytics offer promising approaches for discovering new antifungal scaffolds, improving drug targeting, and overcoming pharmacokinetic limitations73–75. Together, these methods signify a revolutionary redirection of precision-engineered antifungal therapies. Collectively, these advances represent a critical milestone in the treatment of C. auris and provide mechanistically diverse, technology-based options that far exceed the severe limitations imposed by existing antifungal drug classes.

Alternative and adjunctive therapeutic approaches

The drug resistance or difficulty in treating C. auris, especially in the context of multidrug-resistant or biofilm infections, has drawn growing attention to nonconventional strategies beyond classic antifungals. These strategies target a range of biological pathways, including quorum sensing, efflux pump regulation, mitochondrial function, membrane permeability, sulphur metabolism, and metal homeostasis. They could serve as an adjunct to existing pharmacotherapies when conventional treatments are ineffective. Numerous existing drugs have been repurposed with AZO for their synergism, warranting an assessment of such drug combinations. Suloctidil, co-trimoxazole, lopinavir, and aprepitant exhibit variable antifungal or antibiofilm activity and, importantly, potentiate the action of fluconazole or other azoles against resistant isolates49,52. Their different modes of action, ranging from membrane lysis to protease blockade, indicate the potential of host-interfering or stress-responsive modulators, in addition to chemotherapy, for recalcitrant cases. Inhibition of quorum sensing is another hopeful adjunctive approach. Farnesol, a quorum-sensing molecule secreted by Candida albicans, effectively attenuates efflux pump expression and virulence pathways in C. auris, thereby restoring susceptibility to azole agents and reducing biofilm formation70. Anti-virulence therapies such as these may benefit from improving antifungal activity without exerting direct selection pressure for resistance.

Peptide antibiotics remain promising agents because of their broad-spectrum activity and ability to penetrate biofilms. The honeypot-based venom peptide crotamine has demonstrated high in vitro fungicidal and antibiofilm activities, including against planktonic cells65. Histatin 5, a salivary antimicrobial peptide, kills even fluconazole-resistant isolates with potent activity70; it does so by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP efflux. Peptide therapeutics offer promise as an investigational area, though achieving stability and systemic delivery remains difficult. Metabolic and stress-response dysregulators offer additional therapeutic opportunities. The organoselenium compound ebselen acts on sulphur metabolism and has potent antibiofilm activity67. Nitroxoline is also particularly active against urinary isolates, and it binds essential metal ions of homeostasis sensu lato, including hypochrom ergosterol63,64. These agents are limited but particularly attractive for persistent or biofilm-related infections for which traditional antifungals have limited efficacy.

Taken together, these alternative and adjuvant agents emphasise a trend towards multiside strategies that can target the various survival mechanisms of C. auris. Focusing on pathways such as biofilm formation, mitochondrial function, control of efflux pump expression, quorum sensing, and the metabolic stress response may help modify (make more efficient) classical antifungal therapy. While the majority are currently in preclinical development, their substantial mechanistic diversity and potential for synergy underscore their growing importance in the rapidly expanding therapeutic arsenal against this versatile pathogen.

Emerging discovery and delivery technologies

Rising concern over multidrug-resistant Candida auris has heightened focus on cutting-edge research technologies to reinvigorate antifungal discovery by increasing the number of novel, well-validated targets and potent lead compounds for therapeutic development. Meanwhile, traditional methods for discovering new antifungals have lost the battle against this continually evolving organism’s resistance. Accordingly, next-generation platforms (AI-guided drug discovery, nanocarrier-assisted delivery systems, and multi-omics–driven molecular profiling) became instrumental in accelerating innovation in antifungal pharmacology76. These innovations offer unique opportunities to optimise early-stage discovery, circumvent pharmacokinetic barriers, and develop precision-targeted interventions that reflect the biological complexity of C. auris.

AI-assisted drug discovery is transforming the early stages of antifungal development, enabling rapid computational screening of large numbers of compounds and predictive modelling of those with high specificity for fungal targets. Computational techniques, such as machine-learning algorithms, may be applied to large genomic, structural, and phenotypic datasets to identify new fungal vulnerabilities, predict resistance-causing mutations, and/or optimise lead molecules for binding, solubility, and selectivity73,77. Most importantly, AI-empowered analysis has demonstrated value in the discovery of non-conventional antifungal skeletons, synergy drug combinations, and the prediction of evolutionary trajectories that dictate the future behaviour of a given drug. These are particularly important given the genomic diversity and clade-specific resistance patterns observed in C. auris, which challenge standard screening approaches. With the rapid pace of hit-to-lead optimisation and reduced resource requirements, we foresee AI as an integral part of contemporary antifungal development pipelines.

Another new drug discovery strategy for novel antifungal agents is the use of high-throughput screening platforms, such as phage display technologies78. Peptide or small-molecule libraries can be screened using phage display, which can identify ligands targeting a wide range of fungal virulence factors, biofilm-associated proteins, and essential metabolic pathways78. It has been successfully applied to identify antimicrobial peptides and enzyme inhibitors with potential antifungal activity. Phage-display assisted screening has recently been shown to both accelerate lead compound identification and improve target specificity in antifungal drug discovery pipelines. This high-throughput approach may prove a powerful complement to artificial-intelligence-driven drug design, allowing for pharmacological validation of predicted targets and increasing the diversity of antifungal candidates.

Concurrently, further progress in nanotechnology has opened therapeutic avenues to optimise antifungal delivery and overcome still-existing challenges, including poor solubility, rapid clearance, dose-limited toxicity, and insufficient penetration into biofilms or deep body tissues. Nanocarriers, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, dendrimers, and solid lipid nanoparticles, permit the encapsulation of antifungal agents to improve their pharmacokinetics and sustain drug release, as well as to promote more effective drug penetration into biofilm-encapsulated C. auris cells79. Nanocarriers may be specifically designed to exploit fungal-specific mechanisms (metabolism, pH gradients, or cell wall components) to achieve elevated drug concentrations at the target site and reduced systemic exposure and toxicity80. Encapsulation techniques also offer the possibility of reviving old antifungal drugs that were once ineffective by reusing them against resistant isolates81. Importantly, the development of inhalable antifungal nanocarriers for the control of pulmonary colonisation or co-infection in immunosuppressed hosts holds promise82.

In conjunction with AI and nanotechnology, multi-omics technologies are reshaping drug discovery by shedding light on the molecular circuitry hijacked by C. auris to mediate survival, virulence, and resistance. Genome-wide comparative analysis across different clades of C. auris has identified major virulence determinants, efflux pump networks, stress-response pathways, and metabolic adaptations that contribute to drug resistance and environmental persistence83. Proteome analyses identified membrane-associated proteins, biofilm-specific enzymes, and mitochondrial regulators that may be potential drug targets84. Chemical-genetic interactions, Mammalian-based approaches are complemented by metabolomics and lipidomics, providing more detailed ‘maps’ of changes in ergosterol biosynthesis, lipid homeostasis, and oxidative-stress metabolism that delineate separate resistance mechanisms from sensitive isolates85. These rich multi-omics data sets are combined to inform a systems-biology approach that can reveal key pathways, prioritise druggable nodes, and intersect with structure-based antifungal design.

As a whole, AI-based analytics, nanocarrier-mediated drug delivery systems, and omics-guided target identification are the three frontiers of antifungal technology. These technologies not only drive the discovery of new agents but are also essential to precision medicine in overcoming intrinsic and acquired resistance. As the worldwide prevalence of C. auris continues to increase, the pragmatic inclusion of these platforms in antifungal discovery pipelines will be essential for informed, effective, and long-lasting therapy. If used in concert, these new methods may enable accelerated development, greater drug specificity, and personalised antifungal therapy that aligns with a patient’s pathogen genotype, resistance phenotype, and biological niche of infection.

Expert opinion and future perspectives

The ongoing global spread of the pathogen Candida auris further underscores the need for continued work on improved antifungal pharmacology, precision diagnostics, and harmonised surveillance platforms. The ability to rapidly spread and persist in healthcare environments, with subsequent development of multidrug resistance, is a characteristic that makes C. auris unique among pathogenic yeasts, necessitating an overarching, mechanism-based approach. The recent advances in the discovery of antifungal agents, including fosmanogepix, ibrexafungerp, and rezafungin, have been a significant trend towards novel antifungal agents with new targets, better pharmacokinetics, and activity against resistant isolates. Nevertheless, translating these successes into clinical practice will depend on global availability, cost-effective pricing, and the drug-altered PD effect and polycomorbidity in critically ill patients. Combinations will be more important, especially for severe infections, biofilm-related diseases, and when resistance mutations blunt the effect of monotherapy. However, data from well-conducted clinical trials are scarce, and identifying optimal drug combinations, dosing schedules, and treatment windows will be required for combination treatments to become standard of care. Similarly, combining pharmacogenomic and pharmacodynamic modelling should improve personalised medicine by minimising toxicity and maximising target coverage across a range of clinical situations. Diagnostic creativity continues to serve as an anchor for future control initiatives. Rapid molecular assays, revised MALDI-TOF MS libraries, and point-of-care sequencers have the potential to narrow the current diagnostic gap, thereby preventing silent transmission and delaying treatment. But disparities in diagnostic capability across the globe are exacerbating the global burden. To facilitate early detection of clade introductions, outbreaks, and emerging resistance patterns, achieving equitable access to more advanced identification systems and establishing interoperable surveillance networks will be crucial. Environmental detection techniques also need further development to consistently detect low-level contamination and enable accurate decontamination efforts.

In the future, novel discovery platforms, such as AI-driven drug design, nanocarrier-based delivery systems, and multi-omics approaches, will certainly reshape antifungal development. AI may ultimately allow us to predict resistance trajectories and discover new vulnerabilities; nanotechnology may solve age-old problems of drug penetrance, stability, and targeting. Omics-integrated strategies should reveal previously unidentified pathways related to virulence, metabolic processes, and stress responses by precisely targeting strain-specific vulnerabilities. However, C. auris will continue to pose a formidable threat without commensurate investment in infection control, antimicrobial stewardship, and strengthening global health systems. Efficient decontamination, wider screening for high-risk patients, and genomic surveillance can be an obligatory part of health-state preparedness. If these priorities can be met, the next decade could see a new era of precision-guided, proactive control of C. auris and other multidrug-resistant fungal pathogens, rather than a reactive response.

Conclusion

Recent advances in antifungal pharmacology and discovery technologies are rapidly transforming the therapeutic landscape for Candida auris. Advances in our understanding of fungal biology, including cell wall synthesis, mitochondrial function, biofilm-associated resistance, and stress-response pathways, have identified multiple molecular targets that can be exploited to design next-generation antifungals. At the same time, computational and systems-based strategies are enabling and accelerating drug discovery and allowing the identification of novel therapeutic scaffolds with improved specificity and safety profiles.
