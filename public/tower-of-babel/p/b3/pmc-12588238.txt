Optimal Sunitinib Dosing Based on Platelet Count in an Older Patient With Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor
Authors: HIROTAKA SUTO, MIYUKI KAWAMURA, MITSUNORI MORITA, HIDEKI SAKAI, TAKUMA ONOE, KYOKO IKEUCHI, KOJI MATSUMOTO
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimSunitinib, a second-line treatment for gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), is commonly associated with adverse events (AEs) such as myelosuppression in older patients. However, an optimal dosing schedule has not been established. This study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and toxicity management of sunitinib administration based on platelet count.Case ReportAn 83-year-old man receiving imatinib for rectal GISTs was switched to sunitinib (initially 50 mg/day) due to imatinib failure. After eight days, he developed grade 2 anorexia and nausea, prompting a dose reduction to 37.5 mg/day. These AEs persisted, and sunitinib was discontinued 15 days after treatment initiation and later resumed at 25 mg/day after the symptoms improved. Thereafter, the sunitinib withdrawal period was adjusted based on neutrophil and platelet counts. As a result, the only non-hematologic AE of grade 2 or higher was hypothyroidism, with no serious AEs. He achieved a partial response according to Choi criteria but was switched to regorafenib after five cycles of sunitinib due to disease progression. Changes in neutrophil and platelet counts during the withdrawal period of each cycle of sunitinib therapy were predicted using a quadratic regression model and validated using leave-one-out cross-validation. The coefficient of determination (R²) of the neutrophil count prediction model during the withdrawal period was 0.28, and the validated R² for each cycle ranged from -26.34 to -0.11. In contrast, the platelet count model yielded an R² value of 0.86, with validated R² values ranging from 0.42 to 0.88.ConclusionLow-dose sunitinib monotherapy, with dosing intervals based on platelet counts, may be an effective treatment option for managing toxicity in older patients with imatinib-resistant GIST.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) are neoplasms that originate predominantly from the interstitial cells of Cajal within the gastrointestinal tract. The most common driver mutations are KIT (60%-75%) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor α (PDGFRA) (10-15%) (1-3). Imatinib is the recommended first-line or adjuvant therapy for GISTs (4-6). However, secondary mutations, such as V654A and T670I in KIT exons 13/14 and Y823D in exon 17, result in imatinib-resistant GIST and require a change in therapy (7-9). Sunitinib is currently the recommended treatment for imatinib-refractory GIST (10-13), but adverse events (AEs) such as hand-foot syndrome (HFS) and myelosuppression often interfere with treatment (12,14-16). Furthermore, there are reports that the frequency and severity of sunitinib-induced AEs vary widely among multiple cancer types, including GIST, depending on race and age (14,17-20). Therefore, sunitinib dosing schedules have been arranged in various ways (21-24). However, an optimal sunitinib dosing schedule for older patients with GISTs has not yet been established. Here we present a case study of an older patient with GIST who was successfully treated with a platelet count-based sunitinib dosing schedule for toxicity management and disease control (Figure 1).

Case Report

An 83-year-old man was diagnosed with an unresectable rectal GIST (exon 11 W557_K558 deletion) a year earlier in 2023, and treatment with Imatinib was initiated in late October that year. The rectal GIST continued to shrink until June 2024. Details of the diagnosis and initial treatment have been previously reported by Suto et al. (25). However, a computed tomography (CT) in September 2024 showed an increase in the rectal tumor. As shown in Table I, his organ function was well maintained, with only grade 1 renal dysfunction and anemia. Therefore, sunitinib 50 mg/day was started in October 2024 for imatinib-resistant rectal GIST. After eight days of treatment, the patient developed grade 2 anorexia and nausea, prompting a dose reduction to 37.5 mg/day. However, these AEs persisted. Sunitinib was discontinued 15 days after treatment initiation and was resumed at 25 mg/day following symptoms improved. As hypothyroidism was the only non-hematologic toxicity of grade 2 or higher observed after the second cycle, treatment was continued with sunitinib withdrawal periods adjusted based on neutrophil and platelet counts. On CT in December 2024, the efficacy of sunitinib in rectal GISTs was evaluated as stable disease according to RECIST version 1.1, but as partial response according to Choi criteria (26). However, after five cycles of sunitinib monotherapy, a CT scan in late February 2025 indicated disease progression according to RECIST version 1.1 (Figure 2). Therefore, the patient was switched to regorafenib monotherapy as third-line chemotherapy in March 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this manuscript.

Discussion

Herein we present a case of an older patient with GIST whose sunitinib-related toxicity was managed using a dosing schedule based on platelet counts. Sunitinib monotherapy is recommended as second-line therapy for patients with imatinib-resistant GIST (27-29). In previous clinical studies comparing sunitinib with placebo and sunitinib with ripretinib for GIST, sunitinib 50 mg/day for 4 weeks followed by a 2-week withdrawal was recommended as a regimen for imatinib-resistant GIST (10,12). However, the high incidence of grade 3 or higher hypertension, HFS, and myelosuppression often causes sunitinib dose reduction or interruption (12). The rate of dose reduction or discontinuation of sunitinib due to sunitinib-related AEs tends to be higher in older patients and Japanese patients, including cancer types other than GIST (14,17,20). Management of sunitinib-related AEs is an important concern for all cancer types. Numerous schedules of sunitinib administration have been explored for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNET). For example, a study in patients with RCC evaluated sunitinib 50 mg/day for 2 weeks followed by 1 week off, or continuous dosing at 37.5 mg/day (22,23,30). In the phase III study in patients with pNET, sunitinib 37.5 mg/day daily was used as the experimental dose, differing from the phase II study, owing to the frequency of sunitinib-induced AEs and the blood levels of sunitinib (31,32). A phase II trial attempted sunitinib 37.5 mg/day daily regimen in patients with GIST (21). The pharmacokinetics of sunitinib and its metabolite appeared similar between the schedule 4 weeks on, 2 weeks off, and the continuous daily dosing schedule after correcting for dose differences. However, this study was single-arm and did not provide a direct comparison of the efficacy or safety between dosing schedules. In a phase II trial comparing sunitinib 50 mg/day for 4 weeks, followed by a 2-week rest period, and 37.5 mg/day daily in patients with RCC, the time to tumor progression tended to be shorter in the daily group (30). Other retrospective studies on adjusted sunitinib schedules have generally shown no loss of therapeutic effect and improved safety compared to the standard 50 mg/day for 4 weeks followed by a 2-week withdrawal (22,23). Therefore, we arranged a regimen with an intermittent dosing method instead of continuous daily oral administration in this case.

A comparison of neutrophil and platelet counts during sunitinib withdrawal in this patient showed more regular changes in platelet counts. Hypertension, neutropenia, and HFS have been reported to correlate with sunitinib efficacy in patients with RCC and GIST (33-35). However, these studies reported that thrombocytopenia does not correlate with prolonged overall survival (33-35). The data suggest that maintaining a relatively high platelet count is feasible with sunitinib therapy and may not compromise treatment efficacy in this case.

Study limitations. First, the blood levels of sunitinib and its main active metabolite SU12662 were not measured. Therefore, it remains uncertain whether platelet count is the optimal biomarker for adjusting sunitinib dosing schedules. A correlation between the area under the curve (AUC) of the total drug (sunitinib + SU12662) and overall survival has been reported in patients with RCC and GIST (36). The AUCs of total drug or sunitinib alone have also been reported to be associated with increased blood pressure and neutropenia (35,36). Therefore, assessing the plasma concentration of sunitinib (+SU12662) is ideal for predicting therapeutic efficacy and toxicity. However, there was no correlation between platelet counts and the AUCs of total drug or sunitinib alone. Furthermore, thrombocytopenia is the most frequent grade 3 or higher AE caused by sunitinib in Japanese patients with GIST (17). Myelosuppression caused by sunitinib is more frequent in older patients than in non-older patients (37). This suggests that platelet counts, rather than blood levels of sunitinib or SU12662, may be a better indicator of sunitinib adjustment for safe sunitinib monotherapy in older patients with GIST. Second, as with all case reports, the findings of this study have limited external validity. When we compared the neutrophil and platelet counts during the withdrawal period of each cycle in this patient, the platelet count showed more regular changes. However, it is unclear whether other older patients with GIST would show similar changes. In particular, this adjustment approach is not suitable when sunitinib dose adjustment is necessary because of severe nonhematologic toxicity. Finally, it is unclear whether sunitinib was as effective at low doses as in this case. In particular, patients with GIST with KIT exon 11 mutations are known to have an inferior therapeutic response to sunitinib compared to patients with GIST with exon 9 mutations (38-41). Therefore, for patients with GISTs who are intolerant to standard sunitinib regimens, alternative therapies such as regorafenib should be recommended as next-line therapy.

Conclusion

Low-dose sunitinib monotherapy, with dosing intervals based on platelet counts rather than neutrophil counts, may be an effective treatment option for managing toxicity in older patients with imatinib-resistant GIST.
