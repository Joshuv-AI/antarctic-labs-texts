Introduction – Redrawing the Boundaries of Immune Disease

Systemic autoimmune diseases and primary immunodeficiencies/inborn errors of immunity (PIDs/IEIs) have long been viewed as opposing ends of the immunological spectrum. Yet, cumulative evidence is dismantling this dichotomy, revealing a shared landscape of immune dysregulation, genetic vulnerability, and clinical convergence [1–4]. Advances in next-generation sequencing have uncovered numerous IEIs that present with features of systemic autoimmune diseases, such as arthritis, vasculitis, and lupus-like disease, blurring the boundary between both conditions. Diverse mechanisms of immune dysregulation exist in IEIs, including impaired regulatory T cell function, defective apoptosis, dysregulated cytokine signaling, and type I interferonopathies, which mirror pathogenic pathways in polygenic systemic autoimmune diseases. Importantly, identifying IEIs in patients diagnosed with systemic autoimmune diseases carries diagnostic and therapeutic implications, as targeted treatments (e.g., CTLA-4-Ig, JAK inhibitors) may be more effective and less harmful than conventional immunosuppression [5]. On the other side, many PIDs/IEIs can present with or be complicated by autoimmune features due to immune dysregulation. For instance, patients with common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) often develop autoimmune cytopenias, and those with severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) may exhibit autoimmune phenomena due to defective immune regulation. This intersection suggests shared pathogenic mechanisms, such as genetic mutations affecting immune tolerance and regulation [4, 6–9].

This review challenges conventional paradigms by arguing that autoimmune disease may be the presenting feature of an underlying PID/IEI, particularly in cases of early-onset, refractory, or syndromic disease. In an era of personalized medicine, failure to recognize this overlap risks misdiagnosis and mismanagement, with relevant clinical consequences for the patient and the family. These include unchecked immunosuppression in PID/IEI-associated autoimmunity, which can exacerbate infections and malignancy, as well as delayed PID/IEI diagnosis, perpetuating therapeutic inefficacy. Within this evolving framework, immunosuppressive therapies, while essential, introduce additional layers of complexity and risk that warrant careful consideration.

Risks of Immunosuppression in Autoimmune Diseases

Immunosuppressants, including corticosteroids, conventional agents, and biologic/targeted drugs, remain cornerstone therapies of systemic autoimmune diseases, but carry a Faustian bargain. The risk varies depending on the level of immunosuppression, age, comorbidities, and the pathology itself. For instance, patients with systemic autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or rheumatoid arthritis (RA), are particularly vulnerable, especially when treated with high-dose corticosteroids, biologics, or combination immunosuppressive regimens. Striking the right balance between disease control and infection prevention remains a key challenge in clinical practice [10, 11].

Biologic therapies, which target specific components of the immune system, have transformed the treatment of autoimmune diseases, but can also lead to immunosuppression and associated adverse effects, such as increased infection risk and paradoxical immune-mediated phenomena, exemplified by anti-TNF-induced psoriasis [12].

Rituximab-induced hypogammaglobulinemia predisposes to potentially fatal pneumonias, while cyclophosphamide escalates bladder cancer risk [13–15]. Methotrexate is associated with hepatotoxicity, and calcineurin inhibitors with nephrotoxicity [16, 17]. Yet, a major oversight is failing to screen for PID/IEI before initiating immunosuppression. Routine Ig profiling and B-cell phenotyping pre-therapy may help prevent diagnostic delay and inappropriate immunosuppression. This underlines the relevance of an immunological workup before initiating treatment [18].

Additionally, the timing and intensity of immunosuppression are critical, as early and vigorous immunosuppression can be more effective, while advanced disease stages may require escalated, combination, or symptom-directed therapies depending on organ damage and disease chronicity [19].

The primary risks associated with long-term immunosuppression in patients with systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., SLE and RA) and other immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMIDs), such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), are outlined below.

Increased Risk of Infections

Immunosuppressive therapies substantially increase the risk of serious infections, including bacterial, viral, and opportunistic pathogens. While all immunosuppressive agents enhance susceptibility to infection, each class is associated with a distinct infection risk profile. For instance, anti-TNF agents are strongly linked to latent tuberculosis reactivation, JAK inhibitors (JAKinibs) to herpes zoster (prompting vaccination even in patients under the age of 65), anti-IL17 drugs to superficial fungal infections, and rituximab to hepatitis B reactivation. These risks underscore the importance of standardized pre-treatment infectious screening protocols. Accordingly, testing for latent tuberculosis, hepatitis B/C, and HIV, are routinely implemented before initiating biologic or intensive immunosuppressive therapy. Despite these precautions, serious infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in this population. This concern is echoed by professional guidelines: the American College of Rheumatology highlights the increased risk of serious infections with biologics and JAKinibs in patients with rheumatic diseases [20–24]. Beyond established immunosuppressive agents, emerging immunomodulatory approaches, such as CAR-T cell therapies currently being explored in refractory systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., SLE), may also increase susceptibility to cytopenias and infections. Although clinical experience remains limited, these developments reinforce the need for maintaining vigilance not only with approved therapies but also with novel strategies as they enter clinical practice [25].

Malignancies

Long-term immunosuppression is associated with an increased risk of both hematologic and non-hematologic malignancies. Patients with SLE on prolonged immunosuppressive therapy are at higher risk for hematologic malignancies, such as lymphomas and myelodysplastic syndromes. Additionally, there is an elevated risk of non-melanoma skin cancers (NMSC) and potentially melanoma in patients with IBD on immunosuppressive therapy, particularly with thiopurines and still controversial for anti-TNF agents [24, 26, 27]. More recently, JAKinibs have raised safety concerns following large post-marketing studies reporting increased rates of malignancy, particularly lung cancer and lymphoma, leading to regulatory warnings and updated clinical guidelines [28].

Bone Marrow Suppression

Drugs such as azathioprine and methotrexate can cause bone marrow suppression, leading to cytopenias and necessitating regular monitoring of blood counts [29]. Prior testing of thiopurine methyltransferase activity/genotype and nudix hydroxylase 15 genotype can help predict azathioprine-induced leukopenia, and help to calculate safer drug dosage [30].

Organ Toxicity

Particular immunosuppressive agents can cause organ-specific toxicities. For example, methotrexate is mainly associated with hepatotoxicity, while cyclosporine and tacrolimus are associated with renal toxicity [31]. Historically, methotrexate-induced pneumonitis was considered a major concern; however, more recent studies suggest that many cases may instead reflect underlying interstitial lung disease related to the primary condition (e.g., RA), infections, or other non–drug-related causes [16]. Corticosteroid therapy is associated with early bone loss, as significant reductions in bone mineral density can occur within the first months of treatment, contributing to glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis and increased fracture risk [32].

Cardiovascular and Metabolic Risks

Corticosteroids, commonly used in immunosuppressive regimens, are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular complications, including thromboembolic events, as well as a broad range of metabolic and hemodynamic effects such as hypertension, altered glucose metabolism, dyslipidemia, fluid retention, and accelerated atherosclerosis, all of which contribute to increased long-term cardiovascular risk [31, 32]. Additionally, JAKinibs have been linked to an elevated risk of both venous and arterial thromboembolic events, particularly in older patients or those with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors. These findings have led to regulatory warnings and restrictions on use in certain high-risk populations [33].

Pharmacogenomic studies have demonstrated that interindividual genetic variation in drug metabolism, transport, and molecular targets influences both the efficacy and toxicity of immunosuppressive agents, with established clinical utility currently limited to selected therapies. PID/IEI Recognition and Screening in Autoimmune Disease

In this context, recognizing an underlying PID/IEI becomes particularly relevant and we advocate for prompt PID/IEI screening when severe or atypical autoimmune manifestations are present. Clinical red flags would include early-life onset (e.g., juvenile SLE with hypogammaglobulinemia), refractory disease (e.g., steroid-resistant cytopenias in CVID), syndromic features (e.g., ALPS-like phenotypes with FAS mutations), and family history of autoimmunity or recurrent infections, as well as the coexistence of autoimmune diseases. Genetic studies underscore this link. Monogenic PIDs/IEIs (e.g., STAT3 gain-of-function, LRBA deficiency) disrupt immune tolerance, mimicking polygenic systemic autoimmune diseases. A Triad of Dysregulation: Autoimmunity, Immunodeficiency, and Cancer

Malignancy risk is a critical consideration within this immunological interplay. Systemic autoimmune diseases and PIDs/IEIs converge on a pro-neoplastic milieu driven by immune dysregulation, encompassing chronic inflammation, altered immune surveillance, and disease-specific pathogenic pathways. While hematologic malignancies, particularly lymphomas, represent the most consistent signal across several conditions, excess cancer risk is heterogeneous and disease-specific [51].

Autoimmune diseases significantly enhance malignancy risk due to underlying immune dysregulation, which is compounded by immunosuppressive therapy. For instance, SLE is associated with an increased risk of lymphoma compared with the general population (both non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) and Hodgkin lymphoma (HL)) via chronic B-cell activation, but also with selected solid tumors, including lung, thyroid, and virus-associated malignancies, reflecting both immune dysregulation and impaired control of oncogenic infections [51–55].

RA predisposes patients to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, as well as lung, gynecologic, and urinary tract cancers [51, 52].

Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) represents one of the clearest clinical models of autoimmunity predisposing to malignancy. It markedly increases the risk of NHL, particularly marginal-zone (MALT) subtypes, most often arising in the parotid glands, and has also been linked to ovarian cancer, although evidence for this association is less robust. Beyond the overall risk, specific predictors of lymphoma development in SS have been identified, including persistent parotid enlargement, lymphadenopathy, cryoglobulinemia, hypocomplementemia, high anti-SSA/SSB titers, and positive rheumatoid factor [56, 57]. Notably, SS and CVID share a predilection for marginal zone lymphoma, underscoring convergent pathogenic mechanisms across autoimmunity and IEIs [51, 58–61].

Distinct patterns are observed in other systemic autoimmune diseases. Systemic sclerosis shows association with lung cancer, particularly in the context of chronic tissue damage and fibrosis, and also confers increased susceptibility to breast and cervical cancers. Nevertheless, the most pronounced increase in overall malignancy risk is observed in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, especially dermatomyositis, where cancer occurrence often clusters temporally around disease onset [51]. Unlike SS, where lymphoma emerges as a complication of chronic B-cell hyperactivity, dermatomyositis is often associated with malignancy acting as a paraneoplastic driver of disease. Risk is highest in the first years after diagnosis and is strongly associated with specific autoantibodies, notably anti-TIF1γ, anti-NXP2, and, to a lesser extent, anti-SAE. This distinctive association has led to the development of formal cancer-screening guidelines in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, highlighting the need for disease-specific surveillance strategies, unlike other systemic autoimmune diseases [62, 63].

These disease-specific malignancy patterns highlight the necessity of tailored cancer screening protocols.

As discussed in previous sections, immunosuppressive therapies may further contribute to malignancy risk by impairing tumor immune surveillance and reducing control of oncogenic infections [26].

In a subset of patients, malignancy may precede or trigger secondary autoimmune or inflammatory rheumatic manifestations, a phenomenon best exemplified by paraneoplastic syndromes such as dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, and selected forms of vasculitis. In addition, cancer immunotherapies that enhance antitumor immunity, including immune checkpoint inhibitors, may unmask or induce de novo immune-mediated inflammatory diseases [51].

At the interface between autoimmunity and immunodeficiency, disruption of mucosal barriers and host–microbiome interactions provide a unifying framework linking autoimmunity and immunodeficiency. Commensal dysbiosis and gut barrier breakdown, hallmarks of PIDs/IEIs like CVID, may ignite autoimmunity via molecular mimicry and TLR hyperactivation. Should fecal microbiota transplantation precede biologics in selected cases? Emerging data also point to the role of Age-Associated B Cells (ABCs), which accumulate in SLE and CVID, secreting pathogenic autoantibodies. Targeting these cells (e.g., with BTK inhibitors) could spare protective immunity, a tantalizing alternative to broad-based immunosuppression [64–67].

Taken together, these bidirectional interactions and shared mechanisms culminate in complex clinical phenotypes in which autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, malignancy, and treatment-related risks converge. Vasculitis represents one of the clearest examples of this intersection.

In this context, vasculitis associated with immunodeficiency or malignancy represents a particularly complex entity, as vascular inflammation may arise from immune dysregulation, or paraneoplastic mechanisms. This association is relatively rare but clinically significant [68, 69].

Vasculitis can present as cutaneous forms, often necrotizing and leukocytoclastic, or as systemic vasculitis, sometimes associated with cryoglobulinemia, polyarteritis nodosa, or granulomatous angiitis. Hematologic malignancies, particularly lymphomas, myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), and chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), have been reported more frequently in association with vasculitis than solid tumors [68]. In patients with MDS or CMML, vasculitis may manifest with giant cell arteritis, Behçet-like features, or polyarteritis nodosa. Glucocorticoids are typically the first-line treatment, but steroid-sparing agents are often introduced early, not only in relapsing or refractory cases, but also in patients considered at high risk for treatment-related complications, particularly in giant cell arteritis. Evidence on outcomes is scarce, but a French multicenter case-control study suggested that vasculitis in these patients does not significantly affect overall survival, although relapse rates are high and may require aggressive management [70, 71].

Additionally, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) has been reported in patients with lymphoid neoplasia, such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia and T-cell lymphoma, often presenting with severe manifestations like pulmonary-renal syndrome [72].

In AAV patients, the excess mortality observed during the first year after diagnosis is mainly attributable to therapy-associated adverse events, particularly infections and treatment-related organ damage, rather than vasculitis activity itself [73, 74]. After the first year, mortality is more commonly driven by cardiovascular disease, malignancy, and infection [73].

Bridging Mechanism and Management: Towards Precision Medicine

A call to action is warranted: mandate PID/IEI screening for atypical autoimmune disease using immunoglobulin levels, lymphocyte subsets, and genetic testing. In parallel, adopt “immunodeficiency-aware” immunosuppression strategies: monitor Ig/B-cell profiles pre- and post-rituximab, consider immunoglobulin replacement proactively, and incorporate mechanistic therapies such as JAKinibs for STAT mutations or abatacept for CTLA4 haploinsufficiency.

Recent research highlights the intricate relationships connecting systemic autoimmune diseases, PIDs/IEIs, and malignancy. This intersection is characterized by shared immunological pathways, genetic predispositions, and clinical manifestations that significantly impact patient outcomes and therapeutic approaches. Amaya-Uribe et al. (2019) elucidated genetic and immunological mechanisms linking PIDs/IEIs to autoimmunity, particularly mutations impairing immune tolerance by disrupting the balance between regulatory and effector T cells [74]. Costagliola et al. (2021) described autoimmune phenomena across diverse immunodeficiency groups, including antibody deficiencies, combined immunodeficiencies, and innate immunity disorders [1]. These insights facilitate earlier recognition of underlying PIDs/IEIs among patients initially presenting with autoimmune manifestations. Grimbacher et al. (2016) explored monogenic versus polygenic autoimmune disorders, revealing shared genetic pathways between rare immunodeficiencies with autoimmunity and common polygenic autoimmune diseases such as RA and SLE [6]. Maggadottir and Sullivan (2014) illustrated the clinical importance of recognizing subtle immune deficiencies underlying severe, pleomorphic autoimmune disorders [75]. In a study of 1008 rheumatic disease patients, 64 were noted to have persistent hypogammaglobulinemia. Almost half (30/64) were found to have predicted pathogenic variants (33 in total) in known PID/IEI genes. All 33 variants were monoallelic, with 10/33 being in genes associated with autosomal dominant PID/IEI [76].

Therapeutic strategies should prioritize early detection and management of underlying PIDs/IEIs. In SLE, RA, and AAV patients receiving biologics or immunosuppressants, Ig levels should be assessed at diagnosis and monitored regularly during treatment. The baseline timepoint prior to therapy is of critical importance for early detection of PID/IEI, and it is important to also re-assess Ig levels when switching therapies. For example, in a rituximab-treated patient developing hypogammaglobulinemia: without a baseline pre-rituximab IgG measurement it is impossible to assess whether the cause may be rituximab, or prior immunosuppression including glucocorticoids, and/or underlying PID/IEI. In rituximab-treated RA, baseline IgG screening and periodic reassessment are therefore important, and retreatment is usually scheduled at fixed intervals irrespective of peripheral B-cell repopulation [28, 77].

By contrast, in SLE and AAV, monitoring B-cell reconstitution has been increasingly explored to guide treatment decisions, and in some clinical settings it is used to support deferring retreatment in inactive patients, although predictive biomarkers remain to be validated [78]. In AAV, biomarker-triggered retreatment strategies have been tested after an initial fixed rituximab course: retreatment upon B-cell repopulation reduced relapses compared to ANCA-triggered dosing, but at the cost of greater rituximab exposure and a higher incidence of severe COVID-19 [79]. Although not yet established in clinical practice, detailed peripheral B-cell flow cytometry assessing naïve, memory, translational B-cells and plasmablasts thus carries greater potential relevance in these settings [78].

Current guidelines support precision and individualized treatment in PIDs/IEIs. Genetic mutations disrupting immune tolerance mechanisms contribute to autoimmune manifestations. Targeted therapeutic strategies should reflect this mechanistic understanding. Targeted Treatments in Monogenic PIDs/IEIs Associated with Autoimmunity: Pathogenic Mechanisms and Therapeutic Rationale

Despite recent advances, major research gaps persist. These include microbiome and gut barrier dysfunction, pathogenic ABCs and Tfr cell roles, commensal dysbiosis in CVID, and novel monogenic disease mechanisms. Additionally, a detailed study of pathways like NETosis and autoantibody regulation by commensal microbes is essential to refine our understanding of autoimmunity in PIDs/IEIs. Likewise, the development of personalized therapeutic approaches (e.g., JAKinibs for gene-specific immune disorders) must be supported by rigorous clinical trials. For rare IEIs, collaborative approaches, often based on IEI or rare disease registries, or registry disease subgroups, may be needed to deliver such clinical trials.

Mechanistic insights into host–microbiome interactions directly inform diagnostic precision, particularly in PID/IEI patients with autoimmunity presenting with gastrointestinal or systemic inflammatory features. Recognizing dysbiosis and barrier dysfunction in this context supports the rationale for targeted therapies, including immunoglobulin replacement, microbiota-directed interventions (e.g., dietary modification, probiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation in selected cases), and precision immunomodulation tailored to both the immune defect and the microbiome profile [64–67].

Integration of microbiome assessment into PID/IEI management enhances personalized care by enabling risk stratification and early intervention to prevent complications such as IBD-like pathology or systemic autoimmunity. Ultimately, acknowledging the bidirectional relationship between immune dysregulation and the microbiome is key to developing individualized, mechanism-based strategies that optimize outcomes in patients with PIDs/IEIs and autoimmunity.

Similar challenges arise beyond the field of PID/IEI. In systemic autoimmune diseases requiring intensive immunosuppression, such as vasculitis, therapeutic decision-making is likewise complicated by competing risks, expanding treatment options, and limited predictive biomarkers.

The expanding therapeutic landscape for AAV, including glucocorticoids, rituximab, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, methotrexate, and, more recently, avacopan (approved for severe granulomatosis with polyangiitis / microscopic polyangiitis), offers increasingly tailored approaches for both induction and maintenance. However, the diversity of regimens can add complexity to clinical decision-making, particularly in patients with comorbidities or atypical presentations. In this context, vasculitis management might typify what is increasingly being known as the “paradox of choice”, a paradoxical psychological phenomenon where abundance of options may produce indecision, uncertainty, and decreased satisfaction in the treating specialist [68, 87]. Recent international guidelines, including KDIGO 2024 and the EULAR 2022 update, advocate precision, yet real-world delays persist [88, 89]. The solution may lie in biomarker-driven algorithms (e.g., ANCA specificity, Ig levels) to stratify risks and tailor therapy [89].

These guidelines emphasize the need for a multidisciplinary approach and the use of targeted therapies like JAKinibs [89]. The complexity of these diseases and the variety of treatment options can make it challenging to determine the optimal therapeutic strategy, potentially leading to decision fatigue and suboptimal patient outcomes.

In both scenarios, the paradox of choice underscores the importance of shared decision-making, where clinicians and patients collaboratively evaluate the benefits and risks of each treatment option. This approach can help mitigate the negative effects of having too many choices, ensuring that treatment decisions are aligned with the patient’s values and preferences, ultimately improving adherence and satisfaction with the chosen management plan.

Towards an Integrative Perspective on Immune Dysregulation

Mechanistic understanding of immune dysregulation is central to the management of PIDs/IEIs and systemic autoimmune diseases, as it directly informs diagnostic accuracy, therapeutic selection, and risk stratification for complications such as autoimmunity and lymphoproliferation [90, 91]. Immune dysregulation in PIDs/IEIs often manifests as autoimmunity, which may precede or overshadow infectious susceptibility, making recognition of these patterns essential for timely diagnosis and intervention [18].

A working hypothesis at the intersection of PIDs/IEIs and autoimmune diseases, integrating genetic pathways, targeted therapies, clinical screening features, and the microbiome, points to distinct patterns of microbiome-derived metabolites as functional biomarkers and modulators of immune dysregulation in genetically defined PIDs/IEIs presenting with autoimmunity, and that these metabolite signatures can both predict and guide the selection of targeted therapies. This hypothesis is grounded in the observation that PIDs/IEIs with immune dysregulation often display unique clinical phenotypes of autoimmunity, which are not fully explained by genetics alone. The literature highlights the role of dysbiosis and altered gut barrier function in amplifying systemic inflammation and autoimmunity in PIDs/IEIs but does not yet propose that specific microbiome-derived metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan catabolites) could serve as actionable biomarkers to stratify patients for immunomodulatory or microbiome-directed therapies [92, 93]. Such an approach would integrate omics-based microbiome profiling with genetic and immunophenotypic data to refine diagnostic precision and personalize therapy, especially in cases where clinical features prompt immunodeficiency screening in autoimmune presentations [64, 94]. In addition to dysbiosis, altered gut barrier function, and microbiome-derived metabolites, other actionable factors that can trigger autoimmune disease in patients with PID/IEI and immune dysregulation include: immunotoxicants such as silica, mercury, pesticides; smoking, often through mechanisms that involve modulation of the gut microbiome and mucosal immunity [95]; epigenetic modifications in histone, and changes in non-coding RNA [96]; molecular mimicry; Th17/Treg imbalance; age and gender; dietary factors; microbial translocation [93]. Most of these factors could be considered actionable in the sense that they can be targeted or modified through environmental interventions, dietary changes, microbiome-directed therapies, and epigenetic modulation strategies.

This perspective extends current models by positing that the interplay between host genetic defects and the microbiome produces a metabolomic “fingerprint” that not only reflects but also drives the immune dysregulation underlying autoimmunity in PIDs/IEIs. This could open new avenues for non-invasive diagnostics and for the rational selection or combination of targeted therapies and microbiome interventions in this patient population.

In summary, understanding immune dysregulation is critical for therapeutic decision-making in PIDs/IEIs and systemic autoimmune diseases. Recognizing the hidden burden of PIDs/IEIs behind systemic autoimmunity is a clinical imperative.
