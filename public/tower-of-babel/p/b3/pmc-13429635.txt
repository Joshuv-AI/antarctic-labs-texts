Heavy metals, carbon monoxide and PBDE exposure among firefighters: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Elkana Kohn, Mahmod Watad, Dana Barchel, Ahuva Golik, Orna Nagawkar, Tamar Wainstock, Marc Lougassi, Luda Groisman, Efrat Rorman, Gideon Fleminger, Jessica Ross, Frieda Schwartsburd, Solomon Efriem, Malka Britzi, Matitiahu Berkovitch, Sharon Daniel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFirefighters are subject to significant risks to their health, including an increased risk for significant morbidities due to exposure to pollutants. The aim of this study was to examine the magnitude of firefighters’ exposure to various hazardous substances in a large cross-sectional study. Detailed activity records of 108 firefighters, questionnaires blood and urine samples were collected and compared with 104 men with other occupations. Heavy metals, carboxyhemoglobin and PBDE levels were compared between firefighters and controls. Correlations were explored between pollutant levels and individual exposure frequencies to the total number of fire events, and by subtypes. Significant differences between firefighters and controls were observed in levels of Cr (0.41 ± 0.52 vs. 0.2 ± 0.14 µg/g creatinine, p-value = 0.006), Ni (0.55 ± 0.63 vs. 1.13 ± 0.59, p-value < 0.001) and Pb (0.26 ± 0.23 vs. 0.36 ± 0.21, p-value < 0.001). No significant difference was found in other metals, As (12.7 ± 10.7 vs. 11.8 ± 10.8), Cd (0.26 ± 0.14 vs. 0.23 ± 0.10), Hg (0.42 ± 0.43 vs. 0.59 ± 0.81), as well in COHb levels (2.80%±1.66 vs. 3.01%±1.58). Smokers exhibited significantly higher COHb levels. When adjusted for age and education, firefighters had significantly higher levels of As (p < 0.001) and lower levels of Ni (p < 0.001) vs. controls. We found low PBDEs levels, suggesting low exposure compared to firefighters from other countries. Exposure to fire events was not correlated with pollutant levels among firefighters. Israeli firefighters show low exposure to carbon monoxide, heavy metals, and PBDEs compared to international cohorts, though PBDEs levels exceed controls. Introduction

The collapse of the World Trade Center towers on September 11th, 2001 dramatically increased awareness of the occupational hazards of firefighters. Firefighters are at risk of severe morbidity as a result of their occupation1. Causality between firefighting and specific cancer types has been established2, and epidemiologic findings show that firefighters suffer from other cancer types at higher rates than the general population3–6. Other adverse health effects and injuries linked to firefighting include cardiovascular, respiratory, and metabolic morbidity, hearing loss, injuries, and psychological damage7–10.

Life-threatening situations are integral to firefighting. Firefighting involves routine exposures to dangerous situations and hazardous substances. During a single emergency call, firefighters may be exposed to various situations and factors that may adversely affect their health11. They may face various types of fires, including those in open fields or woodland, vehicles, enclosed buildings, and industrial facilities. Furthermore, firefighters risk exposure to various toxic substances, particularly in fires involving vehicles or industrial settings12. Responding to different types of emergency calls poses a significant challenge in exploring the nature of firefighters’ exposures. Exposure measurements during major events or during fire drills13–15 can provide valuable information regarding different types of exposures. Nevertheless, chronic exposures are continuous and more prevalent, therefore, they may significantly affect firefighters’ health. Firefighters are at risk of absorbing chemicals through different pathways, primarily through skin contact and breathing, especially in situations where self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) is not utilized16. Research has revealed high levels of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), as well as metals like antimony, cadmium, and lead on their skin, clothing, and personal protective equipment (PPE) after responding to fires17. This underscores the critical nature of these exposure routes.

Exposure to heavy metals poses significant risks to health, especially in occupational settings with frequent exposures18. Prolonged or high levels of exposure to heavy metals, including lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and mercury (Hg), can lead to serious health problems, including damage to vital organs, cancer, cardiovascular and respiratory diseases18–22. A recent study from Korea found that high Cd levels in firefighters are correlated with metabolic syndrome, emphasizing the effects of chronic exposure on their health23. Cd exposure has also been associated with increased risk of kidney damage and cardiovascular disease in occupational cohorts24. Pb exposure is known to cause neurocognitive deficits and cardiovascular disease, while Hg can affect the nervous system at relatively low exposure levels24.

Firefighters also experience increased exposure to carbon monoxide, an odorless, colorless gas produced during incomplete combustion. Fires in enclosed structures can result in acute exposure, compared to the low, chronic exposure typical among smokers25,26. Carbon monoxide molecules form covalent bonds with hemoglobin to form carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), leading to oxygen deficiency and severe health complications, making it a major public health concern27.

Polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) are found in electronic appliances, textiles, furniture foam, insulating foams, vehicles and broad range of consumer products and industrial instruments28, continuously leaching into the environment from containing products29,30. While the general population is typically exposed to this group of pollutants, firefighters are exposed to significantly higher levels of PBDEs than the general population31,32. PBDEs exposure has been found to have significant health effects, including cancer, reduced male fertility, thyroid toxicity, metabolic and endocrine disruption33–38.

Several studies have examined heavy metal exposure in firefighters. The California Firefighter Occupational Exposures (FOX) project reported Hg concentrations exceeding national reference thresholds in a subset of participants39. A study in Saudi Arabia observed significant differences in urinary heavy metal concentrations between firefighters directly exposed to fire and those not exposed, although values remained within recommended ranges40. Additional investigations in China identified increased levels of Zn, Pb, Mn, and Cd in hair samples collected after firefighting activities16. Furthermore, a NIOSH health hazard evaluation assessed urinary antimony in firefighters wearing flame-retardant uniforms and found concentrations generally within reference ranges41.

The goal of the current cross-sectional study was to characterize firefighters’ occupational exposure to carbon monoxide (as measured by carboxyhemoglobin), heavy metals, metalloid, and PBDE and to compare their exposure levels with those of controls. The study aims to address gaps in the literature regarding the magnitude of exposures during different types of fires and emergency events, providing detailed biomonitoring data to better understand potential health risks associated with firefighting activities.

Results

Study participants included 108 active firefighters and 104 controls. Of the 117 firefighters invited to participate in the study, 9 (7.7%) declined. Of these, 5 provided no reason, whilst 4 were dispatched to an emergency call during the research team’s visit to the fire station. They declined to participate when approached again later. The control group comprised individuals employed across a range of occupations, including office-based roles, high-tech, teaching, management (n = 31, 30%); agriculture, industry, construction (n = 40, 38%); health and services (n = 8, 8%) and other (n = 25, 24%). None were employed in occupations associated with elevated occupational exposure risk, defined as positions requiring periodic biological monitoring (e.g. metal or chemical industries, or workers exposed to radiation). Among firefighters, the mean age was 40.4 ± 9.7 years. They had a mean weight of 84.4 ± 14.6 kg and a height of 176 ± 7 cm. The mean BMI was 23.9 ± 3.6 kg/m², while the mean systolic blood pressure was 123.9 ± 17.1 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure was 76.6 ± 9.8 mmHg, with a mean heart rate of 69.4 ± 12.4 beats per minute. Eight firefighters (7.4%) reported prior respiratory and 11 (10.1%) prior cardiovascular conditions. All conditions were resolved or controlled with appropriate treatment.

Table 1 presents a comparison of basic characteristics between firefighters and controls. Firefighters were significantly older (age of 40.4 ± 9.7 vs. 34.7 ± 5.5 years, respectively, p-value < 0.001), and had significantly fewer education years (12.8 ± 1.9 vs. 15.0 ± 3.1 years, respectively, p-value < 0.001). No differences were found regarding the proportions of smokers between the groups (38.9% smoking firefighters vs. 38.5% of controls, p value = 0.91). No significant differences in COHb levels were observed between firefighters, with mean ± SD of 2.79 ± 1.74 and median (IQR) of 2.0 (1.8–3.0) compared to controls with 2.98 ± 1.83 and 2.15 (1.9–3.2) (p = 0.2; Fig. 1A; Table 2). As expected, smokers had significantly higher levels of COHb (carboxyhemoglobin levels, expressed as percent of total hemoglobin [%]) compared with non-smokers among both the firefighters (means of 3.83%±2.10 and 2.10%±0.71 and medians of 3.05 and 1.90, respectively, p < 0.001, see Fig. 1B, C) and the control group (means of 4.12%±1.99 and median of 3.80 in smokers and 2.38%±0.78 2.30 in non-smokers, p < 0.001). A dose-response relationship was not observed between the total number of events with fire exposure during the last 3 months and COHb levels (r = 0.09, p-value = 0.41, see Fig. 1D). Additionally, no significant associations were found between blood COHb levels and the number of each different firefighting activity (see Supplemental Table S1 and Supplemental Figs. 1–5).

Compared with controls, firefighters had significantly higher urine levels of Cr and Pb, and lower levels of Ni, while As, Cd, and Hg urine levels did not differ significantly (Fig. 2; Table 2). In the multivariable Poisson regression analysis adjusted for age and education, firefighters had significantly higher levels of As (incidence rate ratios (IRR) = 1.32, 95% CI 1.16–1.50, p < 0.001) and lower levels of Ni (IRR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.22–0.62, p < 0.001) compared with controls. No significant differences were observed for COHb, Pb, Hg, Cd, or Cr (Table 3).

No significant differences were observed between firefighters’ roles for COHB and for most metals. Discussion

This cross-sectional study did not find consistent evidence of increased exposure to heavy metals and As metalloid among firefighters compared with a control group. Firefighters, as well as controls, had As or metal levels well below the allowable threshold for occupational exposure levels defined by Israeli and international standards. Overall, no significant differences in metal or metalloid levels were found between firefighters and controls. Similarly, no differences were observed in carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) levels between the groups, although smokers had higher COHb levels. In addition, no associations were found between pollutant levels and exposure to firefighting events or with sociodemographic characteristics. Although Cd levels appeared higher among higher-ranking personnel, this pattern was not maintained after accounting for age.

Firefighters, as well as controls, had metal levels well below the allowable occupational exposure levels defined by Israeli and international standards. There have been numerous studies on firefighters’ exposure to heavy metals, but most measured samples were collected after active firefighting, when levels are likely to be higher than at steady state. A study by Dobraca et al. found that heavy metals were not elevated in firefighters from California24. This study, although using different matrices (blood in the aforementioned study vs. urine in our study), together with our findings, suggest that steady-state levels of heavy metals in firefighters are not higher than in the general population. Further support for this finding comes from the lack of significant association between firefighting activities and metals levels.

The levels of COHb in the firefighters and control groups were similar. As expected, significant differences were observed between smokers and non-smokers, as reported in many studies before25,26,42.

Higher levels of COHb among smokers, regardless of occupation, are consistent with well-established findings in the literature25–27,42. A previous study by Loke et al., measured COHb in 51 firefighters and 21 controls. The researchers reported high levels of COHb in smokers (mean of 5.25% in firefighters and 5.64% in controls), compared to non-smokers, firefighters, and controls (2.11% and 1.67%, respectively)43. Our results are in line with this study (see Table 2), but not with a study by Al-Malki, who found a significant difference in COHb levels of 43 non-smokers firefighters (4.93%) compared with 17 non-smokers controls (3.00%, p < 0.005)37. The relatively high levels of COHb they identified in firefighters is likely due to the sampling timing, which was within a couple of hours of fire exposure after an emergency call.

Notably, the levels of firefighters’ quantifiable PBDEs were much lower than those reported in previous studies. In a study performed by Shaw et al., all samples had detectable levels of PBDE-28, -47, -100 and -153 while PBDE-99 was detected in 92% of the samples (means of 2, 52, 12, 33 and 10 ng/g lipids, respectively)29. Similarly, Park et al. reported 97%, 99%, 90%, 98% and 100% detection rates of the PBDE-28, -47, -99, -100 and − 153 (geometric means of 1.7, 32.3, 6.19, 5.68 and 15.4 ng/g lipids)28. Our study had much lower detection rates compared with these studies, with rates of 0%, 37%, 19%, 5.5% and 30% and means were 0.26, 0.46, 0.32, 0.27 and 0.37 ng/g lipids for PBDE-28, -47, -99, -100 and -153, respectively. These results suggest low exposure to PBDEs among Israeli firefighters compared to colleagues from other countries. Firefighters had higher PBDE levels compared to the control group, in most congeners (PBDE-28, -47, -66, -100, -85, -154) while controls had higher levels of PBDE-99 and PBDE-153 (Table 4). Firefighters had slightly lower total absolute PBDE levels, nevertheless statistically significant, compared with the control group (2.56 ± 0.9 vs. 2.59 ± 3.96) (Table 4). Taking the differences in detection rates and large variation in the control group, interpretation of the results should be done cautiously. It is probable that the statistical significance between groups is not likely to have any clinical significance.

Our investigation’s findings are consistent with previous research36,37 that did not find significant differences in As and heavy metal levels between firefighters and control groups. In addition to the previously described findings, our study adds to the current knowledge, showing that there is no correlation between heavy metal or metalloid levels and frequency of fire events, both in total events and of specific types.

The absence of significant differences in metalloid and heavy metals levels between firefighters and controls, as well as the lack of correlation between metalloid, heavy metals and COHb levels and various types of exposures to fire might be attributed to effective use of the personal protective equipment, but also to other unidentified factors. It is well established that firefighters are at increased risk for cancer, ischemic heart disease, pulmonary disease, and other health conditions. Beyond chemical exposures, additional occupational factors such as shift work, chronic psychological stress, and repeated exposure to extreme heat contribute both independently and collectively to adverse health outcomes. These factors can promote systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation, mechanisms that are also linked to the diseases observed among firefighters. Further studies are warranted to disentangle the relative contributions of these occupational and lifestyle factors to firefighters’ overall health risks.

Our study has some limitations. First, our sampling method was based on voluntary participation, therefore the possibility of selection bias in both the firefighters and the control group cannot be excluded. Additionally, a few firefighters who showed interest in participation were called on emergency during the consent process, and therefore did not participate in the study eventually. Another limitation is that the control group was, on average, younger and more educated than the firefighters group. To overcome this limitation, we corrected for age where it was found significant. Years of education did not show correlation to any of the pollutants, no corrections were made for this variable.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study provides reassuring findings about Israeli firefighters’ exposure to CO, metalloid, heavy metals and PBDEs, with very low levels of all investigated pollutants.

The study also provides valuable insights into the complex relationship between firefighting activities, As and heavy metals exposure, and the efficacy of protective measures. Considering that firefighters are prone to increased morbidity compared to the general population, together with the consistent absence of associations between various fire exposures and levels of pollutants, further research is needed to enhance our understanding of firefighters’ occupational risks.

Methods

Participants and sample collection

The study was approved by the Shamir Medical Center ethics committee (approval number 0167-17-ASF) and prospectively registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05256212). All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ICH-GCP standards, and relevant guidelines and regulations of the ethics committee and regulatory authorities. All participants provided informed consent before any study procedures were undertaken. All methods and procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Firefighters were recruited between 2017 and 2018 from five fire stations in central Israel, while on duty. In each fire-station, recruitment took place at several occasions, to allow all firefighters in the station an opportunity to join the study. A brief overview of the study was provided to the men present at the shift. Interested individuals were given detailed explanation and signed informed consent. The control group was derived from the EHF birth cohort including 263 families (mother-father-newborn triads), in which husbands were also assessed for pollutants exposure. Men from this cohort who were not employed as firefighters served as the comparison group. (approval number 12/12)44,45. All participants completed questionnaires regarding sociodemographic, health and life habits, including diet, physical activity and smoking. They underwent a thorough physical examination and medical anamnesis by a physician, and blood and urine samples were collected. Samples were stored at 4°C until processing in the lab. Blood samples were collected in collection vials with EDTA as anti-coagulant or with no additive. Blood collection vials were centrifuged at 2400 g for 3 min. Whole blood, plasma or serum were aliquoted in cryovials and stored at -80°C until analysis. Urine creatinine levels were enzymatically measured using COBAS 8000 autoanalyzer (ROCHE Diagnostics, IN, USA). Detailed records of firefighting, rescue, training, and other profession-related activities were obtained from each firefighter’s record in the Israeli National Fire and Rescue Authority. Participants from the control group completed the same questionnaires and gave blood and urine samples via an identical procedure.

Samples measurements

Urine samples were analyzed for total arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), chromium VI (Cr), inorganic mercury (Hg), nickel (Ni), and lead (Pb) at the National Public Health Laboratories of the Israeli Ministry of Health, as previously described46. The measurements were carried out using an Agilent 7800 inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS) equipped with an Integrated Sample Introducing System (ISIS) and High Matrix Introducing mode (HMI). Samples were diluted with acid and directly injected into the ICP-MS instrument, followed by helium dilution in the HMI instrument. Metal concentrations were measured using certified analytical standards and internal standard calibration procedures. To ensure accuracy, control material aliquots were analyzed in each series of ten samples. The lab also participated in an annual international proficiency test (G-EQUAS) and validated accuracy for all parameters. Limit of Quantification (LOQ) was determined in the initial validation and was confirmed each working day. LOQ values were: 0.01 ng/ml for Hg; 0.02 ng/mL for As, Cd, Cr, Ni and 0.04 ng/ml for Pb. Analyte levels were corrected to urine creatinine levels.

COHb levels were measured in whole blood samples using COBAS b 221 (ROCHE Diagnostics, IN, USA). Samples were thawed and injected directly into the instrument according to manufacturer instructions.

PBDEs in serum samples were measured at the National Residue Control Laboratory (NRCL), Israel. After thawing the samples, 2 mL were mixed with 5 mL of H2O and 3 mL of formic acid (100%), vortexed and centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 rpm. The supernatant was loaded onto a Plexa 200 mg solid phase column, pre-conditioned with 3 mL dichloromethane followed by 3 mL methanol. After the columns were completely dry, PBDEs were eluted twice with 3 mL hexane in 0.5 mL H2O. Extracts were dried under nitrogen flow and reconstituted in 50 µL hexane. Samples were analyzed using a gas chromatograph (6890 N series, Agilent) equipped with an autosampler (7683B, Agilent) and connected to a mass spectrometer (5975B, Agilent). Chromatographic separation was carried out on DB-XLB column (60 m, 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 µm film thickness, Agilent) using helium as carrier gas. Negative chemical ionization, with methane as the reagent gas, was applied for analyte measurement, and the selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode was operated. Calibration curves were prepared by adding known PBDE concentrations to a pool of serum samples. Nine PBDE congeners were measured: 2,4,4’-tribromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-28), 2,2’,4,4’-tetrabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-47), 2,3’,4,4’-tetrabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-66), 2,2’,3,4,4’-pentabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-85), 2,2’,4,4’,5-pentabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-99), 2,2’,4,4’,6-pentabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-100), 2,2’,4,4’,5,5’-hexabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-153), 2,2’,4,4’,5,6’-hexabromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-154) and 2,2′,3,4,4′,5′,6-hepta-bromodiphenyl ether (PBDE-183). LOQ was 0.02 ng/mL. Cholesterol and triglycerides were measured using a COBAS 8000 autoanalyzer (ROCHE Diagnostics, IN, USA). The lab successfully participated in the AMAP external quality assurance program.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.0. The Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal Wallis tests were used to compare the levels of COHb among smokers and non-smokers, as well as between firefighters and controls, due to the non-normal distribution of the data. Heavy metals and metalloid levels in firefighters and controls were also compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

We also performed multivariable Poisson regression analyses (as the data were not normally distributed) to examine the independent association between being a firefighter (vs. control) and levels of carboxyhemoglobin, metalloid and heavy metals, and calculated incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals, adjusting for age and years of education.

Correlations between different types of fire exposures (total exposure to fire events, controlled fire suppression drills, vehicle fires, open field fires, and fires in enclosed buildings) and urinary levels of heavy metals (Pb, Hg, Cd), As and COHb were assessed using Spearman correlations. To account for differences in urinary elimination rates, analyses were stratified by the relevant time period prior to sampling for each substance: As (previous week), Pb (previous month), Hg (previous three months), Cd (previous two years), and COHb (previous three months). Cr and Ni were not included due to their rapid urinary elimination.

We examined the associations between firefighters’ roles, categorized as command-level, mid-level, and frontline firefighters, and metal levels using the Kruskal–Wallis test. In multivariable Poisson regression, we specifically assessed the association between role and Cd levels, adjusted for age.

Both total PBDE and individual PBDE congeners levels were compared between firefighters and controls using the Mann–Whitney U test. For statistical analysis purposes, values below LOQ were given the value LOQ/√2.
