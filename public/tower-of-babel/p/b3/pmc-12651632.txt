Implementation of a peripheral intravenous catheter bundle to reduce phlebitis and hospital-onset Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia: A quality improvement project
Authors: Jie Li, Maria Theresa Cabahug, Tuodi Wu, Hairu Chai, Rongyan An, Nicole Shu Ning Teoh, Liang Fang Yu, Zaleha binte Alias, Shi Yun Foo, Suhailah binte Nasir, Wenyi Seow, Philomena Liew, Thean Yen Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPeripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) are widely used in hospitals and account for up to one-third of hospital-onset Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (HO-SAB) cases.Local ProblemIn response to increasing HO-SAB, a quality improvement initiative was launched to reduce PIVC-related phlebitis and HO-SAB.MethodsAn evidence-based bundle for peripheral intravenous catheter (PIVC) insertion and care was developed, focusing on four key components: hand hygiene, aseptic technique during insertion, daily monitoring and timely removal of PIVCs, and optimization of maintenance care. The bundle was piloted in two wards using the Model for Improvement, which led to a reduction in phlebitis rates. The same PIVC bundle was subsequently implemented in a phased manner across the hospital, with the implementation process guided by Kotter's 8-step model of change to ensure effective and sustainable adoption.InterventionsInterventions involved staff education, daily PIVC checks, process and outcome monitoring with feedback, ongoing ward engagement, and hospital-wide awareness campaigns. Key implementation measures included bundle compliance, phlebitis rates, and PIVC-related HO-SAB.ResultsPhlebitis rates in pilot wards declined from 7% to 1–2%, with comparable reductions observed after hospital-wide implementation. PIVC-related HO-SAB rates decreased from 0.61 (pre-implementation) to 0.26 episodes per 10,000 patient-days.ConclusionsOverall, the structured care bundle significantly reduced phlebitis and was associated with lower PIVC-related HO-SAB rates.Keywords: Bacteremia, Infection control, Catheters

Introduction

Problem description

Peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) are one of the most used intravascular devices in hospitalized inpatients, with one international prevalence study reporting that 59% of hospital inpatients had at least one PIVC present [1]. Local site infection was estimated to occur in 0.15% (95 % CI: 0.047–0.479) of cannulas, or at a rate of 65.1 per 100,000 catheter-days, based on a recent meta-analysis [2]. In 2017, based on on-going surveillance, our institution identified nine PIVC-related S. aureus bacteremia's, while all-cause PIVC-related phlebitis rates were 5.3% over the same period. Although bloodstream infections (BSIs) caused by PIVCs are uncommon (0.028% of devices, as reported by the same meta-analysis2), the reported mortality from PIVC-related BSI's ranges from 11-13% [3]. PIVCs have been identified as one of the most common causes of hospital-onset Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia (HO-SAB), accounting for up to 36% of such cases [4], and likely associated with significant morbidity and mortality. These findings highlighted the clinical burden posed by PIVC-related complications in our hospital and underscored the need for targeted quality improvement interventions.

Rationale

At the time of project initiation, existing guidelines emphasized the importance of standardized PIVC insertion and maintenance care to minimize the risk of infective phlebitis. Enhancing compliance with evidence-based protocols was anticipated to reduce both local and systemic complications associated with PIVC use, thereby improving patient safety and clinical outcomes.

Specific aims

The quality improvement team was established in April 2018 with the following objectives: (a) to evaluate and implement evidence-based PIVC insertion and care processes (b) to measure and reduce PIVC-related phlebitis, and (c) to measure and reduce PIVC-related HO-SAB. In accordance with existing institutional guidelines, this initiative was classified as a quality improvement project rather than research.

Methods

Context

The quality project was implemented in a 1,100-bed acute-care hospital that provides a comprehensive range of services, including emergency medicine, medical and surgical care, geriatrics, and rehabilitation. At the time of implementation, the facility comprised 31 inpatient wards. The majority of these were multi-bedded units, complemented by a smaller number of single-bedded rooms, surgical and medical intensive care units, and a dedicated isolation ward. The hospital guidelines for PIVC care specified the use of open-system intravenous cannulas with injection ports in conjunction with needleless connectors, with a routine PIVC replacement every three days as the standard of care. These PIVC care practices remained unchanged throughout the study period.

Interventions

The interventions focused on developing and implementing a structured set of evidence-based practices—referred to as a “bundle”—for PIVC insertion and maintenance care to minimize complications. The project then developed optimal strategies for integrating these practices into routine clinical care, with implementation guided by a quality improvement framework to ensure reliable and sustained adoption.

The quality improvement project was structured in distinct phases.

Phase one (Pilot)

A multidisciplinary team was established to ensure representation from the professional groups likely to be affected by the proposed changes, as well as key stakeholders involved in the design, evaluation, and implementation of the final intervention. The team included ward nurses across various levels of seniority, infection prevention and control nurses (IPCNs), an infection control physician, and an executive member of the institutional Improvement Office.

Development of bundle components

The improvement initiative was designed and implemented following the Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model of Improvement [5]. The multidisciplinary team identified three core components for inclusion in the intervention bundle: (1) appropriate aseptic insertion of PIVCs in accordance with best practices; (2) daily inspection of PIVC insertion sites with prompt removal of catheters deemed unnecessary; and (3) standardized maintenance care of PIVCs.

For the first bundle component (aseptic insertion), key practices derived from epic3 guidelines [6] and the 2016 Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice [7] were distilled into an infographic used for staff training, awareness, and ongoing information dissemination. This resource emphasized the following critical elements: appropriate hand hygiene; appropriate skin antisepsis focusing on insertion site disinfection with friction and allowing the site to air-dry; and adherence to aseptic non-touch technique. The second component focused on routine daily inspection of PIVC insertion sites, with documentation of findings in the Electronic Medical Record (EMR). Catheters were recommended for removal if no longer clinically indicated—such as when intravenous therapy was discontinued—or if signs of phlebitis were present. To support consistent clinical decision-making, phlebitis was defined as the presence of at least two of the following at the insertion site: pain, erythema, or swelling. The third component aimed to optimize catheter maintenance care. This was achieved by standardizing the use of transparent, sterile, adhesive polyurethane dressings for catheter securement, along with routine application of intravenous extension sets to reduce manipulation at the insertion site.

Implementation of PIVC bundle

Two general surgical wards with high usage of PIVCs were selected as pilot sites for the improvement initiative. In collaboration with ward nurses, the project team developed and delivered targeted educational refresher sessions based on the bundle components, incorporating a standardized protocol for phlebitis identification. Post-implementation, data were collected using the “Ask 5 – Take 5” monitoring framework, which included periodic sampling of both knowledge and process measures. Monitoring was jointly led by designated ward nurse champions and IPCNs, promoting frontline engagement and adherence to best practices. Post-implementation, process measures that were monitored included maintenance of a clean, dry PIVC site dressing, accurate and timely documentation of PIVC site inspections in EMR and appropriate use of IV infusion extension sets. Outcome measures included the detection of PIVC-related phlebitis through two complementary approaches: routine documentation by ward nurses and independent assessment by IPCNs. The phlebitis rate was calculated as the number of PIVCs removed due to phlebitis (numerator) divided by the total number of PIVCs inserted (denominator), with both data points extracted from EMR nursing records.

PDSA improvement, measurement and results

Following initial implementation, improvements in PIVC care and documentation were gradual. Focus group discussions with ward nurses identified two key interventions: introducing a daily reminder for PIVC inspection during each nursing shift handover and establishing a fixed daily time for routine PIVC inspection and documentation. Implementation of these changes led to more consistent documentation of PIVC site monitoring in EMR nursing notes. Additionally, monthly informal engagement sessions were held with ward nurses, during which audit findings were shared, staff were encouraged to raise questions, and operational challenges were discussed with a focus on exploring solutions. To foster participation and boost morale, snacks such as chocolates, ice cream, and cookies were provided at these sessions. Following these interventions, compliance to bundle components and documented phlebitis rates improved, and concordance between ward nurse documentation and independent IPCN audits increased, indicating better recognition of phlebitis. Monthly updates on process and outcome measures were shared with each participating ward, highlighting improvements achieved over time.

Following implementation, both wards demonstrated significant reductions in phlebitis rates. Pre-intervention, the mean phlebitis rate was 7% in both wards. In Ward A, the rate decreased to a mean of 5.6% in the first six months post-intervention and further declined to 2.9% by 18 months. Phlebitis data was analysed using R software (version 4.5.0) [8]. Interrupted Time Series Analysis (ITSA) performed using segmented regression with Newey-West standard errors showed a significant post-intervention decline in phlebitis of 0.07% per month (P<0.05). Run chart analysis revealed a sustained shift, with six consecutive months below the pre-intervention median (Figure 1). Ward B showed no immediate improvement within the first six months but achieved a mean phlebitis rate of 1.1% by 12 months post-intervention, sustained through 18 months. ITSA indicated a pre-intervention upward trend of 0.11% per month (P<0.05), which reversed post-intervention, with rates declining by 0.37% per month (P<0.05). Run chart analysis similarly demonstrated a significant downward shift (Figure 1).

Run chart of PIVC-related phlebitis rates, Wards A and B.

The pilot phase highlighted the challenges of maintaining consistent PIVC care in a busy clinical environment. These challenges were addressed through initial staff training, integration of a daily PIVC reminder into nursing routines, establishment of a structured timepoint for PIVC inspection and documentation, and fostering long-term engagement and motivation by providing timely feedback that recognized staff improvements and good work.

Phase two to four (Spread of interventions)

The refinements to implementation strategies during the pilot phase guided the selection of eight additional wards for the phased rollout of the PIVC bundle. Phase 2 onboarded four wards in July 2019, followed by four more in Phase 3 in January 2020. After implementing the bundle across ten wards — including the pilot sites — plans for further hospital-wide expansion were halted by the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, with no new wards enrolled after January 2020. The team reconvened in 2022 to resume hospital-wide implementation efforts (Phase 4). The aims of the hospital-wide implementation were.

Implementation of phases 2-4

A. Implementation strategy

Implementation strategy

Kotter's 8-step change model guided the implementation process. The initiative began by establishing a sense of urgency through sharing current data on PIVC phlebitis rates and PIVC-related bacteremia with senior nursing management and ward managers. This step helped to foster consensus for the need for change. Engaging senior nurse leaders was crucial for assessing the feasibility of the PIVC bundle components and for developing a shared vision focused on improving PIVC care and reducing infection rates. Once senior nurse leaders were aligned with this vision, they were motivated to provide clear direction to their respective teams, empowering staff to work toward the common goal of enhancing PIVC care practices. This approach ensured momentum was maintained and anchored the changes within the organizational culture.

B.Creation and implementation of the change package

Creation and implementation of the change package

The implementation team consisted of senior nurse administrators, an IPC doctor, all IPCNs and all ward-based infection control link nurses (ICLNs). A comprehensive educational and PIVC training program was developed, with ICLNs selected as trainers. ICLNs first completed a standardized training program using materials provided by the IPC team. After this preparation, ICLNs delivered training sessions at ward level, supported by ongoing guidance from IPCNs. The training curriculum focused on.

For each ward, multiple training sessions were conducted to target complete coverage of all assigned nurses in each ward. Training materials were also integrated into nursing orientation programs, embedding these practices into the onboarding process for new staff. After implementation, intensive weekly process and outcome monitoring was carried out by ICLNs and IPCNs, with process monitoring further incorporated into routine nursing quality audits during Phase 4 (data provided as supplementary material). Results from independent monitoring sessions were immediately followed by real-time feedback to ward nurses, fostering continuous learning, reinforcing positive behaviors, and generating short-term wins to sustain momentum for improvement.

To maintain awareness and sustain institutional focus throughout the change process, a series of hospital-wide campaigns were regularly implemented using a variety of diverse methods. Initiatives included a TikTok competition focused on PIVC care to creatively communicate the program goals and engage staff, and annual awards to recognize wards achieving significant improvements in PIVC care and outcomes. Educational talks were held on designated “Infection Prevention Control Days” to reinforce key messages and renew commitment, while bi-annual Infection Prevention Workshops incorporated PIVC best practices into their curriculum.

Implementation for each phase started as follows: June 2019 (Phase 2), January 2020 (Phase 3), and September 2022 (Phase 4).

Measures for phases 2-4

Process measures

The same three process measures from the pilot phase were maintained, with post-implementation monitoring conducted by IPCNs and ICLNs, and data submitted via a web-based form. Monitoring and staff engagement sessions were initially intensive for newly on-boarded wards, then tapered as process measure compliance and documented phlebitis rates improved. Monthly analyses assessed both process compliance and phlebitis rates, with ward-level results communicated to nurses and managers through an Excel dashboard, supporting transparency, continuous improvement, and data-driven decision-making. This dashboard provided a comprehensive overview of each ward's performance, facilitating the identification of areas needing improvement. When performance gaps were detected, IPCNs collaborated with wards to address core issues and implement targeted strategies. The dashboard also highlighted wards with improved process or outcome measures, enabling teams to recognize and celebrate short-term successes. This approach supported a culture of continuous improvement while acknowledging positive achievements.

Outcome measures and analysis

Two primary outcome measures were used to assess the impact of hospital-wide implementation.

1.Incidence of PIVC-Related Phlebitis

Incidence of PIVC-Related Phlebitis

Phlebitis rates were primarily measured by extracting PIVC monitoring records from routine nursing documentation in the electronic medical record (EMR). These rates were analyzed by R software, employing ITSA with segmented regression, applying Newey-West standard errors to adjust for autocorrelation. Additionally, independent assessments were conducted through hospital-wide point prevalence surveys (PPS) of PIVC related phlebitis, performed biennially by IPCNs in 2019 and quarterly from 2020 onwards. Due to the limited number of data points, PPS rates were analyzed using run chart analysis.

2.Incidence of PIVC-Related HO-SAB

Incidence of PIVC-Related HO-SAB

PIVC-related HO-SAB was defined as S. aureus bacteremia occurring more than three days after hospital admission, accompanied by clinical documentation of PIVC-related infectious complications. These complications included documented phlebitis or phlebitis observed on inspection by an IPCN, the presence of pus at a PIVC site, or evidence of other localized infections at PIVC sites. Data were collected prospectively through clinical assessments and concurrent chart reviews of all HO-SAB cases. Monthly rates of PIVC-related HO-SAB, reported as infections per 10,000 patient days, were analyzed using ITSA with segmented regression. Newey-West standard errors were applied to account for autocorrelation. The implementation time point was defined as the successful completion of Phase 4. The impact of the Phase 4 implementation was also assessed by run-chart analysis, using aggregated quarterly PIVC-related HO-SAB rates.

Results

Incidence of PIVC-related phlebitis

PIVC-related phlebitis rates were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) system for the period January 2018 to April 2025. ITSA revealed a significant downward trend in phlebitis rates during Phase 2 (−0.1% per month, P = 0.05; see Figure 2). This declining trend persisted through Phase 3, followed by a plateau in Phase 4. Separately, run chart analysis showed that all post-intervention phlebitis rates following the implementation of Phase 4 were at or below the pre-intervention median, indicating a potential shift in process performance.

ITSA segmented regression analysis of hospital-wide phlebitis rates from electronic nursing records.

PPS data from Q1 2019 to Q1 2025 showed phlebitis rates ranging from 0.0% to 1.94%, with a mean of 0.87% (median 0.82%) during the pre-implementation period prior to Phase 4. The highest rates were observed in Q1–Q2 2022, immediately preceding the implementation of Phase 4. Following Phase 4, run chart analysis demonstrated ten consecutive data points below the pre-intervention median of 0.82%, with the average post-intervention rate falling to 0.21% (median 0.27%).

Incidence of PIVC-related hospital-onset S. aureus bacteremia

HO-SAB data were available from January 2018 to April 2025. The mean HO-SAB rate prior to intervention (January 2018–June 2019) was 0.61 cases/10,000 patient days (95% CI: 0.41–0.89) while the post-intervention rate (October 2022–April 2025) was 0.26 (95% CI: 0.17–0.38), corresponding to an incidence rate ratio of 0.42 (95%CI 0.25–0.72, P=0.002). When depicted as a run chart, the PIVC-related HO-SAB rates following hospital-wide implementation showed seven consecutive data points below the pre-intervention median (Figure 3), suggesting a potential reduction in bacteremia rates following the intervention. The segmented regression ITSA model demonstrated no significant change in either the level of HO-SAB (-0.080, P=0.51) or the rate of change of HO-SAB (-0.0040, P=0.49) following hospital-wide implementation in Phase 4 (Figure 4).

Discussion

Summary

Our study implemented a multi-faceted (bundled) intervention for PIVC insertion and maintenance, guided by the Model for Improvement framework. Segmented regression ITSA demonstrated a sustained decrease in phlebitis rates post-intervention, further supported by run chart and point prevalence survey data. Run chart analysis indicated a sustained decline in HO-SAB rate; however, segmented regression ITSA did not identify significant changes in HO-SAB level or trend. Overall, these findings suggest reduced PIVC-related infective complications associated with the intervention.

Our approach combined a comprehensive PIVC care bundle, strong stakeholder engagement, and a continuous improvement cycle involving training, monitoring, feedback, and collaboration with ward leaders. The project highlighted the importance of engaging nurses at multiple levels, linking quality initiatives directly to patient outcomes to foster meaningful change. Tailoring standardized PIVC site assessments to fit ward workflows was key for successful adoption, as effective protocols should be both practical and achievable, developed in partnership with frontline staff. Sharing real-time improvement data promoted ownership and positive momentum, while celebrating incremental successes reinforced a collective commitment to quality and patient safety.

Interpretation

Our project underscores the complexities of implementing quality improvement initiatives in real-world healthcare settings. External factors, notably the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic occurring between the pilot and hospital-wide phases, also influenced multiple variables such as bed occupancy, patient case-mix, and staffing levels. This variability contributed to fluctuations in recorded phlebitis rates during and after the pandemic.

Hospital-onset bloodstream infections (HO–BSI), particularly HO-SAB, are among the most clinically significant complications arising from PIVC infections [4]. In parallel with the reduction in observed phlebitis rates, our study demonstrated a reduction in PIVC-related HO-SAB rates following hospital-wide implementation of the PIVC bundle, with post-intervention rates decreasing to 0.26 cases per 10,000 inpatient days.

A comprehensive 2019 systematic review evaluated the impact of PIVC bundles on PIVC-related complications and BSI [9]. In the review, six of seven studies documented a reduction in PIVC-BSI rates following the implementation of a PIVC bundle. Rhodes et al. observed a reduction in HO-SAB from 0.39 to 0.14 per 10,000 occupied bed days following the implementation of staff education, improved documentation, and standardized equipment availability [10]. Salida et al. demonstrated that a long-term PIVC intervention significantly reduced both HO-BSI incidence and related mortality [11], supporting the effectiveness of PIVC bundles in lowering infection rates, consistent with our findings.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, PIVC-related phlebitis rates were extracted from routine nursing records, which may be subject to inter-observer variability and potential documentation inaccuracies. To address this, independent audits of phlebitis and PIVC care were conducted on every ward following bundle implementation, accompanied by a concurrent audit-feedback cycle. Additionally, quarterly point-prevalence surveys by independent observers (IPCNs) were performed throughout the implementation period to verify hospital-wide phlebitis trends. The concordance observed among EMR data, independent audits, and point-prevalence surveys supports the validity of the routine nursing records. Secondly, the implementation of a comprehensive bundle approach precludes the identification of individual components that may have contributed most significantly to the improvements observed in our primary outcomes. In their review, Ray-Barruel et al. also noted the heterogeneity of bundle components across their identified studies [9]. However, they also emphasized that engagement with relevant stakeholder groups and strong leadership support are crucial factors in the success and sustainability of any intervention. Finally, comprehensive monitoring of all bundle components was performed during the initial implementation phase for each clinical area but was not sustainable in the long term. Thereafter, key elements—clean and dry PIVC dressings and documentation of inspection each shift—were incorporated into routine monthly nursing audits which were performed both at ward level and independently by IPCNs.

Our study's PIVC care bundle was developed based on the best available evidence at the time, despite limited high-quality research guiding optimal PIVC insertion and maintenance care. A recent systematic review found that most infection prevention recommendations lack strong evidence, concluding that until further high-quality studies are available, “commonsense IPC practices” remain the primary strategy for preventing PIVC complications [12]. Our pragmatic intervention focused on essential elements such as aseptic insertion, securement, dressing, and regular site inspection. Since then, several comprehensive guidelines have been published with a much wider scope, including practitioner competency and patient engagement [[13], [14], [15]]. Nonetheless, the core components of our bundle remain closely aligned with these updated standards, supporting its continued relevance and effectiveness.

Conclusion

Our intervention study demonstrated that the implementation of a comprehensive PIVC bundle addressing insertion, maintenance care and monitoring contributed to a reduction in documented and independently observed phlebitis rates. Following the implementation of the quality improvement intervention, there was a corresponding reduction in PIV-related HO-SAB infection.
