Magnetic susceptibility as a proxy for soil contamination: cross-variogram analysis across contrasting land use areas
Authors: Piotr Fabijańczyk, Jarosław Zawadzki, Tadeusz Magiera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper presents a study on spatial cross-correlations between soil magnetic susceptibility and the concentration of selected elements in anthropogenically polluted soils. Determining spatial cross-correlations is crucial for accurate estimation and validation of pollution patterns identified via rapid magnetometric screening. Geostatistical methods based on cross-correlations can reduce the need for costly geochemical analyses and make it possible to analyze also the spatial aspect of phenomena that is not possible with classic metrics such as the Pearson correlation coefficient. The analysis was conducted in areas of southern and southwestern Poland with diverse land cover types and varying degrees of anthropogenic pressure. The study confirmed spatial correlations between magnetic and chemical measurements, especially in soils that have remained relatively undisturbed over time. The spherical model with a nugget effect best described the variograms and cross-variograms of magnetic susceptibility and concentrations of As, Cd, Cr, Co, Cu, Hg, Ni, Pb, Se, Sb, Tl, and Zn in soil. The results showed significant differences in cross-variogram parameters depending on land cover. Observed cross-correlation ranges differed depending on land type and were equal up to 550 m (average 280 m) in industrial and post-industrial areas, up to 300 m (average 170 m) in arable fields, and up to 145 m (average 67 m) in forests and parks.Keywords: Soil pollution, Soil magnetic susceptibility, Cross-variogram, Cross-correlations, Magnetometry, Geostatistics

Introduction

Soil magnetometry is frequently used to detect and assess potential soil pollution by potentially toxic elements (PTEs) emitted from industry, transportation, and other human activities (Karimi et al. 2017; Ayoubi et al. 2018a, b; Ayoubi and Mirsaidi 2018; Dankoub et al. 2012). Due to the presence of magnetic particles in anthropogenic pollutants, field-based soil magnetometry has been proposed as a fast and cost-effective alternative to expensive and time-consuming geochemical laboratory analyses (Ayoubi et al. 2018a, b; Karimi et al. 2011).

Numerous studies have confirmed statistically significant correlations between industrial pollution and soil magnetic susceptibility (κ) (Magiera and Szuszkiewicz 2025; Wang et al 2021; Kiruba et al. 2025; Blaha et al. 2008; Fürst et al. 2009; Reyes et al., 2010). In numerous magnetometric studies, the strength of the correlation between κ and PTE concentrations is typically evaluated using classical statistical measures such as Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients.

One of the main limitations of these traditional correlation coefficients is the small number of chemical samples, which may lead to statistically insignificant results. Additionally, interpreting scatterplots can be challenging, as distinguishing between outliers and values representing extreme pollution is difficult or even impossible. Furthermore, Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients do not account for the geographical coordinates of environmental data. However, spatial information is crucial for accurately interpreting field measurements. Therefore, relying solely on classical statistical measures for analyzing environmental data may result in oversimplified or misleading conclusions.

It is important to note that, although magnetic susceptibility is often strongly correlated with the concentration of many technogenic pollutants, low values of magnetic susceptibility do not necessarily indicate low pollution levels (Magiera et al. 2018).

Many studies on soil magnetic susceptibility have addressed spatial aspects, such as the relationship between magnetic susceptibility and distance from factories, ironworks, roads, waste dumps, and transportation routes like railways. Generally, magnetic susceptibility values decrease with increasing distance from pollution sources (Maity et al. 2021, 2022; Wang et al. 2021). Spatial magnetometric surveys have also been conducted in natural, semi-natural, and agricultural environments such as forests, peatlands, urban parks, meadows, and arable fields. In these settings, a typical goal is to delineate and interpret the distribution of soil pollution (Magiera and Zawadzki 2007; Naimi and Ayoubi 2013).

Thus, spatial aspects of in situ measurements are fundamentally important for developing soil magnetometry as an effective screening technique for soil pollution. Understanding the spatial variability of surface magnetic measurements is also essential for planning the measurement network, especially under field constraints, and for properly analyzing and validating the results. Consequently, geostatistical methods were proposed early in the development of soil magnetometry as particularly beneficial (Zawadzki and Fabijańczyk 2007; Zawadzki et al. 2015).

Although geostatistics was originally developed for mining and exploration in the late 1950 s and early 1960 s, it soon became clear that these methods were also valuable in environmental studies. Geostatistics offers numerous specialized tools for describing the spatial correlation of observed phenomena (Tajgardan et al. 2010). Unlike classical statistics, which rely on random variables, geostatistics introduces the concept of a regionalized variable, which exhibits both deterministic and stochastic properties. Geostatistical measures of spatial correlation, such as variograms and cross-variograms, are computed from a large number of data pairs, in contrast to classical correlation coefficients, which rely only on individual sample values. As a result, geostatistical analyses tend to be more robust and reliable.

In the past two decades, most studies examining the spatial correlations of magnetic susceptibility or PTE concentrations in soil have focused on spatial autocorrelation, often through case studies or separate analyses (Zawadzki et al. 2024, 2015; Zawadzki and Fabijańczyk 2007). However, systematic investigations of spatial cross-correlation between surface soil magnetic susceptibility and PTE concentrations in topsoil remain scarce. This is a critical issue in soil magnetometry, where surface magnetometric measurements are considered “soft” data in geostatistical terms, that is, less precise but more abundant and inexpensive than “hard” chemical measurements.

Magnetometry captures only the total magnetic signal of pollutants in soil. To determine the concentrations of As, Cd, Cr, Co, Cu, Hg, Ni, Pb, Se, Sb, Tl, and Zn in soil, chemical analyses were required. Integration of both measurement types can be achieved through cross-correlation analysis and geostatistical methods.

Evaluating cross-correlations between κ and PTEs is essential for accurately validating field magnetic susceptibility measurements. Precise knowledge of spatial correlation parameters enables assessment of the extent to which magnetometric measurements reflect actual PTE concentrations. Furthermore, understanding these cross-correlations is crucial for integrating magnetometric and chemical data using cokriging methods (Dowd and Pardo-Igúzquiza 2024; Zhang and Yang 2017; Webster and Oliver, 2000; Lark 2002; Rivoirard 2004). In addition to reducing sampling costs, cokriging leverages the spatial cross-correlation between primary and auxiliary variables, which can significantly improve estimation precision in heterogeneous environments, especially where pollutant distributions are influenced by multiple interacting factors. Despite their advantages, cokriging methods have rarely been used in practice due to limited software tools and theoretical complexity. Recently, however, the development of geostatistical software has made these methods increasingly accessible and attractive for soil pollution studies.

The main advantage of cokriging lies in its ability to improve the estimation of spatial distributions of soil pollution. Nevertheless, due to the challenges involved in identifying cross-correlations, its application remains relatively limited (Wang et al. 2024; Sohrabian and Tercan 2014; Chen et al. 2016; Golden et al. 2020).

This study aims to quantify the spatial cross-correlation between soil magnetic susceptibility and the concentration of selected potentially toxic elements (PTEs), including heavy metals, in the topsoil layer (0 to 30 cm) across areas subjected to varying degrees and types of industrial activities known to emit technogenic magnetic particles (TMPs), such as power plants, steelworks, and cement factories. By applying cross-variogram analysis, the research evaluates the extent to which magnetometric measurements can serve as a proxy for soil contamination. The selected sites represent a spectrum of land use and contamination history, including active industrial zones, legacy pollution sites, reclaimed lands, and regions affected by long-range atmospheric transport.

Materials and methods

Study areas

The study areas were located in southern and southwestern Poland within the Upper Silesian Industrial Area and the Jizera Mountains, as shown in Fig. 1. As previously demonstrated (Zawadzki et al. 2024), soil magnetometry is most effective in tree-covered areas, such as forests and parks, but for comparative purposes, selected measurements were also conducted in other types of areas. These sites were selected to ensure variability and diversity in the dominant types of pollution. They included areas near currently operating industrial facilities, locations affected by historical contamination, reclaimed sites, and regions influenced solely by long-range atmospheric transport. Given the importance of mineralogical and lithogenic factors in magnetic susceptibility measurements, the selection of study sites accounted for variability in soil types. As a result, dominant soil types varied substantially across the sites, including forest soils under coniferous and deciduous vegetation (A1, A3), mountain soils with poorly developed horizons (B1), and post-reclamation soils (C).

All study areas were grouped into three categories (Table 1): forested areas (group F): natural forests and urban parks; arable lands (group A): lands suitable for growing crops, typically plowed and cultivated for seasonal farming; and industrial (group I): areas subjected to significant and long-term anthropogenic pressure, past or present.

Two sites located in the Jizera Mountains (Fig. 2) were designated B1 and B2. Site B1 was located on Mt. Granicznik (870 m a.s.l.) and was adjacent to a large mountain meadow (B2). The western and southwestern slopes of Mt. Granicznik are subject to long-range transboundary pollution from Germany (Jänschwalde, Schwarze Pumpe, Boxberg), the Czech Republic (Chvaletice, Ledvice, Mělník, Poříčí), and Poland (Turów) (Magier et al., 2023), whereas the eastern slope is largely sheltered from such pollution.

Site B2 (Fig. 2) was located in a mountain meadow where glass production was active for approximately 100 years. While partially protected from long-range pollution by surrounding mountains, this site was historically affected by emissions from the former glassworks, which operated in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Site C (Fig. 2) was also located in the Upper Silesian Industrial Area, in the eastern part of Dąbrowa Górnicza, in close proximity to the Katowice Metallurgical Complex, which includes a steel plant and a coke plant. Most of the site was occupied by a commercial forest established on a former sandpit through biological reclamation. Residential areas were primarily located along the site’s periphery (Fig. 4). An artificial water reservoir, approximately 0.75 km2 in area, was located in the central part of the site.

Site D (Fig. 2), located in Piekary Śląskie, was situated approximately 5 km from the town of Bytom, also within the Upper Silesian Industrial Area. Measurements were conducted in an arable field adjacent to a public road on its southern side. Measurement network

In all study areas, measurements followed the procedure described in ISO 21226:2019 (2019). Soil samples were collected using the same soil probe from the same depth, as 30-cm-long soil cores. In each study area, at least 30 magnetic susceptibility (κ) sampling points were located. The spacing between sampling points in each area was determined based on previous magnetometric studies and was set to approximately one-tenth of the length of the study area. All sampling procedures in this study fallowed ISO 21226:2019, Soil quality—Guideline for the screening of soil polluted with toxic elements using soil magnetometry, which is the ISO standard for conducting soil magnetometric studies (ISO 21226:2019).

In locations situated near pollution sources, specifically B2, C, and D, additional randomly distributed samples were collected to capture short-range spatial correlations more accurately.

The coordinates of the sampling points were initially recorded using the WGS84 geographic coordinate system. Subsequently, in the laboratory, these coordinates were transformed into appropriate metric coordinate systems: Poland CS2000 zone 5 (EPSG:2176) and Poland CS2000 zone 6 (EPSG:2177).

Measurements of soil magnetic susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility (κ) was measured on the soil surface using a Bartington MS2D sensor according to the procedure described in ISO 21226:2019 (2019). During field measurements, volume magnetic susceptibility (κ) was recorded and expressed in units of [10–5 SI]. At each sampling location, 11 to 15 individual κ readings were taken on the surface of the soil within a circular area of 2 m radius. Each MS2D reading was georeferenced with precise coordinates. To better capture the spatial variability and correlation of κ, the individual readings at each sampling point were not averaged.

In addition, soil samples were collected using a Humax SH 300 soil corer equipped with plastic tubes of 35 mm diameter and length equal to 30 cm. The samples were air-dried, weighed, and placed in plastic containers with a volume of 10 cm3. Laboratory measurements of mass-specific magnetic susceptibility (χ) were then performed using a Bartington MS2B sensor. Mass-specific susceptibility was calculated by applying a density correction factor and expressed in units of [10–8 m3/kg].

Measurements of concentration of PTEs

Concentrations of As, Cd, Cr, Co, Cu, Hg, Ni, Pb, Se, Sb, Tl, and Zn were determined from subsamples taken from the collected soil cores. The selection of these elements was guided by their widespread environmental occurrence and toxicological significance. In particular, As, Cd, Cr, Pb, and Hg are classified as priority metals due to their high toxicity, persistence, and potential to cause systemic organ damage even at low exposure levels (Magiera and Szuszkiewicz 2025; Magiera and Wawer-Liszka 2025). Their inclusion in this study reflects concerns over long-term soil contamination and associated risks to human health and ecological integrity.

Soil subsamples were air-dried at room temperature and subsequently sieved through a 2-mm mesh. A 14 M HNO₃ digestion was applied to minimize the loss of volatile metal chlorides during sample preparation. Elemental concentrations were measured using high-resolution inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (HR-ICP-MS) with the ELEMENT 2 instrument (Thermo Electron). These measurements were made by the University of Trondheim.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures included analysis of reagent blanks, repeat testing, and the use of certified reference material (Soil GBW-07408). As, Cd, Cr, Co, Cu, Hg, Ni, Pb, Se, Sb, Tl, and Zn concentrations in reagent blanks were below detection limits. Accuracy was assessed by comparing measured concentrations to certified values, with recovery rates ranging from 60 to 82% for most elements. The measurement uncertainty was below 5% for As, Ba, Co, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Sb, Sr, Tl, V, and Zn; between 5 and 15% for Cd, Cr, and Hg; and exceeded 20% for Se. All results were corrected for blanks and calculated on a dry weight basis.

Geostatistical methods

Spatial cross-correlations between (κ) and PTE concentrations were analyzed using selected geostatistical measures (Isaaks and Srivastava 1998). The first measure that was used was a cross-variogram, γκ,PTE, which was calculated using the formula:

where N is the number of pairs of κ and PTE values measured at sample points separated by h vector.

All cross-variograms were calculated and modeled to fit a linear model of coregionalization (LMC) (Goulard and Voltz 1992). In this study, the LMC was calculated for two variables. One of them was the value of κ and the second was the concentration of selected PTE. To fulfill the assumptions of the LMC, variograms of κ and PTEs and cross-variograms between these variables were modeled using the same basic model, namely the spherical model with the nugget effect. The selection of the spherical model was based on the results of previous studies related to soil magnetometry, where the spherical model was found to be one of the most suitable models to model variograms of soil magnetic susceptibility (Zawadzki et al. 2015, 2024).

To fit the LMC, it was necessary to model simultaneously two auto-variograms, namely κ and PTE ones, and the cross-variogram between these two variables.

Nugget effect represents the discontinuity at the origin of the variogram. It accounts for measurement error and spatial variability at distances smaller than the sampling interval. A non-zero nugget indicates either microscale variation or observational noise.

Sill is the plateau value reached by a variogram at larger distances. It reflects the total variance of the spatial process, combining both structured spatial variation and the nugget effect.

Correlation range is the distance at which the variogram reaches the sill. Beyond this range, spatial correlation between sample points becomes negligible, and values are considered statistically independent.

Results and discussion

For each land cover group, the discussion of results began with an analysis of the descriptive statistics for κ and PTE concentrations. Supplementary box-and-whisker plots illustrating κ measurements for individual study areas were also included. Subsequently, the results of variogram and cross-variogram analyses for κ and PTEs were presented and discussed. In total, 273 auto- and cross-variograms were calculated, 39 for each of the seven study areas. Analysis was conducted separately for three, previously described groups: forested areas (group F), arable lands (group A), and industrial areas (group I).

The average κ values observed in forest A1 and park A3 were 50.90 × 10–5 SI and 49.60 × 10–5 SI, respectively, suggesting moderate soil pollution (Vodyanitskii and Shoba 2015) (Table 2).

In the forested mountain area B1, κ values were significantly lower compared to those in A1 and A3. The average κ value in B1 was 12.40 × 10−5 SI (Table 2), indicating relatively low soil pollution. Similar values for mountain soils in the Jizera Mountains have been previously reported (Magiera et al. 2019).

The average PTE concentrations measured in forested area B1 were lower than those measured in A1 and A3; however, maximum concentrations of As (37.30 mg/kg), Hg (0.80 mg/kg), and Se (5.90 mg/kg) suggest that these elements may be attributed to long-range atmospheric pollution originating from Germany and the Czech Republic (Magiera et al. 2023).

In area B1, the number of chemical samples was also low (n = 10); however, only the experimental variogram of selenium concentration was modeled using a pure nugget effect. The remaining auto-variograms were fitted with a spherical model, exhibiting a range of approximately 100 m

In the forested area B1 of the Jizera Mountains, the cross-variograms (Table 4) between soil concentrations of As, Cr, Se, Tl, and magnetic susceptibility (κ) indicated weak spatial correlations, even over distances of several tens of meters, which was typical of the correlation range observed for other cross-variograms in the mountain meadow (B2—Jizera mountains). Weak spatial correlation likely resulted from uniformly low magnetic and geochemical values, which reflect background levels associated with the local granitic and gneissic lithology of the Jizera region. This homogeneity reduced spatial contrast and thus weakened the correlation.

In contrast, the concentrations of Cr and Tl exhibited strong spatial correlation and good spatial continuity, among the most pronounced for all analyzed elements in this area. The variogram for Se showed moderate spatial correlation, like those observed for As and Hg.

Soil magnetic susceptibility and elements concentrations in areas from group I—industrial areas

Very high values of magnetic susceptibility (κ), reaching 501.00 × 10−5 and 205.00 × 10−5, were observed in area C, located near the Katowice Metallurgical Complex, and in mountain meadow (area B2), respectively. As previously reported in the literature, such elevated κ values may indicate potential soil pollution. As shown in Fig. 3, the distribution of κ was skewed, with a high frequency of extreme values, suggesting significant anthropogenic contamination.

The substantial difference in average κ values can be attributed to several factors. Area C is still subject to active anthropogenic pressure, whereas B2 was primarily exposed to industrial activity in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with no current industrial operations. Additionally, the elevated κ values in the upper soil layers of area B2 are partly due to the presence of Technosols formed from historical glass industry waste and ashes from local heating (Magiera et al. 2019). The main reason, however, lies in the types of pollution emitted by the ironworks and glassworks. Previous studies have shown that industrial dust from steel production contains significant amounts of Fe and other strongly magnetic elements (Magiera et al. 2018). Consequently, fly ash from the Katowice Metallurgical Complex (area C) exhibits stronger magnetic properties compared to pollutants from the former glassworks in area B2.

Soils near the Katowice Metallurgical Complex were heavily contaminated, reflected by high κ values. As indicated in Table 5, elevated concentrations of Pb and Zn were recorded, with maximum values exceeding several hundred ppm. In contrast, area B2, with an average κ below 14 × 10−5, showed relatively low PTE concentrations. Only Cr, Ni, and As concentrations in B2 exceeded those near the metallurgical complex, attributed to contamination from waste deposits located on the mountain meadow (area B2) (Magiera et al. 2019).

The two areas also differed in sampling density. As shown in Table 1, only 10 chemical samples were collected in mountain meadow B2, whereas nearly 50 samples were collected near the metallurgical complex. The limited number of samples in B2 hindered the calculation of auto- and cross-variograms. For instance, it was not possible to model cross-variograms for Pb and Co, while variograms for Zn, Pb, Hg, and Co were modeled using only a pure nugget effect (Table 6). Consequently, fitting a linear model of coregionalization (LMC) for these pollutants was not feasible.

Most cross-variograms between κ and PTEs were determined, and their parameters satisfied the assumptions of the LMC. Correlation ranges and cross-correlations between κ and PTEs in area C typically extended over several hundred meters. Only As and Hg concentrations showed weak spatial correlation with κ, precluding LMC modeling between these elements and κ.

Soil magnetic susceptibility and elements concentrations in areas from group A—arable areas

In the arable field located in area D, high values of magnetic susceptibility (κ) were observed, with an average κ of 136 × 10–5. This value was significantly higher than the average κ measured in area A2, which was 38 × 10–5. The elevated κ in area D can be attributed to its proximity to metallurgical heaps containing Pb–Zn processing wastes. Due to the absence of physical barriers such as buildings or trees, industrial dust from the adjacent source was evenly deposited on the soil surface. Consequently, high concentrations of potentially toxic elements (PTEs), especially Pb and Zn, were measured in area D, with concentrations exceeding 1000 mg/kg.

In contrast, the second arable field (area A2) exhibited Pb, Cd, and Zn concentrations approximately ten times lower than those in area D. Concentrations of other PTEs, including Tl, As, Cu, Ni, Hg, and Sb, did not differ significantly between the two fields, while Co, Cr, and Se concentrations were comparable (Table 7). Due to the relatively homogeneous spatial distribution of PTE concentrations in both arable fields, it was possible to reliably estimate the parameters of their spatial autocorrelation. As shown in Table 8, the typical range of correlation for PTE concentrations was approximately 250 m in area D and 100 m in area A2. The auto-variograms of κ measured in both areas exhibited similar correlation ranges, around 200 m, consistent with the ranges observed for PTEs.

However, the two arable fields differed markedly in terms of the cross-correlation between κ and PTE concentrations. In area A2, which had been recently plowed, cross-variograms indicated a negative spatial correlation between κ and PTEs. The negative sill values of these cross-variograms suggested an increase in spatial correlation with distance, an unusual pattern for environmental data. This phenomenon was explained by comparing the spatial distributions of κ and PTEs in the field: the western part of the field had κ values roughly half those of the eastern part, while PTE concentrations were higher in the western part. The elevated PTE levels in the low-κ western area are likely the result of recent plowing to a depth of approximately 25 cm, which may have disturbed the original spatial patterns. However, attributing this negative spatial correlation solely to recent plowing may be overly simplistic. While plowing is common across agricultural soils, the observed pattern in A2 is more plausibly linked to multiple emission sources. In addition to deposition from the nearby Łaziska smelter and power plant, field observations suggest a localized wind-driven redistribution of slag material from embankments between fields, which may have contributed to the anomalous spatial structure.

Conversely, in area D, the cross-variograms suggested strong positive spatial correlations between κ and PTE concentrations, with a well-defined correlation range of about 250 m. The arable field in area D is located adjacent to Pb–Zn metallurgical waste heaps rich in sulfides of As, Cd, Hg, Co, Cu, Ni, Se, and Zn. Weathering of these sulfides leads to continuous deposition of contaminated dust onto the surface of the soil. As a result, despite ongoing cultivation activities, significant spatial correlations between κ and PTEs were observed.

Cross-correlation between κ and PTEs in all areas

As shown in Fig. 4, the correlation ranges of magnetic susceptibility (κ), concentrations of potentially toxic elements (PTEs), and their cross-correlation ranges were longest in industrial areas, shorter in arable areas, and shortest in forested areas. This pattern is consistent with previous studies conducted in the USIR region (Magiera and Zawadzki 2007).

However, it is important to note the variability in the range of correlation within each land cover group—industrial, forest, and arable areas. In industrial areas, the longest correlation ranges (400–600 m) were observed primarily in area C, which is the largest study area. In contrast, in the other industrial area B2, correlation ranges were up to just over 200 m, longer than those observed in the forested areas A1, A3, and B1, and comparable to those in arable area D. Notably, area D is located directly adjacent to metallurgical waste heaps from Pb–Zn ore processing, which results in significant anthropogenic influence.

Regarding the cross-variograms, zero nugget-to-sill ratio values corresponded to zero nugget effects. A similar pattern was observed for the variograms of PTE concentrations. The highest nugget-to-sill ratios were found in the spatial variability of magnetic susceptibility. This is likely because soil magnetic susceptibility measurements belong to the category of so-called soft measurements in geostatistics, which are subject to greater uncertainty at small spatial scales and higher variability within individual measurement points. These factors are captured by the nugget effect in variogram modeling.

Discussion of study limitations, uncertainty, and future applications

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. In soil pollution research, where the number of samples is often limited, the constraints of geostatistical modeling must be recognized. For some elements and areas, sparse data distribution and weak spatial variability hindered the accurate modeling of auto-variograms.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate cross-correlations using variogram and cross-variogram analysis, as these methods uniquely capture spatial dependence in soil contamination studies. However, the relatively low sampling density for certain elements and the weak spatial variability observed in some areas limited the robustness of auto-variogram modeling. Consequently, cross-variogram modeling required assumptions informed by prior experience and knowledge of the study area, rather than being based solely on empirical data. This inevitably introduced a degree of uncertainty into the Linear Coregionalization Model (LMC) and may have weakened the reliability of the results.

Another limitation arises from the scope of the analyses. While classical statistical measures such as Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients could provide additional global context, they do not account for spatial coordinates and therefore cannot capture distance-dependent relationships. The focus on geostatistical methods reflects the study’s main aim, but it also means that the discussion does not include a systematic comparison with classical statistics. Future research could integrate both approaches more explicitly, providing a multi-scale perspective on soil contamination patterns.

These limitations are not unique to this study but are common in environmental geostatistics, particularly in research on elemental concentrations in soils. Previous studies have also highlighted the challenges of balancing data availability, spatial resolution, and methodological complexity when modeling contamination patterns in heterogeneous environments. In this context, the findings presented here should be interpreted as an important step toward understanding spatial dependencies in soil contamination, while recognizing that denser sampling networks and complementary statistical approaches would further strengthen the robustness of future analyses.

A further limitation is the scarcity of recent literature directly addressing the spatial relationship between soil magnetic susceptibility and heavy metals. This makes it more difficult to situate the findings within a contemporary framework, but at the same time underlines the importance of continuing such studies. The results should therefore be viewed as a contribution to filling this gap and as a step toward strengthening the role of magnetic proxies in soil contamination research.

Finally, in some areas (A3 and B1), the number of chemical samples was lower due to the small size of the study site (A3) or difficult mountainous conditions (B1). While this reduced the resolution of chemical data, it is partly consistent with the concept of soil magnetometry, where sparse chemical sampling is complemented by more extensive magnetic susceptibility measurements. In addition, some cross-variograms displayed negative sill values, reflecting inverse correlations between magnetic susceptibility and the concentration of certain elements. Sample sparsity also has important implications for the generalizability and robustness of the models. Limited sampling density reduces the ability to capture fine-scale spatial variability, which in turn constrains the extent to which results can be extrapolated to broader areas or different environmental settings. This limitation highlights the need for cautious interpretation of spatial patterns, as models derived from sparse data may be more sensitive to local anomalies and less stable under alternative modeling assumptions.

Future research could address all of these challenges by designing denser sampling networks, integrating multi-source datasets (e.g., geophysical surveys, remote sensing, or proximal soil sensing), and applying advanced geostatistical approaches. Specific applications include the development of cost-effective monitoring strategies for contaminated sites, the refinement of risk assessment frameworks that incorporate spatial uncertainty, and the use of magnetic susceptibility as a proxy in large-scale soil quality assessments.

Conclusions

As the results demonstrate, despite the relatively small number of chemical samples in most of the studied areas, it was possible to determine and model cross-variograms between soil magnetic susceptibility (κ) and PTE concentrations that satisfied the requirements of the linear model of coregionalization. Our findings indicate that the spherical model with a nugget effect provided the best fit for both auto-variograms and cross-variograms of κ and PTE concentrations in soil.

Significant differences were observed in the parameters of cross-variograms between κ and selected PTE concentrations across areas subject to varying levels of pollution. The cross-variograms revealed clearly defined ranges of spatial correlation: the shortest ranges, on the order of a few tens of meters, were found in forested areas and parks, whereas in arable fields, the typical correlation range extended up to several hundred meters. Even longer correlation ranges were noted in areas affected by dust pollution originating from the weathering of sulfide-rich metallurgical waste heaps.

These results confirm that spatial cross-correlation analysis is an effective tool for interpreting the spatial distribution of anthropogenic soil pollution and for validating magnetic susceptibility measurements with geochemical data. Although geochemical analyses remain necessary, the ability to spatially integrate magnetic susceptibility and geochemical measurements offers promising new opportunities for efficient field magnetometry in environmental monitoring.
