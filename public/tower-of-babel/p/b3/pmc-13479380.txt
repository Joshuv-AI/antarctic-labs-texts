Immigration-Related Sociopolitical Stress, Mental Health, and Tobacco Use Among Mexican American College Students
Authors: Seth J Schwartz, C Nathan Marti, Liza Talavera-Garza, Caroline E North, Qingua Yang, Dhiraj Murthy, Srishty Arora, Anna V Wilkinson, Alexandra Loukas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSociopolitical stress associated with the policies and rhetoric coming from recent U.S. presidential administrations is of particular concern vis-à-vis Hispanic individuals, who have been disproportionately targeted for detention and deportation. Such stress may be associated with symptoms of depression and anxiety, and ultimately with tobacco use. However, there is a paucity of research examining how sociopolitical stress is associated with mental health problems and substance use among Hispanic emerging adults. Accordingly, the present study was designed to examine the extent to which, within a sample of Mexican American college students, sociopolitical stress would be linked with tobacco use indirectly through symptoms of depression and anxiety. A cross-sectional sample of 997 Mexican American college students in Texas completed measures of sociopolitical stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety, and cigarette and e-cigarette use. More than 75% of participants agreed/strongly agreed with each of the sociopolitical stress items, suggesting high levels of sociopolitical stress within the sample. Structural equation models indicated that sociopolitical stress was associated directly with depressive symptoms (β = 0.16, p < .001) and indirectly with both cigarette (b = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.002, 0.015) and e-cigarette (b = 0.02, 95% CI = 0.005, 0.033) use and sociopolitical stress was associated directly with anxiety (β = 0.16, p < .001) and indirectly with both cigarette (b = 0.01, 95% CI = 0.001, 0.014) and e-cigarette (β = 0.02, 95% CI = 0.008, 0.037) use. Intervention and policy work is needed to offset the associations of sociopolitical stress associated with recent U.S. administrations vis-à-vis mental health symptoms and tobacco use among Hispanic populations, including college students.Keywords: Sociopolitical stress, Depressive symptoms, Anxiety, Tobacco use, Mexican American, College students

Introduction

Since the 2016 United States (U.S.) presidential election, sociopolitical events and conditions have created considerable stress for Hispanic populations in the country [1]. Changes in immigration policy have introduced concerns for both foreign- and U.S.-born Hispanics. The majority of U.S.-born Hispanic children, adolescents, and emerging adults have at least one immigrant parent, and more than a quarter have close family members who are undocumented [1]. Therefore, stress related to immigration raids and other enforcement activities often impacts the health and well-being of whole immigrant communities – not simply unauthorized individuals [2].

Although the stated intention of immigration enforcement is to identify and deport people who are in the country illegally, a potentially unintended consequence involves increased fear and anxiety among individuals who are legally present and not originally targeted for deportation [3]. Immigration enforcement may also lead to impaired mental health [4], and elevated risk for substance use [5], among U.S.-born Hispanics whose parents have been detained, deported, or are at risk of either. It is therefore essential to examine the extent to which immigration enforcement causes distress among Hispanic individuals.

Research suggests strong positive associations between immigration enforcement and sociopolitical stress – concerns about unwarranted targeting of and mistreatment toward a given immigrant or ethnic minority group – among Hispanic youth [6]. Indeed, sociopolitical stress represents one component of cultural stress theory, which holds that various dimensions of exclusion and acculturative pressure tend to co-occur and to predict mental health problems and substance use [7, 8]. The overwhelming majority of Hispanic youth, including those in undocumented families, are U.S.-born [9]. Hispanics comprise 20% of the U.S. population but 25% of the child, adolescent, and young adult population [10] – suggesting young people as an appropriate group with whom to examine the unintended, indirect consequences of immigration-related sociopolitical stress on Hispanic individuals who are legally present in the United States. However, in 2025, the U.S. Supreme Court issued an emergency ruling that one’s apparent race or ethnicity can be used as a relevant factor in stopping and detaining people who immigration officials reasonably suspect may be in the country illegally. As a result, many Hispanic emerging adults – who may be among the age and ethnic groups most likely to be stopped and detained – have become increasingly fearful [11]. Therefore, Hispanic emerging adults represent an ideal population with which to investigate the association of sociopolitical stress with mental health problems and substance use.

The majority of research on immigration-related sociopolitical stress has been conducted with populations directly affected by immigration enforcement actions – such as undocumented individuals [12] and children of undocumented parents [5]. Some work has connected immigration-related sociopolitical stress with mental health symptoms among Hispanic youth. For example, Montero-Zamora, et al. [13] found that, among Hispanic adolescents in Los Angeles and Miami, sociopolitical stress was associated with depressive symptoms and anxiety, and indirectly with positive attitudes toward alcohol and drug use.

Although similar research with the Hispanic emerging adult population remains scant, it may be worthwhile, to consult more general research literature on culturally related stressors, mental health symptoms, and substance use among Hispanic emerging-adult college students. This population is often characterized by identity instability, where such instability may be exacerbated by culturally related stressors [14] and associated with depressive symptoms, anxiety, and substance use [15]. Further, culturally related stressors [16] have been linked with substance use among Hispanic emerging adults. Many Hispanic emerging adults experience elevated levels of culturally related stressors, especially during presidential administrations that demonize Hispanics and scapegoat them for the country’s problems [17]. Moreover, although college students of color often experience a range of pressures – including worries about grades, relationships, and future plans – these pressures are often worsened in the presence of culturally related stressors [18].

Immigration-Related Sociopolitical Stress, Mental Health, and Tobacco Use

Tobacco products, both electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) and combustible cigarettes, are among the most commonly used substances among college students and other emerging adult populations [19]. Culturally related stressors, such as sociopolitical stress, often compromise mental health [7]. In turn, higher levels of stress, depression, and anxiety are often associated with e-cigarette and cigarette use among college-aged emerging adults [20]. As a result, it is possible that Hispanic emerging adults might use tobacco products, specifically e-cigarettes and cigarettes, at least in part, as a coping mechanism, known as the self-medication hypothesis, for distress they experience as a result of immigration-related sociopolitical stress. Specifically, self-medication refers to using substances to offset or cope with stressful or upsetting experiences. The stress-distress model [21] also provides important insights – it holds that difficult experiences may produce symptoms of depression and anxiety, which may then lead to maladaptive coping mechanisms such as substance use. Although Hispanic college students generally use tobacco at rates comparable to those for Whites [22], the health consequences of tobacco use are more severe for Hispanics than for Whites [23].

Two components of the self-medication hypothesis and stress-distress model have received empirical support in the literature: immigration-related sociopolitical stress is linked with increased symptoms of depression and anxiety [6], which are further associated with tobacco use [24]. Hispanic emerging adults have been shown to use tobacco in response to culturally related stressors [25]. However, these two pieces have not been tested together, which limits our understanding of the indirect mechanisms through which sociopolitical stress is associated with e-cigarette and cigarette use. Further, because anti-immigration rhetoric has escalated following the 2024 U.S. presidential election and the subsequent inauguration [26], the effects of such rhetoric may be even more pronounced than during earlier presidential administrations.

The Present Study

To address these gaps in the literature, we examined the self-medication and stress-distress hypothesis that immigration-related sociopolitical stress would be indirectly related to e-cigarette and cigarette use through symptoms of depression and anxiety within a sample of Mexican American college students in Texas. Mexican American college students are an ideal population for testing these associations for three primary reasons. First, among U.S. Hispanic groups, Mexican Americans have been particularly stereotyped as undocumented and as threatening to U.S. citizens [17]. This is especially true in Texas, which originally seceded from Mexico and where individuals of Mexican descent are often discriminated against [27]. Second, the representation of Hispanics, including Mexican Americans, among U.S. college students has more than doubled from 7% in 2000 to 15% in 2020 [28]. In Texas, which has the second-largest Hispanic population of any U.S. state – and where more than 80% of Hispanics are of Mexican descent – more than 40% of all students at public universities in the state are Hispanic [29]. Third, individuals of Mexican descent are often stereotyped as undocumented invaders [17], and Mexican Americans may fear deportation even as U.S. citizens [30].

It is possible that recent U.S. administrations would create immigration-related sociopolitical stress for Mexican American students – regardless of their citizenship status or place of birth [31]. We would also hypothesize that this sociopolitical stress would be associated with increased depressive and anxiety symptoms, which in turn would be related to cigarette and e-cigarette use.

Methods

Procedure and Study Design

The present cross-sectional study utilizes data from the Vaping, Acculturation, and Media Study (Project VAMoS), a mixed-methods, longitudinal investigation designed to explore the influence of social media and acculturation on tobacco use among Mexican American college students in Texas. As part of VAMoS, we recruited a statewide sample of Mexican American students from six Texas public universities, including four in the three largest cities (Dallas-Fort Worth, Houston, and San Antonio) and two near the Mexican border (Brownsville and El Paso). Undergraduate students from participating universities received an invitation email detailing the study and including a link to a short eligibility screener. To be eligible, students needed to be (1) 18 to 29 years old, (2) currently enrolled in a degree-seeking undergraduate program (part-time or full-time), and (3) self-identify as being of Mexican American, Tejano, Tejana, Chicano, or Chicana heritage. Participants provided electronic informed consent prior to taking part in the study. Participants were advised that their participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw at any time. We collected data from June 4-September 1, 2025, roughly six months after the presidential inauguration. All aspects of the study, including survey administration, data collection, and incentive distribution were conducted online.

Project VAMoS is a multi-wave study; the baseline survey (wave 1) was administered in Fall 2023, with follow-up surveys administered in Spring 2024 (wave 2), Fall 2024 (wave 3), and Summer 2025 (wave 4). Data for the present study were taken from wave 4, roughly six months after the presidential inauguration, and the sociopolitical stress measure was added to the assessment battery beginning at this wave. Participants received a $25 e-gift card for their involvement in each wave. The Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) at the authors’ institutions approved all study procedures. Additional information regarding Project VAMoS’ methods is available elsewhere [32].

Participants

The present sample consisted of 997 Mexican American college students who completed the wave 4 survey (66.8% of the original baseline sample). Socio-demographic characteristics of the 997 participants in the present study are reported in Table 1. Participants’ mean age was 22.14 years (SD = 2.16), 29.0% were male, 35.6% attended a university on the Mexico-U.S. border, and students reported a subjective socioeconomic status (SES) of 4.85 (SD = 1.52, range = 1–10]. Among participants, 9.1% were born outside the U.S., 45.5% had two parents born outside the U.S., 21.2% had one parent born outside and one born in the U.S., and 24.1% had two parents born in the U.S. Wolf et al. [33] note that a sample size of 450 is adequate for most structural equation models (SEM), suggesting that our sample size provided adequate power.

Measures

Demographics were assessed in terms of age in years, sex (0 = “female,” 1 = “male”), which university the participant attended, and generation status (1 = “participant was born outside the U.S.”, 2 = “participant born in U.S. and both parents were born outside the U.S.”, 2.5 = “participant born in U.S. and one parent born in U.S.”, and 3 = “participant born in U.S. and both parents were born in the U.S.”).

Past 30-day cigarette use was assessed with the question, “On how many of the past 30 days did you smoke a cigarette?” Responses range from 0 to 30 days.

Past 30-day e-cigarette use was assessed with the question, “On how many of the past 30 days did you use an e-cigarette to vape nicotine?” Responses range from 0 to 30 days. These past-30-days frequency items are recommended by experts in tobacco control among youth [34].

Sociopolitical stress was assessed using four items from the Multidimensional Inventory of Cultural Stress [13]. Items on this scale asked, “With the current presidential administration,” followed by evaluations of four items pertaining to increased fear in the respondent, worries that Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) and other similar programs will be ended, fear that one’s friends or family members will be deported, and worries about attacks against Hispanics. Participants responded to each item using a 5-point scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree; α = 0.86).

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) instrument [35] (α = 0.91), which includes nine items assessing the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced over the preceding 2 weeks. For example, “Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by any of the following problems… little interest or pleasure in doing things, feeling down, depressed, or hopeless, etc.” (0 = not at all, 3 = nearly every day). The individual item scores are summed to yield a total score ranging from 0 to 27.

Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the GAD-7 (α = 0.93) [36], which contains items measuring the frequency of anxiety symptoms over the 2 weeks prior to assessment. For example, “Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by … feeling nervous, anxious or on edge, not being able to stop or control worrying, etc.” The response options for each item range from 0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day. A severity score index was calculated by summing the responses for each item, resulting in a possible range of 0 to 21.

American identity affirmation (covariate) was assessed at Wave 4 using the American Identity Measure (AIM) [37]. The AIM includes seven items (e.g., “I have a clear sense of what it means to be American”). Participants responded to each item on a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), and scores were averaged across items (α = 0.93). We controlled for American identity affirmation to account for the extent to which the associations of experiences of sociopolitical stress with depressive symptoms and anxiety might have been offset by national identification [38].

Socioeconomic status (covariate) was assessed using the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status [39], where participants select one of ten rungs on a ladder (1 = worst off [lowest income and education], 10 = best off [highest income and education]), to represent their family of origin’s income, education, and jobs. This measure has been used as a subjective proxy for SES among Hispanics [40]. Socioeconomic stress is essential to control for when studying culturally related stressors because individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds often report higher levels of these stressors [41].

Data Analytic Plan

Our first step of analysis was to compute descriptive statistics for the sociopolitical stress items – that is, the percentage of participants who indicated “agree” or “strongly agree” in response to each of the four items. Doing so is important because it tells us how many participants were concerned about the current U.S. administration and about its effects on Hispanic communities.

Second, the sem function in the R lavaan package, version 0.6.20 [42] in R version 4.5.1, was used to test the hypothesis that immigration related sociopolitical stress would be indirectly associated with e-cigarette and cigarette use through symptoms of depression and anxiety. Model parameters were estimated using SEM with full information maximum likelihood (FIML). Variances and intercepts for all exogenous variables were included in the model to retain cases with missing values, thus utilizing all available data and providing less biased estimates than listwise deletion under the assumption that data are missing at random (MAR) [43].

We fit four separate mediation models; two for cigarette use and two for e-cigarette use. Because depressive symptoms and anxiety were strongly intercorrelated, we estimated a separate model for each outcome (a) with depressive symptoms as a predictor and (b) with anxiety as a predictor (see Fig. 1). Thus, we examined the indirect associations of sociopolitical stress with past 30-day e-cigarette or cigarette use via anxiety or depressive symptoms. Mediation models were specified such that depressive symptoms and anxiety were regressed on sociopolitical stress and all covariates, and past 30-day e-cigarette and cigarette use were regressed on depressive symptoms and anxiety (mediators) and on sociopolitical stress. All intervening and outcome variables were natural-log-transformed to reduce skewness and stabilize variance; a constant of 1 was added prior to transformation to accommodate zero values (zero does not have a natural logarithm). Following recommendations from MacKinnon [44] and Jose [45], we used bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals (10,000 bootstrap samples) to estimate standard errors and their corresponding bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals to account for the asymmetrical distribution of the indirect effect estimate. Within each model, we controlled for age (in years, centered at 18), sex (0 = “female,” 1 = “male”), attending a university on the Texas-Mexico border (0 = “no” and 1 = “yes”), generation status (1 = “participant was born outside the U.S.”, 2 = “participant born in U.S. and both parents were born outside the U.S.”, 2.5 = “participant born in U.S. and one parent born in U.S.”, and 3 = “participant born in U.S. and both parents were born in the U.S.”), SES, and American identity affirmation.

Study Models

It is important to address potential issues involving estimating multiple models to test our study hypothesis. A common rule of thumb suggests that alpha levels should be adjusted to account for the number of hypothesis tests to reduce Type I error risk [46]. However, recent methodological sources [47] have suggested that such corrections are only necessary when several tests are conducted and rejection of any single null hypothesis is sufficient to support the research hypothesis. This combination of conditions does not apply here. First, we conducted separate analyses to evaluate separate pathways between sociopolitical stress and tobacco use, where we were substantively interested in all four pathways independently – the significance of any one pathway did not affect our interest in the significance of the other pathways. Second, the practical limitation that depressive symptoms and anxiety were highly correlated, due to comorbidity between the two sets of symptoms [48], precluded including both as intervening variables within a SEM approach [49]. Further, use of combustible cigarettes and use of e-cigarettes represent distinct behaviors with different developmental trajectories [50], and as such, we included them as outcomes in separate models. For these reasons, we did not adjust the Type I error risk for the number of tests conducted.

Examination of the indirect association models indicated that all direct and indirect paths were statistically significant after adjusting for covariates (see Table 2): sociopolitical stress was associated with depressive (β = 0.16, p < .001) and anxiety (β = 0.16, p < .001) symptoms, and in turn, depressive and anxiety symptoms were associated with cigarette and e-cigarette use (βs ranged from 0.07 to 0.12, all p’s < 0.05). Significant indirect path coefficients ranged from 0.01 to 0.02.

Discussion

The present study was designed to examine the extent to which sociopolitical stress is indirectly associated with tobacco use, through depressive symptoms and anxiety, among Mexican American college students. Texas is an ideal state in which to conduct the present study, because it has the largest border with Mexico and the second largest Mexican American population in the United States [51]. Further, given that Mexico is the largest country of origin for undocumented individuals in the U.S [52]., Mexican Americans represent an ideal population to examine the effects of sociopolitical stress associated with recent U.S. presidential administrations. As we detail below, our results support these prior findings.

Consistent with the self-medication hypothesis [53], our results suggest that sociopolitical stress is linked with increased symptoms of both depression and anxiety, which in turn served as intervening mechanisms in the association between sociopolitical stress and tobacco use (both e-cigarette and combustible cigarette use). Also noteworthy was the level of sociopolitical stress (Mean = 4.11 on a five-point scale), which reflects high degrees of concern regarding the policies of the new administration toward Hispanic individuals. Although more than 90% of our sample was U.S.-born, participants nonetheless expressed a considerable amount of discontent regarding the current sociopolitical climate – perhaps because nearly 70% of participants had at least one Mexican-born parent. Indeed, as Pinedo and Escobar [4] have reported, even U.S.-born Hispanics may be adversely affected by anti-immigrant rhetoric and policies. Our results suggest that the Mexican American population in Texas, including individuals born in the U.S., is quite concerned with the actions of the current administration, and perhaps those of other recent administrations as well. Even those individuals who are U.S. citizens, and whose families are not directly at risk for deportation, may be threatened at the group level. Social identity and self-categorization theories [54] hold that threats to the group (Mexican Americans or Hispanics in this case) may be just as salient as threats to the individual person. The actions of recent U.S. presidential administrations may therefore be emotionally damaging to the emotional and behavioral health of Mexican American college students, regardless of any individual student’s citizenship or legal status.

Moreover, given the not-inconsequential risk for tobacco use among college students [19, 55], the possibility that the Mexican American students in our sample were using e-cigarettes and/or cigarettes to cope with sociopolitical stress – and with the depressive and anxiety symptoms they experienced as a result – is alarming, aligning with the self-medication hypothesis and with the stress-distress model. Unger and Falcon [23] note that tobacco use among Hispanic youth has been increasing, and that the health consequences of tobacco use in this population are more severe for Hispanics than for Whites. As a result, sociopolitical stress may contribute to tobacco-related health disparities for Mexican American students, as well as for other Hispanic subgroups.

Limitations and Future Directions

Our results should be interpreted in light of at least five important limitations. First, the present study was cross-sectional. It therefore is not possible to draw directional or causal conclusions based on the current findings. Testing indirect effects with cross-sectional designs may also introduce bias, because it is not possible to control for earlier levels of intervening and outcome variables [56]. The association between tobacco use and mental health may be bidirectional [57], but a longitudinal design is needed to examine this possibility. Second, the students in our sample were attending major universities in the University of Texas and University of Houston systems. Students attending smaller universities and colleges were not included, and we do not know how they might have differed from the participants we surveyed. However, students attended institutions on the border (k = 2) and in the largest cities in Texas (k = 4), providing at least some geographic diversity within the sample. Despite this diversity, our findings should be generalized only to Mexican American college students, and not to Mexican American emerging adults not attending colleges or universities. Third, all variables were assessed using self-reports and validated scales for all constructs, and future work should consider using other assessment methods for mental health and objective verification of tobacco use. Fourth, although the present study was conducted with Mexican Americans, our findings might have been different with other Hispanic groups, such as Cuban Americans or Puerto Ricans. Fifth, the time frames for the depressive symptoms and anxiety measures (past 2 weeks) were different from those for the tobacco use measures (past 30 days), which may have biased the present results.

Despite these and other potential limitations, the present results suggest that sociopolitical stress associated with the policies of recent U.S. presidential administrations represents a key public health concern among Mexican American college students in Texas, which is linked with symptoms of depression and anxiety, and with tobacco use. Identification of the mechanisms could inform future research and policy work. In particular, our findings suggest that it is essential to develop programs to address both mental health and substance use – both of which have been exacerbated by sociopolitical stress tied to the rhetoric associated with recent U.S. presidential administrations.
