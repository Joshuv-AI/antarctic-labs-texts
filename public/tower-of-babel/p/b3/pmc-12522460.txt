A human YEATS4 variant confers resistance to TST and IGRA conversion despite Mycobacterium tuberculosis exposure
Authors: Clément Conil, Jonathan Bohlen, Elouise E Kroon, Marc A Jean-Juste, Jérémy Manry, Matthieu Chaldebas, James M Bean, Kathleen F Walsh, Monica Dallmann-Sauer, Maxime Rotival, Yoann Seeleuthner, Astrid Marchal, Haralambos Mourelatos, Vinicius M Fava, Peng Zhang, Gaspard Kerner, Hanaa Skhoun, Ahmed Abid, Hanane El Ouazzani, Aniss Rafik, Ahmed Aziz Bousfiha, Jamila El Baghdadi, Robert J Wilkinson, Stéphanie Boisson-Dupuis, Daniel W Fitzgerald, Jean W Pape, Marlo Möller, Eileen G Hoal, Jean-Laurent Casanova, Laurent Abel, Erwin Schurr, Aurélie Cobat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite sustained exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), some individuals—so-called resisters—have persistently negative results for the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs). “Resistance to immune conversion” is the best-known clinical correlate for absence of Mtb transmission and protection from tuberculosis (TB). We investigated the human genetic basis of this phenotype by hypothesizing that resisters living with HIV, a major risk factor for TB, would be enriched in genotypes conferring resistance.MethodsWe enrolled two cohorts of people living with HIV (PWH), consisting of 55 resisters and 100 controls—either positive for TST and IGRA or with a history of TB—from South Africa, and 66 resisters and 57 controls from Haiti, two regions where TB is hyperendemic. All study participants underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS). We performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the resister phenotype, focusing on a comprehensive set of 320,629 common protein-altering and regulatory variants.ResultsWe identified a cluster of variants on chromosome 12q15, including rs622656, associated with the resister phenotype in both South Africa and Haiti. A meta-analysis revealed that the C allele of rs622656 was associated with the resister phenotype with an odds ratio (OR) of 4.35 (95% CI: 2.44–7.69) (P = 2.47 × 10−7). The frequency of the C allele in the combined sample was 0.11, and all four CC homozygotes in our sample were resisters. Consistently, we found a significant depletion of homozygous carriers of the rs622656-C resistance-associated allele in three cohorts of HIV-negative TB patients from Haiti, Morocco, and the UK Biobank. In silico analysis suggested that the rs622656-C resistance-associated allele increased the translation of the nearby YEATS4 gene by suppressing an upstream open reading frame. YEATS4 has been shown to play a role in innate lymphoid cell differentiation, a leukocyte subset. We confirmed experimentally that the rs622656-C resistance-associated allele increased the activity of the reporter gene in HEK293T cells.ConclusionsWe report a new cluster of variants on chromosome 12q15, overlapping the YEATS4 gene, strongly associated with the resister phenotype in two populations of PWH. Our results contribute to the understanding of the molecular mechanisms that block transmission of Mtb.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-025-01547-0.Keywords: Whole genome sequencing, Genome-wide association study, Regulatory variants, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, TST, IGRA, Resister, Human immunodeficiency virus, YEATS4

Background

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a major public health problem. In 2023, the World Health Organization estimated the number of new symptomatic TB cases at 10.6 million, 6.3% of these cases occurring in people with HIV (PWH), and the number of deaths due to TB worldwide at 1.3 million [1]. PWH have a significantly higher risk of developing active TB than HIV-negative individuals [2, 3]. The development of active TB occurs in only a minority of HIV-negative individuals exposed to Mtb and is strongly influenced by host genetic factors [4, 5]. Proof-of-principle has been provided by the identification of a Mendelian predisposition (i.e., caused by a lesion in a single gene) to childhood tuberculosis [6] and, more recently, by the discovery of the first common monogenic etiology of TB, homozygosity for a common missense variant of TYK2, which is responsible for about 1% of tuberculosis cases in Europe [7–11]. Several genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have also reported common loci associated with TB disease with modest effect sizes [12–28].

It has been estimated that approximately 1.7 billion individuals display immunity consistent with Mtb infection [29, 30]. Clinical and public health guidelines recommend use of the in vivo tuberculin skin test (TST) and/or in vitro IFN-γ release assay (IGRA) for the diagnosis of Mtb infection. Positive results in these two tests are indirect markers of Mtb infection measuring different aspects of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell-dependent immunity in the periphery [31, 32]. They cannot distinguish between individuals who have cleared the infection and those with residual quiescent infection, but positive results for the TST and/or IGRAs in the absence of clinical evidence of TB usually result in classification as “latent TB infection” (LTBI) for clinical and public health decision-making [33]. At the other end of the spectrum, individuals who remain persistently negative in both assays despite high levels of documented exposure to Mtb are called “resisters” and account for between 7 and 25% of the population in areas of endemic TB [34–43]. However, the “resister” phenotype may reflect several biological and immunological realities. Specifically, TST and IGRAs cannot distinguish between individuals who were never exposed to Mtb; those who are truly resistant to Mtb infection; those who were infected but have not yet mounted, or failed to mount, an immune response; and those who developed a non-IFN-γ-driven T-cell immunity that is not detectable by TST or IGRA [36, 44, 45]. The resister phenotype shares similarities with the “early clearer” phenotype described by others, who propose that early clearers exhibit short-term protection lasting several months, with resisters potentially representing a subset characterized by more robust or longer-lasting protection in the context of well-defined exposure [38, 46, 47]. However, irrespective of actual infection status, TST and/or IGRA negativity is strongly correlated with protection from TB disease in cohort studies of exposed individuals, making this phenotype highly relevant to investigate [48–50].

We and others have shown that the responses to TST and IGRA, especially when considered as a quantitative trait, are highly heritable, demonstrating a significant contribution of genetic factors to these responses. Indeed, heritability was estimated at 92% for the TST in Chile [51], 71% for the TST, and 39% for the IGRA in The Gambia [52], 58% for the IGRA (using ESAT-6 stimulation only) in South Africa [53], 27% for a similar phenotype in Uganda [54] and 45.2% for both TST and IGRA negativity in Uganda [45]. In genome-wide linkage studies, we identified major loci Linked to the quantitative TST response on chromosomal region 5p15 in South Africa [55], the lack of TST reactivity (defined as TST = 0 mm) on chromosomal region 11p14-15 in South Africa and in France [55, 56] and the amount of IFN-γ produced in response to stimulation with BCG and ESAT-6 on chromosomal regions 8q and 3q in France [57, 58]. We recently performed a GWAS in a Vietnamese population with intrafamilial exposure to Mtb, which revealed a significant association between common variants located at 10q26.2 and negative results for the TST and IGRAs [59]. Longitudinal studies conducted in Uganda on persistent negativity of the TST found evidence of linkage with chromosomal regions 2q21-2q24 and 5p13-5q22 [60] and association with variants in the vicinity of ZEB2, GTDC1 [61], NOD2, SLC6A3, and TLR4 [62]. Focusing on negativity to both TST and IGRA, an additional Ugandan study identified regulatory variants associated with this refined phenotype [45]. Finally, one study focused on PWH living in endemic regions of Uganda and Tanzania and identified variants associated with persistent TST negativity both as a binary and quantitative trait in the 5q31.1 region [63]. However, the causative genetic factors underlying the resister phenotype remain largely unknown.

Here, we studied the genetic factors underlying the resister phenotype by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of individuals with an extreme resistance phenotype. We recruited two cohorts of highly exposed PWH from South Africa and Haiti, two regions in which TB is hyperendemic. We defined a comprehensive set of candidate common functional variants obtained by WGS and used these variants to perform a GWAS of the resister phenotype, comparing individuals who remained persistently negative for the TST and IGRA to controls with either LTBI or TB.

Methods

Study samples

We focused on individuals with extreme phenotypes of resistance to immune conversion following stimulation with Mtb-related antigens in vivo or in vitro, a clinical correlate for resistance to Mtb infection in cohort studies of exposed individuals [36, 44, 46]. We defined extreme phenotypes as negativity to TST and persistent negativity to IGRA despite high exposure due to high endemicity settings and high vulnerability due to HIV infection. We recruited PWH with a history of low CD4+ T lymphocyte counts before the initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART) living in areas in which Mtb is highly endemic but displaying no signs of Mtb infection or symptoms of TB [39].

The individuals of the discovery cohort were recruited between 2017 and 2019 from five clinics specializing in the treatment and follow-up of HIV infection in the Cape region of South Africa. The incidence of TB in South Africa was estimated at 677/100,000 population at the time of the study in 2018 [64]. The estimated prevalence of HIV in South Africa in 2018 was 20.4% [65] with 59% of newly detected TB cases occurring in PWH [66]. Epidemiological studies have estimated a 36% cumulative risk of TST conversion at 8 years old in a South African birth cohort of HIV-negative children [67] and showed that 88% of HIV-negative adults aged 31–35 had a TST ≥ 10 mm [68]. TB notification rates in the Cape Town region peak at ages 40–45, with up to 625 per 100,000 among HIV-negative and 8,500 per 100,000 among HIV-positive individuals not receiving ART, which represents a 13.6-fold increase for the latter group [69, 70]. It has been shown that transmission tends to occur within the community rather than within households in Cape Town [71]. The inclusion criteria for this study are detailed elsewhere [39]. Briefly, they included having a history of low CD4+ T lymphocyte counts before the initiation of ART and immune reconstitution due to ART. Given that age serves as a proxy for cumulative infectious exposure and is a well-established risk factor for LTBI [68], participant recruitment prioritized individuals aged 35 to 60 years of age. In this study, individuals with TST = 0 mm and negative results for three IGRA tests were classified as resisters. The first two IGRA tests were performed with a median interval of 170 days (1st quartile (Q1) = 119 days; 3rd quartile (Q3) = 227 days) and the third IGRA was conducted three days after the second one. The TST was administered during the same visit as the second IGRA and read at the same time as the third IGRA. Individuals with TST > 0 and positive results in two IGRA tests (classified as LTBI) or with a history of active TB were used as controls. For the LTBI group, the two IGRA tests were performed with a median interval of 183 days (Q1–Q3 = 76–313 days). In total, 55 resisters, 61 LTBI, and 39 individuals with a history of TB were recruited and underwent WGS.

The individuals in the replication cohort were recruited from the GHESKIO (“Groupe Haïtien d'Étude du Sarcome de Kaposi et des Infections Opportunistes”) centers in Port-au-Prince, Haiti, between 2017 and 2019. They were diagnosed with HIV between 2005 and 2009 and had been followed up for at least 10 years by 2017–2019. Haiti has the highest rate of TB in the Americas [64] and the WHO estimated the incidence of TB to be 176/100,000 population at the time of study in 2018 [64]. Studies conducted in the Port-au-Prince area to assess the TB burden after the devastating earthquake of 2010 estimated the incidence to be as high as 1,920/100,000 in impoverished sections of Port-au-Prince in 2013 (https://endtb.org/haiti) [72]. The inclusion criteria for this cohort included HIV seropositivity and a history of at least six months with CD4+ T lymphocyte counts below 350 cells/mm3 before the initiation of ART. IGRA was performed at inclusion. The results of the TST test performed at the time of HIV diagnosis were available. Individuals with TST = 0 mm, negative IGRA results, and no history of TB were classified as resisters. Individuals with TST ≥ 5 mm and positive IGRA results (LTBI) or with a history of active TB were used as controls. In total, 66 resisters, 20 LTBI, and 37 individuals with a history of TB were recruited and underwent WGS.

Whole-genome sequencing

DNA was extracted from whole blood, and WGS was performed at the New York Genome Center on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten sequencer for the South African samples, and at Psomagen Corporation with a NovaSeq 6000 flowcell type S4 2 × 150 bp sequencer for the Haitian samples. Raw reads were mapped onto the human reference genome assembly hg19 – NCBI build 37 with the Burrows-Wheeler aligner (BWA) [73]. Post-alignment processing procedures from the Genome Analysis Software Kit (GATK version 3.4–46) [74] best-practice pipeline were applied to minimize artifacts that might affect the quality of WGS data. PCR duplicates were removed with Picard tools (broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) [75]. The GATK base quality score recalibrator (BQSR) was applied to correct sequencing artifacts. Individual genomic variant call files (gVCF) were generated with GATK HaplotypeCaller, and the joint genotyping of SNPs and small insertions-deletions (INDELs) was performed with GATK GenotypeGVCFs.

Quality control

Quality control (QC) was performed on the multisample VCF including all samples from the discovery and replication cohorts, to remove low-quality variants and samples with PLINK v1.9 [76] and VCFtools [77]. Consistency between reported sex and the sex inferred from the coverage of the X and Y chromosomes was checked. We estimated the kinship coefficient between all pairs of samples with KING [78]. Individuals with a call rate < 95% were excluded. Genotypes with a ratio of reads for the least covered allele (reference or variant allele) to the total number of reads covering the position (minor read ratio, MRR) of < 20% for heterozygous calls and/or a GATK genotype quality (GQ) < 20% and/or a depth of coverage (DP) < 8 × were considered missing. GATK “Variant Quality Score Recalibration” (VQSR) was used to detect probable artifacts. The metrics used for VQSR included variant confidence normalized by unfiltered depth of variant samples (QD), Fisher’s exact test to detect strand bias (FS), the positioning of REF relative to ALT alleles within reads (ReadPosRankSumTest), the rank sum test for mapping quality (MQRankSum), symmetric odds ratio test strand bias (SOR) and consanguinity between samples (InbreedingCoeff) for insertions and deletions, and root mean square mapping quality over all the reads at the site (MQ) metric for SNPs only. True sensitivity filter levels were set at 99.5 for insertions and deletions and 99.9 for SNPs. We excluded sites: i) with a call rate < 95%, (ii) with a p-value for departure from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium < 1 × 10−6, (iii) with a non-PASS VQSR filter, (iv) located in low-complexity or decoy regions, (v) multiallelic with more than four alleles, (vi) spanning more than 20 nucleotides, (vii) with differential missingness between cases and controls (P < 10−4). In total, we identified 43,383,182 variants across the South African and Haitian cohorts, of which 38,364,946 remained after QC filtering.

Variant annotation

The functional consequence of variants (e.g., synonymous variants, non-synonymous variants, stop codons, insertions/deletions causing frameshift) was determined on transcripts from the GENCODE GRCh37 database [79] using the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) software [80]. Intronic variants affecting branch sites or creating AG dinucleotides predicted to have a strong impact on alternative splicing were searched for using BPHunter [81] and AGAIN [82] tools. The potential functional impact of variants was quantified by the Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion (CADD) score, which integrates various annotation sources [83]. Variants were annotated for their allelic frequency in the African population (AFR) using the gnomAD v2.1 database [84]. Finally, variants were annotated for their regulatory effect on expression (eQTLs) and alternative splicing (sQTL) in lung and whole blood tissues from the GTEx v8 database [85].

Selection of candidate common variants

We reduced the burden of multiple testing by selecting a set of candidate functional variants, which we then tested for association with the resister phenotype. We focused on common protein-altering and regulatory variants with a frequency in the African population of gnomAD v2.1 Africa > 5%, defined as follows: (i) coding non-synonymous variants predicted by VEP to have a moderate or high impact on the protein (stop gain/loss, start loss, frameshift, splice variants, missense or inframe); (ii) intronic variants disrupting branchpoints or creating AG dinucleotides; (iii) regulatory variants affecting the expression (eQTL) or alternative splicing (sQTL) of at least one protein-coding gene in lung tissues or whole blood, with P < 10−20 in the GTEx v8 database. Finally, we also included variants previously identified as associated with the resister phenotype (n = 34) [45, 58, 59, 61, 63] (Additional file 1: Table S1). A total of 320,629 variants met these criteria and were tested for association with the resister phenotype.

Principal component analysis

The genetic ancestry of the samples and population structure were assessed by means of principal component analysis (PCA), as implemented in PLINK v1.9 software [76], with a linkage disequilibrium (LD)-pruned (maximum r2 value for linkage disequilibrium (LD) of 0.2 between pairs of SNPs) set of 3,013,863 high-quality SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5%, using samples from the 1000 Genomes Project (KGP) as the reference.

Genetic association testing

We tested for association between the candidate common variants and the resister phenotype in the discovery sample, with adjustment for sex—and not age, which was included in the selection criteria [39]—using a logistic regression-based likelihood ratio test, as implemented in Regenie [86] and assuming co-dominant (i.e., multiplicative), dominant, and recessive models. Ancestry as measured by PCA appeared homogenous (Fig. 1) and sensitivity analysis showed that PC-adjusted and non-adjusted analysis led to highly similar quantile–quantile (QQ) plots, genomic inflation factors, and association results (data not shown). Therefore, the analysis was not adjusted on principal components in order to retain the simpler model adjusting only for sex to minimize noise and preserve power. We estimated the number of independent tests—calculated as the number of independent variants at an LD r2 threshold of 0.5—at 50,655, giving a Bonferroni-corrected threshold of 0.05/50,655 = 9.9 × 10−7. A p-value < 10−4 was considered to be suggestive of association. Independent clusters were identified by a clumping procedure in PLINK2, with a p-value threshold of 10−4 and an LD r2 threshold of 0.5.

Principal component analysis of the South African and Haitian cohorts using, as a reference, A 2504 individuals from the 1000 Genomes project (KGP) and B 504 individuals of African origin from the KGP. Principal component (PC) scores for the study individuals were predicted from the PC loadings estimated on the reference sample and projected onto the reference sample PC space

Variants for which suggestive evidence of association was obtained were tested for replication in the Haitian sample, with adjustment for sex, with the logistic regression-based likelihood ratio test implemented in Regenie [86] and assuming co-dominant (i.e., multiplicative), dominant, and recessive models. Given the homogeneous genetic ancestry of the Haitian sample, analyses were not PC-adjusted (Fig. 1). The Bonferroni-corrected threshold for replication analysis was defined based on the number of independent clusters identified in the discovery cohort. A meta-analysis of the two cohorts was then performed with METAL [87], using effect size estimates and their standard errors in the two cohorts as input.

Test for depletion of rs622655-C homozygosity in HIV-negative TB cohorts

UK Biobank TB cohort

The UK Biobank is a population-based cohort of ∼500,000 volunteers enrolled between 2006 and 2010 from across the United Kingdom (UK) and for whom detailed health-related information and biological samples, including WGS, are available [88]. We used the field #22137 providing “doctor-diagnosed” TB information to define TB cases (n = 654) and controls (n = 120,576). Analysis was restricted to individuals of European ancestry who are the most represented in the UK Biobank cohort in general and among those with TB more specifically. Ancestry was inferred from PCA [89] and only individuals predicted to be European with a probability > 0.7 were included, leading to a final cohort of 581 TB cases and 107,350 controls. Association testing was done using the logistic regression-based likelihood ratio test implemented in Regenie [86], adjusting for sex and the first 5 PCs.

Moroccan TB cohort

Over the years, our laboratory has collected and whole-exome or whole-genome sequenced a large number of patients suffering from various infectious diseases. Among them, we identified 165 HIV-negative pulmonary TB unrelated patients from Morocco with whole-exome sequencing (WES) recruited following the same criteria as described in [18], and selected from our in-house cohort a set of 375 ethnically matched controls not suffering from mycobacterial disease and predicted to be of North-African ancestry with a probability > 0.9 [89]. All included individuals had DP > 10, GQ > 20, and MRR > 0.2 at the position of rs622656. Association was tested by means of logistic regression using the glm function of the R software, adjusting for sex and the first five PCs.

Haitian TB cohort

We identified from our in-house cohort 78 HIV-negative pulmonary TB unrelated patients from Haiti with WES data available. TB patients were compared to a set of 389 ethnically matched controls, selected from our in-house cohort as individuals not suffering from mycobacterial disease and predicted from the PCA to be of African origin with a probability > 0.9, as described elsewhere [89]. All included individuals had DP > 10, GQ > 20, and MRR > 0.2 at the position of rs622656. Association of rs622565 with TB was tested by means of Firth’s bias corrected logistic regression, in order to obtain finite odds ratio despite low number of carriers, using the logistf function as implemented in R logistf package and adjusting on sex and the first five PCs.

The trans-ethnic meta-analysis for rs622656 was performed with METAL [87], using effect size estimates and their standard errors from the three HIV-negative TB cohorts.

Ribo-seq data generation and analysis

Adapter poly-A sequence nucleotides were trimmed from raw reads with cutadapt [90]. Ribosomal RNA and tRNA reads were removed by bowtie2 alignment with human tRNA and rRNA sequences [91]. The remaining reads were aligned with the human transcript ENST00000247843 of YEATS4 with BBMap [92]. Ribo-seq data were available for eight individuals: three C/T heterozygotes at rs622656 and five T/T homozygotes. For each individual, the number of reads was normalized against sequencing depth (number of alignments per library) and YEATS4 expression level in the sample. Read position was annotated according to the position of the 5′ end of the transcript. Given that rs622656-C allele removes the upstream open reading frame (uORF) in YEATS4 5′UTR, we expected a decrease in the Ribo-seq read counts for this allele, and therefore tested the hypothesis that the mean number of read counts in the window starting at the uORF start codon and ending three codons further forward in the uORF would be greater for T/T than for C/T individuals using a one-tailed Student’s t-test. For the plotting of the data only, data were smoothed with a 10 bp forward-sliding window representing the number of reads in the window. Data were deposited at SRA: PRJNA1004232. The details of data generation, quality control, and preprocessing are provided elsewhere [93].

Evaluation of rs622656-induced effects on the uORF in the YEATS4 5′ UTR

We first subcloned the 5′ untranslated region (5′UTR) of YEATS4 from the genomic DNA of one individual heterozygous (C/T) at rs622656 (identified from the WES data for our in-house cohort) and amplified it by PCR. We then inserted this 5′UTR region into a plasmid vector upstream from the Renilla luciferase (RL) reporter gene and obtained the purified plasmid. We plated HEK293T cells in 96-well plates at a concentration of 250,000 cells/mL (25,000 cells per well) and incubated them for 24 h at 37 °C. They were then cotransfected with (i) a reporter plasmid containing either the T or the C allele of rs622656 and (ii) an internal control plasmid containing the firefly luciferase (FL) gene, with X-tremeGENE™ 9. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The luciferase assay was then performed according to the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System from Promega on a VICTOR® Nivo™ Plate Reader. FL activity was used to normalize the RL activity results and to account for experimental variations. Experiments were performed in nine replicates. Given that the expected effect of uORFs is to inhibit protein translation [94–96] and that the rs622656-C allele removes the uORF, we tested the hypothesis that normalized RL activity would be higher in cells transfected with the plasmid containing the C allele than in those containing the T allele by means of a one-sided paired samples Wilcoxon test.

Results

Whole-genome sequencing for 278 South African and Haitian individuals

We performed WGS on 278 PWH with high levels of exposure to Mtb from two TB hyperendemic areas, South Africa and Haiti. The South African cohort was the discovery cohort. It included 55 resisters with TST = 0 mm with persistently negative results for IGRA after immune reconstitution on antiretroviral therapy, 61 individuals with LTBI, and 39 PWH with a history of TB (see the “Methods” section). The median age at inclusion (Q1–Q3) was 43 (37–45) years for the resisters, 36 (24–41) years for individuals with LTBI and 40 (35–45) years for TB patients, consistent with the selection criteria (see the “Methods” section; Table 1). The proportion of male subjects was similar between the resisters and the control group (16% vs. 18%, P = 0.97) although there was a trend towards a higher proportion of male individuals among TB patients (31%) than among individuals with LTBI (10%). The Haitian cohort included 66 resisters, 20 individuals with LTBI, and 37 TB patients (see the “Methods” section). Median age at inclusion (Q1–Q3) was 40 (34–46) years for the resisters, 44 (33–46) years for individuals with LTBI and 40 (35–45) years for TB patients. The proportion of male subjects was higher among TB patients (65%) than among resisters (32%) and individuals with LTBI (40%) (Table 1). In both cohorts, the individuals with LTBI and the TB patients were grouped together for subsequent analyses, which were systematically adjusted for sex. Principal component analysis indicated homogeneous genetic ancestry of the two cohorts. Haitian individuals formed a cluster close to Western African samples from the 1000 Genomes Project (KGP) dataset, whereas individuals from South Africa formed a distinct cluster (Fig. 1). Hence, further analyses in the two cohorts were not PC-adjusted.

Association study on candidate functional variants in the South African sample

In total, 320,629 high-quality common (MAF > 5%) candidate functional variants (see the “Methods” section)—20,936 protein-altering variants, 299,659 regulatory variants, and 34 candidate variants from the literature—were tested for association with the resister phenotype assuming co-dominant, dominant, and recessive modes of inheritance. There was no inflation of the type I error on the QQ-plot, and the genomic inflation factor was 1.05 under the co-dominant model (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). We estimated at 50,655 the number of independent tests after accounting for LD (see the “Methods” section), leading to a Bonferroni corrected experiment-wide significance cutoff of 9.9 × 10−7. No variant reached this significance threshold, whatever the mode of inheritance (Fig. 2A–C). In the co-dominant model, evidence suggestive of association was obtained for 14 variants (P < 10−4) from five independent clusters (r2 > 0.5) on chromosomal regions 1q32 (n = 1), 10q11.21 (n = 1), 12q15 (n = 10), 17q25.1 (n = 1) and 17q25.3 (n = 1) (Fig. 2A; Additional file 3: Table S2). The strongest association signal was observed for variants rs622656, rs11286051, and rs160829, located in the 12q15 region, which were in perfect linkage disequilibrium (r2 = 1) in the South African cohort, with a higher minor allele frequency (MAF) in resisters (0.16) than in individuals with LTBI (0.04) and TB patients (0). For rs622656, the sex-adjusted odds ratio (OR (95% CI)) of being a resister for CT vs. TT or CC vs. CT was OR (95% CI) = 7.11 (2.98–16.98) (P = 1.01 × 10−5). Under a dominant model, evidence of suggestive association was found for 29 variants, 12 of which belonged to two clusters identified in the co-dominant model, 10q11.21 (n = 1), 12q15 (n = 11), the other 17 belonging to five additional independent clusters on chromosomes 1p36 (n = 1), 2q36 (n = 8), 5q31.1 (n = 6), 10q25.3 (n = 1), and 15q24 (n = 1) (Fig. 2B; Additional file 4: Table S3). Again, rs622656 was the variant for which the OR (95% CI) of being a resister was highest for CC or CT vs. TT (OR (95% CI) = 7.11 (2.98–16.98); P = 1.01 × 10−5). The results for the co-dominant and dominant models were nearly identical, as there was only one CC individual (a resister). Finally, under a recessive model, evidence suggestive of association was found for 17 variants from three independent clusters, none of which overlapped with other models, on chromosomes 2p24 (n = 1), 19p13 (n = 15), and Xp11 (n = 1) (Fig. 2C; Additional file 5: Table S4). Among the 34 candidate variants previously associated with the resister phenotype in other studies (see the “Methods” section), none of them reached the suggestive significance threshold of P < 10−4 (Additional file 1: Table S1). In total, across the co-dominant, dominant, and recessive models, 13 independent clusters were identified for which there was evidence suggestive of association.

The signal from cluster 12q15 is replicated in the Haitian sample

We tested all variants of the 13 independent clusters identified in the co-dominant, dominant, and recessive models for the South African cohort for replication in the Haitian cohort. Replication was validated if the direction of association and the genetic model in Haiti were the same as those in South Africa and if the P-value was below the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P = 3.85 × 10−3, see the “Methods” section). Seven variants displayed significant replication in the Haitian cohort under a co-dominant and/or dominant model; all seven belonged to the 12q15 locus (Additional file 6: Table S5). For rs622656, the C allele was associated with the resister phenotype with an OR under a co-dominant model of 2.56 (1.41–4.76) (P = 2.15 × 10−3) in Haiti. We next performed a meta-analysis with the GWAS statistics and standard errors of the two cohorts (Fig. 3). As shown in Table 2 and Additional file 7: Table S6, under a co-dominant model, seven SNPs displayed genome-wide significant association (P < 9.9 × 10−7). These seven SNPs belonged to the 12q15 cluster and included rs622656 (OR = 4.35 (2.44–7.69); P = 2.47 × 10−7; Fig. 4A). The frequency of the C allele in the combined sample was 0.11 (from 0.07 in South Africa to 0.16 in Haiti, this difference not being statistically significant, t-test P = 0.13). Strikingly, all four CC homozygotes—one from South Africa and three from Haiti—were resisters (Fig. 4B). Similar results were obtained under a dominant model (Additional file 8: Table S7). No variant reached experiment-wide significance under the recessive model (Additional file 9: Table S8).

The association signal is not driven by a synthetic association

It has been suggested that association signals at common variants in GWAS may be the consequence of low-frequency rare variants that create “synthetic associations” by occurring stochastically more frequently in association with one of the alleles at the common site than with the other [97]. We looked at the South African and Haitian cohorts for rare (gnomADv2.1 AFR AF < 0.05) protein-altering variants (i.e., missense, stop gain, start loss, essential splice, frameshift and inframe indels) of the LYZ, YEATS4, and CPSF6 genes and for rare (gnomADv2.1 AFR AF < 0.05) non-coding variants of the MIR1279 non-protein-coding gene close to the 12q15 cluster (Fig. 4A). No rare protein-altering variants of YEATS4 or rare non-coding variants of MIR1279 were identified in either the South African or the Haitian cohorts, consistent with the frequency spectrum of such variants in gnomAD (max AF of protein-altering variants of YEATS4 and any type of variant of MIR1279 < 10−3). We identified one missense variant of CPSF6, Thr372Ala, which was present in the heterozygous state in one control individual from South Africa. We identified three rare missense variants of LYZ—Leu4Val (AF gnomAD v2 AFR 0.0098), Thr88Asn (AF gnomAD v2 AFR 0.0097) and Ala112Gly (0.0011) (Additional file 10: Table S9)—present in 10 resisters and three control individuals in the two cohorts. Two missense variants—Leu4Val and Thr88Asn, present in nine resisters and three control individuals—belonged to the same haplotype as the minor resister allele at rs622656. We investigated whether the signal at rs622656 could be driven by a rare protein-altering variant of LYZ by performing a conditional analysis [98] on the combined cohort, in which we tested the effect of rs622656 while adjusting for (i) the burden score of the three missense variants, (ii) the burden score of the two missense variants on the same haplotype as the common rs622656 resistance allele, or (iii) the most informative rare variant, Leu4Val. In all three cases, although slightly reduced, the effect of rs622656 remained within the range observed without adjustment for the missense variant (OR ~ 4) or significance level (P ~ 10−6) (Additional file 10: Table S10). Conversely, there was a trend toward an enrichment in rare missense variants of LYZ in resisters relative to LTBI + TB in the combined sample (OR = 3.13 (0.98–10), P = 0.05), but this signal decreased after adjustment for rs622656 (OR = 1.22 (0.20–3.35), P = 0.77). These results suggested that the signal at rs622656 could not be explained by rare protein-altering variants of the nearby protein-coding genes LYZ, YEATS4, and CPSF6, or by rare variants of the non-protein-coding MIR1279 gene, instead being driven by the common variants in the 12q15 cluster.

The resister-associated allele is depleted in HIV-negative TB patients

As resistance to immune conversion is strongly correlated with protection from TB disease in cohort studies of individuals exposed to Mtb [48–50], we hypothesized that the resister-associated alleles would be depleted among TB patients as compared with the general population. We tested this hypothesis in three HIV-negative TB cohorts including (i) 581 European TB cases and 107,350 ethnically matched controls from the UK Biobank (UKBB) cohort, with WGS [88]; (ii) an in-house cohort from Morocco of 165 pulmonary TB patients and 375 ethnically matched controls without history of mycobacterial or fungal infection, with WES data; (iii) an in-house cohort from Haiti of 78 pulmonary TB patients and 389 ethnically matched controls without history of mycobacterial or fungal infection, with WES. Only the rs622656 variant, located in the 5′UTR of YEATS4 (Fig. 4A) was covered by WES and was tested for association with TB in the three cohorts. Under the co-dominant model, there was a trend, although not significant, toward a protective effect against TB for the C resister allele. The frequency of the C allele was lower in the TB group as compared with controls in the UKBB (28.8% vs. 30.5%), the Moroccan (28.2% vs. 33.7%) and the Haitian (12.8% vs. 13.6%) cohorts. The frequencies of the rs622656-C allele in these cohorts were consistent with frequencies observed in similar populations from the KGP. When considering a recessive model for the resister allele, there was a significant depletion of the CC genotype among TB patients in the UKBB cohort (6% vs. 9%, OR = 0.92 (0.86–0.99), P = 0.017) and a consistent trend in the Moroccan (9% vs. 13%, OR = 0.74 (0.38–1.46), P = 0.38) and Haitian (0% vs. 2%, OR = 0.32 (0.02–4.69), P = 0.32) samples. The depletion of CC homozygotes among TB patients was significant when combining the three cohorts in a trans-ethnic meta-analysis (ORtrans-ethnic meta-analysis CC vs CT+TT (95% CI) = 0.71 (0.55–0.92), Ptrans-ethnic meta-analysis = 9.4 × 10−3) (Table 3). The modest effect sizes observed in the TB cohorts could be explained by two reasons: (i) many control individuals may not have been exposed to Mtb, especially in populations from regions with lower Mtb endemicity compared to South Africa and Haiti; and (ii) a large proportion of the controls included in these analyses have not been tested with TST or IGRA, and may therefore include positive individuals, making them suboptimal controls for this analysis.

Fine mapping of the LYZ eQTL effect of the resistance-associated variants

The resistance-associated variants of the 12q15 locus are in strong linkage disequilibrium (r2 > 0.8) and were included in the analysis because of their highly significant eQTL effect on the LYZ gene in whole blood (e.g., rs622656 eQTL normalized effect size (NES) = − 0.55; P = 1.2 × 10−53) and the lung (rs622656 eQTL NES = − 0.35; P = 4.7 × 10−21) in GTEx v8. The minor resistance-associated alleles (e.g., allele C of rs622656) were associated with higher expression of LYZ, encoding lysozyme, a well-known antimicrobial enzyme [99, 100]. We first hypothesized that the effect of the 12q15 variants on the resistance phenotype might be mediated by their eQTL effect on LYZ. We searched for all eQTLs of LYZ in GTEx v8 [85] and identified rs4761234 as the strongest and most significant LYZ eQTL in whole blood (NES = − 0.74; P = 5.7 × 10−183) and lung tissues (NES = − 0.49; P = 7.78 × 10−55), the T allele being associated with higher levels of LYZ expression. None of the resistance-associated variants was identified as an independent eQTL of LYZ in refined GTEx analyses after conditioning on rs4761234 [85]. Furthermore, the fine mapping of LYZ eQTLs with SuSiE [101] on GTEx eQTL summary statistics for Europeans and Linkage disequilibrium for KGP Non-Finnish European populations revealed three 95% credible sets of causal variants in whole blood and two in the lung, none of which included any of the resistance-associated variants (Additional file 10: Table S11). These results suggest that the LYZ eQTL effect of 12q15 resistance-associated variants is probably mediated by linkage disequilibrium with rs4761234 in European populations. Population-specific eQTLs tend to be rare [102, 103], but we also screened four additional eQTL expression studies conducted in African populations. The resistance-associated variants did not emerge as the most significant eQTLs for LYZ in any of these studies, in which the top eQTL was always rs4761234 (or a locus in strong LD with rs4761234) [102, 104–106].

The resistance-associated signal is not mediated by the LYZ eQTL effect in whole blood or lung

The rs4761234-T allele associated with high levels of LYZ expression in GTEx v8 had a frequency of 0.96 in South Africans and 0.90 in Haitians. These frequencies are much higher than that in non-Finnish Europeans (NFE) (0.52 in gnomAD v4.1) and consistent with the frequency in KGP indigenous African populations (0.93–0.96). Hence, 87% of our study individuals were homozygous for the rs4761234-T allele and all but one (a resister from Haiti) carried at least one rs4761234-T allele resulting in high levels of LYZ expression. Whatever the genetic model considered, rs4761234-T was not associated with the resister phenotype in our study (P-value for the best model—recessive model for T allele—P = 0.45, OR = 0.21 (0.003–12.71)) (Additional file 7: Table S6; Additional file 8: Table S7; Additional file 9: Table S8). Haplotypic analysis on our cohorts further revealed that the top-ranking rs622656-C resister allele was always in phase with the rs4761234-T high-LYZ expression allele (Additional file 10: Table S12). Interestingly, when the analysis was restricted to individuals with the highest levels of LYZ expression according to GTEx (i.e., rs4761234-TT individuals, n = 241), the signal of association with the resister phenotype at the rs622656 variant was stronger (OR = 5.19 (2.90–9.30); P = 3.1 × 10−8). These results are consistent with the hypothesis that the eQTL effect of rs622656 on LYZ can be explained by the strong linkage disequilibrium between this variant and rs4761234. These results therefore suggest that the effect of the resistance-associated alleles is unlikely to be mediated by an eQTL effect on LYZ in the whole blood and lung.

The resistance-associated signal is not mediated by the YEATS4 eQTL effect in whole blood or lung

The resistance-associated variants mapped to the 5′UTR and intronic regions of the YEATS4 gene. The resistance-associated alleles were also moderately associated with an increase in YEATS4 expression in whole blood (rs622656: NES = − 0.19; P = 2.6 × 10−9) and lung tissue (rs622656: NES = − 0.18; P = 2.3 × 10−7) in GTEx v8. However, as for LYZ, stronger eQTLs of YEATS4 were observed in GTEx v8 in whole blood (rs7136238, NES = − 0.34; P = 1.9 × 10−39) and lung (rs4761239, NES = − 0.39; P = 5.3 × 10−42) than the resistance-associated SNPs. Moreover, rs7136238 and rs4761239 were in almost perfect linkage disequilibrium with the top-ranking LYZ eQTL, rs4761234, in NFE populations. Thus, unsurprisingly, none of the resistance-associated variants belonged to the GTEx v8 set of independent eQTLs for YEATS4 or the SuSiE 95% credible sets for lung and whole blood (Additional file 10: Table S13), suggesting that the YEATS4 eQTL effect of the resistance variants was driven by linkage disequilibrium in European populations. A screening of eQTL studies conducted in African populations did not identify our resistance-associated variants as lead eQTLs. Again, rs7136238 and rs4761239 were not associated with the resister phenotype in our study (P > 0.05) and haplotype analysis revealed that the rs622656-C resistance allele was always in phase with the high-expression YEATS4 alleles (Additional file 10: Table S14). All of these results suggest that the effect of the resistance-associated alleles was unlikely to be due to an eQTL effect on YEATS4 in whole blood and lung.

The rs622656-C resistance allele abolishes a uORF in the YEATS4 5′UTR region and increases protein activity in HEK293T cells

Our in-house 5′-UTR analysis tool (https://hgidsoft.rockefeller.edu/5ULTRA/) identified rs622656C > T as a 5′UTR premature start codon variant in the MANE transcript of YEATS4, creating a uORF, which was then also confirmed by UTRannotator plugin [107] in the Ensembl VEP tool (Figs. 4A and 5A; Additional file 10: Table S15–16). The rs622656-C resistance allele was predicted to abolish a uORF with an adequate Kozak context, thereby increasing the translation of the YEATS4 mRNA into protein (Fig. 5A). We investigated whether this uORF was actually translated by analyzing previously generated Ribo-seq data (see the “Methods” section) for three C/T and five T/T individuals for rs622656. Ribosome profiling detects the position and frequency of translating ribosomes on endogenous mRNAs [108]. Interestingly, there were significantly more ribosome footprints in the uORF region in T/T individuals than in C/T individuals (mean normalized read count for T/T over a window starting from the uORF start site to 3 codons forward (i.e., a total of 4 codons, 12bps) = 0.85 vs. 0.42 for C/T, P = 0.034, Additional file 2: Fig. S2; one-tailed Student’s t-test; see the “Methods” section), suggesting that the uORF is translated. These results are consistent with the hypothesis that the C allele disrupts the uORF. As translated uORFs tend to inhibit the translation of the main coding sequence [94–96], we investigated the effect of the rs622656-C allele on translation of the main ORF located downstream. We transfected HEK293T cells with a plasmid containing the 5′UTR region of YEATS4 with either the rs622656 T or C allele upstream from a main ORF encoding the Renilla luciferase reporter gene (see the “Methods” section). We showed that RL activity was significantly higher with the plasmid containing the rs622656-C resistance allele than with the rs622656-T allele (mean increase of 20.4%; P = 0.037; one-sided paired samples Wilcoxon test; see the “Methods” section) (Fig. 5B; Additional file 10: Table S17). This finding suggests that the rs622656-C resistance allele increases the amount of YEATS4 protein by suppressing the uORF.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the genetic determinants of resistance to TST and IGRA conversion in PWH living in hyperendemic areas for TB who were presumed to have been substantially exposed to Mtb. In this setting, resisters to immune conversion are at low risk of developing TB disease and are epidemiologically and clinically defined as resistant to infection with Mtb [34]. We focused on a set of candidate coding and regulatory variants obtained by WGS and identified seven common variants at the 12q15 locus associated with the resister phenotype in two cohorts of PWH from South Africa and Haiti. Haplotype analysis showed that resistance-associated alleles were in phase with alleles associated with high levels of LYZ and YEATS4 expression in GTEx. Further in silico analysis revealed that the effect of the resistance-associated alleles was unlikely to be due to an eQTL effect on LYZ or YEATS4 in whole blood or lung. We identified rs622656 as an interesting candidate causal variant, with the resistance-associated C allele predicted to increase YEATS4 mRNA translation by disrupting a uORF in the 5′UTR. Ribo-seq data analysis showed that this uORF was likely to be translated. The impact of the rs622656-C allele on protein level was confirmed by luciferase assay in HEK293T cells showing an increase in the activity of the luciferase protein in the absence of the uORF. uORFs are posttranscriptional regulatory mRNA elements defined by a start codon in the 5′UTR that may or may not overlap the main ORF. These elements can play a major role in regulating mRNA translation in eukaryotes, typically by inhibiting translation of the main coding sequence [94–96] and regulating mRNA stability [109]. These data suggest that the rs622656-C allele increases the translation of the main YEATS4 ORF by disrupting the uORF in the 5′UTR. Variants disrupting uORFs have been shown to be under negative selection [110] and start-gain variants in 5′UTRs have been associated with several diseases [111–114].

YEATS4 encodes the YEATS Domain Containing 4 protein. Yeats domain-containing proteins are actively involved in the recognition of histone acetylation patterns and modulating gene transcription through the reconfiguration of chromatin structures [115, 116]. YEATS4, in particular, has been linked to histone acetylation and lung cancer progression [117, 118]. Liu et al. [119] showed that the mouse Yeats4 was strongly expressed in innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and their progenitors. Their findings demonstrated the essential role of Yeats4 in the differentiation of α4β7+ common lymphoid progenitors (CLP) into ILCs. Specifically, Yeats4 was shown to recognize H3K27ac, leading to recruitment of the Dot1-RNA polymerase (Pol) II complex, which acts as a reader for histone acetylation. This recruitment initiates the expression of the transcription factor Lmo4, driving commitment to the ILC lineage. Liu et al. showed that conditional Yeats4−/− KO mice have lower counts of ILCs and IFN-γ-producing ILC1s and impaired IFN-γ production by these cells in mesenteric lymph nodes relative to WT mice. Challenge with S. typhimurium led to the detection of larger numbers of CFUs (in the mesenteric lymph nodes) in KO mice than in WT mice, suggesting that Yeats4 KO impairs the antibacterial function of ILC1s. Finally, the authors reported a “reduced leukocyte infiltration” in the lungs of KO mice relative to WT mice [119], consistent with recent findings of lymphocytosis in the alveoli of resisters [120]. Furthermore, Mtb infection leads to changes in the phenotype and function of lung IL-18Ra+ ILC, resulting in the development of an IL12-STAT1-dependent protective ILC1-like population producing IFN-γ. BCG vaccination promoted the early emergence of ILC1-like cells following secondary challenge with Mtb in mice [121]. Given the role of YEATS4 in CLP differentiation into ILCs, increases in its transcription and/or translation might modify ILC populations and phenotypes, including those of IFN-γ-producing ILCs in particular. Further experiments are required to confirm these effects and their implications during infection, especially in the context of Mtb infection.

TB has exerted strong selective pressure on the human genome, causing approximately one billion deaths in Europe over the last 2,000 years [7, 9, 122, 123]. This selective pressure can lead to drastic shifts in the frequency of TB-associated risk alleles, as illustrated by the significant decrease in frequency of the P1104A variant in TYK2 over the last two millennia [9]. No selective pressure could be detected for rs622656. Based on the AGES tool and methodology, which integrates > 8000 ancient genomes, the frequency of the rs622656-C allele remained stable over the past 2000 years [124]. Consistent results were observed with the absence of detectable selection for the rs622656-C allele over the past two millennia in a study estimating selection on modern DNA from the 1000 Genomes Project [125]. Nevertheless, these results are not surprising as we do not expect strong selective pressure on an allele conferring resistance to Mtb infection. Indeed, among the individuals infected with Mtb, only a minority (< 10%) will develop active TB disease [126]. At the gene level, several constraint metrics indicate that YEATS4 is a conserved gene. First, YEATS4 has a low observed/expected ratio for pLOF variants, indicating strong selection against pLOF (loss-of-function observed/expected upper bound fraction, LOEUF = 0.88) [127]. Second, the negative selection signatures of deleterious YEATS4 mutations are consistent with those of genes underlying autosomal dominant disease (consensus-based measure of negative selection score, CoNeS = − 0.37) [128]. YEATS4 has a low level of accumulated mutational damage in the general population, as indicated by a gene damage index (GDI) of 0.04 [129]. No biallelic pLOF, missense or INDEL variants are found in public databases, and the highest frequency of such variants has a MAF = 2.03 × 10−4 (gnomAD v4.1). Finally, several studies have categorized YEATS4 as an essential gene, defined as genes for which loss of its function compromises the viability or fitness of the organism [130–132]. These population genetics data suggest that YEATS4 is a highly conserved gene, intolerant to deleterious mutations and, therefore, a relevant candidate gene for involvement in the response to major deadly pathogens, such as Mtb.

Previous studies have been conducted to identify the genetic determinants of resistance to TST and/or IGRA conversion, although we failed to replicate their main findings in our cohorts (Additional file 1: Table S1). Particularly, Sobota et al. examined a similar phenotype in PWH and identified variants near IL9 and SLC25A48, with potential cis-eQTL effects on a proximal long non-coding RNA and a potential haplotype implicating a missense variant in IL9. Their study differed from ours in that they relied solely on TST as the phenotypic readout and focused on individuals from Tanzania and Uganda [63]. Differences in ethnic background, as well as in phenotype definition and epidemiological settings, may account for the lack of replication in our analysis. In another study, McHenry et al. identified several suggestive associations with persistent TST and IGRA negativity among HIV-negative individuals [45]. Many of these variants are located in regions with regulatory potential according to in silico regulation prediction and eQTL studies with tissue-specific effects. Overall, replication of findings from other studies is challenging due to the potentially different mechanisms underlying resistance to TST and/or IGRA conversion in different exposure settings. Nevertheless, resistance to immune conversion correlates with protection from TB disease [48–50]. Intriguingly, we observed a consistent depletion of the rs622656-C resistance allele in TB patients in cohorts of HIV-negative individuals from Morocco, Haiti, and the UKBB. In line with our findings, a recent trans-ethnic meta-analysis conducted across European, Asian, and African cohorts reported a significant depletion of the C allele of rs622656 among HIV-negative individuals with TB (P = 0.03; OR = 0.97; additive model) [133]. This suggests that the effect of an allele associated with resistance to immune conversion may also manifest, albeit to a lesser extent, as a reduced frequency among individuals with active TB disease.

Conclusions

Our study identified new common SNPs associated with resistance to TST and IGRA conversion in PWH and identified a new candidate gene, YEATS4. The regulatory impact of these SNPs highlights the importance of examining non-coding regions of the genome for association with this phenotype. Such investigations are essential to provide insight into mechanisms underlying resistance to immune conversion, the best-known epidemiological correlate for absence of Mtb transmission and protection from TB. Importantly, persistently negative TST/IGRA persons with controlled HIV infection are not known to contribute to Mtb transmission. Hence, our study is a step towards understanding the mechanisms that block transmission of Mtb, the gold standard for any TB vaccine. Moreover, the ability to identify individuals who resist conversion of TST and IGRA could enhance the design of vaccine clinical trials, as such individuals are less likely to contribute informative data for assessing the efficacy of a candidate vaccine.
