Preliminary ultrastructural analysis suggests phenotype-specific myofiber alterations in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies
Authors: Andrea Aguilar-Vazquez, Christian Juarez-Gomez, Emiliano Gonzalez-Gauna, Gabriel Medrano-Ramirez, Steven Vargas-Cañas, Angelica Vargas-Guerrero, José Manuel Gutiérrez-Hernández, Guillermo Toriz, Cynthia-Alejandra Gomez-Rios, Jose-David Medina-Preciado, Efrain Chavarria-Avila, Beatriz-Teresita Martin-Marquez, Oscar Pizano-Martinez, Erika-Aurora Martinez-Garcia, Felipe Perez-Vazquez, Flavio Sandoval-Garcia, Mario Salazar-Paramo, Edy-David Rubio-Arellano, Jose Becerra-Jimenez, Gabriela Garcia-Ordoñez, Arahi Gaspar-Ruiz, Alejandra Ramos-Hernandez, Maribell Cabrera-Lopez, Arcelia Llamas-Garcia, Monica Vazquez-Del Mercado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) comprise a heterogeneous group of autoimmune muscle diseases with variable patterns of muscle damage. In this multicentric cross-sectional study, we employed scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to characterize ultrastructural alterations in muscle biopsies from quadriceps (n = 29) and deltoid (n = 41) of 70 IIM patients classified by ENMC (European Neuromuscular Centre) 2004/2011/2023 and EULAR/ACR (European League Against Rheumatism / American College of Rheumatology) 2017 criteria, together with 8 control samples. Qualitative assessment revealed loss of hexagonality and fiber size variability across all samples, while porosities, ruptures, perforations, and sarcolemma remnant emerged as candidates to phenotype-specific distribution. Dermatomyositis (DM) presented relatively preserved ultrastructure, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) exhibited perforations, and inclusion body myositis (IBM) displayed sarcolemma remnant. Perforations were linked to necrosis, whereas fatty replacement was less frequent in samples with ultrastructural alterations. Quantitative morphometry showed higher variability in quadriceps compared with deltoid fibers, and Z-score normalization confirmed widespread myofiber atrophy across IIM subtypes. Building upon previous preliminary observations, this study expands SEM-based analysis to a larger multicentric cohort, revealing preliminary phenotype-specific ultrastructural signatures that may contribute to a better understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of muscle damage in IIM.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Medical research, Pathogenesis, Rheumatology

Introduction

The idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) encompass a clinical spectrum of autoimmune muscle diseases that have been classified over the last century using clinical data, histopathological findings, and the presence of autoantibodies. This heterogeneous group of autoimmune diseases was initially described as a single clinical entity: polymyositis (PM)1.

Over the last decades, different classification criteria has been developed, being one of the most historically recognized the Bohan and Peter2,3, however, these criteria have evolved to encompass distinct entities such as dermatomyositis (DM), inclusion body myositis (IBM), immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), and nonspecific myositis according to the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) and the European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR)4–8. More recently, international efforts, such as the “Classification Criteria for Anti-synthetase Syndrome” (CLASS) project, have also focused on the anti-synthetase syndrome (ASyS), in which myositis represents part of the classical triad in approximately 20% of patients9. This myositis spectrum reflects the growing recognition of the heterogeneity of these muscle pathologies, the need for further knowledge and complementary approaches capable of capturing disease-specific biological features beyond clinical classification alone.

The muscle biopsy remains a valuable tool in the IIM assessment. Nevertheless, it is often considered optional in routine practice due to its invasive nature and the potential limitations in diagnostic pathology accuracy. Moreover, histopathological findings do not always correlate with the clinical phenotype10.

A diagnostic calculator developed by Lundberg et al. demonstrated that a definitive diagnosis can be reached with up to 90% accuracy even in the absence of histopathological data4. Furthermore, the interpretation of muscle biopsy can be particularly challenging and is often subject to interobserver variability10.

In summary, muscle biopsy performance could be viewed as an element almost exclusive to the IIM research context. In this sense, other techniques beyond light microscopy could be explored to gain a deeper understanding of the evaluation of the myofiber damage or integrity. For example, the electron microscopy within the IIM field has been primarily based on Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), with a strong emphasis on IBM11,12 due to its well-recognized histopathological features. Our research group recently published a preliminary report where Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was employed for assessing myofiber ultrastructure changes13. This innovative approach holds the possibility of uncovering novel myofiber alterations that might be unexplored in traditional histological methods (H&E staining). These contributions could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathological mechanisms.

To achieve this, a transdisciplinary team integrating expertise from optical physics, materials engineering, chemistry, and medicine (including rheumatologists, nerve and muscle experts, and pathologists) was recruited. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to perform a SEM-based characterization of myofiber ultrastructure through a qualitative and quantitative approach, including the empirical description of key alterations (loss of hexagonality, myofiber size variability, porosities, ruptures, perforations, and sarcolemma remnants) and the morphometric assessment through the measurement of myofiber cross-sectional area (CSA), as well.

Results

In the present study, we performed a SEM-based characterization of 78 muscle biopsies performed on quadriceps (n = 31) and deltoid (n = 47) muscles. As a control group (CG), 8 muscle samples (two from quadriceps and six from deltoid) of patients who attended rheumatology outpatient clinics and underwent muscle biopsy as part of the clinical approach, with previous written consent were included.

According to EULAR/ACR 2017, the ENMC classifications (2004, 2011 and 2023), and the CLASS project 20244–9, our study cohort comprised a broad clinical spectrum of IIM (n = 70). The cohort included DM (n = 42, including three patients with ADM), PM (n = 11), IBM (n = 3), IMNM (n = 2), overlap myositis (OV; n = 6), cancer-associated myositis (CAM; n = 2), and ASyS (n = 4). The SEM-based ultrastructure characterization included both qualitative and quantitative approaches. First, we empirically and qualitatively assessed the myofiber ultrastructural changes and categorized them as either present or absent. Based on morphological features, these alterations included: (1) loss of the myofiber hexagonality; (2) myofiber size variability; (3) porosities (circular indentations on the myofiber surface); (4) ruptures (elongated surface indentations on the myofiber surface); (5) perforations (deep longitudinal tunneling resembling bamboo stalks in panoramic view), and (6) sarcolemma remnant (tubular endomysial cavities from lost myofibers). Subsequently, we conducted a quantitative morphometric analysis of the myofibers through the measurement of the CSA of individual myofibers within each muscle fascicle. For each patient, we calculated measures of central tendency (mean) and dispersion [standard deviation (S.D.) and coefficient of variation (C.V.)].

Initially, we included all the clinical spectrum of IIM to describe the SEM-based ultrastructure characterization. However, after the clinical experts consensus (Gabriel Medrano-Ramirez, Steven Vargas-Cañas, Angélica Vargas-Guerrero, Maribell Cabrera-López, Arcelia Llamas-García and Monica Vazquez-Del Mercado), we excluded OV, CAM and ASyS, to minimize biological heterogeneity and to focus on primary pathology, thereby improving the interpretability of phenotype specific ultrastructural patterns.

Accordingly, we focused our analysis on DM (including ADM), PM, IBM, and IMNM. Therefore, our study group was composed of 58 muscle samples (DM = 42, PM = 11, IMNM = 2, and IBM = 3) from the quadriceps (n = 20) or deltoid muscles (n = 38).

Qualitative assessment uncover potential differential ultrastructural alterations

As an initial step in the qualitative ultrastructural assessment, we examined the frequency of any ultrastructural alteration across the full spectrum of myositis (DM, PM, IMNM, IBM, ASyS, CAM, OV) and the GC (n = 78). Subsequently, we focused on the 58 selected samples (DM, IMNM, IBM, PM); however, due to severe damage and loss of morphology, we could not assess a quadriceps muscle sample from a PM patient. Therefore, a total of 57 IIM muscle samples were analyzed, considering both the quadriceps (n = 19) and deltoid muscles (n = 38). Next, we analyzed the distribution of specific ultrastructural alterations (porosities, ruptures, perforations, and sarcolemma remnant) across the distinct clinical spectrum of myositis (Fig. 2b-e).

The distribution of the sarcolemma remnant differed significantly across IIM subgroups (p = 0.027); it was only observed in 1 of 3 IBM patients (33%) and 1 of 4 ASyS patients (25%) (Fig. 1d). Finally, the frequency distribution of perforations also differed across IIM spectrum (p = 0.015); it was observed in 1 of 2 IMNM (50%) and 1 of 4 ASyS patients (25%) (Fig. 2e).

This qualitative ultrastructural assessment was also performed in a subset of 58 selected samples (DM, IMNM, IBM, PM). When excluding the non-specific spectrum of myositis, we observed clear differences in the frequency of ultrastructural alterations across myositis clinical phenotypes (p = 0.003). Remarkably, although DM was the most prevalent phenotype in our cohort, it consistently showed a lower prevalence of porosities and ruptures, and a complete absence of perforations and sarcolemma remnant, whereas ultrastructural alterations were present in up to 100% of other clinical phenotypes (Fig. 2a-e).

Since DM appeared to have a better-preserved myofiber, we further grouped our patients into DM and non-DM (PM, IMNM, and IBM). As expected, the general distribution of myofiber ultrastructural changes differed between DM (n = 42) and non-DM (n = 15), being more frequent in non-DM muscles (p = 0.002) (Fig. 2f). Specifically, we also observed a higher frequency of porosities (p = 0.007) and ruptures (p = 0.014) in non-DM biopsies. Due to the small sample size of IBM and IMNM, perforations and sarcolemma remnant may not have reached significance (Fig. 2f).

Quadriceps exhibit higher frequencies of Ultrastructural alterations compared to Deltoid muscles

As previously mentioned, the qualitative ultrastructural assessment encompassed both the quadriceps (n = 29) and deltoid muscles (n = 41). Due to the intrinsic anatomical and functional disparities, a separate analysis was performed. We observed that 22 of 28 (79%) quadriceps muscle samples presented at least one ultrastructural change; porosities being the most frequent (22/28, 79%), followed by ruptures (19/28, 68%), perforations (2/28, 7%), and sarcolemma remnant (2/28, 7%). Among deltoid muscle samples, at least one structural change was present in 21 of 41 (51%) samples. Notably, porosities (18/41, 44%) and ruptures (13/41, 31%) were the most prevalent features in the deltoid muscles. In contrast, no perforations or sarcolemma remnants were detected. Overall, a higher frequency of ultrastructural changes (p = 0.025), including porosities (p = 0.006), and ruptures (p = 0.003), was observed in quadriceps compared to deltoid muscles (Fig. 2g).

Porosities and ruptures in muscles reflect increased damage: lower MMT8 score and higher CPK and AST levels

To determine the clinical significance of ultrastructural alterations, we next explored whether these ultrastructural features reflected the degree of muscle weakness and biochemical evidence of fiber injury. We found that the presence of porosities in the quadriceps muscle was associated with a lower MYOACT score (p = 0.007) (Fig. 3a).

In the deltoid muscle, the presence of porosities was associated with lower MMT8 score (p = 0.011), elevated ALT levels (p = 0.027), and a trend toward higher CPK levels (p = 0.050) (Fig. 3b-d). Likewise, the presence of ruptures in the deltoid muscles was associated with increased CPK (p = 0.037) and ALT (p = 0.035) levels (Fig. 3e-f).

Integrating clinical, histological, and ultrastructural dimensions: necrosis associates with perforations

Due to the well-known discordance between clinical presentation and biopsy findings in IIM, we explored whether ultrastructural findings could align with H&E features. In our study group, histopathologic and clinical diagnoses corresponded in only 58% of cases, mostly in DM. These discrepancies highlight the well-recognized gap between clinical and pathological classification in IIM and underscore the added value of ultrastructural analysis in refining the characterization of muscle injury. Figure 2 illustrates the classical histopathological hallmarks of each phenotype and proposes the corresponding ultrastructural features observed in our cohort.

We conducted a frequency distribution analysis comparing conventional H&E parameters (inflammatory infiltrates, atrophy, necrosis, fatty replacement) with ultrastructural alterations (porosities, ruptures, perforations, sarcolemma remnant). This approach allowed us to identify whether classical markers of tissue damage observed in light microscopy are systematically associated with the ultrastructural features we report under SEM, strengthening the robustness of our findings.

In this analysis, we observed that patients with fatty replacement exhibited a lower frequency of ultrastructural damage (37.5 vs. 70.4%; p = 0.037). This association suggests that fatty replacement, a marker of chronicity and irreversible muscle remodeling in H&E, is less likely to coexist with active ultrastructural lesions, which are preferentially detected in preserved fibers still undergoing injury.

Necrosis exhibited a trend toward association with perforations (12.5% vs. 0%; p = 0.050). These findings suggest that perforations might represent a specific ultrastructural feature of myofiber injury, occurring predominantly in necrotic contexts. Collectively, these associations indicate that our SEM findings are not incidental but instead show meaningful correlations with established histopathological markers of muscle damage, thereby bridging conventional pathology with high-resolution ultrastructural analysis.

Quantitative morphometric assessment: size variability is higher in Quadriceps

To strengthen our SEM-based ultrastructure characterization, we conducted a quantitative morphometric analysis in addition to the qualitative assessment.

Due to severe damage and loss of morphology, the previously mentioned PM-quadriceps muscle sample could not be assessed either morphometrically. Additionally, the presence of perforations in two IMNM-quadriceps muscle samples destroyed the myofibers, hindering the morphometric analysis. In total 67 samples were morphometrically analyzed, considering both the quadriceps (n = 26) and deltoid muscles (n = 41).

As described in detail in the Methods section, the morphometric data obtained included the mean, S.D., and C.V. of each muscle sample. Consistent with the previous qualitative approach, quantitative morphometric analysis was also performed separately for the quadriceps and deltoid muscles, given their distinct anatomical and functional characteristics. When contrasting the morphometric measurements between muscle groups, we observed that quadriceps myofibers exhibited higher variability in size, as indicated by a higher C.V. compared to deltoid myofibers (p = 0.007) (Fig. 3g).

Quantitative Quadriceps morphometry: more preserved size in DM

In the DM quadriceps muscle, we found that longer disease duration, from symptom onset and diagnosis, correlated with (1) lower CPK serum levels; (2) higher percentage of normal muscle fibers, and (3) lower percentage of small muscle fibers. Additionally, we observed a correlation between CPK levels and morphometry parameters; when CPK levels are lower, there are more normal muscle fibers (rs = − 0.689; p = 0.028) and lower small muscle fibers (rs = 0.689; p = 0.028); suggesting a potential link between serum muscle enzyme levels and the myofiber size of the DM quadriceps (Fig. 4a-i).

Conversely, in the non-DM quadriceps muscle, we observed that longer disease duration is correlated with reduced mean CSA (rs = − 0.893; p = 0.007). In these muscle samples, we also observed higher levels of CRP when the mean CSA is smaller (rs = − 0.786; p = 0.036), and higher levels of CPK when the mean CSA of muscle fibers is bigger (rs = 0.929; p = 0.003) (Fig. 4j-l) which denotes a heterogeneous behavior in the non-DM group.

Quantitative Deltoid morphometry: higher proportion of normal-size myofibers in DM

The morphometric data of myofiber-deltoid muscles were also classified according to CSA into small, standard, and large (as described in detail in the Methods section).

Potential associations between deltoid myofiber morphometry and clinical context were explored. Intriguingly, the stratification into DM and non-DM revealed a higher percentage of normal-sized (p = 0.038) and fewer small myofibers (p = 0.038), however, non-DM deltoid myofibers also showed higher standard deviation (p = 0.008), reflecting higher myofiber size variation, which could explain this behavior (Fig. 5a-c).

Ruptures in Deltoid muscles are not present in atrophic myofibers

To comprehensively integrate the SEM-based ultrastructure characterization, we merged both qualitative and quantitative approaches and further explored potential associations between them. We found that ruptures in the deltoid muscle are present in patients with greater myofiber CSA (p = 0.012), higher percentage of normal-sized myofibers (p = 0.008), and lower small myofibers (p = 0.008) (Fig. 5d-f), suggesting that ruptures might not be present in atrophic myofibers (Fig. 2).

We also evaluated the possible correlation between morphometric measurements. We found a significant correlation between the mean and the S.D. of CSA in both quadriceps (rs = 0.627; p = 0.001) and deltoid muscles (rs = 0.812; p < 0.001), suggesting that as myofiber size increases, there is also an increase in size variability (Fig. 5g-h).

Standardized Z-score analysis highlights myofiber atrophy in IIM muscles

To refine the comparison of myofiber sizes, we calculated generalized Z-scores using previously reported CSA means and SD from healthy muscle14,15. This approach standardized our data, enabling a more precise comparison across patients by adjusting for differences in central tendency and dispersion. As a result, Z-scores provided a clearer representation of deviations in myofiber size relative to normal values.

Using this methodology, we observed a predominance of negative Z-scores across our study group, indicating that myofiber sizes were generally smaller than standardized normal values (53/67, 79%), suggesting prevalent myofiber atrophy among IIM patients (Fig. 5a).

When comparing the frequency of atrophic, normal, and hypertrophic myofibers based on Z-score thresholds, we found a difference in frequency distribution, showing a higher prevalence of atrophic myofibers in the deltoid muscles. When comparing according to phenotype we observed a predominance of atrophic myofibers in DM (31/42, 74%). Notably, normal-sized myofibers were observed only in DM (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The IIM has been a rich study field in rheumatology and neurology over the last two decades, despite these diseases being considered orphan or rare diseases due to their low prevalence16. Nevertheless, it is a fascinating field of research that helps clinicians to develop more effective clinical approaches, accurate diagnoses, and informed therapeutic decisions to preserve and restore the quality of life in this patient group.

This research paper focuses on a state-of-the-art approach using a qualitative and quantitative SEM-based ultrastructure characterization. This national cohort was established through the collaboration of third-level attention centers: Nuevo Hospital Civil de Guadalajara, Hospital General de Mexico “Dr. Eduardo Liceaga”, Instituto Nacional de Cardiología “Dr. Ignacio Chavez”, and Instituto Nacional de Neurología y Neurocirugía “Dr. Manuel Velasco Suarez”. Thanks to this multi-center collaborative effort, we were able to recruit a robust sample size considering the low frequency and the type of study, as it represented a discomforting procedure for each patient.

Biopsies were performed on either the quadriceps or deltoid muscles to obtain a broader spectrum of muscle involvement in IIM. The quadriceps, a large and weight-bearing muscle, is frequently affected in conditions such as DM, PM, and IBM, making it an important site for evaluating proximal muscle pathology17. The deltoid, being part of the upper limb musculature, often reflects symmetrical muscle weakness in DM, PM, and IMNM18–20. By sampling both muscle groups, we aimed to capture the heterogeneous nature of muscle involvement in IIM, as well as potential differences in ultrastructural changes between these muscle groups. This approach avoids bias that might result from analyzing a single muscle group, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding of disease progression and muscle pathology in IIM.

As mentioned, our SEM-based ultrastructure characterization included both qualitative and quantitative analyses. When qualitatively assessing the ultrastructural changes, we found that all patients had lost the hexagonal shape of their myofibers and exhibited myofiber size variability, independent of the muscle type (quadriceps or deltoid).

It is important to discuss that, although fiber size variability or loss of hexagonal shape can be detected by light microscopy, SEM provides a three-dimensional surface topography that reveals features such as porosities or ruptures not visible in 2D histological sections. Besides, in several cases, SEM also allowed identification of inflammatory infiltrates, with leukocytes observed around or within ruptured fibers at intermediate magnifications. These findings were cross validated with H&E sections. While TEM is better suited for organelle-level abnormalities, SEM was intentionally employed to evaluate surface fiber integrity, where porosities, ruptures, and perforations could be systematically documented. This complementary perspective justifies SEM as a tool that expands the pathological framework beyond conventional histology and TEM.

We are also conscious of another important factor to consider when talking about muscle damage in IIM: the well-documented spatial heterogeneity of muscle pathology, as perifascicular atrophy in DM. To reduce misinterpretation, we paired SEM images with H&E validation.

Although we did not find a specific association between perifascicular atrophy and ultrastructural alterations, we observed two relevant associations between H&E and SEM findings. We found that muscles with fatty replacement are less likely to coexist with ultrastructural alterations and that perforations are possibly represented as necrosis in H&E.

We consider it very important to highlight and discuss that our ultrastructural findings denote a better conserved profile of muscle fibers in DM patients compared with other IIM subgroups. At first glance, this might seem contradictory to the classical description of perifascicular atrophy in H&E sections, which is considered one of the most characteristic histopathological hallmarks of DM. However, these observations are not mutually exclusive; rather, they reflect different levels of histopathological analysis.

Perifascicular atrophy, resulting from microangiopathy and activation of type I interferon pathways, manifests as a focal reduction in the caliber of fibers at the periphery of fascicles. Importantly, this phenomenon shows a patchy distribution and does not necessarily imply a widespread structural damage or severe disruption of the muscle ultrastructure. Our SEM results suggest that, beyond these focal areas of atrophy, DM muscle tissue is relatively better preserved than in non-DM entities, where the inflammatory process leads to more extensive fiber destruction. This means that the findings in H&E and SEM are not mutually exclusive but may reflect distinct pathophysiological processes (ischemia/interferon in H&E vs. ultrastructural integrity in SEM).

In this context, the coexistence of both findings strengthens the notion that the pathophysiology of DM differs from that of other myositis subtypes: rather than massive necrosis and direct fiber destruction, DM is characterized by selective vascular and immunological alterations that drive perifascicular atrophy but still allow for greater preservation of the global ultrastructural architecture. This contrast may partly explain the clinical differences and distinct therapeutic responses observed in DM compared with other IIM.

Interestingly, we found associations between H&E and SEM findings as an association observed between fatty replacement and the presence of ultrastructural alterations. Fatty infiltration generally reflects chronic and irreversible loss of muscle fibers, where contractile tissue has been substituted by adipocytes. In this stage, fewer myofibers remain available to exhibit ultrastructural damage, explaining the reduced frequency of porosities, ruptures, or perforations under SEM. Conversely, in samples without fatty replacement, a higher proportion of preserved fibers are present and thus more likely to display ongoing structural disruption. These findings suggest that ultrastructural alterations preferentially capture processes of active fiber injury, whereas fatty degeneration represents the end stage of tissue remodeling in which the substrate for such alterations has already been lost.

In addition, an association between necrosis and perforation was observed. Perforations were detected in 12.5% (2/16) of samples exhibiting necrosis, while they were absent in all cases without necrosis (0/54). Although we are aware that perforation-like structures may mimic artifacts such as freezing-induced crystal expansion21, their exclusive presence in necrotic samples argues against a random occurrence. Instead, this association supports the interpretation of perforation as a candidate ultrastructural feature of acute myofiber injury, representing the SEM counterpart of the destructive processes histologically captured as fiber necrosis.

The observation that clinical and histopathological diagnoses did not always match with ultrastructural alterations is consistent with the known discordance between clinical picture and biopsy findings in IIM. Our data suggest that ultrastructural analysis can provide an additional dimension to capture ongoing fiber injury, thereby complementing both clinical and histopathological assessments. This integrative approach may help explain cases of clinical–pathological discordance.

Regarding possible artifacts related to SEM sample preparation (e.g., surface cracks or generalized collapse) were considered. However, it is important to note that the ultrastructural features described in this study (porosities, ruptures, perforations, and sarcolemma remnant) were consistently observed in independent samples and were not uniformly distributed across phenotypes or muscle groups, ruling out the possibility of nonspecific processing artifacts.

One remarkable finding in the qualitative ultrastructural assessment was that DM is the least affected clinical phenotype. As well as the higher prevalence of perforations in IMNM and sarcolemma remnant in IBM. This highlights their potential as a disease-specific ultrastructural signature, to be confirmed in more extensive studies.

In our study, we identified a distinctive ultrastructural pattern characterized by myofiber perforations in a subset of patients clinically classified as IMNM and ASyS. Specifically, perforations were observed in one of two IMNM cases and in one of four ASyS cases analyzed.

The IMNM phenotype comprises three main serological subtypes: anti-HMGCR–positive, anti-SRP-positive, and seronegative22. However, as anti-HMGCR status was not assessed in this cohort, it remains unclear whether myofiber perforations are specifically associated with anti-HMGCR–positive disease or instead represent a broader ultrastructural feature of the IMNM clinical phenotype. Future studies integrating comprehensive autoantibody profiling with ultrastructural analyses will be required to clarify these relationships and to determine the potential specificity of myofiber perforations across myositis subtypes.

It is important to note that no previous studies have previously evaluated muscle damage by SEM in the context of IIM. The closest reference comes from a report describing a structure resembling sarcolemma remnant, observed after intentional digestion of extraocular muscle fibers in a rabbit model designed to examine surrounding connective tissue23. Although the specific digestive method was not disclosed, the resulting ultrastructural image closely resembles the sarcolemma remnant identified in IBM in the present study. This resemblance raises the intriguing possibility that the immune system could be mimicking a similar “digestion-like” process, leading to selective myofiber disintegration.

When we compared the ultrastructural changes according to the type of muscle biopsied, we found more ultrastructural changes in the quadriceps.

When evaluating the C.V. of the CSA between deltoid and quadriceps, as expected we observed more variation in quadriceps myofibers. In addition, we found that disease evolution was strongly correlated with CPK, normal myofibers, and small myofibers in DM. Meanwhile, in non-DM, we observed the mean CSA decreased according to post-diagnosis time.

Disease duration represents an important clinical variable that may influence muscle ultrastructure, particularly through cumulative injury or remodeling processes. In our cohort, although disease duration appeared slightly longer in certain clinical phenotypes, these differences did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, exploratory analyses did not identify consistent associations between disease duration and specific ultrastructural alterations. Notably, within the DM subgroup, longer disease duration was correlated with a higher proportion of normal-sized fibers, a lower proportion of small fibers, and reduced serum creatine kinase levels. These findings suggest that, at least in DM, prolonged disease duration does not necessarily correspond to increased ultrastructural damage but may instead reflect disease stabilization and/or the effects of therapeutic interventions.

In addition, these findings agreed with the favorable prognosis and good therapy response in DM24. The predominance of normal-sized myofibers, the reduction in small fibers, and the decrease in serum CPK levels with longer disease duration suggest effective muscle regeneration or preservation over time in these patients. Remarkably, these findings challenge the paradigm within the field of IIM that muscle damage is irreversible, as Bohan and Peter suggested2,3.

In contrast, the non-DM group (IMNM, IBM, and PM) encompasses a more heterogeneous and typically more aggressive disease spectrum25–27. This is consistent with our morphometric findings, where we observed a progressive reduction in mean myofiber CSA to post-diagnosis time among non-DM cases, suggesting an ongoing and cumulative muscle damage, particularly in IMNM. Our findings could offer valuable insights into traditional clinical approach tools and current classification criteria.

Z-score normalization of myofiber CSA enabled contextualization of our morphometric data relative to healthy reference values, highlighting that muscle fibers in IIM are generally reduced in size, indicating atrophy. Although DM exhibited a relatively better preservation of myofiber compared to other clinical phenotypes, these findings denote that muscle damage is a widespread feature across the IIM spectrum (Fig. 5a–c).

This integrative approach of SEM-based ultrastructural characterization provides a disruptive framework for uncovering hidden morphological patterns in complex muscle diseases. Altogether, our findings could represent a paradigm shift to redefine the landscape of IIMs, unveiling the first evidence of distinct ultrastructural phenotypes across diagnostic subtypes. However, we are aware that these findings must be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size in some subgroups, particularly IMNM and IBM. Their low prevalence in Mexico limited recruitment, but the multicenter design strengthens generalizability. Larger cohorts will be needed to confirm the robustness of these associations.

Methods

Patients

Seventy-eight patients classified with IIM according to ENMC 2004/2011 and EULAR/ACR 2017 criteria were enrolled in this multicentric cross-sectional study4,5,7. The patients were recruited through a collaboration of third-level attention hospitals in Mexico, including: Hospital Civil de Guadalajara “Dr. Juan I. Menchaca”, Hospital General de México “Dr. Eduardo Liceaga”, Instituto Nacional de Cardiología “Dr. Ignacio Chávez”, and Instituto Nacional de Neurología y Neurocirugía “Dr. Manuel Velasco Suárez”.

Informed written consent was obtained from every subject before enrollment in the study. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This protocol was approved by the appropriate Ethical and Research committees (Jalisco state registration number 0657/23 HCJIM/2023) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Autoantibodies detection

Autoantibodies were assessed using a commercial line-blot assay (EUROLINE Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies; EUROIMMUN Medizinische Labordiagnostika AG, Lübeck, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The assay enables the simultaneous detection of twelve myositis-specific autoantibodies (anti–Mi-2α, Mi-2β, TIF1γ, MDA5, NXP2, SAE1, Jo-1, SRP, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, and OJ) and four myositis-associated autoantibodies (Ku, PM-Scl100, PM-Scl75, and Ro-52). Test strips containing immobilized, biochemically characterized autoantigens were incubated with diluted patient serum samples. Bound autoantibodies were visualized through an enzymatic colorimetric reaction. Signal acquisition and analysis were performed using the EUROLineScan software, and results were interpreted based on band intensity in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Muscle samples

Muscle biopsies from 78 subjects with IIM and CG were recruited, comprising 31 quadriceps and 47 deltoid muscle samples. These were obtained by open surgical biopsy, to capture a broader representation of muscle involvement across anatomical sites. The tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stored at 4 °C until further processing for SEM.

Scanning electron microscopy sample processing and image acquisition

Muscle samples were analyzed independently by two blinded experts in SEM at two different centers: Instituto Transdisciplinar de Investigación y Servicios (ITRANS) from Universidad de Guadalajara, and Universidad Autónoma de San Luis Potosí (UASLP).

Samples were first fixed in a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature to preserve cellular structures. After fixation, the samples were rinsed three times in the same buffer. Next, the samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), with each step lasting 10–15 min. Posteriorly, they were collocated in hexamethyldisilane with absolute alcohol (1:1) for fifteen minutes, then tissues were reposed in hexamethyldisilane for fifteen minutes and subsequently were dried with the air flux of the extraction hood (Burdinola, BST1200). The dehydrated tissues were prepared in slides with carbon tape and were then coated with a layer of gold by a sputter coater (Denton vacuum, Desk V). Samples with dimensions of 3 × 5 × 0.5–1 mm³ were consistently mounted on a vertical pin to obtain standardized cross-sectional views. After sample processing, images were acquired using a JEOL JSM-6610LV. The number of individual myofibers observed in the SEM micrographs is roughly between 50 and 100, depending on the magnification employed. Each researcher employed the microscope available at their respective institution.

Qualitative ultrastructural assessment

Myofiber ultrastructural changes were defined and classified based on morphological features into six categories: (1) loss of the myofiber hexagonality; (2) myofiber size variability; (3) porosities; (4) ruptures; (5) perforations, and (6) sarcolemma remnants.

Porosities were defined as circular indentations on the muscle fiber surface, with measured areas ranging from 0.5 to 16.2 μm². Ruptures consisted of elongated surface indentations, ranging from 10.0 to 18.0 μm². Perforations refers to large tunneling disrupting the structural integrity of the myofiber, sometimes exceeding the fiber size, with areas ranging from 137.1 to 9230.0 μm². Sarcolemma remnant was identified as tubular cavities corresponding to the previous location of lost myofibers, with areas between 519.5 and 1826.1 μm².

Quantitative morphometric assessment

The cross-sectional area (CSA) of individual myofibers within the fascicle was measured from SEM images using ImageJ v 2.14.0/1.54f software. As selection criteria, only myofibers in transverse orientation, which enabled accurate area measurement, were included in the analysis.

For each sample, descriptive statistics were calculated, including central tendency measures (mean) and dispersion (standard deviation and coefficient of variation). This approach enabled the assessment of both the average fiber size and the degree of size heterogeneity within the muscle tissue.

Quadriceps muscles size classification

To contextualize the CSA of quadriceps muscle fibers in our dataset, once the CSA of each myofiber was determined, we classified fibers as small, normal, or large using previously reported ranges of CSA from the vastus lateralis muscle in healthy subjects14. In this reference report, the data were stratified by sex and fiber type. Since fiber types could not be distinguished in our dataset, we applied a composite reference. Fibers were considered small when CSA measured < 1105 µ2 in females, and < 1615 µ2 in males; normal when ranged from 1105 µ2 to 7095µ2 in females, and 1615 µ2 to 9548 µ2 in males; and large when CSA measured > 7095 µ2 in females and > 9458 µ2 in males.

Deltoid muscles size classification

To contextualize the CSA of deltoid muscle fibers in our dataset, we categorized each myofiber as small, normal, or large based on previously reported CSA of deltoid muscles in healthy individuals according to age15. Since no CSA normal ranges of deltoid muscles were available and the data were stratified per age, we applied a simple arithmetic mean and an S.D., assuming equal weight for each age group. This approach yielded an estimated reference mean of 2333.6 μm² and a S.D. of 322.96 μm². Values falling within ± 1 SD of the reference mean were considered within the expected morphometric range (normal muscle fibers when CSA measured 2134 µ2 to 2532 µ2). Values beyond this range were interpreted as reduced (small muscle fibers when CSA < 2134 µ2) or increased (large muscle fibers when CSA > 2532 µ2) relative to our reference values.

Z-score normalization of myofiber size

Generalized Z-scores for each myofiber CSA were calculated to facilitate standardized comparisons of myofiber size across individuals. Reference mean and S.D. for Z-scores calculi were obtained from previously published morphometric studies of healthy skeletal muscle13,14.

Since these studies did not report a generalized mean CSA and S.D., we derived a composite reference by averaging the reported mean CSA and S.D.; therefore, the values considered for Z-score calculation were 3491.7 ± 849.22 µ2 for females and 4662.2 ± 1163.00 µ2 for males in the quadriceps, meanwhile 2333.6 ± 198.70 was considered for deltoid. Statistics

Normality of the variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data are presented as mean, minimum, and maximum, or as percentages, as appropriate. Due to the small sample size of the sub analysis, comparisons of quantitative variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL) and GraphPad Prism version 8.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Limitations

We acknowledge that the small size of our cohort limits the robustness of conclusions regarding the specificity of ultrastructural alterations associated with this condition. Further studies with international collaborations are required to expand and validate our analyses.

Disease duration is a clinically relevant factor that may influence muscle ultrastructure; however, the small sample size within each clinical phenotype limits the ability to disentangle the effects of disease duration from phenotype-specific pathological mechanisms. Although exploratory analyses were conducted, larger longitudinal studies will be required to more definitively evaluate the impact of disease chronicity on ultrastructural muscle alterations.

Otherwise, autoantibody profiling was performed using a commercial line-blot assay (Euroline: Autoimmune Inflammatory Myopathies from EUROIMMUN Medizinische Labordiagnostika AG). However, confirmatory assays such as ELISA or complementary techniques were not performed. Consequently, the presence or absence of additional clinically relevant autoantibodies, most notably anti-HMGCR, could not be assessed. Further studies including a full characterization of autoantibodies profile are warranted to directly associate specific ultrastructural features.
