Long-term trends and factors associated with migrant participation in Korea’s National Health Screening Program: a nationwide retrospective population-based study
Authors: Woojin Jang, Hyeon-Kyoung Cheong, YongJoo Rhee, In-Hwan Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigrant health is an increasingly important future determinant of population health in Korea due to growing migrant numbers. Preventive care inequalities affecting migrants may receive limited attention, however because of relative health advantages at their time of arrival and because downstream adverse outcomes may not yet be apparent in this group. In this context, Korea’s National Health Screening Program (NHSP), which is largely free at the point of use, provides a valuable window into early disparities in preventive care participation. We aimed to quantify 15-year disparities in Korea’s National Health Screening Program (NHSP) participation between migrants and non-migrants, identify associated factors, and assess the persistence of these gaps, even among long-term enrollees after covariate adjustment.MethodsIn this retrospective population-based cohort study using linked nationwide administrative data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service and National Health Screening Program records, 1,244,820 migrants and 2,050,771 non-migrants eligible in 2022 were analyzed. Annual participation trends from 2008 to 2022 were examined using crude and age-standardized rates. Multivariable logistic regression among individuals continuously enrolled for ≥ 10 years assessed the independent association of migrant status with ever having undergone screening by 2022.ResultsAge-standardized participation rates were consistently lower among migrants throughout 2008–2022. In 2022, crude participation was 40.3% among migrants and 53.5% among the non-migrant comparison group, whereas age-standardized rates were 31.5% and 38.8%, respectively. Among long-term enrollees, migrants had significantly lower adjusted odds of ever having undergone screening by 2022 (adjusted OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.18–0.19).ConclusionsScreening participation disparities persisted throughout the study period and remained evident even among migrants continuously enrolled in National Health Insurance for more than a decade. After multivariable adjustment, this disparity was more pronounced than the crude comparisons suggested. In the context of Korea’s ongoing demographic change, these findings underscore the importance of early identification and management of migrant screening inequalities. Migrant-tailored, multilingual, and navigation-supported strategies are needed to reduce the risk of preventive care gaps becoming more deeply rooted health inequalities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-02941-9.

Background

Korea is undergoing rapid demographic change, characterized by the world’s lowest fertility and exceptionally rapid population aging. The total fertility rate was 0.75 in 2024 (0.72 in 2023), the lowest of any country, and the proportion of the population aged 65 years or older reached 19.2% in 2024, with Korea becoming a “super-aged” society at the end of 2024 [1, 2]. Against this backdrop, the resident foreign population has grown steadily, reaching approximately 2.65 million—about 5.2% of the total population—at the end of 2024 [3], and the migration-background population is officially projected to rise to 8.1% of the population by 2042 [4]. The health of this growing migrant population will increasingly influence the country’s overall population health [5, 6]. Despite this, inequalities in preventive care among migrants may receive limited attention as many of these individuals arrive with a relative health advantage and because downstream adverse outcomes may not yet be apparent among this group [6, 7]. In this setting, participation in health screening can serve as an early indicator of structural disadvantage, preceding more visible inequalities in morbidity or mortality and therefore warranting earlier stage intervention [8].

Korea offers a particularly informative context in which to examine whether such health inequalities are already emerging. The National Health Screening Program (NHSP), administered through the National Health Insurance (NHI) system, is an organized, population-based program in Korea offering biennial or annual general health examinations. These examinations are provided biennially for most enrollees and annually for non-office (manual) workers, and include anthropometric measurements, blood pressure, blood tests (e.g., fasting glucose, total cholesterol, hemoglobin, and liver enzymes), urinalysis, and a health-behavior questionnaire [9, 10]. Registered foreign nationals residing in Korea for at least six months are required to enroll in the NHI and are formally eligible for the NHSP [9, 11]. Short-term visitors, individuals with irregular immigration status, and certain temporary visa holders are not required to enroll in the NHI and are therefore not captured in the present study [9, 11]. The foreign resident population is predominantly composed of employment visa holders, marriage migrants, and international students, with the composition varying considerably by nationality and period of arrival [3]. The cost of the general health examination component of the NHSP is fully covered by the NHIS at the point of use, minimizing direct financial burdens and making any remaining disparities in participation more likely to reflect structural, informational, and procedural barriers [9]. We surmised that if disparities were observed even under these conditions, they would suggest structural barriers that deserve attention before they become further embedded in the system.

Existing Korean studies on preventive screening in migrant cohorts have largely focused on specific subgroups, particularly women who migrated to Korea through marriage to Korean nationals, and have examined specific outcomes such as cervical cancer screening rather than participation in the general NHSP [12–14]. There is a real scarcity of nationwide analyses, covering the full migrant population eligible for the program, of long-term participation trends or whether disparities persist among individuals with sustained insurance enrollment. This is a meaningful gap as if participation disparities remain even among migrants with long-term NHI continuity—a group for whom insurance instability and early settlement difficulties cannot explain non-participation—this would suggest the presence of structural barriers that persist beyond the initial acculturation period [15]. Clarifying this issue is increasingly important in Korea as it is clear that migrant health will increasingly influence the country’s overall future health profile.

Using nationwide NHI administrative data, this study had four objectives as follows: to compare baseline characteristics between migrants and non-migrants eligible for the NHSP in 2022; to describe annual trends in crude and age-standardized NHSP participation from 2008 to 2022, and characterize changes in the participation gap over time; to assess factors associated with participation among individuals continuously enrolled in NHI for at least 10 years; and to compare lifestyle factors and clinical measurements between migrants and non-migrants who underwent screening in 2022. We hypothesized that migrants would show lower participation than non-migrants and that this disparity would persist after accounting for demographic differences. We further hypothesized that lower participation odds would persist even among migrant individuals continuously enrolled in NHI for at least 10 years after adjustment for socioeconomic and insurance-related covariates.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective population-based study utilized administrative data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), linked to National Health Screening Program (NHSP) records. We conducted four sets of related analyses. First, we compared the baseline characteristics between migrants and a non-migrant comparison cohort among individuals eligible for the NHSP in 2022. Second, we investigated annual crude and age-standardized trends in NHSP participation from 2008 to 2022. Third, to examine screening participation among long-term enrollees, we analyzed factors associated with having ever undergone NHSP screening among individuals continuously enrolled in the NHIS for at least 10 years. Fourth, we compared lifestyle factors and clinical measurements between migrant and non-migrant individuals who underwent screening in 2022.

Study population and cohort construction

Migrants were defined as NHIS enrollees identified by foreign registration numbers. Accordingly, foreign nationals who had acquired Korean nationality were not included in the migrant group as they would be issued a resident registration number and no longer retain a foreign registration number. For the annual trend analysis, the migrant population was treated as a dynamic cohort comprising all foreign nationals registered in the NHIS and eligible for the NHSP in the corresponding year. Because of the NHIS data extraction structure, migrants were not individually matched to Korean nationals in each calendar year. Instead, Korean nationals were selected once in 2008 at an approximately 1:4 ratio using frequency matching by sex and age and were followed through 2022 as the non-migrant comparison cohort. Accordingly, the age distribution of the non-migrant comparison cohort increased over time and age was adjusted for in the multivariable analyses to account for this. Attrition in the non-migrant cohort was limited, reflecting Korea’s single-payer insurance structure, with the cohort size changing only from 2.12 million in 2008 to 2.05 million in 2022.

For descriptive analyses, we included individuals eligible for the NHSP in 2022. The 2022 analytic population comprised 1,244,820 migrants, representing all foreign nationals registered in the NHIS and eligible for the NHSP in 2022, and 2,050,771 non-migrants from the comparison cohort originally selected in 2008 and followed longitudinally thereafter. For the long-term enrollment analysis, we restricted the sample to individuals who had maintained NHI enrollment for at least 10 years. Migrants were required to remain enrolled and alive during the 10-year observation period after initial NHI registration, and comparable follow-up criteria were applied to the non-migrant comparison cohort. In this analysis, age group, income level, disability status, and residential region were all defined at the time of cohort entry — that is, at the time of each individual’s initial registration in the NHIS. For migrants, this corresponds to the year of first registration as a foreign national (identified by a foreign registration number) and therefore varies across individuals; for the non-migrant comparison cohort, it corresponds to the 2008 baseline at which the cohort was assembled and from which it was followed. By design, all participants included in the long-term enrollment analysis contributed at least 10 years of continuous NHIS enrollment before outcome assessment.

Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcome for the annual trend analysis was yearly NHSP participation, defined as the proportion of individuals eligible for the NHSP each year who underwent a NHSP examination during that same year. In the long-term enrollment analysis, the outcome for logistic regression was defined as having undergone at least one NHSP examination by 2022. For the 2022 descriptive comparison, baseline characteristics included sex, age group, income category, healthcare coverage type, disability status, and residential region. Healthcare coverage type was classified as Medical Aid, a government-subsidized public assistance program for low-income groups administered separately from National Health Insurance [16], or, within National Health Insurance, employee-insured or self-employed/locally insured subscribers, with dependents covered under the employee-insured subscriber to whom they are attached [11]. Income category, based on National Health Insurance premium level as a proxy for household income (denoted by quantile rank Q, from Q1 [lowest] to Q20 [highest]), was grouped as Medical Aid, Q1–Q5, Q6–Q10, Q11–Q15, and Q16–Q20, and region was classified as provinces or metropolitan cities. Among individuals who underwent screening in 2022, we additionally compared lifestyle factors and clinical measurements recorded at the examination, including body mass index (BMI), alcohol use, smoking status, reported physical activity, blood pressure, and hemoglobin level. Reported physical activity was assessed using the self-reported physical activity frequency item in the health-behavior questionnaire, with response options of none, 1–2 times per week, 3–4 times per week, 5–6 times per week, and almost daily; this variable was dichotomized as no reported physical activity versus any reported physical activity.

Covariates included in the multivariable logistic regression models were sex, age group, income category, disability status, residential region, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The CCI was derived from diagnostic records available within the NHIS database during the study period.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. For the 2022 descriptive comparison, migrants and the non-migrant comparison cohort were compared using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. Annual participation trends from 2008 to 2022 were presented as crude year-specific rates according to migrant status. In addition, age-standardized annual participation rates were calculated by direct standardization using the 2010 Korean mid-year population in 10-year age groups as the standard population. To reduce bias arising from differences in age composition between groups over time, age-standardized rates were calculated and age was included as a covariate in multivariable analyses. In addition, age-specific participation rates were calculated for selected years (2008 and 2022) to examine whether participation differences were consistent across age groups or driven primarily by differences in age composition (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Among individuals who underwent screening in 2022, lifestyle factors and clinical indicators were compared between migrants and non-migrants. Analyses for each variable were conducted among participants with available data, and the corresponding number of non-missing observations was reported. Two authors (Hyeon-Kyoung Cheong and In-Hwan Oh) directly accessed and verified the de-identified customized dataset. Data were reviewed for analytic consistency before analysis. Detailed internal database field names, extraction syntax, and complete coding algorithms could not be fully reported because the customized NHIS data extraction process was governed by administrative data-access procedures; however, transparent operational definitions for cohort construction, outcomes, and covariates are provided in the Methods and Additional file 1. In the cohort of individuals continuously enrolled for at least 10 years, univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between migrant status and having ever undergone NHSP screening by 2022. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated from the models including sex, age group, income category, disability status, residential region, and CCI. In this model, migration status was included as a covariate alongside sociodemographic and clinical factors, and the adjusted OR for migrant status reflects the independent association of migration status with screening participation after accounting for the other covariates in the pooled sample. Because attrition in the non-migrant comparison cohort was limited and the long-term analysis was restricted to individuals who remained enrolled and alive during the observation period, no additional analytical correction for loss to follow-up was applied. No formal sensitivity analyses were conducted.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.2 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and figures were generated using R version 4.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. This study was approved by the Kyung Hee University Institutional Review Board, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because de-identified administrative data were used (Approval No. KHSIRB-23-209-1[EA]). This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement [17], with relevant RECORD items considered for studies using routinely collected health data [18].

Results

Study population and characteristics of the 2022 analytic population

In 2022, the eligible population for our current study parameters comprised 1,244,820 individuals in the migrant group and 2,050,771 in the non-migrant comparison group. Migrants were more concentrated in younger age bands (30–39 years: 26.5%; 20–29 years: 17.9%), whereas the non-migrant comparison had a higher proportion of subjects aged 30–39 (32.0%) and 40–49 (20.1%). Income distributions differed, with migrants more frequently in Q11–Q15 (45.2%) and the non-migrant comparison group more frequently in Q16–Q20 (29.6%). Compared with the non-migrant comparison group, migrants were less often employee-insured (53.8% vs. 70.8%) and more often self-employed/locally insured (45.9% vs. 27.1%), and had a markedly lower registered disability prevalence (0.4% vs. 3.9%; all p < 0.0001). Time trends in participation (2008–2022)

From 2008 to 2022, crude participation increased in both groups. In the crude analyses, migrant participation was higher than that in the non-migrant comparison group from 2008 to 2012, similar in 2013 (32.5% vs. 32.6%), and lower thereafter. In age-standardized analyses, the migrant participation was lower in each year from 2008 to 2022. In 2022, crude participation was 40.3% among migrants and 53.5% among the non-migrant comparison group, whereas age-standardized participation was 31.5% and 38.8%, respectively. Crude and age-standardized trends are presented in Fig. 1 and detailed in Additional file 1: Tables S1 and S2.

Age-specific participation rates for 2008 and 2022 are presented in Additional file 1: Table S3. In 2022, migrants had lower participation than the non-migrant comparison group across all adult age groups, with the difference ranging from 7.0% points (80 + years) to 13.1% points (40–49 years). In 2008, the pattern was less uniform: migrants aged 20–29 years had higher participation than their non-migrant counterparts (32.8% vs. 19.3%), whereas migrants aged 40–79 years had lower participation than their non-migrant counterparts, with the largest difference observed in the 40–49-year age group (34.3% vs. 54.8%).

Factors associated with ever having undergone national health screening by 2022

Table 2 presents multivariable associations with ever having undergone national health screening by 2022 among individuals continuously enrolled in the NHIS for at least 10 years. Migrants had noticeably lower odds of ever having undergone screening by 2022 (crude OR 0.59, 95% CI 0.58–0.60; adjusted OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.18–0.19). Women had higher odds of participation than men (adjusted OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.27–1.31). Relative to the younger age group (20–29), the older age groups had considerably higher odds of participation (30–39 years: adjusted OR 2.17, 95% CI 1.99–2.36; 50–59 years: adjusted OR 3.71, 95% CI 3.18–4.33). Income categories showed heterogeneous associations relative to Medical Aid (e.g., Q1–Q5: adjusted OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.03–1.11; Q11–Q15: adjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.78–0.84). Registered disability was associated with higher participation than those without disability (adjusted OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.24–1.41). Residence in metropolitan areas was associated with lower participation than the provinces (adjusted OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.88–0.90), and each 1-point increase in the CCI was associated with slightly lower odds of participation (adjusted OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99).

Lifestyle factors among screening examinees in 2022

Among 1,599,380 individuals who underwent screening in 2022 (502,190 migrants and 1,097,190 non-migrant comparison group participants), lifestyle questionnaire data were available for 376,319 (74.9%) and 845,640 (77.1%) participants, respectively. Among individuals who underwent screening in 2022, migrants had a lower prevalence of obesity (37.3% vs. 41.6%) and a higher prevalence of a normal BMI (35.7% vs. 32.7%; p < 0.0001). Alcohol use was less common among migrants than non-migrants (24.6% vs. 49.5%), while current smoking was slightly more common (24.0% vs. 22.0%; p < 0.0001 for both). Reported physical activity was higher among migrants (60.8% vs. 46.1% active; p < 0.0001). Mean clinical measures differed only modestly between the groups (e.g., systolic BP 121.90 vs. 122.20 mmHg; diastolic BP 75.49 vs. 75.87 mmHg; hemoglobin 14.35 vs. 14.41 g/dL; all p < 0.0001). Discussion

Using 15 years of nationwide administrative data, this study of migrant participation in public health screening services in Korea produced three main findings. First, NHSP participation was substantially lower among migrants in 2022, with the age-standardized gap being smaller than the crude gap at 7.3% points (31.5% vs. 38.8%) compared with the non-migrant comparison group. Second, age-standardized participation rates were consistently lower among migrants throughout 2008–2022. Third, even among individuals continuously enrolled in the Korean NHIS for at least 10 years—a group for whom insurance instability cannot explain non-participation—migrants remained significantly less likely to participate after adjustment for age, sex, income, disability, region, and comorbidity (adjusted OR 0.18, 95% CI 0.18–0.19). Taken together, these findings may reflect not merely a utilization gap within a screening program, but an early signal of a broader preventive care inequality affecting a growing segment of Korea’s population. Formal eligibility within a universal screening program was not accompanied by equitable uptake, and the barriers faced by migrants appear to extend beyond eligibility and cost. The marked strengthening of the estimated disparity, from the crude OR (0.59) to the adjusted OR (0.18), suggests that measured covariates did not explain the disparity but rather that compositional differences between groups partially masked its magnitude in unadjusted comparisons. In a country like Korea undergoing sustained demographic change, such a persistent preventive care gap raises concern that health disadvantage may become more deeply structured over time if left unaddressed.

This residual disparity among long-term enrollees, which became more pronounced after adjustment rather than being attenuated, is consistent with the presence of barriers that operate after formal enrollment and beyond the factors captured in administrative data. Engaging with an organized screening program is not a single act but a multi-stage process, and migrants may encounter compounding obstacles at each point along this pathway [8]. Language barriers, lower familiarity with the health system, and practical constraints related to working hours and transportation have been identified as plausible contributors in international studies of migrant preventive service use [19–21]. Because these mechanisms were not directly measured from the present administrative data, they should be interpreted as plausible explanations rather than confirmed determinants. Notably, among those individuals who did participate in health screening, migrants showed a lower obesity prevalence, substantially lower alcohol use, and higher physical activity than their non-migrant counterparts—a pattern consistent with the healthy immigrant effect [7]. This suggests that the participation gap is unlikely to reflect lower health motivation among migrants as a whole. However, because this study captured only migrants enrolled in the Korean NHIS and did not include more vulnerable groups such as undocumented migrants, the magnitude of the disparity in the broader migrant population may be greater than that indicated by the aggregate participation rates observed here.

The overall pattern of lower preventive service utilization among migrants was observed to be consistent with prior findings from the broad international literature [15, 22]. Lower screening uptake has been documented among migrants across diverse healthcare settings, including cervical cancer screening in Norway [23], mammography screening [24], and cervical cancer screening programs across multiple settings [25], with a recurring theme being that the removal of financial barriers alone is insufficient to ensure equitable access [15, 26]. Within Korea, prior work has reported persistent socioeconomic gradients in NHSP participation in the general population [27, 28] and has identified area- and facility-level influences on screening uptake [29]. The present study extends this evidence base by showing that participation disparities for migrants persist across income and insurance strata and remain after covariate adjustment, suggesting that structural factors beyond those captured by administrative data are likely at play.

Our age-standardized analyses revealed consistently lower participation among migrants in Korea throughout the study period. This pattern indicates that disparities in screening uptake were persistent rather than confined to later years, and it highlights the importance of accounting for differences in age composition when comparing migrant and non-migrant populations. Because participation in preventive screening varies strongly by age, failure to consider age composition can obscure persistent disadvantage among migrants. The crossover visible in the crude rates displayed in Fig. 1 is therefore most likely to reflect the younger age structure of the migrant population in Korea, rather than genuinely higher participation. Age-specific analyses for 2008 and 2022 (Additional file 1: Table S3) support this interpretation: whereas in 2008 migrants aged 20–29 years had higher participation than their non-migrant counterparts and the pattern across age groups was mixed, by 2022 migrants had lower participation than the non-migrant comparison group across all adult age groups, with differences ranging from 7.0 to 13.1% points. This shift suggests that the disparity has widened and become more pervasive over time, beyond what age composition alone can explain. At the same time, age structure is unlikely to be the only factor shaping temporal patterns. The composition of the foreign resident population in Korea changed substantially from the mid-2010s onward, expanding in size and becoming more diverse in terms of nationality, visa status, and employment type [3]. By the end of 2024, the largest groups of foreign residents were nationals of China (including ethnic Korean-Chinese; 36.2%), followed by Vietnam (11.5%), Thailand (7.1%), the United States (6.4%), and Uzbekistan (3.6%) [3]. In this context, greater reliance on digital interfaces in accessing health services may have created additional barriers for some migrant groups, particularly when language barriers were compounded by limited digital access or literacy [30]. These interpretations remain plausible rather than proven in the absence of time-varying compositional data and direct measures of digital access, and should be examined in future research.

Our findings suggest that substantively addressing screening disparities among migrants in Korea calls for moving beyond eligibility guarantees toward reducing procedural and informational barriers across the screening pathway. Three directions merit policy attention. First, multilingual communication may represent a foundational step: systematic provision of invitation notices and guidance materials in major migrant languages, together with in-facility interpretation support, can lower informational barriers at the recognition and seeking stages. Second, linguistic accessibility alone is unlikely to be sufficient and indeed evidence from a previous Norwegian randomized trial found that native-language invitation letters did not increase breast cancer screening participation among migrants [31], suggesting that navigational support—encompassing appointment assistance, accompaniment, and result explanation—is a necessary complement [32]. In Korea, community health worker-based navigation has shown promise among women who migrated to Korea through marriage to Korean nationals [14], and extension of such approaches to the broader migrant population merits consideration. Third, for migrant workers whose participation is structurally constrained by inflexible working hours, an expansion of worksite-based or community hub-based screening modalities may offer a practical alternative to facility visits.

This study had several notable strengths. It used nationwide NHI data covering a large and representative sample set, provided the first nationwide analysis of long-term NHSP participation trends among the general migrant population in Korea, and demonstrated that disparities persist even under conditions of sustained insurance enrollment—empirically distinguishing the gap between nominal coverage and actual utilization [26]. Several limitations of our analyses should also be noted, however. First, the administrative data did not include key factors relevant to screening access, such as language proficiency, health literacy, familiarity with the healthcare system, and experiences of discrimination. Second, information on occupation, working hours, and employment type was unavailable, limiting our evaluation of work-related barriers. Third, the binary measure of screening completion did not allow identification of where disengagement occurred along the screening pathway, and the administrative data did not permit ascertainment of individual reasons for non-participation. Fourth, although age-standardized analyses reduced bias arising from differences in age composition, the annual trend analysis still compared a dynamic migrant population with a non-migrant comparison cohort frequency-matched by age and sex in 2008, and followed these subjects without annual re-sampling. Hence, other time-varying compositional changes may still partly explain observed temporal patterns. Fifth, because our analysis was based on data available through 2022, the persistence or evolution of these patterns beyond 2022 could not be determined. Sixth, because the migrant population in Korea examined in this study was limited to individuals enrolled in the NHIS, more vulnerable groups such as undocumented migrants were not captured. If these excluded groups had been included, the overall health screening participation rate among migrants would likely have been lower. Finally, because the multivariable analysis was conducted in the pooled sample of migrants and non-migrants, the model primarily estimates between-group contrasts; migrant-restricted analyses or interaction analyses examining whether covariate associations differ between groups could provide further insight into within-group determinants and represent an important direction for future research.

In conclusion, screening participation disparities persisted throughout the 15-year study period and remained marked even among migrants continuously enrolled in the Korean NHIS for more than a decade, with the adjusted disparity larger than crude comparisons suggested. In the context of Korea’s ongoing demographic change, these findings underscore the importance of identifying and addressing migrant screening inequalities at an earlier stage. Migrant-tailored, multilingual, and navigation-supported strategies are needed to reduce procedural friction across the screening pathway before preventive care gaps turn into more deeply rooted health inequalities.
