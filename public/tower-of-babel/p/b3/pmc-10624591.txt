Combined TMR and RPNI in a vasculopathy patient: A case report
Authors: Logan G. Galbraith, Daniel Najafali, James R. Gatherwright
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
TMR (targeted muscle reinnervation) or RPNI (regenerative peripheral nerve interface) have been the standard after nerve injuries. In this case report, we explain our approach in combining these two techniques (TMRpni) for a patient undergoing left above-the-knee amputation. Using this method, both phantom and nerve pain were reduced in our patient's case. As this technique becomes more well understood and widely adopted, amputee patients may achieve a greater quality of life post operation.

Introduction

Amputations have a significant impact on a patients’ quality of life with high rates of neuropathic pain.1, 2, 3 Managing the residual limb and phantom pain post amputation is challenging and multifactorial. Severed or damaged nerves can form neuromas when the continuity is interrupted and the nerve makes an effort to salvage the lost connection.4 The extent of injury and number of neuromas that form are particularly dependent on the amputation etiology (e.g., traumatic etiology). TMR (targeted muscle reinnervation) or RPNI (regenerative peripheral nerve interface) have been the accepted route for treating nerve injuries. TMR allows the injured nerve to join that of innervated muscle. Literature supports this method as it prevents post operative pain and expands the capabilities of intuitive electronic prosthetics. RPNI has been used to decrease pain after nerve transection which involves directing the injured nerve into a de-innervated muscle graft. TMR requires that the transected nerve be sutured into a nerve innervating another muscle. In this way, the transected nerve has a place to go and is less likely to form a neuroma. However, there is often a mismatch between the size of the spliced nerves allowing for axon escape and ultimately neuroma in continuity.5 RPNI on the other hand requires a nerve to regenerate axons randomly into a muscle target.6 Each technique has its benefits in the setting of amputation and both techniques make teleological sense, but have their own limitations and potential inadequacies. To obviate the size mismatch of TMR and the random formation of the neuromuscular junction, the current technique aims to combine the favorable attributes of both.7 For the patient in our case, a hybrid TMR-RPNI graft was utilized in efforts to prevent neuroma formation due to axon escape and have a more directed reinnervation approach. While both TMR and RPNI have merit when used alone, a hybrid approach may prove superior in certain situations.

Case

The patient was a 75-year-old female undergoing left above-the-knee amputation. In efforts to reduce phantom pain and neuroma formation, a combined TMR-RPNI approach was utilized. After the limb had been removed, the sciatic nerve was dissected along its posterior tibial and peroneal courses. Nerves to the gastrocnemius muscle were dissected and stimulated using a checkpoint stimulator verifying the contraction of the muscle across the nerve repair at the time. A total of 2 nerve/muscle grafts were harvested and placed in saline. Attention was then turned to performing a pedicled nerve transfer of the saphenous nerve and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. This was done using a 6–0 Prolene in an epineurial end-to-end fashion to the previously harvested composite nerve/muscle graft. Both repairs were wrapped with a collagen conduit and tissue was placed around each repair. Both repairs were then placed in a subcutaneous pocket away from the amputation stump.

Notably, the patient suffered from peripheral vasculopathy and chronic kidney disease, a pathologic state that is embodied by many of the patients who end up undergoing traumatic amputation. There was superficial delayed wound healing for a period of 3 weeks; however, the phantom pain was rated as acceptable and nerve pain was nonexistent postoperatively. Specifically, the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) evaluation was performed. VAS for pain was 8–10/10 pain pre-operatively. Immediately in the post-operative period (<6 weeks) VAS ranged from 8 to 10/10 pain, slowly trending to a nadir of 2–3/10 pain at 2 months. This was stable until her re-admission for an unrelated issue. The follow-up interval was limited to 6 months due to her unfortunate passing. This highlights the need to avoid longer, more invasive surgeries with equivocal long-term outcomes. Discussion

It has been established that neuropathic pain improves with employment of TMR and RPNI.5,8 These newly adapted techniques were initially used to provide an interface for prosthetic control via efferent motor signals. Ideal nerve regeneration would provide amplification of the signal and transmission of that signal for appropriate fine motor control. Other factors that should be considered are integration with the surrounding nerve environment and avoiding axonal damage.

While TMR lowers the incidence of phantom and nerve pain, there remain areas of the technique that can go awry. The implementation of the combined (TMRpni) technique overcomes certain limitations of each approach on its own–as emphasized by Kurlander et al.7 RPNI has been noted to be limited by the size of the muscle graft. Too large and it will undergo possible necrosis and too small and it can potentially not amplify an adequate signal. In their anatomic and technical guide, Leach and colleagues advocate for a construct that is approximately 3 cm long, 2 cm wide, and 0.5 cm for appropriate revascularization.9 As mentioned previously, the biggest challenge for TMR remains nerve size mismatch and axonal escape. These axons can be kept contained using a biological nerve wrap that gives escape axons a place to invade instead of allowing their progression to neuroma formation. This muscle wrap works based on the accepted mechanism for RPNI.

The patient in this case reported no nerve pain and minimal phantom pain. We believe that this positive outcome can be attributed to the utilization of this combined technique with little to no neuroma formation. Implementing this technique is straightforward and may be suggested for patients who are candidates for traditional TMR or RPNI. Still there is much data that needs to be collected in order to draw conclusions that would form more precise recommendations. In this case the patient passed away secondary to other disease processes. Another technique detailed by Valerio et al. combines TMR with a pedicled vascularized RPNI deemed vRPNI.10

The reconstruction detailed in this case report is tailored to high-risk, non-free flap candidates, undergoing immediate and/or planned above knee amputation.

Conclusions

With more extensive research and data collection, the recommendations for universal TMR-RPNI technique may soon be clear. However, this procedure remains straight forward with no obvious complications or risks to the patient. With these attributes in mind, the benefits of such a simple procedure should be considered whenever feasible for a patient undergoing amputation or another nerve damaging event.
