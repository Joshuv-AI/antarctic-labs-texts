Integrative Transcriptomic Signature of tRNA‐Derived Fragment Targets and Lactylation‐Related Genes for Prostate Cancer Diagnosis
Authors: Lei Wang, Xiaoxue Hu, Bixi Wang, Yaping Zhang, Jiajia Yang, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundConventional diagnostic strategies for prostate cancer (PCa) are hindered by suboptimal specificity, frequently resulting in overdiagnosis and unnecessary invasive biopsies. We developed an integrative diagnostic framework based on transcriptomic signatures from tRNA‐derived fragments (tRFs), target genes, and lactylation‐related genes (LRGs), two emerging regulators linked to PCa progression.MethodsBulk RNA‐seq data from TCGA‐PCa (499 tumors, 52 normals) and five independent GEO cohorts were analyzed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edgeR and subsequently assessed for functional enrichment. Candidate markers were obtained by intersecting DEGs with predicted tRFs targets and curated LRGs. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) model was trained and validated across external cohorts. RT‐qPCR performed clinical confirmation in 30 prostate tissue samples.ResultsEleven genes overlapped among DEGs, tRF targets, and LRGs, and a seven‐gene diagnostic signature (CSRP1, HMGN4, CALM1, NEFL, MSN, MKI67, ARID3A) was established. The model showed robust performance across all validation datasets, with AUC values of 0.728–0.904, exceeding those of prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) testing. RT‐qPCR supported significant dysregulation of five signature genes in clinical samples, notably decreased NEFL and increased MKI67. Decision curve analysis (DCA) suggested the model could reduce unnecessary biopsies by about 30% at commonly used clinical thresholds.ConclusionThis integrative model, incorporating tRFs‐associated targets and lactylation‐related signatures, demonstrates superior PCa detection accuracy compared to conventional PSA testing. The seven‐gene signature offers a biologically informed, clinically applicable tool to improve early diagnosis while potentially reducing unnecessary biopsies.Keywords: diagnostic model, lactylation, prostate cancer, transcriptomic analysis, tRNA‐derived fragments

Introduction

PCa remains a significant global health challenge, representing one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies in men [1, 2]. Marked geographic disparities in PCa incidence have been observed, largely attributable to differences in screening practices and lifestyle factors, with a higher prevalence reported in developed regions [3]. Although the precise etiology of PCa remains incompletely understood, well‐established risk factors include advanced age, familial predisposition, genetic alterations such as BRCA1/2 mutations, and environmental influences, including dietary patterns [4]. Current diagnostic approaches, including digital rectal examination (DRE), prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) testing, imaging, and biopsy, suffer from limited specificity and high false‐positive rates [5]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more accurate diagnostic approaches to improve early detection and optimize clinical management of PCa.

tRFs are a class of small non‐coding RNAs generated from mature tRNAs or their precursors and are now recognized as functional regulatory molecules rather than passive degradation products [5]. Based on their cleavage sites and biogenesis, tRFs are categorized into distinct subtypes, including 5′‐tRFs, 3′‐tRFs, internal tRFs (i‐tRFs), and tRNA halves (tiRNAs) [6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that tRFs participate in diverse biological processes, such as regulating mRNA stability, modulating chromatin organization and epigenetic states, inhibiting protein translation, and interfering with the biogenesis of other small non‐coding RNAs [7]. In PCa, elevated expression of tRF‐Glu‐TTC‐2 has been associated with enhanced tumor cell proliferation and increased tumor size [8], whereas tRF‐315 has been implicated in cisplatin resistance by suppressing GADD45A‐mediated apoptotic pathways, highlighting its potential as both a therapeutic target and a predictive biomarker [9]. Collectively, these findings suggest that specific tRFs may serve as promising biomarkers for PCa diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of therapeutic response [10]. Notably, emerging studies indicate a close link between tRFs‐mediated regulation and metabolic reprogramming. Lactate‐induced histone lactylation alters chromatin accessibility, while tRFs can directly regulate key metabolic enzymes [11]. On this basis, we hypothesize that tRFs may directly modulate LRGs by targeting metabolic enzymes such as LDHB and PFKP, thereby forming a synergistic regulatory axis with diagnostic potential.

Lactylation is a recently identified post‐translational modification (PTM) characterized by the covalent attachment of lactyl groups to lysine residues, predominantly on histone proteins [12]. This modification is driven by elevated intracellular lactate levels, a hallmark of cancer metabolism resulting from enhanced glycolysis under the Warburg effect. Histone lactylation has been shown to dynamically regulate chromatin structure and gene accessibility in response to metabolic cues, thereby promoting immune evasion in PCa [13]. Increasing evidence links aberrant lactylation to tumor progression, immune escape, and adaptation to hypoxic tumor microenvironments in PCa [14]. Moreover, disordered lactylation patterns have been detected in PCa tissues and cell lines, implicating this PTM in the dysregulation of key oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [15]. As mechanistic insights into lactylation continue to expand, this modification is increasingly recognized as a promising source of diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in PCa.

In this study, we employed an integrative transcriptomic framework to develop a diagnostic model for PCa by combining tRFs‐associated regulatory targets with lactylation‐related gene signatures. Bulk RNA‐sequencing data were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. DEGs were identified using the edgeR package, followed by functional annotation through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. The diagnostic model was constructed using LASSO regression and evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. DCA was further applied to assess clinical utility and robustness. The overarching aim of this study was to establish a reliable and biologically informed diagnostic framework that improves PCa detection and supports more effective clinical decision‐making.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Bulk RNA sequencing datasets were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) as well as the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) [16]. Five independent datasets (GSE237995, GSE62872, GSE70768, GSE72220, and GSE79021) were selected using disease‐specific and experiment‐targeted search criteria. Our previous tRFs sequencing data from GSE315385 were utilized for subsequent target gene prediction. Only publicly available bulk RNA‐Seq datasets meeting our predefined quality and inclusion thresholds were included in the analysis. Detailed information on the datasets was provided in Table S1.

Differential Expression Analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the edgeR package [17]. Normalized read count data incorporating biological replicates and group information were used as input. DEGs were identified based on an absolute log2 fold change > 0.585 and a Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q‐value < 0.05.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

The functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using the clusterProfiler package [18, 19]. The GO enrichment analysis encompassed all three domains, BP, CC, and MF, while the KEGG analysis identified significantly enriched signaling pathways. For both analyses, statistical significance of enrichment was established using Fisher's exact test at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

tRFs Target Gene Prediction

To investigate the regulatory potential of previously identified tRFs—including tiRNA‐1_33‐Gly‐GCC‐1, tiRNA‐1_34‐Glu‐CTC‐1‐M2, tRF‐1_30‐His‐GTG‐1, tRF‐1_31‐Glu‐TTC‐2, and tRF‐1_32‐Lys‐CTT‐1‐M2—putative mRNA targets were predicted using TargetScan [20] (http://www.targetscan.org), and miRanda [21] (http://www.microrna.org). TargetScan identifies conserved 7‐ and 8‐mer seed‐matching sequences, whereas miRanda evaluates sequence complementarity, secondary structure, and binding free energy. Genes predicted by both algorithms were retained as high‐confidence tRFs target genes.

Venn Diagram Construction

DEGs derived from the TCGA prostate cancer cohort (TCGA‐PCa) were integrated with predicted tRFs target genes (n = 1532) and LRGs (n = 487) curated from published literature [22]. Overlapping genes among the three datasets were visualized using the VennDiagram package (version 1.7.3) in R [23]. Genes located in the intersection were selected for subsequent diagnostic model construction.

Model Construction and Validation

A total of 499 PCa samples and 52 normal prostate samples were randomly divided into training and validation cohorts at a 1:1 ratio. Feature selection and model construction were performed using LASSO regression with a binomial family implemented in the glmnet package [24]. The optimal regularization parameter (λ) was determined through ten‐fold cross‐validation based on the minimum binomial deviance. The selected genes were subsequently incorporated into the final diagnostic model. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves generated with the pROC package [25].

External Validation of the Model

The robustness and generalizability of the diagnostic model were assessed using five independent GEO datasets (GSE237995, GSE62872, GSE70768, GSE72220, and GSE79021). ROC curves were generated for each dataset, and the AUC was used as the primary metric for diagnostic accuracy. DCA was performed using the dcurves package to evaluate the model's potential clinical benefit at different threshold probabilities [26].

Clinical Samples and Ethics Statement

A total of 30 prostate tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing biopsy or surgical resection at the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University between January 2023 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Yangzhou University (Approval No. 2025‐YKL01‐K04), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Detailed clinical and pathological characteristics of the cohort are provided in Table S2.

RT‐qPCR Validation of the Model

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme). One microgram of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using a Vazyme reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) was performed with the 2× Taq Pro Universal qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on the QuantStudio 7 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2‐ΔΔCt method. Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). RNA‐seq data from TCGA and GEO cohorts were normalized using the trimmed mean of M‐values (TMM) method. Data pre‐processing involved log2 transformation and normalization; potential outliers were evaluated using standard diagnostic plots. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using a two‐sided Student's t‐test or Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, following an evaluation of normality and variance homogeneity assumptions. For multiple comparisons, the Benjamini‐Hochberg adjustment was applied. Diagnostic performance was quantified by ROC curves and AUC using the pROC package. Clinical utility was evaluated via DCA using the dcurves package. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Workflow for PCa Diagnostic Model Construction and Validation

To identify DEGs between PCa and normal tissues, gene expression profiles from TCGA and GEO databases were meticulously analyzed. Subsequently, a functional enrichment analysis was conducted to elucidate the pertinent biological processes and pathways associated with these DEGs. Simultaneously, target genes of tRFs and LRGs were predicted and subsequently identified. Integrating these transcriptomic data through Venn analysis enabled the selection of candidate genes, which were then used to build a diagnostic model via LASSO regression. The model's diagnostic was assessed through validation across five independent cohorts. Finally, experimental validation via RT‐qPCR was conducted to verify further the expression patterns of the key genes identified by the model. The overall study design is summarized in (Figure 1).

Study design and workflow. This flowchart illustrates the integrated transcriptomic analysis of tRFs‐related target genes and LRGs to develop and validate a diagnostic model for PCa.

Differential Expression Analysis

Bulk RNA‐sequencing data from the TCGA‐PCa cohort, comprising 499 PCa cases and 52 normal controls, were processed using the edgeR package. DEGs were determined using specific criteria, including a |log2fold change (FC)| > 0.585 and q‐value < 0.05. This assessment identified a total of 10,632 DEGs, with 5379 upregulated and 5253 downregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal samples. Hierarchical clustering analysis (Figure 2A) and a heatmap of the top 20 upregulated and 20 downregulated DEGs (Figure 2B) both demonstrated distinct separation between PCa and normal samples. Key DEGs, including SPINK1, APOA2, and SEMG1, were identified as effective markers distinguishing tumor from normal tissue. The scatter plot presented in (Figure 2C) exhibited a balanced distribution of genes exhibiting both upregulation and downregulation. Conversely, the volcano plot shown in (Figure 2D) demonstrated the correlation between the extent of expression variation (|log2FC| > 0.585) and the level of statistical significance (log10 (adjusted p‐value) < 0.05), thereby reinforcing the reliability of the identified DEGs.

GO Enrichment Analysis of DEGs

GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed significant overrepresentation across all three GO categories: BP, MF, and CC. In the BP domain, DEGs were primarily associated with regulatory pathways, including cell differentiation, adhesion, migration, and metabolic processes‐consistent with their potential role in PCa initiation and progression is consistent. Within the MF category, enrichment was largely observed in protein‐binding functions, ion channel activity, and receptor‐mediated interactions. For the CC category, DEGs were notably enriched in cellular structures, including the cell periphery, extracellular matrix, and cell junctions, highlighting their involvement in intercellular communication and in the maintenance of tissue architecture (Figure 3A). The circular diagram (Figure 3B) further visualized Z‐scores, with color intensity reflecting enrichment levels, and depicted enrichment trends across GO categories.

Enriched Signaling Pathways of DEGs

To elucidate the biological roles of the DEGs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted, revealing significant involvement in multiple signaling networks, including neuroactive ligand‐receptor interaction, calcium signaling, and cAMP signaling (Figure 4A). Among these, the neuroactive ligand‐receptor interaction pathway showed the highest enrichment, consistent with neuroendocrine differentiation in PCa and implicating adrenergic receptor‐mediated therapy resistance, in which neurotransmitter signaling promotes oncogenic cAMP/PKA activation [27] (Figure 4B). Several genes, such as ADCY3, CALM1, and CREB3L1, emerged as central nodes within multiple pathways, particularly within the cAMP signaling cascade. A detailed view of this pathway demonstrated distinct expression alterations, with upregulated genes marked in red and downregulated genes in green (Figure 4C). The significant enrichment of signaling pathways, including cAMP and calcium signaling, underscores the critical roles of cellular communication and metabolic adaptation in PCa, providing a mechanistic context for the diagnostic genes identified in our subsequent model.

Identification of Candidate Hub Genes

Using the R package VennDiagram for visualization, the intersection analysis revealed that 11 genes (CSRP1, NEFL, CALD1, MSN, CALM1, LDHB, HMGN4, PFKP, MYH13, MKI67, ARID3A) were commonly shared among all three datasets (Figure 5). These 11 intersecting genes were identified as high‐priority candidates for model construction, as they are dysregulated in PCa, potentially regulated by tRFs, and mechanistically implicated in lactylation processes. In addition, 27 genes overlapped between DEGs and LRGs, while 138 genes were shared between LRGs and predicted tRFs target genes. The observed relationships and shared components among these gene sets provide a valuable foundation for constructing mechanistic models relevant to PCa progression and biology.

Construction and Validation of an Integrated Diagnostic Model Based on tRFs and Lactylation

LASSO regression analysis identified seven genes—CSRP1, HMGN4, CALM1, NEFL, MSN, MKI67, and ARID3A—as optimal diagnostic biomarkers from an initial pool of 11 candidates (Figure 6A). Feature selection was conducted via 10‐fold cross‐validation (λ = 0.0099; Figure 6B), balancing model simplicity with a reduction in overfitting risk. The integrated model demonstrated robust diagnostic efficacy, yielding an AUC of 0.952 in the training cohort and 0.945 in the internal validation cohort, with a pooled AUC of 0.947 (Figure 6C). Analysis of individual gene contributions (Figure 6D) indicated that MKI67, a marker of cellular proliferation, and ARID3A, a transcriptional regulator, were the most influential predictors, with coefficients of 1.32 and 0.89, respectively, consistent with their documented roles in PCa aggressiveness. The model also assigned significant predictive weight to CSRP1 and NEFL, whose coefficients are consistent with their established tumor‐suppressive roles in PCa, such as metastasis inhibition. This ranking of variable importance highlights the biological plausibility of the model and supports its mechanistic relevance to PCa pathogenesis.

External Validation Confirms the Strong Generalizability of the Diagnostic Model

The results indicate that the diagnostic model has strong discriminatory capability across diverse datasets, with AUC values ranging from 0.728 to 0.904 (Figure 7). Its most pronounced performance was observed in the GSE70768 dataset (AUC = 0.904), demonstrating outstanding diagnostic accuracy in this group. The remaining datasets yielded AUC values of 0.833 (GSE237995), 0.728 (GSE62872), 0.748 (GSE72220), and 0.753 (GSE79021). Across the evaluated range (0.728–0.904), the model consistently outperformed PSA testing, which typically achieves an AUC of approximately 0.66–0.70 in large‐scale cohorts and systematic reviews [28]. Collectively, these findings underscore the model's robustness and broad applicability, as validated by independent datasets.

RT‐qPCR Confirms Distinct Expression of Key Signature Genes in Prostate Cancer Tissues

To investigate the expression patterns of key genes in tumors, we performed quantitative RT‐qPCR analysis of five genes in PCa and normal tissues. The results revealed two distinct expression trends among these genes. Firstly, CSRP1, HMGN4, and NEFL were all significantly downregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Figure 8A,B,D). Among them, NEFL showed the most pronounced decrease (***p < 0.001), followed by CSRP1 (**p < 0.01), and HMGN4 (*p < 0.05). This consistent downregulation suggests that these genes may function as potential tumor suppressors or maintainers of tissue homeostasis, and their reduced expression in the tumor microenvironment may be closely associated with tumor initiation and progression.

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using a two‐sided unpaired Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

In stark contrast, ARID3A and MKI67 were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues. The expression level of ARID3A was markedly increased (**p < 0.01, Figure 8C), indicating its possible role as an oncogenic factor involved in tumor progression. Notably, MKI67, a well‐recognized marker of cell proliferation, was highly enriched in tumor tissues with highly significant upregulation (***p < 0.001, Figure 8E), providing strong molecular evidence for the heightened proliferative activity characteristic of tumor cells. Notably, although CALM1 and MSN were selected as essential components of the 7‐gene signature in the large TCGA cohort, their individual expression differences were not statistically significant in our smaller clinical validation sets (n = 30, p > 0.05). This finding underscores the strength of the multi‐gene model, which derives its diagnostic power from the combined expression pattern rather than relying on any single marker.

In conclusion, the varying expression levels of these genes illustrate a molecular framework of the tumor, characterized by a reduction in tumor‐suppressive functions on one side, while simultaneously indicating the enhancement of proliferative and malignant properties on the other.

Discussion

PCa remains one of the most prevalent malignancies among men worldwide and is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems [29]. Current diagnostic strategies, including PSA testing, DRE, and imaging, are limited by insufficient specificity and high false‐positive rates, often leading to unnecessary biopsies and associated complications [30]. Consequently, major clinical guidelines, such as those issued by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), emphasize the urgent need for novel molecular biomarkers to improve early detection and disease monitoring in PCa [31]. To address this critical gap, our study introduces a novel diagnostic paradigm by integrating two frontiers of cancer biology: tRFs‐mediated post‐transcriptional regulation and lactylation‐related metabolic reprogramming. This combination culminated in a robust seven‐gene signature (CSRP1, HMGN4, CALM1, NEFL, MSN, MKI67, and ARID3A) that demonstrates superior diagnostic precision and substantial clinical utility.

These observed gene dysregulations collectively underscore a synergistic interplay between tRFs‐mediated post‐transcriptional regulation and lactylation‐dependent metabolic and epigenetic alterations. This intricate relationship aligns with established hallmark pathways that drive PCa progression. Mechanistically, tRFs are hypothesized to regulate gene expression primarily by degrading mRNAs or inhibiting translation. Concurrently, lactylation, responsive to metabolic stress, epigenetically modulates their expression. This dynamic interplay appears to establish a positive feedback loop, thereby sustaining PCa progression, a mechanism further supported by the observed enrichment of calcium and cAMP‐signaling pathways [32]. We hypothesize that a dynamic feedback loop exists: tRFs modulate the stability of key transcripts involved in calcium homeostasis, while lactylation, responsive to the resulting metabolic shifts, further stabilizes the malignant phenotype through chromatin remodeling.

Functional Modules within the Seven‐Gene Signature. The seven genes identified are not merely statistical markers but represent functional drivers across multiple biological domains:

Proliferation and stemness: MKI67 [33], a marker of mitotic activity, and ARID3A [34], implicated in stem‐like properties, contribute prominently to the model, reflecting the underlying biology of tumor growth and cellular dedifferentiation.

Invasion/metastasis: Downregulation of CSRP1 [35], a metastasis suppressor, coupled with altered expression of MSN [36], a protein involved in cytoskeletal dynamics and invasion, indicates the model's sensitivity to metastatic processes and captures the interplay between metastasis inhibition and promotion.

Metabolic signaling: Dysregulation of CALM1 [37], a key calcium sensor, provides direct evidence of perturbed calcium/cAMP signaling, linking metabolic shifts to downstream signaling cascades.

Epigenetic interface: Reduced HMGN4 expression [38], a chromatin architectural protein, suggests a link to lactylation‐driven epigenetic modulation, underscoring the role of chromatin remodeling in PCa progression.

Microenvironment interactions: The inclusion of NEFL [39], typically associated with neuronal integrity and nerve injury, implies that the model may also reflect processes related to perineural invasion, a common feature in advanced PCa. Collectively, these components illustrate that the model captures not only diagnostic information but also functionally relevant pathways across multiple biological domains.

Our RT‐qPCR validation on 30 clinical specimens further anchors these findings. The significant upregulation of MKI67 and ARID3A, alongside the downregulation of CSRP1, HMGN4, and NEFL, provides high‐confidence experimental evidence that substantiates our bioinformatic predictions. While CALM1 and MSN showed less pronounced differences in our validation set, this may reflect molecular heterogeneity in PCa or the transition from transcript stability to protein‐level regulation, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.

A notable strength of this study is the integration of bioinformatic discovery with experimental validation. RT‐qPCR confirmed the dysregulated expression of five key genes (CSRP1, HMGN4, NEFL, ARID3A, and MKI67) in clinical samples, supporting the bioinformatic results and underscoring the biological relevance of the diagnostic signature.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, reliance on retrospective, publicly available datasets prevents us from making definitive causal inferences, and there remains a lack of independent validation using non‐invasive clinical samples such as blood or urine. Furthermore, the mechanistic relationships among tRFs, lactylation, and the selected genes remain unclear. Lastly, it is essential to evaluate the clinical benefits, cost‐effectiveness, and practicality of integrating this transcriptomic signature into standard clinical practice. Future research should concentrate on large, multicenter prospective trials and detailed mechanistic studies to validate and expand upon these findings.

Furthermore, future studies incorporating single‐cell RNA sequencing will be highly valuable to delineate the precise cell‐type‐specific expression patterns of these seven signature genes and to further dissect the heterogeneous interplay between tRFs and lactylation within the prostate tumor microenvironment.

Conclusion

In summary, this study established and validated a novel transcriptomic diagnostic framework for prostate cancer by integrating tRFs‐associated regulatory targets with lactylation‐related gene signatures. The resulting seven‐gene panel—CSRP1, HMGN4, CALM1, NEFL, MSN, MKI67, and ARID3A—demonstrated robust diagnostic performance across multiple independent cohorts and consistently outperformed conventional PSA testing. Experimental validation further confirmed distinct expression patterns of key genes, with downregulation of CSRP1, HMGN4, and NEFL and upregulation of ARID3A and MKI67 in tumor tissues, underscoring their biological relevance to PCa progression. Although further prospective and mechanistic validation is required, this integrative approach represents a biologically informed and clinically promising strategy for to improve early PCa detection and reduce unnecessary invasive procedures.
