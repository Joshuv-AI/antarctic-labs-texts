Breathing in the Dark: Lessons from the April 2025 Blackout for Electricity-dependent Respiratory Patients
Authors: Salvador Díaz Lobato, Sagrario Mayoralas Alises
Published: 2026

The massive blackout that struck Spain and Portugal on April 28, 2025, exposed the fragility of modern societies when essential infrastructure fails. Beyond the immediate disruption to daily life, the event highlighted an especially vulnerable group: respiratory patients who depend on electrically powered medical devices for survival. These electricity-dependent patients – including individuals requiring continuous invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation, long-term oxygen therapy via concentrators, or dependence on airway-suction and cough-assist devices – face immediate risk when the power supply is interrupted. Their experience during the outage revealed critical gaps in preparedness, coordination, and protection – issues that require urgent reflection and action.

The outage quickly rippled through healthcare facilities, which faced a surge of electricity-dependent patients – primarily those on home respiratory therapies (HRT) – suddenly left without power. Families and caregivers confronted fear, uncertainty, and the isolation of being technologically disconnected, while clinicians and HRT providers improvised to safeguard patients whose lives hinge on power-dependent equipment.1

Electricity-dependent respiratory care is uniquely power-sensitive. When electricity fails, the function of mechanical ventilators, oxygen concentrators, airways suction devices, mechanical cough-assist devices, and humidification systems is immediately compromised – sometimes with life-threatening consequences.

Two studies have analyzed mortality around the blackout. García et al.2 reported 147 excess deaths in the three days following the outage, with a greater relative increase among women than men. Konstantinoudis and Riou3 found a limited excess on the day of the blackout, with mortality rising over the following two days, particularly among older women. Although these patterns are consistent with previous evidence linking power outages to adverse health outcomes in vulnerable populations,4, 5, 6 it is essential to recognize that these analyses are observational and cannot establish a direct causal relationship with certainty. Several potential confounders – such as heat exposure, pre-existing fragility among elderly populations, and concurrent system stress – may have contributed to the observed excess mortality. Acknowledging these limitations helps contextualize the findings while underscoring the need for robust preparedness for electricity-dependent patients.

Shared responsibility, grounded in evidence and experience, is essential. Based on available evidence and expert consensus, patients should understand the electrical autonomy of their devices and maintain a clear backup plan. Healthcare facilities should ensure generator-supported areas to receive patients without home autonomy. HRT providers should maintain updated registries and preventive protocols, while health authorities should coordinate action frameworks across healthcare and non-health emergency resources. These actions reflect both published recommendations on healthcare resilience during outages and practical lessons identified during the April 2025 event.7

Although the blackout affected both Spain and Portugal, the management strategies displayed certain differences that are worth noting. In Portugal, the response was more centrally coordinated at the national level, with earlier activation of integrated civil protection mechanisms and rapid communication between health authorities and emergency services. Spain, in contrast, experienced a more heterogeneous response shaped by the decentralized structure of its regional health systems, resulting in variable degrees of preparedness and coordination across regions. Similarly, the role of HRT providers reflected this structural contrast: in Portugal, their actions tended to follow a more uniform, nationally aligned approach, whereas in Spain their involvement was more variable, mirroring regional differences in protocols and coordination.8 These contrasts highlight the need for a harmonized framework that ensures equitable protection for electricity-dependent patients regardless of administrative differences.

Looking ahead, we need a National Contingency Plan9 that integrates both evidence-based preventive measures and expert-driven strategies informed by the blackout experience. While the scientific literature supports actions such as improving energy autonomy in healthcare facilities and developing interoperable patient registries, other system-level proposals – such as establishing an electrical-shelter network or implementing complementary communication channels – are grounded in expert consensus and operational lessons learned from the event. Explicitly combining available evidence with expert judgment ensures a balanced and actionable framework for improving preparedness. Prioritized components of a national contingency plan.

Essential measures include: Regulatory updates that explicitly recognize electricity-dependent patients as a priority group; autonomous power systems and generators across healthcare and social-health facilities, with dedicated, generator-supported areas to receive patients lacking home autonomy; interoperable, up-to-date patient registries accessible during emergencies (with appropriate consent); contact tree and single command structure to share notifications and streamline decision-making across agencies; alternative communication systems independent of internet or mobile networks (e.g., satellite/TETRA, emergency radio networks such as REMER),10, 11 with secure procedures for exchanging critical information.

Desirable measures include: A structured “electrical-shelter” network with predefined patient assignments to ensure safety while avoiding hospital overload; expansion of oxygen production and packaging and mobilization of material and human resources for rapid surge capacity; periodic drills and community education for families and caregivers, reinforcing safe equipment use and contingency planning for prolonged outages; enhanced inter-agency coordination mechanisms involving non-health emergency resources for complex scenarios.

The blackout revealed not only technical vulnerabilities but also the human and organizational challenges inherent in caring for electricity-dependent patients during large-scale disruptions. For individuals reliant on ventilatory support, oxygen concentrators, or airway-management devices, preparedness is not simply a technical matter – it is fundamental to ensuring continuity of care and maintaining patient safety. Addressing these challenges requires structured planning, coordinated action, and systems that can function reliably under adverse conditions. Moving forward, strengthening these elements through evidence-based strategies and shared responsibility will be essential to improving resilience and ensuring that patients remain protected during future events.
