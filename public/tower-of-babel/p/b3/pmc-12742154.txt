Investigating the potential of Anystis baccarum (Acari: Anystidae) and Orius insidiosus (Heteroptera: Anthocoridae) as biological control agents for Thrips parvispinus (Thysanoptera: Thripidae) in laboratory and greenhouse cage trials
Authors: Taro Saito, Rosemarije Buitenhuis, Kris Godfrey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Thrips parvispinus (Karny) is a tropical thrips species that recently spread across the world. Extensive plant damage caused by T. parvispinus results in serious losses for growers, for this species feeds mostly on young leaves and flowers. Among many host plants, peppers are readily attacked by T. parvispinus across its geographical range. Effective and sustainable control methods for T. parvispinus in pepper production are urgently needed. Among the biocontrol agents available in North America, Orius insidiosus (Say) and Anystis baccarum L. were chosen for this study because both species are known to establish in pepper crops and are able to kill all mobile life stages, including the adults, of other thrips species. We carried out comparative efficacy trials in the laboratory using sweet pepper leaf disks and in the greenhouse using potted sweet peppers. The laboratory trial showed that 1 adult female of O. insidiosus or A. baccarum can kill adult female T. parvispinus at a similar rate, with approximately 21 and 18 thrips out of 40 killed in 24 h, respectively. The greenhouse cage trial showed that releasing either adult female O. insidiosus or adult female A. baccarum at the rate of 1 per 2 plants resulted in successful establishment of the predator population and reduced T. parvispinus population by 80% compared to the untreated control. Further studies are needed to validate this finding in larger scale greenhouse trials without cages, including damage assessment and fruit yield data.Keywords: generalist predator, integrated pest management, pepper thrips, tobacco thrips

Introduction

Thrips parvispinus (Karny) is a tropical thrips species that has spread from South Asia to all over the world in the past few decades, most likely on infested plant material (Mound and Collins 2000, Sridhar et al. 2021, Pijnakker 2022, Buitenhuis et al. 2024, Ahmed et al. 2025). Climate change is also likely helping the species to expand their habitable range (Hulagappa et al. 2022). Despite quarantine efforts, it was first reported in North America in Florida on ornamentals in 2020 and has now established in greenhouse, field and landscape plants in the Southeastern United States (Soto-Adames 2020, Ahmed et al. 2025). Thrips parvispinus has also been found in Ontario greenhouses (Gleason et al. 2023), but as it is thought not to overwinter outside in Canada (Pijnakker 2022), the Canadian government is currently not regulating this pest.

In North America, T. parvispinus has been found not only in tropical ornamental plant species, such as Mandevilla, Anthurium, Ficus, Hoya, but also in field peppers in Florida (Soto-Adames 2020). In its native range, this pest is also associated with significant damage to peppers and other solanaceous crops in Indonesia and India (Hulagappa et al. 2022). Extensive plant damage caused by T. parvispinus results in serious losses for growers, because this thrips feeds mostly on young leaves and flowers (Pijnakker 2022). Pepper plants appear to be readily accepted by T. parvispinus in every area this thrips invaded, although there are some variations in the level of thrips resistance exhibited among different pepper species (Maharijaya et al. 2011). It seems inevitable that T. parvispinus will become problematic in pepper greenhouses in Canada.

However, this is not the first thrips species causing global issues. Western flower thrips, Frankliniella occidentalis Pergande (Thysanoptera: Thripidae), a highly polyphagous species with high reproductive capacity, cryptic habits, and short generation time, has been one of the most important agricultural pests globally since 1970s (Reitz 2009). Much can be learned from its history to shape the management strategies of T. parvispinus. The initial response from the horticultural industry against F. occidentalis invasion was a period of intensive pesticide use, which resulted in the development of resistance to many classes of insecticide in a short period of time (Broadbent and Pree 1997, Jensen 2000, Thalavaisundaram et al. 2008, Gao et al. 2012). For example, F. occidentalis developed resistance against spinosad in only 6 mo since its registration in Canada. As a result, biological control, a pest control practice using natural enemies, has been widely adopted for thrips management in Canadian greenhouses (Murphy et al. 2011, Brownbridge et al. 2013).

Identifying effective and sustainable control methods for T. parvispinus in pepper production has become a priority on a global scale. So far, there are chemical insecticides still effective against T. parvispinus (Ataide et al. 2024); however, as F. occidentalis is still one of the most common and serious pests in Canadian greenhouse crops, management of other thrips species such as T. parvispinus must be compatible with on-going F. occidentalis biological control programs and meet the growing demand for greener, more sustainable agricultural practice by large commercial chains and consumers.

Biological control programs against thrips species regularly use phytoseiid predatory mites, especially Amblyseius swirskii Athias-Henriot and Neoseiulus cucumeris (Oudemans) (Buitenhuis et al. 2024). However, these predatory mites are capable of attacking mostly the eggs and larval first instars of thrips (Saito and Brownbridge 2022, Barbar et al. 2024). Additionally, the adults and second instar larvae of F. occidentalis and T. parvispinus were found to eat eggs of A. swirskii, although the effects on overall biocontrol with this predator have not yet been tested under greenhouse conditions (Le ­Hesran et al. 2025).

Among the biocontrol agents commercially available in North America, there are only a few predators that can eat and sustain their population on both the larvae and adults of other thrips species, which could be useful against T. parvispinus: These are Orius insidiosus (Say) (Isenhour and Yeargan 1981, Baez et al. 2004, Buitenhuis et al. 2024, Summerfield et al. 2024), Anystis baccarum L. (Saito and Brownbridge 2022, Buitenhuis et al. 2024), Dicyphus hesperus Knight (Heteroptera: Miridae), and Nabis americoferus Carayon (Hemiptera: Nabidae) (Saito et al. 2023b). Especially the first 2 predators have been shown to perform well against thrips species on pepper plants. Orius insidiosus has been reported to significantly influence the seasonal dynamics of Frankliniella thrips species population in field-grown pepper in southern United States (Funderburk et al. 2000, Ramachandran et al. 2001, Reitz et al. 2003). In Canada, greenhouse pepper growers either release commercially reared O. insidiosus for thrips control, or have naturally occurring adults flying in from the surrounding landscape during the summer months (Buitenhuis et al. 2024). Anystis baccarum is a relatively new biocontrol agent commercialized in Canada in 2021 and in parts of the United States in 2024. In a greenhouse sweet pepper study, A. baccarum was observed to complete multiple generations in the crop, was functionally compatible with the aphid parasitoid Aphidius ervi Haliday (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) to control foxglove aphids, and actually reduced feeding scars from F. occidentalis (Saito and Brownbridge 2021). Dicyphus hesperus is more commonly used in greenhouse tomato production (McGregor et al. 1999, Sanchez et al. 2002, Shipp and Wang 2006), and N. americoferus showed low affinity towards potted sweet pepper plants infested with aphids when tested in cages (Saito and Buitenhuis 2024). For these reasons, D. hesperus and N. americoferus were excluded from this study.

Based on the characteristics above, it was hypothesized that O. insidiosus and A. baccarum could be effective predators to control T. parvispinus in pepper. First, a comparative laboratory trial was conducted against adult T. parvispinus females to assess the predators’ maximum predation rate. The adult female thrips was used in the laboratory trial since it is not only the reproductive stage, but also the largest, presumably most challenging life stage for any arthropod predators to kill. Second, a comparative greenhouse cage trial was conducted to assess the predators’ performance in potted sweet peppers, where the adult female thrips and predators were released together and the population dynamics of the arthropods were assessed over 8 wk.

Materials and Methods

Rearing of Arthropods Tested

Plants used for this study were all grown without any pesticides or biocontrol agents in a growth chamber (16L:8D, 23 ± 1 °C, 60% relative humidity or RH). Sweet peppers (Capsicum annuum L., var. Currier, Stokes Seeds Ltd, Ontario, Canada) and lima beans (Phaseolus lunatus L. var. Fordhook, Stokes Seeds Ltd) were seeded weekly and maintained until used in 0.5-L pots filled with soil-less growing media (Fafard’s Agro Mix G6, Sun Gro Horticulture, Massachusetts, United States). Gerberas (Gerbera jamesonii Adlam, var. Flori Line Maxi Yellow, HilverdaFlorist, De Kwakel, the Netherlands) were purchased as rooted plugs from a commercial propagator. They were transplanted and maintained until used in 0.5-L pots filled with the soil-less growing media. The fertigation was provided as needed.

Thrips parvispinus specimens were collected from commercial greenhouses. The colony was established and maintained in a growth chamber (16L:8D, 23 ± 1 °C, 60% RH), using thrips-proof screened cages (dome-shaped tents with a square floor, 60 × 60 × 60 cm; BugDorm 2120 F, MegaView Science, Taichung, Taiwan) containing several potted sweet pepper plants. Fresh plants were provided to the colony as needed from a stock in a separate growth chamber. Adult female thrips used for the experiments were collected directly from these plants using an aspirator within 2 h prior to each trial. To ensure the good quality of collected insects, the foliage was agitated over a white tray and only the adult females that were moving quickly were collected.

Orius insidiosus were purchased from Koppert Canada (Ontario, Canada). Upon receipt, adults were maintained in another dome cage containing a potted lima bean plant. Freeze-killed eggs of Ephestia kuehniella Zeller were provided as food. A new batch of insects were ordered for each trial and were used within 5 d from each receipt. Maintaining the insectary-reared insects in a cage for a few days prior to the trials screened out the weak individuals as they die on the cage floor. When the insects were collected for the trials, only individuals with good vigor, that were feeding on the Ephestia eggs and quickly trying to escape the capture, were collected.

Anystis baccarum open-rearing colony was also maintained in a dome cage (16L:8D, 25 ± 3 °C, 50% RH), but on potted gerberas with a mix of prey arthropods [Myzus persicae (Sulzer), Trialeurodes vaporariorum (Westwood), Tetranychus urticae Koch]. As the life stage demographic in a caged colony tends to synchronize due to the cannibalism, a new batch of adult females were collected from the colony for each trial and were used within 5 d from the first observation of adult stage in the colony. This timeline ensured the collected adult mites were fresh, since the adult A. baccarum lives about 3 wk. Only predators that were actively searching for prey on foliage were collected.

Laboratory Efficacy Trial

White opaque polypropylene cups (120 ml, 51 mm diameter, Qorpak, Pennsylvania, United States) were used for the trial arena. The lid of the cup had a circular hole (43 mm diameter) for ventilation, and a 7 cm × 7 cm piece of thrips-proof screen (mesh opening 0.15 mm) was held between the cup and the lid to prevent insects from escaping. There was also a hole in the side wall (1 cm diameter), closed with a foam ear plug, to introduce the arthropods into the trial arena.

One day prior to the thrips introduction, leaf disks (4.5 cm diameter) were freshly prepared from the stock of pesticide/predator-free sweet pepper plants. Each cup then received approximately 5 ml of 2% agar, and a leaf disk was partially embedded, abaxial side up, into the solidifying agar. Using an aspirator, adult female predators of unknown age were collected directly from their respective colonies, individually introduced in the cups and left undisturbed for 24 h as a starvation period. After the starvation period for the predators elapsed, 40 adult female T. parvispnus of unknown age were directly collected from the colony with an aspirator and added to the cups via the side hole. No alternative food or water were provided. The test containers were kept in a plastic storage bin (60 × 41.6 × 16.5 cm) that was placed in a growth chamber (16L:8D, 25 ± 1 °C, 70% RH). The mortality of both T. parvispinus and the predators was recorded 24 h later. Upon the assessment, the lid and screen were removed, and a clean Petri dish was quickly placed over the cup to prevent any surviving arthropods from escaping. The inside of cup was thoroughly examined using a stereomicroscope.

Each trial was replicated 10 times per treatment and was repeated twice (total n = 20/treatment). The observed number of dead thrips was “log (x + 0.1)” transformed and analyzed with a generalized linear mixed model using Proc GLIMMIX (α = 0.05, SAS version 9.4, Cary, North Carolina, United States), with the treatments as a fixed factor and the trial repetition block as a random factor. Tukey’s ­multiple comparison was used to contrast the results.

Greenhouse Cage Trial

The greenhouse trial was carried out from November 2024 until mid-February 2025. The winter season was chosen to reduce the risk of the thrips escaping to any neighboring agricultural/horticultural operations. Any escaped thrips would not survive, reproduce, and spread in the landscape during the Canadian winter. A research greenhouse compartment (25 × 50 m) was set at 16L:8D, 22:19 °C, with supplemental light provided by high-pressure sodium bulbs. The climate data logger indicated the temperature fluctuated ± 3 °C, and the relative humidity was in the range of 70% ± 5% during the trial. When 6 wk old, the sweet pepper plants were moved from the growth chamber to the greenhouse, and 8 plants were randomly selected and placed in a dome cage. Thirty cages were prepared and 3 treatments tested: (i) untreated control with no predators, (ii) adult Orius insidiosus, and (iii) adult Anystis baccarum. The treatments were randomly assigned to each cage, for a total of 10 replicate cages per treatment. The plants were under automated fertigation cycles via irrigation drippers, which were fed through the zipper opening of the cages. Modeling clay was used to seal off any unwanted gaps in the cages.

At the start of the trial, 24 adult female T. parvispinus were collected directly from the colony and released into each cage (ca. 6 T. parvispinus adult females per 2 plants). For the predator treatments, 4 adult females of the corresponding species were also collected and released in each cage in that treatment (ca. 1 female predator per 2 plants, similar to the recommended release rate at ca. 0.5 to 1 per plant for preventative or low thrips pressure situation). The age of the arthropods used was unknown. The plants were allowed to flower during the trial. Every 2 wk, 2 randomly selected plants were destructively sampled. The last 2 remaining plants were sampled at week 8 post release. After the first samples were taken at week 2, all sweet pepper plants were topped immediately, as the plants were reaching the cage ceiling, ensuring the plant height was approximately 45 cm at the main stem. The pruned plant materials were left in the cages, and the lateral shoots were allowed to develop without further intervention. At each destructive sampling, all samples were handled in the same manner on the same dates. A large plastic bag was placed over the 2 selected plants collectively, and the stems were cut at the soil surface while the foliage was in the bag. The pots of the harvested plants were left in the cage. The collected foliage samples were stored only overnight at 4 °C to immobilize any live arthropods on the samples. The arthropod populations on those 2 plants were then determined in next 2 d, using the plant-wash technique (Sutherland et al. 2009). After the cold storage overnight, the plants were washed in 70% ethanol, which was then filtered using a Büchner funnel. The filter paper with arthropods were subsequently stored for a few hours in −20 °C to make sure no thrips were still alive, and the arthropod numbers on the filter paper were counted under a stereomicroscope. The plant-wash data were categorized into T. parvispinus larvae, T. parvispinus adults, O. insidiosus nymphs, O. insidiosus adults, A. baccarum nymphs, and A. baccarum adults. In order to reduce subjectivity, the decision to determine if the specimens were alive or dead at the time of sampling was based on the state of decomposition: ie “alive” specimens were usually plump and intact, not easily breaking apart when handled by needle tips even if the specimen was damaged during the plant harvest and/or ethanol wash; contrastingly “dead” specimens were desiccated or flattened, easily disintegrating when handled by needle tips. The dead specimens were excluded from the counts. These data were used to interpret the temporal age-demographic and population dynamics for each arthropod species. The statistical analysis on the treatment effects, however, was applied to only T. parvispinus data, log-transformed and analyzed with a generalized linear mixed model using Proc GLIMMIX (α = 0.05, SAS version 9.4) with repeated measures. The treatment and time, and their interaction were considered as fixed factors. Negative binomial distribution was assumed, and an AR(1) covariance structure was also chosen to consider the dependency between observations taken over time on the same cage. A least-squares means test was used to evaluate the significance of main effects.

Results

Laboratory Efficacy Trial

Adult T. parvispinus females survived so well in our test arena for 24 h that there was only the mean ± SE of 0.15 ± 0.11 dead observed from the untreated control. No mortality was observed for the predators. The mean number of adult female T. parvispinus eaten and/or killed in 24 h out of 40 thrips was 20.95 ± 0.97 for O. insidiosus and 18.2 ± 0.73 for A. baccarum, causing significantly higher mortality of adult T. parvispinus females compared to the untreated control (F2,2 = 627.8, P = 0.002). The difference in the predation rate between the 2 predator species was not significant. There was not enough variation in the block effect to attribute any variation to the random effect.

Greenhouse Cage Trial

Predator treatment, time, and their interaction all had significant effects on larval T. parvispinus numbers (treatment: F2,26 = 33.33, P < 0.001; time: F3,77 = 76.49, P < 0.001; time* treatment: F6,77 = 5.1, P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Orius insidiosus treatment significantly reduced the number of T. parvispinus larvae by week 2 post release while A. baccarum treatment was still not significantly different compared to the untreated control. The mean number of thrips larvae ± SE at week 2 was 61.7 ± 6.68 (untreated control), 12.3 ± 3.34 (O. insidiosus, 80% reduction compared to the untreated control), and 37.11 ± 6.16 (A. baccarum, 39.9% reduction). Anystis baccarum treatment closed the gap with O. insidiosus treatment by week 4, and both predator species were equally effective from there on.

A) Mean number of larval Thrips parvispinus per 2 potted pepper plants; B) mean number of adult T. parvispinus per 2 potted pepper plants; C) mean number of larval and adult T. parvispinus combined per 2 potted pepper plants; D) mean number of Orius insidiosus and Anystis baccarum per 2 potted pepper plants. Significant differences within a sampling point are indicated by different letters (in order of the legend when listed consecutively).

The adult thrips numbers showed that both treatment and time were significant factors but their interaction was not (treatment: F2,26 = 7.47, P = 0.003; time: F3,74 = 85.23, P < 0.001; time*treatment: F6,74 = 0.89, P = 0.5, Fig. 1B). Orius insidiosus treatment significantly reduced the number of T. parvispinus adults by week 4 while A. baccarum treatment was still not significantly different compared to the untreated control. Anystis baccarum treatment reached the similar efficacy level with O. insidiosus treatment by week 6, and both predator species were still equally effective at week 8.

Predator treatment, time, and their interaction all had significant effects on total T. parvispinus numbers (treatment: F2,26 = 28.34, P < 0.001; time: F3,77 = 103.37, P < 0.001; time*treatment: F6,77 = 3.25, P < 0.006). Due to the large proportion of larval stages in the observed thrips population, the statistical trend was identical to what was observed in the analysis of larval thrips. Orius insidiosus treatment was already effective by week 2, where A. baccarum treatment was still not significantly different compared to the untreated control. The mean number of total thrips at week 2 was 65 ± 6.99 (untreated control), 14.1 ± 3.69 (O. insidiosus, 78.3% reduction compared to the untreated control), and 40.44 ± 6.76 (A. baccarum, 37.8% reduction). Overall, T. parvispinus appeared to reproduce well on this variety of sweet pepper as the original approximately 6 female thrips per 2 plants increased to 1,311.7 ± 270.6 thrips (larvae and adults combined) in 8 wk when there were no predators around. The release rate at 1 female predator per 2 plants, either O. insidiosus or A. baccarum, resulted in approximately 80% thrips reduction at week 8, where the final thrips numbers were 263.9 ± 127.7 or 277.2 ± 55.3, respectively (Fig. 1C).

The population dynamics observed in O. insidiosus numbers indicated that the original O. insidiosus females (release rate at 1 female per 2 plants) successfully oviposited soon after being released, resulted in 1.6 ± 0.4 O. insidiosus nymphs per 2 plants in 2 wk. The second generation of adults started to appear after week 4. By the final sampling at week 8, there were 2.9 ± 1.4 nymphs and 0.8 ± 0.5 adults found per 2 plants (Fig. 1D, blue line).

The A. baccarum population increase was slower than O. insidiosus, resulting in none found at week 2. At week 4, however, 1.9 ± 0.6 nymphs of A. baccarum were observed. The adult A. baccarum finally appeared at week 8, where 2.4 ± 0.5 nymphs and 2.4 ± 1.2 adults were found per 2 plants (Fig. 1D, yellow line).

Discussion

The laboratory trial in this study aimed to expand the knowledge of the host range and efficacy of O. insidiosus and A. baccarum as biocontrol agents for T. parvispinus. It showed that both predators can kill adult T. parvispinus females in similar numbers. More importantly, our results indicate strong potential for O. insidiosus and A. baccarum as biocontrol agents for T. parvispinus. The test arena used in the laboratory trial was designed to ensure a high encounter rate (the frequency of interaction between the predator and prey), with a high prey density in a confined space. Because the encounter rate is among the main determinants of predation rate (Reitz et al. 2006), the result from our laboratory trial is likely in favor of the predators. Other studies that employed similar methods, ie 24 h leaf disk assay, reported that 1 adult O. insidiosus female killed approximately 5 adult F. occidentalis females and 14 adult T. tabaci females out of 20 thrips in 24 h (Summerfield et al. 2024); One adult female of O. laevigatus and O. majusculus killed approximately 18 and 20 F. occidentalis adults, respectively, out of 60 adult thrips in 24 h, while the predation rate was around 2 times higher when more sedentary thrips species, E. americanus or T. setosus, were offered as prey (Mouratidis et al. 2022). In the current study, an adult O. insidiosus female killed approximately 21 adult T. parvispinus females out of 40 thrips in 24 h. An Anystis baccarum adult female killed approximately 5 adult F. occidentalis female out of 10 thrips in 24 h (Saito and Brownbridge 2022); whereas the current study found that approximately 18 adult T. parvispinus females were killed out of 40 thrips in 24 h. Considering these reported efficacies of Orius spp. and A. baccarum with several thrips species, our data suggest that T. parvispinus is not particularly difficult species for these predators to attack.

Our greenhouse cage trial based the predator release rates on the current recommended preventative/low pressure release rate for other thrips species. Releasing adult females of either O. insidiosus or A. baccarum at the rate of 1 predator per 2 plants led to 3.7 O. insidiosus or 4.9 A. baccarum per 2 plants in 8 wk and reduced T. parvispinus population by 80% relative to the untreated control. In studies using intact plants, however, the distribution of predator and prey within the plant structures affects the encounter rate, which has been previously identified as a cause of failures in biocontrol (Jandricic et al. 2016). Our results suggest that the encounter rate between T. parvispinus and these predators on the sweet pepper plant remains high, especially when plant canopies overlap. The number of predators observed at week 8 post release, approximately 4 per 2 plants, is actually similar to the recommended curative release rate. Based on the maximum predation rate determined in our laboratory trial, 4 of either predators can potentially eat 70 to 80 T. parvispinus per day, which seems adequate against approximately 300 T. parvispinus per 2 plants observed at week 8.

Previous studies reported superior efficacy of Orius spp. and A. baccarum (adults are ca. 2 to 3 mm in length for both) compared to phytoseiid mites (adults ca. 0.5 mm in length) for managing thrips species in small-scale greenhouse studies (Skirvin et al. 2006, Doğramaci et al. 2011, Saito and Brownbridge 2022). This is partly because thrips species can exhibit several antipredator behaviors which are effective against phytoseiid mites (Beretta et al. 2024, Le Hesran et al. 2025). Although large generalist predators such as O. insidiosus and A. baccarum have relatively long life cycles, vegetable crops production cycles typically provide sufficient time (at least a couple of months) for the predator population to build up and suppress thrips population growth. The population growth pattern of predators observed in our greenhouse cage trial agrees with the previously reported laboratory studies on development time for these predators. It takes about 20 d from egg to adult for O. insidiosus (Bueno et al. 2006), and about 40 d for A. baccarum (Saito and Brownbridge 2021). At week 2 post release in our greenhouse trial, O. insidiosus nymphs (F1) were already present, but no A. baccarum were observed. Gravid A. baccarum tends to migrate down to the soil surface or even underneath the pots for oviposition, and the eggs take at least 10 d to hatch, which explains the absence of A. baccarum on the foliage at that time. Assuming they matured sometime around week 3, new O. insidiosus adults were already observed amongst older nymphs at week 4. At the same time, A. baccarum nymphs (F1) were finally observed. At week 8, there were already F2 O. insidiosus nymphs present. The F1 A. baccarum presumably matured around week 6 and were observed at week 8. No F2 A. baccarum nymphs were found yet as they would not have hatched from eggs at that time.

Although both predators are similar in size and have a similar host range, they have other characteristics worth considering before incorporating them in IPM programs. Orius insidiosus can fly as adults, tends to stay on the crop, and its prey searching behaviors are well tuned to use thrips nonvolatile semiochemicals (Traczyk et al. 2020). Despite the fact that this predator species enters reproductive diapause at short day lengths, limiting its establishment in the crop without supplemental lights in winter (Buitenhuis et al. 2024), the aforementioned characteristics may help O. insidiosus to establish when thrips populations are low, especially with the presence of pollen from flowers. Indeed, our greenhouse study showed that O. insidiosus treatment was already showing 78.3% thrips population reduction at week 2 when thrips population was still low, which was significantly more efficacious than 37.8% reduction observed in A. baccarum treatment. Anystis baccarum is slower to establish, not able to fly, and prey searching is largely dictated by random tactile sensing. Due to their highly nomadic nature, it is important to maintain a high encounter rate between A. baccarum and its potential prey to keep A. baccarum on the crop. Otherwise, the released A. baccarum would be readily lost in the greenhouse structure. When A. baccarum is released in a crop with low pest numbers where the encounter rate is expected to be low, it must be paired with high application rate of supplemental food items such as E. kuehniella eggs, brine shrimp cysts, and even bulk-formulation of N. cucumeris (via Intra-guild predation). Presence of flowers would not be as beneficial as it is for establishment of O. insidiosus. It has been shown that the supplemental foods (E. kuehniella eggs and pollen) also improve establishment and efficacy of O. insidiosus (Labbé et al. 2018). Anystis baccarum may be more useful in winter months, for it actively hunts and reproduces under short day length and temperature as low as 10 °C (TS personal observation).

As this study focused on laboratory and small cage trials, the results need to be validated in larger scale greenhouse trials without cages, including damage assessment and fruit yield data. Regardless, the results from this study suggest that both A. baccarum and O. insidiosus are effective predators for T. parvispinus management in greenhouse sweet pepper production. These relatively large generalist predators are effective biocontrol agents with wide host ranges, including several other thrips species, phytophagous mites, aphids, whiteflies, small caterpillars, and eggs of various arthropods (Lattin 1999, Cuthbertson et al. 2014, Saito et al. 2023a). This study adds 1 more species to the list of pest species that A. baccarum and O. insidiosus can control. Nevertheless, neither of the predator species alone was able to completely suppress T. parvispinus population. It is for future studies to identify effective combinations of biocontrol agents for more complete T. parvispinus management.
