Chromatin remodeler ISW-1 orchestrates chromosome axis assembly and meiotic progression in C. elegans
Authors: Ruoxi Wang, Yating Sun, Jian Kang, Jing Tian, Shuo Wang, Zixuan Zhu, Li Zhao, Feifei Qi, Jinmin Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring meiotic prophase, chromosomes undergo dynamic morphological changes that coordinate key events, including axis assembly, homolog pairing and synapsis, recombination, crossover formation, and large-scale chromosomal remodeling. Chromatin remodelers are conserved and abundant regulators of gene expression and chromatin dynamics, yet their roles in meiosis remain incompletely understood. Here, we investigate the function of the ISWI chromatin remodeler ISW-1 in Caenorhabditis elegans meiosis. We show that ISW-1 is essential for meiotic progression and physically interacts with components of the chromosome axis. Loss of ISW-1 disrupts axis integrity, impairs synapsis and crossover formation, and reduces physiological apoptosis during oogenesis, defects that are attributable to a non-transcriptional regulatory role. Additionally, ISW-1 deficiency causes chromosome deformation in late prophase oogenic cells and leads to more severe defects in axis formation during spermatogenesis. These findings uncover a critical role for the ISWI complex in orchestrating meiotic chromosome structure and function, in part through direct regulation of axis assembly and chromosome-wide remodeling.

Introduction

Meiosis is a fundamental process in sexually reproducing organisms, enabling the generation of haploid gametes such as sperm and eggs. During meiosis, DNA undergoes a single round of replication, followed by two consecutive divisions: meiosis I (separation of homologous chromosomes) and meiosis II (separation of sister chromatids). To ensure the accurate segregation of chromosomes, a series of tightly regulated events occur during meiotic prophase, including double-strand break (DSB) formation, homologous chromosome pairing, synapsis, recombination, and chromatin remodeling [1]. Defects in any of these processes can result in chromosome missegregation, leading to infertility, miscarriages, and congenital disorders.

During meiotic prophase, cohesin complexes maintain sister chromatid interactions and recruit meiosis-specific proteins to form the chromosome axis [2]. This axis organizes chromatin into a meiosis-specific axis–loop structure, which facilitates homolog pairing, synapsis, recombination, and crossover formation [2–4]. In late prophase I, chromosomes undergo further condensation, forming the characteristic bivalent structure [4, 5]. This process is accompanied by the partial disassembly of the synaptonemal complex (SC), preparing homologous chromosomes for segregation [4]. These dynamic and tightly coordinated chromosomal changes highlight the extensive morphological remodeling that occurs during meiotic prophase, emphasizing the pivotal role of chromatin-remodeling complexes in regulating these processes.

ATP-dependent chromatin-remodeling complexes are highly conserved in eukaryotes and are classified into four subfamilies in higher eukaryotes: switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI/SNF), chromodomain-helicase DNA-binding (CHD), imitation switch (ISWI), and inositol-requiring 80 [6]. These complexes use the energy derived from ATP hydrolysis to drive nucleosome assembly, sliding, and histone exchange, thereby regulating transcription, replication, and DNA repair [7]. Their roles in the transcriptional regulation of meiosis have been revealed in multiple organisms. In mice, members of the SWI/SNF family (e.g. BRG-1 and SMARBI) regulate chromatin remodeling, homologous recombination, DNA damage repair, and gene activation to promote the expression of meiosis-related genes, thereby ensuring the proper progression of meiosis [8–11, 12]. In fission yeast, Hrp1 and Hrp3, members of the CHD family, regulate transcription programs to alter chromatin structure, thereby facilitating meiotic recombination [13]. In Caenorhabditis elegans, CHD-3 and its paralog LET-418 facilitate meiotic progression and maintain genome integrity by ensuring faithful repair of DSBs through homologous recombination [14]. In mice, members of the ISWI, CHD, and INO80 chromatin-remodeling families have also been revealed to promote meiotic progression by regulating meiosis-related gene expression. CHD8 promotes the proliferation of undifferentiated spermatogonia and ensures the proper progression of prophase I during spermatogenesis by transcriptional regulation [15]. INO80 similarly regulates the expression of meiotic genes, and its deficiency impairs DNA damage repair and causes meiotic arrest [16, 17]. The requirement of the ISWI family protein Smarca5 for meiotic progression in both males and females has been revealed [18, 19], yet the specific molecular mechanisms underlying its function remain to be elucidated. Furthermore, whether chromatin-remodeling complexes directly orchestrate meiotic chromosome organization remains unclear.

Here, we investigated the role of chromatin remodeler ISW-1 during oogenesis and spermatogenesis in C. elegans. ISW-1 is the sole ISWI family member in nematodes and shares highly conserved sequence and structure across eukaryotes. Our data suggest that ISW-1 interacts with axis components to facilitate loop–axis structure formation and promotes chromosome-scale remodeling during late prophase, two stages involving major chromosomal reorganization. Although ISW-1 is required for the production of both functional oocytes and sperm, its loss results in defects of differing severity between the two gametogenic programs. These findings suggest that ISW-1 acts as an essential “organizer” of meiotic chromosomes during meiotic prophase, possibly independent of its role in transcriptional regulation.

Materials and methods

C. elegans strains and culture conditions

The Bristol N2 strain served as the wild-type control in this study, and all mutants were generated on the N2 genetic background. Worms were cultured at 20°C on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50. The isw-1::gfp strain was created using CRISPR/Cas9 [clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR-associated protein 9] genomic editing. The plasmids used included a Cas9 expression plasmid [Peft-3::Cas9-SV40_NLS::tbb-2 3′-untranslated region (UTR), 200 ng/μl], guide RNAs (pU6_sgRNA, 100 ng/μl), mCherry co-injection marker plasmids (pCFJ90 and pCFJ104, 5 ng/μl each), and plasmids containing the repair donor templates (200 ng/μl). These plasmids were co-injected into the gonads of young adult N2 hermaphrodites. F1 progeny expressing the mCherry co-injection marker were individually isolated onto single plates and screened for successful genome edits via PCR and fluorescence microscopy. The primer sequences used for PCR screening were as follows: isw-1::gfp(cac81) F:5′-GATTATGTTTTGCATGCGGT-3′; and isw-1::gfp(cac81) R: 5′-ACATCAACCGATGTGTCGAA-3′. The amplicon size is 3416 bp. The strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plate phenotyping

Plate phenotyping was performed to assess brood size, egg viability, and the frequency of male progeny from self-fertilizing hermaphrodites. L4-stage hermaphrodites were individually placed on NGM plates and transferred to fresh plates every 24 h. The number of eggs laid was recorded immediately after each transfer. Unhatched eggs were counted 24 h later, and surviving progeny, including adult hermaphrodites and males, were scored 72 h post-transfer. These data were used to calculate the total number of fertilized eggs, adult brood size, embryonic lethality, and the percentage of male offspring for each P0 animal.

Antibodies used in this study

The following antibodies were used at the indicated dilutions: rabbit anti-SYP-5 {1:1 000 for immunofluorescence (IF), [20]}; rabbit anti-phospho-H3 (Ser10; 1:1 000 for IF; PA5-17869; Thermo Fisher Scientific); rabbit anti-HIM-8 (1:8000 for IF; Novus Biologicals, 41 980 002); rabbit anti-RAD-51 (1:10 000 for IF; Novus Biologicals); guinea pig anti-HTP-3 (1:500 for IF, [21]); mouse anti-HA [1:2000 for western blot (WB); HA-100; NuoyiBio]; mouse anti-FLAG (1:2000 for WB; F1804; Sigma-Aldrich); chicken anti-green fluorescent protein (GFP) (1:2000 for WB; ab13970; Abcam); horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:5000; ZB-5305; ZSGB-BIO); HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-chicken (1:5000; SA00001-6; Proteintech); fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:300; ZF-0311; ZSGB-BIO); and tetramethylrhodamine isothiocyanate (TRITC)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:300; ZF-0316; ZSGB-BIO).

Protein sequence and structure analysis

The domain architecture of ISW-1 was predicted using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Secondary structure prediction was performed with JNet (http://www.compbio.dundee.ac.uk/www-jpred/). Conservation analysis of ISW-1 with vertebrate sequences was conducted using ConSurf (http://consurf.tau.ac.il/), which provided per-residue conservation scores for the full-length ISW-1 protein. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction of ISW-1 homologs were carried out using Clustal Omega. Protein structure predictions were generated using AlphaFold, and structural alignment and visualization were performed in PyMOL.

Time-course analysis of larval development

Synchronized adult worms (36 h post-L4) were transferred to fresh plates to lay eggs for 2 h, after which adults were removed. About 36 h post-egg laying, L2–L3 larvae were transferred to new plates (100 worms per plate). To obtain isw-1 homozygotes, hT2-balanced heterozygotes were allowed to lay eggs, and their F1 progeny were manually selected under a fluorescence stereomicroscope at the L2–L3 stage. Worm development was monitored hourly from ∼40 h post-egg laying, and the number of L4-stage worms was recorded until all larvae reached this stage. Each assay included 100 larvae per replicate.

Whole-worm DAPI staining

Worms at the desired developmental stage were collected into 1.5 ml tubes and washed twice with M9 buffer. After removing the supernatant, worms were fixed in cold methanol (−20°C) for 2 min. Following centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 30 s, the methanol was removed, and worms were subsequently fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde [in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] at room temperature for 25 min. The samples were then washed once with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) and stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole 1 µg/ml in PBST; Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min. After staining, worms were washed five times with PBST (10 min each wash). Finally, the samples were mounted on slides using Vectashield mounting medium (Vector Laboratories) and sealed with nail polish.

Immunofluorescence microscopy analysis of the C. elegans germline

For gonad immunostaining, gonads were dissected from young adult hermaphrodites (24 h post-L4) in dissecting buffer, which contains 25 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 118 mM NaCl, 48 mM KCl, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, 0.1% Tween-20, and 10 mM NaN3 to release the gonads. The dissected gonads were transferred onto polylysine-coated slides and fixed using a freeze-crack method on dry ice. After removing the coverslip, the samples were first fixed in methanol at − 20°C for 1 min, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS at room temperature for 25 min. After washing with PBST for 5 min, the samples were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA; dissolved in PBST) at room temperature for 1 h. After blocking, the samples were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Following three washes in PBST, the samples were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. After washing with PBST, the samples were stained with DAPI (1 µg/ml in PBST) for 10 min and washed for 1 h in PBST. The stained gonads were mounted with Vectashield mounting medium and sealed with nail polish.

Measurement of ISW-1::GFP axis periphery distribution

To analyze the distribution of ISW-1::GFP along pachytene chromosomes, gonads dissected from isw-1::gfp worms were immunostained for HTP-3 and counterstained with DAPI. Z-stack images of whole nuclei were acquired at 0.2 µm intervals using Nikon AX R with NAPARC. Maximum-intensity projections of 2–3 Z-slices were used for quantification in ImageJ. Within each gonad zone, chromosome segments were selected for analysis if they met the following criteria: (i) an HTP-3-labeled axis of at least 1.5 µm could be traced, and (ii) a 20-pixel-wide line did not overlap with neighboring chromosomes. Each selected segment was straightened, and cross-axis fluorescence intensity profiles for each channel were obtained using the Plot Profile function. Mean intensity profiles from all segments in a gonad were averaged for further analysis. At least three gonads were analyzed.

Preparation of germ cell nuclear extracts

To enrich germ cell nuclei, ~1 ml of worm pellet was collected in a 15 ml centrifuge tube and resuspended in ice-cold nuclear extraction buffer [10 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 1 mM EGTA, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, 250 mM sucrose, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and a protease inhibitor cocktail]. The suspension was vortexed to disrupt the worms and release the gonads. Germ cell nuclei were then isolated using a differential centrifugation protocol [22]. The resulting nuclei were resuspended in cell lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 300 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 20 mM KCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 10 min to facilitate protein extraction. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected as the germ cell nuclear lysate for downstream analysis.

Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry analysis

The germ cell nuclear extracts of ISW-1::GFP were incubated with GFP Nanoab Agarose Beads (GNA-25-500, NuoyiBio) for 6 h at 4°C. The mixture was then washed five times with lysis buffer [20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 300 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 20 mM KCl, 1% Nonidet P-40 (NP-40), 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors]. Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling in 1% SDS and 25 mM Tris buffer (pH 7.4) for 5 min. The eluted proteins were precipitated with 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at 4°C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 14 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The precipitate was then washed once with 10% TCA and twice with cold acetone (14 000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C). The final protein pellet was collected and digested with trypsin for mass spectrometry analysis. At least two biological replicates were performed for each sample. To identify specific binding partners, the protein lists were compared with those obtained from at least three unrelated immunoprecipitations (IPs) performed under identical conditions. Protein abundance was quantified by calculating the relative spectral counts in each IP, determined by dividing the total spectral count of a given protein by the summed spectral counts of all proteins in the IP. Proteins identified in at least two replicates and absent from control IPs, or those showing at least an 8-fold enrichment compared with the control, were considered specific binding partners.

Protein co-expression and interaction analysis in 293T cells

The coding sequence of the ISW-1 protein was cloned into the pEGFP-N1 vector, the coding sequence of the HORMA proteins were inserted into a mammalian expression plasmid under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter and fused with an mCherry-HA tag, and the coding sequence of the REC-8 protein was cloned into the p3× Flag-CMV10 vector. Plasmid construction was performed using the homologous recombination method with the ConExpress II One-Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C112) and confirmed by DNA sequencing. The plasmids were co-transfected into 293T cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI; Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were lysed in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, 1 mM EGTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM KCl, 10% glycerol, 1% NP-40, 1 mM DTT, and a protease inhibitor cocktail. The cell lysate IPs were incubated with GFP Nanoab Agarose Beads (GNA-25-500, NuoyiBio) or Flag Nanoab Agarose Beads (FNA-25-500, NuoyiBio) at 4°C for 6 h to perform IP. The immunoprecipitates were separated by sodium dodecylsulfate (SDS)–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and analyzed by western blotting.

To eliminate potential DNA-mediated associations between protein complexes in the immunoprecipitates, the post-immunoprecipitated beads were washed three times with cell lysis buffer, followed by two washes with NT2 buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.05% NP-40). The beads were then incubated with 10 U of DNase I (RNase-free, Beyotime Biotechnology, D7073) in 0.1 ml of NT2 buffer or in a control buffer lacking DNase I at 37°C for 20 min [23]. After treatment, the samples were washed twice with cell lysis buffer and subjected to SDS–PAGE followed by western blot analysis. DNase I activity was validated using purified genome DNA and confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole-nucleus fluorescence intensity measurements

To analyze the axis loading of GFP-tagged HORMA proteins and REC-8 in whole nuclei, Z-stack images of whole germ cell nuclei were acquired at 200 nm intervals. Maximum intensity projection images derived from the 3D stacks were used for intensity quantification [24]. For each nucleus, 2D image data were extracted and saved as “Text images” in ImageJ. These text files were then batch processed using R scripts to evaluate background signal intensities and calculate the fluorescent signal above background for each nucleus. Specifically, pixel intensities within the text image were sorted in descending order, and the mean value of the lowest quartile of pixel intensities was used to define the background intensity. Pixels exceeding this mean background value were adjusted by subtracting the mean background, and the remaining intensities were summed to calculate the total fluorescence for each nucleus. This total fluorescence intensity was then normalized by the mean background value to yield a relative intensity for each nucleus. The average relative signal intensities from nuclei within the same gonadal zone were used for subsequent comparisons.

RNA sequencing

To synchronize the worms, L4-stage animals (~2000 worms per sample) were selected and collected 6 h after reaching the L4 stage. The worms were washed into 1.5 ml EP tubes with M9 buffer, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 1 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The worms were then washed three times with M9 buffer, followed by a single wash with PBS. Subsequently, the EP tubes containing the worms were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manual’s instruction, with three biological replicates for each genotype. RNA was processed using the Optimal Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit (BGI-Shenzhen, China). mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random hexamer primers, and double-stranded cDNA was generated. The cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation. Libraries were amplified by PCR and quality-checked. Single-stranded circular libraries were prepared by circularizing linear DNA molecules, with uncyclized DNA digested. Circularized libraries were amplified using rolling circle amplification to produce DNA nanoballs (DNBs). These DNBs were loaded onto a patterned nanoarray, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated using the T7 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China).

Analysis of germline-enriched genes

Germline-enriched genes were identified using single-cell RNA sequencing data from L2-stage C. elegans [25]. Genes were classified as germline-enriched if their expression levels in the germline were at least 4-fold higher than the average expression across all other tissues, and if they exhibited a minimum expression level of 30 transcripts per million (TPM) in the germline. Based on these criteria, a total of 1 951 genes were defined as germline-enriched.

Quantitative real-time PCR

For each biological replicate, ~200 worms at 6 h post-L4 were collected. The worms were washed sequentially with M9 buffer and water prior to RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the Animal Total RNA Isolation Kit (Beijing Forgene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentrations were measured with a Micro Drop spectrophotometer (BIO-DL, China). cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, R223-01). qPCR was performed with ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312-02) on a LightCycler 480 II System (Roche). The ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the fold change in mRNA expression levels between the wild type and isw-1 mutant, with cdc-42 serving as the reference gene [26]. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Quantification of homolog pairing

Homolog pairing quantification analysis of germ cells in young adult hermaphrodites (24 h post-L4) was performed, with at least five complete gonads analyzed for each genotype. Germ cells in meiotic prophase were divided into five equally sized zones, from the TZ to the end of pachytene. Chromosome pairing status was assessed by immunostaining for the X chromosome pairing center protein HIM-8. Nuclei with two distinguishable HIM-8 foci, spaced at least 0.5 µm apart, were defined as having unpaired X chromosomes.

Quantification of COSA-1 foci

To quantify COSA-1 foci in germ cells, young adult hermaphrodites (24 h post-L4) expressing the COSA-1::GFP marker were dissected to release their gonad arms. The gonads were then fixed and stained with DAPI. COSA-1 foci within germ cell nuclei were quantified using 3D stacks of whole-nucleus images. The number of COSA-1 foci per nucleus was counted, and the distribution of nuclei with different numbers of foci was determined. At least six gonad arms from each genotype were analyzed, and the experiment was conducted with a minimum of three biological replicates.

Quantification of RAD-51 foci

RAD-51 foci in germ cell nuclei were quantified using 3D stacks of whole-nucleus images. Germ cells in meiotic prophase, spanning from the TZ to the end of pachytene, were divided into five equal-sized zones. The number of RAD-51 foci per nucleus within each zone was then assessed. At least three gonads per genotype or condition were analyzed.

Quantification of germ cell apoptosis

To assess germ cell apoptosis, young adult hermaphrodites (24 h post-L4) expressing the apoptotic marker CED-1::GFP were dissected to isolate their gonad arms. The gonads were then fixed and stained with DAPI for nuclear labeling. CED-1::GFP-positive germ cells, indicative of apoptosis, were typically observed at the late pachytene stage of the germline. A minimum of 20 gonad arms per genotype were analyzed, and the experiment was conducted with at least three biological replicates.

Male fertility assay

Male adult worms of the wild type and isw-1 mutants were mated with three wild-type hermaphrodites. After 24 h, hermaphrodite individuals were selected and examined for successful mating (defined as a higher proportion of male offspring). If at least one hermaphrodite successfully mated, the male worm was considered to have fertility. The proportion of male progeny was calculated, and if multiple hermaphrodites showed successful mating, the highest male proportion was recorded. The experiment was repeated at least 10 times.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance is indicated in the figures or figure legends. Graphs and statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad). Differences in means were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

ISW-1 is required for oogenic meiotic progression

The chromatin-remodeling factor ISWI is evolutionarily conserved, with orthologs widely present across eukaryotes (Supplementary Fig. S1). In mammals, two ISWI homologs exist, SMARCA1 and SMARCA5, which share 86% sequence identity. In C. elegans, ISW-1 is the sole ISWI homolog. It contains an N-terminal ATPase/translocase domain (ATD), comprising two RecA-like lobes with small conserved insertions, and two SANT domains at its C-terminus (Fig. 1A). Both the ATD and SANT domains exhibit high amino acid conservation among vertebrate sequences (Fig. 1A). Alignment of predicted structures further reveals strong similarity between the structural domains of C. elegans ISW-1 and those of human SMARCA1 and SMARCA5 (Fig. 1B).

To assess the biological functions of ISW-1, we analyzed worm development using an available isw-1 null mutant [27]. The mutant worms exhibited an ~5 h delay in reaching the L4 stage after egg laying (Supplementary Fig. S2A), and their adult body size was modestly reduced compared with wild-type animals (Supplementary Fig. S2B, C). Whole-worm DAPI staining showed that the germline was more severely affected in the mutants (Supplementary Fig. S2D), suggesting that ISW-1 plays a particularly important role in germ cell development.

The spatially ordered organization of germ cells in C. elegans provides an excellent model for evaluating whether ISW-1 affects the germ cell development process. At the distal end of the gonad, germ cells in the PMT undergo mitotic proliferation. Upon entering meiosis, chromosomes condense and adopt a crescent-shaped configuration within the nucleus, marking the TZ, which corresponds to the leptotene/zygotene stages of meiotic prophase I (Fig. 1C, D). As cells progress into pachytene, chromosomes redistribute along the nuclear periphery. The TZ to pachytene interval is referred to as the early stage of meiotic prophase. Following pachytene exit, chromosomes undergo further remodeling and condensation, forming cross-shaped bivalents by the end of prophase I. At this late stage, six DAPI-stained bodies, representing six pairs of homologous chromosomes, are visible (Fig. 1C, D).

Compared with the wild type, isw-1 mutant hermaphrodites were completely sterile (Fig. 1E), suggesting a critical role for ISW-1 in the germline. Cytological analysis showed that ISW-1 loss did not significantly affect cell proliferation in the PMT region (Fig. 1C, F). However, the number of cells in early meiotic prophase was significantly reduced in isw-1 mutants (Fig. 1G). Moreover, in the late stage of meiotic prophase, normal oocytes were nearly absent, indicating severe defects in the progression of oogenic cells through diplotene and diakinesis. In mutant oocytes, chromatin appeared highly abnormal, exhibiting univalents or malformed bivalents, deformed chromosomes, and diffuse chromatin structures (“chromatin clouds”) (Fig. 1C). These observations suggest that the chromatin remodeler ISW-1 is essential for proper meiotic progression during oogenesis in the C. elegans germline. The sterile phenotype of the mutants can be largely attributed to germline defects, although somatic abnormalities may also contribute.

ISW-1 interacts with components of the meiotic chromosome axis

To further elucidate the specific role of ISW-1 in meiosis, we generated a GFP-tagged strain (isw-1::gfp) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing at the endogenous locus. Phenotypic analysis confirmed that GFP insertion did not affect fertility (Supplementary Fig. S3A–C). Whole-worm imaging showed that ISW-1::GFP was most abundant in late prophase oocytes and embryos, with detectable signals throughout the germline and in certain somatic cells (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Within the gonad, ISW-1::GFP expression was elevated in the PMT, TZ, and late meiotic prophase (post-pachytene), but moderately reduced during the pachytene stage (Fig. 2A), suggesting a dynamic expression pattern during germ cell development. This pattern coincides with stages characterized by extensive chromosomal reorganization, including meiosis-specific loop–axis formation upon meiotic entry and chromosome remodeling in late prophase.

To investigate the precise nuclear localization of ISW-1, we performed immunostaining for the axial protein HTP-3, which recruits other HORMA (Hop1/Rev7/Mad2) domain proteins and is required for synapsis and DSB formation [28], in isw-1::gfp worms. ISW-1::GFP was broadly distributed on the meiotic chromosomes, with a subset of the signal co-localizing with the axis (Fig. 2B). Quantitative analysis of ISW-1::GFP fluorescence on pachytene chromosomes further suggests an enrichment at the periphery of the axis (Fig. 2C).

To gain deeper insight into the potential role of ISW-1 in meiosis, we conducted IP and mass spectrometry analysis to identify potential interacting proteins of ISW-1 (Fig. 2D). As expected, we detected the nucleosome-remodeling factor 1 (NURF-1), a known ISW-1 interactor involved in nucleosome remodeling [27]. Notably, we also identified HORMA domain proteins (HIM-3 and HTP-3) and cohesin subunits (SMC-1 and SMC-3) as potential ISW-1-binding partners (Fig. 2D).

To validate these interactions, we assessed binary associations between ISW-1 and HORMA proteins in 293T cells. In this system, ISW-1 showed varying degrees of interaction, exhibiting stronger binding to HTP-1/2 and HIM-3 than to HTP-3 (Fig. 2E). These interactions are probably mediated by direct protein–protein interactions, as no interaction was observed between ISW-1 and C. elegans DNA-binding pairing center protein HIM-8 [29] when they were co-expressed, and DNase I treatment of immunoprecipitated complexes did not affect ISW-1–HORMA protein interactions (Supplementary Fig. S3E). In addition, we also detected an interaction between ISW-1 and the cohesin subunit REC-8 (Supplementary Fig. S3F).

These findings suggest that ISW-1 directly interacts with multiple core components of the meiotic chromosome axis, potentially contributing to chromosome dynamics through its local chromatin-remodeling activity. It is important to note that some interactions may occur within intact protein complexes in vivo, and discrepancies between in vivo and in 293T cell results are possible.

ISW-1 deficiency impairs meiotic chromosome axis integrity

In C. elegans, HORMA domain proteins (HTP-3, HIM-3, HTP-1, and HTP-2) form a core complex that collaborates with two types of meiosis-specific cohesin complexes, REC-8 cohesin and COH-3/4 cohesin, to mediate assembly of the meiotic chromosome axis (Fig. 3A). Each of these complexes is essential for normal meiotic progression [28, 30–34].

To examine whether ISW-1 influences meiotic axis–loop formation, we assessed the chromosomal recruitment of HORMA proteins (HIM-3, HTP-3, and HTP-1) and the cohesin subunit REC-8 in wild-type and isw-1 mutant backgrounds (Fig. 3B). In isw-1 mutants, the loading of GFP::HIM-3, HTP-3::GFP, and HTP-1::GFP onto the chromosome axis was significantly reduced (Fig. 3C–H). Consistent with this, SIM revealed that ISW-1 depletion severely disrupted HORMA protein assembly, resulting in discontinuous and fragmented signals along the chromosome axis (Fig. 3C, E, G), especially for GFP::HIM-3 (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate that ISW-1 is required for proper recruitment of HORMA domain proteins to the chromosome axis.

Additionally, ISW-1 deficiency affected the loading of the cohesin subunit REC-8. REC-8::GFP signals on the chromosome axis were markedly reduced in isw-1 mutants compared with the wild type (Fig. 3I, J), and SIM analysis confirmed disrupted REC-8 assembly in the absence of ISW-1 (Fig. 3I). These results suggest that ISW-1 is essential for the integrity of the meiotic chromosome axis potentially by supporting the chromatin recruitment and organization of both HORMA domain proteins and cohesin complexes.

ISW-1 deficiency disrupts normal gene expression patterns in the germline

As a chromatin remodeler, ISW-1 probably influences gene expression in the germline, potentially contributing to the observed meiotic defects. To assess the impact of ISW-1 loss on gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on wild-type and isw-1 mutant worms. Since isw-1 mutants fail to produce fertilized eggs, we collected samples 6 h post-L4, a developmental stage when oogenic cells have just entered diplotene/diakinesis but embryos have not yet formed, thus avoiding interference from embryonically expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A).

RNA-seq revealed widespread transcriptional changes in isw-1 mutants, with 3413 genes up-regulated and 3044 down-regulated compared with wild-type controls (Fig. 4A). Notably, many genes involved in meiosis, including SC components, HORMA domain proteins, and cohesin subunits, were broadly down-regulated (Fig. 4A), probably reflecting a reduction in meiotic prophase cells in the mutant germline (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S4B). Consistently, analysis of germline-enriched genes showed an ∼2-fold decrease in median expression levels, with 80% of differentially expressed germline genes down-regulated (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, despite the overall reduction in germ cell numbers, 20% of germline-enriched genes were up-regulated in the mutants, indicating that ISW-1 loss disrupts the normal regulatory pattern of gene expression. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of selected meiotic genes using whole-worm RNA further confirmed the transcriptomic trends observed (Supplementary Fig. S4C).

Because the overall reduction in germline-specific gene expression may result from the decreased number of germ cells, we next performed a differential expression analysis restricted to germline-enriched genes, assuming their expression to be germline-specific. This analysis revealed that the expression levels of core axis components, including htp-3, smc-1, rec-8, coh-3, coh-4, and him-3, were not significantly altered (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, htp-1 and htp-2, which also encode axial proteins, showed increased transcript levels in the mutants (Fig. 4D). However, cytological analysis showed that HTP-1 recruitment to the axis remained reduced, suggesting that the elevated mRNA levels do not translate into higher protein abundance or compensatory loading of axis proteins to account for the observed defects.

In contrast to the axis components, most syp genes, which encode the SC central region proteins, were down-regulated in the germline-restricted analysis (Fig. 4D). The altered expression of these and other germline-enriched genes may, in part, reflect differences in germ cell composition across developmental stages, as evidenced by the reduced pachytene zone in the mutant germline (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S4B).

Integrating the transcriptomic, cytological, and biochemical findings, we propose that the axis defects observed in the mutants may be attributable to the molecular functions of ISW-1 in chromatin remodeling during axis formation and maintenance, rather than to its indirect roles in gene regulation. Nonetheless, a contribution from misregulated gene expression cannot be fully excluded.

ISW-1 deficiency impairs synapsis and crossover formation while suppressing oogenic apoptosis

Meiotic chromosome axis formation is a prerequisite for homologous synapsis and homologous recombination. To assess the impact of ISW-1 loss on synapsis, we examined the localization of the SC central region component SYP-5 by immunostaining. In isw-1 mutants, SYP-5 assembled only on a subset of chromosomes (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S5A). Quantification revealed a mean of ∼4 SYP-5 tracks per nucleus in mutants, significantly fewer than the six tracks typically observed in wild-type germlines (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the average length of each SYP-5 track was significantly shorter in isw-1 mutants compared with the wild type (Fig. 5C), potentially suggesting incomplete synapsis on chromosomes that had initiated SC assembly.

Despite these synapsis defects, homologous pairing, assessed via immunostaining of the X chromosome pairing center protein HIM-8, was established during early prophase but was prematurely lost in late pachytene (Supplementary Fig. S5B, C), a pattern consistent with previously reported defects in synapsis-defective mutants [24, 35]. Analysis of crossover designation using GFP::COSA-1 foci showed a reduced number of foci in isw-1 mutants (Fig. 5D, E), suggesting impaired crossover formation, consistent with the synapsis defects.

Synapsis defects usually cause accumulation of RAD-51 containing recombination intermediates [36, 37]. However, RAD-51 foci were significantly reduced in isw-1 mutants during early meiotic prophase (TZ and early pachytene) and were not significantly changed in mid to late pachytene compared with the wild type (Fig. 5F, G). Given that chromosome axis formation promotes efficient meiotic DSB formation [21], the observed RAD-51 dynamics may reflect a combined effect of reduced DSB formation and altered DSB repair kinetics.

In synapsis-defective mutants, meiotic progression is typically delayed, activating a checkpoint that triggers increased apoptosis in late pachytene [30, 38, 39]. To evaluate apoptotic response in isw-1 mutants, we quantified CED-1-positive germ cells, as CED-1 marks apoptotic corpses. Surprisingly, isw-1 mutants exhibited a slight but consistent reduction in apoptotic germ cells compared with the wild type, suggesting attenuated activation of the meiotic checkpoint (Fig. 5H, I).

To test this further, we introduced the syp-5 mutation, which disrupts synapsis and causes elevated apoptosis (Fig. 5H, I) [20, 37]. In isw-1;syp-5 double mutants, however, the number of CED-1-positive cells was markedly reduced compared with syp-5 single mutants, suggesting that ISW-1 deficiency suppresses checkpoint-induced apoptosis. This is consistent with the model that a properly formed chromosome axis is required to coordinate meiotic events and mediate checkpoint activation [22, 31, 40, 41].

ISW-1 is essential for chromosome remodeling during late meiotic prophase in oogenesis

Oogenic cells in isw-1 mutants exhibit severe defects during late prophase, consistent with the high expression of ISW-1 at this stage. To further characterize these defects, we quantified late prophase nuclei based on chromosome morphology. In wild-type worms, six well-defined bivalents were consistently observed in diakinesis nuclei. In contrast, no normal diakinesis nuclei were observed in the isw-1 mutants. Approximately 15% of mutant nuclei contained compacted chromosomes but with univalents, ~25% showed deformed chromosomes larger than wild-type bivalents, and nearly 60% exhibited large DNA clouds, probably representing merged, severely deformed chromosomes (Fig. 6A). These morphological abnormalities indicate that ISW-1 deficiency severely impairs chromosome remodeling and condensation during late meiotic prophase.

Proper remodeling in late prophase is crucial for ordered meiotic chromosome segregation in the holocentric organism C. elegans. During this stage, chiasma formation partitions chromosomes into long and short arms. Remodeling includes the restriction of LAB-1 localization to the long arms and enrichment of SC and chromosome passenger complex (CPC) components on the short arms [22, 42, 43]. In isw-1 mutants, LAB-1 failed to maintain its normal localization pattern on compacted chromosomes and was completely absent from chromatin forming DNA clouds (Fig. 6B).

In addition, proper localization of the CPC to bivalent short arms is essential for local cohesin removal and homolog segregation during meiosis I [44–46]. Histone H3 phosphorylation at Ser10 (pH3), a CPC substrate, normally marks the short arms in late prophase [47]. However, in isw-1 mutants, pH3 was distributed throughout both compacted chromosomes and chromatin clouds (Fig. 6C), indicating a failure to restrict CPC activity. This is consistent with a chromosome-remodeling defect. These data suggest that ISW-1 may function as a critical regulator of late prophase chromosome remodeling during oogenesis in C. elegans.

ISW-1 deficiency causes more severe defects in axis formation during spermatogenesis

Given the critical role of ISW-1 in oogenesis, we next examined whether it has a similar function during spermatogenesis. Analysis of ISW-1::GFP expression revealed prominent chromosome-associated signals in the PMT and TZ nuclei of the male gonad, with signal intensity decreasing during pachytene (Supplementary Fig. S6A). This expression pattern resembles that observed in oogenic germlines. However, unlike in late prophase oocytes, ISW-1::GFP signal did not increase during late prophase in spermatocytes.

We further analyzed meiotic progression in male gonads using DAPI staining. In comparison with wild-type worms, isw-1 deletion did not visibly affect the PMT region of the germline (Fig. 7A). Strikingly, while wild-type male gonads exhibited a well-defined TZ, this region was nearly absent in the isw-1 mutant gonads. Additional abnormalities were observed during late meiotic prophase following pachytene exit. In wild-type gonads, spermatocytes progress synchronously through diplotene, karyosome, and division zones, ultimately producing post-meiotic sperm. In contrast, isw-1 mutant gonads exhibited disorganized late prophase regions with mixed and unsynchronized germ cell stages (Fig. 7A). Although some post-meiotic nuclei resembling mature sperm were present in the isw-1 mutant gonads, male fertility assays showed that isw-1 mutants were sterile, indicating that the produced sperm were not functional (Fig. 7B, C).

To investigate whether ISW-1 regulates meiotic chromosome axis formation in spermatogenesis, we examined the localization of the HORMA domain protein HTP-3. In isw-1 mutant males, HTP-3::GFP loading onto the chromosome axis was markedly reduced (Fig. 7D; Supplementary Fig. S6B), with a more pronounced reduction than that observed in hermaphrodite gonads (Fig. 3E). The severe axis assembly defects may underlie the near-complete absence of the TZ in mutants, consistent with previous reports showing that failure in axis formation abolishes TZ in the gonads [33, 48, 49]. In line with the disrupted axis formation, synapsis defects, as revealed by SYP-5 immunostaining, were also more severe in spermatogenic than in oogenic isw-1 mutant gonads (Figs 5A and 7E; Supplementary Fig. S6C).

Together, these findings reveal that ISW-1 is essential for proper axis formation and male fertility during spermatogenesis. The sexually dimorphic phenotypes observed in male versus hermaphrodite gonads may reflect differences in the kinetics of meiotic progression, as male germ cells undergo faster prophase progression [50].

Discussion

In conclusion, the chromatin-remodeling factor ISW-1 is essential for meiotic progression in oogenesis and spermatogenesis, exhibiting sexually dimorphic effects (Fig. 8). Although ISW-1 deficiency alters germline gene expression, the largely coordinated expression of core meiotic chromosome axis components suggests that ISW-1 may also play more direct roles in regulating axis assembly and chromosome remodeling. This is supported by our observation that ISW-1 physically interacts with key components of the meiotic chromosome axis.

Proposed model of ISWI function during meiosis in C. elegans. ISW-1, together with NURF-1, possesses chromatin-remodeling activity and interacts with meiotic axis components. On one hand, through its remodeling activity, ISW-1 maintains proper transcriptional programs required for normal meiotic progression. On the other hand, during early meiotic prophase, ISW-1 interacts with HORMA domain proteins and cohesin complexes, and, together with its nucleosome-remodeling activity, facilitates the assembly of a continuous meiotic chromosome axis, thereby promoting key downstream events such as synapsis and crossover formation. In late meiotic prophase of oogenic germ cells, ISW-1 contributes to chromosome remodeling. Loss of ISW-1 results in severe chromosome deformation, ultimately leading to the formation of large chromatin clouds and failure to produce eggs. During spermatogenesis, ISW-1 deficiency causes even more pronounced defects in axis formation. Although spermatocytes can progress through meiotic divisions, they produce non-functional, infertile sperm.

Potential conserved roles of ISWI in meiosis

In mammals, there are two ISWI homologs, Smarca1 (Snf2l) and Smarca5 (Snf2h), which exhibit distinct expression profiles and are thus implicated in different biological processes. Smarca5 is suggested to play a role in DNA replication-related activity, while Smarca1 is associated with cell maturation and differentiation [51]. A deletion mutation disrupting the ATPase domain of Smarca1 does not affect fertility in mice [52]. In contrast, germ cell-specific deletion of Smarca5 leads to infertility in both males and females [18, 19]. In Smarca5 conditional knockout males, pachytene spermatocytes display clear synapsis defects [19]. In females, oocyte-specific depletion of Smarca5 results in a failure to resume meiosis [18].

While transcriptional dysregulation may contribute to the observed defects in meiotic progression in both sexes, a direct role for Smarca5 in regulating meiotic chromosome dynamics remains a possibility. Notably, previous IP analyses of adult mouse testes identified meiotic axis components, including SYCP2 and the cohesin subunit SMC3, among the top SMARCA5-interacting proteins [19], supporting a potential physical association between SMARCA5 and meiotic axis complexes. These observations align with our hypothesis that ISWI directly contributes to axis assembly and chromosome dynamics during meiosis. Future studies examining the precise localization of SMARCA5 on meiotic chromosomes and its dynamic interactions with axis components will be essential to elucidate its molecular functions in meiotic chromosome organization and progression.

Molecular roles of chromatin remodelers in axis formation and chromosome remodeling

Chromatin remodelers are ATP-dependent enzymes that reposition or restructure nucleosomes to regulate DNA accessibility, thereby playing essential roles in processes such as transcription, DNA replication, and repair. In this study, we show that the ISWI-family remodeler is required for proper meiotic axis formation in C. elegans. This requirement may stem from its ability to space or slide nucleosomes, thereby facilitating the binding of core meiotic components to chromatin.

The specific role of ISWI in axis assembly may be further enhanced by its direct interaction with core axis complexes, including the HORMA-domain proteins and cohesin complexes. While nucleosome reorganization alone may be necessary for axis formation, the physical association between ISWI and these complexes suggests a more integrated function. For instance, ISWI may help the chromatin recruitment of axial components or, conversely, the axial complexes may promote the local recruitment of the ISWI remodeler. In the absence of ISWI activity, nucleosomes that normally need to be repositioned to permit binding of lateral axis components may remain obstructive, leading to reduced loading of axis complexes and impaired formation of a stable chromosome axis. It is also worth noting that the interactions between the axis complexes and the ISWI remodeler may be transient in vivo, a property that could enable the dynamic remodeling required during axis assembly. Interestingly, multiple HORMA-domain proteins (HTP-1/2/3 and HIM-3) interact with ISW-1 in mammalian cells, and these interactions may be mediated by their HORMA domains. The specific domains mediating interactions between ISWI and axial components remain to be characterized.

During late meiotic prophase, chromosome remodeling probably involves a combination of processes, including SC disassembly, axis remodeling, and large-scale chromatin reorganization [20, 22]. Whether chromatin-remodeling complexes such as ISWI play a direct role in axis remodeling remains to be determined, but they are likely to participate in broader chromatin reorganization. Indeed, ISW-1 deficiency results in a progressive loss of properly compacted chromosome structure toward the end of meiotic prophase during oogenesis, suggesting that ISWI contributes to maintaining chromatin architecture throughout meiosis.

Sexually dimorphic effects of ISWI deficiency in oogenesis and spermatogenesis

The loss of ISW-1 results in obvious sexually dimorphic phenotypes. Although ISW-1 deficiency affects both oogenesis and spermatogenesis, its impact on spermatogenesis is considerably more severe in the form of axis assembly defects during early prophase. Notably, the early stages of meiotic progression appear similar between the sexes until diplotene entry. However, in late meiotic prophase, while a subset of male germ cells can complete meiosis to produce sperm (albeit infertile), female germ cells consistently fail to generate functional oocytes.

These sexually dimorphic effects may stem from several factors, including differences in the timing of meiotic progression, sex-specific developmental programs of germ cells during late prophase, and potential compensation by other chromatin-remodeling factors.

In C. elegans, the duration of meiotic prophase is shorter in spermatogenesis than in oogenesis, ~20–24 h in males versus 54–60 h in hermaphrodites [50, 53]. The most prominent difference occurs at the pachytene stage, which lasts ~36 h in hermaphrodites but only ~6 h in males [50]. A slower progression rate during oogenesis may provide an opportunity to compensate for meiotic defects, leading to milder phenotypes compared with those observed in spermatogenesis. For instance, rec-8 mutants show more severe meiotic defects during spermatogenesis than oogenesis [33, 34], and defects associated with an smc-1 point mutation are alleviated when meiotic progression slows after depletion of endogenous sperm [54].

Similar sex-specific differences in meiotic timing are observed in other species, such as Drosophila [55], supporting the notion that sexually dimorphic meiotic phenotypes may be a common consequence of differential temporal regulation during meiosis.
