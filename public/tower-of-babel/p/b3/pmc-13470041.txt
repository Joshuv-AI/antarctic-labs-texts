Patient perspectives on tailored tuberculosis services: Lessons from India’s National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme
Authors: Sahjid Mukhida, Chanda Vyawahare, Sameena Khan, Vishwajeet Aundhakar
Published: 2026

To the Editor

We read with great interest the article by Idan et al., Tailoring TB Services for Improved Treatment Adherence in Ghana: A Mixed-Methods Study. 1 The authors should be commended for employing a mixed-methods approach to explore patients’ perspectives on differentiated tuberculosis (TB) service delivery. Their findings underscore the importance of patient-centered interventions, including nutritional support, decentralized drug delivery, peer support, flexible clinic schedules, and community-based services to improve treatment adherence. These recommendations are particularly relevant for high TB-burden countries striving to achieve the goals of the End TB Strategy.

The qualitative findings of the study are noteworthy because they move beyond conventional programmatic indicators and explore what patients themselves perceive as barriers to and facilitators of treatment adherence. Such patient-informed evidence is invaluable for designing differentiated service delivery models that are responsive to local needs rather than being driven solely by health system priorities.

Interestingly, many of the patient-centered interventions identified in the Ghanaian study have already been incorporated into India’s National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP). Over the past few years, India has strengthened its TB programme through free diagnosis and treatment, digital patient monitoring via the Nikshay portal, Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) under the Nikshay Poshan Yojana to provide nutritional support, and the recently strengthened Nikshay Mitra initiative, which encourages community participation in providing nutritional, psychological, and social support to persons with TB. Collectively, these initiatives represent an important shift from disease-centered management toward comprehensive patient-centered care.2,3

However, the Indian experience also illustrates that introducing patient-centered interventions alone does not guarantee their intended impact. A recent mixed-methods evaluation of the DBT programme reported important implementation challenges, including delays in financial assistance, which diminished the anticipated benefits for patients. Likewise, the revised Nikshay Mitra Policy has been advocated as a promising strategy to strengthen community engagement and provide holistic patient support, but its long-term effectiveness will ultimately depend on successful implementation, sustained participation, and, importantly, patients’ acceptance of these services.

This raises an important question that extends the discussion initiated by Idan et al.: How do patients perceive the tailored services that national TB programmes already provide? 1 While the Ghanaian study systematically explored patients’ expectations before the large-scale implementation of differentiated services, similar evidence from India remains relatively limited. 1 Most programme evaluations have focused on treatment outcomes, notification rates, or operational performance, whereas patient-reported experience measures, satisfaction with available services, perceived accessibility, acceptability, and unmet supportive care needs have received comparatively less attention. Yet these dimensions are fundamental to determining whether a programme is genuinely patient-centered. 4

Patient-centered TB care should therefore be assessed not only by the availability of interventions but also by whether these interventions effectively address the priorities identified by patients. For example, nutritional assistance is valuable only if it is delivered in a timely and adequate manner; community support programmes are meaningful only if patients perceive them as accessible, respectful, and beneficial; and digital platforms achieve their intended purpose only when they facilitate rather than complicate patient care. Incorporating routine patient feedback into programme monitoring could help identify implementation gaps that may not be apparent through conventional performance indicators alone. 5

The findings of Idan et al. also offer an opportunity for cross-country learning among high-burden nations. 1 Comparative research evaluating patient perceptions of tailored TB services across different national programmes could identify common priorities as well as context-specific challenges. Such evidence would help refine differentiated service delivery models while ensuring that patient-centered policies are translated into meaningful patient experiences. Furthermore, Developing standardized patient-reported experience measures for TB services could provide an important complement to conventional programme indicators and help evaluate whether differentiated service delivery truly meets patients’ needs.

In conclusion, the study by Idan et al. provides valuable evidence that patients’ perspectives should inform the design of tailored TB services. 1 Building upon this work, future research should evaluate not only the implementation of patient-centered interventions but also how patients experience, value, and benefit from these services. Integrating patient-reported experience measures into routine programme evaluation may represent the next important step toward achieving truly individualized TB care.
