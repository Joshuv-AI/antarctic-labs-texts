Peripheral macrophages and T-cells accumulate in the degenerating optic tract after repetitive head impact
Authors: Bevan S. Main, Sonia Villapol, Ruchelle G. Buenaventura, Alex C. Harvey, Maia Parsadanian, Asamoah Bosomtwi, James D. O’Leary, Dhwani C. Gondalia, Abby I. St Jean, Omar I. Sbaih, Bernard J. Dardzinski, Marcelo Febo, Alexandru Korotcov, Mark P. Burns
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic white matter inflammation is a feature of traumatic brain injury (TBI), consistently observed in rodent models of repetitive mild TBI and persistnat in human TBI patients for years post-injury. We use a high-frequency head impact model (HFHI, 5 impacts per day, over 6 consecutive days) to investigate neuroimmune responses in the optic tract, a white matter region particularly vulnerable to injury-induced degeneration in mouse repeat mild TBI models. We integrated immunohistochemistry, digital spatial proteomics, transcriptomic profiling, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) assessments with non-invasive imaging modalities, including diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and functional MRI (fMRI) to capture a comprehensive view of pathology.HFHI resulted in a sustained inflammatory response within the optic tract, marked by elevated IBA1+ and CD68+ expression that persisted for at least 3 m post-injury. Characterization of these IBA1+ cells revealed that they were F4/80+, indicative of infiltrating peripheral macrophages. Concurrent with this, transient BBB disruption was observed at the optic nerve, potentially facilitating acute peripheral immune cell entry. We identified CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the optic tract, and digital spatial proteomic signatures revealed Granzyme B and CTLA4 expression, indicative of both regulatory and cytotoxic immune activity. Transcriptomic analyses show polarization of T-cells toward CD4+ Th1 and CD8+ Tc1 subsets, as evidenced by increased expression of T-bet and IFNγ. Chronic elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNFα and IL-1β, complement component C3, and the chemoattractants Ccl2, Ccl5, Cxcl3, and Cxcl10 further suggest that synergistic interactions between innate and peripheral inflammatory cascades are occuring and contribute to white matter degeneration after repetitive head impact. DTI and fMRI reveal reductions in fractional anisotropy and disrupted optic tract–visual cortex connectivity, indicating functional consequences of this tract-specific immune activation.Importantly, DTI and fMRI provide sensitive, translational readouts of this neuroimmune pathology, with potential relevance to traumatic optic neuropathy and visual dysfunction in clinical populations. These findings support the potential of targeted immune modulation as a therapeutic approach to target white matter injury.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of long-term neurological disability worldwide, affecting millions of individuals each year. Currently there are no pharmacological treatments for TBI, with the heterogeneity of injury still considered a significant barrier toward the translation of effective therapeutics (Mokbel et al., 2024). Across the spectrum of TBI, the interplay of primary impact and acceleration-deceleration forces can lead to stretching/shearing, torsion, or compression of white matter tracts. Although white matter pathology has been comparatively less studied than grey matter in the preclinical neurotrauma field, it is recognized as a key neurodegenerative feature of clinical TBI, persisting for months to years after injury (Kinnunen, 2011; Johnson et al., 2013; Newcombe, 2022; Lippa, 2023; Tate, 2024; Flusund, 2024).

In addition to primary impact-related injury, neuroinflammation may act synergistically to drive progressive degeneration of white matter, particularly within the corpus callosum and adjacent parasagittal cortex (Johnson, 2013). This inflammatory response can be long-lasting; positron emission tomography (PET) imaging using the microglial activation tracer [11C]PK11195 reveals widespread white matter inflammation lasting up to 17 years post-injury (Ramlackhansingh, 2011). These inflammatory processes may contribute to axonal injury, as they occur alongside elevated levels of degeneration-associated biomarkers including neurofilament light chain (NFL) and UCH-L1 (Papa, 2010; Gao, 2020). White matter damage can also be detected through non-invasive imaging techniques, with diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) revealing persistent microstructural degradation, particularly in critical white matter tracts linked to cognitive function (Kinnunen, 2011; Kraus, 2007).

Recent advances in molecular techniques such as high throughput sequencing, digital spatial profiling and biomarker analyses have provided valuable insights and innovative approaches to investigate the synergistic role of inflammation in white matter pathology following TBI (Armstrong, 2024). Animal models have been central to this work, with numerous studies utilizing mild to severe TBI paradigms that exhibit white matter damage in both mice (Sullivan, 2013; Mierzwa, 2015; Herrera, 2017; Pischiutta, 2018; Ziogas and Koliatsos, 2018; Marion, 2018; Wendel, 2018; Bradshaw, 2021) and rats (Glushakova et al., 2014; Song, 2022). Although these studies primarily focus on corpus callosum pathology after severe TBI, the contribution of chronic inflammation to degeneration in other white matter regions, such as the optic tract, remains incomplete.

Neuroinflammation has previously been observed in models of traumatic optic neuropathy (Costello, 2014; Bernardo-Colon, 2019; Chen, 2022) and optic nerve crush (Guttenplan, 2020; Mazumder et al., 2023; Wang, 2024). Additionally, the optic tract appears especially vulnerable to damage from repeat head impacts (Chen, 2017; Sakthivel, 2023; Qiu, 2022). Despite these findings, key questions remain regarding the temporal dynamics and synergistic role of white matter inflammation in repetitive head impact models. Specifically, does this inflammatory response resolve spontaneously or persist chronically, and can we identify viable molecular targets for intervention specific to this optic tract pathology?

Given the clinical burden of visual impairment within the TBI community (Blanch et al., 2024), our HFHI model offers a platform to investigate optic tract specific molecular mechanisms in the context of repetitive mild head impacts. It enables the study of chronic inflammatory processes in these vulnerable regions and supports the development of targeted strategies to mitigate ongoing damage, with the ultimate goal of improving quality of life for individuals experiencing persistent symptoms following repetitive brain injury.

In this study, we aim to investigate the inflammatory processes driving optic tract damage following HFHI, previously shown to occur independently of amyloid and tau pathology (Winston, 2016; Sloley, 2021). We demonstrate a sustained immune response marked by transient BBB disruption, peripheral macrophage and T cell infiltration, and chronic cytokine and complement activation.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type C57Bl/6 male mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME) and were 3–4 months of age at the time of injury. Experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, precluding assessment of sex as a biological variable. Mice were housed 5 per cage under 12:12 h light:dark cycles with food and water available ad libitum, and maintained at a temperature of 22–24 °C and 40–60% humidity. All procedures were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Georgetown University Animal Care and Use Committee. Experiments adhered to guidelines from the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services.

High Frequency Head Impact (HFHI) model

Mice were anesthetized using 3% isoflurane in oxygen (1.5 L/min) for two minutes and subsequently positioned in a custom pneumatic impact device. Anesthesia was maintained via nosecone delivery of 3% isoflurane for an additional minute, totaling three minutes under anesthesia per session. Head impact was administered using a 10 mm diameter Teflon-tipped pneumatic impactor operating at a velocity of 2.35 m/s and a depth of 7.5 mm. The animal’s head remained unrestrained during impact, with a compliant gel pad positioned to absorb rebound energy and mitigate rotational acceleration.

Each mouse received five successive impacts per session. This protocol was repeated once daily for six consecutive days, resulting in a cumulative total of 30 impacts per animal. Sham animals underwent identical handling and anesthesia protocols without receiving head impacts. The procedure and parameters were adapted from previously established protocols (Winston, 2016; Main, 2017; Korthas, 2022; Chapman, 2024; Chapman, 2021). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, with cage littermates comprising both HFHI and sham animals to minimize potential cage effects.

BBB integrity – Evans blue

To assess BBB permeability following injury, Evans Blue dye was administered intraperitoneally (200 μL of 2% w/v in saline; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) two hours prior to euthanasia, as described previously (Main, 2018). Mice were then transcardially perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove intravascular dye. Brains were rapidly extracted, and the cortex and thalamus/striatum were microdissected from both ipsilateral and contralateral hemi-spheres. Tissue samples were weighed and placed into ice-cold 50% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) prepared in distilled water, then homogenized via sonication. Homogenates were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min to precipitate proteins, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, and fluorescence was measured using a spectrophotometer (excitation: 540 nm, emission: 680 nm). Fluorescence intensity values were normalized to tissue weight, and Evans Blue concentration in each sample was quantified against a standard curve of known Evans Blue concentrations.

Optic nerve isolation

Mice were perfused with ice-cold PBS and brain removed as previously described (Buenaventura, 2022). Optic nerves were isolated as per the established protocol for nerve and chiasm dissection (Pozyuchenko, 2020). Briefly, after gentle brain removal from its posterior pole, incisions were made on the optic nerves where it emerged from the optic canal and where the nerve entered the brain. Tissue was flash frozen and stored at −20C.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI), and Image Processing

MRI, fMRI and DTI experiments were performed on a 7 Tesla Bruker Biospec 70/20 small animal MRI system (Bruker Biospin, Billerica, MA) operating on ParaVision 6.0.1. A 86 mm Bruker birdcage radiofrequency (RF) transmit volume coil was used in conjunction with an actively decoupled 4-channel RF receive array coil for signal receiving. Animals were anesthetized with a mixture of isoflurane and medical air, and their physiological status (respiration rate and rectal temperature) was continuously monitored throughout the imaging session using an SA Instruments monitoring system (SA Instruments, Stony Brook, NY).

MRI and DTI acquisition

To evaluate potential structural alterations and edema, multi-echo T2-weighted fast spin-echo (SE) images were acquired using a Rapid Acquisition with Relaxation Enhancement (RARE) sequence. The following parameters were employed: repetition time (TR) = 4000 ms, echo times (TE) = 10, 30, 50, 70, 90, and 110 ms, RARE factor = 2, field of view (FOV) = 14.4 × 12.8 mm, 25 slices with 0.6 mm thickness, voxel resolution of 100 × 100 × 600 μm, number of averages (NA) = 4, total scan time = 17 min. T2 maps were subsequently calculated in-house using a nonlinear fitting algorithm implemented in MATLAB (Math-Works Inc, Natick, MA). A bias field correction was applied to all echo time images to mitigate intensity inhomogeneities. The T2 maps and the T2-weighted images at TE = 30 ms were used as inputs to a deep learning-based brain extraction algorithm (Roy et al. 2018) to generate initial brain masks, which were then manually refined as necessary. To assess microstructural white matter integrity and potential neuroinflammation, diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were acquired using a 2D echo-planar imaging (EPI) pulse sequence. The sequence parameters were: TR = 3000 ms, TE = 26 ms, NA = 2, 30 non-collinear diffusion-encoding directions, gradient pulse duration (δ) = 5 ms, gradient separation (Δ) = 12 ms, b-value = 1000 s/mm2, two EPI segments with forward and reverse phase encoding, FOV = 14.4 × 12.8 mm, 25 slices with 0.6 mm thickness, voxel resolution = 200 × 200 × 600 μm, NA = 2, total scan time = 17 min. To assess alterations in functional connectivity following injury, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were acquired. Rs-fMRI scans were performed using a single shot gradient-echo EPI (GE-EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 1200 ms, TE = 10 ms, FOV = 19.8 × 12.6 mm, 25 slices with 0.6 mm thickness, voxel resolution = 300 × 300 × 600 μm, 300 repetitions, resulting in a total scan time of 6 min.

Image processing and template creation

A study-specific template was constructed from the masked T2-weighted images (TE = 30 ms) acquired at Day 1 post-injury using the antsMultivariateTemplateConstruction2.sh script (Avants, 2010). Individual images were then nonlinearly registered to this template using the ANTs software package (Avants, 2008), employing registration parameters optimized following the approach detailed by (Anderson, 2019). The calculated transformations were applied to the T2 maps using cubic B-spline interpolation.

Diffusion tensor images were processed using the TORTOISE v3.2.0 software (Pierpaoli, et al., 2010; Irfanoglu et al., 2017). This involved motion correction, eddy current correction, and EPI distortion correction using the DIFFPREP module. Diffusion tensors were estimated using a nonlinear tensor fitting procedure with the RESTORE algorithm, and fractional anisotropy (FA) maps were subsequently calculated. A study-specific FA template was created using the same procedure as described above for the T2-weighted images and co-registered to the T2W study template. Individual FA images were then nonlinearly registered to this FA template.

FA statistical analysis

Voxel-wise statistical analysis of FA images in the template space was performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK). A flexible factorial design was implemented, incorporating factors for group, time, and subject (Gläscher and Gitelman, 2008). Contrasts were defined to examine between-group differences at each time point. For cluster identification, an uncorrected p-value threshold of 0.001 was used, in conjunction with a minimum cluster extent of 200 voxels (FA maps were resampled to 100 μm and smoothed using Gaussian filter). A combined optic tract region of interest (ROI) was derived from statistically significant clusters exhibiting reduced FA values in the repetitive head impact group, as identified by the SPM analysis. FA values for all subjects and time points were extracted from this data-driven ROI, considering only voxels with positive FA values. This SPM-derived ROI primarily encompassed the optic tract, as visualized in Panel A of the MRI/Fig. 6.

Functional connectivity network analysis

Resting state processing was carried out as previously reported (Febo, 2024) using Analysis of Functional NeuroImages (AFNI) (Cox, 1996), FMRIB Software Library (FSL; (Jenkinson, 2002) and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) (Klein, 2009). Binary masks for anatomical and functional scans were created using the 3dAutomask script in AFNI and were used for brain extraction prior to co-registration steps. Times series spikes were removed (3dDespike, AFNI), image repetition frames aligned to the first time series volume (3dvolreg, AFNI), and detrended (high pass filter < 0.009 Hz using AFNI 3dTproject). Independent component analysis (ICA) using Multivariate Exploratory Optimized Decomposition into Independent Components (FSL MELODIC version 3.15) was used to assess structured ‘noise’ or artefact components in each subject scan, in their native space. ICA components showing artefact signal voxels along brain edges, in ventricles, and large vessel regions were used in a soft (‘non-aggressive’) regression approach, as implemented in FMRIB Software Library (FSL 6.0.3) using fsl_regfilt (Jenkinson, 2002). A low-pass filter (>0.12 Hz) and spatial smoothing (0.4 mm FWHM) was next applied to the fMRI scans prior to registration steps. Post-regression ICA was carried out to verify removal of artefact components and preliminary assessment of putative ICA networks in individual scans.

Functional image volumes were temporally averaged to create a mean image that was brain-extracted, bias-field corrected, and used to generate a multi-subject template for subsequent fMRI analyses. We used the ANTs antsMultivariateTemplateConstruction2.sh script to generate a linear co-registration template of 24 mouse fMRI scans and then a final nonlinearly warped template. The linear registration step used the following parameters: gradient step size = 0.1, shrink factors = 4 x 2 x 1, smoothing factors = 2 x 1 x 0 vox, matrix iterations = 30 x 20 x 4, iteration limit = 3, a cross correlation similarity metric, and rigid body followed by affine registration. The nonlinear registration step used similar parameters but with an iteration limit = 4 and a Greedy Symmetric Normalization (SyN) transformation model. Individual co-registration matrices were used to transform each subject’s fMRI scan to template space. All whole brain statistical analyses were carried out in template space at the fMRI scan resolution. The multisubject template and statistical maps were registered to the Duke CIVM mouse atlas (kindly provided by Dr. G. Allan Johnson) for 3D map visualizations.

Networks: Matrices were analyzed using brain connectivity toolbox as previously described (Febo, 2024). We focused our analysis on node-based network measures for a fixed graph density of 15% and included assessments of node strength, node clustering coefficients and node efficiency for weighted undirected 1242 matrices with Fisher’s z converted partial correlation coefficients (rho). As indicated above, statistical analysis was conducted with FSL permutation based randomise tool and GraphPad prism using a two-factor analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) test.

Seed based connectivity analysis: A priori ROIs were defined for the primary visual cortex and superior colliculus using CIVM atlas parcel-lations. Binary masks were generated and mean time series were extracted for each seed. Whole-brain voxel wise correlation maps were created for each subject and Fisher z-transformed. Statistical inference was carried out using PALM (Permutation Analysis of Linear Models, FSL; https://web.mit.edu/fsl_v5.0.10/fsl/doc/wiki/PALM.html), which supports ANOVA-style contrasts using permutation testing. The following contrasts were tested: TBI d1 Reduced – regions with reduced connectivity at 24 h; TBI d1 Increased – regions with increased connectivity at 24 h; TBI × Day Increased d7 – sham increases vs TBI reductions, 7 d vs 24 h; TBI × Day Reduced d7 – sham reductions vs TBI increases, 7 d vs 24 h; TBI × Day Increased d28 – sham increases vs TBI reductions, 28 d vs 24 h; TBI × Day Reduced d28 – sham reductions vs TBI increases, 28 d vs 24 h. Voxelwise p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR, q < 0.05). Post hoc Tukey’s tests were conducted following a confirmatory two-way ANOVA (TBI × Day) in GraphPad Prism. Results are presented as mean ± SEM.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-QPCR)

RNA was then extracted from optic nerve tissue using TRIzol® re-agent (15596026, Invitrogen), as described in detail previously (Buenaventura, 2022). Concentration and purity of the RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (ThermoScientific). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (4368814, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:3 with diethylpyrocarbonate (DEPC) treated H2O and stored at −20 °C for future use. RT-QPCR was performed in triplicate in standard 384-well plates (4309849, Applied Biosystems) using the Prism 7900HT fast sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems). Sybr primers used are outlined in table 1. Taqman probes used included Aif1 (Mm00479862_g1), CD68 (Mm03047343_m1), Gfap (Mm01253033_m1), P2ry12 (Mm01950543_s1), Adgre1 (Mm00802529_m1), Ki67 (Mm01278617_m1), C3 (Mm01232779_m1), Il-1β (Mm00434228_m1), Tnfα (Mm00443258_m1), Tgfβ (Mm01178820_m1) and Arg1 (Mm00475988_m1). RT-QPCR was performed under the following cycle conditions; 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 20 s, (95 °C for 1 s, 60 °C for 20 s) with 40 cycles. SDS 2.4 (Applied Biosystems) was used to generate threshold cycle (Ct) values, and fold change in mRNA expression calculated using the ΔΔCt method as previously described (Romariz, 2023).

NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Protein (DSP) profiling

Brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and coronally sectioned (5 μm) before DSP staining and assay procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, to identify regions of interest (ROI), nuclei were fluorescently labeled using GeoMx Nuclear Stain Morphology kit, (121300303, Nanostring, Seattle, WA), and microglia labeled with IBA1 antibody (Wako, 019–19741, 1:1000). The regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on visual identification of optic tract anatomic regions via the nuclear stain, alongside the identification of IBA1 expression. Within these ROI, the mouse GeoMX Immune Cell Profiling Panel (121300106, Nanostring, Seattle, WA), was used for multiplexing and quantification of 23 individual proteins. Samples were processed on the NanoString GeoMx system and the nCounter max system following manufacturer’s instructions. Data were analyzed using GeoMx DSP analysis suite as per manufacturer instructions.

Immunohistochemistry

Coronal brain sections (20 μm thick) were collected from paraformaldehyde-fixed tissue and processed for immunohistochemistry. Sections were first incubated in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide to quench endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by blocking for 30 min in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 3% normal goat serum and 0.25% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies were diluted in PBS with 0.25% Triton X-100 and 1% normal goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-IBA1 (Wako, #019–19741, 1:1000) or anti-CD68 (Serotec, #MCA1957T, 1:1000). The next day, sections were incubated with species-appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse; 1:2000, Vector Laboratories, #BA-100), followed by the VECTASTAIN Elite ABC kit (1:400, Vector Laboratories, #PK-6100). Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-dia-minobenzidine (DAB; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Quantification of immunolabeling was performed using standardized, unbiased sampling of the optic tract, as previously described (Villapol et al., 2017). For each animal, five single-plane sections spanning the optic tract were analyzed. IBA1+ and CD68+ cells were quantified and expressed as mean cell number per mm2. For proportional area analysis, the extent of immunoreactive signal was calculated as the percentage of total target area occupied by stained cellular profiles. Images were processed in ImageJ using inversion and grayscale thresholding. Background-subtracted thresholds were applied uniformly across samples, and the “Analyze Particles” function was used to compute total immunoreactive area. Data are reported as the percentage of IBA1+ or CD68+ staining per region of interest (Villapol, 2015; Villapol et al., 2014).

Immunofluorescence

Free-floating brain sections (20 μm) were washed three times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual fixative, then incubated in blocking solution containing 5% normal goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBST) for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with combinations of the following primary antibodies diluted in PBST: polyclonal rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:500), polyclonal rabbit anti-P2RY12 (1:1000), polyclonal rat anti-F4/80 (1:200), or polyclonal rat anti-CD3 (Invitrogen, #14-0032-81, 1:200). Following primary incubation, sections were rinsed three times in PBS and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor dyes: anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 or anti-rat Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000; Invitrogen, #S11227). After secondary labeling, sections were rinsed in PBS and counterstained with DAPI (1:50,000 in PBS) to visualize nuclei. Slides were washed in distilled water and mounted onto Superfrost Plus microscope slides (Fisher Scientific, #22–037-246), then coverslipped using Fluoro-Gel with Tris buffer (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA). Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DP72 digital camera and CellSens Standard imaging software (Olympus Corporation). IBA1 and CD3 co-stained slices were mounted using DAPI containing media (VECTASHIELD, #H-1200–10) and images acquired using a Carl Zeiss Microscopy Axio Imager.Z2 Motorized Fluorescence Microscope (Zeiss Microscopy). IBA1 + and CD3 + cells were quantified and expressed as total area or pixels per μm2 of OT area.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.3). Where feasible all outcome assessments and data analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Comparisons between two experimental groups were assessed using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For experiments involving multiple groups, ANOVA was applied, followed by Bonferroni post hoc correction where appropriate. For Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) data, quality control and probe quantification were conducted using the GeoMx DSP analysis suite. ROI-level normalization was evaluated by examining inter-region correlation of housekeeping probes (GAPDH, IgG, and IgG2b), under the assumption that reliable normalizers should remain stable across ROIs and be independent of biological variation. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all comparisons.

Results

HFHI causes an IBA1 and CD68 positive inflammatory response in the optic tract that continues to develop for months after the final impact

Neuroinflammation is a hallmark feature observed across both moderate-to-severe single-impact and repetitive TBI models, with the optic nerve and tracts appearing particularly vulnerable in the context of repeated injury. We have previously identified localized, optic tract-specific inflammation in both our low-frequency head impact model-which does not produce cognitive deficits (Winston, 2016), and our high-frequency head impact (HFHI) model, which is associated with cognitive impairment (Sloley, 2021). To further explore the temporal series of inflammation within the optic tract, we used immunohistochemistry to probe for microglia/macrophage populations. We find HFHI mice display sustained IBA1 and CD68 positive immunoreactivity throughout the optic tract at all timepoints (both acute and chronic) compared to sham mice (Fig. 1 A-C). These cells contained morphologically hypertrophic/bushy microglia with pseudopodia, with the highest percentage of changes occurring at 1 m post injury (Fig. 1D).

F4/80 positive cells infiltrate the optic nerve and tract, influencing neuroinflammatory responses after HFHI

To investigate the broader composition of innate and peripheral immune cells in the optic nerve, mice underwent HFHI or sham procedures before their optic nerves were analyzed for a combination of cell and membrane specific markers. Optic nerve mRNA analysis at 24hrs showed elevated Aif1 (24hr: 1.07 ± 0.12 vs 5.93 ± 1.06, P = 0.0005, t = 4.318, df = 17) and Cd68 (24hr: 1.22 ± 0.32 vs 21.12 ± 5.26, P = 0.0023, t = 3.577, df = 17) in HFHI mice compared to sham. Similar to IHC analysis, this response was sustained chronically, with increased Aif1 (Aif1 – 1 m: 1.06 ± 0.11 vs 2.91 ± 0.36, P = 0.0001, t = 4.819, df = 18) and Cd68 (1 m: 1.08 ± 0.15 vs 6.93 ± 0.99, P < 0.0001, t = 5.79, df = 18) observed in the optic nerves of HFHI mice at 1-month post injury (Fig. 2A-B). The presence of astrocyte mRNA was detected at 24hrs with increased Gfap (24hr: 1.17 ± 0.20 vs 2.07 ± 0.21, P = 0.0078, t = 3.017, df = 17), however this was unchanged at the later 1-month timepoint (Fig. 2C). To further discern microglia from infiltrating myeloid cells, we used the P2ry12 marker (expressed in microglial processes) and Adgre1 (encodes F4/80) which has been characterized as being highly expressed on macrophages. At 24hrs post-injury, HFHI mice show increases in both P2yr12 (24hr: 1.07 ± 0.22 vs 5.59 ± 1.44, P = 0.010, t = 2.883, df = 17) and Adgre1 (24hr: 1.18 ± 0.23 vs 5.60 ± 1.43, P = 0.0103, t = 2.88, df = 17), suggesting a combination of microglia and macrophages are present within the optic nerve acutely (Fig. 2D-E). At 1-month P2yr12 is unchanged, however Adgre1 remains elevated (1 m: 1.13 ± 0.16 vs 2.02 ± 0.28, P = 0.013, t = 2.754, df = 18), signifying the chronic presence of macrophage cell types (Fig. 2E). To investigate proliferation, Ki67 mRNA analysis was performed. Interestingly, HFHI mice display elevated Ki67 expression at 24hrs post-injury (24hr: 1.13 ± 0.69 vs 1.98 ± 0.27, P = 0.022, t = 2.517, df = 17), a response that is not observed chronically at 1-month compared to sham mice (Fig. 2F). Immunofluorescence localized F4/80 positive staining with Iba1 positive cells in the optic tract of HFHI mice at 3-months post injury (Fig. 2G), further demonstrating the chronic presence F4/80 + macrophages within the optic tract. P2RY12 microglial-specific staining was primarily observed outside of optic tract structures (Fig. 2H).

Digital spatial proteomics shows macrophage and lymphocyte signatures in the optic tract after HFHI

To generate a high-resolution proteomic profile following HFHI, we utilized NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) technology. Regions of interest (ROIs) were identified by IBA1 positive staining across multiple sections containing optic tracts in both Sham and HFHI animals (Fig. 3A). Principal component analysis of immune-related proteins revealed distinct clustering driven by injury (HFHI) and post-injury timepoints (24 h and 1 month) (Fig. 3B). This separation corresponded with significant alterations in inflammatory protein expression, including upregulation of 9 proteins at 24 h and 12 proteins at 1-month post-HFHI (Fig. 3C-D). CD11b (1-month: 88.57 ± 9.82 vs 632.28 ± 112.2, P = 0.0007) and MHC-II (Sham: 2.14 ± 0.43 vs 1-month 7.30 ± 1.04, P = 0.0014) were increased compared to sham (Fig. 3E). Additional upregulated proteins included the peripheral pan immune marker CD45 (1-month: 22.70 ± 2.05 vs 137.8 ± 32.06, P = 0.0048), the macrophage marker F4/80 (1-month: 11.90 ± 7.19 vs 48.39 ± 9.45, P = 0.0032) and proliferation marker Ki67 (24hr: 6.68 ± 1.00 vs 16.75 ± 0.72, P = 0.0007. 1-month: 6.68 ± 1.00 vs 17.94 ± 1.72, P = 0.0003) (Fig. 3F). Proteins associated with lymphocytes including the B cell marker CD19 (1-month: 2.06 ± 0.48 vs 9.29 ± 1.40, P = 0.0007) and CD11c (1-month: 10.26 ± 0.68 vs 97.88 ± 15.89, P = 0.0002) significantly increased compared to sham (Fig. 3F). Hierarchical clustering heatmaps revealed all these dynamic changes across all profiled proteins, with injury status and post-HFHI timepoint emerging as the primary factors driving group separation (Fig. 3G).

T-cells infiltrate the optic tract following HFHI, contributing to localized neuroimmune activity

To further investigate adaptive immune responses, mice underwent HFHI or sham procedures before brains were analyzed by immunofluorescence to visually confirm the presence of T-cells in the optic tract. At 1-month post-HFHI, we observed increased CD3+ cell expression within the optic tract (Fig. 4A), frequently localized in proximity to activated IBA1+ cells. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in IBA1 immunoreactivity at 1-month post-HFHI compared with sham controls (Sham: 43.77 ± 14.70 vs 1-month: 367.60 ± 75.56; P = 0.0136), alongside a significant increase in CD3+ staining (Sham: 0 vs 1-month: 173.60 ± 27.76; P = 0.033), consistent with T-cell infiltration (Fig. 4B). To further characterize T-cell subtypes, proteomic and transcriptomic approaches were taken. Digital spatial proteomics showed increased CD4 (24hr: 2.29 ± 0.38 vs 5.12 ± 1.04, P = 0.049. 1-month: 2.29 ± 0.38 vs 5.91 ± 0.56, P = 0.015) and CD8a (1-month: 14.87 ± 1.24 vs 29.24 ± 5.80, P = 0.040), alongside markers of CD8 T-cell activation GZMB (1-month: 112.1 ± 5.13 vs 242.5 ± 43.76, P = 0.024) and the regulatory protein CTLA4 (1-month: 11.21 ± 2.01 vs 32.27 ± 3.75, P = 0.0043) (Fig. 4C).

RT-qPCR was used to conduct transcriptomic profiling of 19 markers and transcription factors that delineate distinct T-cell populations (Fig. 4D-F). The most prominent signatures of CD4 subtypes contained genes involved in the Th1 response, particularly Tbx21 (T-bet) (Sham: 1.13 ± 0.20 vs 1-month 4.18 ± 1.43, P = 0.047), IFNγ (Sham: 1.06 ± 0.19 vs 1-month 3.80 ± 1.22, P = 0.042), Cxcl3 (Sham: 1.056 ± 0.16 vs 1-month 24.27 ± 4.09, P = 0.0002), and Cxcl10 (Sham: 1.27 ± 0.40 vs 1-month 5.81 ± 2.11, P = 0.048), which were all elevated 1-month post-HFHI compared to Sham (Fig. 4D). Markers associated with Th2, Th17 or Treg cells were unchanged. T-bet and IFNγ are also associated with CD8 T-cells (Fig. 4E), suggesting the optic nerves and tracts may contain a combination of both Th1 and Tc1 T cells. T-cell chemoattractants were also elevated including Ccl2 (Sham: 1.12 ± 0.23 vs 1-month 3.49 ± 1.11, P = 0.048) and Ccl5 (Sham: 1.08 ± 0.20 vs 1-month 3.99 ± 1.18, P = 0.028) compared to sham (Fig. 4F).

HFHI increases proinflammatory cytokine, chemokine and complement expression in the optic nerve

Given both innate and adaptive immune cells bi-directionally facilitate the secretion of cytokines and chemokines, we sought to investigate the transcriptional inflammatory profile of immune cells that are present within the optic nerve after HFHI. mRNA analysis shows the complement chemokine C3 is elevated in HFHI mice at both 24hrs (24hr: 1.18 ± 0.22 vs 4.12 ± 0.748, P = 0.0023, t = 3.587, df = 17) and 1-month (1 m: 1.11 ± 0.15 vs 7.74 ± 0.98, P < 0.0001, t = 6.698, df = 18) post-injury (Fig. 5A). Assessment of pro-inflammatory cytokines revealed an increase in Il-1β acutely (24hr: 1.60 ± 0.46 vs 9.64 ± 1.59, P = 0.0002, t = 4.643, df = 17), (Fig. 5B) alongside TNFα which is increased at both 24 h (24hr: 1.14 ± 0.16 vs 9.18 ± 1.68, P = 0.0003, t = 4.507, df = 17) and 1-month (1 m: 1.48 ± 0.37 vs 2.09 ± 0.28, P = 0.0006, t = 4.126, df = 18) compared to sham mice (Fig. 5C). Alongside pro-inflammatory cytokines, we analyzed profiles of Tgif (TGFβ) and Arg1 for alternatively activated immunoregulatory cells. After HFHI, we observed an increase in Arg1 at 24hrs (24hr: 1.32 ± 0.29 vs 21.75 ± 6.50, P = 0.0085, t = 2.974, df = 17) (Fig. 5D), and increases in Tgif at both 24hrs (24hr: 1.22 ± 0.32 vs 4.46 ± 0.67, P = 0.0006, t = 4.218, df = 17) and 1-month (1 m: 1.06 ± 0.11 vs 1.60 ± 0.21, P = 0.043, t = 2.178, df = 18) post injury compared to sham mice (Fig. 5E).

HFHI causes acute changes in BBB permeability, specific to the optic nerve white matter

Our results suggest the infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the optic nerve and tract after injury. We therefore sought to investigate if BBB integrity in the optic nerve is compromised following repetitive head impacts. To do this, wild-type mice were subject to HFHI and barrier integrity was assessed by Evans Blue (EB) analysis. Levels of EB extravasation were increased by 199% in the optic nerve of HFHI mice at 2hrs post-injury (116.9 ± 27.43 vs 316.2 ± 90.64, P = 0.039, t = 2.615, df = 15) compared to sham animals. This was not maintained chronically, with no change observed between sham and HFHI at 1-m post injury in the optic nerve (Fig. 6A-D). This suggests that this permeability is transient and spontaneously resolves over time without re-injury.

Optic tract fractional anisotropy declines over time following HFHI

To investigate the effects of mild TBI on white matter integrity and neuroinflammation, multi-echo T2-weighted MRI and DTI were performed at 1d, 7d, and 30d post-injury. No overt structural damage or edema was evident on either conventional T2-weighted images or quantitative T2 maps (not shown). Initial voxel-wise FA analyses were conducted using SPM. The SPM analysis revealed significant clusters of reduced FA that coincided with the optic tract region of HFHI mice (yellow region in Fig. 7A). A data-driven ROI, encompassing the combined statistically significant clusters of reduced FA across all three time points, was defined as the optic tract ROI. This ROI was used to extract FA values for all groups and time points.

A two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of head impact [F(1,14) = 80.54, P < 0.0001], time [F(2,28) = 6.82, P = 0.0042], a significant main effect of time [F(2,28) = 6.93, P = 0.0036], and a significant head impact x time interaction [F(2,28) = 10.56, P = 0.0004] (Fig. 7B). Post-hoc analyses demonstrated that HFHI resulted in a significant decrease (range 11–19% decrease) in optic tract FA compared to sham at all three time points (p < 0.0001 for all three timepoints). Furthermore, within the HFHI group, a significant reduction in FA was observed between 24 h and Day 7 (P = 0.04), and Day 7 and 1-month post-injury (P = 0.047), with an overall decline of 9% in FA between 24 h and 1 m post-HFHI (P < 0.0001) – indicating a progressive decline in white matter integrity that continues after the head impacts have ceased. These findings provide compelling evidence that repetitive head impacts induce sustained and progressive white matter disruption in the optic tract of mice, a region known to be vulnerable to axonal injury.

HFHI alters local functional connectivity in visual networks, but not whole brain connectivity

We first examined seed-based functional connectivity in the primary visual cortex and superior colliculus, two regions heavily innervated by retinal inputs and central to visual processing and eye motor responses. Group-averaged seed maps demonstrated widespread correlations with these regions in both sham and HFHI mice. Statistical comparisons revealed significant HFHI × timepoint interactions (Fig. 8A). Relative to shams, HFHI mice showed bidirectional alterations in functional connectivity, depending on the timepoint post-impact. At 1d post-injury, connectivity between the primary visual cortex and distributed cortical and subcortical regions was significantly enhanced (*p < 0.05 vs sham, Fig. 8B), as was connectivity between the superior colliculus and subcortical brain regions. By 7 d, connectivity between the visual cortex / superior colliculus and subcortical brain regions had significantly decreased (**p < 0.05 vs sham, Fig. 8B). At 1-month post-injury, connectivity returned to baseline levels. These data indicate that HFHI induces dynamic and region-specific remodeling of visual connectivity across the first month post-HFHI.

To assess whether these local changes reflected broader alterations in global network organization, we next analyzed whole-brain functional connectivity. Three-dimensional network visualizations revealed consistent patterns of node strength and connectivity across time in both sham and HFHI groups, with no evidence of large-scale reorganization (Fig. 8C-D). Heat map representations of pairwise correlations confirmed stable connectivity matrices across sessions (Fig. 8E), and weighted network metrics (network strength, path length, efficiency, clustering, modularity and assortativity) showed no significant group differences at a density threshold of 15% (Fig. 8F). Comparison of empirical networks with randomized surrogates further demonstrated that global topology was preserved following HFHI, with the networks exhibiting non-random small-world organization (Fig. 8G). Thus, while HFHI disrupted local connectivity in visual and superior colliculus networks, the overall structure of brain-wide functional connectivity remained intact over the 1 m post-HFHI period.

Discussion

In this study, we show that chronic white matter inflammation in the optic nerve and tract following repeated mild head impacts includes peripheral immune cell infiltration and resident immune cell activation. Utilizing the high-frequency head impact (HFHI) model, we identify an IBA1+ cellular response, which contained a limited contribution from resident microglial populations expressing P2RY12. Instead, we find a distinct population of peripheral F4/80+P2RY12− macrophages that infiltrate the CNS through mild, repetitive disruptions to the optic nerve BBB. This macrophage infiltration is accompanied by lymphocyte entry into the optic tract, with digital spatial profiling revealing a sustained CD45+, CD4+ and CD8+ cellular signature post-injury. Notably, these infiltrating macrophages and lymphocytes contribute to a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment localized specifically to the optic tract, with no evidence of immune activation in surrounding gray matter.

We show that peripheral immune cell entry into the optic tract is facilitated by acute BBB dysfunction following mechanical impact. Evans Blue extravasation reveals BBB permeability at 24 h post–HFHI, which resolves spontaneously, with no leakage observed at chronic timepoints (1-month). This is consistent with our previous findings in a severe TBI model, where the BBB similarly undergoes spontaneous repair (Main, 2018). We propose that daily transient disruptions of the BBB, localized to the optic nerve, permits immune cell infiltration, while its subsequent restoration limits their egress. These microvascular changes enable peripheral monocytes to enter and migrate along the optic tract, where they establish residence, proliferate, and contribute to the overall neuro-immune response. These infiltrating cells persist for weeks to months post-injury, maintaining their pro-inflammatory immunophenotype into the chronic phase.

The synergistic peripheral, adaptive and innate cellular responses observed in our study likely contribute to the localized functional deficits detected in the optic tract. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) reveals reduced fractional anisotropy (FA), consistent with axonal injury, while functional MRI (fMRI) demonstrates decreased connectivity with the visual cortex, implicating chronic tract-specific inflammation to a broader network dysfunction. These imaging patterns carry important translational relevance, as similar alterations have been reported in human TBI patients with traumatic optic neuropathy (TON). The optic nerve is notably vulnerable to repetitive head trauma, with TON occurring in up to 8.0% of TBI cases (Blanch et al., 2024; Ventura et al., 2014; Saliman et al., 2021). Furthermore, TBI significantly increases the risk of developing TON, with survivors facing a threefold higher risk compared to non-TBI controls (Chen, 2017). Collectively, our findings offer valuable insights for future longitudinal investigations of the optic tract, with the potential to identify chronically activated adaptive immune biomarkers as targets for therapeutic intervention.

Head impacts are known to damage white matter tracts through a range of mechanical forces that vary with impact location and the physical characteristics of the applied force. Despite the breadth of brain injury models developed to capture this mechanical heterogeneity, a central question remains unanswered: what drives the continued degeneration of white matter long after the initial trauma? Previous studies have sought to address this, with optic tract damage reported in preclinical TBI models following both single impact paradigms (Koliatsos, 2011; Mohan, 2013; Haber, 2017; Evanson, 2018) and repetitive injury models (Sakthivel, 2023; Qiu, 2022; Namjoshi, 2014; Tzekov, 2016; Xu, 2016; Bolton Hall, 2016; Briggs et al., 2016; Velayudhan, 2022; Gold, 2018). These studies have primarily utilized morphological approaches including silver staining, Fluoro-Jade labeling, and immunolabeling for pathogenic proteins such as APP, to identify white matter pathology. In our own work, we have similarly observed optic tract injury following low-frequency head impact, indicated by increased silver and Fluoro-Jade labeling (Winston, 2016). Yet, the initiating factors, mechanisms driving progression, and functional consequences of this damage, particularly in relation to secondary injury pathways, remain poorly defined. While dysregulated or sustained neuroimmune responses are well-established contributors to the pathophysiology of moderate to severe TBI (Mokbel et al., 2024), the specific nature of neuroimmune crosstalk between central and peripheral immune compartments within the optic tract, an area especially vulnerable to repetitive mild head trauma, has yet to be fully characterized.

Here, we show that HFHI leads to a sustained elevation of IBA1, F4/80 and CD68 protein expression specifically within the optic nerve and tract, beginning as early as 24 h post-injury and increasing through 3 months. This is in line with previous works that show macrophage infiltration plays a synergistic inflammatory role in grey matter after blast-induced TBI (Murugan, 2020). Following blast, macrophage infiltration was observed from 4hrs post injury, persisting up to 1-month and mediated in part by CCL2-CCR2 signaling and by BBB disruption (Murugan, 2020). This macrophage infiltration has also been observed in CCI models (Villapol et al., 2017). Similarly, we detect F4/80+ macrophages at all time points, both at the mRNA and protein levels. Although markers of innate immune activation are present, our data indicate this response is transient with Gfap and P2ry12 expression are elevated only at the mRNA during the acute phase and return to sham levels by 1-month post-HFHI.

In addition to F4/80+ macrophages, small numbers of circulating lymphocytes infiltrate the brain and have consistently been recognized as key contributors to inflammation following moderate/severe neurotrauma (Xu, 2021; Gelderblom, 2009). Notably, recruitment and polarization of CD4 + T-helper cells within the injured brain have been reported (Braun, 2017; Fee, 2003), and the post-injury balance of T-helper cell subsets is critical in shaping the inflammatory response. Specifically, pro-inflammatory TH1 and TH17 subsets have been shown to exacerbate neuroinflammation whereas regulatory T-cells (Treg) may exert anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects by limiting immune activation (Xu, 2021; Guerriero, 2019). Indeed, after TBI, pan T-cell deletion, modulation of CD4 + Cd11b + cells or altering the Th17/Treg balance has been shown to be beneficial, attenuating neuroinflammation and improving outcomes in mice (Ayerra, 2025; Wang, 2025; Jiang, 2024). Interestingly, we observed increased CD4 + protein expression in the optic tract following HFHI, suggesting the presence of a T-helper cell response. In addition, key markers of Th1 CD4 T-cells including T-bet, IFNγ were observed suggesting that these cells alongside cytotoxic CD8 + Tc1 lymphocytes may contribute to the holistic inflammatory response in the optic nerve and tract. To our knowledge, this represents the first evidence of an optic tract–specific T cell response in a mild, repetitive head impact model.

Together with CD4 expression, our proteomic analysis revealed a prominent CD8 + T-cell signature within the optic tract, further supported by elevated levels of Granzyme B (GZMB), a key CD8 + effector molecule, and CTLA4, a negative regulator of T-cell activation. This is particularly notable given prior evidence that GZMB+ CD8+ T-cells infiltrate the brain following severe TBI, where they contribute to myelin damage and neurological dysfunction (Daglas, 2019). Temporal data also suggest that CD4+ Th17-like responses can precede and amplify CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity by promoting GZMB expression (Daglas, 2019). In our study, CD4 + protein expression was detected at 24 h post-injury in the optic tract, preceding the emergence of CD8 + and GZMB-positive signatures. Mechanistically, infiltrating CD8 + have been shown to infiltrate the brain as early as 24 h (promoted by astrocyte-derived IL-15), and mediate neuronal apoptosis via GZMB and caspase-3 activation in severe TBI (Wu, 2021). Although apoptosis markers were not assessed in this study, these inflammatory pathways may contribute to secondary injury and align with our previous findings of neuronal degeneration in the optic tract, as indicated by increased silver and Fluoro-Jade staining after HFHI (Winston, 2016).

To explore how peripheral immune cells infiltrate the optic tract post-injury, we assessed BBB permeability using Evans Blue. We observed transient BBB disruption acutely, which resolved over time, consistent with findings in other repetitive mild injury models (Qiu, 2022; Smith, 2018; Smith, 2016). These results suggest that early interventions aimed at stabilizing BBB integrity may help limit immune cell infiltration. This permeability coincided with elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, a cytokine known to reduce tight junction proteins such as claudin and occludin, contributing to endothelial barrier compromise (Bodnar, 2021). We also observe evidence for the chronic activation of the complement cascade, with C3 elevated both acute and chronically. The functionality of this complement activation and whether opsonization of white matter synapses by macrophages/ microglia for phagocytic removal occurs in the optic tract is worthy of further investigation. Indeed, complement cascades have been shown to propagate morphological changes in the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus after TBI (Borucki et al., 2024), coinciding with deficits in visual system pathology, an avenue worthy of future exploration. Future work will expand beyond the BBB to explore the role of meningeal interfaces as potential entry points and immunological niches for neuro-immune interactions in the optic nerve. The meninges surrounding the optic nerve sheath may be particularly susceptible to mechanical forces from primary injury (Brinker, 1997) (Rossinelli, 2023). Though underexplored, one study has reported meningeal enhancement as early as 24 h following repetitive CHIMERA injury (McNamara, 2022), suggesting that this barrier and the unique neuro-immune environment within it, may contribute to optic tract specific adaptive immune responses (Fernandez Blanco, 2022) (Pisa, 2022).

DTI and fMRI reveal that optic tract inflammation after HFHI is not a transient or subclinical event, but instead produces measurable structural and functional disruption that evolves over time. Other groups have used DTI to track axonal injury after TBI, including in repetitive mTBI models, where they observe disrupted white matter pathways in multiple brain regions (Bennett et al., 2012; Criado-Marrero, 2024). In the present study, we used voxel wise analysis to identify a single region with aberrant FA signal. This region was localized to the optic tract, and we observe a progressive decline in FA localized to the optic tract, beginning acutely and worsening over a one-month period, consistent with ongoing axonal degeneration in the absence of additional impacts. The progressive decline in FA mirrors the progressive inflammation and immune activation that we identified histologically, and suggests that early cellular infiltration sets in motion a degenerative process that continues independent of ongoing trauma. fMRI further reveals continuous, longitudinal changes in connectivity occurring in brain areas connected to the optic nerve and tract. Our data is suggestive of an increased visual pathway activity at 1d post-HFHI that might be due to aberrant firing of the optic pathways due to the initial damage induced by injury. By 7d post-HFHI, the connectivity in the visual cortex and superior colliculus is significantly reduced compared to sham mice, which we interpret as reduced optic pathway function after injury. However, at 1 m post-HFHI there appears to be a stabilization of the visual pathway networks back to sham levels. Given that changes to local visual connectivity are not accompanied by changes to global network organization, which remain stable after HFHI, we interpret these data to be suggestive of visual cortex synaptic plasticity rather than global changes in neurovascular coupling caused by HFHI. Our data is also reinforced by other fMRI connectivity research in mild TBI. Sakthivel et al. report widespread, brain-wide disruptions in functional connectivity in CHIMERA mice, with a transient course and apparent recovery over time – however it is noted that the CHIMERA model produces widespread pathological changes and is considered more severe compared to our HFHI model (Sakthivel, 2023). Together, these imaging results support the concept that white matter injury after repetitive head trauma is both persistent and functionally significant, and they highlight non-invasive imaging as a translationally relevant tool for tracking immune-driven degeneration in TBI.

Diffusion tensor imaging and functional MRI reveal that optic tract inflammation after HFHI is not transient nor benign, but instead leads to measurable, progressive disruptions in white matter structure and network connectivity. We observed a significant and worsening decline in fractional anisotropy within the optic tract over time, beginning acutely and continuing through at least 1 m post-injury, despite the cessation of impacts. This sustained reduction in FA is consistent with prior human studies demonstrating chronic white matter microstructural damage after TBI (Kinnunen, 2011; Johnson, 2013) and supports the view that axonal degeneration is not self-limiting. Importantly, our results extend these findings by localizing the damage to the optic tract, a region that has received comparatively less attention in the context of repetitive mild TBI despite its known vulnerability to shear forces and its association with traumatic optic neuropathy (Chen, 2017; Qiu, 2022; Blanch et al., 2024). Functional MRI analysis revealed reduced connectivity with the primary visual cortex, indicating that immune-mediated structural damage translates into broader network dysfunction. These findings echo human connectomic data linking TBI-related white matter injury to impaired functional integration across brain regions (Flusund, 2024), and demonstrate that immune responses targeting a single tract can have cascading effects on distributed neural systems. Together, these imaging results validate histological and molecular evidence of chronic immune activation, and underscore the translational potential of non-invasive imaging to detect and monitor tract-specific white matter degeneration after repetitive head trauma.

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. Experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, precluding assessment of sex as a biological variable. Given that sex has been shown to influence neuroimmune responses after brain injury in mice (Villapol et al., 2017), we are restricted in our ability to determine whether the observed effects in this study are sex-specific or broadly applicable. Future studies including female mice are needed to determine whether these inflammatory mechanisms in white matter tracts are conserved or sexually dimorphic. In addition, although P2RY12 is widely considered microglia-specific and is not expressed by circulating monocytes, its expression is dynamically regulated in a state-dependent manner under pathological conditions (Boche and Gordon, 2022). As a result, reduced P2RY12 expression may reflect activated microglial states that were not fully captured by evaluating P2RY12 or F4/80 expression independently. Finally, our identification of macrophages and lymphocytes within the optic tract reflects associative and potentially synergistic contributions to the broader neuroinflammatory response rather than definitive causal relationships. Targeted depletion or lineage-specific manipulation of macrophage and adaptive immune cell populations will be required in future studies to determine their relative causal contributions to chronic white matter inflammation.

In conclusion, our findings identify a sustained and compartmentalized immune response within the optic nerve and tract that emerges early in the injury cycle and persists long after the initial mechanical insult has resolved. Alongside microglia, infiltrating peripheral macrophages and lymphocytes dominate this response, establishing a chronic, pro-inflammatory microenvironment that aligns with progressive white matter degeneration and functional disruption. Despite spontaneous repair of the BBB, the immune cells that gain early access to the optic nerve and tract remain entrenched, perpetuating a pathological cascade that does not resolve with time. Critically, this study provides the first evidence of a coordinated cytotoxic and regulatory T-cell and B-cell signature within the optic tract following repetitive mild TBI, implicating adaptive immune mechanisms in the failure to resolve white matter inflammation. This work adds to a growing body of evidence that repetitive head impacts can initiate a delayed but enduring immune response that selectively targets vulnerable white matter regions, and that this response-once established-is self-sustaining in the absence of continued injury. Critically, these data challenge the notion of spontaneous recovery in mild repeat TBI and underscore the need to therapeutically intervene at the level of immune modulation to halt ongoing degeneration and restore tract function.
