Bridging Precision and Practice: A Systematic Approach to Total Body Irradiation in Routine Clinical Settings
Authors: Karthikeyan Nithiyanantham, Sambasivaselli Raju, Saurabha Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the feasibility of delivering total body irradiation (TBI) in a standard linear accelerator bunker using a treatment planning system (TPS) and a conventional multileaf collimator assembly. The aim was to address dose homogeneity and lung shielding within practical clinical constraints.Materials and Methods:TBI was performed using conventional anterior–posterior beam delivery, dividing the whole-body irradiation into three to four sections with patients positioned on the treatment couch. Whole-body computed tomography images, acquired with Vac-Loc immobilization, were used for planning in the TPS. The source-to-surface distance was maintained between 120 and 135 cm. The moving-junction technique provided uniformity at section junctions, and the multileaf collimator (MLC) was employed for customized lung shielding. Patient setup and shield positioning were verified with an electronic portal imaging device.Results:The plan resulted in ≥ 92% of the target volume received ≥ 90% of the prescribed dose while V110 remained <7%. The mean lung dose was kept below 8 Gy. The prescribed dose rate at the mid-plane was approximately 1.8 Gy/min. Point dose measurements showed agreement with the TPS calculations within 2%, indicating high dosimetric accuracy and reliable dose delivery.Conclusions:Conventional linac setups, combined with TPS-guided planning, MLC-based lung shielding, and moving-junction delivery approaches, provide a practical solution for TBI with acceptable dose uniformity and lung sparing, supporting implementation in standard radiation oncology practice.

INTRODUCTION

The rising use of hematopoietic stem cell transplant in the setting of hematologic malignancies such as leukemia, lymphoma, and myeloma has led to an increased global demand for total body irradiation (TBI) techniques. TBI is a well-established component of conditioning regimens before hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.[123] However, due to the complexity of delivering a uniform prescription dose to the entire body, TBI is not routinely practiced in every radiation oncology facility. Numerous studies have explored various methods of TBI planning and delivery, each with its respective advantages and limitations.[45]

Among the diverse techniques, the extended source-to-surface distance (SSD) setup remains the most widely practiced approach. This method requires treatment bunkers larger than standard facilities, along with multiple accessories to accommodate TBI delivery. Ensuring accurate critical organ shielding at these extended SSDs presents significant challenges. To address these limitations, some centers have adopted volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) for on-couch TBI delivery. While VMAT offers precision, it requires 7–8 isocenters, high monitor units (MUs), prolonged treatment times, and respiratory motion management.[67891011]

In contrast, the conventional anterior-posterior/posterior-anterior (AP-PA) forward planning approach permits most radiation oncology facilities to deliver TBI without requiring additional equipment, costly software licenses, or custom bunker modifications. This method has been employed over extended periods using standard Linac infrastructure, accompanied by essential accessory setups. Nevertheless, challenges remain in delivering a uniform dose throughout the patient’s body on the treatment couch, notably in optimizing critical organ sparing, managing junctional doses, patient positioning, and addressing support system limitations.[1213]

The AP-PA beam technique for TBI delivery is well established, and our approach aligns with this methodology. The integration of treatment planning systems (TPSs) and multileaf collimators (MLCs) has enhanced TBI planning and delivery accuracy and confidence. Recent studies have demonstrated good agreement in dose calculation accuracy at extended SSDs across various commercially available TPS platforms while engaging it for TBI planning.[14151617]

We developed a forward-planned TBI technique dividing the whole-body volume into three or four contiguous sections based on patient height. Junctional doses were managed using the feathering technique, and lung shielding was accomplished with MLCs. The accuracy of MLC-based lung shielding was verified with electronic portal imaging devices (EPIDs). This modified approach has been successfully implemented in over 120 patients over the past decade, with clinical outcomes correlating well with standard response expectations. Our approach demonstrates that with thoughtful planning and resource optimization, effective TBI delivery is achievable even in facilities with limited infrastructure.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Prescription and simulation

Patients aged 2–43 years with acute myeloid leukemia and acute lymphoblastic leukemia were treated using the described TBI technique. Dose prescriptions ranged from 2 to 12 Gy. For the low-dose regimen protocol, TBI was administered either as a single 2 Gy fraction or as 4 Gy delivered bi-daily in two fractions with 2 Gy per fraction without applying any shielding. Table 1 shows the detailed patient cohort stratification. In the need of a high-dose regimen, the prescription involved a total dose of 12 Gy, delivered in 6 fractions over 3 days with two fractions per day spaced 6 h apart by following the COG ASCT0431 PBMTC ONC051 protocol.[18] According to this protocol, the high-dose regimen demands a mean lung dose of 7–8 Gy. To achieve the mean dose, the lung volume received open beam irradiation for the first 3 fractions, followed by beam delivery with lung shielding for the remaining 3 fractions.

The planning target volume (PTV) encompassed the entire body with a 2 mm clip margin from the skin surface. Treatment was delivered using 4 MV or 6 MV photon beams from an Elekta Synergy linear accelerator equipped with an Agility MLC system. Photon energy selection was based on patient age and anteroposterior body separation. Patients were immobilized using a full-body vacuum cushion (Vac-Lok) system, and CT simulation was performed in the head-first supine position with a slice thickness of 5 mm.

For patients shorter than 175 cm, the entire body length could be scanned. In taller patients, the excess length at the feet could be excluded from scanning, but needs to be recorded to ensure appropriate planning margins in the most inferior treatment section. Precise patient positioning was ensured using skin and Vac-Lok markings aligned with laser systems during simulation, facilitating reproducible setups.

The CT datasets were contoured for the whole body, skin, PTV, lungs, heart, kidneys, and other relevant organs. Lung shielding was applied bilaterally with a 1 cm margin contraction to maintain dose coverage of the ribs during respiratory motion. Fractionation, hyper-fractionation, and organ shielding have been shown to improve the therapeutic ratio of TBI while reducing toxic effects such as pneumonitis and decreasing treatment-related mortality.[192021] Various lung shielding techniques employed by centers aim to maintain median lung doses between 8 and 10 Gy to mitigate pneumonitis risk.[22]

Planning method

TBI was planned using an AP parallel opposed beam configuration, with the body segmented into multiple contiguous sections. The patient’s total body length was divided into three or four sections designated as Sections A, B, C, and D based on patient height, as illustrated in Figure 1. Section A extends from the vertex of the skull, including a 2 cm superior margin, down to the diaphragm inferiorly. Subsequent fields for Sections B, C, and D were arranged so that the beam divergence intersected at the patient’s mid-plane, ensuring uniform dose distribution.

Schematic diagram showing the different irradiation sections and the junction regions between adjacent fields in the total body irradiation (TBI) treatment plan

Each section was planned using the SSD technique, with SSDs ranging between 120 and 135 cm. Section A incorporated both open and shaped beams, where the shaped beams employed MLCs for shielding the 1 cm contracted volume of lung, as shown in Figure 2. To compensate for the thoracic regions shielded during lung blocking, additional positive beams targeting the right and left chest walls were planned separately. Dose homogeneity was further optimized by creating field-in-field segments tailored to the patient’s anatomy, and observed dose heterogeneity, as illustrated in Figure 2. Sections B, C, and D were treated using open beams supplemented with appropriate segments to minimize hotspots. For Sections C and D, which encompass the lower limbs, the beam isocenter was generally positioned near the mid-lower legs, with careful contour adjustments to accommodate this placement and ensure accurate dose delivery.

Junctional dose management

The junctional dose between adjacent sections was managed using the moving junction technique, also known as the feathering technique. Each field was composed of five equally weighted segments with variable jaw settings to minimize dose heterogeneity at the junctional regions. The positions of the X1 and X2 jaws are detailed in Figure 3. The feathering effect distributes the dose over an 8 cm length, with average shifts of 2 cm, resulting in the junctional dose being delivered at five different levels across fractions. This systematic field border shifting reduces dose heterogeneity at the junctions by approximately a factor of five. Therefore, the dose feathering was effectively achieved through this field-in-field technique. Because dose heterogeneity at the junctions is addressed by moving segments in each fraction, the need to create multiple distinct treatment plans is eliminated.

Plan implementation

All planning processes in the TPS were performed with the patient in the head-first supine orientation. During treatment delivery, patients were positioned head-first supine for Sections A and B, and feet-first supine for Sections C and D due to longitudinal movement limitations of the treatment couch. It is important to adjust the collimator settings appropriately after repositioning the patient on the couch for Sections C and D. The Vac-Lok immobilization system and patient skin markings were used to ensure reproducible positioning throughout the treatment course.

Following the established protocol, the first three fractions (fractions 1-3) of Section A were delivered without lung shielding, while the subsequent three fractions (fractions 4-6) utilized lung shielding through the MLC system. Due to lung shielding, parts of the thorax and arms were inevitably shielded; these areas were subsequently treated separately using subfields for the right and left sides, excluding the lung volume. Sections B, C, and D were treated with the same set of beams for the entire treatment course.

Lung shielding with MLC in Section A was verified by comparing EPID images to digitally reconstructed radiographs from the TPS, as illustrated in Figure 4. Compared to other extended SSD TBI delivery methods, this approach builds confidence in accurately reproducing lung blocks using MLCs and enables precise verification. The anterior beam entry position for each section was localized using longitudinal shift values treated with the same set of beams for the entire treatment course.

RESULTS

Over the past decade, numerous patients have been treated using this computed tomography (CT)-based three-dimensional (3D) field-in-field TBI technique. Early treatment plans were generated using the Elekta CMS XiO TPS, with subsequent plans utilizing the Monaco TPS following the introduction of its 3D planning module. Dose calculations were performed using the Superposition algorithm in XiO and the Collapsed Cone algorithm in Monaco, respectively. As a planning approach, it was observed that the 90% of the PTV was generally covered with more than 92% of the prescribed dose, while maintaining mean lung doses between 7.5 and 8 Gy. Patient-specific lung shielding using MLCs within conventional fields allowed optimal control of lung dose as well as prescription dose coverage. However, the use of conventional MLC lung shielding with AP/PA beams over three fractions impacted heart dose coverage, with mean heart doses ranging from 9.2 to 10.1 Gy. The volume receiving doses exceeding 110% of the prescription dose (V110) was restricted to within 7% by incorporating a limited number of subfields with low MU weighting. Dosimetric verification was performed through phantom studies to validate the TPS-calculated doses. A 30 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm polymethyl methacrylate phantom was used for dose measurement. The phantom was positioned at a treatment SSD of 130 cm, with the detector placed at a depth of 10 cm. The dose measurement was performed by simulating the treatment field on the phantom. The maximum scatter contribution was accounted for using this phantom, although the treatment field size was larger than the phantom dimensions. The observed difference between calculated and measured point doses was within 2%, and off-axis dose verification at 5 cm, 10 cm, and 20 cm distances from the treatment field central axis remained within 2% of the TPS values. The total MUs required to deliver the entire plan, consisting of four sections, ranged from 1550 to 1720 MUs. The average treatment time, inclusive of patient setup and image verification, was approximately 30–40 min.

DISCUSSION

Globally, the method of delivering TBI varies significantly. Some centers treat patients in a standing position, while others use a lying-down position at an extended SSD. Depending on the delivery method, patients are treated with single or multiple pairs of opposing fields at extended SSD. In the case of isocentric techniques or rotational treatments, multiple isocenters are used to cover the entire body in five or more sections.

To manage heterogeneity effects, some clinics use compensators or rely on inherent patient positioning methods. Treatment planning also varies, ranging from conventional manual planning to CT image-based 3D planning using field-in-field techniques or intensity-modulated beams. Many authors have stated that CT-based 3D planning with intensity-modulated radiation therapy and inverse planning is more effective in improving dose uniformity and organ sparing.[2627]

These intensity-modulated beam delivery methods, such as VMAT, are commonly used with the standard isocentric approach. However, delivering TBI with inverse planning can raise concerns due to uncertainties in long treatment volumes and junctional doses, complexity in image verification, challenges in implementation, intricacies in dose rate evaluation, and dosimetric verification.

Especially, the lung sparing process with modulated beam can be more impactful in thoracic region dose uniformity and chest wall dose delivery accuracy when compared to conventional techniques, while no motion management system is available. Nevertheless, inverse planning methods have drawbacks, including long planning times due to extensive optimization, complex dose calculations, and prolonged delivery durations.[28]

The technique presented in this study emphasizes improving delivery accuracy, patient comfort, and feasibility in terms of infrastructure and workflow within a busy radiotherapy department. In addition, validation of the TPS modeling at extended SSD during commissioning of the TBI program ensured the accuracy of dose calculations. The AP delivery method is advantageous, as AP body thickness varies less along the patient’s length, typically between 20 and 25 cm, compared to lateral body separations that can reach up to 50 cm in adults. This results in improved lung sparing when using MLC and enhances patient reproducibility.[2029] During the initial phase of implementing this technique, treatment planning was quite timeconsuming, often requiring 6–8 h. With continued handson practice and a deeper understanding of the underlying concepts, we have significantly reduced the planning time. At present, the process can be completed in the TPS within 45–60 min.

Skin dose management is particularly critical in patients with Graft-Versus-Host Disease, where adequate skin dosing is essential. For better dose coverage in superficial regions, an appropriate energy level can be selected. It is noteworthy that while delivering the prescribed dose to the whole body, it is important to enhance energy deposition near the skin surface.[30] Unlike segmental delivery in intensity-modulated beams, the standard couch-based TBI treatment method delivers a relatively higher skin dose due to broader beam coverage.[31]

One of the most common toxic effects of a single large-dose TBI (8–10 Gy) is radiation-induced interstitial pneumonitis, which occurs in approximately 50% of cases and is fatal in half of those cases.[32] Our method recommends using the lowest possible dose rate during treatment with open beams (unshielded lung) for the initial treatment section to mitigate such toxicities. Although a controlled dose rate is generally recommended for TBI delivery to manage lung toxicities such as pneumonitis, no clear evidence or implications regarding the effect of dose rate have been observed.

For broader clinical implementation, TBI techniques should prioritize simplicity, clarity, accuracy, and reasonable cost and workload, characteristics that our described method aims to fulfil. Detailed clinical factors associated with this type of TBI technique have not been discussed, as this paper focuses primarily on technical implementation.

Limitations of this technique include variability in patient anatomy, such as height and weight, and the complexity of organ shielding required. The feasibility of applying this technique depends on the specifications of the individual treatment unit, which will define its applicability to patients with diverse body dimensions.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates that TBI delivered using a CT-based parallel opposed field-in-field planning technique at an extended SSD on a couch is a practical and effective approach that can be implemented in virtually all standard linac facilities. This technique eliminates the need for additional specialized equipment, separate licensing, or custom-built treatment bunkers, thereby reducing infrastructural and financial barriers to broader clinical adoption. Over more than a decade of clinical use, this method has yielded consistent and satisfactory treatment outcomes without any reported TBI-related complications with this technique. These findings underscore the technique’s potential to enhance accessibility and efficiency of TBI delivery, making it a viable option for busy radiotherapy centers aiming to balance accuracy, safety, and resource management. Future studies exploring long-term clinical outcomes and broader patient populations would further validate its effectiveness and facilitate optimization.
