Pre-infusion risk factors predict severe infectious complications of CAR T-cell therapy in pediatric and adult patients with B-ALL
Authors: August A. Culbert, Flavia Gava, Yannis K. Valtis, Toshihisa Satta, Surabhi Vora, Joseph M. Rocco, Veronique Nussenblatt, Sara K Silbert, Haneen Shalabi, Bonnie Yates, Jae H. Park, Adam J. Lamble, Kai Rejeski, Nirali N. Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the curative potential for chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells in B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), efficacy can be limited by life-threatening adverse events such as severe infections. As immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity and secondary immunodeficiency may be particularly profound in B-ALL, understanding pre-infusion risk of severe infection is imperative. The ALL-HEMATOTOX (ALL-HT) score is a recently validated tool designed to predict CAR-associated hematotoxicity in B-ALL, but the relationship between ALL-HT and severe infections post-infusion has not yet been comprehensively assessed.MethodsIn this multicenter, retrospective analysis, we evaluated ALL-HT and other pre-infusion variables for an association with severe infection through day+60 (D+60) in patients with B-ALL treated with CD19 and CD22-based CAR T-cell constructs across three institutions. Infections were identified by microbiology, histopathology, or as a clinical syndrome and graded by CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) V.5.0. Severe infections were defined as grade≥3. Multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazard models were constructed to identify independent risk factors for infection.ResultsAcross 350 patients receiving CAR T-cells, 79 (23%) developed a severe infection within day+60 post-CAR, including 8 patients with grade 4 (life-threatening) infection and 5 patients with grade 5 (fatal) infections. Bloodstream infections were the most common, comprising 67% of those with severe infections. In multivariable analysis, pre-infusion factors associated with severe infection included older age (OR 1.35 (1.1–1.6), p=0.002), prior severe infection (OR 2.1 (1.2–3.7), p=0.009), and a higher ALL-HT score (OR 1.15 (1.01–1.31), p=0.04). Patients classified as high-risk (HR) by ALL-HT had a greater risk of infection compared with low-risk patients (HR 1.4 (1.1–2.7), p=0.014).Multiple severe infections occurred in 24 patients (7%). In a subanalysis, ALL-HT risk of infection was primarily driven by baseline thrombocytopenia, with a cut-off of≤50 000 platelets/µL strongly predicting risk of infection (HR 2.2 (1.3–3.6), p=0.002). Post-infusion infection risk was driven by a longer duration of neutropenia (OR 1.26 (1.1–1.4), p<0.001).ConclusionsOlder age, prior infection history, baseline thrombocytopenia, and higher ALL-HT scores are strong independent risk factors for severe infection among patients with B-ALL receiving CAR T-cells. These factors may guide individualized risk mitigation to prevent severe infections in this high-risk patient population.Trial registration numberNCT03827343.

Introduction

Infections are a leading cause of non-relapse morbidity and mortality in patients treated with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy.14 Previous studies have identified several post-infusion associations with infection in patients receiving CAR T-cells, such as prolonged steroid use, hypogammaglobulinemia, and severity of cytokine release syndrome (CRS).2 5 6 More recently, the association between cytopenias, particularly prolonged neutropenia, and infection has become more well-established.58 However, as most studies have evaluated post-CAR infusion variables as risk factors for infection, the ability to risk-stratify patients to mitigate infectious complications is limited.

Accordingly, the CAR-HEMATOTOX (CAR-HT) score, a model based on pre-infusion variables developed to predict post-CAR cytopenias,9 was also effective in predicting risk of infection in adults with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, mantle cell lymphoma, and multiple myeloma.7 9 However, as most studies on post-CAR T-cell infection have focused on adult patients or composite cohorts of B-cell malignancies (ie, lymphoma, B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), and myeloma), it is unknown whether CAR-HT is useful in predicting infection in pediatric cohorts, specifically in B-ALL. Moreover, CAR-HT has limited discriminative power in B-ALL (most patients are categorized as high-risk). Whether the ALL-HEMATOTOX (ALL-HT) score, a modified CAR-HT model incorporating baseline bone marrow (BM) disease burden,10 is associated with infection warrants study.

Therefore, with a focus on identifying pre-infusion risk factors, we aimed to comprehensively characterize infections in a large, multicenter cohort of pediatric and adult patients with B-ALL treated with CAR T-cell therapy. We further aimed to assess the utility of ALL-HT and CAR-HT models in predicting infection risk in this unique patient cohort, with the goal of identifying biomarkers of infection that may help to tailor risk-mitigation strategies to prevent infections in patients receiving CAR T-cells.

Methods

Study design

This was an IRB-approved, multicenter, retrospective analysis (NCT03827343) of children, adolescent and adult patients with relapsed or refractory (r/r) B-ALL treated with CAR T-cell therapy across three institutions (National Cancer Institute (NCI), Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC), and Seattle Children’s Hospital (SCH)) between July 2012 and June 2024. Additional details and treatment years for each institution are provided in the online supplemental information. The primary objectives were to evaluate the efficacy of the ALL-HT and CAR-HT score in predicting both risk and severity of infection in patients with r/r B-ALL treated with CAR T-cell therapy, and to identify pre-infusion risk factors for severe infection. Exploratory objectives included evaluating post-infusion factors such as acute toxicities post-CAR, and use of anticytokines and their association with the incidence of severe infection.

Patient demographic information, clinical variables (eg, baseline disease burden, prior severe infections), data on post-CAR T-cell infections, antimicrobial use, and clinical course were obtained via internally maintained protocol databases and electronic medical records. Infectious complications were assessed from CAR-infusion (day 0) through day 60 (D+60) post-infusion.

CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were classified according to American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria.11 The degree of neutropenia was graded using immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT) guidelines, with patients ICAHT grade≥3 considered as severe.12

The CAR-HT and ALL-HT scores were determined for each patient using the methodology and variables previously described (ie, using hemoglobin, platelets, absolute neutrophil count (ANC), C-reactive protein (CRP), and ferritin or bone marrow disease percentage).9 10 Laboratory values were chosen from the first day of lymphodepletion (LD) or up to 3 days prior. This window was extended to within 7 days prior only for ferritin values, as several patients did not have a value within 3 days. Patients were excluded from analysis with ALL-HT and CAR-HT if there were missing laboratory values which precluded calculating either score. Patients were stratified into high-risk (HR) or low-risk (LR) groups for both CAR-HT and ALL-HT, based on a cut-off of≥2 for CAR-HT and≥4 for ALL-HT. For patients without CRP values in ALL-HT, the cut-off point was shifted to≥3.

Infections

An infection was defined as a distinct clinicopathological entity, either bacterial, viral, or fungal, based on microbiologic or histopathologic data or as a clinical syndrome of infection.13 14 The onset of infection was documented based on the date of diagnostic or clinical confirmation and subsequently categorized into four groups: viral, bacterial, fungal, and Clostridioides difficile. The severity of infection was graded on a 5-point scale based on Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) guidelines.15 16 A severe infection was defined as a CTCAE grade 3 infection or greater (online supplemental table 1). Patients meeting clinical, laboratory and/or imaging criteria for invasive fungal infections (IFI) were graded according to Infectious Diseases Society of America consensus guidelines.17

Prior severe infections occurring within 90 days of LD chemotherapy were identified via manual chart review by physicians at each institutional site. Infections were classified as severe if they met CTCAE V.5.0 criteria for grade>3.

For patients with multiple (>1) infections, the infection of the highest grade was selected as the representative infectious complication for analysis. Data for all infections (total number of infections) were summarized separately from individual patient infections. Details on infectious prophylaxis are described in the Supplemental Methods (online supplemental table 2).

Preventative measures

Patients who received prophylactic treatment against bacterial, viral, fungal, or pneumocystis infections prior to CAR T-cell infusion were included if prophylaxis started before LD chemotherapy, regardless of duration. The use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) was institution-dependent and typically guided by local protocols or patient clinical assessment. These approaches are detailed in online supplemental table 2.

Hypogammaglobulinemia was defined as IgG levels<400 mg/dL and was documented within 30 days post-CAR infusion. Immunoglobulin replacement was generally administered if IgG levels were less than 400 mg/dL.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographics and infections in R Studio (V.4.4.2), and GraphPad Prism (V.9.3.1). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact or Pearson’s χ2 tests, and continuous variables between two groups were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum or Mann-Whitney U tests. For all analysis, non-parametric tests with p values <0.05 were used to determine statistical significance. Univariate analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the predictive capacity of individual variables. Optimal thresholds for continuous predictors were determined using the Youden J statistic (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Multivariable logistic regression models for severe infection were constructed and optimized using stepwise regression for pre-infusion and post-infusion variables, and Cox proportional hazards models were developed to determine predictors of time of severe infection onset. Cumulative incidence curves were generated in Prism, with log-rank tests used to assess curve separation.

Results

Patient demographics

Among 369 patients infused with CAR T-cells, 350 patients with B-ALL met criteria for inclusion in the study and were analyzed (figure 1 and online supplemental figure 1). The median age was 17 years (IQR 9–25), and 229 (65%) were male (table 1). Patients received CAR T-cell products across three institutions: NCI (n=160, 46%), MSKCC (n=106, 30%), and SCH (n=84, 24%) (online supplemental figure 1B). Median BM disease burden prior to CAR-T infusion was 17% (IQR 1–71%). Most patients were heavily pretreated, with 92% of patients having received two or more lines of therapy, 49% receiving prior allogenic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and 20% having received a prior CAR T-cell infusion. Most patients received a combination of fludarabine and cyclophosphamide-based lymphodepleting chemotherapy (n=255, 73%), followed by higher-dose cyclophosphamide as a single agent (n=83, 24%) at doses between 1.5–3 g/m2.18 The remaining patients (n=12, 3%) received LD regimen with either bendamustine, fludarabine plus cytarabine with filgrastim, or ifosfamide plus etoposide. The most common CAR T-cell product was a CD19-28z-based construct (47%), followed by CD19-BB (31%) and CD22-BB (22%). 99 patients (28%) had a prior severe (grade≥3) infection within 90 days of LD chemotherapy.

Characterization of the overall infection burden

Across 132 (38%) patients with at least one infection, a total of 200 distinct infections were identified. Collectively, this resulted in an average of 0.57 infections across all patients, and 1.51 infections per patient in those with infection. Across all 200 infections, bacterial infections (n=93 infections, 46%) were most common, followed by viral infections (n=71 infections, 36%) (online supplemental figure 2A and B). Out of all infections, 113 infections (57%) were classified as severe (grade≥3) and 87 were classified as mild (ie, Grade 1 or 2) (online supplemental figure 2A-C). Bloodstream infections were most common across the cohort (40%), followed by respiratory tract infections (33%) (figure 1B). In patients with severe infections, bloodstream infections comprised 67.3%, while respiratory infections accounted for 15.9% (figure 1C). Among bacterial infections, Enterococcus, Enterobacter and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus aureus were the most frequently identified pathogens (online supplemental figure 2D). Fungal infections accounted for 7% (n=14) of all infections, and 10.6% (n=12) of severe infections, with Aspergillus being the most common etiologic agent.

Characterization of per-patient infectious complications

A total of 132 patients (38%) developed an infection after CAR T-cell infusion (table 1). 49 of 132 patients (37%) had more than one infection (range 2–5). Across all patients, severe infection was seen in 79 patients (23%). This included eight patients with grade 4 (life-threatening) infections and five patients with grade 5 (fatal) infections (online supplemental table 3). Among the 79 patients with severe infection, 24 (30.3%) had more than one severe infection (range 2–5) (online supplemental table 4). Across all patients, bacterial infections were most common, accounting for 76% (n=60) of all severe infections (figure 1D). Most patients (n=109, 83%) developed infections within the first 30 days after CAR infusion (online supplemental table 5).

Regarding clinical outcomes after CAR T-cell infusion, hospitalization duration was longer in patients with severe infections compared with those without severe infection (median 27 vs 17 days, p<0.001). In addition, patients with severe infections had shorter 2-year overall survival (OS) compared with patients without infections (median OS (mOS) 12.4 vs 23.5 months, p=0.006) (figure 1E). Event-free survival (EFS) was also shorter among patients with severe infections (median EFS (mEFS) 3.8 vs 11.4 months, p=0.01) (figure 1F).

Details on infection type, etiology, and grade from Day 0 to Day 60 post-infusion are summarized in figure 2A, B and C for each institution. The cumulative incidence of any-grade and severe infection across cohorts was 40.1% and 24.3%, respectively (figure 2D and E). Most patients received viral (71%), fungal (71%), and pneumocystis jirovecci pneumonia (PJP) (98%) prophylaxis pre-CAR, while only 28% received antibacterial prophylaxis prior to CAR T-cell infusion (online supplemental figure 2E). All subsequent analyses are based on the most severe infection per patient.

Pre-CAR infusion factors associated with severe infections

Given the significant reduction in OS and EFS in patients with severe infection, we investigated pre-infusion risk factors associated with severe infection. Patients who developed severe infections (n=79) were older (median age 21 vs 16 years, p=0.003), had higher baseline BM disease burden (median 39% vs 9%, p=0.006), higher baseline ferritin values (2630 ng/mL vs 1339 ng/mL, p=0.003), and lower baseline platelet count (70,000 /µL vs 130,000 /µL, p<0.001) compared with patients without severe infection (table 2, figure 3A–G). When adjusting for patients with low BM disease burden (ie, <5%), patients with severe infections had worse 2-year OS (mOS 20.8 vs 50.6 months, p=0.0046); however, high disease burden patients had comparable OS (mOS 9.5 vs 13.4 months, p=0.22, online supplemental Figure 3). Notably, there was no observed difference in baseline absolute neutrophil count (ANC) (1.50 vs 1.23 k/uL, p=0.8) or in the use of antimicrobial prophylaxis among patients with severe infections. Patients with severe infection were also more likely to have received high-dose cyclophosphamide LD regimen (35% vs 17%, p<0.001).

Regarding medical history, patients with a prior severe infection within 90 days of LD chemo had a higher incidence of severe infections post-infusion (42% vs 24%, p=0.002, figure 3H). Specific details on prior severe infections are summarized in further detail in online supplemental table 6.

ALL-HT is a significant predictor of severe infection

Given the robust association between post-CAR neutropenia and infections,19 20 alongside baseline laboratory parameters, ALL-HT and CAR-HT risk scores were calculated for each patient as previously described by our team in Nair et al.10

Across 171 (49%) patients classified as HR by ALL-HT, rates of severe infection were higher than in the 179 (51%) LR patients (28% vs 17%, p=0.016), with most infections (86%) occurring within the first 30 days after CAR infusion (figure 3I). In contrast, CAR-HT did not show predictability of severe infection in our study in part due to 88% of patients being classified as CAR-HT HR (table 2, online supplemental figure 4). In addition, 25% of the cohort had an undetermined CAR-HT risk score due to missing data of CRP, ferritin, or both. When assessing cumulative incidence of severe infections, patients with HR ALL-HT were more likely to develop infection within the first 60 days post-CAR infusion (HR 1.74, 95% CI 1.1 to 2.7, p=0.012; figure 3J).

When evaluating individual components of ALL-HT and CAR-HT using ROC curve analysis, the strongest associations for baseline laboratory values which predicted severe infections were platelet count, BM disease burden, and ferritin (online supplemental figure 5). Given the variability in BM disease burden across the study cohort (median 17% blasts, IQR 1–71), we assessed the optimal cut-off for BM disease by optimizing the Youden J statistic. Stratifying patients by a cut-off of≥69% BM disease resulted in a greater risk of severe infection within 60 days post-CAR infusion (HR 2.8, 95% CI 1.6 to 4.7, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure 6). Stratifying by M3 marrow (≥25% marrow blasts) (HR 1.4, 95% CI 0.94 to 2.3, p=0.088) was less informative in this regard. To assess the impact of antibacterial prophylaxis, we evaluated cumulative incidence of severe infection by ALL-HT risk scores among patients receiving pre-CAR antibacterial prophylaxis versus none. Prophylaxis did not associate with incidence of infection in either patients with LR nor HR ALL-HT (online supplemental figure 7).

Multivariable analysis incorporating pre-infusion variables

Using multivariable logistic regression, two models for pre-infusion risk factors for severe infection were calculated. In the first model, variables independently associated with severe infection included older age (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.62, p=0.002; transformed for every 10-year increase), prior severe infection (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.20 to 3.69, p=0.009, and a higher ALL-HT score (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.31, p=0.040) (figure 3K, online supplemental table 7 and figure 8). A second multivariable model with platelet count replacing ALL-HT was determined to evaluate the independent effect of baseline thrombocytopenia on infection risk (figure 3L). In this model, the association between older age and prior severe infection was maintained (p=0.004 and p=0.016, respectively), while a lower baseline platelet count emerged as a strong predictor of severe infection (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.22, p=0.005) (online supplemental table 8 and figure 8).

When stratifying patients by high versus low-risk platelet count, a cut-off of≤50,000 cells/µL significantly predicted the outcome of severe infection (HR 2.2, 95% CI 1.3 to 3.6, p=0.002) (online supplemental figure 9). Similarly, stratifying patients by age≥18 years old or prior severe infection status significantly predicted severe infections (online supplemental figure 9). These associations were maintained in a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model evaluating time to severe infection (online supplemental table 9).

A total of 24 (7%) patients had multiple severe infections (MSI) (online supplemental table 10 and figure 4A). Most infections were bacterial (figure 4B and C) and occurred within 30 days post-CAR. Patients with MSI were also more likely to be classified as ALL-HT high risk (75% vs 45%, p=0.008) (figure 4I). The specific pathogens identified among patients with MSI are listed in online supplemental Table 4. Finally, patients with MSI had shorter 2-year overall survival compared with patients with no infection (mOS 8.5 vs 23.5 months, p<0.001) and compared with patients with a single severe infection (mOS 8.5 vs 16.8 months, p=0.02, figure 4J).

Indeed, a subanalysis comparing pediatric (<18-year-old, n=187) versus adult (≥18-year-old, n=163) patients illustrated that adults were more likely to develop severe infections than pediatric patients (29% vs 17%, p=0.009) (figure 5A).

Subanalysis of preventative measures

Patients receiving antibacterial prophylaxis (n=97, 27.7%) were younger (median age 13 vs 18, p=0.003) and had lower baseline ANC values (median 0.80 vs 1.5, p=0.008) compared with patients without prophylaxis. Rates of prior severe infections were comparable between groups (22% vs 31%, p=0.08) (online supplemental table 11 and 12). Notably, all grade 5 (fatal) infections (n=5, 100%) occurred in patients who did not receive prophylaxis; however, the proportion of patients with either grade 4 or 5 infections was comparable (2.1% vs 4.3%, p=0.5). Finally, C. difficile infection was more common among patients receiving prophylaxis (24% vs 8.1%, p=0.027).

A total of 249 patients (71%) received viral prophylaxis. Across 101 (29%) who were not using antiviral prophylaxis, 6 (5.9%) patients had herpes simplex virus (HSV)/varicella zoster virus (VZV) reactivation, of which two were considered severe (online supplemental Table 13).

IgG levels were quantified within 30 days post-CAR in 254 patients (72.5%), of whom 37 (10.5%) had levels<400 mg/dL. Rates of severe infection did not differ significantly between patients with and without hypogammaglobulinemia (18% vs 23%) (p=0.48). However, this analysis is limited by missing IgG data in 27.5% of the cohort (online supplemental Figure 11).

Post-CAR infusion factors associated with severe infections

To understand how post-infusion factors shape infection risk, post-infusion variables associated with severe infection were assessed (online supplemental Table 14). Briefly, patients with severe infections had higher rates of severe (grade≥3) CRS (28% vs 13%, p=0.002), neurotoxicity (48% vs 35%, p=0.030), and corticosteroid use (52% vs 34%, p=0.005). Anti-cytokine agents such as tocilizumab (51% vs 31%, p=0.001) were also used more frequently.

In the overall cohort, we observed a low rate (8.3%, n=29) of immune effector-cell associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-like syndrome (IEC-HS), which did not significantly differ across patients with severe infections versus none (p=0.11). In patients receiving CD-22-BB-based CAR products, IEC-HS associated with severe infections (67% vs 30%, p=0.008), consistent with prior literature and suggesting an increased infection risk due to heightened hematotoxicity and/or use of immunosuppressive agents.

Additionally, the relationship between post-CAR neutropenia and infections was explored. In a multivariable model including post-infusion variables such as severe CRS, neurotoxicity, and steroid use, the duration of severe neutropenia (days ANC≤500 µL) was the only variable independently associated with severe infection (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.44, p<0.001 figure 5G and online supplemental Table 15). Indeed, patients with severe infections had a longer median duration of severe neutropenia (22 vs 12 days, p<0.001, figure 5G. When stratified by ICAHT risk status, patients with severe ICAHT (grade≥3) post-CAR were more likely to develop severe infection (30% vs 15%, p=0.002, figure 5H–5I).

Lastly, we assessed infection outcomes stratified by CAR T-cell efficacy and clinical response. Across 281 patients achieving a complete response (CR), 63 (22%) had severe infection. This compares to 16 (25%) patients with severe infection in those without CR (n=65, p=0.7).

Discussion

In this multicenter, retrospective analysis of 350 patients with r/r B-ALL who received CAR T-cells, we comprehensively characterized infectious complications and evaluated pre-infusion predictors of severe infection, specifically the clinical utility of ALL-HT and CAR-HT models. While prior studies have provided valuable insight into risk factors for infections post-infusion, these findings are limited by disease heterogeneity, variability in the key outcome, and limited numbers of pediatric patients. Specifically, studies have previously identified that a greater number of prior therapies21 and higher CD19 CAR T-cell dose are associated with infection16; however, these studies are limited by a single institution design and contained primarily adult patients, with limited numbers of patients with B-ALL. Additionally, while post-infusion associations with infection such as severe CRS,5 ICANS grade≥2,2 and steroid use21 inform infection risk, this offers little opportunity for pre-emptive intervention. Lastly, the prognostic utility of cytokine measurements to predict and distinguish infection from CRS2224 is hampered by limited ability to assess cytokines in real-time.

Our study addresses these limitations by providing a focused analysis of pre-infusion variables associated with severe infection in a large, multicenter cohort of pediatric and adult patients with B-ALL. Collectively, we observed an overall infection rate of 38%, with severe infections occurring in 23% of patients, consistent with prior reports.20 23 Patients with severe infection post-CAR had significantly worse 2-year OS and EFS compared with patients without infections, reinforcing the importance of identifying pre-infusion risk factors to develop toxicity mitigation approaches. Key predictors of severe infection included older age, cyclophosphamide-only LD chemotherapy, prior severe infection, and a higher ALL-HT risk score. Based on these findings, we developed two unique multivariable models incorporating easily available baseline parameters.

In the first model, we found that a higher ALL-HT score is strongly associated with an increased risk of severe infection. As previously described, ALL-HT is a validated, clinically relevant model for post-CAR hematotoxicity using readily available patient laboratory values, including BM disease burden, baseline cytopenias, and markers of inflammation.10 In combination with older age and prior severe infection, our finding that ALL-HT associates with severe infection suggests that patients at higher risk for hematotoxicity are also at higher risk for severe infection. Indeed, patients classified as high-risk by ALL-HT were significantly more likely to develop infection within 60 days compared with low-risk patients. In contrast, CAR-HT demonstrated poor predictive ability in our cohort, largely due to a high proportion (88%) of patients being classified as high-risk. When evaluating the components of ALL-HT associated with severe infection, baseline thrombocytopenia had the strongest magnitude of association, and a cut-off of≤50,000/uL significantly predicted the risk of severe infection post-CAR.

As the platelet count may be a surrogate marker for baseline marrow reserve or inflammation and is more readily available than a BM result and in a timely manner, it may be easier to incorporate than the full ALL-HT score.25 The second multivariable model therefore evaluated the role of baseline platelet counts in lieu of the full ALL-HT score in predicting severe infection. In this second model, a reduction in platelet count was strongly associated with an increased risk of infection. Due to significant variability in BM involvement in our cohort, lower cutoffs (≥5% or ≥25% blasts) were not informative for infection risk. Notably, a very high BM involvement (≥69%) was significantly associated with infection risk. Collectively, these findings reinforce the important role of age, prior infection, and bone marrow reserve on post-CAR T-cell infection risk.

With regard to the association between age and infection risk, the odds of severe infection increased by approximately 35% for every 10-year increase in age in the multivariable model. In a subgroup analysis of adult versus pediatric patients, we found that adults (≥18 years) were almost twice as likely to develop severe infections (29% vs 17%, respectively). This age-related discrepancy in infection suggests there may be inherent differences in clinical practice parameters (eg, caregivers at the bedside and more inpatient management in children), immune response (eg, resilience), or disease biology between patients with pediatric and adult B-ALL that are unaccounted for, emphasizing the importance of further investigation of age-specific risk stratification for infection prophylaxis in the post-CAR setting. Importantly, this aligns with previous findings in the HSCT setting, where adults exhibited a higher infection-related mortality at day+100 (6.2%), particularly due to bacterial infection, while viral infections were more common in pediatric patients (2.5%).26

Alongside older age, a prior severe infection within 90 days of CAR infusion also emerged as a significant risk factor for post-CAR infections. These results are consistent with literature in HSCT patients, where a history of prior bacterial infection is associated with higher risk of post-transplant complications and has been used to guide risk-adapted strategies.2730 This mechanism is likely multifactorial, possibly reflecting disruption of mucosal integrity following antibiotic treatment, which may promote gut bacterial translocation. These associations have been observed in cohorts of both adult and pediatric patients. Overall, infection history is a practical and accessible clinical variable, making its integration into a pre-infusion risk assessment a feasible approach to improve infection risk stratification in CAR T-cell patients.

While we observed a higher rate of severe infections among patients receiving higher-dose cyclophosphamide (Cy)-only lymphodepletion compared with those receiving a fludarabine/cyclophosphamide combination, likely due to greater myelosuppression associated with higher chemotherapy doses,6 31 this was not further pursued given the limited and declining use of high-dose Cy-only regimens in this patient population. However, as additional agents are incorporated into LD, the role of conditioning will remain important to continue to assess in terms of infection risk.

Finally, we found that patients with MSI had a similar risk profile to patients with severe infections overall. Specifically, older age, baseline thrombocytopenia, and BM disease continued to correlate strongly with MSI, suggesting an ongoing relationship between these variables and heightened infection risk.

Interestingly, we observed no difference in rates of infections based on antimicrobial prophylaxis patterns, and prophylaxis usage did not differ significantly across institutions—with most patients receiving viral and fungal prophylaxis and approximately 28% receiving antibacterial prophylaxis. Given the known risks of broad-spectrum prophylactic antibiotics (ie, antibiotic resistance, disruption of the gut microbiome),32 33 future studies are needed to evaluate the usage of different prophylaxis guidelines in CAR T-cell patients to potentially guide antibiotic de-escalation guidelines. Importantly, all five patients who developed grade 5 (fatal) infections had MSI and did not receive antibacterial prophylaxis. While this observation is suggestive, it remains unclear whether prophylaxis could have prevented these outcomes, and further investigation is needed. The ALL-HT score, or simply the presence of severe thrombocytopenia and high BM disease burden, may help identify suitable candidates for prophylactic anti-infectives. This may be particularly imperative in patients with low disease burden, where infection impacted long-term outcomes.

Post-infusion, severe ICAHT—reflecting protracted and deep neutropenia—emerged as the strongest factor associated with severe infection, even after controlling for confounders such as severe CRS, neurotoxicity, and steroid use. While post-CAR severe ICAHT was associated with infections, pre-infusion baseline ANC was not, suggesting that the chronicity of severe neutropenia and not a single pre-infusion value drives infection risk. Importantly, in our study, bacterial infections were most common, consistent with an impact of neutropenia. However, despite an average of 15.5 days of severe neutropenia, invasive fungal infections (IFI) were rare. Comparable to other studies, IFI rates following CAR T-cell therapy are low, affecting<5% of patients across both anti-CD19 and anti-B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) constructs in a larger cohort of 2358 CD19 and 328 BCMA recipients.34 Additionally, a smaller study of 280 CD19 recipients reported an IFI rate of 1.8%.35 This low rate of fungal infection contrasts with reports in chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, where prolonged neutropenia is often associated with much higher rates of invasive fungal disease.36 This likely reflects differences in sequelae of cytotoxicity-induced neutropenia from that which is cytokine-mediated. Nevertheless, these findings emphasize the importance of close monitoring and proactive management of cytopenias in the post-CAR setting. The use of G-CSF to reduce the duration of neutropenia, whether prophylactic or therapeutic, varied by institution—limiting our ability to understand the role in mitigating infection risk. Prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the use of G-CSF as supportive therapy, and identify patients who would benefit most.

Beyond limitations inherent to retrospective analyses, including missing clinical data, potential variability in documentation practices, and a restricted follow-up period of 60 days post-infusion, our multicenter design and inclusion of a large, heterogeneous B-ALL cohort enhance the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Validation in additional B-ALL cohorts as a key next step will be essential.

In conclusion, this study revealed that among pediatric and adult patients with B-ALL treated with CAR T-cell therapy, increased age, a history of prior severe infection, and increased ALL-HT risk score or pre-CAR thrombocytopenia are predictive for severe infection. By incorporating readily available variables—such as age and prior infection with either ALL-HT or baseline thrombocytopenia—our models serve as practical and robust tools for identifying high-risk patients prior to CAR infusion. Future research should explore strategies to mitigate the risk of severe infections in patients with B-ALL undergoing CAR T-cell therapy, particularly among high-risk groups, and investigate the distinct infection risk profile of pediatric and adult patients.
