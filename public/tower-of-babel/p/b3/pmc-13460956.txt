Shift‐Specific Patterns of Nursing Workloads in the Emergency Department: AI Powered Analysis
Authors: Younhee Kang, Hyunggon Park, Inkyung Park, Dayoung Choi, Suemin Kim
Published: 2026

Introduction

Nursing workloads in the Emergency Department (ED) have reached critical levels, representing a persistent challenge that undermines effective workforce management (Pourmand et al. 2023). ED nurses perform patient care within an environment characterised by unpredictable patient surges, high acuity of care, and frequent task interruptions (Alomari et al. 2021). Under these conditions, a significant proportion of ED nurses report experiencing a moderate to severe mental workload (Hao et al. 2025). This excessive burden stems from chronic overcrowding, the constant presence of family caregivers, rapid patient turnover, and consistently high patient‐to‐nurse ratios (Hetherington et al. 2024).

Specifically, the ED is frequently saturated with critically ill patients requiring immediate and complex interventions, forcing nurses to simultaneously manage clinical exigencies and the non‐clinical demands of caregivers. Furthermore, high‐risk emergency interventions, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation, occur at unpredictable intervals, leading to a severe concentration of workloads during specific periods (Park et al. 2024). This structural overcrowding in the ED contributes to a cycle of treatment delays, staffing shortages, and cumulative nurse burnout (Pearce et al. 2023).

Despite the recognition of these challenges, the internal structure and temporal distribution of ED nursing workloads remain insufficiently elucidated. Nursing workloads in the ED fluctuate significantly depending on outpatient clinic hours, patient acuity, and individual nurse competencies (Clopton and Hyrkäs 2020; Saaiman et al. 2021). A defining characteristic of ED nursing is the necessity for repeated assessments and care adjustments as patients' clinical statuses evolve rapidly following initial treatment (Udod et al. 2021). Importantly, nursing demands are frequently concentrated in specific shifts rather than being evenly distributed throughout the day (Peta and Bradford 2025). Excessive workloads during peak periods hinder timely care readjustment and disrupt workflow efficiency, underscoring the importance of temporal variability in ED nursing practice. However, most existing nursing workload assessment tools fail to capture this variability as they rely primarily on patient‐centered metrics such as the volume of nursing interventions per patient (Iordache et al. 2020) or on daily mean workload levels derived from subjective questionnaires (Kaya and İşler Dalgıç 2022).

This limitation is further reinforced at the policy level. Globally, legislated staffing regulations define workforce requirements using static indicators, including bed occupancy, nurse‐to‐patient ratios, and patient severity (American Nurses Association 2020; British Columbia Ministry of Health 2024; Hospital Nurses Association 2024). While ED staffing is operationalised on a shift‐by‐shift basis, prevailing workforce allocation frameworks do not account for shift‐level fluctuations in care demand, leading to a structural misalignment between allocated resources and actual clinical demand (Mehra et al. 2024). In practice, such structural incongruity leads to inefficient resource utilisation during some shifts, while other shifts can involve overwhelming workloads that trigger burnout (Brassington et al. 2025), missed nursing care (Udod et al. 2021), diminished health status for nurses (Chang 2024), and compromised patient safety.

In summary, although the ED nursing workloads are severe and linked to various negative outcomes, empirical analysis of the workload structure according to shifts remains lacking. Given that both care demands and staffing allocations are determined at the shift level, analysing nursing workloads according to shifts is a fundamental prerequisite for optimizing ED staffing and ensuring the quality of care. To address ED workforce challenges at their source, it is essential to move beyond generalised workload assessments and develop objective models that accurately reflect shift‐specific nursing workload patterns. Such approaches are critical for establishing equitable, responsive, and evidence‐based staffing standards tailored to the unique operational dynamics of the ED.

Study Purpose

This study aimed to characterise and differentiate workload patterns across shifts and to provide evidence to facilitate optimisation of nursing manpower allocation in EDs.

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was conducted to identify shift‐specific differences in nursing work characteristics in the ED, by integrating multiple objective features related to nurses, patients, work characteristics, locations, and time to develop a machine learning‐based shift classification model. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies (von Elm et al. 2007).

Setting and Participants

The study was conducted at a single general hospital during routine clinical practice in a 24‐h ED, from October 30, 2023, to October 24, 2024. The hospital comprises approximately 1000 beds and 20 clinical departments, with an ED providing care to patients with varying levels of acuity. Real‐time location tracking of individual nurses was implemented at the shift level. The shift durations were as follows: day shift, 8 h; evening shift, 7 h 30 min; night shift, 9 h 30 min, including flexible rest periods.

Study participants were registered nurses providing direct patient care in the ED. Nurses with less than 3 months of clinical experience, those primarily involved in administrative roles, or those performing non‐clinical duties were excluded. Recruitment was conducted through study announcements posted across hospital departments, supplemented by in‐person explanations provided by the researcher. Eligible nurses who expressed a willingness to participate and provided informed consent were enrolled. Participation was explicitly stated to be voluntary and not to affect employment status or performance evaluation.

Data Source and Variables

Data were derived from the Surveillance and Measurement for Assessing Real Tasks in Nursing Using Real‐time Systems and Enabled Technologies (SMART‐NURSE) project (Park and Kang 2025). Data collection was conducted at the shift level using smartphones, Bluetooth‐based beacons, and smartwatches. To ensure measurement accuracy and optimise system performance of the device‐based system, only one nurse was tracked per shift (Park and Kang 2025). Simultaneous tracking of multiple nurses was constrained by signal interference and limitations in real‐time processing and synchronisation. Accordingly, the number of shift cases corresponds to the one‐nurse‐per‐shift design, which enabled the acquisition of high‐resolution, high‐quality time‐series data suitable for robust machine learning‐based analysis.

The collected data were categorised into four domains: nursing time, nurses' physical activity, demographic and work‐related characteristics, and location transitions.

Nursing Time

Nurses carried a smartphone throughout their working shifts, while Bluetooth‐based beacons installed in designated areas of the ED detected proximity signals emitted from the smartphones. Using a triangulation‐based approach, location information was matched to each timestamp and recorded on a second‐by‐second basis. In accordance with the estimated location‐based stay time, nursing time was quantified and subsequently classified as: (1) direct nursing time, (2) indirect nursing time, (3) resting time, and (4) moving time.

Nurses' Physical Activity

Continuous physical activity data were collected using smartwatches worn by nurses during each shift. These data included heart rate, movement distance, and energy expenditure expressed as metabolic equivalents (METs), recorded at one‐second intervals.

Demographic and Work‐Related Characteristics

Following each shift, participants completed a self‐administered questionnaire assessing demographic and work‐related characteristics. Demographic characteristics include gender, age, total work experience (months), and work experience in current department (months). Work‐related characteristics include the number of assigned patients, emergency events, admissions, and discharges that were measured at the shift level.

Mean patient acuity for each shift was calculated based on the Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (Choi et al. 2019). The scale is divided into five levels (KTAS 1–5), with lower levels indicating greater urgency in the patient's clinical condition: KTAS 1 (resuscitation), KTAS 2 (emergent), KTAS 3 (urgent), KTAS 4 (less urgent), and KTAS 5 (non‐urgent). Each level reflects the severity of the patient's status, the risk of deterioration, and the required timeliness of medical intervention. In this study, the mean KTAS score of the patients assigned to each nurse was calculated per shift.

Perceived stress levels were assessed at the end of each shift using a 19‐item Nurses' Stress Scale (Kang et al. 2024) which consists of 19 items across four domains: workload (9 items), emotional labor (3 items), personal characteristics (3 items), and organisational factors (4 items). Each item was rated on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much). Domain scores were calculated by averaging item scores within each domain, and the total stress score was obtained by summing the domain scores, with a possible range of 4 to 16. Higher scores indicate greater negative psychological responses.

Location Transitions

Location transitions were quantified as movements between predefined areas. ED areas were categorised as: patient rooms; the nursing station; the treatment preparation room, medication preparation room, treatment room, triage room, dirty utility room, and isolation room; and the corridors. All directional transitions, including movements into and out of the ward, were counted. Transitions occurring immediately before or after moving or resting time were treated as stay time at the corresponding locations.

Data Analysis

In total, 238 shift cases were initially collected, and all data were transmitted to and stored in a local database system. Data were collected using wearable devices and Bluetooth‐based beacon signals, generating high‐resolution time‐series data at a per‐second level. Given the continuous, device‐based nature of data collection, variability in data quality was anticipated (e.g., signal instability, device non‐wear, synchronisation issues). To address these issues, the downloaded data were preprocessed using a multi‐stage pipeline implemented in Python (Python Software Foundation 2020), focusing on data accuracy, completeness, and temporal alignment. During preprocessing, 56 shift cases were excluded based on predefined data quality criteria, including substantial missingness in time and location variables and poor‐quality questionnaire responses. Consequently, a total of 182 shift cases were retained for the final analysis.

The processed data were subsequently used to develop a multiclass classification model to predict working shifts (day, evening, and night) using eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). The dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a ratio of 7:3. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. To enhance model interpretability, Shapely Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to identify and quantify the key features contributing to shift classification.

To examine shift‐specific differences in nursing workload characteristics, one‐way analysis of variance was conducted for continuous variables across day, evening, and night shifts. Post hoc analyses were performed as appropriate when statistically significant differences were identified. Additionally, frequency analysis was applied to quantify location transitions for each shift. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 31 software.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ewha Womans University Medical Center (EUMC 2021‐12‐010‐012) and Ewha Womans University (ewha‐202111‐0031‐04). Trained research assistants with registered nurse licensure provided participants with detailed explanations regarding the study purpose and data collection procedures. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. Data were de‐identified at the point of collection, securely stored, and accessible only to the research team. No identifiable information was shared with the institution, and the data were used solely for research purposes, not for performance management.

Predictors of Shift Classification

The number of admissions emerged as the most predictive feature for shift classification, with the highest mean absolute SHAP value of 0.4690. This was followed by the number of discharges (0.3869), indirect nursing time (0.3836), and the number of assigned patients (0.3806), which exhibited comparable levels of feature importance. Direct nursing time ranked fifth, with a mean SHAP value of 0.2256 (Figure 1).

For the day shift, the number of discharges showed the highest overall contribution, followed by the number of assigned patients, direct nursing time, the location transition from the isolation room to the triage room, and patient acuity. For the evening shift, the number of assigned patients exhibited the largest mean absolute SHAP value, followed by the number of admissions, indirect nursing time, patient acuity, and the location transition from the medication preparation room to the patient room. For the night shift, the number of admissions and indirect nursing time showed the highest mean absolute SHAP values, followed by moving distance, resting time, and the location transition related to the treatment room. Across individual shift cases, SHAP values varied in both direction and magnitude, demonstrating heterogeneous case‐level contribution patterns in all target shift classes.

Indirect nursing time showed a distinct tiered pattern, being highest during the night shift, followed by the day and evening shifts (F = 17.823, p < 0.001). The number of assigned patients was significantly lowest during the day shift (F = 34.541, p < 0.001). Direct nursing time was significantly higher during the evening and night shifts than in the day shift (F = 9.987, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study utilised a machine learning classification model to identify shift‐related differences in ED nursing workloads. The high classification performance, coupled with SHAP‐based feature identification, provides robust quantitative evidence that these differences are embedded in measurable clinical patterns rather than subjective perception. The consistent alignment between statistical variance comparisons and machine learning predictors reinforces the structural distinctness of nursing workloads across different shifts.

The most decisive features distinguishing workloads across shifts were the number of admissions and discharges. Specifically, the number of admissions was substantially lower during the night shift compared with the day and evening shifts, whereas the number of discharges was lowest during the day shift. This pattern reflects routine patient management practices in general hospitals, where scheduled admissions are concentrated during daytime hours and patients who complete discharge preparation in the morning are typically discharged in the afternoon—coinciding with the evening shift (Pryce et al. 2021). This trend is also observed in paediatric EDs, with the number of admissions and discharges peaking during the evening shift (Janhunen et al. 2020), reinforcing the evening shift as a period of high clinical activity. In this context, the total number of assigned patients was identified as a major shift‐determining feature, as it fluctuates continuously in response to admission and discharge flows.

From the perspective of nursing time allocation, indirect and direct nursing time functioned as key features in classifying shifts. Direct nursing time increased during the evening and night shifts, which appears to be largely explained by higher patient volumes, as supported by the importance of patient numbers in shift classification. Because ED patient arrivals extend beyond regular administrative hours, substantial direct nursing activities persisted throughout the night shift. Given that the evening shift is constrained by the shortest working window despite managing the highest volume of patient arrivals, indirect nursing activities may be inevitably sidelined, suggesting that administrative tasks likely accumulate and carry over to the night shift. While moving and resting times differed significantly across shifts, their relatively small proportion in terms of total working time contributed minimally to shift discrimination.

The rankings of the five most frequently visited locations remained consistent across all shifts, suggesting that spatial movement patterns were not a primary driver of shift‐level workload differences. Patient rooms were the most frequently visited areas regardless of the shift, reflecting the chronic overcrowding of the ED and the high volume of patients requiring rapid diagnosis and medical or surgical intervention (Sartini et al. 2022). The proportion of shift time spent in patient rooms peaked during the evening shift, indicating that patient inflow, census, and nursing workloads were most concentrated during this period. Similarly, frequent visits to treatment and medication preparation rooms appear closely linked to the ED's requirement for rapid, repeated responses to emergent clinical situations. This overall similarity in movement patterns likely reflects the inherently unpredictable environment of the ED (Saaiman et al. 2021). Such unpredictability is largely driven by the predominance of patients who require dynamic care adjustments (Udod et al. 2021), which in turn necessitates constant movement between the preparation and patient rooms.

Taken together, these findings suggest that while the composition of nursing workloads differs markedly across shifts, the clinical locations of work remain relatively similar. Specifically, the day shift was associated with lower patient volumes, fewer discharges, and reduced direct nursing time. In contrast, the evening shift, which is characterised by increased patient volume and treatment demands, showed the highest frequency proportion of patient room transitions and the longest direct nursing time. These results align with previous research indicating that physical demands and nursing performance levels increase over the course of the day (Bakhoum et al. 2021). During the night shift, although admissions were fewer, patient volume remained elevated owing to carryover from previous shifts and discharges continued, resulting in direct nursing time comparable with those of the evening shift. However, because discharge processes are generally consuming less direct nursing time than admissions (Iordache et al. 2020), indirect nursing time was longest during the night shift.

The observed differences in activity patterns across shifts may be interpreted in terms of demand‐ and supply‐driven mechanisms. Demand‐led patterns refer to workload primarily driven by patient‐related demand, whereas supply‐led patterns reflect the influence of available time and workflow flexibility. The elevated workload, particularly during the evening shift, appears to be largely demand‐led, reflecting increased patient turnover and concurrent task demands. In contrast, patterns observed during the night shift may be more supply‐led, as reduced admission rates and overall task demands allow nurses greater temporal flexibility, despite lower staffing levels, to engage in indirect nursing activities and routine care. This distinction provides a useful framework for understanding how temporal variations in workload are shaped by both patient demand and the availability of time.

Consequently, these findings underscore the necessity of restructuring emergency nurse staffing systems to align with expected shift‐specific nursing workloads, rather than relying exclusively on conventional indicators such as occupied bed counts or overall clinical severity. The pronounced shift‐level variations in nursing time allocation, task composition, and physical activity levels indicate that nursing workloads are driven not solely by general unit characteristics or patient flow, but dynamically by shift‐specific patterns of nursing time and physical exertion. From a hospital management perspective, these results suggest that uniform staffing models may systematically underestimate workload during high‐intensity periods, particularly during the evening shift, while failing to account for the distinct administrative and recovery demands characteristic of night shifts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that shift‐specific nursing workloads in the ED can be accurately distinguished using multidimensional, real‐world nursing activity data. Departing from conventional workload assessments reliant on proxy indicators such as patient volume or acuity, this study adopted a nurse‐centered approach to objectively quantify workloads by capturing nurses' actual movements and classifying nursing activities in real clinical settings. Furthermore, through integrating machine learning techniques, this study introduced a novel analytical framework for identifying shift‐level workload differences that have been rarely applied in previous emergency nursing research. The classification model demonstrated strong predictive performance, and the identified features effectively reflected the operational realities of ED nursing practice. The findings revealed pronounced and structurally distinct workload patterns across shifts, providing clear empirical evidence that staffing models should be designed with explicit consideration for shift‐specific demands.

The methodological framework presented in this study may be extended to other clinical departments to examine whether similar shift‐level workload heterogeneity exists across diverse care settings. Future research should further expand this approach by incorporating additional nurse‐level physical characteristics and department‐specific operational factors that were not included in the present analysis. Through providing objective, data‐driven evidence of workload variation across shifts, this study offers a robust empirical foundation for optimizing emergency nursing workforce allocation and advancing more adaptive, evidence‐based staffing policies. Ultimately, such efforts have the potential to improve workforce efficiency, mitigate nurse fatigue and burnout, and enhance patient safety and quality of care in emergency care environments.
