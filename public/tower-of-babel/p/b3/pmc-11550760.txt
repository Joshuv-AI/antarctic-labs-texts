Asphaltene deposition effects on reservoir rock wettability and modification strategies
Authors: Sahar Farajollahi, Mohammad Bazvand, Elham Tahernejad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Asphaltene deposition, a widespread challenge in the petroleum industry, detrimentally affects oil production, transport, and processing. Our study investigates asphaltene's impact on sandstone and carbonate reservoir rock wettability using ASTM-D6560 extraction and heptol solutions. A significant part of our focus was to examine wettability change due to asphaltene damage and the use of nanoparticles to improve this situation. Qualitatively, advanced techniques reveal altered rock composition, and contact angle measurements, along with zeta potential assessments, highlight significant wettability changes. Quantitatively, asphaltene-infused solutions induce a quantifiable increase in contact angles. Specifically, with gamma alumina nanoparticle addition, contact angles rise from 68° to 87° for sandstone and 110°–142° for carbonate rocks. Temperature variations within our experimental range also induce notable wettability changes. These findings establish a clear link between asphaltene deposition and quantifiable wettability modifications, providing crucial insights for optimizing oil recovery processes and enhancing well performance in the petroleum industry. The study's results suggest that nano-gamma alumina can be an effective solution for improving wettability and mitigating asphaltene deposition issues in carbonate rocks, offering promising prospects for the oil and gas industry.

Introduction

Crude oil is the lifeblood of the global energy sector, powering economies and industries worldwide. Its extraction and transportation, however, are fraught with challenges, particularly the complex phenomena of asphaltene and wax deposition []. The deposition of these components can lead to flow restrictions, operational issues, and substantial costs in the oil and gas industry. Understanding the underlying mechanisms of asphaltene and wax deposition is crucial for optimizing oilfield operations and ensuring the efficient and cost-effective extraction of crude oil.

In the realm of asphaltene and wax deposition, extensive research has been conducted to explore various aspects and factors influencing their formation and deposition []. Several studies have shed light on these intricate phenomena, and here we delve into the key findings in the literature. One notable area of research is the role of nanoparticles in mitigating asphaltene deposition. In 2017, Shojaati et al. conducted experiments to investigate how nanoparticles, specifically Fe3O4, γ-Al2O3, and NiO, influence asphaltene deposition. Their findings demonstrated that the presence of nanoparticles reduced asphaltene instability and, consequently, decreased asphaltene deposition. Furthermore, an increase in nanoparticle concentration led to a shift in the onset of asphaltene precipitation towards higher volume percentages, suggesting the potential of nanotechnology in controlling asphaltene deposition.

Pounaz et al. (2017) focused on understanding the interaction between rock formations and asphaltene stability, particularly in the presence of non-organic impurities such as clay. Through advanced microscopic techniques and energy dispersion spectroscopy, they established a direct connection between excessive negatively charged particles within the reservoir and increased asphaltene deposition. They also found that larger asphaltene particle sizes were associated with a higher likelihood of deposition and highlighted the role of electrostatic repulsion in enhancing asphaltene stability. Li et.al [1] explored the deposition of asphaltene due to the presence of carbon dioxide (CO2) and its effects on alterations in wettability. By varying temperature and aging time, they revealed that asphaltene deposition in porous materials was a time-dependent process. At higher temperatures, asphaltene particles aggregated into larger entities with a broader size distribution, depositing on the inner surfaces of the porous medium. This research shed light on the time-dependent nature of asphaltene deposition and its relationship with temperature.

Managing hydrocarbon reservoirs presents substantial challenges, especially concerning asphaltene deposition and optimizing oil recovery strategies. This collection of studies investigates diverse approaches aimed at understanding and mitigating these challenges. Kazemzadeh et al. (2014) introduce advanced numerical methods for solving differential equations that govern asphaltene dynamics, underscoring the critical role of robust computational models in predicting behavior and optimizing mitigation strategies. Building on this foundation, Kazemzadeh et al. (2017) propose innovative solutions: exploring the application of COFs in membrane technology to mitigate asphaltene deposition, while Kazemzadeh et al. (2021) introduce TPMDS, a novel solvent package demonstrating exceptional effectiveness in asphaltene removal under diverse conditions.

Shifting focus to enhanced oil recovery (EOR), Kazemzadeh et al. (2015) [2] delve into strategies tailored for carbonate reservoirs. They review laboratory studies involving injection fluids such as water, hot water, CO2, and associated gas, aimed at maximizing recovery efficiency while monitoring asphaltene precipitation dynamics. In another study, Kazemzadeh et al. (2024) investigate CO2 and hydrogen storage in depleted reservoirs, analyzing interfacial tension and wettability alteration, crucial for sustainable reservoir management. Furthermore, Kazemzadeh et al. (2022) examine surfactant flooding and nanoparticle applications: reviewing field projects of surfactant flooding in carbonate reservoirs to enhance oil recovery and evaluating the potential of SiO2, NiO, and Fe3O4 nanoparticles for mitigating asphaltene adsorption, with SiO2 emerging as a promising enhancer of recovery efficiency.

Together, these studies provide comprehensive insights and innovative strategies to address asphaltene-related challenges and optimize oil recovery processes in hydrocarbon reservoirs. This introduction lays the groundwork for exploring advancements in reservoir management techniques essential for sustainable energy production and environmental stewardship.

The papers collectively suggest that asphaltene deposition and reservoir rock wettability are important factors in oil recovery. Hosseini et.al [3] investigates the effect of asphaltene deposition on relative permeability during water alternating gas (WAG) injection, highlighting the need for understanding relative permeability changes in reservoirs with specific asphaltene content. Wang et.al [4] explores the interactions between dolomite surfaces and oil, indicating that asphaltenes possess a native positive charge and adhere to regions of the surface with negative charge. Mikhailov et.al [5] examines the influence of asphaltenes on the wettability of different rock types, emphasizing their contribution to wettability changes. Norouzpour et.al [6] focuses on the isolated effect of asphaltene content on enhanced oil recovery during low salinity water flooding of carbonate reservoirs, highlighting the importance of polar compounds like asphaltene and resin in oil recovery from carbonate reservoirs. Bahmani Nia et al. (2021) investigated the mechanisms of asphaltene adsorption on sandstone surfaces, utilizing both static and dynamic tests. Their research found that asphaltene adsorption on quartz surfaces was directly linked to nitrogen content and asphaltene polarity. They also observed a 35 % reduction in asphaltene adsorption as flow rates increased twofold in dynamic adsorption, implying physical adsorption on quartz. Moreover, in a three-phase system, competitive effects between water, asphaltene, and active sites on quartz surfaces were observed, leading to over 30 % reduced adsorption.

The effect of reservoir rock types on asphaltene deposition was examined by Punase et.al [7]. Their research focused on the structural and quantitative aspects of asphaltene deposits under dynamic conditions during oil injection into porous media. They developed a kinetic model capable of predicting changes in permeability over time, highlighting the substantial impact of reservoir rock types on asphaltene deposition. Dolomite-rich reservoirs exhibited the highest deposition, while silica-rich rocks had the least.

Despite significant contributions to our understanding of asphaltene and wax deposition, several critical gaps and uncertainties persist in the current body of knowledge. Firstly, the intricate interplay between asphaltene and wax deposition and how changes in one component affect the other remain a subject of ongoing investigation. While some studies suggest interconnected mechanisms, the precise nature of this interaction is not yet fully understood. Secondly, the role of various reservoir rock types in asphaltene deposition has been documented, but further in-depth research is required to identify the geological factors contributing to these differences. A more comprehensive analysis is necessary to provide a deeper insight into how different rock formations impact asphaltene deposition.

This paper aims to address these knowledge gaps by conducting a holistic and in-depth investigation into the interaction between asphaltene and wax deposition. It will focus on understanding how changes in one component influence the other, elucidating the precise mechanisms of their interconnected behavior. Furthermore, the paper will provide an extensive analysis of the influence of reservoir rock types on asphaltene deposition, aiming to uncover the geological factors that underlie these differences. This analysis will serve to provide a comprehensive overview of how distinct rock formations impact asphaltene deposition. Additionally, the paper will assess the efficiency and effectiveness of nanoparticle treatments in controlling asphaltene deposition, taking into account various reservoir conditions and different crude oil types. Methodology

Materials

In this project, two different asphaltene samples were employed, originating from various types of crude oil. Additionally, thin sections of sandstone and carbonate rocks, exposed to an asphaltene solution, were utilized, along with synthetic brine designed to emulate the composition of a specific Iranian oil field. The measurement of contact angles, which represent interfacial properties between oil, water, and solid surfaces, required a combination of these materials. The aqueous phase consisted of synthetic brine representative of an Iranian oil field, while the solid phase comprised thin sections of sandstone and carbonate rocks exposed to an asphaltene solution. The oil phase included two different crude oil samples that adhered to the solid surface. The asphaltene solution, referred to as heptol, was prepared using toluene and n-heptane. The density of the utilized crude oils was measured using a pycnometer. To effectively measure the density of heavy crude oil type A using a pycnometer, we followed standard procedures designed for such oils. The pycnometer method involves accurately weighing the pycnometer both empty and filled with the sample oil at a specific temperature. By knowing the volumes and masses involved, we calculated the density. Regarding the API gravity data provided in Table 1, this information was obtained using standard industry methods. API gravity is a measure of how heavy or light a petroleum liquid is compared to water, and it's typically determined at a specific temperature (often 60 °F or 15.6 °C). This measurement helps classify crude oils into categories such as light, medium, or heavy, based on their density relative to water.

Determination of the asphaltene content in crude oil and its extraction from crude oil

The asphaltene content data provided in Table 1 was determined using the standard method (IP 143) ASTM D6560, which specifically measures C7 asphaltenes. The procedure is as follows:1.Sample Preparation:1.The crude oil sample was weighed, and for each gram of crude oil, 30 mL of n-heptane was added.2.A magnetic stir bar was placed inside the sample container for thorough mixing, and the sample was heated under a condenser for 65 min.3.The sample was then removed from the condenser, and a cotton pad was placed on top of the container to keep it in the dark for 150 min.2.Filtration and Drying:1.The sample was filtered using filter paper, which was then placed in an oven at 110 °C for 30 min and in a desiccator for an additional 30 min for cooling and moisture absorption.2.The filter paper was washed with n-heptane to remove any non-asphaltene substances. This washing process was conducted using a Soxhlet system with a condensation rate of 2–4 drops per second. The washing continued until the condensed solvent became completely transparent.3.Solvent Evaporation:1.After washing, the filter paper was placed in an oven at 110 °C for 30 min and then in a desiccator for an additional 30 min to evaporate any remaining solvent.2.The filter paper was then washed with toluene in the Soxhlet system for one day to ensure complete removal of asphaltenes.4.Final Steps:1.The fluid containing dissolved asphaltenes in toluene was transferred to a dish and left under a hood for 2–3 days to ensure complete solvent evaporation. It was then placed in an oven at 110 °C for 30 min and in a desiccator for an additional 30 min.2.The mass difference of the dish before and after these steps represented the mass of asphaltenes in the crude oil sample.

FTIR analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy serves as a versatile tool for analyzing the functional groups present in asphaltene molecular structures. This analytical technique covers a broad frequency range, from 4000 to 400 cm⁻1, allowing detailed exploration of molecular bonds within asphaltenes. Preparation of the solutions

To prepare the heptol solution using toluene, n-heptane, and asphaltenes, a systematic procedure was followed. The presence of n-heptane in the ternary system of n-heptane-toluene-asphaltenes is essential for asphaltene precipitation and the investigation of its adsorption on rock surfaces, causing wettability changes. The addition of n-heptane to the toluene solvent destabilizes the asphaltenes and promotes their rapid aggregation in a shorter timeframe.

Toluene served as the asphaltene solvent. To determine the volume percentage of toluene and n-heptane, experiments were conducted. Using the lowest asphaltene concentration, namely 4 g per liter, homogeneous solutions of 200 mL were prepared according to the following procedure:

For each type of oil, 8.0 g of asphaltenes were dissolved in 160 mL of toluene, maintaining a 20:80 ratio (n-heptane to toluene). The mixture was stirred vigorously with a magnetic stirrer for 1 h to ensure complete dissolution. Subsequently, 40 mL of n-heptane was gradually added to the solution, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min. The solution was then placed in an ultrasonic bath for 20 min to achieve stability. The same procedure was followed for n-heptane percentages of 30 %, 40 %, and 50 % (Tavakoli et al., 2014). Following this, the contents of each of the containers, each containing 200 mL of heptol solution with a specific ratio of n-heptane to toluene, were divided into four separate containers, each holding 50 mL. This division was carried out to investigate the impact of aging time on the deposition amount. Subsequently, these containers were filtered through filter paper after 24, 72, 120, and 168 h [8]. Preparation of thin sections of rock samples

After determining the rock type using a rock cutting machine, rectangular rock samples were cut. The dimensions of the cross-sectional surface of the rectangles were 2 × 3 centimeters with a thickness of 3 mm. After cutting the rocks to the desired dimensions and aging them in saline water for one week to ensure their wettability, the initial contact angle was measured. Then, the rock samples were placed in heptol solutions with varying concentrations to investigate the impact of asphaltene deposition.

Measurement of the contact angle and zeta potential

For measuring the contact angle of the oil droplet on the thin rock sections, a device available at the Research Institute of Petroleum and Gas at Sahand University of Technology was utilized. This device consists of several components, including a light source, an experimental chamber, a quadrupod, and the oil injection nozzle.

In addition to utilizing the contact angle method to examine the wettability alterations of carbonate and sandstone rocks, the zeta potential analysis was employed for result validation and consistency with the contact angle measurements. Gamma alumina nanoparticles were utilized to alter the wettability of carbonate rock samples, making them more water-wet from their original oil-wet state due to asphaltene deposition. Various concentrations of gamma alumina nanoparticles were initially mixed on a magnetic stirrer for 30 min. Subsequently, to disperse and stabilize the nanoparticles in distilled water, these nanoparticle solutions were subjected to ultrasonication for 60 min.

After three days, it was observed that solutions containing 0.3 %, 0.5 %, and 0.7 % by weight of nanoparticles remained stable, while solutions containing 1 % and 1.5 % by weight became unstable and underwent phase separation. A total of 9 thin sections of carbonate rock were placed in formation water with a salinity of 118,440 ppm at a temperature of 80 °C for one week. FTIR analysis results

Asphaltene, in its molecular structure, comprises different functional groups. Being a hydrocarbon molecule, asphaltene predominantly contains hydrocarbon functional groups, such as paraffins, aromatics, and naphtenes. The asphaltene molecule consists of multi-nuclear aromatic rings fused with alkyl side chains. The polarity of asphaltene arises from the presence of acidic or basic functional groups. Carboxylic acids, esters, ketones, aldehydes, amines, amides, sulfoxides, carboxylates, sulfones, pyridines, and sulfonates are common functional groups often observed in the molecular structure of asphaltene. Many reactions and interactions involving asphaltene molecules are influenced by these functional groups.

Oxygen is the primary heteroatom in asphaltene. It is typically found in functional groups like carboxylic acids, carbonyls, and esters in asphaltene. The presence of oxygen in the asphaltene molecule gives it acidic properties, thanks to its high electronegativity, negatively charging the surface of the molecule. However, the polarity of the asphaltene molecule, and the percentage of oxygen, depends on the position of oxygen in the molecule, bond length, resonance, and the percentage of other heteroatoms.

Sulfur, on the other hand, is found in higher concentrations in asphaltene and resin due to its presence in the third period of the periodic table. It exhibits a stronger gravitational effect on electrons and has a lower electronegativity compared to oxygen. Nevertheless, sulfur functional groups like thiols and sulfoxides, akin to oxygen-based functional groups, possess polar bonds and disrupt electron balance around the molecule.

Nitrogen is one of the elements found in acidic/basic/neutral components of asphaltene, with the highest concentration in asphaltenes and resins. The topology of the asphaltene molecule affects molecular aggregation. The reactions and interactions of asphaltenes depend not only on the presence of heteroatoms and the type of asphaltene but also on the heteroatoms' arrangement in the molecules. According to Figure (a), it can be observed that as the temperature increases from 25 to 50 °C, the average contact angle initially decreased slightly, but from 50 to 80 °C, the slope of this decrease is more linear and pronounced. The reason for the reduction in asphaltene deposition with increasing temperature can be attributed to the change in asphaltene solubility. The adsorption bonds begin to break as temperature rises, leading to increased solubility. This reduction in deposition may result from specific interactions between asphaltene molecules. Thermal fracture of asphaltene clusters associated with smaller particles increases solubility. The number of hydrogen bonds decreases with increasing temperature, indicating the disruption of related bonds [10]. The concentration of asphaltene has a significant impact on the wettability of reservoir rocks. With an increase in asphaltene concentration in the heptol solution, as shown in Figure (b), the contact angle increases, and the system shifts towards oil-wettability. In Figure (c), with an increase in aging time of thin sections of carbonate and sandstone rocks in the heptol solution, the average contact angle increases up to a certain point, then stabilizes at a constant value. Fourth, the variable is the rock type. As shown in Figure (d), the average contact angle for carbonate rock is higher than sandstone. The reason for this can be attributed to the positive nature of carbonate rock, making it more oil-wet compared to sandstone. Carbonate rocks are generally oil-repellent. If exposed to a choice between oil and water, they prefer oil and become oil-wet. Experimental evidence shows that carbonate particles usually absorb an organic protective coating that shields them against dissolution in acidic formation waters. Due to having a positive charge on the surface, carbonate rocks have the potential to adsorb considerable amounts of anions. In the dolomite (carbonate) rock sample, the highest level of adsorption occurs through magnesium present in the rock and sulfur found in asphaltene. Therefore, the presence of magnesium in dolomite can increase the adsorbed sulfur content in the asphaltene portions, while in the sandstone sample, the highest adsorption may occur between sulfur and potassium and also between oxygen and silicon [11]. In Figure (e), the average contact angle for rocks aged with a heptol solution containing asphaltene deposits from oil A is greater than the contact angle for rocks aged with a heptol solution containing asphaltene deposits from oil B. The reason for this is the heavier nature of oil A and, according to the results of FTIR analysis, asphaltene from oil A, with more aromatic and polar components compared to asphaltene from oil B, has a higher capacity to adsorb on the rock surface.

Zeta Potential Results

Zeta potential is an electrokinetic potential measured at the slipping plane located in the diffuse layer, separating the charged surface from the electrolyte solution. It is considered a vital parameter in geophysical monitoring, controlling wettability, and the adsorption of polar components in aquifers and oil reservoirs. In an ionic solution, particles with a net charge move through the solution at a specific speed under the influence of an electric field. This movement is called electrophoretic mobility and is used to calculate the zeta potential. Therefore, zeta potential represents the slipping potential of a particle in a medium, providing information about the type and magnitude of the surface charge on a suspended particle. Zeta potential values can indicate changes in surface properties, such as alterations in wettability. This is a qualitative technique and has little to no quantitative correlation with wettability, except for changes in zeta potential magnitude and sign, which could be due to fluid adsorption on particle surfaces or the dissolution of surface ions. In the best-case scenario, it can only be used as a qualitative assessment.

Reservoir rocks consist of various mineral substances, and their surface charges can vary. Rock minerals and asphaltene molecules inherently have a surface charge, and for reactions that could change wettability, these two interacting molecules must possess opposite charges.

Experiments show that active materials at the natural surface of crude oil are often soluble enough in water and can be adsorbed on rock surfaces as they pass through the thin aqueous layer on the surface. Although active materials are found in a wide range of oil cuts, their presence in heavier oil cuts, such as asphaltenes and resins, is more common. Carbonates carry a positive electric charge at low PH and a negative electric charge at high PH. Due to the breakdown of the acidic groups present in the oil in the environment of carbonate reservoirs, and the negative charge at the water-oil contact surface, the reason for the change in wettability can be attributed to the adsorption of negatively charged acidic substances on the positively charged carbonate rock surface.

Zeta Potential Results for both carbonate and sandstone rocks are shown in Fig. 23, Fig. 24. The zeta potential for clean sandstone without asphaltene is −17 mV. Absorption of asphaltene from oil B on the sandstone surface changes the zeta potential to −20 mV. Asphaltene from oil A affects the sandstone surface more and increases the zeta potential to −25 mV. For carbonate rock, the zeta potential is −5 mV in the clean state, without asphaltene. This is due to the presence of ions such as Ca2+, Mg2+, and CO3− in the dolomite rock structure. These ions neutralize each other inside the rock, resulting in a net charge of zero within the rock. Ca2+, Mg2+, and CO32− ions are in contact with the rock minerals on one side and, on the other side, with the rock surface. The side in contact with the minerals is neutralized, but the surface site of the rock carries Ca+, Mg+, and CO3− ions. When this rock is placed in distilled water, H+ and OH− ions from the distilled water react with Ca+, Mg+, and CO3− ions present in the rock's surface sites, neutralizing them. However, due to the dissolution of dolomite rock in distilled water, CO32− ions enter the aqueous phase and are absorbed on the surface site of the dolomite rock, resulting in a negative charge. Analyzing the results of the effect of nano gamma alumina on wettability improvement

To investigate the impact of gamma alumina nanoparticles on improving wettability, in order to change the wettability of rock surfaces that have become oil-wet due to asphaltene deposition, gamma alumina nanoparticles can be employed to make the surface more water-wet and enhance oil recovery. After confirming the oil-wetting of thin rock sections by measuring the contact angle in a heptol solution with an asphaltene concentration of 8 gr/l at a temperature of 25 °C for 144 h, the rock sections were exposed to a solution of nano gamma alumina with different concentrations, and the oil-rock contact angle was measured at different time intervals. Using the chart in Fig. 28, it can be observed that as the concentration of nano gamma alumina and the aging time of the rock in the nano gamma alumina solution increase, the rock surface tends to become more water-wet. The change in wettability is due to physical and chemical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and oxygen (hydroxyl group) bonding with the nanoparticles absorbed on the rock surface, as well as the presence of structural, van der Waals, and electrostatic surface forces. As the concentration of nanoparticles increases, the shift towards water-wettability due to the greater adsorption of nanoparticles is more pronounced, although this change is initially more significant and linear. Nanoparticles, with their high surface-to-volume ratio, have a high capacity for adsorbing asphaltenes, thus preventing their agglomeration and deposition. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study examined the impact of asphaltene deposition on the wettability of carbonate and sandstone samples under various conditions, such as different crude oils, ambient pressure, and a brine with a constant salinity of 118,000 ppm, across different temperatures. While the reported contact angle measurements are not exact, they serve as valuable indicators of wettability changes. The key findings are summarized as follows:1.The data indicate that when the volume percentage of n-heptane is below 40 %, the asphaltene deposition increases gradually with the rise in n-heptane content. However, beyond 40 % n-heptane, the deposition rate rises more steeply. Consequently, a 60-40 ratio of n-heptane to toluene was selected for subsequent experiments with both types of asphaltenes, as it provided a notable increase in deposition.2.The research revealed a transition in the wettability of carbonate and sandstone samples from being strongly water-wet to becoming oil-wet due to the adsorption of asphaltene present in two different crude oils. After aging the rock samples for seven days in a brine solution specific to a reservoir with a salinity of 118,000 ppm, increasing asphaltene concentrations from 4 g/L to 8 g/L resulted in higher contact angles. For carbonate and sandstone samples in contact with heptol solutions containing asphaltene from crude oil B, contact angles increased to 87° and 68°, respectively, while for samples in contact with heptol solutions containing asphaltene from crude oil A, the angles increased to 142° and 110°, respectively.3.Minor changes in contact angles were observed as the temperature increased during the asphaltene deposition process on rock surfaces, specifically from 25 to 50 °C. However, a more substantial change in the slope of contact angle variations was noticeable between 50 and 80 °C.4.Asphaltene from crude oil A, characterized by a lower API and a higher content of heteroatoms and functional groups, exhibited more significant deposition on carbonate and sandstone surfaces and had a more pronounced impact on altering wettability.5.The zeta potential of sandstone, in its clean state, was −17 mV. After the adsorption of asphaltene from crude oil B, it shifted to −20 mV, while it reached −25 mV after the adsorption of asphaltene from crude oil A. The presence of acidic compounds in the adsorbed asphaltene had a negative effect on the zeta potential.6.To modify the wettability of asphaltene-wet carbonate rocks, aqueous solutions of nano-gamma alumina at various concentrations were used. Solutions with concentrations of 1 % and 1.5 % w/w destabilized and underwent phase separation after three days. In contrast, solutions with concentrations of 0.3 %, 0.5 %, and 0.7 % w/w remained stable and successfully altered the wettability of carbonate rocks to become water-wet. The most significant wettability change was achieved using a solution with a concentration of 0.7 % w/w nano-gamma alumina.

The findings of this study have significant implications for the oil and gas industry. Asphaltene deposition is a major challenge in petroleum production, causing issues such as formation damage, reduced permeability, and altered wettability, which can severely impact oil recovery efficiency. Understanding the conditions that exacerbate asphaltene deposition and its effects on rock wettability is crucial for developing effective mitigation strategies.1.Enhanced Oil Recovery (EOR): The transition of rock wettability from water-wet to oil-wet due to asphaltene adsorption highlights the need for tailored EOR methods. The application of nano-gamma alumina to alter wettability back to water-wet conditions presents a promising approach to improve oil recovery in carbonate reservoirs.2.Reservoir Management: Insights into the effects of asphaltene from different crude oils and the role of temperature in deposition processes can guide reservoir management practices. Operators can optimize production strategies by adjusting operational conditions to minimize asphaltene-related issues.3.Chemical Treatment Solutions: The successful use of nano-gamma alumina solutions to modify wettability provides a potential chemical treatment for asphaltene deposition problems. This approach can be integrated into existing reservoir management protocols to enhance oil recovery and maintain production efficiency.4.Cost-Effective Operations: By reducing the impact of asphaltene deposition and improving wettability, the industry can achieve more cost-effective operations, lower maintenance costs, and reduced downtime due to formation damage.
