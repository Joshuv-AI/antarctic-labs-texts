Effectiveness and safety of extended upadacitinib induction therapy in adult patients with moderate-to-severe active ulcerative colitis
Authors: Nabil El Hage Chehade, Tara Alleyasin, Bonnie Zagorski, Thien Nguyen, Nirmal Patel, Melissa Ferrari, Emily Singh, Mazer Ally, Rebecca Matro, Gauree G Konijeti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResponses to induction therapies for moderate-to-severe active ulcerative colitis (UC) are sufficient, but some patients benefit from prolonged induction to achieve clinical outcomes before transitioning to maintenance dosing. Upadacitinib (UPA), an oral JAK1 inhibitor, is approved in the USA for treatment of moderate-to-severe active UC non-responsive to anti-TNF therapy. However, standard 8-week induction regimen with UPA 45 mg/day may not be sufficient for some patients. We aimed to assess the effectiveness and safety of extended UPA induction for moderate-to-severe active UC.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of adults with moderate-to-severe active UC who received UPA 45 mg/day beyond 8 weeks’ induction. Patients with disease-related complications and those lost-to follow-up data after extended induction were excluded. Primary endpoint was clinical response (partial Mayo score (PMS) reduction by ≥ 3). Secondary endpoints included clinical remission (PMS ≤ 2), endoscopic response (Mayo endoscopic score decrease by ≥ 1), improvement in fecal calprotectin (FC) to <150 ug/dL, and specific adverse events.ResultsNine patients were included for analysis. All patients previously failed ≥ 1 biologic therapy, and 5 failed ≥ 2 biologic therapies, of which 1 also failed tofacitinib. Duration of extended UPA induction ranged from 12-24 weeks. Clinical response rate was 78% (7/9) and FC improved to <150 ug/dL in 71% (5/7). At the time of this report, 3 patients completed 12 weeks UPA induction but have not yet transitioned to maintenance therapy. Of these, 1 achieved clinical response, 1 achieved clinical remission, and 1 has planned colectomy for steroid-refractory UC. Five patients completed UPA extended induction (range 12-24 weeks) and transitioned to UPA 30 mg/day with stable clinical remission and FC <150 ug/g. Finally, 1 patient discontinued UPA induction after 16 weeks due to non-response and underwent total proctocolectomy. Adverse events included dental abscess (n = 1) and mild COVID-19 infection (n = 2). No patients developed Herpes zoster, VTE, or MACE.ConclusionOur retrospective study suggests extended UPA induction, from 12-24 weeks, is effective in achieving clinical response/remission in patients with moderate-to-severe active UC with a tolerable safety profile. Our clinical response/remission rates are higher than that reported for extended induction in the U-ACCOMPLISH/U-ACHIEVE clinical trials. Larger studies and longer-term follow-up are needed to examine the efficacy and safety of this approach.Keywords: moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis, upadacitinib, extended induction, clinical response, clinical remission, endoscopic response, adverse effects

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a subtype of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), characterized by chronic relapsing and remitting episodes of inflammation limited to the mucosal layer of the colon, almost invariably involving the rectum.1–2 The treatment goal for patients with active UC is to achieve glucocorticoid-free clinical and endoscopic remission, and prevent disease complications (toxic megacolon, fulminant colitis, colonic perforation, dysplasia, colorectal cancer [CRC]).3–6 Despite the availability of multiple therapeutic options for patients with UC, achieving short-term and long-term disease control remains challenging in a subset of patients.3,7–8 Even with newer biological agents, approximately two-thirds of patients with UC do not attain or maintain remission after 1 year.9–11 Induction periods typically range between 6 and 12 weeks, but studies from multiple IBD therapies have demonstrated that prolonged induction can still capture response in a select group of patients with moderate-to-severe disease.12–14 This approach would offer them effective and continued maintenance therapy. Without trial of extended induction, we often switch therapies prematurely. Thus, there remains an unmet need for additional therapeutic options/strategies for patients with UC.

Upadacitinib (UPA) is an oral, selective, reversible, small-molecule Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitor engineered to preferentially inhibit JAK1, which has been used for the treatment of various autoimmune disorders including UC.15–16 The most common adverse effect of UPA is development of acne vulgaris. Other common adverse effects include upper respiratory tract infections, Herpes zoster, hypercholesterolemia, and liver function tests (LFT) abnormalities. Serious adverse effects (SAE) include increased risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE), venous thromboembolism (VTE), and allergic reactions.15–16 Results from two identical phase 3 double-blind randomized controlled trials involving adult patients with moderate-to-severe active UC with an inadequate response, loss of response, or intolerance to biologic therapy (U-ACHIEVE and U-ACCOMPLISH) demonstrated that 8 weeks’ induction therapy with UPA 45 mg/day was superior to placebo in inducing clinical and endoscopic remission/response without any new safety signals identified.17–18 Responders subsequently entered the U-ACHIEVE Maintenance study, in which they received either UPA 15 or 30 mg/day or placebo. At 52 weeks, UPA demonstrated greater efficacy versus placebo across various endoscopic, clinical, histologic, and QOL endpoints.19 As a result, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved UPA, in 2022, for the treatment of adults with moderate-to-severe active UC who were unresponsive or intolerant to treatment with anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) drugs. Recommended regimen consists of UPA induction dose of 45 mg/day for 8 weeks followed by maintenance dosing of 15 or 30 mg/day.20

Responses to UPA induction therapy for moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis (UC) are sufficient, but some patients may benefit from prolonged induction to achieve clinical outcomes before transitioning to maintenance dosing.21 In the U-ACHIEVE Induction/U-ACCOMPLISH trials, patients not achieving clinical response after 8 weeks’ induction therapy were eligible to receive open-label UPA 45 mg/day for an additional 8 weeks prior to transitioning to maintenance dosing.22 Data involving these patients demonstrated that >50% of patients who did not achieve clinical response to UPA 45 mg/day at week 8 eventually achieved this parameter at week 16.22 However, the rates of Herpes zoster infection were increased with longer duration (16 weeks) of exposure to UPA 45 mg/day compared with the same population during the first 8 weeks of UPA induction.22

Real-world data on extended UPA induction beyond 8 weeks in adults with moderate-to-severe active UC remain scarce. As a result, the aim of our retrospective study was to characterize the effectiveness and safety of extended UPA induction at single-center institution.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Scripps institutional board review. Data collection and analysis was compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We conducted a retrospective case series involving patients seen at Scripps Clinic by Scripps Clinic Medical Group gastroenterologists, in San Diego, California. Inclusion criteria included adult patients 18 years or older with moderate-to-severe active UC who previously failed at least one biologic therapy and received extended UPA induction beyond 8 weeks. Moderate disease activity was defined as 4-6 loose bloody stools per day, mild anemia not requiring transfusion (hemoglobin > 10 g/dL), non-severe abdominal pain, no or minimal signs of systemic toxicity.2 On the other hand, patients with severe disease activity were those with >6 loose bloody stools per day, moderate anemia (7.00 g/dL < hemoglobin < 10.0 g/dL), abdominal cramps, and an elevated CRP/ESR without signs of systemic toxicity who were managed in an outpatient setting.2 Exclusion criteria included UC patients with major complications (toxic megacolon, colonic perforation, prior colectomy, and fulminant ulcerative colitis), patients receiving concomitant corticosteroids, and those with acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC) managed in an inpatient setting. Patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn’s disease who received extended UPA induction beyond 8 weeks were excluded from our analysis. Patients who were lost to follow-up after extended UPA induction were excluded as well.

All included patients had their initial IBD clinic follow-up at 8 weeks following UPA induction. Patients then had serial follow-ups every 4 weeks as long as they were maintained on UPA 45 mg/day. During those visits, patients were transitioned to maintenance UPA dosing (30 mg/day) only if they achieved clinical remission with UPA 45 mg/day. All patients had undergone a colonoscopy within 6 months prior to extended UPA induction and had Mayo endoscopy subscore of 2-3. Only 3 patients underwent subsequent colonoscopy at around 12 weeks after initiation of extended UPA induction.

Data extraction and clinical endpoints

Demographic and clinical characteristics such as disease extent, prior therapies, and duration of extended UPA induction were summarized as counts and percentages. The primary endpoint was to assess clinical response rate defined as partial Mayo score (PMS) reduction by ≥ 3. Secondary endpoints included clinical remission defined as PMS ≤ 2, endoscopic response defined as Mayo endoscopic score decrease by ≥ 1, improvement in fecal calprotectin (FC) to <150 ug/dL, and adverse events including infection, VTE, MACE, and colectomy. Only descriptive statistics were used due to the small sample size.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

A total of 9 adult patients with moderate-to-severe active UC who received extended UPA induction beyond 8 weeks were included in the study. The mean age was 39.89 years. 33.3% of patients were male (n = 3) and 66.7% were Caucasians (n = 6). In terms of disease extent, all patients had ulcerative pancolitis (100%, n = 9). One patient had a history of dysplasia noted on prior colonoscopies (11.1%). In terms of prior therapies, all patients were previously on corticosteroids (100%, n = 9), while 77.8% were previously prescribed 5-aminosalicylates (n = 7). All patients previously failed at least one biologic therapy (n = 9) while 55.6% failed ≥ 2 biologic agents (n = 5), of which one also failed tofacitinib (11.1%). The proportion of patients who previously failed adalimumab, infliximab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab were 33.3%, 33.3%, 77.8%, and 22.2%, respectively. Durations of extended UPA induction included 12 weeks (n = 4, 4.44%), 16 weeks (n = 4, 44.4%), and 24 weeks (n = 1, 11.1%). All patients had a pre-UPA induction FC of >300 ug/dL. Discussion

Our retrospective analysis is the first to report real-world data on extended UPA induction in patients with moderate-to-severe active UC. Our data demonstrated that extended UPA induction beyond standard 8 weeks was effective in achieving clinical remission/response in more than two-thirds of patients. In addition, no SAE were reported and side effects were limited to mild infections in 22.2% of patients.

Our results align with previously published data from U-ACCOMPLISH and U-ACHIEVE phase 3 trials in which a subset of patients with moderate-to-severe active UC received extended UPA induction. In the report published by Panaccione et al., patients without clinical response to 8 weeks’ UPA 45 mg/day induction therapy (n = 127/663, 19.2%) were eligible for an additional 8 weeks of induction therapy.22 Of those, 60.00% (75/125) achieved clinical response at week 16 and were eligible for enrollment in the U-ACHIEVE Maintenance trial, in which they were randomized to receive either UPA 15 or 30 mg/day. However, at week 16, only 5.6% achieved clinical remission (n = 7), while 14.3% (n = 18) achieved endoscopic response.22

It should be noted that our “short-term” clinical remission/response and endoscopic response rates were higher than that reported at 16 weeks for extended induction in the U-ACHIEVE/U-ACCOMPLISH trials. One plausible explanation for this discrepancy is that clinical and endoscopic remission are considered “long-term” treatment targets according to the STRIDE II recommendations and it may be premature to assess these parameters directly following completion of extended induction.23 This can be confirmed by the improved clinical remission (43.6%, n = 17), endoscopic response (51.3%, n = 20), and endoscopic remission (17.9%, n = 7) rates at 52 weeks in patients from the U-ACHIEVE/U-ACCOMPLISH trials who were transitioned to maintenance UPA 30 mg/day following 16 weeks’ extended UPA induction.19 In addition, our reported endoscopic response rate may have been hyper-inflated as only 33.3% (n = 3) of included patients in our study underwent colonoscopy at around 12 weeks after initiation of extended UPA induction. Endoscopic reassessment of disease activity is typically performed 9-12 months following therapy initiation, as part of the STRIDE II treat to target approach, which explains why very few patients underwent colonoscopy following extended UPA induction.23 Another plausible explanation lies in the fact that patients with moderate-to-severe active UC refractory to biologic therapy are considered a difficult-to-treat patient population with variability in terms of their therapeutic response. In fact, a study conducted by Armuzzi et al. examined treatment patterns among 1602 patients with moderate-to-severe active UC in the USA and Europe.24 Their findings suggest that the percentage of patients treated with combination therapy varied between 10% and 20% and that among patients using a biologic therapy, 10%-40% of patients were prescribed a higher than indicated dose, reflecting ongoing challenges experienced by clinicians to control their disease.24 Based on these findings, we speculate that given our small sample size, patients included in our study may not be fully representative of the overall population of patients with moderate-to-severe active UC who may receive extended UPA induction. Nonetheless, our patients had persistent moderate-to-severe disease activity despite multiple biologic and immunosuppressive therapies and thus represent a more refractory population, which makes them more likely to require prolonged higher UPA dosing.

In terms of safety outcomes, our study demonstrated a favorable safety profile of extended UPA induction in adults with moderate-to-severe active UC. There were no reported cases of Herpes zoster, MACE, or VTE. However, our safety data should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size and shorter follow-up duration. In the report by Panaccione et al. (2023), 3.9% (n = 5) developed Herpes zoster and 3.9% (n = 5) developed LFT abnormalities at 16 weeks following extended UPA induction.22 No patients developed VTE, MACE, or malignancy. The authors reported that exposure-adjusted event rates of Herpes zoster increased with longer duration of UPA 45 mg/day exposure during the 16-week extended induction period.22 In the light of these findings, clinicians should be aware of the possible increased risk of Herpes zoster infection in these patients and take this into account prior to extending UPA induction. It is also advised that patients are kept up-to-date on all immunizations prior to starting UPA.25

Compared to previous literature, the main strength of our study is that it is the first retrospective case series that examined real-world data on extended UPA induction in adults with moderate-to-severe active UC. Nonetheless, our study had several limitations. The small sample size limits our ability to generalize our findings on the effectiveness and safety of extended UPA induction in this difficult-to-treat patient population. Another limitation was that included patients received extended UPA induction for different durations (12, 16, and 24 weeks), which introduces heterogeneity that may have affected the interpretation of our findings. Our retrospective study was observational in nature and did not include any formal statistical analysis, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the external validity of our findings. In addition, we have only examined “short-term” effectiveness and safety endpoints and did not assess long-term maintenance outcomes beyond 1 year of therapy. As we and others continue to utilize extended UPA induction for patients who could benefit from this approach, future studies will be able to include a larger sample size as well as examine long-term real-world maintenance efficacy and safety outcomes.

Conclusion

Our retrospective case series suggests that extended UPA induction, from 12 to 24 weeks, is effective in achieving clinical response/remission in patients with moderate-to-severe active UC with a tolerable safety profile. Our clinical response/remission rates are higher than that reported for extended induction in the U-ACCOMPLISH/U-ACHIEVE clinical trials, likely owing to major methodological differences in our study (small sample size, retrospective observational nature). Larger studies with better methodology and more homogenous patient population and longer-term follow-up are needed to examine the effectiveness and safety of this approach.
