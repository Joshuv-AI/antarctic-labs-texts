Non-clinical evaluation of pmIL12 gene therapy for approval of the phase I clinical study
Authors: Bostjan Markelc, Tanja Jesenko, Simona Kranjc Brezar, Masa Omerzel, Ursa Lampreht Tratar, Andrej Rencelj, Urska Matkovic, Katarina Znidar, Spela Kos, Kristina Levpuscek, Ziva Pisljar, Ursa Kesar, Tilen Komel, Tim Bozic, Aneja Tuljak, Rosana Hudej, Matjaz Peterka, Urska Kamensek, Andrej Cör, Gorana Gasljevic, Alenka Nemec Svete, Natasa Tozon, Gregor Sersa, Maja Cemazar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Immunotherapeutic drugs are promising medicines for cancer treatment. A potential candidate for immunotherapy is interleukin-12 (IL-12), a cytokine well known for its ability to mediate antitumor activity. We developed a plasmid encoding human IL-12 devoid of an antibiotic resistance gene (phIL12). For the approval of phase I clinical trials in basal cell carcinoma (BCC), the regulatory agency requires non-clinical in vivo testing of the pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties of the plasmid. As human IL-12 is not biologically active in mice, a mouse ortholog of the plasmid phIL12 (pmIL12) was evaluated. The evaluation demonstrated the antitumor effectiveness of the protein accompanied by immune cell infiltration. The plasmid was distributed throughout the body, and the amount of plasmid diminished over time in all organs except the skin around the tumor. The therapy did not cause any detectable systemic toxicity. The results of the non-clinical evaluation demonstrated the safety and efficacy of the pmIL12/phIL12 GET, and on the basis of these results, approval was obtained for the initiation of a phase I clinical study in BCC.

Introduction

Advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs) are medicines for human use that are based on genes, tissues or cells1,2. They offer groundbreaking new opportunities for the treatment of many diseases, including cancer. Among ATMPs, gene therapy represents an innovative approach for treating cancer through the insertion of a recombinant gene into the body1. The recombinant gene can act as an immunotherapeutic drug, stimulating the patient’s own immune system to fight cancer.

A perfect candidate gene for gene immunotherapy is interleukin-12 (IL-12), a cytokine well known for its ability to mediate antitumor activity by inducing the activation and cytotoxicity of T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, Th1 cell differentiation and the inhibition of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)3–6. Early clinical trials involving systemic administration of recombinant IL-12 cytokines yielded disappointing results due to significant toxicity associated with elevated production of proinflammatory cytokines7. Consequently, the systemic administration of IL-12 has demonstrated limited therapeutic potential. An alternative approach is local, intratumoral administration of IL-12. However, the use of recombinant cytokines is hindered by high production costs and the need for frequent dosing. A promising alternative is the use of cytokine-encoding plasmids, which allow the patient’s body to produce the cytokine of interest over an extended period at the target site at non-toxic levels.

An effective way to deliver a gene encoding IL-12 into cells is to use plasmid DNA coupled with gene electrotransfer (GET), a well-established electroporation-based gene delivery method8,9. GET is a promising non-viral approach for gene transfer in which plasmid DNA is transfected into different cells or tissues via electrical pulses10. Compared with viral gene transfer, GET is a safer and more cost-effective method of gene transfer that overcomes the relatively low transfection efficiency of plasmid DNA in tissues11,12. This technique has shown great potential in the treatment of various diseases, including infectious diseases, genetic disorders and cancer13. The GET procedure has already proven safe and effective for the delivery of plasmid DNA in the clinical setting14–17. The safety and efficacy of the IL-12 GET have been demonstrated in clinical trials in the USA using the tavokinogene telseplasmid (tavo), which encodes human IL-12 under the control of the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter and contains an antibiotic resistance gene that acts as a selection gene in the manufacturing process16–18. In Europe, to comply with the guidelines of the European Medicines Agency (EMA), the use of antibiotic resistance genes in the final product should be avoided, if possible, as their use may adversely impact other clinical therapies in the target population2.

In compliance with the EMA guidelines, we developed a plasmid encoding human IL-12 devoid of the antibiotic resistance gene (p21-hIL-12-ORT), a drug product named phIL1219,20, which is being investigated in a phase I clinical trial in basal cell carcinoma in the head and neck region21. In the phIL12 plasmid, the expression of IL-12 is driven by the tumor-specific promoter of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1 (CDKN1a) gene, which is more commonly known as p2119,20,22. To generate the non-clinical data necessary to endorse the start of the first-in-human clinical study with the phIL12 drug product, a mouse ortholog of the phIL12 plasmid (p21-mIL-12-ORT), named pmIL12, was also constructed since human IL-12 is not biologically active in mice20. The production process for both plasmids was optimized, and the plasmids were prepared to be of clinical grade20. The biological activity of the expressed transgenes, the level of expression and the plasmid copy number were determined in vitro in the human squamous cell carcinoma cell line FaDu and the murine colon carcinoma cell line CT26, demonstrating effective delivery of plasmids via GET into the cells, which induces the expression of the transgene and its biological activity20.

The results of the in vitro evaluation provided the basis for the evaluation of the plasmids in vivo. The protocol was designed as a combined non-clinical evaluation study determining the pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, systemic toxicity and immunogenicity following GET with three different doses of pmIL12 in mice (a mouse pmIL12 ortholog was used to ensure the biological activity of the transgene in mice). The non-clinical evaluation in mice was conducted according to the following guidelines: EMA/CAT/80183/2014, EMA/CAT/852602/2018, EMEA/CHMP/GTWP/125459/2006, EMA/CPMP/ICH/286/1995, EMA/CHMP/ICH/646107/2008, EMEA/273974/2005, CPMP/BWP/3088/99, CPMP/SWP/1042/99 Rev 1 Corr and Reflection paper: Expectations for Biodistribution (BD) Assessments for Gene Therapy (GT) Products, EMA Scientific Advice (EMA/CHMP/SAWP/19705/2020) and the Principles of Good Laboratory Practice (GLP).

The aim of the present study was to perform the non-clinical evaluation of our gene therapy medicine in order to proceed to a phase I clinical study. For this purpose, we evaluated the pharmacodynamics of pmIL12 GET by confirming the expression of the transgene and evaluating its antitumor effectiveness and immune cell infiltration at the application site as a measure of the biological action of the transgene. The pharmacokinetics were determined by following the biodistribution and persistence of the plasmid in several organs and tissues. The systemic toxicity and immunogenicity of the therapy were determined by evaluating the general health of the mice, gross pathology during necropsy, histopathology and blood tests (complete blood count, biochemistry and serum IgG and IgM titers).

Results

The expression of the mIL-12 transgene decreased significantly over time in both males (Fig. 1A) and females (Fig. 1B), with no statistically significant difference in expression between individual doses at any time point. The effects of the mIL12 transgene were evaluated in terms of antitumor effectiveness and immune cell infiltration. In both males and females bearing CT26 tumors, pmIL12 GET significantly increased the probability of survival (Fig. 1C,D) at all three concentrations compared with the control untreated group. Similarly, pmIL12 GET at all three tested concentrations significantly reduced the tumor volume in both males (Fig. 1E) and females (Fig. 1F) and delayed tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 2). There was no statistically significant difference in survival probability between males and females (Supplementary Fig. 3A–C) or in tumor growth delay (Supplementary Fig. 3D–F) at any of the three tested concentrations.

Immune cell infiltration was assessed in the tumor and the skin around the tumor. IL-12 is known to activate numerous effector cells and inhibit the immunosuppressive environment in tumors; therefore, we anticipated increased immune cell infiltration in tumors after pmIL12 GET. Two experienced pathologists evaluated the degree of infiltration on a scale of 0–3, where a score of 0 indicated the absence of immune cells, and a score of 3 indicated strong infiltration with immune cells (a representative image demonstrating strong infiltration with immune cells is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1). Compared with that in control untreated tumors and the skin of naïve mice, increased immune cell infiltration in the skin surrounding the tumors was observed two days after therapy (Fig. 1G). In the tumors, increased immune cell infiltration was also observed compared with that in the control untreated tumors two days after pmIL12 GET (Fig. 1H). At later time points, immune cell infiltration in treated tumors was similar to that in control untreated tumors (Fig. 1I,J).

Pharmacokinetic properties of the pmIL12 GET

Two days after pmIL12 GET into CT26 tumors, the presence of pmIL12 was confirmed in all analyzed organs and tissues except the eyes and lungs (Fig. 2A). The highest percentage of positive samples was in the skin above and around the tumor, where pmIL12 was detected in 100% of the samples at all three tested doses of pmIL12 (Fig. 2A). This was followed by the liver, with 40–50% positive samples, and the heart and gonads, with 10–40% positive samples (Fig. 2A). Eight days after GET, the presence of pmIL12 was confirmed only in the skin above and around the tumor with 90–100% positive samples and in the liver (10% positive samples in the high-dose group) and reproductive organs (10% positive samples in the high-dose group) (Fig. 2B). Fourteen days after GET, the presence of pmIL12 was confirmed only in the skin above and around the tumors (50–90% positive samples) (Fig. 2C). In the long-term experimental group, i.e., 21 days after GET, which was the average time to sacrifice of the mice in this group, the percentage of positive samples in the skin above and around the tumor decreased to 30–60% (Fig. 2D). Systemic toxicology

Following therapy, no differences in mouse weight were observed between the treated and untreated control groups for mice of either sex (Fig. 3A,B). No signs of pain, discomfort, or behavioral alterations were detected in the mice during the course of the treatment. During the complete necropsy of the mice, gross examination of organs and tissues revealed no differences between the control group and the groups treated with pmIL12 GET. Similarly, the histopathological analysis of organs/tissues conducted by two experienced pathologists revealed no pathological changes in organs between the control group and the groups treated with pmIL12 GET. Only a local immunogenic response was observed in the tumors and skin around the tumor, as reported in Fig. 1G–J, which can be attributed to the mode of action of the IL-12 transgene. Furthermore, no significant changes associated with the therapy were observed in any of the analyzed CBC, DIFF or BC parameters (Supplementary table). Discussion

For the initiation of a phase I clinical trial of phIL12, the regulatory agency required non-clinical in vivo testing for the determination of the pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic and toxicological properties of this ATMP. This study reports the results of a non-clinical in vivo evaluation of a mouse ortholog of the plasmid phIL12 (pmIL12) delivered by GET in mice. As human IL-12 is not biologically active in mice and therefore phIL12 could not be tested in mice23, we had to prepare a murine ortholog of plasmid phIL12 (pmIL12), which was evaluated in this study. This in vivo study involved optimization of the manufacturing process and in vitro confirmation of the mRNA and protein expression levels, biological activity, and potency of the IL-12 protein, which is produced from the phIL12 and pmIL12 plasmids after GET20. In vitro and in vivo evaluations were performed on clinical-grade plasmids originating from the same production batch, and the results were reported to regulatory authorities in a joint report. In the in vivo study, the pharmacodynamic properties of the pmIL12 transgene were evaluated, followed by an evaluation of safety by determining the pharmacokinetics of the pmIL12 GET and a general toxicology evaluation.

One of the most challenging parts of the planning of the study was selecting the most relevant tumor model because of the lack of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) models (intended use of phIL12 GET). We sought the most commonly used model in the development of cancer immunotherapy with immune profiles similar to those of BCC and SCC. The development was guided by scientific advice and protocol assistance provided by the Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) on the recommendation of the Scientific Advice Working Party (SAWP). On the basis of their guidance, we chose a well-characterized CT26 tumor model in BALB/c mice because of their similar immune tumor landscape24. Rodents, especially mice, are considered the gold standard in cancer research. Immunocompetent mice, rather than human cell lines, were used for in vivo experiments, as immunocompetent mice do not form tumors. Thus, in vivo testing was performed only on the CT26 tumor model. The CT26 xenograft model in BALB/c mice provides a syngeneic in vivo testing system commonly used for the development and testing of immunotherapeutic approaches24,25.

CT26 tumors are strongly immunogenic tumors with the upregulation of many immune activation genes, including panleukocyte (CD45), T-cell (CD3, CD4, CD8), and myeloid cell (CD11c, CD11b) genes, suggesting the presence of immune effector cells in the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, CT26 cells display increased expression of T-cell activation genes (CD25 and CD62L) and dendritic cell (DC) activation genes and costimulatory genes (CD80, CD86, OX40L, GITRL, CD40, and CD137L)26. Similarly, human skin tumors, that we intend to treat with phIL12 GET, are also highly immunogenic and are profoundly sensitive to immunotherapy27. Moreover, the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with a favorable ratio of CD4+ cells, and the presence of CD11c-positive cells were also demonstrated in BCC and SCC patient samples, which was also demonstrated in a CT26 tumor model26,28–30. Therefore, the CT26 tumor model and human nonmelanoma skin cancers (BCC and SCC) share a similar baseline immune profile, which is one of the fundamental tumor characteristics that mediates the response of tumors to immunotherapy. When non-clinical immunotherapy results from experimental murine models are applied to human cancers, translation of the results from models with similar immune profiles is more relevant than translation from histologically identical models, where the baseline immune profile differs greatly. Therefore, the CT26 tumor model was recognized as the most appropriate murine tumor model for our non-clinical evaluation, considering that there are no commercially available murine nonmelanoma skin cancer tumor models that can form tumors in immunocompetent mice.

The protocol of the presented non-clinical study was conducted according to Scientific Advice received from the Committee for Advanced Therapies (CAT) of the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and guidance from the Agency for Medicinal Products and Medical Devices of the Republic of Slovenia (JAZMP). The study was conducted under strict quality measures following good laboratory practices (GLPs). The primary focus of the data was on the safety of the new drug (in our case, the pmIL12/phIL12 plasmid delivered by GET) in relation to the dosage; therefore, it was agreed that the experimental groups lacked control groups with electric pulses alone and GET of the control noncoding plasmid. These control groups were studied in previous studies on different tumor models31–34. Both control groups were also studied in a CT26 tumor model, which was used in the present non-clinical study, where they induced minor antitumor effects compared with the therapeutic group GET with an IL-12-encoding plasmid34.

For electric pulse delivery, an electrical pulse generator CLINIPORATOR™ (IGEA S.p.A., Carpi, Italy) was used. This device was selected because it holds authorization for use in the clinical environment for gene therapy and electrochemotherapy. The electric pulse parameters (8 square wave electric pulses, a voltage-to-distance ratio of 1300 V/cm, a pulse duration of 100 μs, and a frequency of 5 kHz) used clinically in electrochemotherapy were used35,36. The 5 kHz pulse frequency is used for better patient compliance, as it causes less discomfort to the patient. Compared with gene therapy pulses comprising four trains of pulses, the high-voltage pulse parameters were demonstrated to be equally efficient for the transfection of CT26 tumors with a GFP-encoding plasmid, with each train comprising one high-voltage (HV) pulse and one low-voltage (LV) pulse (HV-LV)37,38. The choice of high-voltage pulse parameters was further supported by gene therapy studies performed with a similar plasmid encoding IL-12 (tavo) in the US. In these studies, the plasmid was delivered via a similar pulse protocol (6 electric pulses, voltage-to-distance ratio of 1300 V/cm, pulse duration of 100 μs)16,39. Furthermore, this pulse protocol is successfully used for the treatment of canine tumors via electrochemotherapy combined with IL-12 GET40.

The first part of the study was a proof-of-concept study. Pharmacodynamical evaluation was performed via confirmation of transgene expression and its mode of action. IL-12 stands out as one of the most potent proinflammatory cytokines involved in mediating the antitumor activity of immune cells. Although it has been extensively researched for its potential in cancer immunotherapy, IL-12 has the ability to engage multiple effector mechanisms and counteract tumor-induced immunosuppression3,41,42. Among its effects, IL-12 induces the activation and cytotoxicity of T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells, induces Th1 cell differentiation and hampers immunosuppressive cells such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) or myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)3. The majority of IL-12-induced effects are orchestrated through the secretion of interferon-gamma (IFNγ), which in turn exerts cytostatic and antiangiogenic activities, and elevates the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) I and MHC II on tumor cells, facilitating enhanced recognition and lysis. Therefore, in our study, antitumor effectiveness and immune cell infiltration were selected to confirm the mode of action of the transgene. The pmIL12 GET in mice demonstrated significant antitumor effectiveness, as supported by prolonged tumor growth delay and survival of the mice. The therapy also induced a local immune response, as immune cell infiltration was observed in the tumors and in the skin around the tumors shortly after the therapy, confirming the mode of action of the transgene. Similarly, a pronounced antitumor effect was demonstrated by Heller et al. during a non-clinical evaluation of a plasmid DNA encoding IL-12 with an antibiotic resistance gene, which is used in the USA39. However, their therapy involved repetitive sessions on days 1, 5 and 8 and was performed in B16F10 melanoma. In this study, only the infiltration of lymphocytes, an indicator of the mode of action of the IL-12 transgene, was determined. However, other studies on the CT26 tumor model as well as the melanoma model43,44 have already shown that there is an increase in the CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations following intratumoral gene electrotransfer of the IL-12-encoding plasmid. Furthermore, T-cell subpopulations following intratumoral gene electrotransfer of an IL-12-encoding plasmid were extensively studied by Shi et al. in a melanoma model via immunohistochemistry (IHC) and flow cytometry45. The pIL-12 GET resulted in significant expansion of the total number of CD3+ T cells, which included CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. Further characterization of the tumor microenvironment evaluated the presence of T regulatory cells (Tregs) and T cells positive for PD-1. There was a significant decrease in the percentage of PD-1-positive cells, both CD4+ and CD8+ cells, and Treg cells following treatment with pIL-12 GET. Consistent with the IHC results, the evaluation of cell populations by flow cytometry revealed a reduction in Treg cells among CD4+ T cells and PD-1 + T cells among CD4+ or CD8+ T cells after pIL-12 GET. Therefore, our results and previously published results show that the GET of plasmid DNA encoding IL-12 successfully induces the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells into tumors and their activation.

This pharmacokinetic study significantly contributes to the understanding of the safety profile of pmIL12 GET. In line with the European guidelines, understanding the biodistribution profile of a gene therapy product following in vivo administration is an important element of the nonclinical development that supports early-phase clinical trials in the target population. Evaluation of the biodistribution of pmIL2 in the blood was omitted from the analysis since it has been demonstrated that plasmid DNA rapidly degrades upon entering the bloodstream (within 15 min) and is undetectable after 1 hour46. Our study demonstrated that the pmIL12 plasmid was distributed throughout the body after GET, and the plasmid was present in 8 out of 10 tested organs/tissues 2 days after GET. However, after 14 days, it was cleared from the nontarget organs and remained only in the target organ, i.e., in the skin above and around the tumor where the GET was performed. However, the number of plasmid copies in the skin significantly decreased over time, demonstrating the elimination of the plasmid. Furthermore, special emphasis was placed on the assessment of the administered gene therapy product in the gonads of both sexes to minimize the risk of the transfer of the gene therapy product to the offspring. In our study, the plasmid persisted in the gonads until day 8 and was completely eliminated by day 14. This finding demonstrates an important safety aspect of this therapy, with minimal risk for the transfer of the transgene to the offspring.

The evaluation of systemic toxicity demonstrated the good safety profile of pmIL12 GET. The general well-being of the mice was maintained throughout the entire observation period. Furthermore, no evidence of systemic toxicity was detected in the blood or organs of the animals. Therefore, we concluded that single pmIL12 GET administration at all three doses tested is safe. A similar safety profile was demonstrated after repetitive administration of a plasmid encoding IL-12 into B16F10 tumors39.

There were several limitations of our study. One of the limitations was that human IL-12 is not biologically active in mice. Therefore, non-clinical evaluation could not be performed using the gene therapy drug phIL12, which encodes a human IL-12 cytokine. On the basis of scientific advice from the EMA, we had to construct a mouse ortholog of the plasmid phIL12 (pmIL12) to conduct a non-clinical evaluation in mice. The plasmids were identical, except for the transgene, which encoded mouse IL-12 in the case of the pmIL12 plasmid. Another limitation is the use of a CT26 mouse colon carcinoma model instead of basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) models (intended use of phIL12 GET). However, owing to the lack of relevant mouse BCC and cutaneous SCC tumor models, we selected a mouse model with a similar immune landscape, which is thought to be even more important in the evaluation of immunotherapeutic drugs as the origin of tumor cells. Furthermore, we could not use human cell lines for tumor induction, as they do not form tumors in immunocompetent mice, which is essential because of the mode of action of phIL12/pmIL12. Another limitation associated with the use of the CT26 tumor model was the lack of long-term observations and toxicity for up to 6 months because all of the mice reached the humane endpoint before that time. However, as genotoxicity studies are not considered essential to support exploratory clinical trials for therapeutics intended to treat patients with advanced cancer (EMA/CHMP/ICH/646107/2008), the current long-term observations that we were able to achieve in this study provide sufficient data to support an exploratory clinical trial in advanced cancer.

In conclusion, the in vivo evaluation demonstrated that pmIL12 GET produced the biologically active mIL-12 protein, which increased the infiltration of immune cells into the tumor and the skin above and around the tumor. This effect was accompanied by the antitumor effectiveness of pmIL12 GET, as the therapy induced a delay in tumor growth and prolonged mouse survival at all three tested doses. The plasmid was distributed throughout the body, and pmIL12 was present in all the analyzed organs/tissues except the eyes and lungs on day 2 post-GET. Fourteen days after GET, it was present only in the target organ, i.e., the skin above and around the tumor. Therapy did not cause any detectable systemic toxicity, and all the measured parameters were within the range of those of naïve and control mice. The results of this pharmacodynamic, pharmacokinetic and toxicological study demonstrated the safety and efficacy of phIL12 GET, and on the basis of these results, approval was obtained for the initiation of a phase I clinical study in BCC21.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental setting

6-week-old BALB/cAnNCrl mice were purchased from Charles River (Calco, Italy) and were kept in quarantine for 7 days in an individually ventilated cage system (IVC) upon arrival under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. During the experiments, the mice were kept on separate IVCs in cages. They were checked daily, and the water and food were changed twice per week. The temperature and humidity of the rooms were controlled and maintained between 20 and 24 °C and 55 ± 10% humidity. The experiments were approved by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Food of the Republic of Slovenia (U34401-6/2020/5) and followed the EU directive 2010/63/EU, ARRIVE and PREPARE guidelines.

After quarantine, the mice were shaved with clippers on one flank, and tumors were induced via the subcutaneous injection of 0.3 × 106 CT26 cells (CRL-2638™, ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) in 100 µL of saline with a syringe with a 27G needle. Tumor growth was followed daily by caliper measurements starting on day 7 after tumor induction until the tumors reached a volume between 60 and 70 mm3. The tumor volume was calculated with the following formula: a × b2 × π/6. Once the tumors reached 60–70 mm3, the mice were randomized into experimental groups.

The experiments were performed on both female and male mice. For each experimental condition and at each collection time point, except for long-term observation, the group consisted of 5 females and 5 males for organ/tissue sample collection and for blood collection for complete blood count analysis. Additionally, for each experimental condition and at each collection time point, 5 females and 5 males were included for serum collection. For long-term observation, the number of animals of each sex was 6 in each group, and 12 were used for tumor growth curves and determination of survival probability. Furthermore, for each experimental condition on day 8 post-GET, 5 females and 5 males were included for the analysis of IgG and IgM antibody quantity. An additional 18% of the mice of each sex were included to account for mice in which tumor induction was not successful or in which GET was poorly executed (current during treatment less than 1 A).

The treatment schedule, number of mice per condition and doses tested are given in Table 2 and Fig. 4. The mice were randomly assigned to 5 groups (Table 2): mice without tumors (naïve mice), control mice with tumors (CTRL), 0.5 mg/ml pmIL12 GET (pmIL12 low dose), 1 mg/ml pmIL12 GET (pmIL12 middle dose), and 2 mg/ml pmIL12 GET (pmIL12 high dose). The high dose in this setting represents the maximum feasible dose (MFD) for this product, which is limited by the plasmid production process, which is 2 mg/ml in saline. This represents a 4 × higher dose than the low dose. The other two doses were selected as twofold dilutions of the MFD, specifically, 1 mg/ml and 0.5 mg/ml. The choice of dose range and dispensing volume was further supported by data from clinical studies conducted in the USA, where gene electrotransfer of a similar drug product (tavokinogene telseplasmid; “tavo”) in phase II clinical trials employed a concentration of 0.5 mg/ml with a dispensing volume equivalent to one-fourth of the tumor volume47, a regime that was also used in our non-clinical study. The plasmid pmIL12 was delivered to tumors via GET on day 0. GET

The delivery of the plasmid pmIL12 by GET was extensively described in our previous studies19,20. The biological activity and potency of the batch of pmIL12 used were confirmed20. Prior to GET, the volume of each tumor was assessed, and subsequently, one quarter of the determined volume was designated the precise volume for the injection of the plasmid16. The plasmid pmIL12 was slowly (for 5 s) injected intratumorally with an insulin syringe. The application of electric pulses followed 5 min after the injection. An electrical pulse generator CLINIPORATOR™ (IGEA S.p.A., Carpi, Italy), which holds authorization for use in the clinical environment with a designated CE, was used to deliver the electric pulses. The electric pulse protocol included 8 square wave electric pulses, a voltage-to-distance ratio of 1300 V/cm, a pulse duration of 100 μs, and a frequency of 5 kHz. GET was performed via a stainless-steel plate electrodes with a 6 mm gap between them. This is the same electric pulse protocol that is used in the clinic for electrochemotherapy35. Good contact between the electrodes and the tumor was assured by applying a conductive gel to the electrodes. During the procedure, the mice were under 2% (v/v) isoflurane anesthesia (Isoflurane; Piramal Healthcare UK Limited, London, UK) delivered via VetFlo inhalation anesthesia (Kent Scientific Corporation, Torrington, CT, USA).

Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamic properties were evaluated by confirming transgene expression, determining antitumor effectiveness and evaluating immune cell infiltration as a measure of the action of the IL-12 transgene.

For determination of antitumor effectiveness, tumor diameters were measured three times per week, tumor volume was calculated with the formula described above, and tumor growth curves were constructed. The mice were euthanized when the tumor volume reached 1000 mm3, and this time point was considered the humane endpoint for the construction of Kaplan–Meier curves. Tumor growth delay was calculated as the difference in the tripling time of tumors between the control group and the treated group.

At the designated time points (Fig. 4), the tumor and skin above and around the tumor were collected. One half of each tumor sample was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tumor samples were crushed using a pestle, and RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following isolation with a PeqGOLD Total RNA Kit (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA). The RNA was transcribed into cDNA, and qRT‒PCR was used to determine the expression of the transgene IL-12 as previously described20.

The other halves of each tumor and skin sample were fixed in formalin (BD Pharmingen, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) overnight and embedded in paraffin. Two-micron-thick tumor sections were cut from each paraffin block and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and the degree of immune cell infiltration (granulocytes and lymphocytes), an indicator of the mode of action of the IL-12 transgene, was assessed by two independent pathologists blinded to the experimental groups. The degree of infiltration was scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating no infiltration and 3 indicating strong infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Pharmacokinetics

For determination of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution and persistence of the pmIL12 plasmid in several organs and tissues were evaluated. We conducted a qRT‒PCR test to quantify the number of plasmid DNA (pmIL12) copies present in various organs after pmIL12 GET into CT26 tumors. The collection points were 2, 8 and 14 days after pmIL12 GET into CT26 tumors (Fig. 4). Additionally, we quantified the number of plasmid DNA copies in the mice monitored beyond 14 days (long-term monitoring). The organs were in this case collected when the tumor volume reached 1000 mm3, which was our humane endpoint for animal euthanasia (Fig. 4). Multiple tissues and organs were collected during necropsy: the skin above and around the tumor, liver, gonads, heart, spleen, kidney, brain, inguinal lymph nodes, eyes and lungs. For paired organs, one was taken for pharmacokinetic analysis, and the other was taken for systemic toxicology ("Systemic toxicology" section). For the remaining organs, one half was taken for pharmacokinetic analysis, and the other half was taken for systemic toxicology ("Systemic toxicology" section). The tissue was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and crushed with a pestle. DNA was extracted, and the plasmid copy number was determined as previously described20.

The determination of the number of pmIL12 plasmid DNA copies in organs was carried out in the following sequence. First, organs/tissues collected on day 2 after pmIL12 GET in the high-dose group (2 mg/ml pmIL12) were analyzed, as were samples from all control (untreated) and naïve mice. To confirm the presence of pmIL12 plasmid DNA in a specific organ/tissue in the high-dose group, the middle-dose group was further analyzed (1 mg/ml pmIL12). If the presence of plasmid DNA was confirmed at this concentration, the samples in the low-dose group (0.5 mg/ml pmIL12) were analyzed. At the remaining time points, the same sequence of sample analysis at the remaining time points was followed.

Systemic toxicology

As an indicator of systemic toxicity, the weight of the mice and their behavior were assessed using the mouse grimace scale48. The general health of the mice was determined through examination of the coat and demeanor. A full macroscopic evaluation was performed, paying special attention to the general health of the mice and the presence of edema and skin irritation at the application site.

A full necropsy of the animal viscera and carcass and gross examination of all organs and tissues were performed. At the designated time points (Fig. 4), the other half of the same organs that were collected for the determination of plasmid copy number were fixed in formalin overnight and embedded in paraffin. Two-micron-thick tissue sections were cut from each paraffin block and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). HE-stained sections were carefully evaluated by two independent pathologists, who evaluated changes in the collected tissues. The evaluation was performed in the same order as for plasmid copy number determination, starting with the high dose of plasmid and day 2. When no specific pathological changes were detected in the group receiving the highest dose of the plasmid, the organs/tissues in the lower dose groups were not processed, and the samples were stored in paraffin at room temperature.

At designated time points (Fig. 4), blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus during inhalation anesthesia to determine changes in the complete blood count (CBC), white blood cell differential count (DIFF), biochemical parameters (BC) and IgG and IgM titers. For BC analysis, samples were thawed and analyzed via an RX DAYTONA + Chemistry Analyzer (Randox Laboratories Ltd., Crumlin, UK). Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis and graphical presentation. The normality of the distribution was determined via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Owing to the normal distribution, a two-tailed t test was used for determination of differences between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for determination of differences between three or more groups. To determine whether the proportion of pmIL12-positive organs/tissues was statistically significant, Fisher’s exact test was used. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
