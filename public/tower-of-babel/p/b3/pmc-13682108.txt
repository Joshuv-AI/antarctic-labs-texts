Ten-year longitudinal study of incarceration among a community-based cohort of people who inject drugs
Authors: Jocelyn Chan, Michael Curtis, Nico Clark, Matthew Hickman, Peter Higgs, Jocelyn Jones, Michael Livingston, Lisa Maher, Brendan Quinn, Ashleigh C Stewart, Jack Stone, James Trauer, Mark Stoové, Paul Dietze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople who inject drugs are over-represented among incarcerated populations. However, there are few studies elucidating patterns of incarceration among people who inject drugs, especially among community-based cohorts. Furthermore, data on drug use and injecting risk behaviours in carceral settings are limited globally. We aimed to describe lifetime patterns of incarceration, as well as drug use and injecting risk behaviours while incarcerated, among a cohort of people who inject drugs in Melbourne, Australia (SuperMIX), stratified by key demographics.Baseline and annual follow-up interviews conducted between 2008 and 2021 collected data on demographics, drug use, health service use and criminal justice system interactions from 1328 participants. We report a descriptive account of lifetime incarceration and incarceration episode characteristics (including duration and drug use). We also compared the health and social characteristics of people who inject drugs by self-reported incarceration history.ResultsWe found a high prevalence of incarceration, with 67% reporting a lifetime incarceration. Prevalence of recent incarceration remained above 20% across the 10-year follow-up. Participants with a history of incarceration showed higher rates of marginalisation and over-representation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. Nearly one-fifth of incarceration episodes involved injecting drug use, with a majority (75%) of these episodes involving syringe sharing while incarcerated.ConclusionsResults show minimal progress in reducing incarceration rates among people who inject drugs, especially Aboriginal people. Our findings point to the urgent need for evidence-based harm reduction programs in carceral settings, as well as broader policy reform to reduce the over-incarceration of people who use drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40352-026-00440-1.Keywords: Injecting drug use, Epidemiology, Incarceration, Cohort

Background

People who inject drugs are over-represented among incarcerated populations, owing in large part to the criminalisation of drug use (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), 2022; Baranyi et al., 2022). Globally, an estimated 58% of people who inject drugs have a history of incarceration (Degenhardt et al., 2023). In Australia, just over 1% of the population report ever having injected an illicit drug (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), 2024a), but almost one-third of people entering prison report a history of injecting drug use (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), 2022). For people who inject drugs, incarceration can increase risks of social, physical and mental health harms (Brinkley-Rubinstein, 2013). Incarceration has been associated with increases in injecting risk behaviour (Wood et al., 2005), and incident blood-borne viral infection both in prison and on release (Hajarizadeh et al., 2024; Stone et al., 2018, 2023). In addition, on release, people who inject drugs experience increased risk of poor mental health (Stewart et al., 2022), relapse to injecting drug use (Curtis et al., 2022; Stone et al., 2018), overdose (Curtis et al. 2023a, b, c; Keen et al. 2021; Winter et al. 2015), contact with emergency healthcare (Curtis et al. 2023a, b, c), and mortality (Borschmann et al., 2024; Hill et al., 2022), particularly immediately post-release (Merrall et al., 2010). Social harms associated with incarceration include homelessness and reduced employment prospects (Baldry et al., 2006; Connell et al., 2023). Incarceration can also have a negative impact on the family of the person incarcerated, affecting children’s development and their engagement in school, and can contribute to intergenerational inequalities (Herreros-Fraile et al., 2023).

While the overlap between injecting drug use and incarceration is well documented (Fazel et al., 2017), there are few studies elucidating patterns of incarceration among people who inject drugs, especially among community-based cohorts (Gjersing & Bretteville-Jensen, 2021). Australian work on the topic has been limited to cohorts of people who use drugs followed-up post-release or cohorts accessing treatment (Kirwan et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2021; Winter et al., 2019). One study that recruited incarcerated men who injected drugs found 22% were re-imprisoned within three months of release and 50% were re-imprisoned at least once within 24 months of release (Stewart et al., 2021), while another reported a reincarceration rate of 33.5 per 100 person-years (Winter et al., 2019). Similarly, in a cohort of men with a recent history of opioid use who were followed-up after release from prison, 90% were reincarcerated over a 10-year period, with a median of five imprisonment episodes per participant (Larney, Toson et al., 2012). Examination of incarceration in community-based cohorts is needed to improve generalisability of findings beyond those already engaged with treatment or prior to first incarceration episode.

The differential rates of incarceration across sociodemographic groups of people who inject drugs are a further area that remains poorly characterised. Gender inequality and racism intersect with other known social and ecological drivers of criminal justice involvement (Degenhardt et al., 2023), however studies that take an intersectional approach to describing patterns of incarceration among people who inject drugs are scarce. Among a cohort of people with opioid dependence in Australia, men and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people spent a substantially greater proportion of follow-up time in prison (23% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander men, 8% for non-Indigenous men, 8% for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women, and 2% for non-Indigenous women) (Degenhardt et al., 2014). These results reflect a longstanding pattern of over-incarceration for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people where, despite comprising only 4% of the Australian total population, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people account for 33% of the prison population (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2023). This disparity is even more marked for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, who represent only 7% of the Australian population but comprise 65% of youth in detention (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), 2024b). This occurs within a context of ongoing social exclusion stemming from colonisation, the dispossession of land, and decades of systemic and structural racism resulting in disruption of culture and kinship systems (Cuneen, 2022).

The drug use and injecting risk behaviours of people who inject drugs while they are incarcerated are similarly under-explored globally (Austin et al., 2023; Carpentier et al., 2018). Available studies from both Australian and international contexts, report reduced drug availability and use during incarceration but also increases in syringe sharing (among those injecting) (Culbert et al., 2015; Cunningham et al., 2018; Kinner et al., 2012). However, no studies have examined drug use and injecting behaviours in the youth detention system.

Taken together, these gaps highlight the need for research that captures incarceration patterns and associated risk behaviours across a broader and more representative population of people who inject drugs. Therefore, this study aimed to describe lifetime patterns of incarceration, as well as drug use and injecting risk behaviours while incarcerated, among a cohort of people who inject drugs in Australia, stratified by gender and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status. We also aimed to compare the health and social characteristics of people who inject drugs by self-reported incarceration history.

Methods

Setting and participants

The Melbourne Injecting Drug Use Cohort Study (SuperMIX) is an ongoing longitudinal cohort study in Melbourne, Australia, with annual follow-up of people with a history of injecting drugs. Initial enrolment occurred between 2008 and 2010, with ongoing recruitment to expand the cohort since 2017. Respondent driven and convenience sampling were implemented in street-based drug markets, needle and syringe programs, and primary healthcare services providing care to people who use drugs across Melbourne. Respondent driven sampling is a peer-referral sampling method designed to reach marginalised populations, making it well-suited for recruiting community-based cohorts of people who inject drugs (Columbia Mailman School of Public Health, 2026). To be eligible, participants were required to be residents of the state of Victoria, aged 18 years or older, have injected an illicit drug at least monthly in the previous six months, provide a valid Medicare (Australia’s universal health care system) number for health service data linkage, and be able to give informed consent. Illicit drug use, shortened to drug use throughout this manuscript, is broadly defined to include use of illegal drugs, non-medical use of pharmaceuticals, and the harmful use of other psychoactive substances (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), 2026). Annual follow-up was conducted via face-to-face or, during the COVID-19 pandemic period only, telephone interviews. Interviewer-administered questionnaires covered socio-demographic characteristics, drug use history, general health, health service utilisation and incarceration characteristics. Full details of the SuperMIX study are described elsewhere (Horyniak et al., 2013; Van Den Boom et al., 2021). Latest available data from participants recruited from April 2008 to November 2020 and followed until March 2021, were used for this analysis.

Variables

Outcome variables included any history of incarceration, including police cells, remand, prison and youth detention, which were documented at baseline and since last interview at each follow-up. Participants were asked to report details of up to four of their most recent incarceration episodes at baseline and follow-up interviews, including incarceration type, year of entry, duration of incarceration, and injecting drug use and syringe sharing while incarcerated.

Sociodemographic variables included gender (male/female or non-binary), Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander identity, housing status and employment status. Drug use variables included age at first injection, main drug used in the last month and alcohol use (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, AUDIT) (Conigrave et al., 1995). Health and health care utilisation variables included anxiety (Generalised Anxiety Disorder [GAD-7]) and depression (Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ-9]) (Stocker et al., 2021) scores, whether ever tested for hepatitis C (HCV), self-reported HCV status, history of non-fatal opioid overdose, current opioid agonist treatment (OAT) and general practitioner (GP) attendance for reasons other than OAT prescription in the past 12 months. See Supplementary Table 1 for detailed list of variables including relevant cut-points for categorical variables.

Statistical analysis

We summarised the socio-demographic, drug use, and health characteristics of participants stratified by self-reported incarceration history at baseline. We also described incarceration experiences (reported either at baseline or at follow-up) by gender and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status separately – this included duration and type of incarceration, as well as risk behaviours per incarceration episode (injecting drug use and syringe sharing). We presented continuous variables as median and interquartile range and categorical variables with number and proportion. Differences in characteristics between people with and without a history of incarceration at baseline were tested using Pearson’s chi square test for categorical variables or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables.

We calculated the annual prevalence of participants reporting an episode of incarceration since last review at each follow-up interview over time (i.e., after the baseline interview). The number of people who reported at least one episode of incarceration in a year was divided by the number of people who completed a follow-up interview. Annual prevalence was stratified by gender and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status separately. We evaluated for trend using the chi-squared test for trend in proportions. The pooled prevalence of incarceration since last interview over the study period was compared by gender and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander status using Pearson’s chi-squared test.

Results

Incarceration data was available for 1307 of 1328 SuperMIX participants. The median time between interviews was 372 days (interquartile range 328–462 days). The majority reported a history of incarceration at baseline interview (n = 872/1307, 66.7%) (Table 1). Overall, 37.2% (n = 486) of participants did not complete a follow-up interview within two years of baseline. A history of incarceration was associated with greater loss to follow-up (39.9% vs. 31.7%, p = 0.004), as were male gender, unstable accommodation, amphetamine use as main drug of choice and not being currently on OAT (all p < 0.05). Participant characteristics by incarceration status

Compared to people who had not experienced incarceration at baseline, those who did were more likely to be male (74.9 vs. 53.6%; p < 0.01) and Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander (14.7 vs. 6.9% not Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander p > 0.01), and had an older age distribution (p < 0.01) (Table 1). Individuals with histories of incarceration reported greater socio-economic disadvantage, including lower educational attainment (40.2 vs. 20.7% completed ≤ 9 years of education; p < 0.01), and higher rates of unemployment (91.4 vs. 83.4%; p < 0.01) and unstable accommodation (35.5 vs. 27.0%; p < 0.01) at baseline interview. Patterns of substance use, including the main drug used in the last month and prevalence of risky alcohol use (AUDIT score ≥ 8) (Conigrave et al., 1995), were similar between the two groups; however, people who had experienced incarceration reported a slightly younger age at first injection of drugs (17 vs. 18 years; p < 0.01).

Incarceration experiences

Nine hundred and ten participants provided details for 2260 incarceration episodes across both baseline and follow-up interviews. Women were slightly younger at first reported incarceration (26 vs. 28 years; p < 0.01) and a greater percentage of women reported only one episode of incarceration (27.7% vs. 18.1%; p p < 0.01), while more men reported four or more episodes of incarceration (31.3% vs. 16.5%; p < 0.01). Women reported a shorter duration of incarceration across reported incarceration episodes (median 1.4 vs. 4.1 months; p < 0.01). A greater percentage of incarceration episodes did not proceed beyond police cells for women compared to men (26.4% vs. 17.5%; p < 0.01). Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people reported an older median age at first incarceration (29 vs. 27 years, p = 0.01) but longer median duration of incarceration (4.1 vs. 3.0 months, p < 0.01). A greater percentage of incarceration episodes were in prisons for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people (72.2% vs. 62.9%, p = 0.01).

Injecting drug use was reported in a substantial minority of incarceration episodes (19.4%, Table 2), of which most episodes (75.2%) involved syringe sharing. Injecting drug use was more common in prison (n = 375, 26.5%) and juvenile justice settings (n = 18, 21.7%), compared to remand centres (n = 33, 12.1%) and police cells (n < 5, < 1%) (Supplementary Table 2). The percentage of incarceration episodes involving injecting drug use increased with duration of incarceration, reaching 50.8% of people incarcerated 36 months or more. Sharing of injecting equipment was highest in prison and remand centres and universally high regardless of duration of incarceration (Supplementary Table 2). Heroin was the most commonly injected drug while incarcerated (n = 255, 60.0%), followed by buprenorphine-naloxone (n = 111, 24.2%), buprenorphine (n = 103, 24.2%), methamphetamine (n = 86, 20.2%), and methadone (n = < 5, < 1%). Heroin use has declined over time as buprenorphine-naloxone has increased (Supplementary Table 3). Men were more likely to inject in custody compared to women (21.0% vs. 13.6%; p < 0.001). The percentage of people reporting injecting drug use in custody was similar among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people (20.7%vs. 19.2, p = 0.57). Reports of syringe sharing (75.2% of episodes involving injecting drug use) were frequent among all groups (Table 2).

Incarceration trends over time

The annual percentage of participants reporting an episode of incarceration since last interview ranged from 21 to 35% during the study period. No statistically significant change in trend was observed in the percentage of participants reporting an episode of incarceration since last interview for both men (p = 0.98) and women (p = 0.87) (Fig. 1a), which ranged from 24 to 36% for men and from 13 to 33% for women. Between 2009 and 2022, the percentage of men reporting incarceration since last interview was significantly higher than for women (30.5 vs. 21.0%, p < 0.01). The percentage of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people reporting incarceration since last interview was significantly higher than for non-Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander people from 2009 to 2022 (42.1 vs. 25.9%, p < 0.01). While the percentage of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people reporting an episode of incarceration since last interview increased from 33% in 2010 to 55% in 2022, this trend was not statistically significant (p = 0.61 (Fig. 1b). Over time, the duration of incarceration decreased (6 month median duration of incarceration episodes before 2000 and 3 month median duration for episodes after 2015). Consistent with this result, a greater percentage of incarceration episodes were located in police cells over time (15% of episodes before 2000 and 21% of episodes after 2015). Discussion

Among a cohort of people who inject drugs, 67% reported a lifetime history of incarceration at recruitment and 27% reported an incarceration episode since last interview, with no detectable trend over ten years of follow-up. These prevalences are markedly high, consistent with global estimates of 58.4% (95% CI 52.0–64.8) and data from comparable settings, including Canada (59% lifetime, ~ 30% recent) (Milloy et al., 2008) and Norway (45% over a five-year period) (Gjersing & Bretteville-Jensen, 2021). Lower incarceration rates were reported in an Australian cohort of people treated for opioid dependence in New South Wales − 37% had at least one episode of incarceration over a 12-year period, including 14% in any given year (Degenhardt et al., 2014). These figures contrast against substantially lower community rates of incarceration, underscoring the disproportionately high risk of incarceration among people who inject drugs. In Australia, the national imprisonment rate in 2025 was 214 per 100,000 adults in June 2023, representing less than 1% of the national population, with Victoria’s rate being 113 per 100,000 adults (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2025).

Consistent with previous reports, we identified high rates of re-incarceration, such that most people with a history of incarceration reported multiple incarceration episodes. (Degenhardt et al., 2014) Others have shown that the high rates of incarceration, characterised by short and repeated episodes, seen in our cohort are associated with substantial health and social harms in the period post-release from prison in the context of typically rapid returns to injecting drug use (Stewart et al., 2021). These findings highlight the importance of transitional care post-release from prison to reduce harm for people who inject drugs (Macdonald et al., 2024). Peer support represent a promising intervention for this transitional period, with a recent scoping review identifying positive post-release outcomes (Treitler et al., 2025).

Also consistent with previous reports, we found key differences in the baseline characteristics of participants who had experienced incarceration – with an over-representation of markers of marginalisation among those who had been incarcerated (Gjersing & Bretteville-Jensen, 2021). Those who had been incarcerated were twice as likely to have less than nine years of education and started injecting drugs at an earlier age.

The high rates of incarceration and re-incarceration in this cohort of people who inject drugs should be interpreted in the context of an ongoing reliance on a criminal justice approach to drug use in Australia. Despite Australia’s current National Drug Strategy 2017–2026 outlining a commitment to harm minimisation through “balanced adoption of effective demand, supply and harm reduction strategies” (Commonwealth of Australia (Department of Health), 2017). Australian drug policy remains heavily weighted towards law enforcement approaches with nearly 65% ($3.5 billion) of the 2021/2022 budget spent on legal countermeasures (Ritter et al., 2024). Consideration of drug law reform is needed, including removal of criminal penalties for drug possession and non-custodial sentences for non-violent drug-related offences, given the Johns-Hopkins-Lancet Commission on Drug Policy and Health (Hayhurst et al., 2019; Kearley & Gottfredson, 2020; Mitchell et al., 2012) found no evidence that the threat of incarceration is an effective deterrent against drug use and strong evidence of considerable harm (Csete et al., 2016).

As expected, we found higher rates of incarceration among men and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people who inject drugs. Our results are consistent with broader community trends showing rates of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander incarceration, already disproportionally high, are increasing, while incarceration among non-Indigenous Australians has been steady or decreasing since 2019 (Productivity Commission, 2024a). There has been no progress towards the Closing the Gap target of reducing the prevalence of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander young people in detention by at least 30% by 2031. (Productivity Commission, 2024a)

The over-incarceration of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people is rooted in colonisation and its legacies of intergenerational trauma and socioeconomic disadvantage, as well as ongoing systemic racism within the carceral system (Yoorrook Justice Commission, 2023). Recommendations to address this issue consistently call for the transfer of decision-making power and resources to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in line with their right to self-determination under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous People (Productivity Commission, 2024a, 2024b; Yoorrook Justice Commission, 2023). Specific reforms recommended by the Yoorrook Justice Commission, Victoria’s truth-telling commission, include the establishment of a new independent police oversight authority, bail law reform to ensure remand is used as a last resort and introduction of legislation to raise the minimum age of criminal responsibility (Yoorrook Justice Commission, 2023). Research also indicates in-reach services from Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisations (ACCHOs) may improve health care delivery to Aboriginal people in prison and facilitate continuity of care on release (Pettit et al., 2019).

Nearly one-fifth of incarceration episodes involved injecting drug use, rising to 50.8% among those incarcerated for 36 months or more, with the majority (75%) of these episodes involving syringe sharing. Previous studies have also reported reductions in injecting drug use but increases in needle sharing while in prison (Carpentier et al., 2018; Cunningham et al., 2018); however, ours is the first to show that, concerningly, this pattern of drug use is also present within the juvenile justice system. The high rates of custodial injecting drug use and needle sharing in this cohort highlight an urgent need to expand harm reduction interventions in prison settings. Strong evidence exists for the use of OAT to reduce injecting drug use, syringe sharing and mortality among people with opioid dependence in prison and on release; additionally, although the evidence is weaker, OAT has also been associated with reductions in recidivism (Hedrich et al., 2012; Larney et al., 2012; Larney, 2010; Macdonald et al., 2024). However, access to, and post-release retention in, OAT remains suboptimal (Curtis et al. 2023a, b, c; Marshall et al. 2023). Key recommendations from a recent Australian consensus statement include prompt recognition and treatment of opioid withdrawal and support for OAT continuation on release (Chan et al., 2025). There is also evidence that prison-based take-home naloxone programs are both effective in reducing the risk of opioid overdose (Bird et al., 2016) and are acceptable to people who inject drugs (Curtis et al., 2018), supporting the expansion of such programs in Australian correctional facilities. Furthermore, despite ample evidence of the harm reduction effectiveness of needle and syringe programs (NSP), and strong advocacy, and demonstrated cost-saving preventive health benefits of prison NSP (Chaillon et al., 2022; Houdroge et al., 2024, 2025), there are currently no formal NSPs operating in any Australian prisons and globally there were only 11 countries where NSP was available in at least one prison in 2024 (Harm Reduction in Prisons Working Group, 2023; Harm Reduction International, 2024). Needle and syringe programs are particularly important for people who use drugs other than opioids and for whom OAT is not indicated; amongst a sample of men who regularly injected drugs recruited from Victorian prisons found crystal methamphetamine was the most commonly injected drug both in the month prior to, and during, imprisonment (Kirwan et al., 2019).

A key strength of this study is the availability of longitudinal data for a community-based cohort of people who inject drugs over a 10-year period. However, our study had several limitations. Firstly, incarceration data were drawn from self-report for up to four carceral episodes – this may result in under-reporting of episodes, particularly for shorter episodes of police custody or juvenile detention, or inaccuracies around timelines or characteristics of historical episodes of incarceration. Future planned work includes linkage with administrative custodial data which will allow robust calculation of incarceration rates. (Van Den Boom et al., 2021) Furthermore, while our study reported on prevalence of injecting drug use in prison, we did not have important contextual data on OAT in prison. Also, prevalence of injecting drug use in prison was obtained retrospectively from a community-based sample, with participants reporting in-prison injecting across an approximately 12-month recall period rather than concurrently while incarcerated, which may introduce selection bias and recall bias. Loss to follow-up is common among research with people who use drugs (37.2% did not complete a follow-up interview within two years of the baseline interview in this cohort) and may introduce attrition bias. Detailed analyses of attrition in this cohort have been reported elsewhere. (Abdelsalam et al., 2024) In summary, disproportionately higher attrition among more disadvantaged participants and those with a history of incarceration, suggests the burden of incarceration in this cohort may be underestimated. Lastly, our findings represent an urban Australian cohort and are time and settings dependent. Outcomes, including in-prison injecting behaviours, may not be generalisable to rural, regional or international settings given variations in population profile, drug availability and custodial contexts.

Conclusions

Our results indicate little progress in addressing the high rates of incarceration over this period for non-Aboriginal and particularly, Aboriginal people, who inject drugs. While existing evidence indicates a strong association between incarceration and poor health outcomes, further research is needed to strengthen causal interpretation of the effects of incarceration. Among those incarcerated, a substantial proportion reported both injecting drug use and syringe sharing while incarcerated. Further research is also needed on interventions, policies and practices that can reduce incarceration and/or mitigate further harm for this population.
