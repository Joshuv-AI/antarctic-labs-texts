Structure-Function Correlation of Virtual Reality and Standard Perimetry With Peripapillary RNFL OCT in Glaucoma Patients
Authors: Maral Namdari, Bryce Baugh, Bangyan Zhang, Jacob Bell, Matthew Vierkant, Catherine Johnson, Michael Seth Jensen, Gregory Barashyan, Kasper Koblanski, Jens Nilson, Chaorong Wu, Norman Zabriskie, Rachel Simpson, Iqbal Ike K. Ahmed, Brian C. Stagg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the structure–function relationship of a virtual reality–based visual field test (Radius XR) with standard automated perimetry using either the SITA Fast or SITA Faster strategy, based on peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pp-RNFL) thickness measured by spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT).MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 116 eyes from 71 patients with glaucoma or suspected glaucoma. Participants underwent 24-2 visual field testing with Radius XR (RATA Fast) and a single standard automated perimetry strategy (either SITA Fast or SITA Faster). Peripapillary RNFL thickness was measured with SD-OCT within six months. Global and sectoral structure–function relationships were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models, with visual field locations mapped to corresponding peripapillary RNFL sectors based on the Garway–Heath map.ResultsStructure–function associations between global pp-RNFL thickness and mean deviation were observed across testing modalities, with marginal R² values of 0.36 for Radius XR (RATA Fast), 0.43 for SITA Fast, and 0.29 for SITA Faster derived from linear mixed-effects models. Sectoral analyses demonstrated the strongest associations in the supratemporal and inferotemporal regions across all modalities.ConclusionsRadius XR achieved structure–function correlations comparable to standard automated perimetry, particularly in clinically relevant RNFL sectors, supporting its role in glaucoma assessment.Translational RelevanceVirtual reality–based perimetry provides structure–function relationships consistent with standard perimetry while improving accessibility, facilitating broader integration of functional testing into clinical and community-based glaucoma care.

Introduction

Standard automated perimetry (SAP) remains the gold standard for detecting and monitoring visual field (VF) loss in glaucoma.1–3 The Humphrey Field Analyzer (HFA; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA) is the most widely used SAP platform, with white-on-white threshold testing serving as the clinical benchmark.4 The Swedish Interactive Thresholding Algorithm (SITA) improved testing efficiency by incorporating Bayesian inference, reducing test duration while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.1,5 Despite these advances, SAP is limited by its high cost, large footprint, and requirement for trained personnel. Testing also requires patients to maintain a fixed seated position and sustained visual attention, which can lead to discomfort and decreased reliability, particularly among elderly or mobility-limited patients.6–11

Virtual reality perimetry has emerged as a portable alternative for both in-office and remote testing.10–13 The Radius XR (Inspire model; Radius XR Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA), a U.S. Food and Drug Administration–registered device, features a lightweight headset design and delivers threshold-based VF assessments under standardized photopic conditions.11 Importantly, the multi-center NOVA trial validated the Radius XR, demonstrating that the RATA-Standard algorithm provides VF sensitivity measurements and glaucoma staging outcomes highly comparable to HFA SITA-Standard, with excellent agreement in disease severity classification and strong correlation in mean deviation.11

Beyond functional loss detected by VF testing, glaucoma is marked by structural damage to the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and optic nerve head (ONH). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) enables objective assessment of these changes, and establishing reliable structure-function (SF) correlations between RNFL thickness and visual sensitivity is essential for accurate staging and early detection.14–16 Numerous studies have examined the SF relationship in glaucoma, often mapping VF regions to specific sectors of the optic disc.16–22 Garway-Heath et al.23 provided a widely adopted mapping framework, whereas Hood and Kardon17 proposed a linear SF model that links RNFL thickness to VF sensitivity and accounts for residual “non-neural” RNFL in advanced disease caused by glial and vascular components. These frameworks improve the precision of SF correlation analyses and enhance clinical interpretation.

Although the NOVA trial validated the accuracy of Radius XR against HFA, its ability to capture SF relationships, particularly with OCT-derived RNFL parameters, remains unexplored. In this study, we compared global and sectoral SF relationships between Radius XR, SAP (HFA SITA Fast and Faster), and SD-OCT-derived RNFL measurements, applying the Garway-Heath mapping and Hood-Kardon linear models. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to investigate the SF correlation of a VR-based perimeter with both standard perimetry and OCT, aiming to extend the clinical utility of Radius XR in glaucoma care.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study included 116 eyes from glaucoma or glaucoma suspect patients, recruited between October 2023 and April 2024 from four University of Utah Moran Eye Center clinics: the main campus, Farmington, Redwood, and Redstone Health Centers. Participants were adults aged 18 years or older scheduled for a 24-2 SITA Fast or SITA Faster Humphrey VF Test as part of routine clinical care. Eligibility was confirmed through medical record review by the principal or sub-investigators. Demographic data, visual acuity, best-corrected visual acuity, and refraction were recorded. Glaucoma severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson (HPA) criteria, as detailed in Table 1. Although glaucoma severity was classified according to HPA criteria, SF analyses were performed on the pooled dataset to maximize statistical power and minimize bias from small subgroup sizes.

Inclusion criteria required best-corrected visual acuity of 20/60 or better, spherical refractive error between −6.00 and +5.00 diopters, and cylinder correction within ±2.0 diopters. Exclusion criteria included significant physical limitations (e.g., restricted spinal mobility, severe arthritis) affecting VF testing; current eye infection; recent ocular surgery within the past three months; hearing impairments interfering with headset use; medications affecting vision or reaction time; and concurrent retinal diseases, non-glaucomatous optic neuropathies, or secondary glaucoma.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Utah and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

The Functional and Structural Measures

VF Testing

VF testing was conducted sequentially on the same day using the Radius VR-based test (Radius XR, RATA-Fast strategy) and the HFA (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) with either SITA-Fast or SITA-Faster protocol, each utilizing the 24-2 pattern. To reduce potential order effects, the testing sequence was randomized. All examinations were administered by experienced technicians, and standardized instructions were provided prior to testing. The Radius XR protocol includes a built-in 30-second practice session as part of its standard testing workflow to familiarize patients with the VR interface. HFA testing was preceded by routine technician instruction consistent with standard clinical practice. VFs were considered reliable based on SAP–derived criteria (fixation losses <25%, false positives <25%, and false negatives <30%).24,25 Eyes not meeting these criteria were excluded, and the corresponding Radius XR examinations from the same eyes were also excluded to maintain consistency across modalities.

HFA

Testing used the SITA Fast or SITA Faster algorithm on the 24-2 grid, which samples 54 locations at 6° intervals. A Goldmann III white stimulus was presented on a 10 cd/m² background, and threshold sensitivities were estimated using adaptive Bayesian staircase procedures. The measurable range extended from 0 to 50 dB.

Radius XR VF Test

The Radius XR is a US Food and Drug Administration–registered VR perimeter with a lightweight 170g headset, dual 1600 × 1600 liquid crystal displays, a 60 Hz refresh rate, and a 100° field of view (Fig. 1). Real-time monitoring was facilitated by a Samsung Galaxy Tab Active 3 (Samsung Electronics, Suwon-si, South Korea), with patients signaling stimulus detection via a handheld Bluetooth controller. Unlike HFA, the Radius XR includes a focus lever for refractive adjustment, eliminating the need for corrective lens.

The Radius XR headset, controller, and tablet used for testing.

Testing uses a photopic background luminance of 10 cd/m², consistent with HFA standards, and measures sensitivities within a 15–40 dB range, whereas HFA covers a broader 0–50 dB range. The RATA-Standard and RATA-Fast algorithms, analogous to HFA's SITA Standard and SITA Fast, present Goldmann III stimuli across 54 test points, using a staircase method with Bayesian updating. Responses are fitted to a Weibull function, concluding at each location once predefined error thresholds are met. Fixation monitoring is achieved via blind spot stimulus presentation, compensating for the absence of eye-tracking, and false-positive and false-negative rates are tracked to ensure reliability. The mean test duration for the RATA-Fast protocol in this study was approximately 3.6 ± 0.3 minutes.

A Structural Measurements: SD-OCT and PP-RNFL Thickness

PP-RNFL thickness was assessed using 3.5 mm circular OCT scans centered on the optic nerve head, obtained within six months of VF testing. Scans were acquired using a 12° diameter protocol on the HEYEX 2 platform (Heidelberg Eye Explorer; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Automated real-time averaging was used to enhance image quality. Only high-quality images with no segmentation artifacts and a quality score ≥20 were included in the analysis. Global and sectoral RNFL thicknesses were evaluated across the temporal, superior-temporal, inferior-temporal, nasal, superior-nasal, and inferior-nasal optic nerve head regions.

Structure-Function Mapping

The Garway-Heath Map for Correlating Field and Optic Disc Locations

For both VR-based and SAP testing, VF locations were anatomically mapped to ONH sectors using the Garway-Heath map (Fig. 2), which divides the ONH into six regions based on known SF relationships. Sectors corresponding to the nasal disc lie largely outside the 24-2 field, while temporal disc sectors fall fully within it. Specifically, the macular region corresponds to the temporal disc sector (311°–40°), the superior arcuate VF to the inferior temporal disc sector (271°–310°), and the inferior arcuate VF to the superior temporal disc sector (41°–80°).

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographic characteristics, including age, gender, and race, were summarized. Continuous variables, such as age, visual field test duration, and RNFL thickness, are reported as means with standard deviations, whereas categorical variables, including gender and ethnicity, are presented as frequencies and percentages. To investigate SF relationships, linear mixed-effects regression models were performed between RNFL thickness and visual sensitivity in both linear and decibel (dB) scales, with results reported as marginal R² values. Because both eyes from some participants were included, inter-eye correlation was addressed by incorporating a random intercept for patient. Ocular laterality was included as a fixed-effect.

For each eye, the average visual sensitivity in each Garway-Heath sector was calculated by first converting dB values to a linear scale using the Lambert transformation: 1/Lambert=100.1×Db

The linear values were averaged within each visual field sector to correspond with RNFL thickness measurements, and these averaged sensitivities were used in both linear and dB scales for analysis. The Hood and Kardon model was used for sectoral analysis to quantify the structure-function relationship between RNFL thickness and visual field threshold sensitivity. This approach allowed us to analyze the relationship between RNFL thickness and threshold sensitivity (dB) in each sector, with results presented in Figure 3, as detailed in Supplemental Methods.

For global visual field indexes, including mean deviation (MD), visual field index (VFI), and pattern standard deviation (PSD), paired associations between RATA-Fast and SAP were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficients. To account for inter-eye correlation, we used patient level bootstrapping with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Heatmaps based on Garway-Heath sectorial mapping were also created to visualize PP-RNFL correlations with 24-2 visual field data for all three testing protocols. Furthermore, difference maps were generated to compare the structural-functional relationships between RATA and SITA Fast, as well as RATA and SITA Faster, highlighting discrepancies in RNFL thickness correlations across these modalities (Fig. 4). Additionally, Figure 5 presents scatter plots illustrating the relationship between global RNFL thickness and MD for each perimetry protocol (RATA Fast, SITA Fast, and SITA Faster). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). A two-sided P value < 0.01 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 116 eyes from 71 patients were included in the analysis. All eyes underwent 24-2 RATA-Fast testing, and each eye was additionally evaluated using either the SITA Fast (n = 66) or SITA Faster (n = 50) protocol. Six eyes were excluded because of insufficient OCT image quality, defined as a quality score below 20. In addition, 20 eyes were excluded because of unreliable SITA examinations based on predefined reliability criteria (fixation losses ≥25%, false positives ≥25%, or false negatives ≥30%), and the corresponding RATA examinations from these eyes were also excluded from analysis.

Table 1 shows the baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the study sample. The mean age of participants was 68.3 ± 14.7 years, with 49% males and 51% females. Mean spherical refractive error was −1.3 ± 1.6 D, and mean cylindrical error was 0.6 ± 0.5 D. Based on HPA criteria, glaucoma severity was classified as mild in 38.0%, moderate in 19.0%, and severe in 8.0%. Additionally, 35.0% of eyes were classified as glaucoma suspects.

Descriptive functional and structural data across the three protocols are summarized in Table 2. MD values were −3.5 ± 3.0 dB for RATA Fast, −3.9 ± 6.1 dB for SITA Fast, and −3.9 ± 4.8 dB for SITA Faster. VFI values were 85.9% ± 7.6% for RATA Fast, 88.4% ± 15.7% for SITA Fast, and 90.0% ± 13.0% for SITA Faster. PSD values were 3.0 ± 1.5 dB, 4.5 ± 3.3 dB, and 4.6 ± 4.1 dB for RATA Fast, SITA Fast, and SITA Faster, respectively. Bootstrap Spearman correlations were calculated for paired global VF indexes, demonstrating strong associations between RATA-Fast and the corresponding SITA strategy (either SITA Fast or SITA Faster) (all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S1). Test durations varied among protocols, with SITA Faster demonstrating the shortest average duration (2.9 ± 0.7 minutes), which was approximately 25.6% shorter than SITA Fast, P < 0.01. RATA Fast had a mean test duration of 3.6 ± 0.3 minutes, which was 7.7% shorter than SITA Fast, P = 0.12. In terms of RNFL thickness, global mean values were similar across the protocols, with RATA Fast (74.1 ± 18.0 µm), SITA Fast (73.6 ± 17.8 µm), and SITA Faster (74.8 ± 18.7 µm). Sectoral RNFL thickness measurements showed variability, with the superotemporal and inferotemporal quadrants consistently exhibiting the highest mean thicknesses across all protocols.

Discussion

We investigated the SF relationships between pp-RNFL thickness measured by SD-OCT and VF threshold sensitivities obtained from VR-based perimetry (RATA Fast) and SAP (SITA Fast and SITA Faster). The analysis was performed at both sectoral and global levels, with test locations mapped using the Garway-Heath grid. The Radius XR device has already undergone clinical validation for glaucoma staging in the NOVA study,11 which confirmed its diagnostic reliability compared to SAP.

Our previous studies showed that patient-reported experience favored Radius XR over HFA, as measured with the VFPEQ,26 and that RadiusXR RATA-Fast achieved comparable functional outcomes to SITA-Fast and SITA-Faster.27 Although prior studies have predominantly focused on functional outcomes when comparing virtual reality perimetry with SAP,9–12 this study is the first, to our knowledge, to evaluate SF correlations between these perimetric protocols.

In our study, sectoral SF associations demonstrated regional variation across protocols, with the superotemporal and inferotemporal sectors exhibiting the highest R² values. In the superotemporal sector, R² values were 0.67 for SITA Fast, 0.54 for RATA Fast, and 0.44 for SITA Faster. In the inferotemporal sector, the corresponding values were 0.56, 0.51, and 0.37, respectively. VF sensitivities were analyzed using both linear and logarithmic scales to assess SF relationships. Comparable associations were observed when visual sensitivity was expressed on both linear and decibel scales, with modestly stronger coefficients noted with linear scaling. For example, in the ST sector, linear scaling yielded R² values of 0.67 for SITA Fast, 0.54 for RATA Fast, and 0.44 for SITA Faster, compared to 0.65 0.46, and 0.40, respectively, on the decibel scale. These findings align with Garway-Heath et al.,18,28 who reported that linear scaling better defines the SF relationship. Bowd et al.19 similarly demonstrated that although logarithmic fits are valid, they are generally not superior to linear fits when VF sensitivity is expressed in decibels, supporting the robustness of linear models for such analyses. Figure 3 demonstrates a floor effect in advanced disease, whereby the SF relationship becomes attenuated at low levels of VF threshold sensitivity. This pattern is consistent with RNFL-based broken-stick SF studies reporting weakening of the SF relationship once functional measurements reach low sensitivity levels.29–31 A recent study demonstrated that VF locations with sensitivities near or below 20 dB exhibit markedly increased variability over time, limiting their capacity to reflect meaningful change.31

Our findings align with prior studies evaluating SF relationships in glaucoma using SD-OCT-derived RNFL measurements, demonstrating consistent associations across key sectors such as ST and IT regions.15–18,32–35 Rao et al.15 demonstrated that the IT and ST sectors exhibited the strongest associations with VF sensitivity, emphasizing the susceptibility of arcuate fiber bundles to glaucomatous damage. Similar patterns were reported by Miglior et al.,36 and Bowd et al.,19 with R² values ranging from 0.33 to 0.61, depending on disease severity, measurement scales, and patient populations. A recent study on glaucoma progression37 further supports the diagnostic relevance of these regions, showing that IT and ST RNFL sectors exhibited the fastest rates of thinning in patients with moderate to advanced glaucoma.37 Additionally, Nassiri et al.38 observed moderate correlations (r = 0.60) between neuroretinal rim thinning in the superonasal sector and corresponding VF regions, indicating sector-specific differences in SF relationships. Strong associations observed in these regions across protocols underscore their clinical importance in monitoring glaucoma progression and reinforce the value of evaluating RNFL thickness for detecting structural change in early to moderate disease.

Lower SF associations were observed in the temporal and nasal sectors across testing modalities. In the linear scale, R² values for temporal and nasal sectors were 0.18 and 0.10 for RATA Fast, 0.27 and 0.15 for SITA Fast, and 0.03 and 0.01 for SITA Faster. In SITA Faster, associations in these regions were weak and did not reach statistical significance. The relatively thinner RNFL in the temporal region and the known challenges in reliably assessing peripheral visual field measurements may partly contribute to these findings. The inferonasal and superonasal sectors exhibited moderate associations across protocols. These sectoral differences may reflect anatomical variability in RNFL distribution and regional differences in visual field sensitivity.

Associations between global RNFL thickness and functional indices (MD and VFI) were observed across protocols. Global RNFL thickness was associated with MD with R² values of 0.43 for SITA Fast, 0.36 for RATA Fast, and 0.29 for SITA Faster. Similarly, R² values for VFI were 0.39, 0.33, and 0.24, respectively. These findings indicate consistent global SF associations across testing strategies.

These findings are consistent with the NOVA clinical validation trial, which reported a strong correlation (r = 0.94) between MD values obtained from SITA-Standard and the RATA-Standard algorithm,11 supporting the potential of VR-based perimetry to approximate conventional SAP testing.11 In the present study, RATA Fast demonstrated significant correlations with MD obtained from SITA Faster (ρ = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.62–0.96; P < 0.001) and SITA Fast (ρ = 0.71; 95% CI, 0.51–0.87; P < 0.001). The lower magnitude of correlation relative to NOVA likely reflects differences in testing strategy, normative databases, and study population characteristics. Similar associations were observed for VFI, with significant correlations between RATA Fast and SITA Faster (ρ = 0.89; 95% CI, 0.73–0.96; P < 0.001) and between RATA Fast and SITA Fast (ρ = 0.81; 95% CI, 0.66–0.89; P < 0.001). In our study, RATA demonstrated moderate correlations with RNFL thickness (R² = 0.33 globally), comparable in magnitude to those observed with SITA Fast (R² = 0.39) and SITA Faster (R² = 0.24). Notably, VFI's design to emphasize central visual field sensitivity may contribute to the variability in correlations observed across protocols. These findings suggest that whereas RATA shows promise as a VR-based perimeter, further refinement of its algorithms and expansion of its normative database are needed to enhance its clinical utility for glaucoma management.

This study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations. The modest sample size and cross-sectional design limited evaluation across the full spectrum of glaucoma severity and precluded longitudinal assessment. The relatively small number of severe cases reduced the precision of estimating SF relationships at the lower end of the dynamic range, particularly for the Hood–Kardon model. Although all severity stages were included in the pooled analysis due to sample size limitations, further stratification substantially reduced subgroup sizes and limited the feasibility of robust severity-specific analyses. Future studies with larger sample sizes across different disease stages are needed to better evaluate stage-specific SF relationships. Additionally, the study population was predominantly White, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to more diverse populations. The device's measurable range of 15–40 dB may also restrict sensitivity at the extremes of disease severity; however, within this range Radius XR demonstrated correlations comparable to SAP. As reported in a prior validation study,11 clinically meaningful information may still be obtained from global indices and changes in scotoma size, although caution is required when interpreting pointwise sensitivities near the measurement floor.11 Radius XR relies on blind spot monitoring without real-time eye tracking. In the absence of standardized and validated reliability thresholds for the RATA Fast algorithm in prior studies, independent exclusion criteria for Radius XR were not applied. Future studies should establish reliability criteria for VR-based perimetry to enable independent quality assessment. In addition, weaker correlations were observed in peripheral regions, particularly nasally, underscoring the need to refine perimetric strategies to better capture localized defects. The cross-sectional design of the present study precludes assessment of functional progression, which requires longitudinal follow-up, as well as test–retest repeatability, which requires repeated Radius XR testing under stable conditions. Additionally, incorporation of macular OCT parameters may strengthen SF analyses, particularly in central visual field regions relevant to early glaucomatous damage.

Importantly, the Radius XR platform supports multiple languages; however, this study did not specifically evaluate language-specific usability. Although formal validation in non-English-speaking and culturally diverse clinical settings is still needed, future studies should assess usability, accessibility, and performance of VR-based perimetry across different languages and cultures.

Conclusions

Radius XR using the RATA protocol achieved performance comparable to established SAP strategies within its sensitivity range of 15–40 dB. The strongest SF correlations were observed in the superotemporal and inferotemporal sectors, reinforcing their clinical importance in glaucoma monitoring. Although current thresholds and fixation monitoring remain areas for improvement, the device already shows promise as a portable and patient-centered complement to conventional perimetry. With further refinement, VR-based perimetry has the potential to transform glaucoma management by enabling accessible, reliable, and frequent testing across diverse clinical settings.
