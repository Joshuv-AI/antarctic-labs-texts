Correlative study of hepatic venous diameter/liver volume index and extent of hepatic steatosis in living related liver donors: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Subhash Gupta, Ruchi Rastogi, Ram Singh, Akshita Gupta, Sudha Reddy, Peush Sahni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic steatosis is a common incidental finding during pretransplant donor evaluation and a key determinant of graft suitability. During review of donor imaging, we observed that hepatic steatosis was frequently associated with smaller hepatic venous diameters. This study aimed to quantitatively assess the relationship between total hepatic venous diameter and hepatic steatosis and to develop a noninvasive predictive index based on hepatic venous diameter and liver volume.MethodsThis retrospective study included 768 prospective liver donors who underwent pretransplant evaluation over 3.5 years. Total hepatic venous diameter (THVD) and total liver volume were measured on computed tomography, and hepatic fat content was quantified using magnetic resonance (MR) proton density fat fraction (PDFF) sequences. We analyzed the correlation between the THVD-to-liver volume ratio (THVD/LV) and hepatic steatosis and evaluated diagnostic performance.ResultsHepatic steatosis (MR-PDFF >6.4%) was present in 22% (169/768) of donors; most had a body mass index of 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2. A moderate but statistically significant negative correlation was observed between hepatic steatosis and THVD/LV (r=–0.266, P<0.001). For the screening of hepatic steatosis, the THVD/LV index demonstrated an accuracy of 59.5% (95% confidence interval, 55.9%–63.0%), sensitivity of 72.2%, and specificity of 55.9%, with a high negative predictive value (NPV) of 87.7%.ConclusionsThe THVD/LV index may serve as a simple, noninvasive parameter for screening hepatic steatosis in living liver donors, given its high NPV. Its application could improve donor-recipient matching and aid preoperative risk assessment for transplant outcomes.Keywords: Hepatic veins, Liver transplantation, Fatty liver, Magnetic resonance imaging, Radiology

INTRODUCTION

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is recognized as a leading cause of chronic liver disease, with a global prevalence estimated at 25.2% [1]. MASLD is defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis involving more than 5% of hepatocytes in individuals who consume minimal to no alcohol [1,2]. In India, the reported prevalence ranges from 9% to 53% [1,3], increasing the likelihood of encountering hepatic steatosis and MASLD during the evaluation of prospective living liver donors [4].

Although liver biopsy has long been the gold standard for assessing steatosis and fibrosis in MASLD, it is not routinely performed in prospective living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) donors due to its invasive nature [5]. As a result, noninvasive imaging modalities—such as ultrasound, noncontrast computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance (MR)-based techniques—are increasingly used for evaluation. Among these, advanced MR-based techniques, including MR spectroscopy and proton density fat fraction (PDFF), have emerged as superior modalities for quantifying hepatic fat content. These methods demonstrate strong correlation with histologic fat assessment and have been increasingly incorporated into donor screening [5,6]. Prior studies have reported associations between hepatic vein waveform patterns, parenchymal steatosis, and venous congestion—parameters that are important for assessing graft quality [7–9]. One proposed mechanism is that engorged, fat-laden hepatocytes may compress the hepatic vein walls, leading to measurable hemodynamic changes [9–11]. However, despite recognition of this relationship, few studies have quantified it.

During preoperative imaging of living liver donors at our center, we observed relatively smaller hepatic venous diameters in proportion to liver volume (LV) in donors with steatotic livers. A similar pattern has been noted intraoperatively in recipients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)-related cirrhosis, in whom the inferior vena cava (IVC) and hepatic veins appeared smaller than in recipients transplanted for other indications. Given the high prevalence of hepatic steatosis in the Indian population, a similar trend could reasonably be expected among LDLT donors, underscoring the need for routine fat quantification during pretransplant imaging. We aimed to assess the relationship between hepatic venous diameter, LV, and hepatic steatosis and to evaluate whether a derived ratio could serve as a noninvasive predictive index for identifying steatotic grafts during donor evaluation.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional and Hospital Ethics and Scientific Committee at Max Super Speciality Hospital, Saket, New Delhi, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (IRB No. BHR/RS/MSSH/MHIL/SKT-1/MHEC/RADIO/22-07). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because this was a retrospective study based on previously recorded, anonymized clinical data.

Study Design

A retrospective, cross-sectional observational study was performed. The study population comprised potential donors for LDLT who were evaluated between February 2017 and January 2022. As part of the routine pretransplant workup, all donors underwent both noncontrast and multiphasic contrast-enhanced abdominal CT, as well as noncontrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for liver fat fraction estimation. Individuals with any known liver disease, cardiac disease, or diabetes mellitus were excluded, along with those unable to undergo MRI due to claustrophobia.

Data Collection

Demographic data (age and sex) and anthropometric measurements (body weight and height) were extracted from the electronic medical record system. Body mass index (BMI; kg/m2) was calculated by dividing body weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. Obesity was defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Imaging Assessment

Hepatic steatosis was assessed using MR-PDFF, obtained using the IDEAL-IQ method on a 3T MR scanner (Discovery MR750 3.0T; GE HealthCare). At least 12 regions of interest (ROIs) were placed in the hepatic parenchyma in both lobes, excluding fissures and major vessels, and the mean value was recorded as the parenchymal fat fraction. Total LV of the donor liver before hepatectomy was calculated on the hepatic venous phase of contrast-enhanced CT using the manual trace method. Total hepatic venous outflow diameters were calculated by measuring the maximum diameters of the right, middle, and left hepatic veins near their ostia at the IVC (Fig. 1A). Accessory hepatic veins were also measured when present. The sum of the diameters of all measured hepatic veins was used to represent the total hepatic venous diameter (THVD). All donors fasted for 4 to 6 hours before CT and had no significant cardiovascular abnormalities on detailed systemic workup that could have confounded hepatic venous diameter.

Definitions

The sum of the diameters of all hepatic veins was defined as THVD. The hepatic veins included the right hepatic vein (RHV), middle hepatic vein (MHV), left hepatic vein (LHV), and inferior hepatic veins (IHVs).

Total hepatic venous diameter (THVD)=RHV+MHV+LHV+IHVs

Hepatic steatosis on MR-PDFF was defined as absent when ≤6.4% and present when >6.4%. The THVD/LV index was defined as shown below [12].

Statistical Analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel ver. 16 (Microsoft Corp.) and SPSS ver. 20 (IBM Corp.). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships among hepatic steatosis, LV, BMI, and THVD. A significance threshold of P<0.05 was used, and comparisons were evaluated using the Student t-test. Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed to identify variables independently associated with hepatic venous diameter.

RESULTS

A total of 768 donors were included in the analysis, of whom 407 (53%) were female. The mean±standard deviation age was 33.5±10.2 years, weight was 67.7±12.7 kg, height was 163.2±9.6 cm, and BMI was 25.41±4.32 kg/m2. Overall, 599 (78%) donors were classified as having no hepatic steatosis (MR-PDFF ≤6.4%), and 169 (22%) were classified as having hepatic steatosis (MR-PDFF >6.4%); this validated threshold corresponds to histologic steatosis ≥5% [13]. Hepatic Steatosis Correlates Significantly with LV, BMI, and THVD/LV

Hepatic steatosis showed statistically significant positive correlations with both BMI (r=0.262, P<0.001) and LV (r=0.369, P<0.001) (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). This trend was consistent across sexes, with Pearson correlation coefficients of r=0.264 in men and r=0.267 in women (both P<0.001). When analyzed categorically using the WHO Asia-Pacific BMI classifications [14], the prevalence of hepatic steatosis increased progressively with BMI (Supplementary Table 1). More than 74% of steatotic donors fell into obesity class I or II, reinforcing the role of BMI as a clinically useful screening indicator. However, a small but notable subset of donors with steatosis had a normal BMI (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), highlighting the importance of considering additional risk factors when evaluating hepatic steatosis in living donors.

In steatotic donors, hepatic venous diameters appeared disproportionately narrow relative to LV (Fig. 1A). Although THVD alone did not correlate significantly with hepatic steatosis, introducing a composite predictive index that combines THVD and LV (THVD/LV) strengthened and clarified the association. The THVD/LV index showed a moderate negative correlation with hepatic steatosis (r=–0.266, P<0.001) (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table 3), suggesting that as liver fat content increases, the effective cross-sectional area for hepatic venous drainage decreases. This relationship may help identify early venous congestion in grafts from steatotic donors.

On linear regression analysis, THVD/LV was evaluated as a potential screening tool for hepatic steatosis. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.673 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.63–0.72), comparable to that of BMI (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Table 4). Diagnostic performance included an overall accuracy of 59.5% (95% CI, 55.9%–63.0%), sensitivity of 72.2%, and specificity of 55.9%. The negative predictive value (NPV) was 87.7%, supporting the utility of this index as an adjunctive screening tool for hepatic steatosis in potential donors (Supplementary Table 5). DISCUSSION

This study assessed the relationship between hepatic venous diameter and hepatic steatosis while accounting for LV and BMI, both of which are known to increase with steatosis. Although prior research has described hepatic vein waveform changes in steatotic livers [10], this study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to quantitatively evaluate THVD in relation to liver fat content using preoperative imaging in living donors.

Our findings suggest that relatively smaller hepatic veins may serve as a noninvasive imaging marker of hepatic steatosis in LDLT donor candidates, potentially supporting early risk stratification and graft selection. Although elevated BMI is an established predictor of hepatic steatosis, a clinically important subset of individuals—particularly in the Asia-Pacific region—develop MASLD despite having a normal BMI. This phenotype, termed “lean MASLD,” has been associated with increased mortality in patients with MASH [1,15,16]. While metabolic overlap exists across MASLD phenotypes, substantial variability in body composition and genetic predisposition complicates diagnosis and prognostication. These considerations underscore the need for improved preoperative screening strategies that extend beyond conventional BMI thresholds to capture the full clinical spectrum of MASLD [1,2].

While MR-PDFF has limited ability to differentiate MASLD from MASH, this had minimal impact on our objective because individuals with more than 30% steatosis had already been excluded from donation eligibility [12,17,18]. In our practice, donors with MR-PDFF >10% are advised to undergo presurgical lifestyle modification for weight reduction, consistent with international standards. The commonly accepted threshold of <30% steatosis for donation is based on histopathology and corresponds to approximately 18% on MR-PDFF due to the nonlinear relationship between MR-based fat estimation and histopathological fat quantification [18,19]. Although MR-PDFF remains the reference standard, we propose THVD/LV as a supplementary parameter rather than a replacement, given its modest discriminative ability (area under receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.673). It may add value during initial CT-based donor evaluation before MR-PDFF is performed. In particular, its correlation with hepatic steatosis and high NPV suggests utility for identifying donors with subtle or early-stage fatty infiltration who might otherwise be overlooked during initial evaluation [20]. Early identification could support optimization of perioperative management strategies and donor-recipient matching in settings constrained by organ scarcity.

As limitations, this retrospective analysis had missing data that could not be imputed for accurate parameter development. In addition, most donors had only mild (grade I) steatosis, and steatosis categorization was binary (steatotic vs. nonsteatotic) without histopathologic confirmation [20]. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that the THVD/LV index may be a useful noninvasive parameter and could lay the groundwork for further studies aimed at improving donor-recipient selection strategies.

In this study, we quantitatively evaluated the relationship between THVD and hepatic steatosis. Our findings suggest that THVD/LV may serve as a simple, noninvasive predictive index for hepatic steatosis, with potential utility in pretransplant donor evaluation. Given its high NPV, this index may be used as an adjunctive screening tool alongside standard screening measures. These preliminary results underscore the need for larger, prospective studies encompassing a broader spectrum of steatosis severity to validate and refine this parameter.
