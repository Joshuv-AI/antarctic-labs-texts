Tracheal transplantation: lessons learned that may apply to lung transplantation
Authors: Eric M. Genden, Ya-Wen Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewThe purpose of this review is to explore the lessons learned from experimental and human tracheal transplantation to determine if this information may be applied to lung transplantation.Recent findingsExperimental work in animal models and the recent human tracheal transplantation suggests that a robust tracheal vascular supply prevents anastomotic complications. Further, this work demonstrates that tracheal allografts undergo a progressive chimerism as recipient epithelium repopulates the allograft. In contrast to most vascularized composite allografts such as hand and face transplantation that experience high rates of rejection, the tracheal allograft did not demonstrate rejection. This may suggest that tissue chimerism plays a role in evading immune-mediated allograft rejection.SummaryWhile anastomotic complications and chronic allograft rejection are the most common complications related to lung transplantation, the findings associated with tracheal transplantation may have implications for both reducing complications associated with lung transplantation.

INTRODUCTION

Extensive defects of the trachea may occur congenitally, secondary to intubation trauma, or because of a burn injury. The management of long segment tracheal defects has been a longstanding challenge. The misconception that the trachea is merely an airway conduit has led investigators to experiment with alloplastic conduits and decellularized allografts. These approaches are problematic because alloplastic materials fail to biologically integrate leading to infection, erosion, and extrusion [1]. Similarly, the use of decellularized allografts and aortic isografts lack structure to support the inspiratory pressures and functional ciliated epithelium leading to obstruction [2,3]. The lessons learned from over 50 years of experimentation are that successful long segment tracheal replacement requires a rigid structure, a structure that will biologically integrate, and a structure that will support functional ciliated epithelium.

Tracheal transplantation has been recognized as the ‘holy grail’ of tracheal reconstruction [4] however, studies in the late 1960s and 1970s evaluating the tracheal blood supply suggested that there was no single vascular pedicle to provide the revascularization necessary for transplantation [5,6] This scientific dictum prompted an era of experimentation with tissue engineered conduits [7]. While techniques in tissue engineering have successfully achieved the fabrication of cartilage-bearing biological scaffolds, the goal of achieving a functional tracheal composite comprised of cartilage and ciliated epithelium that will biologically integrate, remains elusive [8].

The challenges of tissue engineering influenced experimental work re-examining the tracheal blood flow in the animal model in the late 1990s [9]. This work found that the superior and inferior thyroid vessels traversed the muscular component of the esophagus and nourished the entire length of the trachea. Using this information, a series of long segment isografts and allografts were reported in the animal model [10,11] followed by the first single-staged human vascularized tracheal transplantation in 2021 [12▪▪]. The recent single-staged vascularized tracheal transplantation provided interesting information related to the vascular supply and the biological dynamics of the airway allograft that may apply to lung transplantation.

Airway complications following lung transplantation continue to contribute to postoperative morbidity and mortality. Improvements in organ preservation, surgical technique, and the medical management of the recipient have reduced complications to a reported range of 10% to 15% with an associated mortality of 2–3% [13,14]. Despite advancements in this area, tracheal anastomotic breakdown, infection, and chronic lung allograft dysfunction continue to represent common complications associated with lung transplantation. The etiology of these complications is debated however some have suggested that a compromise in vascular supply may be contributory. The early experience with lung transplantation demonstrated that bronchial healing and anastomotic dehiscence presented a significant challenge. While some surgeons have advocated for direct bronchial artery revascularization [14], others have promoted techniques such as wrapping the anastomosis with vascularized tissue, adjusting the immunosuppression regimen, and aggressively resecting the donor bronchus down to the secondary carina to reduce the risk of anastomotic complications [15,16]. There is long-term data that suggests that bronchial artery revascularization is effective however some have labeled it an ‘abandon operation’ because of the tedious nature of revascularization that is both technically challenging and associated with longer operative and ischemic times [17–20].

Tracheal airway surgeons have confronted similar challenges including anastomotic stenosis and dehiscence following tracheal resection. These complications have a significant impact on patient outcomes with a morbidity rate of 9–45% and mortality rates of 0–5% [21–25]. Like the experience in lung transplantation, ischemia has been cited as a contributing etiology to tracheal anastomotic complications and approaches to improve vascularity, including wrapping the tracheal anastomosis with a vascularized flap, have been proposed to reduce complications [25,26]. In contrast, our experience in both animal models and the human experience, albeit limited, has not been compromised with anastomotic complications. The technique involves procuring the inferior thyroid artery with the associated muscular esophagus to preserve the tracheo-esophageal vascular plexus that has proven important to tracheal revascularization. The esophageal segment that is procured is split and the mucosal ling is stripped leaving the muscular component with its associated vascular plexus intact. The rich tracheal blood supply that is achieved with this procurement technique may contribute to the anastomotic healing. Microvascular supercharging is not a new technique. It has been well described to enhance blood flow in colonic free flap reconstruction of the esophagus and has significantly reduced ischemic-mediated anastomotic complications [27]. These findings support the contention that enhancing the blood supply to the tracheal component of the lung allograft through techniques like bronchial artery revascularization, may deserve further consideration. Like the anatomy of the inferior thyroid artery, the bronchial artery provides branches to both the airway and the adjacent esophagus. Maintaining this vascular plexus by including a segment of the esophageal muscle may improve vascular flow to the lung allograft as we have experienced in the tracheal allograft. Like the inferior thyroid vasculature, the bronchial arteries exhibit significant variability in their origin, number, and pathways, making it challenging to create a standardized description [28]. Bronchial arteries can arise from various and diverse locations, such as the internal mammary, thyrocervical, innominate, and subclavian arteries, as well as directly from the aortic arch. The diverse anatomy may complicate a revascularization procedure revascularization may warrant reconsideration in reducing complications of lung transplantation.

The second challenge associated with lung transplantation, chronic allograft dysfunction, is a significant complication of lung transplantation characterized by progressive decline in lung function. Chronic allograft dysfunction is a major obstacle to long-term graft survival despite advances in transplantation techniques and immunosuppressive therapies. The immunological mechanisms, such as alloimmune responses, chronic inflammation, and fibrosis, have been debated, as have the approaches to mitigating this complication. Establishing immune tolerance has long been considered the panacea for chronic allograft rejection. To this end, various strategies for inducing immune tolerance, including donor-specific tolerance and regulatory T cell-based therapies, have been explored with marginal success. Tracheal transplantation may provide insight into the relationship between airway rejection and the role of allograft epithelium, the dominant target of cell-mediated rejection.

Vascular composite allografts (VCA) such as hand and face transplantation are defined as the transplantation of multiple tissues types, such as skin, muscle, bone, nerve, and blood vessels, as a single functional unit. VCA transplantation is fraught with rates of acute rejection as high as 85% and rates of chronic rejection ranging between 15% and 50% [29–31]. The rate of VCA rejection is significantly higher than rejection rates of most solid organs and may reflect the complex antigenicity of the different tissues, in particular the skin, that elicit a strong rejection response. In contrast, the VCA human tracheal transplantation did not elicit acute or chronic rejection. In human tracheal transplantation, allograft was monitored with routine bronchoscopic biopsies, tracheal endoscopy, and free cell DNA assessment (dd-cfDNA). Free cell DNA assessment has been used in monitoring renal transplantation as a sensitive measure of graft inflammation and injury and has been used as a surrogate to assess allograft rejection; dd-cfDNA levels <1% reflect the absence of active rejection (T cell–mediated type ≥IB or antibody-mediated rejection), and levels >1% indicate a probability of active rejection [32]. Although there is no data using this technique in VCA transplantation, we correlated dd-cfDNA with routine tracheal biopsies. Using these metrics, the tracheal composite allograft, which was composed of thyroid tissue, muscle, cartilage, mucosa, and vasculature, did not demonstrate acute or chronic rejection. Interestingly, two weeks following transplantation, the tracheal biopsies demonstrated a rapid migration of recipient basal cells into the allograft, and by four weeks, the basal cells had differentiated into ciliated epithelium that was functional and restored normal mucociliary clearance. Three months after transplantation, recipient-derived epithelium populated 98% of the allograft. The allograft eventually settled at 20% recipient-derived epithelium and maintained its chimeric status.

The migration of recipient mucosa into the allograft established a form of allograft chimerism. This finding is significant because the tracheal epithelium has been identified as the target of immune-mediated rejection in animal models, and one may hypothesize that the chimeric state may in some way, protect against T cell–mediated and antibody-mediated rejection. Experimental animal data demonstrate that following allograft re- epithelialization, long-term withdrawal of immunosuppression does not lead to chronic tracheal rejection even in the presence of alloantigen specific cytotoxic T-lymphocyte responses and that the re-epithelialized grafts may contain donor elements that impact the generation of immunity [33]. It also suggests that the migration of basal cells to sites of airway injury may be protective against acute injury and potentially chronic obliterative disease. Qu et al. demonstrated that in the animal model, the integrity of airway epithelium is essential to safeguard against the development of obliterative airway disease [34▪▪] Others have suggested that lung transplant chimerism may be a mechanism involved in the repair of alloimmune-mediated tissue injury after transplantation [35]. Transplant chimerism has been documented in both animal and human transplant models and has been proposed as a mechanism to induce immune tolerance. Hayes et al. found that following lung transplantation, donor and recipient tissue-specific stem cells could be identified in 91% of anastomotic sites and 93% of bronchial airways [36]. The implications of epithelial chimerism on both tracheal and lung allografts is unclear however given the epithelium of the conducting airways is host to a rich population of constitutively class II MHC (Ia) bearing dendritic cells [37], the role of epithelial chimerism deserves further study. On area of investigation is our lab is understanding how this network of tissue antigen presenting cells and epithelial chimerism impact cellular rejection. The lessons learned from tracheal transplantation and the work of others may suggest that the induction of tissue chimerism or application of autologous stem cell therapy to protect the transient sites of ischemic and/or immune-mediated airway injury may protect against cellular immune-mediated rejection and the associated inflammation that may ultimately lead to chronic rejection [38▪▪]. Most recently, we are examining the role of combining autologous stem cell therapy with traditional vascularized allograft scaffolds.

As the field of stem cell-directed differentiation advances, the refinement of techniques to generate functional airway and alveolar stem cells emerges as a critical area of focus. Key efforts are directed toward enhancing the protocols for differentiation to consistently produce specific cell types that not only mimic the sophisticated structures and functions of native tissues but also maintain long-term viability and functionality posttransplantation. This endeavor is vital for improving the efficiency of differentiation processes and ensuring the practical application of these cells in clinical settings. By effectively integrating these stem cell-derived tissues into transplantation strategies, there is potential to significantly address and potentially reduce the incidence of severe complications such as chronic allograft dysfunction. Such integration could lead to better graft-host compatibility, reduced immunogenicity, and lower rejection rates, ultimately contributing to enhanced graft longevity and improved overall outcomes for transplant recipients. Moreover, the development of a chimeric environment within lung allografts using recipient-derived stem cells presents a novel approach that could revolutionize the field. This strategy might facilitate immune tolerance and minimize the necessity for lifelong immunosuppression, thus improving the quality of life for recipients and reducing the burden of associated adverse effects. Additionally, the exploration of tissue-engineered tracheal replacements, which incorporate both robust structural supports and biologically active, stem cell-derived epithelia, promises to transform the management of long segment airway defects. These replacements could provide more durable and functionally integrated solutions than the currently available options, potentially setting a new standard in tracheal reconstruction.

CONCLUSION

The convergence of advanced stem cell biology with sophisticated surgical techniques holds immense promise for overcoming the persistent challenges in lung and tracheal transplantation. As such, continued interdisciplinary collaboration and technological innovation are crucial to translating these experimental strategies into tangible clinical realities, reshaping the landscape of organ transplantation, and opening new avenues for treating complex respiratory conditions.
