Development of Uncertainty Sensitivity: Early Competence or Protracted Development?
Authors: Qianqian Wan, Vladimir M. Sloutsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe development of uncertainty sensitivity, a critical cognitive ability, presents a challenging paradox: Some evidence points to its emergence in infancy, whereas other evidence indicates much later emergence and protracted development. To resolve this paradox, we propose that early uncertainty sensitivity relies on perceptual cues to ignorance (e.g., occluded information), whereas uncertainty monitoring in the absence of such cues develops later. We tested this hypothesis using a random dot kinematogram task that manipulated uncertainty through signal‐to‐noise ratios, without perceptual cues to ignorance or knowledge. Participants aged 4–6 years and adults (N = 189) made perceptual judgments of the directionality of dot movement or opted out under varying uncertainty levels. While all groups demonstrated above‐chance accuracy in perceptual judgments, children and adults differed markedly in their responses to uncertainty. Unlike adults, most children's decisions were not affected by uncertainty. These results suggest that without perceptual cues to ignorance, children struggle to detect, monitor, and respond to uncertainty. We propose that early uncertainty sensitivity found in some studies relies on perceptual cues to ignorance (i.e., bottom‐up attentional mechanisms), with strategic uncertainty monitoring developing more gradually. Our findings help reconcile contradictory results in uncertainty sensitivity development and highlight the role of perceptual cues and metacognition in children's decision‐making under uncertainty.Summary Children's uncertainty sensitivity often relies on perceptual cues to ignorance, whereas autonomous uncertainty monitoring without such cues develops later.Without perceptual cues to ignorance, only a minority of 4‐ to 6‐year‐olds exhibit uncertainty‐driven behavior, despite above‐chance perceptual discrimination.Our findings reconcile contradictory evidence about the early versus late emergence of uncertainty sensitivity in the developmental literature.

Introduction

The human environment is highly probabilistic, with uncertainty—the lack of sufficient information to make exact judgments—being its inherent property. Will my plane depart on time? Will my paper be published? Will my car reach 100,000 miles without breaking? The answers to these questions all involve substantial (albeit different) levels of uncertainty. The ability to identify and respond to uncertainty influences how organisms obtain environmental information, making it critical for various cognitive processes: learning, inference‐making, forecasting, decision‐making, and estimation. This fundamental ability shapes the development of adaptive behavior by enabling learners to identify knowledge gaps, seek additional information, and adjust their strategies when facing ambiguous situations. However, current literature on sensitivity to uncertainty presents a striking paradox in how this ability emerges during development and when. Understanding this paradox is crucial for theoretical models of cognitive development and designing effective interventions to support children's learning under uncertainty.

On one side of this paradox, evidence suggests that sensitivity to uncertainty emerges early in development, possibly during infancy (Goupil et al. 2016; Goupil and Kouider 2016). This ability to identify, monitor, and estimate uncertainty guides children's active learning and information‐seeking behaviors across contexts. Young children and even toddlers systematically adjust their sampling patterns of hidden rewards in stable versus probabilistic environments, indicating early emerging sensitivity to uncertainty (Poli et al. 2024; Schulz et al. 2019). Similarly, toddlers adjust their evidence requirements when identifying occluded objects, requiring less evidence to distinguish between dissimilar objects, such as a cow and broccoli, than similar ones, such as an elephant and bear (Leckey et al. 2020). This adaptive decision‐making pattern has been consistently observed across multiple studies with infants and young children (Bramley and Ruggeri 2022; Gweon et al. 2010; Jones et al. 2021; Poli et al. 2022, 2020), providing substantial evidence for an early‐emerging sensitivity to uncertainty.

On the other side of the paradox, an equally compelling body of research suggests that uncertainty sensitivity emerges late in development. Studies demonstrate that preschoolers and even elementary school children often struggle to recognize uncertainty in seemingly simple situations. Children consistently underestimate uncertainty when designing simple experiments (Chen and Klahr 1999), make overconfident inferences in ambiguous situations, and fail to appreciate multi‐causal relationships (Kuhn et al. 2009; Leahy 2023; Leahy et al. 2022). In a paradigmatic study, Fay and Klahr (1996) presented children with a fundamental uncertainty task involving two boxes containing two different shapes (e.g., stars and cars in one box, stars and leaves in another). When asked to determine the source of a star piece, only 35% of preschoolers recognized the task's inherent uncertainty. Even more striking, children tend to disregard uncertainty when some information sources are unavailable, while others provide positive perceptual matching information. For instance, in Fay and Klahr's (1996) Experiment 3, when one visible box had stars, another had cars, and two boxes remained closed, children, when asked if they could definitively determine the source of either a star or a car, consistently failed to recognize the inherent uncertainty of the situation (as shown in Figure 1, left). Specifically, they provided erroneous (and overconfident) responses identifying one possible candidate (i.e., a respective box) and failing to appreciate the uncertainty of the situation (as the boxes that remained closed could also be the source of the target object). These findings, in direct contrast to the evidence for early emergence, suggest a fundamental limitation in children's ability to detect uncertainty and point to protracted development of this ability.

Schematics on the proposed distinctions between studies finding late and early emergence of uncertainty sensitivity. Cues to knowledge (positive matching) capture attention in late‐emergence literature. In contrast, cues to ignorance (inaccessible information) trigger novelty‐seeking behavior/curiosity and sensitivity to subjective uncertainty.

The stark contrast between these two bodies of evidence raises a critical question: Why do very young children appear uncertainty‐driven in some situations, yet much older children appear uncertainty‐insensitive in others? Some researchers have attempted to resolve this paradox by arguing that only one set of findings meets ecological validity standards (see Ruggeri 2022 for extensive arguments). We, however, propose alternative explanations for the apparent contradiction between these two bodies of literature.

A careful analysis of early‐emergence research settings reveals a critical pattern: These studies typically incorporate perceptual cues to ignorance, such as occluded or degraded information sources (Coughlin et al. 2015; Ghetti et al. 2013; Leckey et al. 2020). The consistent presence of such cues throughout a task creates a general context signaling that information may be unavailable, which can trigger information‐seeking behavior by alerting children to their ignorance about potential outcomes. Importantly, this information‐seeking behavior does not necessarily depend on one's ability to evaluate their own knowledge (i.e., metacognition) or the objective uncertainty of the information (i.e., indeterminacy of the outcomes).

Here, we contrast subjective uncertainty, which represents one's state of ignorance, with objective uncertainty, which refers to the indeterminate state of the world in a probabilistic environment. How can objective and subjective uncertainty be estimated in principle?

For objective uncertainty, we suggest at least three routes to estimating it. First, one may have knowledge of probabilities, and, according to this knowledge, it is impossible to know with certainty the outcome of a toss of a fair coin. Second, one may have a sufficiently large sample of outcomes to indirectly estimate variability in the outcomes and thus uncertainty with respect to the next toss. For example, non‐human animals, as a result of learning, can distinguish between consistent and variable outcomes (Hampton 2009; Smith 2005). And third, it could be estimated through subjective uncertainty.

For subjective uncertainty, we suggest two routes to estimating it. First, it could be estimated metacognitively (“I do not have enough knowledge to give a definitive answer”). And second, it could be estimated from explicit cues to ignorance. For example, I do not know the content of a closed box, or I do not know the capital of Uganda.

Importantly, because children do not have explicit knowledge of probabilities, and typical experiments on sensitivity to uncertainty do not involve large samples of outcomes, most often the estimate of objective uncertainty is through subjective uncertainty. If this is the case, then explicit cues to ignorance could trigger basic attentional processes, particularly a preference for novelty, thus reducing the need to access their own state of knowledge metacognitively. Consequently, children's seemingly uncertainty‐driven behavior in hidden‐information scenarios may be triggered by cues to ignorance.

Critically, research supporting the late emergence of uncertainty sensitivity does not feature perceptual cues to ignorance, although it sometimes features perceptual cues to knowledge. In these scenarios, the presence of a clear perceptual match may eliminate children's reasons to suspect ignorance. While cues to ignorance sometimes appear in this research (e.g., closed boxes in Fay and Klahr 1996; Experiment 3), they may become less attention‐capturing when competing with cues to knowledge, such as an open box containing the pieces in question (see Figure 1 for a schematic illustration). These cues to knowledge may signal children that they know the answer (as it is right in front of their eyes), leading to overlooking sources of uncertainty (known as the “positive capture effect”) and overconfident responses (Fay and Klahr 1996; Klahr and Chen 2003).

We propose that these divergent findings reflect a fundamental distinction in how children process uncertainty: they rely primarily on bottom‐up perceptual cues rather than estimating objective uncertainty through logical or probabilistic knowledge (or thinking) or metacognitive understanding of their own ignorance. When explicit cues to ignorance are present, the cues trigger a sense of subjective uncertainty, and subsequently more cautious decisions. Conversely, when perceptual cues suggest the availability of knowledge, they capture attention and lead to overconfident decisions. This framework generates a clear prediction: Children should struggle to recognize even high levels of uncertainty when there are no explicit cues to ignorance.

To test this prediction, we examined uncertainty sensitivity in a context that systematically manipulates uncertainty while minimizing perceptual cues to ignorance (such as occluded or degraded information). We used a random dot kinematogram (RDK) task where varying motion coherence levels created different degrees of uncertainty. In this task, coherence represents the signal‐to‐noise ratio: at zero coherence, an equal number of dots move in either direction, creating maximum uncertainty about the motion direction. As coherence increases, a larger proportion of dots move in the same direction, reducing uncertainty about the actual motion signal. Thus, the RDK paradigm allows us to parametrically manipulate uncertainty through the strength of the perceptual signal itself, without relying on external cues such as occlusion or degradation. Importantly, unlike occlusion that signals that “you cannot know,” the RDK presents all information—just with varying noise—requiring participants to evaluate their confidence along a continuum from “probably know” to “probably do not know.”

Participants judged the directionality of dot movement or could opt out if uncertain. This paradigm assesses sensitivity to uncertainty by manipulating signal‐to‐noise ratios in a basic visual motion perception task. If participants are sensitive to uncertainty, they should show two behavioral patterns: (1) slower responses at higher uncertainty levels (low‐coherence trials with low signal‐to‐noise ratios) due to limited available evidence, and/or (2) strategic opt‐outs at higher levels of uncertainty.

Although previous metacognition research using opt‐out designs has shown that children as young as three can opt out when feeling uncertain (Balcomb and Gerken 2008; Ghetti et al. 2013), our design has several critical differences. Most importantly, we offered no explicit cues to ignorance (such as occlusion) while manipulating uncertainty through signal‐to‐noise ratios. To examine developmental trends and maintain comparability with previous research, we tested children aged 4 to 6 years and adults.

Methods

Participants

The study included 189 participants across four age groups: 47 four‐year‐olds (M = 55.24 months, SD = 3.87, 22 girls), 49 five‐year‐olds (M = 65.10 months, SD = 4.87; 20 girls), 47 six‐year‐olds (M = 79.35 months, SD = 4.75; 23 girls), and 46 adults (M = 19.1 years; 26 females). Data from participants who did not complete the task or performed at chance level accuracy (2 four‐year‐olds, 4 five‐year‐olds, 2 six‐year‐olds, and 1 adult) were excluded. Sample size (45 participants per group) was determined to detect medium effects on task accuracy (0.80 power, α = 0.05). Participants had normal or corrected vision, and children were screened for developmental disorders. All provided appropriate consent/assent, with children receiving small toys and adults receiving course credit for participation.

Materials and Design

Stimuli consisted of RDKs displaying 50 white dots against a gray background. Dots had a 10‐frame lifetime, moving at 11 pixels per frame. Perceptual difficulty was manipulated through coherence levels (0.02–0.98), generated from a beta distribution (α = β = 0.5), with lower coherence indicating higher uncertainty. The study used a mixed design with age as a between‐subjects factor and coherence as a within‐subjects variable. Primary dependent variables were response accuracy, response time, and opt‐out decisions.

Procedure

The experiment was conducted in a quiet room with participants seated approximately 60 cm from the display. Children used a 27‐inch touchscreen, while adults used a 21‐inch monitor (both at 1920 × 1080 resolution, 60 Hz refresh rate).

Figure 2 illustrates the trial sequence. Participants were introduced to the task through a cover story about helping tadpoles find their parents. They were told that correctly identifying the direction of dot motion would help the tadpoles reunite with their parents. The importance of accuracy was emphasized throughout the experiment. On each trial, participants could either select a left or right arrow to indicate motion direction or choose a glass jar to opt out (described to children as placing tadpoles in a waiting jar). Corrective feedback followed each response: for correct answers, animated stars were added to the participant's accumulated total from previous trials; for incorrect answers, stars were deducted from their running total. Opt‐out responses showed tadpoles in a glass jar with no change to the star count. This cumulative reward system incentivized task engagement while reinforcing the consequences of response choices.

Temporal structure and response options of the perceptual decision‐making task. Note: Each trial began with a fixation cross (with a duration of 350–1200 ms), followed by the presentation of moving dots. Participants were instructed to determine the predominant direction of motion (left or right) by tapping the corresponding arrow, or to opt out by selecting the glass jar. The response window was 7 s (including stimulus presentation). Feedback was provided after each response, with time‐out prompts encouraging faster responses on subsequent trials. The moving dots were described to children as "tadpoles swimming" to enhance task engagement.

Each trial consisted of a fixation cross (350–1200 ms), followed by the RDK stimulus (1500 ms), with participants having 7 s to respond. The experiment comprised 10 practice trials and 30 experimental trials with randomized coherence levels.

Results

Our analyses were conducted using both frequentist and Bayesian approaches, primarily reporting frequentist results supplemented by Bayes factors (BF) to quantify evidence strength. Bayesian analysis was also adopted to further investigate the bimodality in the opt‐out response distribution. Following Morey et al. (2016), we interpreted BF values as follows: BF < 1/3 indicates evidence supporting the null hypothesis, 1/3 ≤ BF ≤ 3 suggests inconclusive evidence, and BF > 3 provides at least moderate support for the hypothesis. All reported BFs compare alternative hypotheses to null hypotheses. Both analytical approaches yielded consistent results.

Sensitivity to Perceptual Difficulty

Accuracy Analysis

We employed a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a logistic link function to examine how coherence and age influenced accuracy across completed trials, excluding opt‐out and time‐out responses. Figure 3 presents the model‐predicted accuracy by coherence level across age groups. The model, using 4‐year‐olds as the reference group, incorporated coherence, age group, and their interaction, with random intercepts and slopes for coherence by participants. This comprehensive model significantly outperformed reduced versions (ps < 0.001, BFs > 8.0 × 104).

Logistic curves of response accuracy against coherence level across age groups.

The analysis revealed greater accuracy at higher coherence levels (β = 2.28, SE = 0.23, z = 10.06, p < 0.001, OR = 9.77, 95% CI [1.84, 2.72], BF = 4.80 × 108). Age group comparisons demonstrated developmental progression: both adults (p < 0.001, BF = 5.39 × 102) and 6‐year‐olds (p = 0.004, BF = 6.33) achieved significantly higher accuracy than 4‐year‐olds, while 5‐year‐olds performed similarly to 4‐year‐olds (p = 0.13, BF = 0.37). The coherence‐age interaction revealed that the improvement in accuracy as coherence level increases is steeper for adults than 4‐year‐olds (β = 2.64, SE = 0.72, z = 3.66, p < 0.001, OR = 14.07, 95% CI [1.23, 4.06], BF = 2.11 × 102). At the same time, other child groups showed no significant differences from 4‐year‐olds (ps > 0.1, BFs < 0.82).

Contrast analyses revealed distinct developmental thresholds for performance: children exhibited above‐chance accuracy (0.5) at coherence levels exceeding 0.25 (ps < 0.04, BFs > 6.33), whereas adults demonstrated above‐chance performance at lower coherence levels (0.1; ps < 0.001, BFs > 59.60), indicating age differences in perceptual sensitivity. Importantly, all age groups achieved above‐chance accuracy at medium to high coherence levels, confirming task competence across the age groups.

Response Time Analysis

Response time has been used to infer task difficulty (e.g., Leckey et al. 2020; Ratcliff and Starns 2013). Slower responses were associated with lower signal‐to‐noise ratio trials, potentially due to slower evidence accumulation or the processing of more ambiguous evidence. Conversely, a higher signal‐to‐noise ratio typically results in faster responses, as clearer evidence facilitates quicker decisions.

A linear mixed model revealed significant effects of both coherence and age compared to reduced models (ps < 0.001, BF > 1 × 106). As shown in Figure 4, higher coherence was associated with faster responses, β = −0.22, SE = 0.03, t(170) = −7.63, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.27, −0.16], BF = 3.14 × 106. All older groups responded faster than 4‐year‐olds: adults (β = −0.47, SE = 0.04, t(175) = −11.65, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.55, −0.39], BF = 2.28 × 109), 5‐year‐olds (β = −0.12, SE = 0.04, t(176) = −2.85, p = 0.005, 95% CI [−0.19, −0.04], BF = 1.07), and 6‐year‐olds (β = ‐0.22, SE = 0.04, t(175) = −5.37, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.30, −0.14], BF = 155.05). The age‐by‐coherence interaction was not significant compared to the reduced model, χ 2(3) = 6.53, p = 0.08, BF = 1.1 × 10−4.

Mean response times (s) against coherence level across age groups.

Uncertainty‐Driven Behavior

Opt‐Out Responses Analysis

If participants' decisions were truly adaptive and driven by uncertainty, we would expect more opt‐out decisions on low‐coherence trials. GLMM analysis with a logistic link revealed significant effects of coherence, age, and their interaction on opt‐out likelihood, with the model significantly outperforming reduced versions (ps < 0.001, BFs > 6.91 × 104).

As shown in Figure 5, 4‐year‐olds exhibited some tendency to opt out at lower coherence levels (β = −3.07, SE = 0.92, z = −3.35, p < 0.001, OR = 0.05, 95% CI [−4.86, −1.27], BF = 1.41 × 104). Adults exhibited significantly higher opt‐out rates than 4‐year‐olds (β = 3.69, SE = 0.64, z = 5.79, p < 0.001, OR = 4.00, 95% CI [2.44, 4.94], BF = 622.79), whereas 5‐ and 6‐year‐olds exhibited patterns comparable to 4‐year‐olds (ps > 0.09, BFs< 0.5). The relationship between coherence and opt‐out behavior was more pronounced in adults relative to 4‐year‐olds (β = −12.18, SE = 1.61, z = −7.57, p < 0.001, OR < 0.001, 95% CI [−15.34, −9.03], BF = 2.64 × 106).

Logistic curves of probability of opt‐out response against coherence level across age groups.

Combining opt‐out and time‐out responses in the analysis found minimal change to the adult‐child differences: adults remained significantly different from 4‐year‐olds (p < 0.001, BF = 56.4), whereas 5‐ and 6‐year‐olds were similar to 4‐year‐olds (ps > 0.87, BFs < 0.29). The interaction effect persisted, with adults displaying stronger coherence‐based opt‐out patterns compared to children (p < 0.001, BF = 8.37 × 106). The consistency of results with and without including time‐out responses ruled out the possibility of time constraints preventing opt‐out decisions.

Bimodality in Opt‐Out Response Distribution

The distribution of opt‐out response (area under the curve, or AUC scores; see Figure 6) appears bimodal, suggesting participants fall into distinct subgroups rather than varying continuously in their sensitivity to coherence levels. To investigate this pattern, we analyzed participants' AUC scores, which quantify how well opt‐out decisions discriminate between coherence levels. A score of 0.5 represents chance‐level discrimination, whereas a score of 1.0 represents perfect discrimination. Because AUC values below 0.5 indicate systematically incorrect opt‐out responses (i.e., opting out of the easy trials), we set these to 0.5 and then rescaled the 0.5–1.0 range to 0–1. This transformation allowed us to focus on above‐chance performance while simplifying interpretation.

Density distribution of rescaled area under the curve (AUC) for opt‐out responses on coherence across age groups. The AUC values have been linearly transformed from their original range of 0.5–1.0 to 0–1 for enhanced interpretability. A value of 0 (where most children cluster) indicates chance‐level performance, meaning no systematic relationship between opt‐out decisions and coherence (signal‐to‐noise ratio). A value of 1 represents perfect discrimination, where opt‐out decisions perfectly align with coherence levels. Higher values indicate greater sensitivity to coherence when making opt‐out decisions.

To identify and characterize potential subgroups, we used Bayesian beta mixture models, which naturally accommodate the bounded nature of AUC scores. In beta mixture models, each mixture component represents a distinct subpopulation of participants with similar opt‐out behavior patterns. We compared models with 1, 2, and 3 components using Bayesian model selection with leave‐one‐out cross‐validation information criterion (LOOIC).

Model comparison strongly favored the 2‐component model (LOOIC = 1405.44, SE = 131.94) over both the 1‐component model (ΔLOOIC = −169.80, negative values indicate worse fit) and the 3‐component model (ΔLOOIC = 1.02), indicating that the added complexity of a third component provided negligible improvement in model fit.

The two‐component model identified the following distinct groups. Component 1 (low sensitivity group) showed minimal ability to discriminate between easy and difficult trials (mean AUC = 0.01, SD = 0.032, φ = 9.4, α = 0.1, β = 9.3), with responses clustered near chance level. Component 2 (uncertainty‐driven group) demonstrated clear sensitivity to coherence levels (mean AUC = 0.59, SD = 0.138, φ = 11.7, α = 6.9, β = 4.8), with performance well above chance.

The proportion of children showing uncertainty‐driven behavior (Component 2) remained well below the adult level. As illustrated in Figure 7, significantly fewer children than adults showed uncertainty‐driven behavior (Component 2): 33.3% of 4‐year‐olds, 42.2% of 5‐year‐olds, and 48.9% of 6‐year‐olds, compared with 88.9% of adults (χ 2(3) = 32.68, p < 0.001, BF = 4.38 × 106).

Proportion of participants in high versus low opt‐out AUC groups. The components were derived from the Bayesian beta mixture model with two components (selected via leave‐one‐out cross‐validation information criterion, or LOOIC).

Further analyses examined whether opt‐out patterns reflected uncertainty monitoring or associative learning (see Supplementary Materials). Participants with low AUC scores (who opted out inconsistently) exhibited significantly slower opt‐out response times compared to their correct and incorrect responses, suggesting reactive opt‐out decisions made after unsuccessful evidence sampling. In contrast, participants with high AUC scores (who show coherent‐dependent opt‐out behavior) showed no such slowing, suggesting proactive opt‐out decisions made without extended sampling attempts. Additionally, as shown in the Supplementary Materials, learning analyses ruled out associative learning as an explanation for opting‐out behavior in participants with high AUC scores: adults and children who demonstrated uncertainty‐driven behavior (high AUC group) did so from the very beginning of the task. These patterns collectively indicate successful uncertainty monitoring rather than slow associative learning, allowing for consistent opt‐out under low coherence levels.

General Discussion

Our study examined children's sensitivity to uncertainty using a RDK task that deliberately removed perceptual cues to ignorance while manipulating uncertainty through motion coherence levels. We predicted that without such cues, children would struggle to recognize uncertainty and to make uncertainty‐based strategic decisions. Our findings strongly support this hypothesis, revealing a protracted development of uncertainty‐driven behavior, with minimal progression between ages 4 and 6 but substantial differences between children and adults.

The task design, which required participants to judge the direction of dot movement or opt out when uncertain, revealed striking developmental differences. While all age groups showed basic perceptual sensitivity to uncertainty—evidenced by coherence‐dependent response times and accuracy—this sensitivity did not translate into adaptive behavioral strategies in children. Despite receiving feedback that could help them learn the association between low coherence and lower accuracy, children's strategic opt‐out responses remained strikingly limited. The Bayesian mixture model analysis highlighted this dissociation: only about a third to a half of all children (33.3% in the youngest group to 48.9% in the oldest group) showed strong discrimination in their opt‐out decisions based on uncertainty levels, compared to the vast majority of adults (i.e., 88.9%).

This pattern suggests that detecting perceptual difficulty is insufficient for strategic uncertainty‐based decision‐making. According to our theoretical framework, (objective) uncertainty can be estimated through three routes: explicit probabilistic knowledge, experience of a large sample showing variability, or subjective uncertainty. Since children lack explicit knowledge of probabilities and our task provided a limited number of trials, they would need to rely on subjective uncertainty, which itself requires either metacognitive self‐assessment or explicit cues to ignorance. Our results indicate that without explicit cues to ignorance (such as occluded information), children cannot metacognitively evaluate their own knowledge state to recognize that perceptual difficulty signals objective uncertainty. The development of uncertainty sensitivity that can operate independently of these general cues may depend on mature metacognitive abilities that enable participants to rely on their own subjective assessment of difficulty to identify an objectively uncertain situation.

Our findings help reconcile competing accounts of uncertainty monitoring development by demonstrating protracted development in uncertainty‐driven behavior in the absence of explicit perceptual cues to ignorance. Studies reporting the early emergence of uncertainty sensitivity often relied on paradigms with perceptual cues to ignorance, such as occluded or degraded stimuli (Poli et al. 2024; see Ghetti et al. 2013, for a review). In these contexts, children's adaptive responses may be facilitated by attention capture by missing information, thus increasing the overall sense of uncertainty. When these contextual cues are removed, as in our RDK paradigm, most children struggle to translate perceptual difficulty into strategic action, revealing the developmental trajectory of increasingly autonomous uncertainty monitoring abilities.

The reported findings also support an account documenting protracted development of uncertainty sensitivity, with children often failing to recognize or respond adaptively to uncertainty late into childhood (Fay and Klahr 1996; Chen and Klahr 1999). Our RDK task extends these findings by isolating the specific developmental bottleneck: even when uncertainty stems directly from the perceptual signal itself—without competing cues to knowledge, working memory demands, or complex reasoning requirements—children fail to recognize that such perceptual difficulty indicates uncertainty. In other words, the reported results suggest that, early in development, the challenge is likely to be the immature metacognition, the ability that exhibits protracted development (O'Leary and Sloutsky 2017, 2019; Wan et al. 2025).

The reported findings have important implications for understanding early learning and decision‐making. Previous development tasks often manipulated information through perceptual occlusion (e.g., hidden rewards in sequential decision‐making tasks; Blanco and Sloutsky 2021; Schulz et al. 2019; category learning tasks; Blanco et al. 2023; Wan and Sloutsky 2024). Such designs include multiple factors: explicit cues to ignorance that capture attention, working memory demands for maintaining hidden information, and genuine uncertainty monitoring. By eliminating both perceptual occlusion and working memory demands, the present study allows us to examine young children's ability for uncertainty monitoring per se. The results suggest that sophisticated exploration in early decision‐making (e.g., Poli et al. 2024) may be supported in part by basic attentional responses to salient cues of missing information, with more autonomous uncertainty monitoring developing gradually.

Although our findings support protracted development of uncertainty monitoring when perceptual cues to ignorance are absent, there are several issues that are worth considering. First, it is possible that varying perceptual clarity in our RDK paradigm could support associative learning rather than metacognitive monitoring, where children learn through feedback that low‐coherence trials tend to yield incorrect responses. However, our data argue against this interpretation. Children who failed to show coherence‐dependent opt‐out behavior showed no learning across the experiment, while those demonstrating uncertainty‐driven behavior showed this pattern from the first half of trials and displayed faster opt‐out than correct responses, indicating proactive monitoring rather than learned associations.

Our findings also suggest a more nuanced relationship with occlusion paradigm results. Rather than presenting contradictory evidence for early uncertainty sensitivity, the distinction may reflect context‐supported versus autonomous uncertainty detection. Recent longitudinal work shows that implicit behaviors in 2‐year‐olds predict explicit uncertainty monitoring at age 3 (Leckey, Gonzales, et al. 2025; Leckey, Selmeczy, et al. 2025), supporting continuity in underlying processes. Our paradigm, which removes contextual scaffolding, may reveal a more stringent test of uncertainty monitoring that develops later, suggesting a developmental progression from context‐supported to autonomous uncertainty detection, rather than the absence versus presence of metacognition.

Future Directions and Conclusion

The reported findings raise important questions for future research. How are individual differences in uncertainty sensitivity related to metacognitive development and/or probabilistic reasoning? How do children transition from relying on perceptual cues to ignorance to developing more autonomous sensitivity to uncertainty? Moreover, what is the role of executive function, working memory, and metacognition in this development?

Overall, our findings reveal that, in the absence of perceptual cues to ignorance, only a small minority of young children exhibit sensitivity to uncertainty. By using an RDK task that eliminates both perceptual cues to ignorance and associated working memory demands, we demonstrate that the development of strategic responses to objective uncertainty follows a protracted developmental trajectory. This research advances our understanding of the development of sensitivity to uncertainty. It helps reconcile the contradictory findings, with some suggesting an early onset of uncertainty sensitivity and others arguing for protracted development.
