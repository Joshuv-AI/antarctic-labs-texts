From visualization to prediction: Dissecting metabolic heterogeneity with DDRTree
Authors: Yonghua Yu, Hong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discriminative dimensionality reduction tree (DDRTree) framework provides a powerful approach to unravel metabolic heterogeneity and delineate clinically relevant subtypes. In this issue, Qiu et al.1 and Jia et al.2 apply it to type 2 diabetes and obesity, identifying subtypes with distinct risk profiles.

Main text

Metabolic diseases are characterized by profound heterogeneity across organs, disease phenotypes, and individuals, posing major challenges to precision prevention and treatment. Systematic characterization of this heterogeneity has therefore become a central research priority. Data-driven clustering approaches have been widely applied to stratify patients into discrete phenotypic clusters.3 In type 2 diabetes (T2D), the seminal framework proposed by Ahlqvist et al. that used k-means clustering to six routine clinical variables to define five distinct subtypes,3 has since been replicated and validated in diverse populations. Building on this, subsequent investigations identified prediabetes subgroups defined by dominant pathophysiological features such as insulin deficiency, insulin resistance, or obesity. Despite their utility, these “hard-clustering” strategies assume within-group homogeneity and fail to capture the continuum of disease risk. Moreover, they are limited in modeling disease progression or predicting individualized outcomes. The ADOPT trial further suggested that precision medicine in T2D is likely to have the most clinical utility when it is based on an approach that uses specific phenotypic measures to predict specific outcomes, rather than assigning patients to subgroups.4 These limitations underscore the need for innovative analytic frameworks.

The discriminative dimensionality reduction tree (DDRTree), originally introduced in single-cell transcriptomics for reconstructing developmental or phenotypic trajectories,5 has been increasingly adapted to clinical research for dissecting heterogeneity using population-level data. The first application in T2D by Nair et al.6 demonstrated its capacity to visualize metabolic variation, which was subsequently validated in the German Diabetes Study and the Ludwigshafen Risk and Cardiovascular Health cohort, thereby confirming its robustness.7 Evidence from the nationwide China Cardiometabolic and Cancer Cohort (4C) study further revealed marked difference in tree structures across ethnicities, highlighting the importance of population-specific models.8 In the current issue, Qiu et al.1 extended DDRTree-based subtyping to one of the most critical of clinical endpoints—mortality. Leveraging data from the China Chronic Disease and Risk Factors Surveillance, they identified distinct subtype-specific mortality patterns: obesity with hypertension conferring the highest risk of cardiovascular death, hyperglycemia with dyslipidemia associated with mortality from ketoacidosis or coma, and other subtypes associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease-related deaths. Strikingly, replication in the UK Biobank confirmed that these associations, although cardiovascular mortality was highest among individuals with dyslipidemia and obesity, are consistent with findings from the Scottish study.6 These results indicate that differences in genetic background, lifestyle, and medical practices may shape disease heterogeneity differently across populations.

From tracking the progression of diabetes to charting the metabolic landscape of obesity, DDRTree continues to expand our understanding of metabolic heterogeneity. In this issue, Jia et al.2 further extended this approach to 18,733 obese participants from the nationwide 4C study, establishing an innovative framework for obesity subtyping. Using ten routine continuous metabolic traits, they identified and validated five distinct metabolic phenotypes, with varying risks of developing T2D, CKD, and CVD. Importantly, they also addressed methodological rigor by demonstrating that a sample size exceeding 9,000 participants is required to achieve clustering stability. Furthermore, by integrating metabolomic data into the DDRTree-derived structure, they showed metabolite gradients that aligned closely with specific obesity subtypes. These findings not only strengthen the biological plausibility of obesity heterogeneity but also highlight the potential of DDRTree-based phenotyping to inform precise interventions. The concept of metabolically healthy obesity has long been debated, with varying definitions yielding inconsistent clinical outcomes. DDRTree addresses the limitations of arbitrary cutoffs by preserving the continuous nature of metabolic variables, thereby retaining their predictive value for disease risk while providing a more comprehensive characterization of heterogeneity within obese populations.9

From a methodological perspective, the DDRTree framework offers a powerful approach for dissecting clinical heterogeneity at multiple levels. It first integrates key clinical variables that capture the dimensions of disease variation, then performs dimensionality reduction to visualize a continuous tree representing disease trajectories across the population, and finally positions each individual within this structure for personalized prediction of outcomes. Compared with conventional clustering methods, DDRTree provides intuitive visualization, accommodates continuous and individualized representations rather than forcing discrete categories, and allows for the estimation of future risk progression, thereby bridging population-level insights with individual-level prognostication.

To fully utilize this approach, several factors must be carefully considered. Robustness depends on the selecting clinical variables that truly capture population heterogeneity, and stable trees require large, representative cohorts. Additionally, given that tree structures vary across ethnicities, constructing population-specific models is crucial for accurate stratification. Looking forward, DDRTree could be applied to other diseases, and its integration with multiomics data may further reveal the molecular mechanisms underlying disease progression.

Beyond DDRTree, other dimensionality reduction and soft-clustering approaches are increasingly demonstrating their value in precision disease subtyping. For instance, in obesity, combining uniform manifold approximation and projection-based dimensionality reduction with Gaussian mixture modeling has been shown to enhance the stratification of population subgroups and improve predictive performance, with findings recently highlighted in Nature Medicine.10 Integrating these approaches with genetic, metabolomic, or multiomic data holds further promise, enabling deeper mechanistic insights and more accurate, individualized risk predictions across diverse populations. Future studies may consider applying advanced dimensionality reduction approaches, such as t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding, TriMap, and PHATE, integrated with machine learning models, including neural networks, to achieve more refined stratification of metabolic diseases.

In summary, DDRTree offers a powerful and flexible framework for characterizing metabolic heterogeneity, enabling both population-level insights and individualized risk assessment. The framework enables intuitive visualization of population subtypes, achieves stable and reproducible clustering, and supports individualized risk prediction. When integrated with multiomics data, it further provides a lens to explore the molecular basis of disease heterogeneity. Crucially, the true strength of positioning individuals within the tree does not lie primarily in improving predictive accuracy over raw variables, but in enhancing the stability, generalizability, and interpretability of the model, thereby offering a robust platform for precision medicine.
