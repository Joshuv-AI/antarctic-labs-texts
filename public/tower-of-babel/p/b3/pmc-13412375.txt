Left atrial remodelling and diastolic unloading after transcatheter tricuspid repair
Authors: Prayuth Rasmeehirun, Andromahi Zygouri, Paul-Calin Craciun, Guillaume L’Official, Vincent Auffret, Guillaume Leurent, Erwan Donal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsSevere tricuspid regurgitation (TR) alters ventricular interaction and left-sided filling dynamics. However, the longitudinal effects of transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) on left atrial (LA) remodelling and diastolic physiology remain poorly characterized. We aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in LA structure, function, and diastolic haemodynamics following T-TEER.MethodsWe studied 125 consecutive patients undergoing T-TEER within the TRI-FR trial and registry (median age 78 [73–81] years; 60.8% women; 73.6% atrial fibrillation). Comprehensive echocardiography was performed at baseline, pre-discharge, and at 6 and 12 months. Longitudinal changes in LA volume index (LAVI), peak atrial longitudinal strain (PALS), E/e′ ratio, LA stiffness index (LASI), and forward flow indices were analysed using linear mixed-effects models.ResultsLeft atrial volume index decreased immediately after T-TEER (estimated change −8.1 ml/m2; 95% CI −14.6 to −1.6; adjusted P = .033), but this reverse remodelling was not sustained at 12 months. In contrast, LA reservoir function deteriorated early and remained impaired throughout follow-up [PALS −1.2%; 95% confidence interval (CI) −2.0 to −0.3; adjusted P = .029]. Estimated LV filling pressure increased acutely (E/e′ + 3.26; 95% CI 2.34–4.18; adjusted P < .001), while LASI remained elevated at 12 months (ratio 1.22; 95% CI 1.05–1.42; adjusted P = .016). Stroke volume index and cardiac index did not change significantly over time (P = .969 and P = .562, respectively). Early mitral regurgitation worsening occurred in 20% of patients but was transient and unrelated to changes in diastolic or forward-flow parameters.ConclusionsTranscatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair induces immediate anatomical LA unloading but does not restore LA mechanical function. Despite early reductions in LA volume, filling pressure surrogates increased, LA stiffness remained elevated, and forward flow did not improve. These findings demonstrate a dissociation between structural and functional remodelling after TR correction and highlight the importance of serial assessment of LA mechanics and diastolic physiology following T-TEER.

Introduction

Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is no longer considered a benign right-sided valvular abnormality, as its significant impact on patient prognosis is increasingly recognized.1 The pathophysiology of advanced TR involves a dual haemodynamic burden, characterized by backward failure with systemic venous congestion and reduced forward cardiac output, both of which contribute to exercise intolerance and heart failure progression.2 While the advent of transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has provided an important therapeutic option for patients at high surgical risk,3,4 clinical attention has largely focused on procedural feasibility and early procedural success. In contrast, the complex mechanisms by which T-TEER influences biventricular physiology and post-procedural haemodynamics remain incompletely understood.

Although right-sided remodelling after T-TEER is of clear clinical importance, the haemodynamic consequences of relieving severe TR extend beyond the right heart through ventricular interdependence and altered left-sided filling conditions. Accordingly, this study was intentionally designed to address the less well-characterized but mechanistically important question of left-heart adaptation following TR correction.

Whether relief of TR leads to true diastolic improvement or instead results in a complex transition in left-sided filling dynamics remains uncertain. Tricuspid regurgitation involves the left heart through adverse ventricular interaction within the constrained pericardial space. Chronic right-sided volume overload leads to right ventricular (RV) and right atrial (RA) dilation, promoting interventricular septal flattening or a leftward septal shift during early diastole. This displacement may reduce left ventricular (LV) transmural pressure and impair LV filling. This mechanism is particularly relevant in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), where impaired LV compliance and external constraint can promote post-capillary pulmonary hypertension, further increasing RV load and exacerbating TR. Together, these interactions suggest that significant TR can actively influence left-sided haemodynamics rather than merely coexist as a bystander, raising the possibility that tricuspid intervention may modify septal geometry and diastolic filling conditions.5

Despite these insights, the longitudinal evolution of LV diastolic function following T-TEER remains incompletely characterized. This is particularly challenging in the acute post-procedural period, when haemodynamic conditions are highly dynamic. Standard Doppler indices such as the E/e′ ratio, while reflecting instantaneous filling pressures, are influenced by loading conditions and may vary early after intervention.6 Furthermore, early worsening of mitral regurgitation (MR) has been observed following T-TEER in a subset of patients7; however, whether this reflects pressure-mediated diastolic perturbation related to increased effective preload or transient, geometry-driven adaptations remains unclear. In this context, left atrial (LA) metrics—including LA volume index (LAVI), reservoir function assessed by peak atrial longitudinal strain (PALS), and LA stiffness index (LASI)—may provide a more integrated assessment of diastolic burden and chamber compliance over time.8 However, serial data tracking these markers from the acute phase through long-term follow-up remain limited.

Accordingly, this study was designed to define the time-dependent left-heart physiological response to T-TEER in patients with advanced TR. Using serial comprehensive echocardiography from baseline to 12 months, we investigated the evolution of LA remodelling, diastolic filling pressure surrogates, and forward flow adaptation through LAVI, PALS, and LASI. Secondary objectives were to examine early MR changes after intervention, their potential haemodynamic relevance, and the durability of structural adaptation over time.

Although clinically important, the prognostic significance of these remodelling trajectories cannot be reliably addressed in the present mechanistic, exploratory study and warrants dedicated investigation in larger outcome-oriented cohorts.

Methods

This was a single-centre retrospective analysis of a prospectively enrolled cohort derived from the TRI-FR randomized clinical trial and the associated TRI-FR registry. The study population consisted of consecutive patients with severe, symptomatic TR who underwent T-TEER at Rennes University Hospital between March 2021 and December 2025.

To ensure a comprehensive and representative real-world population, the cohort included patients randomized to the interventional arm of the TRI-FR trial as well as patients initially assigned to medical therapy who subsequently crossed over to receive T-TEER. Patients were included irrespective of residual TR severity following the procedure, and all TR aetiologies—including atrial, ventricular, and mixed forms—were eligible. Patients with permanent atrial fibrillation and those with prior surgical or transcatheter mitral valve interventions were not excluded.

The only exclusion criterion was inadequate echocardiographic image quality precluding reliable assessment of LA volume or strain parameters. All patients provided written informed consent for participation in the original trial or registry.

Data collection and follow-up

Clinical and echocardiographic data were collected prospectively according to standardized trial and registry protocols, while the present evaluation of LA mechanics and stiffness was performed retrospectively. Serial transthoracic echocardiographic examinations were performed at four predefined time points: baseline, pre-discharge, 6 month follow-up, and 12 month follow-up.

All echocardiographic images were acquired using standardized techniques and analysed offline by an independent core laboratory (CoreLab, CIC 1414, University Hospital of Rennes, France). Readers were blinded to all clinical data, baseline characteristics, and patient outcomes. To ensure consistency across the four time points, longitudinal assessments were performed using predefined measurement anchors and a uniform analysis protocol applied across all examinations.

Echocardiographic acquisition and measurements

Transthoracic echocardiographic examinations were performed using a Vivid E95 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Horten, Norway) equipped with a 4Vc transducer. When clinically indicated, transoesophageal echocardiography was performed using a 6VT-D probe. All studies were digitally stored and analysed offline using EchoPAC version 206 [automated function imaging (AFI)-LA module; GE Healthcare].

TR and MR assessment

Tricuspid regurgitation severity was assessed using a comprehensive multiparametric approach integrating colour Doppler jet characteristics, vena contracta width, proximal isovelocity surface area when feasible, hepatic vein flow patterns, and continuous-wave Doppler signal density and contour. To better characterize advanced disease stages, TR grading was expanded using a five-grade severity classification (mild, moderate, severe, massive, and torrential).

Mitral regurgitation severity was assessed using a multiparametric approach in accordance with current guideline recommendations and classified into four grades: mild (1+), moderate (2+), moderate-to-severe (3+), and severe (4+).

LV function and diastolic indices

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and volumes were calculated using the biplane method of disks (modified Simpson’s rule) from apical four- and two-chamber views. Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS) was measured using two-dimensional speckle-tracking (EchoPAC AFI module) from the three standard apical views to provide a load-independent assessment of myocardial systolic function. Left ventricular diastolic function was assessed using pulsed-wave Doppler and tissue Doppler imaging. Mitral inflow early diastolic velocity (E) was measured at the leaflet tips in the apical four-chamber view. Early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) was obtained at both septal and lateral annular sites, and the average value was used for analysis. The E/e′ ratio was calculated as a surrogate marker of LV filling pressure. Stroke volume index (SVi) was calculated as stroke volume indexed to body surface area and used as a surrogate of forward flow.

LA volume, mechanics, and stiffness

Left atrial volume was measured at ventricular end-systole using the biplane area–length method, excluding the LA appendage and pulmonary veins, and indexed to body surface area (LAVI).

Left atrial strain analysis was performed using two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography with AFI-LA. Peak atrial longitudinal strain, reflecting LA reservoir function, was measured from apical four- and two-chamber views using ventricular end-diastole as the zero-reference point.

The LA stiffness index (LASI) was calculated as (E/e′)/PALS.

For Doppler and strain parameters, measurements were averaged over at least three cardiac cycles in sinus rhythm and five cardiac cycles in atrial fibrillation, whenever feasible.

Beyond right-heart reverse remodelling, correction of severe TR may trigger a complex haemodynamic transition affecting LV filling conditions, atrial mechanics, and ventricular interaction—physiological dimensions that remain substantially less characterized than right-sided structural recovery.

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local Ethics Committee. All patients provided written informed consent permitting the use of clinical and imaging data for subsequent research analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with visual inspection of distribution plots. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median [interquartile range]. Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages.

To evaluate the longitudinal evolution of echocardiographic parameters—including LAVI, PALS, E/e′ ratio, LASI, LVGLS, SVi, and cardiac index—across the four predefined study time points (baseline, pre-discharge, 6 months, and 12 months), linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used. Time was modelled as a categorical fixed effect to avoid assumptions of linear temporal change and to capture potentially non-linear physiological adaptation following T-TEER. A patient-level random intercept was included to account for repeated within-subject measurements and inter-individual baseline variability.

A parsimonious random-intercept structure was intentionally selected, given the sample size and incomplete longitudinal observations, to provide robust estimation while avoiding overparameterization and model instability. This modelling framework allowed inclusion of all available repeated measurements without requiring complete-case analysis and handled missing follow-up data under the assumption that data were missing at random.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between clinically relevant sequential time points (baseline to pre-discharge, pre-discharge to 6 months, and 6 to 12 months) were performed using Holm-adjusted contrasts to account for multiple testing.

To assess the consistency of observed physiological patterns across clinically relevant subgroups, prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed. First, analyses were repeated in patients with procedural success (residual TR ≤2+) and separately in those with mild-or-less residual TR (≤1+) to evaluate the influence of residual regurgitation severity. Second, to minimize confounding by concomitant MR, analyses were repeated in patients without moderate-or-greater baseline MR. Third, given the known influence of atrial rhythm on atrial mechanics, analyses were stratified according to baseline rhythm (sinus rhythm vs atrial fibrillation). Fourth, to exclude potential confounding from changes in LV systolic function, longitudinal GLS trajectories were examined in the overall cohort and relevant subgroups. Results of these sensitivity analyses are presented in the Supplementary material.

For secondary exploratory analyses, the association between acute changes from baseline to pre-discharge in MR grade and contemporaneous changes in diastolic indices (ΔE/e′ and ΔLASI) was assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients (ρ), given the ordinal nature of MR grading and non-normal variable distributions. Bootstrap resampling was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Additional exploratory analyses evaluated whether longitudinal physiological trajectories differed according to baseline LV systolic function by incorporating baseline LVEF category as a fixed effect and testing interaction terms with time.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Model assumptions were assessed through visual inspection of residual Q–Q plots and residual-vs-fitted plots.

The cohort was elderly, with a median age of 78 [73–81] years, and predominantly female (60.8%). A high burden of cardiovascular comorbidities was observed, including atrial fibrillation or flutter in 92 patients (73.6%), hypertension in 67 patients (53.6%), and moderate renal dysfunction, with a median estimated creatinine clearance of 55 [41–68] ml/min. Baseline N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels were markedly elevated, with a median value of 1789 [1012–2654] ng/l. Background medical therapy was indicative of contemporary heart failure management, with loop diuretics prescribed in 95.2% of patients, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in 73.6%, and β-blockers in 71.2%.

Baseline echocardiographic characteristics

Left ventricular systolic function was overall preserved at baseline, with a median LVEF of 55.0 [49.0–60.0]%. Based on LVEF categories, 89 patients (71.2%) were classified as having HFpEF, 24 (19.2%) as heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction, and 12 (9.6%) as heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Right ventricular systolic function was at the lower limit of normal, with a median tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion of 17.0 [14.0–20.0] mm and a median RV fractional area change of 43.0 [39.0–47.0]%.

Baseline TR was severe or greater in all patients. Using the five-grade classification, massive (4+) or torrential (5+) TR was present in 116 patients (92.8%), including 91 patients (72.8%) with massive TR and 26 patients (20.8%) with torrential TR.

Baseline LA and diastolic parameters (primary outcomes)

Crucially for the primary analysis, baseline indices demonstrated advanced LA remodelling and diastolic burden. Left atrial volume index was available in all 125 patients (100%), with a median value of 65.0 [51.0–84.0] ml/m2. Peak atrial longitudinal strain was analysable in 123 patients (98.4%), with a median value of 8.0 [6.0–12.0]%. The E/e′ ratio was available in 116 patients (92.8%), with a median of 9.93 [7.97–14.31]. The LASI, derived from E/e′ and PALS, could be calculated in 114 patients (91.2%), with a median value of 1.19 [0.74–1.98].

At baseline, moderate-or-greater MR (≥2+) was present in 44 patients (35.2%).

LA volume and reservoir function

Left atrial volume index showed a significant overall time effect (P = .023). Model-estimated mean LAVI decreased significantly from baseline to pre-discharge (estimated mean change: −8.1 ml/m2; 95% CI −14.6 to −1.6; Padj = .033), corresponding to a reduction from 69.5 to 61.4 ml/m2 (Figure 1A). No significant changes were observed between pre-discharge and 6-month follow-up (Padj = .72). At 12 months, LAVI remained numerically lower than baseline but did not reach statistical significance (estimated mean change: −4.0 ml/m2; 95% CI −8.1 to 0.1; Padj = 0.11). This suggests early unloading without durable structural reverse remodelling.

In contrast, LA reservoir function did not improve following intervention. Peak atrial longitudinal strain exhibited a significant overall time effect (P = .0089), driven by an acute decline from baseline to pre-discharge (estimated mean change: −1.7%; 95% CI −2.5 to −0.9; Padj = .0002). Peak atrial longitudinal strain continued to decrease modestly at 6 months and remained significantly lower at 12 months compared with baseline (estimated mean change: −1.2%; 95% CI −2.0 to −0.3; Padj = .029; Figure 1B). In contrast to volume changes, LA functional reserve deteriorated and failed to recover.

LV filling pressures and atrial stiffness

Markers of left-sided filling pressure demonstrated a pronounced acute response after T-TEER. The E/e′ ratio showed a highly significant overall time effect (P < .001). From baseline to pre-discharge, E/e′ increased sharply (estimated mean change: +3.26; 95% CI +2.34 to +4.18; Padj < .001), followed by a significant reduction between pre-discharge and 6 months (estimated mean change: −2.14; 95% CI −3.17 to −1.12; Padj = .0003). No further significant change was observed between baseline and 12 months (Padj = .20; Figure 1C).

A parallel pattern was observed for LASI, which was modelled on the logarithmic scale. Left atrial stiffness index increased significantly at pre-discharge (ratio 1.38; 95% CI 1.22–1.56; Padj < .001), indicating acute atrial stiffening. Although LASI decreased modestly at subsequent follow-up, it remained significantly elevated at 12 months compared with baseline (ratio 1.22; 95% CI 1.05–1.42; Padj = .016; Figure 1D), reflecting persistent diastolic constraint despite procedural success.

In summary, successful T-TEER was associated with early LA volume reduction but was accompanied by an acute increase in left-sided filling pressure surrogates and atrial stiffness, followed by only partial mid-term normalization. Notably, LA reservoir function failed to recover and instead declined over time, highlighting a dissociation between anatomical unloading and functional atrial recovery.

Secondary haemodynamic outcomes: forward flow

Forward flow indices showed no significant temporal variation during follow-up. In LMMs, neither SVi nor cardiac index demonstrated a significant overall time effect (Table 3). Model-estimated SVi remained stable from baseline (33.6 ml/m2) to pre-discharge (34.1 ml/m2) and throughout follow-up (33.8 ml/m2 at both 6 and 12 months; Ptime = .969). Similarly, cardiac index did not differ significantly from baseline (2.6 l/min/m2) at any subsequent time point (Ptime = .562).

MR evolution and relationship with acute haemodynamic changes

Among patients with baseline MR grade ≤3, worsening by at least one grade at pre-discharge occurred in 24 of 123 patients (20%). As expected, TR reduction was substantial and sustained over time, whereas MR severity remained globally stable without evidence of progressive deterioration.

To explore potential mechanisms underlying early MR worsening, exploratory correlation analyses were performed between changes in MR grade and contemporaneous changes in diastolic and flow parameters from baseline to pre-discharge. No association was observed between ΔMR and ΔE/e′ (ρ = 0.002, 95% CI −0.171 to 0.192, P = .983) or between ΔMR and ΔLASI (ρ = 0.042, 95% CI −0.154 to 0.241, P = .697). Similarly, no correlation was found between ΔMR and changes in cardiac index (ρ = 0.000, 95% CI −0.187 to 0.196, P = .996) or SVi (ρ = −0.010, 95% CI −0.175 to 0.157, P = .910). No significant associations were observed between changes in MR grade and changes in diastolic or flow parameters from baseline to pre-discharge.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed in clinically relevant subgroups, including patients with successful TR reduction, those with mild-or-less residual TR, patients without moderate-or-greater MR, and those in sinus rhythm at baseline. In all subgroups, the acute increase in E/e′ and the absence of change in SVi persisted, while LVGLS remained stable over time (Supplementary Tables S2–S4).

Discussion

Transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair induces a distinct haemodynamic response rather than immediate diastolic recovery. This observation suggests about a dissociated pattern characterized by early anatomical unloading of the left atrium (reduced LAVI) alongside an increase in filling pressure surrogates (E/e′) and persistent impairment of atrial reservoir function (PALS). Importantly, these changes occurred independently of MR fluctuations and without any increase in forward flow, as both SVi and cardiac index remained unchanged. This pattern is most indicative of redistribution of intracardiac volume and exposure of pre-existing LV diastolic dysfunction following relief of chronic right-sided volume overload, rather than true improvement in LV filling or cardiac output.

The apparent rise in filling pressures following TR reduction can be understood through altered ventricular interaction. In advanced TR, chronic right-sided volume overload leads to septal displacement, which limits effective left-sided filling and may attenuate measured filling pressures.9 Acute reduction of TR with T-TEER relieves this constraint, restores interventricular geometry, and increases effective pulmonary venous return to the left heart.10 In a normal ventricle, this increase in preload would augment stroke volume. However, in our cohort—predominantly composed of patients with HFpEF—the left ventricle operates on the steep portion of the pressure–volume relationship and exhibits limited preload reserve. This concept is well established: invasive pressure-volume studies have demonstrated that HFpEF patients have a steep end-diastolic pressure-volume relation,11 and during preload augmentation, they fail to increase stroke volume despite a rise in filling pressure.12,13 Consequently, the augmented preload in our patients results in higher filling pressures (E/e′, LASI) without a corresponding increase in stroke volume. This lack of forward flow augmentation is consistent with prior meta-analyses reporting only modest increases in stroke volume after tricuspid intervention (mean increase ∼4 ml)14 and supports the concept that preload reserve is limited in HFpEF. Given the observational design, these findings support physiological association rather than mechanistic proof, and the proposed interpretation of limited LV preload reserve should be viewed as a plausible, hypothesis-generating explanation rather than a demonstrated causal mechanism.

A dissociation between LA structural and functional remodelling following T-TEER is observed. While LA volume decreased (LAVI reduction), reservoir function (PALS) remained persistently impaired. This pattern suggests that early LAVI reduction reflects an anatomical response to relief of right-sided volume overload and ventricular interaction,15 whereas atrial function does not recover in parallel. In advanced TR, right-sided volume overload may impose external constraint on left-sided chambers through interventricular interaction.16 Following T-TEER, alleviation of this constraint may allow the LA to adopt a smaller volume despite a concurrent rise in filling pressure surrogates (E/e′), reflecting the interplay between external constraint and intrinsic diastolic properties.

In contrast, persistently impaired PALS highlights the limited reversibility of atrial functional abnormalities. This indicates the presence of underlying atrial myopathy,17 likely related to chronic pressure exposure and the high prevalence of atrial fibrillation in this cohort. In this context, LAVI reduction primarily reflects changes in loading conditions, whereas the absence of PALS recovery, together with the rise in E/e′, indicates persistent diastolic limitation of the left heart.

Importantly, these findings should be interpreted in light of the known limitations of atrial strain assessment in populations with atrial fibrillation and concomitant valvular disease.6 Rather than relying on absolute values, our analysis focuses on within-patient longitudinal changes, which are less sensitive to these limitations and allow identification of consistent physiological patterns over time. Left ventricular GLS remained stable across all timepoints and subgroups. The absence of change in GLS provides supportive evidence that the observed alterations in PALS are unlikely to be driven by changes in LV longitudinal shortening and instead reflect intrinsic atrial dysfunction and loading conditions. In this framework, LA strain may provide complementary insight into residual atrial dysfunction beyond volume-based metrics. Figure 2 conceptually illustrates how relief of right-sided volume overload may expose persistent left-sided diastolic constraint despite apparent anatomical unloading. Because atrial fibrillation strongly influences LA size and strain, interpretation of atrial mechanics in this setting necessarily requires caution. However, the predominance of atrial fibrillation in our cohort reflects the expected phenotype of advanced TR patients undergoing T-TEER and therefore enhances, rather than diminishes, the clinical relevance of the study. While analyses in sinus rhythm are informative, they should be considered exploratory given limited sample size and should not supersede observations derived from the full representative cohort.

Evolution of the left atrial (LA) function according to the phenotype

Early changes in MR after T-TEER were common but directionally inconsistent. These changes were not associated with shifts in filling pressure surrogates (E/e′), LASI, or forward flow, suggesting that MR is unlikely to be a primary driver of the observed diastolic response. We should remain cautious as for instance, LASI is mathematically derived from E/e′ and PALS, it should be viewed as an integrative composite descriptor rather than an independent confirmatory marker of diastolic physiology.

One might hypothesize that MR worsening could result from left-sided chamber enlargement. However, no consistent pattern of worsening was observed, and LA volume decreased early after the procedure. Instead, MR fluctuations are more likely related to transient geometric adaptations. Relief of chronic right-sided volume overload may permit a rightward shift of the interventricular septum, restoring a more physiological LV geometry and altering mitral annular configuration without requiring chamber dilatation.

These findings are consistent with observations from the TriValve Registry, where MR worsening was infrequent (≈10%) and associated primarily with procedural or geometric factors rather than haemodynamic deterioration.7 Taken together, early MR changes after T-TEER most likely represent a secondary geometric phenomenon rather than a primary determinant of left-sided haemodynamics or forward flow.

These findings have several important clinical implications for the management of patients undergoing T-TEER. First, early post-procedural changes in diastolic indices should be interpreted with caution. An acute rise in left-sided filling pressure surrogates should not be considered indicative of procedural failure or new-onset deterioration, but rather may represent an expected physiological response following effective TR reduction. Second, the dissociation between LA volume reduction and persistently impaired reservoir function highlights the limitations of relying solely on volume-based metrics for post-procedural assessment. In this context, LA strain may provide complementary information by identifying patients with advanced atrial disease and limited functional reserve who may remain symptomatic despite anatomical improvement. Third, the largely transient and non-predictive nature of MR changes suggests that early MR worsening after T-TEER should prompt careful longitudinal follow-up rather than immediate intervention.

These observations underscore the importance of a comprehensive, time-aware echocardiographic follow-up strategy after T-TEER that integrates diastolic indices and atrial functional assessment beyond conventional chamber size evaluation. A key strength of this study is the serial assessment across multiple timepoints, which reveals a non-linear and time-dependent physiological response that would not be captured by conventional baseline-to-follow-up comparisons alone. In particular, the early rise in filling pressure at pre-discharge followed by partial normalization highlights the dynamic nature of post-procedural adaptation. Without these intermediate assessments, the exposure of underlying diastolic dysfunction and the dissociation between structural and functional atrial remodelling would likely remain unrecognized. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of our HFpEF-predominant cohort, in whom limited preload reserve likely constrains forward flow augmentation despite effective TR reduction.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its observational design and the absence of a control group preclude causal inference regarding the mechanisms underlying the left-sided physiological adaptations observed after T-TEER. Second, left-sided filling pressures and diastolic function were assessed using echocardiographic surrogate parameters rather than invasive haemodynamic measurements. Although these indices remain load dependent, the longitudinal within-patient design likely reduced the impact of interindividual variability.

Third, previous surgical or transcatheter mitral valve interventions were not systematically recorded and therefore could not be incorporated into the analyses. Because prior mitral intervention may influence LA remodelling, atrial mechanics, filling pressures, and residual MR, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded.

Fourth, the study was designed to investigate physiological adaptation rather than clinical outcomes. Consequently, it was not powered to evaluate associations between remodelling trajectories and long-term prognosis or symptom improvement, and outcome-based subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, the cohort consisted predominantly of patients with advanced secondary TR, a high prevalence of atrial fibrillation, and extensive atrial remodelling. While this reflects the population currently referred for transcatheter tricuspid intervention in routine practice, the findings may not be generalizable to patients with earlier-stage disease or preserved atrial substrate. The aim of the present study was therefore not to characterize intrinsic atrial recovery in an idealized sinus rhythm population, but rather to describe the left-sided haemodynamic adaptation following TR correction in the patients most commonly encountered in contemporary structural heart disease practice.
