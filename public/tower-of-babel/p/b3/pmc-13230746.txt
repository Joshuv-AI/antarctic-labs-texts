Targeting IP6 signaling to destabilize homologous recombination proteins to overcome PARP inhibitor resistance
Authors: Seon-gyeong Lee, Yuri Seo, Seula Jeong, Yuheon Chung, Sukyeong Kong, Minyoung Kim, Joon Ho Rhlee, Sihyeon Um, Bijoy P Mathew, Saikat Maiti, Malleswara Rao Kuram, Mohamed Ahmed Abozeid, Areum Park, Ji-Na Yoo, Keon Woo Khim, Kyuwon Son, Enkhzul Amarsanaa, Kyunghan Kim, Sehoon Hong, Jiyeon Choi, In Bae Park, Eun A Lee, Ji Hwan Jeon, Jun Hong Park, Joo Seok Han, Chan Young Park, Seyun Kim, Jang Hyun Choi, Sung You Hong, Min-Duk Seo, Hyuk Lee, Joo-Yong Lee, Kyungjae Myung

ABSTRACT
AbstractHomologous recombination (HR) is crucial for maintaining genomic integrity and is tightly regulated, yet the role of ubiquitin-dependent degradation in HR proteins remains poorly understood. Through high-throughput screening for compounds that modulate the DNA replication stress response, we identify ML367 and its derivative, UNI418. Kinase profiling and detail molecular analyses reveal that UNI418 inhibits PIKfyve and PIP5K1C, reducing inositol hexaphosphate (IP6) levels and triggering Cul4A-dependent degradation of RAD51, CtIP, and CHK1. Further analysis identifies WDR5 as a DCAF protein that facilitates Cul4A-mediated proteolysis of RAD51 and CHK1. Functionally, UNI418 suppresses HR, enhances tumor sensitivity to PARP inhibitors (PARPis), and re-sensitizes PARPi-resistant tumor cells in both in vitro and in vivo xenograft models. These findings reveal a Cul4A-WDR5-dependent proteolysis pathway regulating HR protein stability via phosphatidyl inositol signaling. This mechanism offers a promising therapeutic strategy for overcoming PARPi resistance and improving combinatorial cancer treatment strategies.Subject terms: Targeted therapies, DNA, Small molecules, Homologous recombination

Introduction

Genomic instability is a hallmark characteristic of most cancer cells, which is supported by mounting evidence indicating that impaired DNA repair fosters mutator phenotypes and chromosomal abnormalities, thereby accelerating cellular transformation1,2. Homologous recombination (HR) is a high-fidelity DNA repair pathway that resolves DSBs using a homologous DNA template. This process is essential for maintaining genome stability, particularly during the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle when a sister chromatid is available3. RAD51, which is a major recombinase in HR, functions by forming nucleoprotein filaments on single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and facilitating strand invasion into homologous sequences, enabling error-free double-strand break (DSB) repair. Given its essential role in genome integrity, RAD51 expression is tightly regulated at multiple levels, including transcription, post-transcriptional modifications, and post-translational mechanisms. RAD51 expression is regulated by various transcription factors in response to DNA damage and cell cycle progression. The tumor suppressor p53 represses RAD51 transcription under genotoxic stress, preventing excessive HR that could lead to genomic instability4. E2F1 positively regulates RAD51 expression, particularly during the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle, when HR is most active5. Compared to the well-established understanding of RAD51 mRNA regulation, the regulation of RAD51 protein levels remains relatively unexplored. The E3 ubiquitin ligase RFWD3 has been identified as a ubiquitin ligase that affects both the single-stranded DNA-binding protein RPA and RAD51 on chromatin6.

Cullin 4 A (Cul4A) is a scaffold protein in the CRL4 ubiquitin ligase complex7, which includes Damage-Specific DNA Binding Protein 1 (DDB1) and DDB1-CUL4 Associated Factors (DCAFs). Cul4A plays a crucial role in maintaining genome stability by targeting key regulatory proteins for ubiquitin-mediated degradation. Dysregulation of Cul4A has been implicated in various cancers, promoting cell proliferation, genomic instability, and therapy resistance.

The increased genomic instability observed in cancer frequently originates from deficiencies in one or more DNA repair pathways, often providing cancer cells with a selective advantage in advancing toward a more malignant phenotype. These defects compel cancer cells to rely on the remaining functional DNA repair mechanisms for survival, particularly considering the heightened proliferation rates8. Exploiting this vulnerability presents an opportunity for targeted intervention synthetic lethality approaches. In this context, inhibiting a key survival DNA pathway becomes lethal upon inhibition of a redundant pathway9.

The synthetic lethal approach is particularly successful in targeting BRCA-and other HR-deficient cancers10,11. Mutations in either BRCA1 or BRCA2 predispose individuals to the development of breast or ovarian cancers, and approximately 10% of breast and 15% of ovarian cancers are associated with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations. The BRCA1 and BRCA2 proteins play crucial roles in HR, DNA replication fork protection, and ssDNA gap suppression3,12. Mutations in these genes result in compelling tumor cells to rely on alternative repair mechanisms which relies on PARP1-dependent ssDNA break/gap repair, replication fork protection, translesion synthesis, or DSB repair12–16. Inhibition of PARP1 in breast and ovarian cancers with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations results in significant cell death in these tumors while sparing normal cells8. The approval of clinically used PARP1/2-trapping inhibitors (commonly referred to as PARP inhibitors, PARPis), such as Olaparib, Niraparib, Rucaparib, Veliparib, and Talazoparib, by the US FDA marks a pivotal advancement of treatment for breast and ovarian cancers carrying BRCA mutations12. These inhibitors trap PARP1 at ssDNA breaks or gaps, inducing cell death in HR-deficient cancer cells17. However, despite the efficacy of those compounds, tumors often develop resistance to PARPi12,18. This resistance can arise through various mechanisms, including the restoration of HR including via secondary mutations or BRCA gene reactivation, re-expression of BRCA expression, or inactivation of DNA damage sensor proteins such as 53BP1. Additional resistance mechanisms include upregulation of translesion synthesis and drug metabolism-related processes, such as enhanced drug efflux18. There are currently no effective treatment options for PARPi-resistant tumors, and it is imperative to explore strategies for overcoming resistance to PARPi.

ATAD5 (also known as ELG1 in yeast) is an essential PCNA unloader that maintains genome stability during DNA replication. Loss or depletion of ATAD5 results in persistent PCNA retention on chromatin, replication fork stalling, and elevated replication stress, ultimately contributing to genomic instability and tumorigenesis19. Importantly, ATAD5-luciferase expression of the reporter cell line is markedly induced under replication stress, and this increase has been widely used as an established molecular marker for replication stress responses20. In 2010, we reported that a small molecule ML367 selectively suppresses the elevation of ATAD5-luciferase protein levels in the reporter cell under replication stress conditions21.

We have exploited induction of the ATAD5 protein during replication stress as a marker to screen for compounds that induce replication stress response22–24. In the current study, we identified ML367 and its derivative UNI418 as an inhibitory compound of replication stress response and revealed that they inhibit PIKfyve and PIP5K1C kinases, leading to reduced cellular inositol hexaphosphate (IP6) levels and subsequent Cul4A-dependent degradation of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP, revealing a mechanism of HR inhibition. We also found that WDR5 is a DCAF protein of Cul4A determining substrate specificity of Cul4A to RAD51 and CHK1. Due to the inhibition of key proteins for HR, gap repair, and DNA replication stress response proteins, UNI418 induced selective toxicity in PARP1-deficient or PARPi-resistant cancer cells and tumors in xenograft models. We propose that this pathway for regulating HR by the phosphatidyl inositol signaling pathway may provide a vulnerability for BRCA-deficient tumors.

Results

Identification of a compound, UNI418 that inhibits DNA replication stress response

Our prior investigations revealed that ML367 functions as an inhibitor of the DNA replication stress responses elicited by 5-fluorouridine (5-FUrd)21. Subsequent screening of approximately 100 structurally analogous derivatives of ML367 yielded compounds with enhanced efficacy in mitigating DNA replication stress. Among these derivatives, UNI418 was identified as the most effective agent (Fig. 1a, b). The capacity of ML367 and UNI418 to attenuate DNA replication stress was quantitatively evaluated utilizing the ATAD5-luciferase assay (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Notably, UNI418 suppressed the 5-FUrd-induced DNA replication stress response more potently than ML367, as reflected by a lower IC50 for inhibition of ATAD5 luciferase expression. This finding is supported by the observed reductions in phosphorylation of RPA32 at serine residues S4 and S8, as well as CHK1, reflecting the suppression of the DNA replication stress response upon treatment with ML367 or UNI418 under DNA replication stress conditions such as camptothecin (CPT), hydroxyurea (HU) and 5-FUrd (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 1b). Interestingly, UNI418 treatment reduced the level of CHK1 as well as its phosphorylation, even in the absence of DNA replication stress, suggesting it affects CHK1 expression. In summary, UNI418 is a inhibitory compound of the responses to DNA replication stress.

UNI418 inhibits PIKfyve and PIP5K1C

Given that UNI418 possesses a core 4-aminoquinazoline moiety—a structural motif commonly observed in kinase inhibitors25, known for its affinity towards the ATP binding pocket—we postulated that UNI418 might exert its biological effects through kinase inhibition. To elucidate the specific kinase(s) targeted by UNI418, we conducted comprehensive kinase-profiling analyses using the DiscoverX kinome profiling service (https://www.discoverx.com/)26. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, kinase profiling analysis reveals that UNI418 caused the potent inhibition of PIKfyve, a lipid kinase. To verify the result, we performed PIPK profiling analysis and found that UNI418 inhibited PIP5K1B, PIP5K1C, and PIKfyve27. The calculated dissociation constant (KD) of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C was 0.74 nM, and 61 nM, respectively (Fig. 2b). Notably, a third lipid kinase, PIP5K1B, demonstrated only partial inhibition under the conditions tested (data not shown). In kinase-profiling analyses, UNI418 demonstrated inhibitory activity against 11 additional kinases. Subsequently, we conducted a more detailed assessment of 9 kinases among them for potential inhibition by UNI418 utilizing Thermo Fisher Scientific’s SelectScreen 11-point titration Profiling analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2a). None of them exhibited inhibition by UNI418 with IC50 values falling below the 1 nM range (PIKfyve) and only CSF1R, FLT3, and MINK1 exhibited inhibition by UNI418 with IC50 values falling below the 100 nM range (PIP5K1C). To discern potential off-target effects of UNI418 on these three kinases as well as BUB1 and GCN2, which were not further analyzed through profiling, we examined the protein levels of CHK1 following knockdown of their expression (Supplementary Fig. 2b). In contrast to the impact observed with PIKfyve knockdown, silencing the expression of these kinases did not significantly reduce CHK1 level.

To determine whether UNI418 directly engages with these lipid kinases, we conducted a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis. For this purpose, we immobilized the maltose-binding protein (MBP)-tagged kinase domain of the respective proteins onto a CM5 chip, a widely utilized platform for ligand immobilization. The purity and integrity of the recombinant proteins were confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3a). UNI418 was then injected as an analyte over the chip surface, and changes in resonance units (RU), which reflect molecular mass accumulation on the sensor surface, were monitored. Sensorgrams were generated to visualize the interaction kinetics. To ensure accurate measurement of compound-specific binding, sensorgrams were blank-subtracted using signals obtained from injections containing DMSO alone at the same concentration used to solubilize UNI418. This subtraction removed refractive-index changes and bulk shifts attributable to DMSO, allowing the binding response induced specifically by UNI418 to be assessed. The blank-subtracted sensorgram data unequivocally demonstrated the direct interaction of UNI418 with both PIKfyve and PIP5K1C, corroborating the findings regarding UNI418’s inhibitory activity against these lipid kinases (Fig. 2c). There was minimal interaction between UNI418 and MBP, which was fused to the kinases used in this study for purification (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We sought to observe evidence for the interaction of UNI418 with the two kinases, PIKfyve and PIP5K1C in cells. To achieve this, we employed the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA), a technique designed to measure the stabilization of a protein to heat denaturation upon ligand binding28. We treated cell lysates with either UNI418 or DMSO and subjected them to varying temperatures. The presence of the target proteins in the soluble fraction was then analyzed using western blotting. Our results revealed that after treatment with UNI418, ectopically expressed PIKfyve and PIP5K1C exhibited increased stability at higher temperatures than the DMSO (Fig. 2d). This observation suggests that UNI418 interacts with PIKfyve and PIP5K1C within cellular environments, corroborating our in vitro findings.

UNI418 reduces the level of phosphorylated inositol species in cells

Lipid kinases, including PIKfyve and PIP5K1C, play crucial roles in phosphorylating inositol-containing molecules, which are integral to various cellular signaling pathways as second messengers29,30. Specifically, PIKfyve phosphorylates PI(3)P to produce PI(3,5)P2, while PIP5K1C catalyzes the phosphorylation of PI(4)P and PI(3,4)P2 to generate PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P3, respectively (Fig. 2e)31.

In our investigation, we initially assessed the levels of phosphoinositides following UNI418 treatment using the phosphoinositides analysis service in the Creative Proteomics Lipidomics (https://lipidomics.creative-proteomics.com/). Notably, the PIP, PIP2, and PIP3 levels were all reduced upon treatment with UNI418 or the PIKfyve inhibitor Apilimod (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Given that PI(4,5)P2 is enzymatically cleaved by phospholipase C to generate I(1,4,5)P3, which is subsequently phosphorylated to form more phosphorylated inositol species including I(1,2,3,4,5,6)P6 (IP6), also known as inositol hexaphosphate32, we hypothesized that UNI418 may inhibit IP6 production, which has been linked to the regulation of CHK1 protein level33. Consistent with our hypothesis, UNI418 treatment, as well as Apilimod or UNC3230, which inhibits PIKfyve and PIP5K1C, respectively, resulted in a reduction of IP6 as well as IP3, IP4, and IP5 in cells (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 4b, c).

To identify the interaction residues of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C with UNI418, we modeled their potential interactions. We identified two possible interaction pockets in both PIKfyve and PIP5K1C: one in ATP binding pocket and the other predicted binding pocket using RosettaDock and AutoDock programs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5a)34,35. To determine which pocket interacts with UNI418, we introduced amino acid substitutions in these regions. The purified mutant proteins were analyzed using CETSA to assess their interaction with UNI418. The ATP-binding pocket mutation (ABM) in PIKfyve (ABM1, L2020A) disrupted its interaction with UNI418, whereas the wild-type (WT) protein and other mutants—ABM2 (I2875A, K1877A), predicted binding pocket mutation (PBM)1 (D1951A), and PBM2 (K1953A, R1959A)—retained their interaction with UNI418 (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Similarly, in PIP5K1C, the ABM1 mutation (L320A) abolished its interaction with UNI418, while the WT protein and other mutants, ABM2 (O186A, K188A) still interacted with UNI418 (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Collectively, these results indicate that UNI418 interacts with both PIKfyve and PIP5K1C through their ATP-binding pockets. This result aligns with the presence of a core 4-aminoquinazoline moiety in UNI418, which is known to interact with ATP-binding pockets25.

UNI418 treatment down-regulates the level of RAD51 and CtIP as well as CHK1 and inhibits HR

Unexpected downregulation of CHK1 protein by UNI418 treatment, along with reduced CHK1 phosphorylation in response to DNA replication stress (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b), suggests that inhibiting PIKfyve and PIP5K1C may activate proteasomal degradation of CHK1. Previous study has shown that Cul4A plays a role in ubiquitinating various proteins in response to DNA damage and regulating CHK1 protein levels33. To explore this further, we investigated whether UNI418 downregulates CHK1 protein by activating proteolysis. First, we examined whether UNI418 treatment affects the levels of other DSB repair-associated proteins. In addition to CHK1, we found that RAD51 and CtIP levels were also reduced following UNI418 treatment (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). BRCA1 level was also mildly reduced upon UNI418 treatment. This reduction was not observed in other DSB repair proteins such as MRE11, Ku80 and DNA-PKcs.

CtIP and RAD51 are essential for HR, with CtIP initiating DNA end resection to generate ssDNA tails required for HR. RAD51 binds to the resected ssDNA to form a nucleoprotein filament that facilitates homology search and strand invasion. Upon ionizing radiation (IR)-induced DSBs, CtIP and RAD51 rapidly localize to damage sites36. To assess the impact of UNI418 on HR efficiency, we examined CtIP and RAD51 accumulation at DNA damage-induced foci following IR exposure. After 10 Gy IR, RAD51 and CtIP formed distinct nuclear foci that co-localized with the DNA damage marker γH2AX. However, UNI418 treatment significantly reduced RAD51 foci formation and overall nuclear RAD51 intensity (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 6b), as well as CtIP foci formation (Supplementary Fig. 6c) correlating with decreased protein levels (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 6a). These results suggest that UNI418 downregulates HR efficiency.

To further investigate the mechanism underlying UNI418-mediated HR inhibition, we analyzed the distribution and abundance of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP in chromatin and soluble fractions following hydroxyurea (HU) treatment, with and without UNI418 (Supplementary Fig. 6d). UNI418 reduced RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP protein levels in both soluble and chromatin fractions.

Proteolysis mediates this reduction, as co-treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 restored RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP levels (Fig. 3c). Notably, UNI418 treatment did not alter RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP mRNA levels, indicating that it specifically affects protein stability rather than transcriptional regulation (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Protein degradation kinetics studies using cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiment further confirmed that UNI418 accelerates HR protein degradation compared to control cells (Fig. 3d). Previously, inositol polyphosphate multikinase (IPMK) has been identified as a key regulator involved in modifying I(1,4,5)P3 to I(1,3,4,5)P4, and subsequently to I(1,3,4,5,6)P5. Notably, IPMK has been implicated in the regulation of nuclear export of RAD51 mRNA37. To investigate whether UNI418 influences RAD51 mRNA export, we isolated nuclear and cytoplasmic mRNA fractions from RPE1 cells treated with UNI418. Upon UNI418 treatment, we observed no significant alteration in the cytoplasmic levels of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP mRNAs, while a modest increase in CHK1 and CtIP mRNA levels was detected in the nuclear fraction (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, assessment of mRNA localization using RAD51 RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed no significant perturbation upon UNI418 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These results collectively suggest that UNI418 does not inhibit RAD51 mRNA transport, thus excluding this mechanism as a mode of action for UNI418 in regulating RAD51 expression.

Next, we investigated whether HR frequency would be affected by UNI418 treatment. We employed DSB repair reporter assays designed to quantitatively assess HR and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair pathways. These assays utilize a GFP-based readout to signal successful DSB repair via HR or NHEJ following targeted cleavage by the I-SceI restriction endonuclease38. UNI418 treatment significantly reduced HR repair, with only a marginal impact on NHEJ repair (Fig. 3e). Notably, this reduction in HR frequency was less pronounced than that observed upon siRAD51-mediated knockdown, consistent with the partial reduction of RAD51 protein levels induced by UNI418 (Supplementary Fig. 7c).

Lastly, we investigated whether UNI418 would cause cells sensitive to PARP1 deficiency and PARPi, Olaparib. We determined the IC50 of UNI418 for selectively targeting PARP1-deficient cells. We subjected colorectal tumor HCT116 cells and their isogenic counterparts lacking PARP1 (HCT116 PARP1-/- cells, generated via targeted gene deletion, Supplementary Fig. 7d) to varying concentrations of UNI418 and measured cellular survival. Notably, the IC50 values of UNI418 in HCT116 and HCT116 PARP1–/– cells were measured at 1.65 ± 0.23 μM and 0.44 ± 0.04 μM, respectively (Fig. 3f). Furthermore, treatment with UNI418 induced a greater extent of apoptotic cell death in HCT116 PARP1–/– cells compared to their WT (Fig. 3g).

To examine the basis of this dose-dependent hypersensitivity, we performed dose-response analyses of UNI418 (0–5 μM) and assessed the levels of RAD51, CHK1, CtIP, Cul4A, and γH2AX in both cell types. HR-associated proteins exhibited a gradual, dose-dependent reduction in WT cells, whereas PARP1–/– cells showed a more rapid and pronounced decrease at lower UNI418 concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In parallel, γH2AX accumulation was markedly enhanced in PARP1–/– cells relative to WT cells. These results indicate that the heightened sensitivity of PARP1-deficient cells to UNI418 arises from their increased reliance on HR for genome maintenance, rather than from nonspecific off-target toxicity.

Consistent with the enhanced efficacy of UNI418 against PARP1-/- cells, we also observed a synergistic cytotoxic effect of UNI418 when combined with the PARPi, Olaparib in HCT116 WT cells, resulting in increased apoptotic cell death (Fig. 3h,i and Supplementary Fig. 8b). The increased concentration of Olaparib exhibited a combination index lower than 1, as determined by the CompuSyn algorithm39, indicating synergism (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

Collectively, these findings suggest that UNI418 promotes proteolytic degradation of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP, impairing HR repair mechanisms resulting in enhanced cytotoxicity in PARP1 inhibition condition.

PIKfyve and PIP5K1C inhibition down-regulates HR

To determine that PIP5K1C and PIKfyve, the lipid kinases inhibited by UNI418, are indeed responsible for mediating the UNI418-induced proteasomal degradation of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP, we conducted experiments involving the knockdown of these kinases using siRNA. Upon treating cells with siRNA targeting PIP5K1C or PIKfyve, we observed a significant reduction in the protein levels of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP, akin to the effects induced by UNI418 treatment (Fig. 4a, b). UNI418 treatment did not further decrease the levels of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP under PIKfyve knockdown conditions. Notably, the knockdown of PIP5K1B did not elicit substantial changes in the levels of RAD51 or CHK1 proteins. Furthermore, consistent with the observed reductions in HR proteins, HR efficiency was significantly diminished upon knockdown of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve, but not PIP5K1B (Fig. 4c).

We next examined whether inhibition of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve influences cellular sensitivity to the PARP inhibitor Olaparib. Cells depleted of either kinase exhibited increased sensitivity to Olaparib compared with control cells, and dual depletion of both kinases resulted in a more pronounced reduction in cell viability, indicating an additive effect between PIP5K1C and PIKfyve (Fig. 4d). Under the CellTiter-Glo viability assay following 72 h Olaparib treatment, BRCA2 knockdown cells also showed increased sensitivity to Olaparib, with a reduction in viability similar in scale to that observed upon combined depletion of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve. Notably, clonogenic survival assays performed with extended Olaparib exposure showed substantially stronger hypersensitivity in BRCA2-depleted cells, consistent with canonical HR-deficient phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 8d).

Importantly, UNI418 treatment alone reduced cell viability to a similar extent as single or combined knockdown of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve, and no additional reduction was observed upon further kinase depletion (Fig. 4e). This lack of additivity indicates that UNI418 and genetic depletion of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve act through the same functional pathway, supporting a shared mechanism involving inhibition of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve activity.

Together, these findings suggest that UNI418 exerts its effects on RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP through the inhibition of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C. The observed reductions in HR proteins and HR efficiency upon knockdown of these kinases, as well as the increased sensitivity to Olaparib upon their depletion, underscore the crucial role of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C in mediating UNI418-induced effects on DSB repair and cellular response to DNA damage.

Reduction of IP6 level regulates Cul4A-dependent proteolysis of HR proteins

Cul4A is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that facilitates the degradation of target proteins. Its activity is tightly controlled by neddylation, a post-translational modification that enhances its ability to recruit substrate adaptors and promote ubiquitination40. A previous study has shown that Cul4A plays a role in ubiquitinating various proteins in response to DNA damage including CHK133. We explored the possibility of Cul4A for proteasomal degradation of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP in response to UNI418 through IP6 level change. UNI418 treatment increased the ratio of neddylated Cul4A to unneddylated Cul4A (Fig. 5a). Conversely, supplementation with IP6, both in cells or extracts, counteracted the UNI418-induced Cul4A neddylation (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 9a). Furthermore, the depletion of PIP5K1C and PIKfyve, akin to UNI418 treatment, increased the ratio of neddylated Cul4A (Fig. 5b). Importantly, neither UNI418 treatment nor knockdown of PIKfyve or PIP5K1C led to an increase in the absolute level of neddylated Cul4A. Instead, both procedures augmented the ratio of neddylated Cul4A to unneddylated Cul4A, as observed in previous studies41. This observation suggests that the unneddylated form of Cul4A may potentially play an inhibitory role in neddylated Cul4A, or that neddylated Cul4A quickly ubiquitinates its substrate, leading to turnover.

To address whether Cul4A can directly regulate HR proteins such as RAD51 and CtIP, we investigated the impact of Cul4A depletion on the levels of RAD51, CHK1 and CtIP in the presence or absence of UNI418. Upon depletion of Cul4A using siRNA, RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP levels increased compared to control cells (Fig. 5c). In contrast, their level increase was less pronounced under UNI418 treatment conditions. These findings suggest that Cul4A is responsible for the degradation of RAD51, CHK1 and CtIP upon UNI418 treatment. Furthermore, supplementation of cells with IP6 antagonized UNI418-induced HR protein degradation and promoted RAD51 foci formation upon IR treatment (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 9b). UNI418-mediated reduction in HR frequency was also restored upon additional supplementation of IP6 (Fig. 5e). Moreover, in the presence of IP6, the hypersensitivity of PARP1–/– cells to UNI418 was fully suppressed, indicating that reducing IP6 levels is a key contributor to the HR defects induced by UNI418 (Supplementary Fig. 9c). However, it is important to note that IP6 exhibited toxicity in both WT and PARP1–/– cells.

Inositol-pentakisphosphate-2-kinase (IPPK) plays a pivotal role in catalyzing the conversion of IP5 to IP6. To elucidate whether UNI418 is primarily acts by a reduction in IP6 levels, we anticipated that depletion of IPPK would phenocopy the effects induced by UNI418. Indeed, upon depletion of IPPK using siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 9f), we observed a reduction in the levels of HR proteins even in the absence of UNI418 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Moreover, IPPK-depleted cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to Olaparib, mirroring the phenotype observed upon UNI418 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9e).

WDR5 is a DCAF of Cul4A for proteolysis of RAD51and CHK1

Cul4A substrate specificity is determined by its interacting proteins, known as DCAFs. With over 60 predicted DCAFs42, we aimed to identify the one responsible for RAD51 proteolysis. To this end, we screened available siRNAs targeting DCAF genes and monitored RAD51 and CHK1 levels. Among them, WDR5 silencing consistently increased RAD51 and CHK1 levels (Fig. 6a). In contrast, WDR5 knockdown did not increase CtIP levels, which suggest CtIP is regulated by other DCAF. Additional proteasome inhibition using MG132 showed further enhancement of the observed effects, suggesting that residual protein levels due to incomplete knockdown of WDR5 and Cul4A may have masked the full recovery of RAD51 and CHK1. Consistent with the role of WDR5 as a potential DCAF for Cul4A, the simultaneous silencing of Cul4A and WDR5 did not further elevate RAD51 and CHK1 levels. However, while WDR5 knockdown increased RAD51 and CHK1 levels, its overexpression did not result in a corresponding decrease (data not shown). The substrate of an E3 ubiquitin ligase transiently interacts with its E3 ligase43. We examined the potential interaction between RAD51 and CHK1 with WDR5. In the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132, WDR5 was able to pull down RAD51 and CHK1 as well as Cul4A and its interacting partner, DDB1 (Fig. 6b). To assess whether a slight increase in RAD51 and CHK1 levels influences sensitivity to PARPi, cell survival was measured following exposure to a high concentration of Olaparib after silencing WDR5, Cul4A or both. Silencing of WDR5, Cul4A, or their combination conferred resistance to Olaparib. (Fig. 6c). Collectively, we identified WDR5 as a key determinant of RAD51 and CHK1 proteolysis through Cul4A, highlighting its regulatory role in HR.

Previously, RFWD3 was identified as an E3 ligase that facilitates the degradation of RAD51 following the completion of HR6. To determine whether both RFWD3 and Cul4A contribute to RAD51 proteolysis, we assessed RAD51 levels upon siRNA-mediated depletion of RFWD3 (Supplementary Fig. 9g) and Cul4A, individually and in combination. Silencing Cul4A resulted in increased RAD51 levels under both basal and IR-induced conditions (Fig. 6d). In contrast, RFWD3 depletion led to RAD51 accumulation exclusively in response to IR. Notably, co-silencing RFWD3 and Cul4A did not further augment RAD51 levels beyond those observed upon individual knockdowns. These findings suggest that Cul4A governs RAD51 homeostasis under normal physiological conditions, whereas RFWD3 primarily mediates RAD51 degradation in response to DNA damage.

UNI418 inhibits tumor growth in PARP1-deficient tumors and enhances the efficacy of PARPi in combination therapy

Building on the premise that UNI418’s superior efficacy in killing PARP1-deficient cells and its synergistic effect with PARPi in vitro (Fig. 3g,h,i,j) could translate into effective in vivo antitumor activity, we conducted a series of tumor xenograft experiments. To this end, we implanted either WT or PARP1-/- HCT116 cancer cells into male NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/J mice to generate xenograft tumors. Upon the tumors reaching an approximate diameter of 0.5 cm, we administered 4 ml/kg of vehicle or 10 mg/kg of UNI418 directly into the tumors every other day for a duration of 12 days. Consistent with our initial hypothesis, the application of UNI418 markedly suppressed tumor growth exclusively in the PARP1-/- xenografts, with no significant effect observed in the WT tumors (Fig. 7a). Notably, the co-administration of 50 mg/kg Olaparib with 10 mg/kg UNI418 resulted in the inhibition of HCT116 tumor growth, an outcome not achieved with either agent alone (Fig. 7b). Importantly, there was no significant alteration in the body weights of the mice throughout the treatment period, irrespective of whether placebo or active drug treatment was used (Supplementary Fig. 10a). The pharmacokinetic data of UNI418 was not yet applicable for therapeutic use suggesting the need for further development for clinical application (Supplementary Fig. 10b). These findings collectively underscore the potential of UNI418, particularly in conjunction with Olaparib, as a promising therapeutic strategy against cancers harboring PARP1 deficiencies and possibly extending to broader applications in other tumor types.

UNI418 kills olaparib-resistant ovarian cancer cells

Ovarian and breast tumors deficient in BRCA1 or BRCA2 are known to exhibit sensitivity to PARPi such as Olaparib8. Nonetheless, the therapeutic management of these malignancies frequently culminates in the development of PARPi-resistant tumors, posing a significant challenge to effective clinical outcomes. In this context, PEO-1 and Capan-1 cell lines, derived from BRCA2-deficient ovarian adenocarcinoma and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma respectively, demonstrated susceptibility to PARPi treatment (Fig. 7c, f). However, C4-2 and C2-2 clones, which originated from PEO-1 and Capan-1 cells, respectively and harbor a reversion mutation in the BRCA2 gene, exhibited resistance to PARPi, underscoring a pivotal mechanism of therapeutic evasion44,45. Given the established selectivity of UNI418 for PARP1-/- cells (Fig. 3g, h), we posited that UNI418 might effectively target PARPi-resistant cancer cells, thereby re-sensitizing tumors that have acquired resistance through BRCA2 reversion mutations to PARPi therapy. In alignment with our hypothesis, the PARPi-resistant clones C4-2 and C2-2 displayed enhanced sensitivity to UNI418 in comparison to their parental PEO-1 and Capan-1 counterparts (Fig. 7d, g). Moreover, co-administration of UNI418 with Olaparib significantly augmented the sensitivity of the PARPi-resistant C4-2 and C2-2 cells to Olaparib, relative to treatment with Olaparib alone in the parental cell lines (Fig. 7e, h and Supplementary Fig. 11). Crucially, this increase in drug sensitivity was paralleled by a marked elevation in apoptotic cell death among the PARPi-resistant C4-2 cells following UNI418 treatment (Fig. 7i). These findings collectively suggest that UNI418 holds considerable promise as a therapeutic agent capable of overcoming one of the common pathways of acquired resistance to PARPi therapy of BRCA-deficient tumors.

In summary, our findings reveal that UNI418 exerts its effects by inhibiting PIKfyve and PIP5K1C to reduce IP6 levels. This reduction in IP6 levels enhances Cul4A neddylation, consequently activating the E3 ligase pathway. Activated Cul4A initiates the degradation of HR proteins, ultimately leading to HR defects. In particular, the major recombinase RAD51 and CHK1 are targeted by Cul4A with its specificity determined by a DCAF, WDR5. Importantly, these HR defects render PARPi-resistant cells hypersensitive to UNI418 treatment. This comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying UNI418’s action provides valuable insights into its potential therapeutic applications for treating PARPi-resistant cancers (Fig. 7j).

Discussion

Compounds identified through phenotypic screening have proven valuable tools for elucidating biological pathways and advancing clinical applications for treating diseases. Anticancer drugs targeting DNA replication and cellular proliferation have played pivotal roles in uncovering fundamental scientific principles in DNA repair and cell division. For instance, the exploration of the natural product rapamycin has facilitated the elucidation of the mTOR signaling pathway—a central regulator of cellular metabolism46. UNI418 therefore represents not only a promising therapeutic candidate but also a molecular tool capable of revealing a regulatory axis between phosphoinositide metabolism and HR.

Our current investigation of UNI418 has unveiled a compelling connection between DSB repair pathway and the inositol phosphate pathway, which were previously studied separately. Notably, inositol phosphates play pivotal roles within the nucleus as cofactors regulating mRNA transcription, transport, and proteolysis30,47. An illustrative example of the specific regulatory involvement of inositol phosphate within the nucleus is the modulation of a particular E3 ubiquitin ligase, Cul4A, through de-neddylation by IP6. Our study and previous research identifying the regulation of E3 ubiquitin ligase by inositol phosphate48 underscores the possibility that numerous ubiquitin ligases might be similarly regulated by inositol phosphate to modulate protein levels within the cell. In such a scenario, cellular signal transduction could effectively regulate protein homeostasis through proteolysis.

Our findings indicate that the direct downregulation of IP6 is the primary driver of Cul4A activation, leading to the degradation of HR proteins. This is supported by our observation that the knockdown of IPPK, the enzyme responsible for catalyzing the phosphorylation of IP5 to produce IP649, resulted in decreased levels of RAD51, CHK1, and CtIP, as well as enhanced sensitivity to PARPi in cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Recently, Suramin, an anti-helminthic drug, and its derivative NF449 have been identified as potent inhibitors of IPPK50.

Cul4A, in conjunction with its interacting protein DDB1, plays a crucial role in ubiquitinating various substrates. The other component of this complex, known as DCAF, is responsible for determining substrate specificity42. Notable DCAFs of the CUL4 complex include DDB2, CSA, and COP1, which target nucleotide excision repair by ubiquitinating XPC, as well as components of the DNA damage checkpoint pathways51. Over 60 additional DCAFs have been identified, all sharing several domain structures and are believed to act as substrate receptors for Cul4 proteins42. The identification of WDR5 as a selective Cul4A-dependent degradation of RAD51 uncovered in this study suggests the existence of a specific DCAF responsible for targeting various DNA metabolism proteins for degradation.

UNI418, which restores PARPi sensitivity in PARPi-resistant tumors, inhibits two lipid kinases, PIKfyve and PIP5K1C. CHK1, a key effector of ATR-mediated replication stress responses, has been shown to synergize with PARPi52–54. Consistent with this, UNI418 partially reduces CHK1, RAD51, and CtIP levels—yet this modest depletion causes a pronounced HR defect due to coordinated degradation across multiple HR components55. Furthermore, genetic depletion of PIKfyve or PIP5K1C did not produce additive effects with UNI418 treatment, consistent with a shared mechanism mediated by IP6-dependent Cul4A activation rather than independent checkpoint inhibition. Thus, compounds capable of suppressing IP6 production—such as inhibitors targeting PIKfyve, PIP5K1C, or IPPK—may synergistically sensitize cells to PARPi therapy by simultaneously destabilizing the HR network.

The tumor regression induced by UNI418 may rely not only on HR protein degradation but also on pleiotropic effects associated with PIKfyve and PIP5K1C inhibition. These lipid kinases regulate the generation of PI(3,5)P2 and PI(4,5)P2, which are essential for endosomal and lysosomal membrane dynamics as well as autophagy activation56. Therefore, UNI418-mediated disruption of cellular homeostasis may contribute to the observed antitumor response57. It’s worth noting that inhibitors of PIKfyve with non-specific targets are not uncommon57–59. Phosphatidyl inositol phosphates (PPIns) play diverse roles in cellular functions such as cell growth, proliferation, motility, intracellular trafficking, and survival30,47. Consistent with this, UNI418-mediated inhibition of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C impedes the cellular entry of SAR-COVID19, which relies on endocytosis for infection and is influenced by inositol phosphate signaling27. Notably, the binding of IP6 to the ubiquitin ligase Cul4A induces Cul4A de-neddylation, leading to the inactivation of its E3 ubiquitin ligase function48. Recent studies have highlighted the role of PPIns in regulating DSB repair and DNA damage responses (DDRs). For instance, IPMK, crucial for inositol 1,3,4,5,6-pentakisphosphate biosynthesis32, regulates the nuclear export of RAD51 mRNA in response to DNA damage. Additionally, the cooperation of IPMK with phosphatidyl inositol 3 phosphate is necessary for ATR recruitment and activation37,60. Inhibition of phosphoinositide-3-kinases induces significant DNA damage in DDR-deficient tumors by depleting nucleosides61. It was reported that the binding of IP6 to DNA-PKcs stimulates NHEJ repair62. Although UNI418 downregulates IP6 levels in cells, it seems to have minimal impact on DNA-PKcs-dependent NHEJ, as evidenced by the minor decrease in NHEJ activity upon UNI418 treatment (Fig. 3e) and no apparent alteration in Ku80 recruitment kinetics to DNA damage following ionizing radiation treatment. In summary, our findings unveil an additional layer of DSB repair regulation through the complete and partial inhibition of PIKfyve and PIP5K1C, respectively. This inhibition affects the HR-dependent DSB repair pathway by downregulating HR enzymes.

In addition to these mechanistic findings, accumulating evidence suggests that IP6 metabolism itself may differ across cancer types. While UNI418 does not directly target IP6K or IPMK, dysregulation of these upstream enzymes has been associated with tumor biology and clinical prognosis63. IP6K2 functions as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer, whereas IP6K1 upregulation correlates with aggressive recurrence and poor survival outcomes64. These data suggest that distinct nodes within the inositol phosphate network are differentially exploited by malignancies. Tumors heavily dependent on HR and IP6 metabolic wiring may therefore be uniquely vulnerable to agents—such as UNI418—that reduce cellular IP6 pools. Future comparative metabolomic and genomic analyses across tumor lineages will be essential for defining biomarkers that predict UNI418 sensitivity and guide clinical application. Taken together, these findings establish a mechanistic link between inositol phosphate signaling, HR dependency, and exploitable therapeutic vulnerabilities in DNA repair–dependent cancers, including those with BRCA dysfunction or PARPi resistance.

BRCA1/2-defective triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) tumors exhibit aggressiveness and have a poor 5-year prognosis65. These particular TNBC subtypes demonstrate favorable responses to PARPi66. However, recent clinical trials and observations have indicated that many patients develop chemo-resistance following prolonged treatment with PARPi. Various mechanisms contribute to PARPi resistance, including drug export mechanisms commonly observed in recurrent drug-resistant tumors. Additionally, PARPi-resistant tumors emerge through the activation of alternative recombination-related pathways including homologous recombination, DNA replication fork protection, and ssDNA gap repair18,67. Since alternative recombination-related mechanisms predominantly underlie most instances of PARPi resistance, targeting such pathways in PARPi-resistant tumors represents a promising strategy. Consistently, inhibiting recombination-related mechanisms exerts a more profound effect on viability in PARPi-resistant tumors compared to normal cells68. Numerous inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy in blocking recombination-related mechanisms including Mirin or B02, which inhibit MRE11 or RAD51, respectively69,70. Alongside these inhibitors, UNI418, which indirectly regulates recombination proteins through its effect on lipid kinases, could serve as an alternative approach for treating PARPi resistance.

Remarkably, BRCA2-reverted cells showed increased sensitivity to UNI418 relative to BRCA2-defective parental cells, despite their restored HR proficiency. BRCA-reverted tumors often retain genomic instability from prior BRCA loss, leading to persistent replication stress71,72. Such cells remain heavily reliant on HR and replication stress response machinery, making them disproportionately vulnerable to Cul4A-mediated HR protein degradation6. Accordingly, UNI418 triggered selective apoptosis and strong synthetic lethality in BRCA-reverted cells in combination with Olaparib (Fig. 7i).

In summary, our study identifies a molecular mechanism to regulate HR, involving Cul4A-dependent proteolysis of HR proteins through the modulation of inositol phosphate pathways, with UNI418 serving as a tool compound. Additionally, we introduce UNI418 as compound with activity against PARPi-resistant tumors, achieved by selectively degrading HR proteins. Since targeting lipid kinases enhances PARPi efficacy in resistant tumors, monotherapy targeting the inositol phosphate pathway or combination therapy with PARPi and lipid kinase inhibitors offers promising strategies for combating PARPi-resistant tumors. Looking forward, optimization of UNI418 analogs and identification of IP6 metabolic biomarkers may enable clinical translation for PARPi-resistant tumors. Our results therefore position the inositol phosphate axis as an unexplored therapeutic platform for inducing HR deficiency and overcoming PARPi resistance.

Membranes were incubated with a primary antibody detecting the protein of interest at 4 °C overnight. After washing with Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS) with 0.05% Tween (TBS-T), the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody at room temperature for one hour. The images were detected by the Odyssey imaging system (Li-COR Biosciences) or ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad).

SelectScreen kinase assay

Kinase assays were performed using the SelectScreen® Kinase Profiling platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Kinase-tagged T7 phages were produced in an E. coli BL21-derived host, and additional kinases were expressed in HEK293 cells and subsequently DNA-tagged for qPCR detection. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads were incubated with biotinylated small-molecule ligands to generate affinity resins, followed by blocking with buffer to minimize nonspecific binding. Binding reactions were assembled by mixing kinases, ligand-coupled beads, and test compounds in binding buffer (20% SeaBlock, 0.17 × PBS, 0.05% Tween-20, 6 mM DTT). Test compounds were applied as an 11-point threefold serial dilution series, with a final DMSO concentration of 0.9%. Reactions were carried out in 384-well plates at a final volume of 20 µL and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Beads were then washed and resuspended in elution buffer containing 0.5 µM non-biotinylated ligand. The concentration of eluted kinases was quantified by qPCR, and dissociation constants (KD) were determined from concentration–response curves.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR experiment used a Biacore T200 instrument. 10 mg/ml of purified proteins were diluted in 10 mM sodium acetate, pH5.0. and immobilized in a CM5 chip (GE Healthcare) using standard amine coupling. HBS-EP+ buffer, which consisted of 0.1 M HEPES, 1.5 M NaCl, 0.03 M EDTA, and 0.5% v/v Surfactant P20, was used as a running buffer. After immobilizing the protein, UNI418 was diluted into the PBS-P20 with 5% DMSO solution to create a series of concentrations. UNI418 was injected into the channels, and the binding responses were recorded in RU.

Cloning, protein expression, and purification

The gene encoding the kinase domain of human PIKfyve and PIP5K1C was amplified from GFP-hPIKfyve and GFP-PIPK1 gamma 90 (GFP-PIP5K1C) plasmid purchased from Addgene (#121148, #22299) and cloned into expression vector pVFT4S which contains a poly-histidine tag, a maltose binding protein (MBP) tag and a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease recognition site. The plasmid was transformed into Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) Codon Plus cells. Cells were grown at 37 °C until OD600 reaches 0.8 in LB media containing 50 mg/ml kanamycin and 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added. Cells were further cultured at 15 °C for 15 h. Grown cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7500 x g for 10 min. Collected pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.3, 300 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.5% Triton X-100) and disrupted by sonication. The lysate was pelleted by centrifugation at 36,000 x g for one hour. Cell debris was removed, and the supernatant was loaded on amylose resin (NEB), and bound proteins were eluted with 20 mM maltose. Eluted proteins were diluted to a final salt concentration of 50 mM NaCl, then purified using an anion exchange column (HiTrap Q HP, 1 × 5 ml, GE Healthcare Life Sciences) equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.3, 50 mM NaCl, 2.5% glycerol, 0.03% Triton X-100 in an ÄKTA prime plus device (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Protein elution was performed with a salt gradient from 50 mM to 1 M NaCl. Further purification was proceeded using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 prep-grade column in an ÄKTA purifier (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) with storage buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH7.3, 300 mM NaCl, 2.5% glycerol). The plasmid expressing full length WDR5 was obtained from Addgene (#29759 and #59974).

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

HEK293T cells were treated with 5 μM UNI418 for 1 h prior to harvesting. Cells were collected, washed with PBS, and resuspended in PBS. Equal volumes of the cell suspension were aliquoted into microcentrifuge tubes and heated to the indicated temperatures for 3 min to denature and precipitate target proteins. Heated samples were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. After two freeze–thaw cycles, the tubes were briefly vortexed, and the resulting cell lysates were subjected to SDS–PAGE.

High performance liquid chromatography for detection of inositol phosphates

For HPLC analysis, 1 × 105 cells/60 mm dish of RPE-1 (human retinal pigment epithelial cell) were treated with 60 μCi [3H] myoinositol (NET1177001MC, PerkinElmer). Intracellular inositol phosphates were extracted with acid extraction buffer (1 M HClO4, 3 mM EDTA and 0.1 mg/ml IP6) and neutralized with neutralization buffer (1 M K2CO3 and 3 mM EDTA). The lysates were centrifuged for 10 min, and a soluble fraction was resolved by HPLC. Lipid pellets were lysed with 0.1 % Triton X-100 in NaOH for overnight. Each fraction was mixed with Ultima-Flo AP liquid scintillation cocktail (6013599, PerkinElmer), and radioactivity was counted in the scintillation counter. The quantity of inositol phosphates was presented as total counts/min (CPM) normalized by total lipid contents or inositol counts.

After fixation, EdU incorporation was detected using the Click-iT EdU imaging kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were subsequently blocked with 5% FBS and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBS-T), cells were incubated with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI using permanent mounting medium (Vector Laboratories). Images were acquired using an LSM 880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss). For experiments involving EdU labeling, only EdU-positive nuclei were included in downstream quantitative analyses, as indicated in the figure legends.

Determination of DNA DSB repair frequency

To evaluate DNA DSB repair frequency, GFP-reporter assays were performed. U2OS cell lines stably integrated with reporter constructs (DR-GFP for HR and EJ5-GFP for NHEJ, from Dr. Jeremy Stark) were used, as previously described73. For the assay, 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded in 1 ml of complete media in a 12-well plate. After 12 h, cells were transfected with 0.5 µg of the I-SceI expression plasmid (pCAG-I-SceI) using 3 µl of Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) in 0.1 ml of antibiotic-free medium. Six hours post-transfection, the media was replaced with fresh medium containing UNI418. GFP-positive cells were quantified by flow cytometry using a Becton Dickinson FACSVerse two days post-transfection.

Cell viability assays

4 × 103 cells plated in each 96 well were treated with chemicals at the indicated concentration for 72 h. Cell viability was measured using a CellTiter-Glo assay kit (Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was determined at luminescence using Synergy NEO2 Hybrid Multi-Mode Reader (Biotek).

Annexin V/ PI staining assays

Cells were harvested and re-suspended with Annexin-binding buffer. Re-suspended cells were immediately incubated with Annexin V- Alexa FlourTM 488 and propidium iodide at room temperature for 15 min. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using Becton Dickinson FACSVerse.

siRNA

All siRNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Bioneer, Genolution or Sigma-Aldrich. These siRNAs were transfected into the cell using lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) following manufacturer’s protocol. The sequences of the siRNAs are provided in Supplementary table 3.

Immunoprecipitation

HCT116 cells were transfected with vectors encoding FLAG-WDR5 24 h. MG132 (Sigma Aldrich, C2211-5mg) 10 μM was treated 3 h and cells were harvested 24 h after transfection. Whole-cell lysates were prepared by lysing cells with buffer X (100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 % NP40, and 5 mM MgCl2) supplemented with the Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 50 units of Benzonase for 1 h at 4 °C. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation (15,000 x g, 4 °C, 10 min). FLAG-tagged proteins were incubated with anti-FLAG M2 agarose affinity beads (Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at 4 °C with constant rotation. The beads were washed three times using buffer X, and anti-FLAG bead-bound proteins were eluted with buffer X containing 0.15 mg/ml FLAG peptide. Coimmunoprecipitated proteins were loaded onto SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting. The proteins were detected by means of a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Mice experiments

Six weeks old NOD.CB17-Prkdcscid/J male mice were purchased from the Orientbio. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free condition with a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 40-60%. To induce subcutaneous human colon cancer xenografts, 1 × 106 HCT116 PARP1+/+ or PARP1–/– cells in 200 µl of 50% Matrigel (#354234, Corning) were injected into right flanks in each mouse. Tumors in flanks were established at day 20 and were randomized into four groups for treatment (n > 3 mice/group). We measured the tumor sizes before treatment to ensure no clear size differences between each group (tumor size >0.5 cm). Mice received subcutaneous injections every other day with vehicle (a mixture of NMP, PEG300, and 30% aqueous hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin solution at 1:4:5 volume ratio, 4 ml/kg), UNI418 (10 mg/kg), Olaparib (50 mg/kg), or UNI418 + Olaparib. All mice were sacrificed after 12 days, and tumors were removed, measured, weighed, and photographed. The body weight of all mice was not significantly different. All animal procedures were approved and performed according to the guidelines provided by the Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Statistical analysis

Each statistical analysis for different experiments is described in each Figure legend. Statistical analysis was performed by two-way ANOVA, one-way ANOVA or ttest using GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as means ± SEM of duplicate or triplicate samples and significance was set at *P = 0.05, **P = 0.01, and ***P = 0.001.
