Mycobacteriumabscessus CYP123 is a steroid hydroxylase with an implication in host infection
Authors: Bjoern Brixius, Ozha Aziz, Audrey Shao, Emma Bocquillon, Adriana Mirtchev, Brittany N Ross, Simone Brixius-Anderko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterium abscessus (Mab, MAB) poses a rising health threat worldwide. Infections occur in hospital settings, affecting immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients alike. Individuals with underlying lung diseases, such as cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis, are particularly at risk. Mab is intrinsically multidrug resistant to most antibiotics, has high treatment failure to current treatment regimens, and lacks a vaccine. Targeting bacterial metabolism has historically resulted in successful therapies. We discovered that the cytochrome P450 isoform CYP123 encoded by MAB_1216c is required for host infection. To determine the role of CYP123 during host infection, we generated highly active recombinant CYP123 and found CYP123 interactions with steroid hormones, which are key players in host immune response. All tested steroids induced a reverse type I shift when titrated to the enzyme. Their binding affinity was dictated by the presence of hydroxyl groups at certain positions in the steroid scaffold. Metabolism assays with a surrogate redox system revealed that CYP123 is a steroid hydroxylase and can convert 11-deoxycorticosterone and progesterone to a single monohydroxylated product, respectively. Mab infection has been associated with fungal coinfection, and cytochrome P450 enzymes have been shown to interact with azoles. We found that CYP123 binds to various triazole and azole drugs in the low micromolar range. Our results indicate that Mab CYP123 can interfere with host endobiotics with a potential implication in host cell reprogramming and can bind antifungal therapeutics possibly leading to worse polymicrobial infections. CYP123 could emerge as a potential drug target for an orthogonal approach to treating Mab infections.Significance StatementInfections with the pathogen Mycobacterium abscessus are on the rise with limited treatment options. The M. abscessus cytochrome P450 CYP123 was identified to play an essential role for host infection. Steroids do not only bind to CYP123 but are also metabolized to monohydroxylated products implicating the potential to interfere with steroidogenesis and immune antagonism by this bacterium.Key words: Antifungal compounds, Cytochrome P450, Mycobacterium abscessus, Steroid hormone biosynthesis

Introduction

Bacterial infections with the pathogen Mycobacterium abscessus (Mab) pose a rising health threat worldwide. Mab is a nontuberculous mycobacterium and is closely related to Mycobacterium tuberculosis.1,2 Infections occur in hospital settings and can affect immunocompromised and immunocompetent individuals. Once infected, patients develop painful skin lesions, which often extend to inner organs. Underlying lung conditions, such as cystic fibrosis, favor poor outcomes and high morbidity.

The treatment of Mab infections is difficult because of multidrug resistance. Despite being related to M. tuberculosis, it is intrinsically resistant to all antitubercular compounds.3,4 A cocktail of antibiotics is the standard of care and include macrolide and aminoglycoside antibiotics, which both target the ribosome. The treatment duration can span months to years with only 20%–50% success rate despite continuous susceptibility to the antibiotics.5 The underlying mechanisms for antibiotic resistance are still poorly understood. The formation of a biofilm, which hinders drug penetration, the expression of enzymes degrading antibiotics, and the change in gene expression are considered to contribute to drug resistance.6 Hence, there is an urgent need to identify new drug targets. In addition to antibiotic tolerance, Mab actively reprograms the host cells, which results in immune evasion, reprogramming of the cell metabolism, and manipulation of apoptotic signaling pathways.7,8 Understanding how Mab hijacks host cells is key for target identification and future drug design, and shedding light on the function of metabolic enzymes is historically a fruitful path to identify bacterial vulnerabilities.

Cytochrome P450 (P450) enzymes are hemethiolate monooxygenases. P450 enzymes are found in all domains of life and assume a plethora of different functions.9 In humans, they contribute to drug metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and the oxyfunctionalization of fatty acids, bile acids, and vitamins.9 Although bacteria possess functional P450 enzymes, their roles are still poorly understood.10 Work on bacterial P450 enzymes used for biotechnology revealed that they can metabolize a variety of different substrate classes such as fatty acid and steroids.11,12 Some isoforms are capable to convert drugs such as human isoform.13 M. tuberculosis P450s metabolize cholesterol and bile acids, as well as fatty acids, and enable the bacterium to generate energy from host endobiotics.14,15 Most interestingly, a very recent study focused on the Mab protein expression in bacterial extracellular vesicles shows the presence of 9 of the 25 Mab P450 enzymes.16 This raises the question whether Mab P450 enzymes contribute to host cell reprogramming and drug resistance or are important for the metabolism of nutrients during infection.

Here, we present the first study focused on Mab P450 enzymes and their potential role during infection. Mab has 25 P450 enzymes. Using a Mab gene mutant library, we found that several P450 enzymes show a role during a murine abscess infection. Here, we focus on examining the putative function of the top hit CYP123 using recombinant Mab CYP123. Our work using in-depth functional studies reveals CYP123-mediated binding and metabolism of steroid hormones that are crucial signaling molecules for modulating the host immune response, respectively. We provide the first evidence that Mab has the potential to hijack the steroidogenic pathway by metabolizing host steroids.

Materials and methods

Tn-seq data

Tn-seq data were previously published and are available in datasets S5 and S6.17 Data for Mab P450 enzymes were expressed and plotted from highest to lowest abscess fitness using GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 (GraphPad).

MAB RNA expression analysis

Available transcriptomic data were pulled from European Nucleotide Archive. All data were analyzed using the pipeline regardless of whether they are in vitro or in vivo. Briefly, reads smaller than 22 bp were removed (cutadapt v3.3), mapped (bowtie2 v2.4.2) to the human genome to remove reads for tissue culture or in vivo samples and to decoy remove reads that from other organisms that may be present. Reads that did not map to the human or decoy.fna were mapped to MAB American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) 19977 and reads tallied using featureCounts v1.6.3. Only samples that had reads mapping to ≥3000 coding DNA sequence were included in downstream analysis. To determine the expression across all samples, raw data were transformed to transcripts per million and percent of all transcripts calculated per gene, ranked in descending expression and plotted. The code is available at https://github.com/brross60/RNAseq/blob/main/trim_map_invivo.sh.

Experimental materials

Steroids, triazole, and azole compounds were purchased from Millipore Sigma. Chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma Aldrich or comparable. The CYP123 cDNA and vector were purchased from GenScript.

Generation of recombinant CYP123 and redox partner proteins

The cDNA of Mab CYP123 (gene: MAB_1216c, UniProt ID: B1MKL2, amino acid sequence in the Supplemental Material) was codon optimized for recombinant expression in Escherichia coli (E. coli). A 6× histidine tag was added to the C-terminus to facilitate purification. The cDNA was then cloned into a pET17b vector, which was transformed into the E. coli strain C43(DE3). After transformation, cells were plated on Luria Bertani agar containing the pET17B selection marker carbenicillin (100 μg/mL). For the seed culture, 30 mL of Luria Bertani medium containing carbenicillin was inoculated with 4–5 colonies of transformed E. coli and grown overnight shaking at 37 °C but no longer than 16 hours. For the main culture, 400 mL of terrific broth medium (for 1 liter: 24 g of technical yeast extract, 12 g of peptone from casein, and 4 mL of glycerol) buffered with 2× potassium phosphate buffer (for 100 mL 20× solution: 4.62 g of K2HPO4 and 25.08 g of KH2PO4) was placed in three 2.8-L Fernbach flasks, respectively, with the respective antibiotic. Each flask was then inoculated with 4 mL of seed culture and incubated shaking at 37 °C and 210 rpm. When an optical density at 600 nm of 0.5 was reached, the protein expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside, and 1 mM heme precursor δ-aminolevulinic acid was added. The temperature was decreased to 27 °C, and the cultures were incubated shaking at 190 rpm for another 48 hours. The cells were collected at 6690g at 4 °C for 15 minutes and stored at –80 °C until further use.

To initiate purification, the cell pellet was thawed, resuspended in 300 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol, 500 mM sodium acetate, 1.5% [w/v] 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl)dimethylammonio]-1-propanesulfonic acid [CHAPS], 1.5% [v/v] Tween 20, 0.1 mM dithiothreitol [DTT], 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride [PMSF], 0.1 mM EDTA), and homogenized with a Dounce homogenizer. To generate spheroplasts, 0.6 mg/mL lysozyme was added under gentle mixing and incubated stirring for 30 minutes at 4 °C. The cells were kept on ice and sonicated for 10 minutes at an amplitude of 13% in 30-second intervals. To solubilize the protein from the E. coli membrane, the lysed cells were stirred at 4 °C for 60 minutes. The suspension was then centrifuged at 100,000g for 45 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. For immobilized metal affinity chromatography, two 5 mL Histrap FF (Fast Flow) columns (Cytiva) were used. The Ni/NTA column was equilibrated with 3 CV (column volumes) equilibration buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol, 500 mM sodium acetate, 1% [w/v] CHAPS, 1% [v/v] Tween 20, 0.1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM EDTA), and the supernatant was loaded with a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The column was then washed with 5 CV of equilibration buffer and then with 7 CV of wash buffer (20 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol, 1% [w/v] CHAPS, 1% [v/v] Tween 20, 10 mM imidazole, 0.1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM EDTA). The protein was eluted with 8 CV of elution buffer (20 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol, 1% [w/v] CHAPS, 1% [v/v] Tween 20, 150 mM imidazole, 0.1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM PMSF, 0.1 mM EDTA). Fractions with high Soret absorbance at 420-nm versus 280-nm total protein absorbance were collected. The fractions were pooled and exchanged into size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol, 500 mM NaCl) via a centrifugal device (Amicon, 30 kDa cutoff). The protein was then loaded on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva) in SEC running buffer (50 mM potassium phosphate [pH 7.4], 500 mM NaCl, 20% [v/v] glycerol) with a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The protein eluted as a monomer was exchanged into storage buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate buffer [pH 7.4], 20% [v/v] glycerol) with a centrifugal device and concentrated for storage at –80 °C.

The protein purity was assessed by SDS-PAGE (estimated molecular weight of 47 kDa) and the ratio between the Soret peak absorbance at 417 nm versus total protein absorbance at 280 nm. The resulting Reinheitszahl was on average 1.5.

Recombinant human adrenodoxin (Adx) and Adx reductase (AdR) were expressed and purified as described before.18

Spectroscopic ligand binding assays

Spectral ligand binding assays were conducted to determine the affinity of CYP123 to azole and triazole drugs and steroids. For this, 1 or 2 μM recombinant CYP123 (concentration calculated from Soret absorbance) was dissolved in 1 mL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for analysis and placed into a quartz cuvette (Starna). After ligand addition (azoles or steroids, dissolved in DMSO), the solution was incubated for 8 minutes to reach steady-state conditions. A reference cuvette was used accounting for DMSO absorbance. Then, a spectrum was recorded from 350 to 500 nm in difference mode. The resulting change in absorbance between maximum and minimum was then plotted against the respective ligand concentration and analyzed with GraphPad Prism using a one-site specific binding fitting.

Determination of steroid metabolism

To determine if CYP123 can metabolize steroids, metabolism assays were performed with 11-deoxycorticosterone, 21-deoxycortisol (Reichstein’s substance S; RSS), 17OH-progesterone, and progesterone (Prog) (dissolved in DMSO). For this, 1 μM CYP123 (concentration calculated from the CO difference spectrum), 1 μM human AdR, and 20 μM human Adx were reconstituted in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) containing 100 μM 1,2-dilauroyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DLPC). Before use, the buffer was sonicated for 5–10 minutes so that DLPC micelles could form. Subsequently, 100 μM of the respective steroid was added for the reaction. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5 minutes. The total reaction volume was 500 μL. The reaction was started with 1 mM NADPH and allowed to proceed for 10 minutes shaking at 800 rpm. The reaction was stopped with 0.5 mL chloroform, and steroids were extracted twice and dried under vacuum. For liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis, the samples were reconstituted in 60 μL of 100% acetonitrile. Twenty microliters for 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) analysis and 10 μL for Prog analysis were injected on a reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography column (Restek Roc C18 column, particle size: 5 μm, 150 × 4.6 mm) using an acetonitrile-water gradient (phase A: 10% [v/v] acetonitrile, phase B: 100% acetonitrile). For analysis of DOC metabolism, the gradient setting is as follows: 0–5 minutes 51% B (step), 5.01–25 minutes 70% B (step), all at 40 °C and a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. We used the following gradient setting for the analysis of Prog metabolism: 0–3 minutes 10% B (step), 3.01–20 minutes 70% B (ramp), 20.01–35 minutes 95% B (ramp), all at 40 °C and a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Steroids were detected by a single quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu LC-MS 2050) and identified by their mass fragments.

Results

MAB requires cytochrome P450 enzymes during infection

The reference genome, MAB American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) 19977, encodes 25 genes representing 22 different P450 enzymes. A recent study using Mab mutant libraries identified genes that are differentially regulated during bacterial growth and on host infection using mouse models (Fig. 1A).17 All 25 P450s are nonessential for survival. Extracting information for the 25 Mab P450 enzymes outlines the impact of single variants on infection (Fig. 1B, blue). Of the 25 P450s, 9 have a fitness defect of –2 log2 fold change. Fitness during in vitro was included to validate that these genes specifically provide fitness during infection (Fig. 1B, green). Gene expression is a surrogate for bacterial functionality. To ascertain when these genes are expressed, we downloaded 248 publicly available RNAseq profiles and reanalyzed the data using an in-house pipeline. MAB_1216 (Fig. 1C) and MAB_0913 (Supplemental Fig. 1) are highest expressed during macrophage infection, growth, and when in biofilm. MAB_1211c is highest expressed during growth and in biofilms, whereas MAB_0101 has minimal changes in expression (Supplemental Fig. 1). As a comparison, MAB_3625, which does not have a fitness defect in the abscess when mutated, is expressed during growth in standard media and in lung sputum or synthetic sputum media (SCFM2) (Supplemental Fig. 1). Together, the isoform CYP123 (MAB_1216c) has the largest fitness defect during host infection and is expressed during infection, indicating a role during infection (Fig. 1B). There is only little information about the function of CYP123 in MAB and its homologue in M. tuberculosis; therefore, we carried out functional characterization on this enzyme to determine its metabolic capacity.

Generation of recombinant CYP123

To determine the interaction of CYP123 with ligands, we generated recombinant enzyme for the first time. Because it has been previously reported that CYP123 is located in the MAB membrane fraction, we designed a purification protocol accordingly and added detergents to the immobilized metal affinity chromatography purification buffers to achieve a complete solubilization from the E. coli membrane and high protein stability.16 Using a single purification step, 900–1000 nmol protein per liter expression culture could be obtained. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed high purity after SEC and a single band below 55 kDa, confirming the correct estimated size of 47 kDa (Fig. 2A). Spectral analysis revealed a final Soret absorbance maximum at 418 nm (Fig. 2B) and, when reduced and complexed with carbon monoxide, an absorbance maximum at 450 nm, indicating functional protein and a correctly inserted heme prosthetic group (Fig. 2B, inset).

Recombinant CYP123 was generated in E. coli to high purity and the correct molecular weight (approximately 47 kDa) via 2 chromatographic steps as indicated by SDS-PAGE analysis (A). Purified CYP123 exhibits a typical Soret absorbance maximum at 417 nm (B) and at 450 nm when reduced and complexed with CO (B, inset) indicating functional enzyme. IMAC, immobilized metal affinity chromatography.

CYP123 binds azole and triazole drugs

Azoles and triazoles are nitrogen-containing antifungal drugs that typically bind P450 enzymes with high affinity. Azoles are also a powerful tool to probe P450 active site flexibility and extent because they come in different shapes and sizes. The free electron pair of the azole terminal nitrogen tightly coordinates with the heme iron. This typically results in the observation of a type II shift, which is characterized by an increase in absorbance around 434 nm and a decrease around 414 nm. Here, we tested the ability of the triazoles posaconazole and fluconazole and the azoles clotrimazole and ketoconazole to bind to CYP123 (Fig. 3B). Titration of clotrimazole resulted in a type II spectral response with a dissociation constant (Kd) of 2.29 μM and an Amax (measure of the ligand-bound protein population) of 0.02 (Fig. 4D). To our surprise, all other tested compounds did not induce a type II, but a reverse type I spectral shift indicated by an absorbance minimum around 390 nm and a maximum around 420 nm (Fig. 4, A–C). The lack of a type II shift indicated that the compounds do not coordinate with the heme iron with a free electron pair but potentially coordinate with the axial P450 water, as suggested for a reverse type I shift.19 Titration of ketoconazole and posaconazole resulted in a Kd of 2.02 μM and 1.21 μM and an Amax of 0.018 and 0.02, respectively. In contrast, titrating the triazole fluconazole to the protein revealed a Kd of 86.76 μM and a decreased Amax of 0.006, indicating a much weaker binding to CYP123 and a lower ligand-bound protein population.

Titration of clotrimazole to recombinant CYP123 resulted in the formation of a typical type II shift, whereas titration of the 3 remaining compounds resulted in a reversed type I shift. CYP123 binds steroid hormones

To test the interaction of CYP123 with steroid hormones, we chose 7 steroids and conducted spectral ligand binding assays (Fig. 3A). With all tested steroids, titration of the ligand to the protein resulted in a reverse type I shift with an absorbance maximum around 420 nm and a minimum around 393 nm (Fig. 5). Four steroids, DOC, 21-deoxycortisol (RSS), 17OH-progesterone (17OH-prog), and Prog, display a dissociation constant in the lower micromolar range with 25.72, 35.82, 41.42, and 34.3 μM, respectively (Fig. 5, A–D). The 3 remaining steroids, corticosterone (B), cortisol (F), and 11αOH-progesterone, exhibited up to 10-fold higher Kd values with 188.4, 332.4, and 249.0 μM, respectively (Fig. 5, E–G).

CYP123 is catalytic activity toward steroid hormones

P450 enzymes require redox partner proteins that deliver the electrons from NADPH needed for the reaction. MAB and the closely related organisms Mycobacterium marinum and M. tuberculosis harbor multiple genes encoding putative ferredoxin and ferredoxin reductase variants (as indicated by UniProt).20 Mining and testing to identify the correct redox protein partners specific to CYP123-mediated catalysis are tedious and would require years of work. Here, we used the human adrenal Adx and AdR as a surrogate redox system. In the human, these proteins deliver electrons to the mitochondrial P450 enzymes. Adx and AdR were previously successfully used as redox partner proteins for a plethora of bacterial P450 enzymes, for in vitro studies or in a whole cell biocatalyst.21, 22, 23

The in vitro reaction required the addition of the lipid DLPC (1,2-dilinoleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), which forms micelles and is usually used to stabilize membrane proteins for the reaction. MAB CYP123 was not active without DLPCs, which further confirms its membrane association. With the reconstituted redox system, we tested if the 4 steroids with the highest binding affinity (indicated with a star in Fig. 3A) are metabolized by CYP123. 17OH-prog and RSS did not result in product formation. However, incubation with DOC (m/z 331, which represents its average molecular weight of 330.46 g/mol) resulted in the formation of one metabolite with an additional mass of +16, which indicates a hydroxylation (m/z 347) (Fig. 6A, Supplemental Fig. 2A). Notably, an additional mass fragment occurred with an m/z of 372 for DOC and an m/z of 388 for the metabolite. This mass fragment likely results from a conjugation with acetonitrile (+41), which is the mobile phase for LC-MS analysis. The DOC metabolite shares the same retention time as RSS, which has an additional 17-hydroxylation.

Metabolic assays with CYP123 and human adrenodoxin reductase and adrenodoxin as surrogate electron transfer proteins were conducted with 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) (A) and progesterone (Prog) (B) as a substrate, respectively. SIM chromatograms (selected ion monitoring, positive ion mode) after liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis revealed a conversion of DOC (m/z 331) into a monohydroxylated product (m/z 347) that elutes at the same retention time as the steroid 11-deoxycortisol (RSS) (A). Prog (m/z 315) was also converted to a monohydroxylated product (m/z 331) that eluted at the same retention time as the steroid 17OH-progesterone (B).

Prog (m/z 315, which represents its average molecular weight of 314.46 g/mol) was converted to one metabolite with an additional mass of +16 (m/z 331), which confirms a hydroxylation reaction catalyzed by CYP123 (Fig. 6B, Supplemental Fig. 2B). Here, the product shares the same retention time with 17OH-prog, which has an additional hydroxyl group at carbon 17.

Discussion

The rising health threat by pathogens like Mab and the high prevalence of multidrug resistance emphasize an urgent need to find new therapeutic targets to battle bacterial infections. Despite the design of new antibiotics, Mab develops strategies to quickly evade drug action via largely unknown mechanisms. Thus, it is of utmost importance to better understand bacterial metabolism, the molecular mechanism of host infection, and how antibiotic resistance is mediated. One group of metabolic enzymes in Mab that has not been studied yet is P450 enzymes, which we addressed here. CYP123 has the strongest fitness defect when mutated and is expressed in vivo. Recently, evidence was provided in 2 independent studies that the Mab isoform CYP123 is upregulated in extracellular vesicles released during infection.16 Bacterial vesicles assume a pivotal role for bacterial virulence, survival, and antibiotic resistance and can access any body part through the blood stream. It is evident that Mab CYP123 is crucial for infection and bacterial survival in the host, but the physiological function was still unknown. Given the substrate promiscuity of some bacterial P450 enzymes, CYP123 could be capable of metabolizing host endobiotics and xenobiotics. To elucidate a potential physiological function of CYP123 and its role in bacterial infection and survival, we implemented the first procedure to produce recombinant CYP123. Because CYP123 was identified in the Mab membrane fraction, we treated it as a membrane-associated protein and used detergents for lysis and the first purification step to solubilize the protein from E. coli membranes and further stabilize it. The detergent was completely depleted during SEC. Overall, we produced high-quality and active CYP123 for subsequent functional assays.

Azoles and triazole bind to CYP123 in an unusual mode

Azoles are commonly used to assay P450 functionality because these are nitrogen-containing compounds that are known to coordinate tightly with the P450 heme iron. Because a plethora of azole derivatives exist in different sizes with different chemical properties, they are excellent tools to probe active site flexibility. When titrated to a P450 enzyme, the coordination of the nitrogen electron pair with the heme usually results in a type II spectral response and enforces the iron low spin state. Only clotrimazole induces a classical type II spectral shift as expected for nitrogen-containing compounds. The remaining 3 compounds induced a reversed type I spectrum when titrated to CYP123. The induction of a reverse type I shift by nitrogen-containing compounds has been previously observed in other mycobacterial P450 enzymes. The indole moiety of the compound LP10 (α-[(4-methylcyclohexyl)carbonyl amino]-N-4pyridinyl-1H-indole-3-propanamide) induced a reverse type I shift when titrated to M. tuberculosis CYP125A1.24 The crystal structure of CYP125A1 in complex with LP10 revealed that the indole nitrogen interacts with the water molecule, which is coordinated as the sixth axial ligand over the heme iron. Instead of replacing the water that normally results in a high-spin type I shift, LP10 stabilizes it, thus resulting in a reverse type I shift. Similar has been observed for CYP121A1 binding of fluconazole, which is an essential P450 enzyme in M. tuberculosis.25 In CYP121A1, an intricate water network plays a pivotal role for its catalytic function with residues outside of the active site further stabilizing the network.26 Curiously, mutating CYP121A1 Asn-85 to a serine residue converts the substrate-induced type I into a reverse type I shift. It is possible that such a water network also exists in the CYP123 active site. Because both proteins only share 23% sequence identity, a direct comparison is not possible without a protein crystal structure, which would provide experimental evidence.

Our study on azole and triazole interaction is related to another aspect of Mab infections. Infections are often concomitant with fungal infections with species of Aspergillus, especially in cystic fibrosis patients.27 The synergy between fungal and bacterial infection is still poorly understood. However, a study focusing on the interaction between Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans during coinfection revealed that genes mediating antibiotic and antifungal resistance were drastically upregulated when cocultured.28 Azoles and triazoles are the first line of therapy for the treatment of fungal infections. In general, they suppress lanosterol biosynthesis by inhibiting the P450 enzyme CYP51. Posaconazole, clotrimazole, and ketoconazole have been highly effective for the treatment of invasive fungal infections caused by multiple fungal species including Aspergillus.29, 30, 31 In contrast, fluconazole is ineffective against Aspergillus infections which is primarily targeting Candida infections.32 Fluconazole has the weakest affinity to CYP123 with a Kd of 87 μM. The other 3 compounds bind with a dissociation constant in a low micromolar range. It is possible that CYP123 also acts on antifungal compounds that are administered to battle fungal coinfections. Future studies are needed to examine the interplay between bacterial and fungal colonization and a potential impact on drug resistance. In addition, the potential of CYP123 to metabolize antifungal compounds needs to be investigated, which would indirectly mediate antifungal resistance.

CYP123 binds and metabolizes steroid hormones

Steroid hormones play a crucial role in the human body. Glucocorticoids like F regulate stress, immune response, and important metabolic processes,33 and mineralocorticoids like aldosterone regulate water and salt homeostasis.33 Five different human P450 enzymes are involved in F and aldosterone biosynthesis from cholesterol (Supplemental Fig. 3). Interestingly, the bacterial CYP123A9 variant in Rhodococcus sp. P14 contributes to the biodegradation of estradiol by converting estrone to 16OH-estrone.34 Because of the existing connection of CYP123 to steroids and because steroid hormones are powerful key players in immune regulation and are generally immunosuppressive, we decided to test the interaction of CYP123 with 7 different corticosteroids to evaluate the potential of bacterial hijacking of the host steroid hormone biosynthesis. Steroids are known substrates of bacterial P450 enzymes that have been used for the biotechnological production of new steroidal molecules and metabolites.12,23,35 Different bacterial P450s are able to perform a hydroxylation reaction of every core carbon of the steroid scaffold. For our studies, we chose the glucocorticoid F, the F precursors 21-deoxycortisol and 17OH-progesterone, the aldosterone precursors DOC and corticosterone, the precursor for both corticoid classes Prog, and the metabolite 11αOH-progesterone (Fig. 3A). To our surprise, all steroids induced a reverse type I shift as observed for azole and triazole compounds (Figs. 3 and 5). It was previously speculated that compounds with a hydroxyl group might be able to replace or interact with the axial water via the lone oxygen electron pair and thus stabilize the low spine state, which is reflected by a reverse type I shift. This has been recently speculated for CYP4A11 binding of its product 16OH-palmitate, which induces a reverse type I shift, while the substrate palmitate resulted in a typical type I shift.19 However, Prog is not hydroxylated but, like the other tested steroids, carries a ketone group at carbon 20. It is possible that this ketone group, rather than a hydroxyl group, serves as a hydrogen acceptor and interacts with the axial water in this instance.

In terms of binding affinity, it is remarkable that all molecules without a hydroxylation at 11β position (Prog, DOC, RSS, and 17OH-prog) exhibit a Kd in the low micromolar range. These molecules have hydroxyl groups at position 17, 21, or none at all. Notably, the Kd values for RSS and DOC are in the same range as for CYP11B1 and CYP11B2, which convert both compounds in the body, respectively.36,37 The presence of a 11β-hydroxylation decreased the binding affinity up to 10-fold for F and B. To evaluate if the reason for decreased affinity can really be related to the 11β-hydroxylation and no other hydroxylation position, we tested the compound 11αOH-progesterone, which has no other hydroxyl groups. The presence of the 11-hydroxylation drastically decreased the binding affinity compared with Prog.

We then conducted metabolism assays with Prog, DOC, RSS, and 17OH-prog with a reconstituted redox system using Adx and AdR as surrogate electron transfer proteins. Although CYP123 did not metabolize 17OH-prog and RSS, both Prog and DOC are substrates and showed the formation of metabolites (Fig. 6). DOC and Prog metabolism yielded a product with a mass difference of +16, suggesting a monohydroxylation. Because the DOC metabolite shares the retention time and mass with RSS and the Prog metabolite with 17OH-prog, respectively, the hydroxylation might occur on carbon 17. This would support the theory that the steroids coordinate with the axial water via their 20-oxo functional group, which could position the steroids for hydroxylation at carbon 17. Even though 17OH-prog and RSS, both with a 17-hydroxylation, bind to CYP123, they are not metabolized, suggesting that those compounds might be products. A hydroxylation at carbon 11 disturbs binding to CYP123 potentially due to unfavorable steric conformations in the active site. Although there is evidence that CYP123 acts as 17-hydroxylase, the structures of these metabolites have to be analyzed in the future to confirm these hypotheses. In addition, the physiological redox partner proteins of CYP123 need to be identified because the native redox system will provide a clearer picture of the catalytic ability.

The CYP123-mediated functionalization of Prog and DOC implies the potential to manipulate the steroidogenic pathway. If the products are indeed RSS and 17OH-prog, they feed directly into F synthesis, respectively (Supplemental Fig. 3 shows the steroidogenic pathway). Most steroids have a moderate to high affinity to the glucocorticoid receptor, which mediates an anti-inflammatory response. Even though the classical steroidogenesis takes place in the adrenal gland, it has been long known that other tissues can also produce steroid hormones, known as extra glandular steroidogenesis. The synthesis of glucocorticoids and the presence of CYP11A1, which kicks off steroidogenesis via cholesterol side chain cleavage, have been confirmed in intestinal cells and lung tissue.38, 39, 40, 41 Intriguingly, the occurrence of steroidogenesis in these tissues is related to inflammation and immune response, which is highly relevant to Mab infections of the lungs. It has been postulated that steroidogenesis in immune cells such as macrophages is crucial for the control of inflammation.42 Thus, it is possible that Mab CYP123 interferes with extraglandular steroidogenesis in infected lungs and immune cells in situ to manipulate the immune state. Moreover, because CYP123 has been found in Mab extracellular vesicles, the enzyme reaches all body parts including the adrenal gland and could interfere with steroid hormone biosynthesis. Unfortunately, steroidogenesis in immune cells is still heavily understudied. In addition, there is no published information about the steroid hormone levels in Mab patients. The only information available is that patients with lung infections caused by Mycobacterium avium exhibit lower estradiol levels likely promoting infection as estradiol has been implicated to protect against infections.43 Because a CYP123A9 in Rhodococcus can metabolize estrone, future studies will be conducted to examine the potential of CYP123 to metabolize sex hormones.

In conclusion, we generated recombinant MAB CYP123 and conducted the first functional assays to probe CYP123 with steroids and antifungal drugs. The ability to metabolize steroids implies a crucial role of CYP123 in manipulating the host immune response and paves the way to drug discovery targeting MAB P450 enzymes.
