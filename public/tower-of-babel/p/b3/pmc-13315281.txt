Prospective association between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity among Chinese middle school students: the mediation of trait anger and moderation of moral identity
Authors: Zhendong Wan, Tiancheng Li, Shuanghu Fang, Siliang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrounded in trait activation theory and the social cognitive theory of moral identity, this two-wave prospective study examined whether bullying victimization is prospectively associated with malevolent creativity over time, whether trait anger serves as a prospective indirect association in this link, and whether moral identity moderates the indirect pathway. A sample of 1,092 Chinese middle school students (Mage = 15.15 years, SD = 1.47) completed self-report questionnaires at two time points separated by three months. Bullying victimization, moral identity, and malevolent creativity were assessed at T1; trait anger and malevolent creativity were assessed at T2. Prospective indirect effects were tested using Hayes’ PROCESS Models 4 and 58. The findings revealed that: (1) Bullying victimization (T1) was positively and prospectively associated with malevolent creativity (T2) (β = 0.10, p < 0.001). (2) Trait anger (T2) served as a prospective indirect link, accounting for 57.58% of the total effect (indirect effect = 0.19, 95% CI [0.15, 0.24]). (3) Moral identity (T1) moderated both paths: higher moral identity weakened the association between bullying victimization and trait anger (β = -0.14, p < 0.001) and between trait anger and malevolent creativity (β = -0.12, p < 0.001). Findings highlight moral identity as a protective factor that buffers the risk from bullying to malevolent creativity. Limitations include the concurrent assessment of trait anger and malevolent creativity at T2, which precludes causal mediation claims.Keywords: Bullying victimization, Malevolent creativity, Trait anger, Moral identitySubject terms: Education, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Creativity plays a crucial role in promoting social progress and advancing human civilization, especially in our rapidly developing era. Creativity significantly enhances college students’ competitive edge1 and also drives scientific invention, artistic endeavors, and academic research2. However, creativity is a double-edged sword—studies show that creative people are more likely to engage in socially undesirable or unethical behavior3. Creativity can also have a darker side when combined with harmful intentions, leading to the emergence of malevolent creativity. Malevolent creativity refers to the use of creative thought for malevolent or harmful purposes4,5. Despite its novelty and originality, malevolent creativity is inherently destructive, carrying forces that can inflict damage6–8. Manifesting in everyday interactions9, malevolent creativity can result in negative outcomes, including physical and psychological harm, property damage, and disruption of social norms through acts such as terrorism10,11. Therefore, malevolent creativity is a significant topic deserving scholarly attention.

The psychological literature has made substantial progress regarding malevolent creativity, laying essential groundwork for subsequent research. However, some gaps warrant deeper examination. Previous research primarily focused on college students’ malevolent creativity, examining factors such as emotional intelligence, attention bias, anger, aggressiveness, and justice sensitivity12–16. In contrast, malevolent creativity among middle school students, who are more influenced by school and family supervision, possess lower cognitive maturity, are prone to cognitive biases, and exhibit relatively weaker dialectical thinking, has received comparatively limited attention. Although some research on this demographic exists, these studies typically have small sample sizes17 and primarily focus on student groups in economically developed urban areas18–20. Large‑sample evidence on the psychological mechanisms of malevolent creativity in general middle school populations remains scarce, limiting generalizability and highlighting the need for further research. In addition, existing research on external factors influencing malevolent creativity has primarily addressed social exclusion, psychological abuse, and neglect within the family environment, and parental rejection15,21,22, However, the impact of adverse school-related experiences, particularly bullying, commonly encountered by middle school students, has received insufficient attention. Moreover, rural junior high school students who are subjected to bullying may be more inclined to develop malevolent creative ideas23,24. Existing studies found that the bullying victimization rate of Chinese middle school students has increased from 7.9% to 13.61%25,26. Given that middle school students are in a critical period of moral and emotional development, with immature emotional regulation abilities and unstable moral cognition, bullying victimization may have a more pronounced impact on their malevolent creativity compared to other age groups. Hence, further investigation of the relationship between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity in this population, particularly using prospective designs, is essential.

Regarding internal mechanisms, previous studies have identified coping styles26 and aggressiveness17 as mediators in college students, and forgiveness and self-control14,27, as moderators. Additionally, one study highlighted the mediating role of hostile attribution bias in the relationship between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity among rural junior high school students23. However, other internal mechanisms remain unexplored, particularly the prospective indirect association of trait anger, an important risk factor for malevolent creativity, and the moderating role of moral identity, which is closely associated with unethical behaviors. Importantly, nearly all prior studies on bullying victimization and malevolent creativity are cross-sectional, revealing only concurrent correlations without temporal order. Thus, it remains unclear whether bullying victimization prospectively increases malevolent creativity over time, and when trait anger and moral identity play a role in this process. To overcome this limitation and clarify the developmental relationships between these variables, a two-wave study is necessary. Given that middle school students are in a critical period of psychological development, this study employs a 3-month prospective design with two waves of data collection. This design allows us to examine prospective associations from bullying victimization (T1) to trait anger (T2) and malevolent creativity (T2), while controlling for baseline malevolent creativity (T1) and further exploring the moderating role of moral identity (T1). However, because trait anger and malevolent creativity were measured concurrently at T2, causal mediation cannot be claimed. Thus, we focus on testing whether trait anger serves as a prospective indirect association in this two‑wave design. In contrast, moral identity(T1) was measured before trait anger and malevolent creativity (T2), so its moderating role is not subject to the same temporal ambiguity.

Bullying victimization and malevolent creativity

As is well known, the emergence of malevolent creativity is intricately linked to external situations and internal factors that individuals encounter, with external situational factors directly triggering malevolent creativity8. Among these, bullying victimization is a significant risk factor23,28. Bullying victimization typically involves intentional and repeated physical or psychological aggression exerted by individuals in a dominant position, reflecting a power imbalance29,30. According to the Sensitivity to Mean Intentions (SeMI) model31, victims who are highly sensitive to fairness in adverse environments tend to identify, focus on, and perceive harmful information more readily. Consequently, these individuals are more prone to deviant behaviors32 and may exhibit higher levels of malevolent creativity15 as a form of protest against perceived injustices. Studies indicate that individuals exposed to threatening situations, such as bullying, demonstrate increased malevolent creativity25. In addition, Zhang et al.19 found that middle school students exposed to more negative school environments exhibit greater malevolent creativity. These findings highlight bullying victimization as a crucial risk factor for malevolent creativity among middle school students. Based on this evidence, we hypothesize:

H1: Bullying victimization (T1) is positively and prospectively associated with malevolent creativity (T2) in Chinese middle school students.

The prospective indirect association of trait anger

Bullying victimization not only directly affects malevolent creativity but also indirectly influences it through internal psychological factors such as trait anger. Trait anger differs from state anger; specifically, trait anger is a stable personality characteristic that reflects an individual’s predisposition to experience anger with greater intensity and for prolonged durations frequently33,34. Individuals high in trait anger tend to become easily irritated even by minor provocations, experiencing anger more frequently, intensely, and persistently than others. In contrast, state anger is transient and situation-specific, subsiding once the triggering event resolves35. According to the Trait Activation Theory36,37, latent personality traits are activated by relevant situational triggers, subsequently influencing behavior. When individuals encounter bullying, such unfair and provocative contexts can activate the latent trait of anger. Existing research has confirmed a positive correlation between bullying victimization and trait anger38–40, suggesting that adolescents experiencing greater bullying victimization exhibit higher trait anger. Furthermore, trait anger is positively associated with malevolent creativity10,41,42. Specifically, trait anger positively correlates with malevolent creativity, suggesting that middle school students with elevated trait anger are more likely to display higher levels of malevolent creativity16,43,44. This relationship aligns with the Integrated Cognitive Model45, which proposes that individuals high in trait anger tend to interpret and dwell on hostile situational cues negatively and may develop hostile biases, thereby promoting malevolent creativity. Therefore, for middle school students who have poor emotional regulation skills, the unfair and provocative external event of being bullied will activate their latent anger. When trait anger is continuously triggered, middle school students are prone to form hostile cognitive biases, and then convert this anger into malevolent creative behaviors to vent their dissatisfaction and resist the perceived injustice, possibly forming a prospective indirect pathway from bullying victimization through trait anger to malevolent creativity. Based on this, we propose the hypotheses:

H2: Trait anger (T2) serves as a prospective indirect association between bullying victimization (T1) and malevolent creativity (T2).

The moderating role of moral identity

While trait anger is significantly associated with middle school students’ malevolent creativity10, not all middle school students with high trait anger exhibit equally high levels of malevolent creativity. This discrepancy suggests the need to investigate protective factors that might mitigate the progression from bullying victimization to malevolent creativity. Among these, moral identity emerges as a crucial protective factor, serving as a moderator that can buffer the negative impacts of adverse experiences on individual psychology and behavior46,47. Moral identity refers to a self-concept structured around core moral traits, such as “kindness,” that represent an individual’s cognitive schema of moral qualities48. Moral identity remains relatively stable and serves as a psychological driver of moral behavior. According to the social cognitive theory of moral identity, individuals with high moral standards have an inhibitory mechanism that suppresses immoral behavior, thereby preventing them from engaging in unethical actions49,50. High moral identity individuals tend to internalize societal moral values and uphold rigorous moral standards51. Evidence suggests that those with elevated moral identity are inclined to interpret bullying as a violation of moral norms52, decreasing tendencies to attribute bullying experiences to personal inadequacies. They typically demonstrate a stronger commitment to fairness and justice, making them less susceptible to anger. In addition, those with high moral identification have advantages in emotion regulation; they can manage their emotions effectively53, thus controlling angry impulses and diminishing the activation of anger traits. For middle school students, moral identity, as a cognitive pattern that is not yet fully developed but is gradually forming, also plays a moderating role in this prospective indirect association process. Therefore, we hypothesize that middle school students with a stronger sense of moral identity tend to view bullying as a violation of moral norms, perceive the “unfairness” caused by bullying as less severe, and thereby weaken the trait-based anger emotions triggered by being bullied. Moreover, studies indicate that individuals with higher moral identity exhibit stronger prosocial tendencies and fewer antisocial behaviors than those with lower moral identity54,55. High moral identity also strengthens self-control, thereby reducing unethical conduct. For example, research shows that at higher moral identity levels, the association between individual trait anger and cyberbullying, a form of unethical behavior, is weakened56,57. This evidence implies that moral identity may similarly moderate the relationship between trait anger and malevolent creativity, another form of unethical behavior. Specifically, when trait anger is activated, high moral identity can help middle school students regulate their angry impulses, suppress the tendency of unethical behavior, and thus weaken the positive association between trait anger and malevolent creativity. Based on this evidence, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3: Moral identity (T1) moderates the relationship between bullying victimization (T1) and trait anger (T2) among Chinese middle school students.

Hypothesis 4: Moral identity (T1) moderates the relationship between trait anger (T2) and malevolent creativity (T2) among Chinese middle school students.

In summary, there is a need for more comprehensive studies to fully consider the influence of the external environment, personality traits, and cognition on malevolent creativity. Methods

Participants and procedure

This study employed cluster sampling and conducted a 3-month follow-up study of junior high school and senior high school students at a middle school in Wuhu City, Anhui Province. Anhui Province is located in central China, with an educational development level between the more developed eastern regions and the less developed western regions. Participants completed online questionnaires via wjx.com. Lie-detector questions were inserted approximately every 20 questions in the questionnaire. Then, based on participants’ responses to these lie-detector questions, questionnaires with genuine responses were screened out. The ethics committee of Anhui Normal University reviewed and approved this study. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants completed the online questionnaire during regular school hours in school computer labs as part of a computer science class activity, under the supervision of teachers and research staff. Before data collection, the school distributed detailed informed consent letters to parents/guardians via official parent‑WeChat groups. Only students whose parents provided consent were eligible to participate. At the beginning of the online survey, students also provided their own electronic assent. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Although trait anger is generally considered a relatively stable personality characteristic, previous longitudinal research with adolescents suggests that bullying victimization can increase trait anger within as little as two to three months in adolescents38,39. In addition, middle school students are undergoing significant emotional and psychological development, which may make them particularly sensitive to changes in anger following repeated experiences of victimization. Therefore, a three-month interval is both theoretically justified and practically appropriate for school-based longitudinal monitoring.

Based on the requirements of the longitudinal research paradigm58,59, in accordance with the suggestion of Wen et al.60, the moderating variables should be limited to the dependent variable during the tracking study to avoid reverse causality. Additionally, given the actual situation of the students in this school, the independent variable “bullying victimization”, the moderating variable “moral identity”, and the dependent variable “malevolent creativity” were measured at T1 (September 2025). The first batch of questionnaires collected was 1260. After eliminating the invalid data, 1161 valid questionnaires were retrieved. At the T2 time point (December 2025), the prospective indirect association variable, trait anger, and the dependent variable, malevolent creativity, were investigated. For objective reasons, such as some participants transferring schools or taking sick leave, not all participants in the second survey were able to complete it. As a result, some participants were lost, and a total of 1180 valid questionnaires were collected. After comparing and reviewing the results of two sets of questionnaires, it was finally determined that the total number of valid questionnaires was 1092, including 513 male subjects (47.0%) and 579 female subjects (53.0%), with an average age of 15.15 ± 1.47 years, with 501 (45.9%) in junior high school and 591 (54.1%) in senior high school. Additionally, following the methodology of previous studies61, a t-test was conducted to compare scores on the main study variables between lost participants and those successfully tracked. The results demonstrated no statistically significant difference (t = 0.07 ~ 0.59, ps > 0.05), indicating that no systematic loss of participants occurred in this study. Of note, because trait anger and malevolent creativity were measured concurrently at T2, the indirect pathway does not support causal mediation and is interpreted as a prospective indirect association.

Measures

Bully/victim scale –Chinese version

The Bully/Victim Questionnaire, developed by Olweus62 and revised by Peng63, was used in this study. The questionnaire consisted of 6 items, such as “some students hit, kick, push, bump into, or threaten me,” etc. The scale is based on a 5-point Likert format, with response options ranging from 0 (“no”) to 4 (“Five times or more”). Higher scores reflect greater severity of school bullying experiences. The Cronbach’s α of this scale was previously reported to be 0.82 among Chinese middle school students64. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale (T1) was 0.86.

Malevolent creativity scale–Chinese version

The Malevolent Creativity Behavior Scale was developed by Hao et al.65. The questionnaire consisted of 13 items, which were divided into three dimensions: hurting others, joking, and lying. For example, “I’ve thought of unusual ways to hurt people who stand in the way of my goals”. The scale uses a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (always), with a higher score indicating a higher level of malevolent creativity. It has also been reported that the Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.94 among Chinese middle school students17. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the scale(T1) was 0.86, and the scale(T2) was 0.87.

Trait anger scale–Chinese version

The Trait Anger Scale, developed by Spielberger66 and revised by Luo67, was used in its Chinese version. The scale consists of 10 items, such as “When I’m angry, I tend to use foul language.” Participants rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (“not at all”) to 4 (“always”). A higher score indicates a higher level of trait anger. Previous research among Chinese middle school students reported a Cronbach’s α of 0.945. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale (T2) was 0.94.

Moral identity scale–Chinese version

The Moral Identity Scale, developed by Aquino & Reed48 and revised by Wan68, was used in its Chinese version. The questionnaire includes 16 items, such as “It makes me feel great to be a person with such qualities”. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”). A higher score indicates a higher level of moral identity. The scale demonstrated an internal consistency of 0.72, as reported in a study by Gui et al. on Chinese middle school students69. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale (T1) was 0.95.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and statistical analysis were conducted using SPSS 27.0, including a common method bias test, descriptive statistics, and correlation analysis. Model 4 in the SPSS plug-in PROCESS was used to test the prospective indirect association of trait anger (T2), and, controlling for other variables, Model 58 was used to test the model of moderated prospective indirect association. Consistent with the design limitation noted in Participants and procedure, the concurrent measurement of trait anger and malevolent creativity at T2 means that the indirect path in this model is cross-sectional. Hence, we interpret the results as prospective indirect associations, not causal mediation. The significance of all regression coefficients was tested using the bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval.

The bifactor model requires adding a method factor as a global factor in addition to the original design factors. If the CFA model with multiple scales combined shows a substantial improvement in fit indices after incorporating the method factor (CFI and TLI increases exceeding 0.1, RMSEA and SRMR decreases exceeding 0.05)70, it suggests that there was a serious common method bias.

As shown in Table 1, baseline confirmatory factor analysis models were first estimated for both T1 and T2 data, followed by bifactor models with an added method factor. For T1, model fit changes were: ΔCFI = 0.05, ΔTLI = 0.04, ΔRMSEA = -0.01, ΔSRMR = -0.02. For T2, the changes were: ΔCFI = 0.05, ΔTLI = 0.06, ΔRMSEA = -0.02, ΔSRMR = -0.02. In both waves, the improvements in fit indices did not meet the criteria for severe common method bias. Thus, common method bias was not a serious concern in this study.

Test of the moderated prospective indirect effect model

Firstly, the variables were standardized using the SPSS plugin PROCESS 4.2 model 4. After controlling for malevolent creativity (T1), the prospective indirect association of trait anger (T2) between bullying victimization (T1) and malevolent creativity (T2) was tested. The results showed that bullying victimization(T1) significantly positively correlated with trait anger (T2) (β = 0.47, t = 18.70, p < 0.001), and malevolent creativity(T2) (β = 0.14, t = 5.16, p < 0.001); Trait anger (T2) positively correlated with malevolent creativity (T2) (β = 0.40, t = 14.26, p < 0.001). The bias-corrected percentile bootstrap test (5,000 resamples) indicated that the total prospective association was 0.33, the direct prospective association was 0.14, and the prospective indirect association via trait anger (T2) was 0.19 (95% CI [0.15, 0.24]). The indirect pathway accounted for 57.58% of the total prospective association. This proportion should be interpreted with caution, however, because trait anger and malevolent creativity were measured concurrently at T2. Accordingly, we refer to this pathway as a prospective indirect association rather than a causal mediation effect.

Secondly, using the SPSS plugin PROCESS 4.2 (model 58), the moderating role of moral identity (T1) was tested after controlling for malevolent creativity (T1). As shown in Table 3, the interaction term between bullying victimization (T1) and moral identity (T1) was significantly associated with trait anger (T2) (β = -0.14, t = -7.83, p < 0.001), and the interaction term between trait anger (T2) and moral identity (T1) was significantly associated with malevolent creativity (T2) (β = -0.12, t = -5.17, p < 0.001). These results indicate that moral identity (T1) can moderate the relationship between bullying victimization (T1) and trait anger (T2), as well as the relationship between trait anger (T2) and malevolent creativity (T2) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study employed a two-wave prospective design to examine the prospective associations between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity in middle school students, as well as the underlying mechanisms. The results revealed three main findings: (1) Bullying victimization (T1) was positively and prospectively associated with malevolent creativity (T2); (2) Trait anger (T2) served as a prospective indirect association between bullying victimization (T1) and malevolent creativity (T2); (3) Moral identity (T1) played a moderating role in the first and second halves of this prospective indirect path.

The relationship between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity

The current study confirmed a positive prospective association between bullying victimization (T1) and malevolent creativity (T2) in middle school students, supporting Hypothesis 1. It is consistent with prior studies indicating that middle school students are more susceptible to malevolent creativity when exposed to high levels of bullying victimization25. This once again supports the Sensitivity to Mean Intentions model32, which suggests that the perception of unfair treatment is associated with negative creativity. In contrast, the perception of fair treatment fosters positive creativity. According to this theory, middle school students who encounter bullying in a threatening environment not only easily perceive unfair environmental cues but also develop a suspicious mindset upon detecting malice, which in turn activates their malevolent creativity to defend fairness and their own interests71.

The prospective indirect association of trait anger

The study also demonstrated that trait anger (T2) forms a prospective indirect association between bullying victimization (T1) and malevolent creativity (T2), supporting Hypothesis 2. Specifically, bullying victimization was prospectively associated with higher levels of trait anger, which in turn was concurrently associated with higher malevolent creativity. According to the trait activation theory36,72, individuals’ latent traits become activated in response to specific external situations. Consequently, more frequent bullying experiences make students prone to anger, further activating their underlying anger trait. Additionally, based on the integrated cognitive model45, individuals with high trait anger are prone to cognitive attribution biases that produce hostile interpretations, thereby leading to associations with malevolent creativity. Therefore, when trait anger in middle school students is activated due to bullying, they are more likely to interpret bullying-related information in a hostile manner, intensifying their malevolent creative tendencies.

The moderating role of moral identity

Finally, the results confirmed Hypotheses 3 and 4, showing that moral identity (T1) moderated the relationship between bullying victimization (T1) and trait anger (T2), as well as the relationship between trait anger (T2) and malevolent creativity (T2). Specifically, compared with middle school students with a high level of moral identity, those with a low level are more likely to exhibit high levels of trait anger and malevolent creativity after being bullied. This study also demonstrates that moral identity, as an internal protective factor, is associated with a reduced risk of malevolent creativity in middle school students and is associated with both phases of the mechanism. Moreover, our research supports the social cognitive theory of moral identity49,50, which posits that individuals facing situations that challenge morality or fairness and who possess high levels of moral identity have enhanced perceptions of fairness and emotion regulation skills53, enabling them to more effectively prevent unethical behavior. Based on this theory, middle school students with high moral identity show lower activation levels of trait anger when facing campus bullying victimization, due to their understanding of fairness and ability to effectively regulate their emotions; simultaneously, by enhancing self-control, they can inhibit the occurrence of malevolent creativity, a form of unethical behavior. In addition, the moderating role of moral identity can be interpreted using the SeMI model. According to this model, victim sensitivity can be considered a personality structure31. Individuals with high victim sensitivity are particularly sensitive to hostile cues, and their defensive motivation weakens the emotion regulation and behavioral inhibition functions of moral identity, thereby strengthening the positive associations between bullying victimization and trait anger, and between trait anger and malevolent creativity. Conversely, adolescents with low victim sensitivity can fully leverage the protective role of moral identity by interpreting bullying as a moral norm violation and effectively inhibiting anger-driven malevolent creativity. Moral identity, as a buffer, is associated with lower trait anger and malevolent creativity among bullied middle school students.

Implications and limitations

There is no doubt that this study holds significant theoretical value and practical worth. On the one hand, this study explores the malevolent creativity of middle school students in relation to the external environment, personality traits, and cognition, thereby helping to identify prospective correlates and potential pathways of malevolent creativity, thereby enriching theoretical research on malevolent creativity. On the other hand, this study provides practical guidance for educators in preventing malevolent creativity among middle school students. Educators could mitigate bullying behaviors, decrease trait anger, and may help reduce malevolent creativity by fostering a supportive campus environment, encouraging students to internalize values centered on “respect for life”, stimulating prosocial behaviors, and cultivating emotional regulation skills. In addition, the moderating role of moral identity suggests that educators could promote the internalization of moral traits, foster a sense of justice and responsibility, enhance sensitivity to bullying behaviors, inhibit the moral disengagement rationalization process, and reduce malevolent creativity through initiatives such as moral identity workshops73. Furthermore, adopting moral education models similar to those used in European schools and workplaces could strengthen interventions targeting middle school students’ moral identity, thereby reducing reactive behaviors74.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Firstly, reliance on self-report measures may introduce social desirability bias75, leading individuals to conceal or minimize their involvement in harmful creative behaviors. This limitation potentially affects data authenticity and the accuracy of the findings. Future research should consider adopting diverse methods, including parental, peer, and teacher nominations, as well as experimental approaches to enhance data accuracy. Secondly, this two-wave design limits causal inference76. Trait anger and malevolent creativity were measured simultaneously at T2, and trait anger was not measured at T1. This fails to control baseline anger and makes the indirect path cross-sectional, precluding temporal precedence. Omitted variable bias may also be present. Moreover, malevolent creativity (T1) may lead to subsequent bullying victimization (unverified). This reverse pathway introduces endogeneity, suggesting our observed prospective association could be partially bidirectional. Future research should adopt a three-stage design (e.g., T1: bullying victimization and malevolent creativity; T2: trait anger; T3: malevolent creativity) with autoregressive control to clarify causal relationships. Thirdly, the sample was limited to junior and senior high school students in Wuhu City, Anhui Province. Given the vast socioeconomic and educational diversity across Chinese provinces, the present findings may not be generalizable to rural areas, western regions, or more developed eastern regions, which constrains the external validity of this study. In addition, the present study did not address potential cultural biases in assessing moral identity and malevolent creativity within Chinese versus Western contexts. Future research should extend sampling to other regions in China and consider cross-contextual behaviors related to malevolent creativity among middle school students77. This would help clarify whether students from diverse contexts or cultural backgrounds exhibit similar responses in terms of malevolent creativity after experiencing bullying, thereby enhancing the external validity of the findings. Finally, this study examined bullying within school environments but overlooked cyberbullying, an increasingly prevalent issue among middle school students. Therefore, future studies should explore how cyberbullying influences malevolent creativity among middle school students.

Conclusion

This prospective study demonstrates a positive association between bullying victimization and malevolent creativity, and that trait anger serves as a prospective indirect association. Moral identity buffers this risk by weakening the associations between bullying victimization and trait anger, as well as those between trait anger and malevolent creativity. Interventions targeting moral identity and anger regulation may help reduce malevolent creativity during adolescence.
