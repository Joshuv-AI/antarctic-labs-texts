Dysregulated B cell homeostasis and the involvement of B cell-activating factor in the progression of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Yongshun Wang, Ling Cui, Wenjing He, Yuchen Zhou, Ziya Zhao, Huangli Lu, Diyang Ke, Qidi Wu, Wen Deng, Zhou Zhou, Fan Xu, Haibin Zhong, Fen Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is a major cause of vision loss and is characterized by inflammation induced microvascular damage, but the contribution of B-cell-mediated inflammation and its key factor BAFF in the DR progression remains poorly understood. In the present study, we first found that DR patients exhibited increase in frequency of B cell and a concurrent reduction in Breg frequency compared to controls, accompanied by significantly elevated BAFF concentrations in both serum and aqueous humor, with levels peaking in proliferative DR. To further elucidate the role of B cell and BAFF, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments. In vitro, high-glucose stimulation of BV-2 microglial cells induced upregulation of BAFF and its receptor BAFF-R, and treatment with recombinant BAFF significantly enhanced microglial activation, as evidenced by increased Iba-1 expression. In vivo, STZ-induced diabetic mice showed the similar trends of the B cell and Breg imbalances observed in patients, with alongside marked upregulation of retinal BAFF and BAFF-R expression, consistent with the clinical findings. Importantly, BAFF neutralization significantly attenuated retinal vascular leakage in STZ-diabetic mice. Collectively, these findings suggest that BAFF-driven microglial activation and B cell-mediated inflammation are critical contributors to retinal vascular disruption in DR.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, B cell-activating factor (BAFF), Regulatory B cells (Bregs), Microglial activation, Immune inflammationSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Endocrinology, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a common microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), is a leading cause of irreversible vision loss in working-age adults, and it affects hundreds of millions of individuals worldwide, representing a major global public health challenge. DR progresses from the early non-proliferative stage to advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), characterized by pathological neovascularization and fibrovascular proliferation. It is widely acknowledged that chronic inflammation serves as a central mechanism in the pathogenesis of DR, driving retinal disturbances through sustained low-grade inflammatory responses and cytokine imbalances that lead to vascular and neuronal damage1,2.

Most research on inflammation in DR pathogenesis emphasizes chronic low-grade innate immune responses primarily driven by resident retinal microglia and infiltrating macrophages/monocytes, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and VEGF) leading to blood-retinal barrier (BRB) breakdown, vascular permeability, pericyte loss, and neuronal damage3–5. Adaptive immunity, particularly T cell-mediated inflammation, is also widely studied, with CD4+ T helper subsets (e.g., Th1, Th17, and follicular helper T cells) promoting cytokine storms, leukocyte adhesion, and neovascularization, while regulatory T cell (Treg) dysfunction exacerbates chronic inflammation and fails to suppress effector responses6–8. However, B cell-mediated inflammation receives less attention in the processes of DR.

Regulatory B cells (Bregs) represent a specialized subset of B lymphocytes that mediate immunosuppressive functions, primarily via secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-10 (IL-10). Bregs have been found to play a role in maintaining immune tolerance and preventing excessive inflammation in various autoimmune and chronic inflammatory conditions9,10. Importantly, in autoimmune conditions, the number and frequency of Bregs are usually impaired, resulting in a pro-inflammatory shift that amplifies immune dysregulation11.

B-cell activating factor (BAFF) plays a central role in B cell -mediated inflammation. BAFF belongs to the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily and is necessary for B cell survival, maturation, and differentiation. BAFF binds three cognate receptors with distinct cellular distributions and functional consequences:BAFF-receptor (BAFF-R), transmembrane activator and calcium modulator and cyclophilin ligand interactor (TACI), and B cell maturation antigen (BCMA)12,13. BAFF-R is the predominant receptor that mediates stimulatory signals in B cells via activation of the NF-κB1 and the PI3K/AKT pathway, collectively reinforcing B cell survival, maturation, and activation12,14. At physiological concentrations, BAFF maintains peripheral B cell homeostasis and can support Breg survival and induction. However, at pathologically elevated concentrations—as observed in multiple autoimmune diseases—BAFF preferentially drives the expansion of autoreactive and pro-inflammatory B cell subsets, then simultaneously impairing Breg maintenance15. Although BAFF has been extensively studied in systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome , its role in DR remains poorly defined15.

The present study is performed to examine the involvement of B cell and its key factor BAFF in the progression of DR in patient, in further, we aimed to investigate BAFF’s contributions to DR mechanisms and its potential as a therapeutic target STZ induced diabetic mice model.

Results

Frequency of B Cells and Bregs, and BAFF concentrations in patients with diabetic retinopathy

To elucidate the involvement of B cells and B cell-mediated humoral immunity in DR, we enrolled patient with NPDR and PDR, demographic data including age and sex distribution showed no significant differences across groups. However, patients with PDR exhibited significantly longer diabetes duration and higher HbA1c and fasting glucose levels compared to NPDR groups, indicating more severe and prolonged glycemic dysregulation in advanced DR (Table 1). We first isolated PBMCs from whole blood samples of subjects. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to quantify the frequency of CD19+ B cells. As illustrated in Fig 1A, representative dot plots depict the gating strategy for CD19+ B cells in control and DR groups, with mean frequencies of 2.5% and 5. 5%, respectively. Statistical analysis revealed a significant increase in B cell frequency in DR patients compared to controls (P < 0.01; Figure 1C).

We next assessed the frequency of Bregs, defined as CD19+CD24+CD38+ cells, within the B cell compartment (Fig 1B). Quantitative data demonstrated that the mean Bregs frequency in controls was 0.9 %, approximately 10-fold higher than in the DR group (0. 08 %; P < 0.001; Fig 1D). These findings indicate an expansion of total B cells accompanied by a marked depletion of the Bregs subset in DR patients.

We further evaluated levels of BAFF, a key regulator of B cell survival and function, in serum and aqueous humor samples. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) measurements showed escalating serum BAFF concentrations across disease stages: approximately 300 ng/mL in healthy controls, 520 ng/mL in NPDR, and 808 ng/mL in PDR (P < 0.05 for NPDR vs. control; P < 0.001 for PDR vs. control; Fig 1E). A similar trend was observed in aqueous humor, with BAFF levels significantly increased in PDR patients, however, there was no significant difference between controls and NPDR, although BAFF levels showed a slight upward trend in NPDR (Fig 1F).

Taken together, these results suggest that dysregulated B cell homeostasis—characterized by increased overall B cell frequency, diminished Bregs, and elevated BAFF levels—may contribute to the immunopathogenesis of DR, particularly in PDR stage.

Elevated B cell frequency and reduced regulator B cell frequency in STZ-diabetic mice indicate dysregulated B cell-mediated immune balance

To corroborate the observed alterations in B cell and Bregs frequencies in human DR, we established an STZ-diabetic mouse model and analyzed immune cell populations in PBMCs and freshly isolated splenocytes. Flow cytometric analysis identified CD19+CD45+ B cells in splenocytes (Fig 2A). The mean frequency of B cells in non-diabetic controls was 28%, whereas it increased significantly to approximately 40% in STZ-diabetic mice (P < 0.01; Fig 2B). Concurrently, Bregs, gated as CD19+CD45+CD1d+CD5+ cells, were quantified using flow cytometry (Fig 2C). Statistical analysis revealed a marked reduction in Bregs frequency from 4.1% in controls to 2.2% in STZ-diabetic mice (P < 0.01; Fig 2D). Comparable trends were observed in PBMCs samples, with a significant elevation in total B cell frequency and a concomitant decrease in Bregs in STZ-diabetic mice relative to controls (P < 0.05 for both; Fig 3). These findings in the mice model similar the finding in human sample, suggesting that immune dysregulation—characterized by B cell increase and Bregs depletion—contributes to inflammation in DR.

Upregulation of BAFF and BAFF-R implicates their involvement in STZ-diabetic mice

To examine the expression of BAFF and its receptor (BAFF-R) in DR, we assessed retinal tissues from STZ-diabetic mice at 6 months post-induction. Western blot analysis of retina demonstrated a significant upregulation of BAFF protein levels, approximately 1.5-fold higher than in age-matched non-diabetic controls (P < 0.01; Fig 4A). Immunofluorescence staining of retinal cryosections further confirmed elevated BAFF immunoreactivity across multiple layers, including the inner plexiform layer (IPL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), along with a concomitant increase in BAFF-R expression (Fig 4B). These observations suggest that the BAFF/BAFF-R axis is activated in vivo during chronic hyperglycemia, potentially contributing to retinal pathology in DR.

To validate these findings, we employed the BV-2 retinal microglial cell line as an in vitro model. Consistent with the in vivo data, exposure to high glucose (25 mM) for 48 hours significantly induced BAFF and BAFF-R protein expression in BV-2 cells, as quantified by Western blotting (**P < 0.01; Fig 4C). Immunofluorescence microscopy corroborated this upregulation, revealing enhanced BAFF and BAFF-R signals in high-glucose-treated cells compared to controls (Fig 4D).

To explore whether elevated BAFF influences microglial activation—a hallmark of neuroinflammation in DR—we treated BV-2 cells with recombinant BAFF and assessed Iba-1 expression, established marker of microglial reactivity. Representative images showed a marked increase in Iba-1-positive cells in BAFF-treated cultures relative to untreated controls, where Iba-1 immunoreactivity was sparse (Fig 4E). Quantitative analysis of the Iba-1-positive area fraction confirmed a statistically significant elevation in the BAFF-treated group (P < 0.01; Fig 4F).

Collectively, these in vivo and in vitro results indicate that hyperglycemia drives BAFF/BAFF-R upregulation in the diabetic retina and microglia, which may promote microglial activation and thereby exacerbate inflammatory processes in early DR.

BAFF blockade partially attenuates diabetes-induced retinal vascular leakage in STZ-diabetic mice

To determine whether neutralization of BAFF confers protection against diabetes-associated retinal vascular leakage, STZ-diabetic mice were treated with the BAFF-specific neutralizing monoclonal antibody Sandy-2 (2 mg/kg, i. p., every 2 weeks) starting one month after diabetes confirmation.

Fundus photography, fluorescein angiography (FFA), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) were performed among the three groups. Fundus photography exhibited no discernible alterations retinal morphology among healthy controls, STZ-diabetic mice, and STZ-diabetic mice receiving Sandy-2 (Fig. 5A). Similarly, late-phase FFA showed no significant vascular leakage, neovascularization, or avascular areas in any group (Fig. 5B). However, we can still observe capillary dilations resembling microaneurysms in STZ-diabetic retinas, a feature that was not appeared in control group (Fig. 5B). Whole-mount retinas stained for CD31 was performed, and it seems that reduced capillary density in the STZ-diabetic group was observed (Fig. 5C). However, Quantitative analysis of vessel density did not reach statistical significance (P >0.05; Fig. 6C).

To more sensitively detect functional disruption of the blood–retina barrier, we performed the Evans blue leakage assay. STZ-diabetic mice exhibited a significant 3.5-fold increase in retinal vascular permeability compared with non-diabetic controls (P < 0.01). Notably, BAFF neutralization with Sandy-2 significantly attenuated this diabetes-induced leakage (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D–E), indicating that BAFF contributes to early barrier dysfunction.

In vivo OCT angiography (OCTA) centered on the optic nerve head demonstrated a trend toward reduced peripapillary vessel density (PVD) in the radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) plexus of STZ-diabetic mice (P < 0.05). Although Sandy-2 treatment slightly improved PVD compared with the untreated diabetic group, the difference did not achieve statistical significance (Fig. 6A–B).

Taken together, these data demonstrate that BAFF blockade does not prevent the retinal vessel alterations in STZ-diabetic mice but significantly ameliorates diabetes-associated retinal vascular hyperpermeability, suggesting a predominant role for BAFF in promoting blood–retina barrier breakdown rather than overt capillary dropout at this stage of disease.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated dysregulated B cell homeostasis in DR, characterized by increased total B cell frequency and decreased Bregs frequency in PBMCs from PDR patients, alongside elevated BAFF levels in serum and aqueous humor, with the highest concentrations observed in PDR cases. These alterations were recapitulated in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice, where similar shifts in B and Bregs frequencies were evident in both PBMCs and splenocytes. Furthermore, we observed upregulation of BAFF and its receptor (BAFF-R) in the diabetic mouse retina and in high-glucose-exposed BV-2 microglial cells in vitro, with recombinant BAFF promoting microglial activation as indicated by increased Iba-1 expression. Therapeutically, BAFF neutralization using the monoclonal antibody Sandy-2 partially attenuated diabetes-induced retinal vascular leakage.

Our findings of increased B cell frequency and diminished Bregs in PBMCs from PDR patients align with emerging evidence implicating B cell dysregulation in DR pathogenesis16–19. Study reported by Amira M. Shalaby20 and colleagues reported that altered regulatory B cell subsets was observed in type 1 diabetes mellitus, consistent with our results, our study further demonstrates a progressively impaired balance between B cell and Bregs in DR patients, especially in advanced DR stages. Additionally, increased B cell-produced antibodies like IgA and IgM in vitreous humor of type 2 diabetic DR patients, as found by Liu et al.16, supports our observation of B cell dysregulation. The marked reduction in Bregs, which typically exert anti-inflammatory effects through IL-10 secretion and cell-contact mechanisms, may exacerbate retinal inflammation by failing to suppress pro-inflammatory responses21,22. Mechanistically, this imbalance could stem from chronic hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and cytokine milieu alterations23 which favor effector B cell survival while impairing Bregs differentiation24,25, as observed in other autoimmune diseases like SLE, RA and so on26,27. Validation in the STZ-diabetic mouse model revealed parallel B cell expansion and Bregs depletion in PBMCs and splenocytes, reinforcing the findings of our human observations.

BAFF is a key regulator of B-cell survival, maturation, and immune tolerance and signals through three receptors: BAFF-R (BR3), TACI, and BCMA. Among these receptors, BAFF-R plays the dominant role in maintaining mature B-cell survival. Activation of BAFF-R triggers several intracellular signaling pathways, including the canonical and non-canonical NF-κB pathways as well as PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling cascades, which promote B-cell proliferation, differentiation, and antibody production28. Importantly, the biological effects of BAFF depend on its concentration and receptor engagement. when BAFF levels become pathologically elevated—as observed in multiple autoimmune diseases, excessive BAFF signaling might preferentially promotes the survival of autoreactive or pro-inflammatory B-cell subsets while disrupting immune balance.

The concurrent elevation of BAFF in serum and aqueous humor observed in our study supports this immune imbalance in DR patients29–31. Elevated systemic BAFF levels and the imbalance between effector B cells and Bregs may create a pro-inflammatory circulating immune environment. Circulating immune mediators, including cytokines, antibodies, and complement components, could influence retinal vascular integrity and contribute to blood-retinal barrier (BRB) disruption32–34.

Our observation extends prior work by associating BAFF with PDR severity, supporting the rationale for exploring targeted therapies35.Except the potential interaction between systemic immune dysregulation and local retinal inflammation in DR, the retinal cells itself may also contribute to local BAFF elevation. Indeed, immunofluorescence staining revealed BAFF and BAFF-R overexpression across several retinal layers, including IPL, OPL, outer segment region and the RPE layer. Our data in vitro demonstrated that microglia resident in retina could express a larger amount of BAFF when induced by inflammatory cytokines, suggest that BAFF signaling in the diabetic retina may involve multiple retinal cell populations rather than being restricted to immune cells. However, other retinal cell types—including neurons and RPE cells—may also participate in, future studies will be required to further define the cellular sources and targets of BAFF within the retina.

In our study, despite employing multiple imaging modalities—including funduscopy, fluorescein angiography (FFA), and CD31 immunostaining via confocal microscopy—in STZ-diabetic mice at six months post-induction, we observed no retinal vascular alterations reminiscent of those in PDR patients. Consistent with prior reports, rodent models of diabetes predominantly manifest early-stage NPDR hallmarks, such as pericyte loss, acellular capillary, basement membrane thickening, microaneurysms, and blood-retinal barrier (BRB) disruption36–40. Nevertheless, utilizing the Evans blue extravasation assay, we detected increased retinal vascular permeability in STZ-diabetic mice, indicating functional BRB impairment despite the absence of advanced structural changes. Additionally, in vivo optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) focused on the optic nerve head disclosed diminished peripapillary vessel density (PVD) within the radial peripapillary capillary (RPC) plexus in STZ-diabetic mice. Although BAFF neutralization with Sandy-2 did not reverse the reduced PVD in STZ-diabetic mice, it partially attenuated diabetes-induced retinal vascular leakage, indicating its benefit on BRB integrity. This suggests that BAFF may preferentially contribute to early DR pathological changes, such as potentially BRB damage, rather than later-stage manifestations like peripapillary vascular density alterations. Overall, these insights position BAFF as a novel therapeutic target for early DR, potentially complementing anti-VEGF strategies by addressing immune-mediated aspects of vascular dysfunction.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although systemic Breg reduction was identified in both patients and STZ-diabetic mice, the presence or infiltration of Bregs within retinal tissue was not directly examined, as lymphocytes are extremely scarce in the retina and no definitive Breg-specific markers are currently available for tissue immunostaining. Second, although age and sex distribution were comparable across groups, several clinical variables, including diabetes duration, HbA1c, fasting glucose, prevalence of hypertension, and current medications (insulin therapy), differed across groups and were not controlled for. Whether elevated BAFF levels are influenced by these confounding factors requires further investigation. Third, due to the limited total circulating blood volume in mice and the low frequency of Bregs in peripheral blood, we assessed B cell and Breg frequencies using both PBMCs and splenocytes in the mice model, whereas only PBMCs were analyzed in humans. Although the spleen serves as a major peripheral lymphoid organ in mice and provides a representative B cell pool, differences in sampling compartments between species may limit direct interspecies comparability.

Materials and methods

Patients

Patients with DR were enrolled from the Department of Ophthalmology at Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital between January 2023 and August 2025. DR patients were categorized according to the International Clinical Grading Scale for Diabetic Retinopathy into the following groups: non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) patients and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) patients41. Patients with a history of intraocular surgery, retinal laser photocoagulation, or VEGF therapy, as well as those with glaucoma, other ocular diseases, or systemic diseases, were excluded. Healthy subjects undergoing cataract surgery served as the control group. Participants with systemic diseases were excluded from the control group. Aqueous humor and serum samples were collected from each participant. Sampling was completed prior to initiating any treatment. The study protocol adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study received prior approval from the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital, and all patients provided written informed consent. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks old) were procured from the Medical Laboratory Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University (Nanning, China). The mice were housed in standard conditions with access to food and water ad libitum, maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and kept at a constant temperature of 28°C. Diabetes was induced through intraperitoneal administration of streptozotocin at a dosage of 55 mg/kg, dissolved in 0.1% citrate buffer (pH 4.5), for five consecutive days. The control group received an equivalent volume of citrate buffer without STZ42. Blood glucose levels and body weight were monitored weekly using an electronic balance and an active blood glucose meter from day five post-injection. Mice with blood glucose levels exceeding 300 mg/dL were considered diabetic and included in subsequent experiments. To evaluate the effect of BAFF neutralization on diabetes-associated retinal vascular dysfunction, diabetic mice were randomly assigned to receive either BAFF neutralization therapy or vehicle control. One month after diabetes confirmation, mice in the treatment group were administered the BAFF-specific neutralizing monoclonal antibody Sandy-2 (anti-mouse BAFF IgG+, clone Sandy-2) by intraperitoneal injection at 2 mg/kg body weight every 2 weeks for the duration of the study43. At the study endpoint, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation using a dedicated euthanasia chamber connected to a compressed CO₂ cylinder equipped with a pressure-reducing regulator and a calibrated flow meter. CO₂ (100%) was delivered using a gradual-fill method at 5 L/min, followed by cervical dislocation. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration and heartbeat. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region People’s Hospital (Approval No. KY-GZR-2025-159) and complied with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Cell culture

BV-2 cells were purchased from Procell and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and passaged when they reached 80–90% confluence. Cells were cultured in either high-glucose DMEM (25 mM glucose) or low-glucose DMEM (5.5 mM glucose), both were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin-streptomycin. The high-glucose group was used as the experimental group, while the low-glucose group served as control. After 48 h of treatment, the in vitro model was established and cells were harvested for subsequent assays44.

Western blotting

Retinal samples from STZ-diabetic mice and BV-2 cells were lysed in a lysis buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, protease inhibitor, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Samples were thoroughly homogenized and lysed on ice for 1 h prior to centrifugation. The supernatant was collected, mixed with an equal volume of loading buffer, and boiled at 100°C for 5 min to denature proteins. Proteins were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane, and blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 hour. The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-BAFF antibody. On the following day, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at 25°C for 1 h. Signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Afterwards, eyes were enucleated from mice and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Retinas were dissected in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 and 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), then blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies including IB4 and antibodies against BAFF and BAFF-R. After washing, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 555 and Alexa Fluor® 488 secondary antibodies at room temperature, then mounted on slides for imaging using confocal microscopy. For cryosections (10 µm), sections were dried, washed, permeabilized with 0.2% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 with 5% serum in PBS, then incubated with primary antibodies against BAFF and BAFF-R at 4 °C overnight followed by Alexa Fluor secondary antibody staining at room temperature. Retinal layer localization was identified relative to GCL, INL, IPL, ONL and OPL in standard retinal IF protocols. For BV-2 cells, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 3% BSA and 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS, incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against BAFF, BAFF-R, and Iba-1, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI nuclear stain at room temperature. Confocal images were acquired using a Nikon confocal microscope and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

BAFF levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum and aqueous humor samples were collected and added to ELISA plates. Absorbance was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader.

Optical coherence tomography and fluorescein angiography

Mice were anesthetized with a ketamine-xylazine mixture (100 mg/kg ketamine: 4 mg/kg xylazine) administered intraperitoneally (i.p.). Pupils were dilated with 1% compound tropicamide powder. To prevent corneal drying, rigid contact lenses with a base optical zone radius (BOZR 1.70), diameter (DIAM 3.20), and power (PWR 0.00) suitable for mouse corneas were used. Before and after imaging, mice were placed on a water-circulating heating pad to maintain body temperature. OCT angiography was performed using a Heidelberg HRA+OCT with a 30° lens and Blue Mouse adapter (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Software versions 6.7 to 6.9 were employed. Horizontal and vertical linear OCT scans were conducted within an 8.8 mm range centered on the optic disc in high-resolution mode, with an automatic real-time resolution of 100. Subsequently, sodium fluorescein (100 mg/kg) was administered via intraperitoneal injection, and the system was switched to FFA mode to observe changes in retinal vascular morphology.

Fundus Color Photography Examination: Mice were anesthetized as described above, and pupils were dilated with compound tropicamide. Carbomer gel was applied to the cornea to prevent drying, and the mice were placed on the imaging platform. Fundus images were acquired using a Xinlian biomicroscope fundus camera. Afterward, the mice received an i.p. injection of atipamezole (0.25 mg/kg) and were kept on a heated pad until ambulatory.

Evans blue detection kit

Evans blue extravasation was assessed according to a previously published protocol (20). Briefly, Evans blue dye was dissolved in 0.9% NaCl at a concentration of 30 mg/mL and administered via iliac vein injection at a dose of 45 mg/kg under anesthesia. Following injection, Evans blue was allowed to circulate for 2 h. During the circulation period, 300 μL blood samples were collected from the tail vein at 30-min intervals to determine Evans blue concentrations. At the end of circulation, mice were perfused transcardially with 0.05 mol/L citrate buffer containing 1% paraformaldehyde. The eyes were enucleated after the mice were euthanized as previously described, and retinas were carefully dissected from the eyecups under a stereomicroscope. Isolated retinas were dried at 45 °C for 5 h, and retinal dry weight was recorded. Each pair of dried retinas was then incubated in 150 μL formamide at 70 °C for 18 h to extract Evans blue dye. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 18, 000 × g for 50 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for spectrophotometric measurement of Evans blue content. Blood samples were centrifuged at 18, 000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the resulting plasma was diluted with formamide for spectrophotometric determination of Evans blue concentration. Quantification of retinal Evans blue leakage was performed as previously described and normalized to retinal dry weight, mean plasma Evans blue concentration, and circulation time, with results expressed as μL plasma × g retinal dry weight⁻1 × h⁻145.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood from mice was collected in EDTA tubes. Spleens were mashed through 70 µm strainers, and Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated by Ficoll-Paque density gradient. RBCs were lysed with commercial RED lysis buffer. Cells were washed, resuspended in PBS with 2% FCS, and incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD1d, CD5, CD45, and CD19 (1: 500) for 30 min at room temperature. After washing, data were collected on FACSCanto II and analyzed in FlowJo. Gating strategies followed standard immunophenotyping protocols for B cell subsets. Human PBMCs were isolated similarly, stained with antibodies against CD19, CD38, and CD24 (1: 500) for B cell subpopulation analysis. Data acquisition and analysis were performed as above.

Statistics

Results are presented as mean ± SEM. For two-group comparisons, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was performed followed by appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.
