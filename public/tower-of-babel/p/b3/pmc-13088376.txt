Cost of an Audit and Feedback intervention to increase uptake of maternal and child health services in Mozambique's Primary health care

ABSTRACT

Background

Global efforts to reduce preventable mortality among woman and children remain central to achieving the 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), as these interventions directly impact current and future population health In Mozambique [1]. The Ministry of Health (MoH) has developed and implemented various evidence-based maternal, newborn, and child health (MNCH) guidelines across more than 1000 primary healthcare facilities, providing basic maternal and child health services free of charge or at a reduced cost [2]. Despite these efforts and high service coverage (> 95% antenatal care attendance, 75% facility-based deliveries, and 80% child immunization coverage) [3], high under-five and maternal mortality persist [4–6].

Implementation science bridges the gap between research evidence and real-world practice by developing contextually relevant strategies to implement proven interventions [7]. A common strategy used in implementation science is Audit and Feedback (A&F), which improves clinical practice through three key steps: measuring performance against standards, comparing results to benchmarks, and delivering targeted feedback to drive improvement [8–10]. While systematic reviews demonstrate that previous A&F research has primarily targeted physicians and clinical teams in high-income settings [9], research is limited on A&F models that target clinic-level performance, as well as on A&F in low and middle-income countries (LMICs).

Although costing studies and economic analysis are increasingly conducted in implementation research there is a lack of data on the financial and economic costs of implementing an A&F strategy in LMICs [11–13]. Understanding the costs of implementing A&F strategies is essential for decision-making and planning to scale and sustain these improvement approaches, many which are funded by external aid. Cost estimates can serve as inputs for cost-effectiveness analyses and budget impact analyses to evaluate the efficiency and affordability of implementation. This study’s objectives are to estimate the total implementation cost and cost per intervention components of A&F model that targeted clinic-level performance in Mozambique and compare its total financial cost against district health budgets.

Methods

The Integrated District Evidence-to-Action (IDEAs) program

The Integrated District Evidence-to-Action (IDEAs) program was a system-level (facility and district focused) A&F strategy implemented in central Mozambique from 2016 to 2020 that adapted best practices from the A&F literature [14, 15]. Unlike traditional clinician-focused A&F models common in high-income countries [9, 10, 12], IDEAs focused on health facility performance with districts coordinating and providing feedback for health facilities in their catchment. The program featured regular performance audits linked to facility and system-level indicators, with feedback delivered through a structured process involving frontline supervisors from participating health facilities as well as district and provincial health teams. This approach was used as clearly defined; priority-aligned targets coordinated with Ministry of Health (MoH) guidelines [14] and was complemented by supportive supervision and financial support. IDEAs was implemented in partnership with Comité para Saúde de Moçambique, a Mozambican non-governmental organization that supported MoH staff with IDEAs implementation.

IDEAs consisted of a multi-component program that involved three key cyclical and iterative steps repeated every six months (see Fig. 1). The first step, facility and district readiness assessments, assess health facility readiness including staffing; availability of essential medicines, equipment and supplies; and data quality. The readiness assessments are aligned with the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy (words in italics below), specifically related to assessing for readiness and identifying barriers and facilitators, and conducting local needs assessments strategies [16]. This step was contracted with a local organization. The second step, A&F meetings, held at the district-level, brought together facility-based maternal and child health (MCH) managers, nurses, and district/provincial teams (averaging 29 participants) to review performance and develop action plans. The Audit and Feedback meetings correspond directly to the ERIC taxonomy of strategies, specifically audit and provide feedback, while also incorporating facilitation of problem-solving and elements of developing and implementation blueprint through the creation of action plans informed by performance data [16]. The third step was targeted at facility support from the district using IDEAs-supplied financial assistance to help three facilities chosen based on their performance in service delivery indicators. These three facilities received up to two supervisions per cycle. During supervision visits, action plans were reviewed, obstacles to guideline implementation were identified, and technical assistance was provided to address context-specific barriers. This step is mapped to ERIC strategies, such as providing clinical supervision, providing local technical assistance, and altering incentive or allowance structures, to incentivize adoption and enable implementation activities [16]. More details about the IDEA study design can be found in Dinis et al. [15].

Study setting

Mozambique is comprised of eleven provinces, the highest level of administrative divisions. Each province is comprised by multiple districts [17]. According to the 2017 Mozambique population and housing census, the population was 28.8 million [17]. There are over 1500 health facilities in Mozambique, with 96% providing primary health care [18]. IDEAs was implemented in Sofala and Manica provinces, from 2016 to 2020 covering a total of 12 higher population districts and 154 health facilities, (See Additional File: Figure S1) [15]. These provinces have a combined population of over 4 million.

Study design

We conducted a cost analysis of the IDEAs program from the Ministry of Health (payer) perspective following the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERs) guidelines [18] (Additional file: Table S1). Costing followed a mixed-method approach using gross- and micro-costing, with top-down and bottoms-up cost allocation. In a Top-down cost allocation, the total intervention expenditure, including costs incurred above the service level, was divided across activities to calculate average unit costs [19]. In a Bottom-up cost allocation, unit costs were derived by identifying and measuring the specific quantities of inputs used for each activity [19]. Using both approaches helps address data limitation when detailed input data is missing, top-down fills gaps; when overall cost data is uncertain, bottom-up provides a check. We used activity-based gross costing to understand aggregated, high-level costs using a top-down approach for each of the core activities within IDEAs. We chose gross costing since one of the activities in IDEAs (step 1: facility and district readiness assessment) was contracted out and did not have detailed financial information on inputs. We used activity-based microcosting for the other two activities in IDEAs (step 2 and 3), where we had input-level data [20, 21].

Cost categories and costing approaches

Costs are reported in 2020 US dollars. For all other cost categories, a 3% discount rate was applied across all years to express expenditures on a common 2020 USD basis; no currency conversion or local inflation adjustment was applied, as these costs were already recorded in U.S. dollars [19, 21, 22]. Capital costs were annualized over a five-year useful life using the same 3% discount rate [19, 21]. This approach follows standard practice in global health economic evaluations for discounting and standardizing costs, and avoids the need for country-specific inflation adjustments when expenditure data are already denominated in USD. Program overview data were sourced from study team. Data were sourced from project expenditure reports, consultation with IDEAs project team and publicly available reports. Project expenditure reports were prepared by Comité para Saúde de Moçambique. Surveys related to programmatic implementation were collected and stored in Redcap™ [23]. Detailed cost assumptions can be found in the Additional file: S2 Table.

Gross costing

Using a top-down approach, costs for the three core areas of activities (facility and district readiness assessment, A&F meetings, and targeted facility support) were aggregated using program expenditure data, which were divided by the number of intervention districts (n = 12) [24]. In addition, we estimated total training and intervention operational management costs, aggregated and divided by district. Using the annual expenditure reports, we categorized activities into activity, capital, and “other”. For activity allocation, we created subgroups based on whether the activity involved district meetings, facility and district readiness assessments, targeted facility supports supervision, activity operation, or training. To estimate average annual cost per district, we aggregated actual costs by year and intervention activities across all intervention districts (n = 12). We divided adjusted estimates by twelve to determine the average annual cost per district for each intervention component.

Microcosting

Two of the three core IDEAs activities (A&F meetings and Targeted facility support) had granular level inputs that we used to estimate activity costs and cost drivers for these two activities using a bottom-up approach [19, 24]. Activity inputs were classified as accommodation, consultancy, equipment and supplies, food, overhead, per diem, personnel, transportation, venue, or other. The third activity, targeted at facility support, was classified in two categories within it that we call supportive supervision and district support.

Staff time estimates and costs

We retrospectively estimated staff time for IDEAs Steps 2–3 (A&F meetings and supportive supervision). Staff hours were estimated by assigning each role a fixed annual workload and multiplying this by the number of staff in that role. Total hours were then converted to full-time equivalents (FTEs) using 160 monthly working hours per person. Activities were categorized into IDEA Audit and Feedback Meetings (n = 29), which included the Provincial heads of MCH and PMTCT, District heads of MCH and PMTCT, Facility heads of MCH, Facility MCH nurses, and the District M&E officers, and Supervision visits (n = 5), which involved the Provincial head of MCH, the District head of Health, the District head of MCH or PMTCT, and a driver. Annual staff costs were calculated by applying monthly salaries to the FTEs and adjusting all 2019 costs to 2020 values using a 5.7 percent inflation rate for Mozambique [25]. Costs were then converted to 2020 USD using the 2020 exchange rate of 69.50 Meticais per USD [26]. While the program was implemented as one initiative, as mentioned above the readiness assessments (Step 1) were conducted by an external contractor that did not provide detailed records of staff involvement or hours worked for this component. For the remaining activities, we worked directly with the IDEAs program team to identify participating personnel and their roles, then calculated annual hours using attendance records and position-specific time allocations.

Comparison against district health budget

We estimated the total 2020 district health budget by applying the reported 7% increase to the 6.5 billion Meticais budget from 2019, reported in Mozambique’s 2019 UNICEF annual budget brief report [27, 28]. The resulting total was then dived by Mozambique’s 180 districts to get an approximate annual health budget per district, which we used to contextualize IDEAs program annual per district costs against standard district health expenditures.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the average cost per district for each intervention area. The average district consisted of 12 primary care facilities. We also aggregated these costs to estimate the total average annual cost (based on calendar years). We differentiated between capital and recurrent costs, reporting all figures in 2020 US dollars, and calculated the average annual expenditure for each category per district. We then used this total cost per district in 2020 USD to compare against a standard district health budget as indicated above.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted a one-way or deterministic sensitivity analysis to identify which parameters have the most significant impact on the overall cost, results were visualized using tornado diagram [29]. The length of each bar reflects the extent of cost variation, with longer bars having higher sensitivity. We applied a standard 25% increase or decrease to selected parameters to assess sensitivity.

Scenario analysis

We conducted scenario analysis to evaluate the potential cost for different delivery options (see Table 1). The cost structure for each scenario was calculated based on yearly expenses per district (using 2020 US dollars).

Results

Between 2016–2020, there were a total of nine IDEAs cycles. By 2017 (year 2), the program reached full implementation of A&F meetings (100% of target), while facility participation remained consistently high at 97–98% annually. Over the five-year period, the program maintained an average of twelve participating facilities per district for A&F meetings each year, while support visits averaged 4.7 per district (Table 2).

Cost results

The total estimated cost of the IDEAs program from 2016 to 2020 was $2,224,341 (2020 USD), with most $1,702,648 (77.0%) spent on recurrent costs, and $495,323 (22.0%) spent on capital costs with the remaining on staff time costs. Each district had an average cost of $41,967, including $28,438 (68.0%) for recurrent expenses and $8255 (20.0%) for capital expenses and $5274 (13.0%) for staff time.

Of the $41,967 average annual IDEAs program cost per district, A&F meetings accounted for $10,893 (25.9%), with participant per diems representing 52.0% of this cost. Capital expenditures totaled $8255 (20.0%), primarily for vehicle purchases. Targeted support costs were divided between district-level allocations ($5700 or 13.6%, with most of this amount spent for action plan cash transfers) and facility supervision ($3623 or 8.6%, mostly for accommodation costs). Facility and district readiness assessment cost were $6351 (15.1%). All figures are presented in 2020 US dollars. Annual variations reflected implementation phases: 2016 (start-up) showed minimal costs during initial rollout, 2017–2019 demonstrated full implementation with stable expenditures, while 2020 saw reduced activity due to COVID-19 restrictions (See Fig. 2).

Discussion

The IDEAs program demonstrated the feasibility of integrating a modified A&F strategy into existing government health systems without requiring additional personnel. The program's costs were primarily driven by recurring expenses, such as per diems, food, and accommodation, which accounted for a significant proportion of total expenditures. Despite these costs, the use of existing staff and alignment with government-led primary health care activities highlight the program’s potential sustainability and scalability. This study provides a detailed costing analysis of a system-level, A&F intervention, combining gross-costing and microcosting approaches to capture both financial and economic costs in resource limited settings. Our estimates offer valuable insights for policymakers on resource allocation, particularly regarding recurrent activities like supervision and meetings.

The average annual cost of implementing the IDEAs intervention was $41,966 per district, representing approximately 6.6% of annual standard district budgets. A more detailed breakdown of district health budgets would have better informed decision-makers by enabling comparisons across specific cost categories, rather than relying solely on broader annual budget figures. Key cost drivers included per diems for A&F meetings (averaging two weeks annually) and district-level grants for facility supervision (1.5 weeks annually). In most SSA public sector health settings, per diems for system-level interventions, such as IDEAs, provide a form of motivation for nurses, doctors, and other healthcare workers, who earn relatively low salaries compared with their counterparts in the private sector, to remain engaged. However, we acknowledge that this is a contested area in health interventions within SSA, with varying perspectives on how it should be approached [30–32]. Sensitivity analyses highlight capital expenses, readiness assessments, and per diems as the most influential parameters, providing decision-makers with adaptable scenarios (e.g., phasing out lump-sum support or leveraging existing health information systems).

The Ministry of Health could lower costs by: 1) reducing A&F meeting participants to cut per diems; 2) substituting supervision grants with routine monitoring or affordable incentives; and 3) minimizing facility supervision through staff reductions or other program partnerships.

The IDEAs strategy was designed and evaluated using the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoptions, Implementation, Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework [33], which is published elsewhere [14, 15]. Here, we briefly summarize those results and situate the current costing findings within the broader RE-AIM context. Reach was relatively strong, with 56% of health facilities engaged (target 57%) and 69% and 73% of pregnant women reached at first and fourth ANC visits (target 70%). The main costs related to Reach were facility participation in A&F meetings. There were no additional program costs for reaching pregnant women because the strategy focused on managerial and facility readiness rather than demand-side activities. Adoption was high across all 12 districts and 154 facilities, and the associated costs, including A&F meetings, per diems, and staff time, were captured within district expenditures. Implementation fidelity assessments showed low fidelity for readiness assessments and for completing micro-interventions in the action plans. Costing data reflect this, with 15% of total costs allocated to readiness assessments and 14% to action plan support. Despite these allocations, facilities had difficulty completing planned actions, indicating potential misalignment between the challenges encountered and the support provided, or that funds were redirected to broader operational needs. Although facility participation in Maintenance activities was documented, the specific costs attributable to Maintenance could not be clearly measured within this costing analysis.

A comparable intervention in Kenya, involving training, supervision, and face-to-face meetings, cost $28,250 per hospital over 18 months [34]. This intervention, with an average of 32 health workers receiving both initial and follow-up training, cost an average of US$12,417 (44%). Local facilitator costs for supportive supervision were US$5698 (20%), and other facility-based supervision costs were US$10,136 (36%), resulting in a total implementation cost of US$28,250 per hospital for 18 months. While direct comparisons are limited due to differences in staff time inclusion and scope, both studies underscore the significant costs associated with in-person meetings and supervision.

While there are limited economic evaluation studies of A&F from LMICs, several studies from high-income countries have evaluated the costs of implementing A&F meetings. We identified a couple of such studies from these settings. In Quebec, Canada, a hypothetical A&F intervention in 57 trauma centers cost $1.48 million over five years, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of $160 per low-value practice avoided [35]. A study in North Carolina de-implementing unnecessary glucose monitoring reported total A&F costs of $16,775 over 12 months and $22,967 over 27 months for 20 clinics [36]. In this same study, non–site-based costs were $14,374 for 12 months and $17,565 for 27 months, driven by data programming and coding updates. Site-based costs were lower, totaling $2401 and $5402, respectively. In these studies, cost drivers were mainly technical or product-related, unlike in our study, where per diem, supervision, and cash support for action plans were the main expenses.

Economic evaluations of A&F interventions in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remain scarce; our study helps address this gap, contributing to the limited evidence base for LMICs. We provide information on the cost of implementing a system-level A&F strategy in an LMIC, using real-world data from routine service delivery. We used program expenditure reports, staff consultations, and publicly available reports to develop a comprehensive cost assessment. We applied a mixed costing method that combined top-down and bottom-up approaches. This allowed us to estimate costs for all core program components and provide detailed input-based cost calculations where reliable data were available. Scenario and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore how changes in key cost drivers could affect total implementation costs. Overall, our findings offer practical guidance for district-level planners considering how to adapt and scale IDEAs. Also, to our knowledge this is the first costing study of an A&F strategy in Mozambique’s primary care settings, where other studies could use our estimates for reference.

However, there are limitations. Limited cost disaggregation, especially for readiness assessments (Step 1), may have led to underestimation. However, we attempted to account for costs related to readiness assessments through overall contract payments. Expenditure data excluded indirect costs that implementing partners might find useful, however, the analysis was conducted from the Ministry of Health perspective. If implementing partners are interested, any overhead costs need to be added to the implementation costs based on their internal standards. Recall bias could have affected staff time reporting. If implementing partners are interested, any overhead costs would need to be added to the implementation costs based on their internal standards., and recall bias could have affected staff time reporting. The generalizability of findings is limited to contexts with similar health systems. Cost comparisons would be more useful if detailed district budgets were available to provide better context on the added cost of IDEAs. Further breakdowns by catchment population, number of facilities, and staffing could improve the relevance and accuracy of estimates for diverse settings with varying resource availability. This cost analysis provides important foundational perspective; however, the ultimate value of the approximately $42,000 annual investment per district can only be fully understood through future cost-effectiveness analyses that incorporate clinical and patient-reported outcomes.

Policymakers and donors should consider, taking into account recurring costs in routine budgets and exploring cost-optimization or reducing strategies, such as leveraging virtual tools or streamlining meeting structures, to enhance sustainability. Because recurrent costs such as perdiems and supervision expenses make up the largest share of total expenses, their central role underscores the importance of strengthening routine health system financing structures so that essential implementation activities can be sustained beyond donor funding cycles and they are less prone to cuts. Also, the strategies in this study are activities that can easily be integrated in routine quality improvement efforts without heavy investment in capital costs. Our cost data could support future cost-effectiveness and budget impact analyses to project health outcomes and costs related to district-level A&F implementation in similar settings. Future research should examine long-term sustainability of integrating such interventions into government-led programs and test alternative implementation approaches across diverse low-resource settings to inform scaling decisions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a detailed costing analysis of a system-level, A&F intervention, combining gross-costing and microcosting approaches to capture both financial and economic costs in resource limited settings. Through this mixed method costing approach, we aimed to address existing gaps in the limited implementation cost data on A&F interventions in sSA. These cost estimates could serve as inputs for cost-effectiveness and budget impact analysis to evaluate the efficiency and affordability of implementation.
