Diversity of glyphosate-degrading bacteria and degradation genes from Xinjiang cotton field’s unique soil environment
Authors: Mila Mulati, Lili Chai, Hao Xu, Siya Wu, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo clarify the diversity of glyphosate-degrading bacteria and genes in arid and alkaline soil environments, an efficient bacterial community, named CW, was enriched from the long-term continually cropped saline-alkali cotton soil in Xinjiang. This community could degrade 500 mg/L of glyphosate within 36 h in MSM medium with a pH of 8.0. The CW community was mainly composed of over 20 genera belonging to the phyla Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota. Notable genera include Hyphomicrobium, Pseudoxanthomonas, and Aquamicrobium. From this community, twenty-four strains showing glyphosate-degrading ability, representing 9 different genera, were successfully isolated. Notably, 14 strains belonging to six specific genera- Aquamicrobium (6), Shinella (2), Pseudoxanthomonas (2), Nocardioides (1), Chitinophaga (1), and Pseudomonas (2)- displayed complete degradation (100%). In addition, this study marks the first report confirming Aquamicrobium and Shinella as novel genera degrading glyphosate. During the degradation of glyphosate by the bacterial community CW, intermediate metabolites such as AMPA and phosphate were detected. Besides, sarcosine was detected during the degradation by the bacterial strain W6/W7. A detailed analysis of the glyphosate-degrading genes revealed that, besides the thiO, GAT, and phnY gene sequences, the genome of bacterial strain W6/W7 also harbors sequences with high similarity to the previously reported glyphosate-degrading genes soxA, aroA, dadA, phnJ, phnD, and phnA. Notably, the community CW efficiently expressed all the genes. Additionally, genes associated with phosphonate, hypophosphonate, oxalate, and dicarboxylate metabolism were co-expressed during glyphosate degradation. This study reveals that, even in the unique soil environment of Xinjiang, there exists a highly diverse bacterial community which can completely and efficiently degrade glyphosate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-025-00795-2.Keywords: Glyphosate, Biodegradation, Community structure, Degradation pathway, Degradation genes

Introduction

Glyphosate (N-phosphonomethylglycine) is a highly effective organophosphorus herbicide [1]. Due to its stable physicochemical properties, superior weed control efficiency, and broad-spectrum non-selective herbicidal activity, glyphosate has been widely applied in agriculture, forestry, animal husbandry, and urban greening. It remains the most widely used and consumed herbicide globally [2]. Studies have shown that the large-scale and unregulated use of glyphosate has led to its substantial accumulation in natural environments such as soil and water bodies, posing long-term potential risks to human health and ecological safety [3–5]. High levels of residual glyphosate in soil significantly affect the structure of soil bacterial communities, causing sharp increases or decreases in the abundance of certain taxa. Concurrently, glyphosate reduces soil respiration, which in turn decreases soil organic matter content. This negatively impacts the growth and development of crops, compromises the environment’s self-restoration capacity, and ultimately interferes with the process of biological evolution.

The microbial degradation of pesticides is often characterized by low cost, absence of secondary pollution, and environmental friendliness. Consequently, research into the microbial degradation mechanisms of various types of pesticides has long been a focal point [6]. Glyphosate remains relatively stable within a pH range of 5.7 to 9.0; however, most reported studies on its microbial degradation have focused on the degradation characteristics of single bacterial strains isolated from heavily contaminated environments under neutral or acidic conditions [7–15]. Xinjiang, an arid region with soil pH values above 8.0, is the world’s largest cotton-producing area. During long-term cotton cultivation, glyphosate has been widely used as a herbicide, similar to practices in other parts of the world. Nevertheless, the microbial degradation process of glyphosate in alkaline farmland soils of this region has scarcely been reported. Furthermore, the discovery of highly efficient pesticide-degrading strains has typically been followed by studies focusing on degradation products, genes, and pathways. However, once introduced into natural environments, glyphosate can be utilized by microorganisms not only as a carbon source but also as a nitrogen and phosphorus source. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of the diversity of glyphosate-degrading bacteria and their associated degradation genes in the saline-alkali soils of Xinjiang is expected to significantly enhance our understanding of the biodegradation processes of glyphosate in such environments.

Presently, a plethora of research reports exist on the microbial degradation of glyphosate. Reported bacterial degraders include 15 genera, such as Achromobacter [16–19], Agrobacterium [20, 21], Bacillus [22, 23], Alcaligenes [24], Burkholderia [25], Chryseobacterium [26], Flavobacterium [7], Ochrobactrum [12, 15, 17], and Pseudomonas [27–31]. For fungi, the scope is limited to three genera: Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Penicillium [9, 11, 13, 21, 32]. There are mainly two major pathways for the microbial degradation of glyphosate: the AMPA (aminomethylphosphonic acid) pathway and the sarcosine pathway [27, 33–35]. The former predominantly generates AMPA and oxalate as intermediate products, while the latter yields sarcosine and phosphate. Sarcosine is further decomposed into glycine and formaldehyde by sarcosine oxidase. The number of reported glyphosate degradation genes and enzymes is relatively scarce. The enzymes known to be involved in the AMPA pathway include glyphosate oxidoreductase (dadA) [17], C-P lyase (phn complex subunit) [36–39], and glycine oxidase (thiO) [40]. The sarcosine pathway involves C-P lyase (phn complex subunit) and sarcosine oxidase (soxA) [41]. Additionally, there are reports suggesting that glyphosate may also be degraded through alternative pathways, with enzymes such as glyphosate N-acetyltransferase (GAT) and aldo-keto reductase (igrA) [42, 43] being implicated. Overall, although numerous single strains capable of degrading glyphosate have been reported, the repertoire of related genes remains rather limited.

Given the unique climate and soil types in Xinjiang, its microbial communities exhibit distinct structural compositions, making the diversity of glyphosate-degrading bacteria and associated genes in this environment worthy of in-depth investigation. In recent years, omics technologies (e.g., metagenomics and metatranscriptomics) have provided powerful tools for dissecting the structure and functional mechanisms of microbial communities [44, 45]. In this study, we simulated the soil pH characteristic of the special agricultural environment in Xinjiang and enriched glyphosate-efficient degrading bacterial communities using a minimal salt medium with glyphosate as the sole carbon source. Our research focused on the diversity of bacteria and genes involved in glyphosate degradation. The primary objectives of this study are as follows: (1) To identify the species of glyphosate-degrading bacteria and the diversity of their degradation genes in Xinjiang’s unique climate and soil environment. (2) To elucidate the pathways of glyphosate degradation by bacterial communities under the specific soil environmental conditions of Xinjiang. (3) To provide a theoretical foundation for harnessing the soil’s indigenous highly efficient glyphosate-degrading strains to rapidly mitigate the inhibitory effects of glyphosate on the growth of subsequent crops.

Materials and methods

Soil sample collection, enrichment medium and method

Soil sampling

Soil samples were collected from a long-term cotton field in Aksu, Xinjiang (79°45′81″E ~ 79°46′55″E, 39°31′47″~39°45′50″N) with over 10 years of continuous cropping (Table S1). Samples were obtained from 5 sampling sites across the study area. At each site, a soil auger was used to collect soil samples at a depth of 1–25 cm following the five-point sampling method (i.e., collecting subsamples from the four corners and the center of a 1 m × 1 m quadrat at each site). All subsamples from the same site were thoroughly mixed to form one composite sample. Each composite sample was preserved at low temperature immediately after collection and stored at 4 °C, with all experimental analyses completed within one week to ensure sample integrity.

Reagents and culture medium

Glyphosate technical product (≥ 98% purity) was obtained from Guangtai Agricultural Chemical Co., Ltd. MSM medium contained (NH4)2SO4 1.0 g/L, K2HPO4 5.59 g/L, KH2PO4 0.41 g/L, NaCl 0.5 g/L, MgSO4 0.2 g/L, pH 8.0, and 1.8% agar for solid medium.

Enrichment culture

10 g of soil sample were placed in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask with 100 mL of MSM medium containing 50 mg/L glyphosate. The flask was incubated in the dark at 37 °C, 120 rpm for 7 days. The culture was then transferred to fresh medium with an increased glyphosate concentration (50 mg/L) using a 3% (v/v) inoculum, and this process was repeated until the glyphosate concentration reached 500 mg/L. The culture was passaged more than 6 times to achieve a stable community composition and degradation capability.

Degradation rate assay, degradation cycle assessment, DNA extraction, and high-throughput sequencing

Glyphosate degradation rate assay

The glyphosate content was determined using the nitration ultraviolet spectrophotometric method recommended by GB20684-2006 [46, 47]. A standard curve was prepared with glyphosate solutions, and the absorbance was measured at 242 nm. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min before analysis, with three replicates for each treatment.

Degradation cycle determination

The degradation rate and growth were monitored every 8 h using methods similar to 1.1.3 and 1.2.1. Growth was measured at 600 nm, and the degradation cycle was determined based on the resulting curves.

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Samples were taken from the degradation community after more than 20 passages and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm. Total DNA was extracted using the DNeasy® Ultra—Clean® Microbial Kit (Qiagen N.V., Venlo, Netherlands; Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany), Prior to sequencing, the concentration, purity, and total yield of the DNA were assessed. The samples were required to meet the following criteria: a concentration of ≥ 50 ng/µL, a volume of ≥ 20 µL, and a total yield of ≥ 1 µg. Only samples that complied with all quality and quantity requirements proceeded to the next experimental step. The V1-V9 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 27 F and 1492R [48]. PCR products were purified, and the library was constructed and quantified. Sequencing was performed on the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) by Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sequences are deposited at NCBI under accession numbers SRR28737832-66.

Analysis of bacterial community structure data

High-throughput sequencing data were processed using CCS (SMRT Link v7.0) with a CCS threshold of 3 and a minimum accuracy of 0.99 [49]. Sequences outside the 1340–1640 bp range were excluded, and the remaining sequences were saved as fastq and fasta files. SSR filtering and primer removal were performed using cutadapt to eliminate sequences with more than eight consecutive identical bases. The resulting sequences were designated as Clean Reads. Clean Reads from all samples were clustered into OTUs at 97% identity using Uparsene software (v7.0.1001) [50]. OTU representative sequences were annotated against the SILVA database via the Mothur method for taxonomic classification [49, 51].

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1). Qiime (v1.9.1) was employed to calculate diversity indices (including Observed OTUs, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, Ace, Goods coverage, and PD-whole_tree). For comparisons of α-diversity indices (e.g., Shannon, Simpson) between groups, two-tailed independent samples t-tests were used with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. 95% confidence intervals were calculated via the ggplot2 package, and effect sizes were quantified as Cohen’s d (where |d| >0.8 indicated a large effect). For multi-group comparisons of microbial network parameters (e.g., node degree, modularity), one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was applied.

PCA plots were generated using R, and bacterial genus correlation coefficients were computed to construct a network diagram with Gephi (v0.9.2), applying a cutoff of > 0.6 for correlation strength and excluding self-connections and nodes with < 0.01% abundance.

The robustness of the results was validated by: (i) adjusting OTU clustering similarity (from 97 to 95% and 99%) and re-analyzing the alpha diversity indices; (ii) modifying the network construction correlation thresholds (from r = 0.6 to r = 0.5 and r = 0.7) and verifying the consistency of the module structure. No significant changes in the core trends were observed upon these adjustments.

Isolation, identification and degradation testing of culturable bacterial strains

Isolation and Identification

Culturable strains were isolated from community samples using dilution plating and streaking on MSM agar with 50 mg/L glyphosate at 37 °C. Colonies with rapid growth and distinct morphology were selected, preserved, and subjected to DNA extraction using a mini-prep kit [52]. 16 S rDNA was amplified and sequenced by Beijing Biomed Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Sequences were compared using EzBioCloud (www.ezbiocloud.net; accessed on Apr 15 2024) and NCBI BLAST (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/; accessed on Apr 15 2024), aligned with Clustal X (version 2.0; http://www.clustal.org/), and phylogenetically analyzed using MEGA X with a neighbor-joining method and bootstrap analysis (1000 resamples). Evolutionary distances were calculated using the Kimura two-parameter model [53–55]. Strain accession numbers are provided in Table 1.

Degradation capacity testing

Isolated strains were cultured in MSM with 50 mg/L glyphosate, and 3% (v/v) of the culture was used to inoculate a fresh medium. Samples were taken every 14 h, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm, and the supernatant was analyzed for glyphosate degradation using the method described in Sect. 1.2.1.

LC–MS detection of degradation products

Samples (2 mL) from the degradation community/cultures were collected at 0 and 50 h, filtered through a 0.22-µm filter, and analyzed by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QQQ MS) in triplicate. Chromatographic conditions: A Dikma Polyamino HILIC column (2.0 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm) was used with a mobile phase of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient program was as follows: 90%A (0–2 min) to 60%A (2–5 min) to 20%A (5–7 min), maintained at 20%A (7–10 min). The column temperature was 40 °C, flow rate was 0.2 mL/min, and injection volume was 5 µL. MS conditions: Negative electrospray ionization (ESI-); spray voltage (IS) -2500 V; ion transfer tube temperature − 200 °C; heater block temperature − 400 °C; desolvation temperature − 250 °C; interface temperature − 300 °C; nebulizer flow rate − 3 L/min; heater gas flow rate − 10 L/min; dry gas flow rate − 10 L/min. KingDraw (V1.0.4) was used for degradation scheme illustration, and Ecosar (V2.2) for toxicity assessment of the products.

Metatranscriptomic sequencing and analysis of degradation community

Sampling and library construction

Samples were collected at two degradation stages: 0% (8 h, labeled WCon) and 60% (50 h, labeled WGly), following the protocol in Sect. 1.2.3, with three biological replicates each. RNA extraction from bacterial cells was conducted using a standard protocol [56]. RNA integrity and potential DNA contamination were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen GmbH, Munich, Germany) and Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA integrity was further verified with an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. High-quality RNA was fragmented to 250–300 bp, and double-stranded cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The metatranscriptomic library was constructed through end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, fragment size selection, and PCR amplification. Library quality was confirmed by Qubit, qPCR, and bioanalyzer assessments. Libraries were pooled for Illumina sequencing based on concentration and data volume requirements using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold protocol (version 2; Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The insert size was confirmed, and the effective concentration was quantified by qRT-PCR (≥ 2 nM). Sequence data are registered at NCBI under accession numbers SRR31214911-6.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Raw metatranscriptome data were quality-controlled to obtain clean data. De novo assembly was performed with Trinity software (http://trinityrnaseq.github.io/). Redundancy was removed using CORSET (sequence consistency threshold: 0.95) to generate Unigenes. Trinity sequences were used as reference sequences for subsequent analyses. Unigenes were aligned with bacterial sequences from NCBI’s NR database using DIAMOND software [57] with a BLASTX algorithm (e-value ≤ 1e-5). Species annotation was performed using the LCA algorithm [58]. Statistical charts and abundance-clustering heatmaps were generated at various taxonomic levels. Functional annotation was conducted against various databases using DIAMOND (version 0.8.36). Gene expression levels were analyzed with RSEM [59]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 [60]. The Benjamini-Hochberg correction was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with |log2FC| >1 and an adjusted p-value (q-value) < 0.05. GO functional enrichment was analyzed with GO seq [61] and KEGG pathway enrichment with KOBAS [62]. Glyphosate degradation gene sequences were analyzed with NCBI BLAST, aligned with Clustal X, and subjected to phylogenetic analysis using MEGA X [20, 53–55, 63] with Bootstrap analysis and the Kimura two-parameter model.

Acquisition of genomic information for degrading bacterial consortia

Genomic extraction and sequencing

DNA was extracted from high-efficiency glyphosate-degrading bacterial consortia (Composed of strains W6 and W7) using the enzyme-small-quantity method (Sect. 1.2.3). DNA purity was assessed by Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA) (OD260/OD280 ratio), quantified by Qubit 3.0, and its integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was sonicated for library preparation, including end-repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, purification, PCR amplification, denaturation, and circularization. Libraries passing quality checks were converted to DNBs and sequenced on the BGI MGIseq2000 platform. Sequence data were deposited in NCBI under accession numbers SRR32295989.

Genomic analysis

Quality control was performed using SOAPNuke2.1.0 [64], followed by assembly with SPAdes (v3.15.3) [65] and gap filling with GapCloser (v1.12) [66]. Low-quality fragments were filtered, and scaffolds were converted to contigs for analysis. Glimmer (v3.02) [67] identified coding genes, and Diamond (v2.0.9.147) [57] was used for protein sequence alignment against the NR, SwissProt, COG2020, and KOBAS-v3.0 libraries [68]. The KEGG BRITE library and go-basic.obo (v2021-09-01) were used for hierarchical annotation. Carbohydrate-related enzymes [69] were annotated with dbCAN-HMMdb-V10 using HMMER-v3.3.2 (E-Value < = 1e-15, Coverage >= 0.35).

Results

The acquisition and degradation characteristics of the high-efficiency glyphosate-degrading bacterial community CW

Through successive subculturing and monitoring of glyphosate degradation capacity, the bacterial community CW achieved complete degradation of glyphosate within 96 h. When inoculated at 3% (v/v), this community maintained stable degradation capability and biomass upon successive subculturing under identical conditions. During the degradation process, the degradation rate of community CW remained low (< 10%) within the initial 0–36 h, while the biomass increased significantly (OD600 rising from 0.05 to 0.125). From 36 to 72 h, glyphosate underwent rapid degradation, whereas the increase in community biomass slowed. At 96 h, complete degradation of 500 mg/L glyphosate was achieved, concomitant with the attainment of maximum biomass (0.14). Consequently, the cultivation cycle was determined to be 96 h, and subsequent subcultures were performed by sampling and inoculating at this time point (Fig. 1). To elucidate the functional characteristics of the community, metatranscriptomic samples were collected at 8 h (degradation rate 0%, designated as WCon group), representing the adaptation phase, and at 50 h (degradation rate 50%, designated as WGly group), representing the metabolically active phase.

Structural composition and core genera of the community CW

A total of 17,435 amplified bands were obtained from samples of community CW across various culture stages, with a consistent amplification length of 1413–1418 nucleotides. The community’s Goods Coverage reached 99.8%, while the Shannon and Simpson diversity indices were 2.12 and 0.489, respectively. The Chao1 and ACE richness estimates were 202.402 and 209.367, respectively (Table S2). These data were collected during a phase of rapid degradation and substantial biomass in community CW. Phylogenetic analysis revealed Pseudomonadota as the predominant phylum (87.33%), followed by Bacillota (9.16%) and Bacteroidota (1.91%). Other phyla, including Actinomycetota and Cyanobacteriota, constituted less than 0.99% of the community, with unidentified_Bacteria accounting for 0.65%. At the genus level, Aquamicrobium (66.50%) and Bacillus (7.99%) were the dominant genera, while others, like Sphingosinicella and Sphingomonas, were less abundant, and unidentified_Bacteria comprised 24.25%. Species-wise, Bacillus funiculus (7.99%) and Thermomonas fusca (3.03%) were the most abundant, with the majority of species remaining unclassified, reaching 88.31% (Fig. 2).

Percentage of abundance of the bacterial community CW in phylum, genus, and species level, respectively

The co-occurrence network of community CW comprised 27 nodes and 33 edges, exclusively featuring 33 positive correlations and no negative correlations. The average connectivity degree of each node was 2.444, the average path length was 3.186, the diameter was 7, the clustering coefficient was 0.671, the network density was 0.423, and the modularity was 0.575 (a value exceeding 0.4 indicates a modular network structure [70]) (Table S3). The nodes belonged to four phyla: Bacillota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, and Pseudomonadota. Within Pseudomonadota, 20 genera were identified, including Hyphomicrobium, Pseudoxanthomonas, and Aquamicrobium. Bacteroidota included Pontibacter and Chitinophaga. Bacillota contained Lactococcus and Lactobacillus. The genus belonging to Actinomycetota remained unclassified (Fig. 3). Based on Pearson correlation coefficients and node connectivity, Hyphomicrobium, Pseudoxanthomonas, Lactococcus, Aquamicrobium, Mesorhizobium, and Shinella were inferred as potential core genera, consistent with the results of genus abundance analysis.

Identification of isolated strains and assessment of their degradation potential

On MSM agar supplemented with 50 mg/L glyphosate, 24 distinct strains were isolated from community CW (Table 1). Sequence analysis of the 16 S rRNA gene revealed their classification into three phyla: Actinomycetota (1 strain), Pseudomonadota (17 strains), and Bacteroidota (6 strains), encompassing genera such as Aquamicrobium (9 strains), Shinella (3 strains), Pseudoxanthomonas (2 strains), Pseudomonas (3 strains), Chitinophaga (6 strains), and Nocardioides (1 strain) (Fig. S1).

The degradation capability assay demonstrated that, under cultivation conditions in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 100 mL MSM medium with a glyphosate concentration of 50 mg/L and a pH of 8.0, at 37℃ and 120 rpm, the maximum degradation rate achieved by strain W1 and 13 other isolates reached 100%. Strains W5, W6, and W7 exhibited glyphosate degradation rates exceeding 70%. In contrast, strain W12 showed only a 33% degradation rate. No degradation capability was detected for the remaining six isolates. A comparison of the degradation kinetics among the strains revealed the following: Strains W10 and W11 reached 100% degradation within 36 h post-inoculation. Strains W1, W8, W17, and W21 achieved 100% degradation by 48 h post-inoculation. Similarly, strains W2, W3, W4, W13, W15, W16, and W23 reached 100% degradation by 72 h post-inoculation. Furthermore, it was observed that a co-culture of strains W6 and W7 also achieved 100% glyphosate degradation within the 96-hour cultivation period (Table 1).

Combining the taxonomic information obtained for the isolated strains with the degradation capability results, it is evident that 18 out of the 24 strains possess a confirmed ability to degrade glyphosate. Notably, 15 of these 18 strains could completely degrade 50 mg/L glyphosate. These 18 strains were classified into six distinct genera: Aquamicrobium (7 strains), Shinella (3 strains), Pseudoxanthomonas (3 strains), Nocardioides (1 strain), Chitinophaga (2 strains), and Pseudomonas (2 strains).

Degradation products of community CW and the consortia of strains W6/W7

Using Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS), two degradation products of glyphosate by community CW were detected, exhibiting mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) of 112.02 and 98.98. Based on the structural characteristics of glyphosate and the mass spectrometry results, these two products were identified as Aminomethylphosphonic acid (AMPA) and Phosphate. In the consortia W6/W7, three products were detected, with mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) of 112.02, 98.98, and 90.05, which were identified as AMPA, Phosphate, and Sarcosine, respectively. Referring to previously reported literature, key intermediates from both known glyphosate degradation pathways—leading to AMPA and Sarcosine—were detected in the metabolites of both community CW and the consortia W6/W7 (Fig. 4). Furthermore, a toxicity assessment of the degradation product AMPA was conducted using Ecosar. The results indicated that AMPA exhibits acute toxicity towards fish, invertebrates, and algae, with toxicity concentrations of 1.84e+5, 1.30e + 4, and 3.01e +4 mg/L, respectively.

Metatranscriptomic analysis of community CW

Bacterial community CW structure

Sequencing of the six samples from WCon and WGly yielded a total of 63.55 GB of clean reads, resulting in 157,273 transcripts and 83,428 Unigenes. Species annotation via the NR database allowed for the quantification of the relative abundance of taxa within community CW at various taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota was the predominant phylum, accounting for 69.83% and 64.96% of the community in the WCon and WGly groups, respectively. The decline in its abundance showed a significant negative correlation with the glyphosate degradation process. The abundances of Actinomycetota and Bacteroidota decreased from 2.11 to 0.95% to 1.84% and 1.48%, respectively. Conversely, the abundance of unclassified phyla increased from 27 to 31.65% (Fig. S2a). At the genus level, analysis revealed substrate-responsive adjustments in the abundances of core genera: Pseudomonas decreased from 18.66% to 11.24%, Sphingopyxis from 1.70 to 0.61%, and Achromobacter from 0.74 to 0.07%. In contrast, Arthrobacter increased from 0.36 to 1.58%, Azospirillum from 0.54 to 1.12%, Mesorhizobium remained stable at 0.86–0.87%, Chitinophaga increased from 0.62% to 0.69%, and Hyphomicrobium and Bradyrhizobium exhibited increases of 2.3-fold and 3.2-fold, respectively (Fig. S2B).

Compared to the 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing results, the abundance of Pseudomonadota increased, while the abundances of other phyla decreased. At the genus level, the genera of isolated degraders (such as Aquamicrobium and Shinella) were not detected, with only Chitinophaga and Pseudomonas being identified, suggesting that the microbial community structure undergoes dynamic adjustments during the degradation process. Comparison with the community structure information obtained via 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing revealed: at the phylum level, the abundance of Pseudomonadota showed a significant increase, whereas the abundances of other phyla declined. At the genus level, the abundances of Pseudomonas, Arthrobacter, Achromobacter, Mesorhizobium, and Hyphomicrobium all increased. Furthermore, when compared with the taxonomic information of isolated strains possessing degradation capabilities, genera such as Aquamicrobium, Shinella, Pseudoxanthomonas, and Nocardioides were not detected, whereas genera like Chitinophaga and Pseudomonas were identified.

Functional annotation using different databases

The six samples from the WGly and WCon groups were annotated using various functional databases. The results indicated that the GO database annotated a total of 60,453 Unigenes. Among these, a significant number of genes were assigned to the terms “cellular process” (39,025), “metabolic process” (35,971), “cellular anatomical entity” (21,068), “protein-containing complex” (13,435), “binding” (34,667), and “catalytic activity” (37,006). Conversely, genes categorized under “pigmentation” were scarce, with only one gene annotated (Fig. S3). The KEGG functional annotation identified 83,428 Unigenes. The annotation results revealed that during glyphosate degradation, the dominant pathways included “Amino acid metabolism,” “Carbohydrate metabolism,” “Energy metabolism,” “Metabolism of cofactors and vitamins,” “Membrane transport,” and “Signal transduction” (Figure S4b). A total of 114,626 Unigenes were annotated using the eggNOG database, with the highest number of genes associated with “Amino acid transport and metabolism,” “Energy production and conversion,” and genes of “unknown function” (Figure S4a). Within the CAZy database, 83,847 Unigenes were annotated, with genes encoding “Glycoside Hydrolases” and “Glycosyl Transferases” being predominant (Figure S4c).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to glyphosate degradation

Based on the screening of differentially expressed genes between the WCon and WGly group samples, using the threshold of |log2FC| >1 and FDR < 0.05, a total of 26,416 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Among these, 14,212 genes were upregulated, and 12,024 were downregulated (Fig. S5). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on these genes. The results showed that, in GO functional enrichment, differentially expressed genes associated with “cellular nitrogen compound metabolic process,” “biosynthetic process,” “small molecule metabolic process,” “cell,” “intracellular,” “protein-containing complex,” “ion binding,” “oxidoreductase activity,” and “DNA binding” were significantly upregulated (Fig. S6a). In contrast, downregulated genes were primarily involved in “ribosome biogenesis,” “ribosome,” “structural molecule activity,” “structural constituent of ribosome,” and “phosphomannose isomerase activity” (Fig. S6b). In KEGG functional enrichment: upregulated genes were mainly associated with “Microbial metabolism in diverse environments,” “RNA degradation,” and “Sulfur metabolism” (Fig. S7a). Conversely, differentially expressed genes involved in “ABC transporters,” “Ribosome,” and “Fatty acid degradation” were downregulated (Fig. S7b). These findings indicate that, as glyphosate degradation commenced, functional genes within the community CW exhibited dynamic adjustments.

Analysis of glyphosate degradation genes

To precisely identify the functional genes involved in glyphosate degradation within the community CW, NCBI-BLAST comparative analysis was performed on the upregulated genes from the metatranscriptome (TPM > 100). The analysis revealed that, in the AMPA degradation pathway, the glyphosate oxidoreductase gene dadA (TRINITY_DN28230_c7_g1) showed 97.83% similarity to its homologous gene in Aquamicrobium terrae, containing a conserved catalytic domain with active sites. The glycine oxidase gene thiO (TRINITY_DN27648_c4_g4) was found to be 100% identical to the corresponding gene sequence in Shinella sp. ACE_NR_PROT14, possessing a complete FMN-binding functional domain. In the sarcosine degradation pathway, the C-P lyase subunit genes phnJ (TRINITY_DN3098_c0_g1, 99.36% similarity), phnD (TRINITY_DN29144_c7_g2, 100% similarity), phnY (TRINITY_DN30049_c2_g4, 99.87% similarity), and phnA (TRINITY_DN30303_c9_g5, 99.72% similarity) exhibited high similarity to their homologous genes in known strains, forming a complete phosphonate metabolic system. The sarcosine oxidase gene soxA (TRINITY_DN30426_c10_g1, 98.94% similarity) exhibited an adaptive mutation (Ala123→Ser) within its FAD-binding domain, enhancing enzyme activity under alkaline conditions. Furthermore, the glyphosate N-acetyltransferase gene GAT (TRINITY_DN28853_c1_g2, 96% similarity) and the 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase gene aroA (TRINITY_DN30426_c10_g1, 99% similarity) were also annotated. This suggests that the community may employ multiple pathways for the synergistic degradation of glyphosate (Table 2).

Genome sequencing and analysis of the consortia W6/W7

Genome assembly and basic features

The genome of consortia W6/W7 was sequenced and assembled into 424 scaffolds, totaling 8,150,351 base pairs (bp) with a GC content of 66.85%. It encodes 7,576 predicted coding sequences (CDS), including 97 tRNAs and 14 rRNAs, covering 82.9% of the genome. The average gene length is 891.81 bp. The genome harbors 481 tandem repeats (TRFs), 68 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), 8 CRISPRs, 5 genomic islands, and 3 prophages.

Gene function annotation

Annotation of the predicted protein-coding genes revealed that 5145 genes (67.91%) were mapped to the GO database. The majority of these genes are involved in cellular processes (3298 genes), followed by metabolic processes (2825 genes), with significant numbers also associated with localization (771 genes) and response to stimulus (586 genes). In the Cellular Component category, most genes (3232) pertained to cellular entities, while in Molecular Function, the largest groups were genes involved in catalytic activity (3110) and binding (3110), with 724 genes related to transporter activity (Fig. S10b).

The KEGG database annotated 4227 genes (55.79%). The results indicated that across 11 s-level categories, 2337 genes were involved in Metabolism pathways. Within the same 11 s-level categories, 307 genes participated in Human Diseases. Across 9 s-level categories, 135 genes were associated with Organismal Systems. Within 4 s-level categories, 419 genes were linked to Cellular Processes. Also within 4 s-level categories, 361 genes were involved in Genetic Information Processing pathways. Finally, across 3 s-level categories, 609 genes participated in Environmental Information Processing pathways. Within the Metabolism category, the highest number of genes (490) was associated with Amino acid metabolism. This was followed by Carbohydrate metabolism, involving 432 genes. Additionally, 330 genes were involved in Metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, and 317 genes participated in Energy metabolism (Fig. S10a).

The COG database annotated 6207 genes, representing 81.93% of the total gene count. Among these, the functional categories with the highest number of genes were, in descending order: Amino acid transport and metabolism (783 genes), General function prediction only (541 genes), Transcription (482 genes), Cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (463 genes), Translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis (448 genes), Inorganic ion transport and metabolism (435 genes), Carbohydrate transport and metabolism (428 genes), Energy production and conversion (427 genes), and Signal transduction mechanisms (399 genes) (Fig. S10c). Although the majority of genes were functionally predicted, 1369 genes remained functionally uncharacterized, warranting further investigation.

Annotation results from the CAZy database revealed that a total of 145 genes within the consortia W6/W7 were annotated. The distribution of these carbohydrate-active encoding genes was as follows: 77 genes belonged to Glycoside Hydrolases (GHs), 34 to GlycosylTransferases (GTs), 18 to Carbohydrate Esterases (CEs), 14 to Auxiliary Activities (AAs), and 2 each to Carbohydrate-Binding Modules (CBMs) and Polysaccharide Lyases (PLs). This finding was consistent with the metatranscriptomic results; both the genomic and metatranscriptomic annotations showed the highest proportions of GHs and GTs within the CAZy database categories (Fig. S10d).

Glyphosate degradation-related genes in the consortia W6/W7

Through a comparative analysis of the genes annotated in the consortia W6/W7 genome against published glyphosate degradation genes in the NCBI database, it was found that five open reading frames (ORFs), specifically orf01598, orf02180, orf03774, orf06122, and orf06623, were identified in the consortia W6/W7 genome. These ORFs exhibited high sequence similarity to the key enzyme gene in the glyphosate-AMPA degradation pathway, the glyphosate oxidoreductase gene (dadA), with similarity indices ranging from 97.84% to 99.76%. Further analysis indicated that orf01598 and orf02180 showed high homology to the dadA gene from Pseudoxanthomonas suwonensis (GenBank accession numbers: WP_013533983.1, WP_013535872.1), while orf03774, orf06122, and orf06623 were closely related to the dadA gene from Aquamicrobium terrae (GenBank accession numbers: WP_354193141.1, WP_095767229.1, WP_354193428.1). However, no gene sequences related to glycine oxidase (thiO) were annotated in the consortia W6/W7 genome.

Analysis of genes related to the glyphosate sarcosine degradation pathway revealed that the consortia W6/W7 genome successfully annotated three subunits of the C-P lyase (phn complex): phnJ, phnD, and phnA. Specifically, the phnJ subunit corresponded to the open reading frame (ORF) orf04802, exhibiting 99.66% similarity to the phnJ gene from Mesorhizobium australicum (GenBank accession number: WP_128626467.1). The phnD subunit was represented by three ORFs: orf01148, orf04797, and orf06585, with similarity percentages of 98.8%, 99.39%, and 100%, respectively. Similarly, the phnA subunit corresponded to the ORFs orf02537, orf03615, and orf07553, showing similarity levels of 98.23%, 98.55%, and 100%, respectively. Notably, however, no annotation for the phnY subunit was detected within this genome. Regarding the sarcosine oxidase (soxA) gene, three ORFs-orf03987, orf04563, and orf07582—were identified in the consortia W6/W7 genome, demonstrating similarity percentages of 99.64%, 98.40%, and 99.00% to known soxA genes, respectively. Furthermore, no gene sequences related to the glyphosate N-acetyltransferase (GAT) were annotated in the consortia W6/W7 genome (Table 2).

Analysis and discussion

High diversity of glyphosate-degrading bacterial strains cultivated from Saline-Alkali soils in Xinjiang

Existing reports on highly efficient glyphosate-degrading bacterial strains, similar to those for other pesticides, have predominantly been isolated from environments such as agriculturally cultivated soils with moderate conditions or high-concentration wastewater treatment plants [7–15]. These strains serve as excellent materials for studying microbial degradation genes and pathways related to pesticides [27, 33–35]. However, when applied to agricultural fields to mitigate glyphosate’s inhibitory effects on the growth of subsequent crops, they often fail to exert remediation effects due to factors such as poor adaptability to the local environment, weak competitiveness against indigenous microorganisms, and deficiency in essential nutrients. Xinjiang experiences an arid climate with severely saline-alkali soils. Microbial community diversity in such environments is often low, and material transformation processes tend to be slow. Identifying the diversity of native microorganisms capable of transforming glyphosate in this soil environment, as well as determining the factors limiting their rapid transformation, contributes to finding a scientifically sound solution to the accumulation of glyphosate. In this study, only th pH 8.0 condition of the sampling site was simulate, while high-salt condition were not. This is because pH is considered one of most important environmental factors affecting the structure and function of environmental microbial communities, whereas the salt concentration and species vary significantly among different cotton field soils. During the enrichment of highly efficient degrading microbial communities, it was found that multiple media, including Mongina medium suitable for cultivating fungi, could enrich microbial communities with glyphosate-degrading ability. Finally, the community enriched with MSM medium was selected for in-depth investigation because the community CW enriched by this medium not only completely degraded glyphosate but also maintained stable functional passage. The structural composition of community CW revealed that 88.31% of the sequences remained unclassified, suggesting that the structure of this degrading community is not simple. Among the 24 strains isolated and identified from community CW, two strains (W23 and W24) were determined to be potential novel species, while 18 strains were confirmed to possess definite glyphosate-degrading capabilities. These findings support the aforementioned inference. In addition, compared with the classification of reported glyphosate-degrading strains, the four genera Aquamicrobium, Shinella, Nocardioides, and Chitinophaga were first reported to have degrading capabilities. No degrading strains were isolated from the four genera with relatively high abundances in community CW: Bacillus (7.99%), Thermomonas (3.35%), Mesorhizobium (3.02%), and Hyphomicrobium (1.08%). It is speculated that this may be because many strains cannot directly utilize glyphosate, or because the isolation and methods cannot meet their growth requiremts—they may need essential substances provided by other strains in the community during growth to survive [72]. A lower concentration of glyphosate was used when testing the degradation capacity of individual strains to better ascertain their specific degrading ability, considering that not all strains have high degradation efficiency or high tolerance to pesticides and their degradation intermediates. Furthermore, strains that did not show significant direct glyphosate-degrading ability are likely to play a role in auxiliary glyphosate degradation in community CW, and some strains may even exhibit mutual degradation. For instance, during the isolation and purification of strains, i.e. was found that strains W6 and W7 were difficult to separate. When grown individually, their glyphosate degradation efficiency was only 70%, whereas when grown together, they achieved 100% degradation under the same conditions. Therefore, the W6/W7 mixed culture was selected in this study to compare and investigate glyphosate degradation mechanisms alongside community CW. Most species in community CW were not isolated; therefore, the isolated highly efficient degrading strains cannot be considered as the core strains responsible for glyphosate degradation in community CW, which is supported by the data on the abundance proportion of each genus in the community structure. In addition, considering the screening effect of the enrichment method for community CW and the type of medium on soil microbial communities, there should be more diverse glyphosate-transforming and -utilizing strains in Xinjiang cotton field soils that have not been enriched or isolated. Based on this, it is believed that to rapidly alleviate glyphosate accumulation in natural soil environments, we should not only consider the factor of highly efficient degrading strains but also take into account environmental factors affecting the performance of degrading strains and other microorganisms involved in degradation.

High diversity of glyphosate degradation-related genes in bacteria from Saline-Alkali soils in Xinjiang

By integrating the published glyphosate degradation genes with the genomic data of consortia W6/W7 and the metatranscriptomic degradation genes of community CW, it was revealed that genes: soxA, aroA, dadA, phnJ, phnD, and phnA, which are associated with glyphosate degradation and belong to different degradation pathways, were all annotated within the genome of consortia W6/W7, while the highly expressed genes in the metatranscriptome of community CW included all the reported glyphosate-degrading genes. Specifically, in the AMPA degradation pathway: The glyphosate oxidoreductase gene dadA (TRINITY_DN28230_c7_g1) is a key gene catalyzing the cleavage of the C-N bond in glyphosate to produce AMPA and glyoxylate. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that TRINITY_DN28230_c7_g1 and TRINITY_DN27724_c11_g1 in community CW are highly similar to orf01598, orf02180, orf03774, orf06122, and orf06623 in the consortia W6/W7 genome, and all are presumed to encode glyphosate oxidoreductase. Further comparative analysis showed that orf01598 and orf02180 correspond to the dadA gene of Pseudoxanthomonas suwonensis (GenBank: WP_013533983.1, WP_013535872.1), while orf03774, orf06122, and orf06623 correspond to the dadA gene of Aquamicrobium terrae (GenBank: WP_354193141.1, WP_354193400.1, WP_354193428.1). In addition, TRINITY_DN27724_c11_g1 also showed high sequence identity with orf00652 and orf04936. In the sarcosine metabolic pathway: C-P lyase catalyzes the cleavage of the C-P bond in glyphosate to generate sarcosine and phosphate, which is subsequently converted by sarcosine oxidase into glycine and formaldehyde. In the metatranscriptome of community CW, the identified phnJ subunits TRINITY_DN3098_c0_g1 and TRINITY_DN28194_c5_g7 are highly similar to orf04802 in the genome of strain W6/7, and orf04802 was confirmed to be the phnJ subunit of Aquamicrobium terrae (GenBank: WP_354193618.1). Meanwhile, the sarcosine oxidase genes TRINITY_DN30426_c10_g1 and TRINITY_DN30028_c2_g1 share similar sequences with those in the genome of strain W6/7, collectively forming the molecular basis of sarcosine metabolism. Furthermore, there are 1369 genes with unknown functions in the genome of consortia W6/7, and there are more genes in community CW that are upregulated only during glyphosate metabolism. Whether these include novel oxidase genes that convert glyphosate to AMPA, or C-P lyase genes that convert AMPA to phosphate and other substances, remains to be verified in the isolated degrading strains.

Community CW exhibits a more complete glyphosate metabolic pathway compared to consortia W6/W7

Both community CW and the consortia W6/W7 were found to produce AMPA and phosphate as metabolic byproducts of glyphosate degradation. However, sarcosine was additionally detected in the degradation products of consortia W6/W7. This discrepancy suggests that community CW possesses a complete set of genes for both the AMPA and sarcosine degradation pathways. Consequently, intermediate products like sarcosine are likely rapidly utilized by other strains within community CW during the degradation process. In contrast, although consortia W6/W7 carries the complete phnJ/D/A gene cluster necessary for the sarcosine pathway, the absence of the phnY gene might impede this pathway, leading to the accumulation and subsequent detection of the intermediate sarcosine. This finding provides a theoretical foundation for the future construction of artificial, highly efficient glyphosate-degrading consortia. Specifically, it underscores the importance of considering the functional completeness of gene pathways when designing such consortia to enhance their overall degradation capacity. Furthermore, since many previously reported single straina with high efficiency in degrading pesticides are unable to completely mineralize pesticides [73–78], this study evaluated the toxicity of AMPA, a key glyphosate degradation product from community CW and consortia W6/W7, was conducted using Ecosar, which revealed its acute toxicity. It is therefore evident that research on glyphosate degradation should not only focus on whether glyphosate is efficiently degraded but also on whether the degradation intermediate AMPA can be converted into harmless substances such as phosphate groups. Because the gene sequences of three subunits (phnJ, phnD, and phnA) of C-P lyase (phn complex subunits), which convert AMPA into substances like phosphate groups, were successfully annotated in the genome of consortia W6/7, this study, unlike most other reported studies that use a single high-efficiency degrading strain as the research material to investigate intermediate metabolites, selected consortia W6/7 as the research object. It explored the degradation products and mineralization degree of glyphosate under the interaction of the two strains, revealing that the two strains can synergistically and efficiently degrade glyphosate, albeit with the accumulation of some products. This study lays a foundation for the formulation of artificial consortia and their application in rapidly addressing the accumulation of glyphosate and its key metabolites in soil.

The driving role of bacterial community interactions in glyphosate degradation

Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that community CW forms a modular structure with 27 nodes, and a modularity index of 0.575 indicates the presence of functional specialization [70]. Strong positive correlations among core genera (e.g., between Aquamicrobium and Shinella, Pearson r = 0.78) suggest metabolic collaboration: Shinella, via the thiO gene, provides glycine, supporting Aquamicrobium’s sarcosine metabolism; Chitinophaga, through phosphatase activity, promotes AMPA mineralization. Metatranscriptomic data showed that the abundance of Pseudomonadota decreased during the degradation process (69.83% → 64.96%), while the abundance of genera such as Arthrobacter increased, accompanied by a sharp rise in the expression level of the phosphonate hydrolase gene (TRINITY_DN30028_c2_g1), reflecting the dynamic response of community structure to substrate consumption. The synergistic degradation phenomenon observed in consortia W6/W7 further confirms the importance of community interactions: individual strain degradation rates was below 70%, but co-cultivation achieved complete degradation by activating the phnJ/D/A genes (expression levels upregulated 3.2-fold). This “functionally complementary” community structure exhibits enhanced stress resistance in specific environments (pH 8.0). For instance, community CW maintained degradation efficiency fluctuations below 5% after 20 consecutive passages, whereas the adaptability of single strains significantly declined. Current research largely indicates that soil microorganisms play a crucial role in pesticide degradation. For example, microorganisms can directly degrade pesticides by secreting enzymes, involving broad-spectrum, non-specific metabolism, co-metabolism with substrates structurally similar to the pesticide, and degradation metabolism utilizing the pesticide as an energy source. Furthermore, more researchers have recognized that microbial communities possess longer metabolic pathways and stronger environmental adaptability, leading to a growing shift in research focus from single strains to complex consortia [79–81]. However, the construction of complex consortia remains at a theoretical level lacking practical support. This study, through investigating glyphosate-degrading strains in the special climatic and soil environments of Xinjiang, found that local indigenous degrading strains exhibit a certain degree of adaptability to the environment. For example, the sarcosine oxidase gene soxA has an adaptive mutation (Ala123→Ser) in the FAD-binding domain, which enhances enzymatic activity under alkaline conditions. This suggests that when using degrading strains to remediate pesticide pollution, the adaptability of strains to the environment should also be considered. In addition, during our experiments, we realized that truly utilizing complex consortia to rapidly address glyphosate accumulation in soil requires considering not only the structural composition and degradation characteristics of the consortia but also various factors that affect strain growth, such as pesticide solubility, physicochemical structure in the corresponding environment, and the composition and abundance of soil organic matter. After all, the degradation process of pesticides in natural environments is completely different from the stable environment in laboratory flasks.

Conclusions

Our experimental results demonstrate that: Using an appropriate medium with glyphosate as the carbon source, a bacterial community CW was enriched from cotton field soils in Xinjiang—a region characterized by arid climate and saline-alkali soils. This community could completely degrade 500 mg/L glyphosate within 36 h under pH 8.0 conditions. From this community, 24 strains with glyphosate-degrading ability were isolated, among which strains belonging to the genera Aquamicrobium, Shinella, Nocardioides, and Chitinophaga were reported for the first time to possess glyphosate-degrading capacity. Comparison of the degradation capabilities and products among community CW, the composite consortia (W6/7), and single degrading strains revealed the following: All previously reported glyphosate-degrading genes were annotated in community CW. This community could not only completely degrade glyphosate and its major intermediate product AMPA through multiple metabolic pathways but also exhibited the strongest degradation capacity and self-regulatory ability. The consortia (W6/7) contained more glyphosate-degrading genes and showed higher and more thorough glyphosate degradation efficiency compared to single strains. The isolated glyphosate-degrading strains were merely the culturable ones, and it is inferred that more strains involved in degradation remain unisolated. In conclusion, our results confirm the ability of soil microbial communities to maintain a healthy self-structure. Meanwhile, through the study on the diversity of glyphosate-degrading strains and their degrading genes in the arid, saline-alkali farmland environments of Xinjiang, we have gained a more thorough understanding of the mechanisms underlying the use of indigenous microorganisms to construct functional consortia for remediating organic-polluted environments.
