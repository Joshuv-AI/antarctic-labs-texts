Celtic Invasive Plants database
Authors: Claudia González-Toral, Luz Madrazo-Frías, Aránzazu Estrada Fernández, Ricardo López-Alonso, Mauro Sanna, Candela Cuesta, Eduardo Cires, Juan Viruel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alien Invasive Species (AIS) are among the principal threats to humanity due to their substantial ecological, social, and economic impacts. International efforts to harmonize national AIS checklists and databases is often hindered by fragmented data across multiple platforms. The Celtic Fringe, a biogeographically coherent unit within the European Atlantic Floristic Region, includes part of the Iberian Peninsula, France and the British Isles, and is notable for its extensively documented flora. Here, we present the first unified AIS checklist and georeferenced occurrence database for the entire Celtic Fringe, with occurrences mapped to a 10 × 10 km UTM grid resolution. Occurrence data were aggregated from public datasets, while the harmonized AIS checklist of 271 taxa was developed through the integration of national and international sources. The resulting database comprises 164,974 occurrences, each enriched with taxonomic, floristic, and administrative metadata to facilitate use across multiple geographic and governance levels. This harmonized and standardised resource is designed to support AIS management at local, national, and transnational scales, while facilitating conservation planning and research on invasion dynamics.

Background & Summary

Alien Invasive Species (AIS) are among the most urgent global threats to biodiversity, human well-being, and socio-economic stability due to their extensive ecological, health-related, and economic impacts. The accelerating globalisation of trade, transport, and tourism has facilitated both the unintentional and deliberate introduction of non-native species across ecosystems worldwide. Such introductions can trigger biological invasions, often resulting in irreversible consequences1–3. AIS occur across all major taxonomic groups, with terrestrial invertebrates and vascular plants representing the most abundant and impactful invaders4–6. Their spread and impacts are further amplified by anthropogenic disturbances and climate change, which together intensify their threat at a global scale7. Moreover, global AIS richness continues to rise with no signs of saturation. Notably, over one-third of all first records have been reported since the 1970s8. Recent estimates suggest that even a relatively small subset of AIS imposes a global economic burden of 2,215 billion USD, with some species also causing cascading social consequences and profound environmental alterations1,2,9,10.

The European Atlantic Floristic region, characterized by high rainfall, an oceanic climate, and distinct floristic affinities, includes several subprovinces along western Europe. These extend across parts of the Iberian Peninsula, France, and the British Isles11–17. Within this region, the Celtic Fringe has long been recognized as the climatic and floristic core12,15,18–21. The Celtic Fringe comprises territories from six countries (Portugal, Spain, France, United Kingdom, the Republic of Ireland and Denmark (i.e., Faroe Islands)), and is home to some of the most extensively documented floras in the world13,19,22,23. However, because national checklists often cover multiple floristic regions (e.g., Mediterranean and continental), species recognized as invasive at the national scale may not necessarily be invasive within the Celtic Fringe. This highlights the need for regional harmonization.

In this study, we present the first integrated AIS checklist and spatial database for the Celtic Fringe. We compiled data from national and international checklists and occurrence records, which were standardized and mapped onto a unified 10 × 10 km UTM grid. Each record includes taxonomic, floristic, and administrative metadata, enabling flexible use across geographical and policy-relevant scales. This resource offers a crucial tool for local, national, and transnational management of biological invasions, supports conservation prioritization, and advances our understanding of species distribution patterns under ongoing ecological change. Furthermore, by aligning with frameworks such as the Union List of Invasive Alien Species of Union Concern24–26 and the Environmental Impact Classification for Alien Taxa (EICAT)27, this work contributes to regulatory decision-making at broad scales.

Methods

Area of study: the Celtic Fringe of the European Atlantic Floristic Region

The study focuses on the Celtic Fringe within the European Atlantic Floristic Region, an area characterised by high precipitation and mild temperatures influenced by the Atlantic Ocean11,12,15,18. This region is marked by low seasonality and limited temperature variation, conditions that have fostered distinctive floristic assemblages along the western coast of Europe12–16,28.

Although the number and delimitation of the subprovinces comprising this region remain debated, four subprovinces (Cantabrian Atlantic, Orocantabrian, French Atlantic, and Britannic) are widely accepted as forming the core of the European Atlantic Floristic Region12,14–16,19. The Cantabrian Atlantic subprovince encompasses areas of northern Spain and Portugal as well as Gascogne, Aquitaine, and des Landes in France. The Orocantabrian subprovince includes most of the Cantabrian Range and surrounding areas in northern Spain. The French Atlantic subprovince covers much of northwestern France, including Normandy, Brittany, and the Paris Basin. The Britannic subprovince comprises the British Isles and the Faroe Islands12,14–16,28.

For this study, the outer delimitation of the Celtic Fringe was established by the boundaries of European Environment Agency (EEA)13, Rivas-Martínez, Penas & Díaz14, and Fernández Prieto et al.16. These boundaries encompass territories in Portugal, Spain, France, the United Kingdom, the Republic of Ireland, and Denmark (specifically the Faroe Islands). They were chosen to include transitional zones influenced by Mediterranean, Central European, and Alpino-Caucasian elements16,20,28, thereby capturing the complex bioclimatic gradients of the western Atlantic region. Nevertheless, the boundaries of this subregion remain contentious, particularly regarding southwestern and southeastern transitional areas where mixed climatic conditions (e.g., Temperate and Mediterranean) and shared floristic elements coexist15,16,18,21,29. In this context, we adopted the outer delimitations proposed by European Environment Agency (EEA)13, Rivas-Martínez, Penas & Díaz14 and Fernández Prieto et al.16. By including both French and Iberian boundaries, we account for areas in contact with the Western Mediterranean bioclime and Alpino-Caucasian and Central European Floras, while also integrating insular and mainland continental territories14,16,18,20. This sensu lato approach to the outer boundaries includes transitional high-mountain zones with diverse climatic features28. Overall, this area encompasses territories belonging to six countries (Portugal, Spain, France, the United Kingdom, Ireland and Denmark (i.e. the Faroe Islands)), five of which are currently members of the European Union (EU).

The inner boundaries of the Celtic Fringe delimiting its subprovinces were establishing following Rivas-Martínez, Penas & Díaz14 and the more detailed Cantabrian Atlantic-Orocantabrian boundary of Instituto Geográfico Nacional30.

AIS Checklists sources and preliminary AIS checklist standardization

To generate a unified invasive species checklist for the Celtic Fringe of the European Atlantic Floristic Region, we compiled official national and international checklists from the six constituent countries: Portugal, Spain, France, the United Kingdom, Ireland, and Denmark (i.e., Faroe Islands). Only vascular plant species (Tracheophyta) explicitly labelled as “invasive” were considered. Species considered invasive in other regions, such as Mediterranean or oceanic islands, but not within the Celtic Fringe were excluded.

The official Portuguese invasive species checklist was obtained from the Ministry of Environment and Energetic Transition of the Portuguese Government31. The Spanish checklist was compiled from several publications of the Ministry for the Ecological Transition32–35. For France, the sources included the “Espèces Introduites Envahissantes” of the “Référentiel taxonomique version 18” (Tax Ref v18) from the Inventaire National du Patrimoine Naturel (INPN)36, which compiles the AIS checklists from the different Conservatoire Botanique National as well as updated ministerial orders (arrêtés) from the French Ministry of Ecological Transition (Minitère de la Transition Écologique)37–39. We only considered those Tax Ref v18 species officially listed in “France métropolitaine” (i. e. continental France). Data from the United Kingdom were obtained from the Natural England report40, the Great Britain Non-Native Species Secretariat (NNSS) Species of Special Concern41 and the comprehensive compilation of all the UK checklists provided by the Royal Horticultural Society (RHS)42,43. For Ireland, the checklist was retrieved from the National Biodiversity Data Centre of Ireland and included the updates from the Government44–47. In the case of the Faroe Islands (Denmark), the two legally binding checklists (the Union Concern checklist and the Denmark checklist) were sourced from the Ministeriet for Grøn Trepart24,26,48–52. Finally, the European Union list of invasive alien species of Union Concern and its updates were also incorporated (Union Concern Checklist hereafter)24,26,50–52 was also considered.

All checklists were updated until December 2025.

All taxon names were standardised using the World Checklist of Vascular Plants (WCVP) using the Taxamatch algorithm, as implemented in the Taxonomic Name Resolution Service (TNRS) v 5.3.153–59. This process yielded a preliminary checklist of invasive vascular plants for the Celtic Fringe (AISCelt checklist).

Input data

Georeferenced records of vascular plants identified as invasive in the Celtic Fringe (i. e. belonging to the AISCelt checklist), including the Iberian Peninsula, France, the British Isles, and the Faroe Islands, were compiled from both national and international biodiversity databases. These included the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) (https://www.gbif.org)60–77, iNaturalist filtering observations with research quality degree (https://www.inaturalist.org/)78–130, Tela-Botanica131, and the Inventaire National du Patrimoine Naturel (INPN)36. Occurrence data for Ireland were sourced from various datasets within the National Biodiversity Centre132–138. GBIF data were updated through April 2025.

Updates to these national checklists and data sources will be used for future updates of the dynamic version of this database.

National AIS checklists and species status

Because the Celtic Fringe spans countries that also encompass other floristic regions, such as the Mediterranean Region, the preliminary AISCelt checklist was filtered to exclude species absent from the target subprovinces. After standardizing the occurrence data, we used occurrence records to confirm the presence of each taxon within the Celtic Fringe subprovinces.

Some species are native to one part of the Celtic Fringe but invasive in another. These were categorized as Native and Alien Invasive Species of the Celtic Fringe (NAIS). Identification of NAIS taxa relied on a combination of occurrence records, native range data from Plants of the World Online139, and regional floras and checklists: Flora Iberica140, the Atlas of the vascular flora of the Iberian Peninsula (AFLIBER)22, the Tracheophyta database of France métropolitaine36,131, the Botanical Society of Britain & Ireland and the BIFloraExplorer database23,141, Botany of the Faeröes142–144 and the latest revision of the Plant Communities of the Faroe Islands145. NAISCelt taxa were identified and validated using a combination of the occurrence data, native range data from POWO139, and regional floras and checklists22,23,43,142,144–146. During this validation process contradictory information between local checklists and POWO was resolved by favouring the local checklist sources of native status. When a taxon was absent in both the local checklists and POWO, it was considered as alien due to the absence of records. Finally, when a taxon was absent in both the local checklists and was labelled as “Introduced” in POWO, this was considered as a double confirmation of “Alien” status.

Each taxon occurrence was assigned a “native” status when it was within the species’ natural range, or “alien” when found outside its native range and/or listed as invasive in that country. Afterwards, we treated separately each country occurrences dataset to categorize the status of the NAIS taxa in each country. Thus, NAIS occurrences were treated as having “native” status when NAIS taxa were within their natural range and “alien” when the NAIS taxon was not native to the specific country and considered invasive in that country.

Standardizing species occurrences

After assembling the preliminary AISCelt checklist, we generated a standardized georeferenced occurrence database at a 10 × 10 km UTM grid resolution (see Fig. 2). This 10 × 10 km resolution has proven effective in balancing the trade-off between geographic precision of occurrences and the loss of species records, and in mitigating the biases derived from the differing data mobilization policies147,148.Fig. 2Scheme representing the process of building the Celtic Invasive Species database.

Scheme representing the process of building the Celtic Invasive Species database.

Both modern and historical herbarium records (i.e. some GBIF accessions) were considered as input data, discarding only records without associated coordinates. GBIF species records of cultivated individuals were also considered as input data, since the introduction of many plant AIS is closely related to ornamental or landscape management purposes149.

We took a conservative approach to species distribution griding accuracy by retaining records with coordinate uncertainty equal to or below 5,000 meters. Occurrence coordinates were assigned to the corresponding UTM 10 × 10 km grids using the mgrs Python package (version 1.4.3)150. These grids were filtered using the list of UTM 10 × 10 km grids contained within the target area, thus automatically removing records with erroneous coordinates (e.g. found in the ocean).

Taxon names were again standardised to WCVP-accepted names using the TNRS v 5.3.1 implementation of the Taxamatch algorithm53–56,58,59,139. Only occurrences matching the validated AISCelt checklist were retained, and duplicates were removed. This preliminary dataset was used to estimate species richness per grid and assess potential biases towards countries’ centroids or biodiversity institutions through a visual inspection. This process produced the final curated version of both the checklist and the occurrence dataset.

Taxonomic, floristic and administrative grid data

Each taxon record includes its validated author name and genus (WCVP)56,58,59, family name (POWO backbone) and POWO taxon ID and URL139. Their superior taxonomic levels were obtained following the backbone of The Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) IV151. Since our target taxonomic group is vascular plants (tracheophyta), we used this backbone to generate (1) a major phylum taxonomic category (‘Phylum’) which specifies whether the taxa belongs to Spermatophyta or Pteridophyta and (2) a major subphylum taxonomic category (‘Subphylum’) specifying if the taxa belongs to Angiosperms, Monilophyta or Gymnosperms151. Additionally, we used the APG IV backbone for taxa orders to label the POWO backbone valid family name of each taxa139,151.

Each taxon was annotated with its listed or non-listed status in both national invasive species checklists and the Union Concern list, and with its native or alien status within the Celtic Fringe. In the cases where national invasive species checklists listed genera instead of individual species, for example the Spanish checklist refers to “Azolla spp.”32–35 and the Portuguese to “Acacia spp.”31, all the species belonging to the genera were considered as listed in the national checklists. Taxon alien or native status within the Celtic Fringe and the individual countries was also annotated, and so was their AIS/NAIS denomination. Because the United Kingdom comprises areas in Great Britain and Ireland, the native/alien status of the NAIS occurring in Northern Ireland were treated separately following the same criteria as those of the Republic of Ireland.

The floristic subprovince classification was assigned according to the subprovince boundaries defined by Rivas-Martínez, Penas & Díaz14, Fernández Prieto et al.16 and Instituto Geográfico Nacional30. Each 10 × 10 km UTM grid was annotated accordingly.

Administrative data were extracted for each grid (except for the England smaller administrative units) using the osmdata R package version 0.2.5152, which retrieves information from OpenStreetMap data153. These data included the country (or the associated country of a self-governing territory), the official designation of Crown Dependencies or self-governing territory where applicable (United Kingdom and Denmark), the largest administrative units below the country level (Distrito of Portugal/Comunidad Autónoma/Régions/Provinces of Ireland/England Combined Authorities & England Combined County Authorities & England Councils & Scotland Council Areas & Wales Principal Areas & Northern Ireland Districts/Sýslas) and the second largest administrative unit beneath country or self-governing territory where applicable (Provincia/Département/County of Ireland/ County of England/Kommuna). The English first and second largest administrative units beneath country were obtained using the United Kingdom maps (level 4) provided by geodata R package version 0.6.6154 to generate the extanding Combined Authorities, Combined County Authorities and Councils and the sf R package version 1.0.20155,156 to locate the administrative units of the grids centroids.

Once obtained, the first and second largest administrative units beneath country were visually inspected against the 10 × 10 km UTM grid resolution to detect (1) the units that would be too small to be consistent with this resolution and (2) the grids which comprised a complete unit large enough to be consisting with the resolution, but that were labelled as another unit due to the location of the grid centroid. In the case of units too small, these were merged with one or more other units in the same geographic location and the label was changed to name combining all the names of the merged units linked by “&”. Thus, the smaller units are presented both in the occurrences and mpas as a merged unit. This affected mainly small England counties and Faroe Island Kommunas. This affected small England Boroughs. In the case of the Greater London boroughs, these were grouped using the London sub-regions established by the 2004 London Plan and its amendments157–159. When the units contained in a single grid that were not hit by the centroid of this, we corrected the labels by relocating the grid into this unit.

The presence of natural protected areas within each grid was also recorded. Centroid coordinates were cross-referenced with maps of nationally designated protected areas160, the Natura 2000 protected areas161 and the Pan-European network of protected sites (Emerald Networks)162. The sf R package v. 1.0–18155,156 was used to calculate the distance from each centroid to the nearest protected area. If the distance was 5,000 m or less, the presence of a protected area was recorded. Because this database focuses on vascular plants, polygons representing exclusively marine protected areas were discarded prior to distances estimation.

For each protected area, we recorded the name, national reserve type code, and, where available, additional details from the World Database of Protected Area (WDPA) (https://www.protectedplanet.net/en)163–168. These included the national and English designations of the area, associated WDPA identifiers (WDPA_ID), designation type, IUCN and United Nations Environment World Conservation Monitoring Centre (UNEP_WCMC) categories where applicable169,170, national protected-area type codes, management authority, government type, and ecosystem type.

Since different types of protected areas can overlap partially or entirely, we created three categories to describe protected-area presence within a grid: (1) presence of any protected area (national or Natura 2000), (2) presence of a Natura 2000 site, and (3) presence of a nationally designated protected area. Because partially overlapping Natura 2000 and nationally designated areas often have different names, we generated one column for nationally protected areas and another for Natura 2000 protected sites.

Finally, each taxon was annotated with its listed or non-listed status in both national invasive species checklists and the Union Concern list, as well as with its native or alien status within the Celtic Fringe.

Data Record

All files forming the Celtic Invasive Plants database are available at EU Open Research Repository {Zenodo} (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18630660)171 as open access downloadable files. This database is structured into three folders: Database_and_Checklist, which includes the static versions of the database and checklist, Features and Maps.

The complete static version of the Celtic Invasive Plants database171 is provided in a downloadable tab separated CSV file (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025.csv). Each Celtic Invasive Plants database171 entry corresponds to a species occurrence and includes its 10 × 10 km UTM Grid name and centroid coordinates. The complete Celtic Invasive Plants checklist is provided as a separate file in which each entry corresponds with a different taxon (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025_CHECKLIST_&_VERIFICATION.csv).In both the database and checklist files, each species entry is associated to (1) its APG IV and WCVP-POWO valid taxonomic hierarchical groups (‘Phylum’, ‘Subphylum’, ‘Order’, ‘Family’, ‘Genus’, ‘Species with author’, ‘Taxa), (2) POWO taxon ID and direct URL of the taxon, (3) the Celtic Fringe origin status (‘Native’ vs ‘Alien’), (4) the Celtic Fringe Taxa Category (‘AIS’ vs ‘NAIS’), and (5) the listed status of the taxon in the Union List of Invasive Alien Species of Union Concern and in the local checklists. These two files treat the listed status in the local checklists in different manners: the Celtic Invasive Plants database171 stores this information in a single binary column (Officially_listed) which specifies if the taxa are listed in the area of the occurrence, while the complete Celtic Invasive Plants checklist treats each country official checklist(s) as individual categorical columns (Official_Status_{Country}) specifying the also the name under which the taxa have been listed and native or alien status if the taxon is classified as a NAIS. The Celtic Invasive Plants database171 file includes administrative and floristic information associated to each entry’s 10 × 10 km UTM Grid: (1) the country administrative levels of the grid (Country, Constituent Country or Crown Dependency, Administrative Unit level II and Administrative Unit Level III), (2) the presence and type of the protected areas and their associated metadata if these are present in the 10 × 10 km UTM Grid (e.g. Name of the protected area in English and in the native language, WDPA ID(s), type of majoritarian ecosystem…) and (3) floristic subprovince in which the centroid is located. The complete Celtic Invasive Plants checklist presents the “synonym” column detailing a list of synonyms of the valid WCVP valid taxa name found during the taxonomic standardization. The database and the complete checklist are accompanied by a comprehensive metadata file describing the information stored in each column, the type of variable and the different possible values In addition, the results and the references used during the NAIS validation process are summarized in two files (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025_NAIS_VERIFICATION.csv and Reference_NAIS_TAXA_VALIDATION.txt). In this file each NAIS taxa represents a single entry and their native or alien status reported by each country column (Status_{country}). The information obtained from each country reference is displayed specifying the category or categories used by these sources.

These basic files of the database are complemented by a series of feature files specifying (1) the unique values per column of the whole database (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025_UNIQUE_VALUES.csv), (2) a csv file of the unique values per country (6x) (unique_values{Country}.csv) and (3) a file detailing the all the included features of each Protected Area included in the target area (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025_PROTECTED_AREAS.csv). The modifications generated by the process of relocation or merging of the Admin units II and/or Admin units III information can be verified, as we included a file detailing the initial values for 10 × 10 km UTM grids, the type of modification and the resulting value (CIP_DB_APRIL_2025_Grids_merges_ and_relocations.csv).

A complete map containing the 10 × 10 km UTM grids polygons and the polygons used to label the occurrence’s floristic areas, political administrative units and protected areas has been generated (ALL_MAPS_CIP_2025.gpkg). These files contain 10 layers: Celtic_Fringe, Subprovinces, Countries, UK_Crown_dependencies, Admin_Units_II, Admin_Units_III, Parks_and_Reserves, Grids_Zone_29, Grids_Zone_30 and Grids_Zone_31. The Celtic_Fringe and Subprovinces layers were built by modifying the EEA map of the Biogeograhical regions in Europe13 using our target area outer and inner boundaries. The Countries, UK_Crown_dependencies, Admin_Units_II, Admin_Units_III layers were obtained by merging different polygons of the maps provided by geodata R package version 0.6.6154. The Parks_and_Reserves layer was generated by selecting the nationally designed protected areas160, the Natura 2000 protected areas161 and the Pan-European network of protected sites (Emerald Networks)162 polygons of the protected areas present in the database target area and joining them in a unique layer. We labelled the polygons by adding the binary columns Natura_2000 and National_Nature_Reserve. The Grids_zone_{UTM zone} layers were built by projecting the 10 × 10 km UTM grids using the coordinates of the grids centroids and the utm (version 0.8.1) and geopandas (version 0.8.1) Python packages172,173. We associated the UTM grid name, Celtic Fringe subprovince the political administrative units and protected areas information to each polygon of these layers.

Technical Validation

The quality and reliability of the Celtic Invasive Plants database171 are supported by three core components: (1) the accuracy of taxonomic standardization, (2) the consistency of species distribution data, and (3) the verification of species’ invasive status across different national and international checklists.

We associated the listed status and names of each species across all AIS Checklists used to build the database, allowing alien species not included in any checklist to be readily identified and removed. Valid names were manually reviewed, and the POWO database was consulted to determine the native/alien status of each taxon within the Celtic Fringe and its constituent countries. Where POWO data were unclear, local floras and checklists were consulted. Together, these steps ensured that the three principal aspects affecting database quality were thoroughly cross-checked and validated throughout the process.

Usage Notes

The Celtic Invasive Plants database171 was developed to support a wide range of applications in invasion ecology, conservation planning, and biodiversity policy. The dataset enables the rapid extraction of invasive species lists for specific regions within the Celtic Fringe, whether defined by floristic subprovince, national or subnational administrative units, or designated natural reserves. Such filtered inventories can inform the design of management strategies, optimize early detection and rapid response systems, and prioritize areas for monitoring and control.

The dataset also allows researchers and policymakers to compare the listed status of taxa across national checklists and the EU Union Concern list. This comparison facilitates the identification of invasive species that, although not officially recognized in some countries, may represent significant threats based on their confirmed presence and invasive behavior in neighboring regions with similar ecological conditions.

Beyond regulatory applications, the database supports broader ecological and biogeographic analyses. The inclusion of floristic subprovince classification enables the exploration of spatial variation in invasion patterns across distinct ecological zones. Meanwhile, the harmonized taxonomic backbone ensures compatibility with other regional or global biodiversity datasets, supporting integration into meta-analyses, modelling efforts, and conservation assessments.

To accommodate future research needs, the authors plan to maintain and periodically update the dataset. Users are encouraged to report any errors, new data, or proposed corrections to the corresponding author. Updates will incorporate new occurrence records, taxonomic changes, and revised species statuses as national or international lists are amended.

The grid and taxa data also allow users to generate full AIS inventories focusing on different administrative divisions. For example, the database can be used to compile an inventory of EU Concern taxa (EU_Concern = “YES”) occurring in a particular country, lower administrative units or protected area. Similarly, the database can help identify unlisted species that may pose a local threat (Officially_listed = ‘NO’) or NAISCelt taxa (Celtic_Fringe_Taxa_Category = ‘NAIS’).

Overall, this database provides a foundational tool for tracking, understanding, and mitigating the impacts of alien invasive vascular plants in one of Europe’s most ecologically distinctive regions171.
