Tropical Atlantic and Pacific sea surface temperature gradients modulate the summer Atlantic Niño-winter ENSO connection
Authors: Ji Ma, Shu Gui, Lei Cai, Yali Yang, Ying Min, Yu Lian, Meng Li, Ruowen Yang, Kexu Zhu, Hao Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The relationship between the boreal summer Atlantic Niño and subsequent winter El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) has strengthened in recent decades, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. In this study, we identified tropical Atlantic and Pacific sea surface temperature (SST) gradients as the key drivers of the summer Atlantic Niño-winter ENSO connection. In detail, zonal SST gradients over the tropical Atlantic, driven by wind-evaporation-SST feedback, regulate the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection by modulating the cross-basin Walker circulation between the tropical western Atlantic and eastern Pacific. Under weak zonal SST gradients, the Gill-Matsuno-type response to the Atlantic Niño exhibits stronger ascending motion over the tropical western Atlantic than under strong gradient conditions. The meridional SST gradients over the tropical eastern Pacific enhance anomalous descent that helps to establish the cross-basin Walker circulation. Consequently, the enhanced Pacific Walker circulation contributes to the development of La Niña and strengthens the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection. Model sensitivity experiments confirm this mechanism. This study has identified new drivers of tropical Atlantic-Pacific cross-basin linkages that have significant implications for ENSO prediction.

Introduction

The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the dominant mode of interannual climate variability and has profound effects on global climate.1,2,3,4 Physically, ENSO arises from air-sea interactions over the tropical Pacific that amplify initial sea surface temperature (SST) anomalies via the Bjerknes and thermodynamic feedbacks.5,6,7,8 Analogous air-sea feedbacks over the tropical Atlantic generate the Atlantic counterpart to ENSO, which is termed the Atlantic Niño/Niña.9,10,11 Although ENSO has a dominant effect on the equatorial Atlantic, recent evidence has demonstrated a robust feedback of the Atlantic Niño on ENSO.12 The Atlantic Niño affects ENSO by modulating cross-basin Walker circulation over the eastern Pacific (i.e., the westward pathway) and eastward-propagating Kelvin waves that induce warmer SST anomalies (SSTAs) in the northern Indian Ocean and result in low-level easterly anomalies over the western Pacific (i.e., the eastward pathway).12,13,14,15 The boreal summer Atlantic Niño/Niña affects the development of ENSO in the subsequent winter, which is an important parameter in ENSO predictability.16,17

The nature of the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship is debated. Some studies have suggested that the Atlantic Niño-ENSO correlation could be a statistical artifact because ENSO could trigger an opposite-signed Atlantic Niño in boreal summer during its development.18 Other studies have identified the feedback of the Atlantic Niño on ENSO for different types of Atlantic Niño. For example, the Atlantic Niño can be classified into central and eastern types based on its zonal SST patterns.19 The central type of Atlantic Niño has a stronger effect on ENSO than the eastern type due to the stronger atmospheric response over the tropical Pacific. The Atlantic Niño can also be classified into equatorial and expanded types based on its meridional SST patterns.20 The expanded type of Atlantic Niño involves SST warming over the southern tropical Atlantic that strengthens the local convective heating and enhances remote atmospheric responses over the tropical Pacific.

The Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship has experienced significant multidecadal variations due to long-term changes in the tropical Pacific and Atlantic. For example, greenhouse warming attenuates the tropical Atlantic-Pacific teleconnection by increasing the tropical Pacific mean SST, which suppresses the seasonal migration of the Pacific Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone and weakens oceanic heat transport.21 The Atlantic multidecadal oscillation (AMO) enhances the Atlantic capacitor effect, which is a key pacemaker of the biennial variability of ENSO.22 The positive phase of the Pacific decadal oscillation (PDO) further enhances the effect of tropical southern Atlantic SSTs on ENSO by weakening the Pacific Walker circulation and intensifying air-sea interactions over the central-eastern Pacific.23 Furthermore, the interdecadal modulation of the Atlantic-South America-Pacific mean state has a key role in controlling the effect of the Atlantic Niño on ENSO by modifying both the characteristics of the Atlantic Niño and the associated air-sea feedback processes.24 The air-sea coupling can modulate the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship more effectively than the multidecadal oceanic modes, which exhibit non-significant correlations with the Atlantic Niño.25

The SST gradients intrinsic to ENSO and the Atlantic Niño drive the air-sea coupling that in turn modulates the conditions over the tropical Atlantic and Pacific via the cross-basin Walker circulation.26,27,28 For example, the tropical Pacific cold tongue mode, characterized by intensified zonal SST gradients over the tropical Pacific, weakens the trade winds over the western equatorial Atlantic by modulating the Walker circulation.29,30 Nevertheless, the basin-scale SST contrast between the western and eastern tropical Atlantic or Pacific is too coarse to capture the finer grid-scale variations in SST gradients. The effect of SST gradients on the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship remains poorly understood.

In this context, this study addressed the following two questions: (1) is the multidecadal variation in the relationship between the boreal summer Atlantic Niño and subsequent winter ENSO associated with significant changes in tropical Atlantic-Pacific SST gradients? (2) If so, what is the underlying physical mechanism? This study shows that tropical Atlantic-Pacific SST gradients drive cross-basin Walker circulation that modulates the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The materials and methods section presents the data and methodology. The results section presents the results, including multidecadal shifts in the relationship between the boreal summer Atlantic Niño and subsequent winter ENSO, the link to tropical Atlantic zonal SST gradients, the associated Gill-Matsuno-type response, and the results of numerical experiments (EXPs). The discussion section provides a brief discussion, and the conclusion section presents our conclusions.

Materials and methods

Data

This study used monthly data of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), the Cooperative Institute for Research in Environmental Sciences (CIRES), the Department of Energy (DOE), and 20th Century Reanalysis v.3 (20CRv3).31 These data include the zonal and meridional winds at different pressure levels, the 10-m zonal wind, the 10-m meridional wind, and the surface latent heat flux (LHF). The NOAA 20CRv3 data have a horizontal resolution of 1° and cover the period 1870–2015. The monthly SST data were obtained from the Hadley Centre Global Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature (HadISST) dataset. The HadISST data have a horizontal resolution of 1° and cover the period from 1870 to the present.32 The research period was set to 1885–2009, allowing for 31-year sliding correlations using the HadISST data. The research period also falls within the time range of the NOAA 20CRv3 data.

Methods

The Atlantic Niño index (ATL3) is defined as the SSTAs averaged over (3°S–3°N, 20°W–0°).33,34 The Niño3.4 index is defined as the SSTAs averaged over (5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W).35,36 For the observational analysis, the Niño3.4 index provided by the National Center for Atmospheric Research is used.37 The ATL3 index is derived from the HadISST dataset. This study focuses on the ATL3 index during July-August-September (JAS), which is a key period for the development of the Atlantic Niño teleconnection.38 The Niño3.4 index during the December-January-February (DJF) is used to represent the boreal winter ENSO. The numerical labels (0) and (1) denote the current year and the following year, respectively. For example, the JAS of the current year is marked as JAS (0), and the subsequent DJF is denoted as DJF (1).

To extract the interannual signal, a 9-year high-pass Fourier filter is applied to the data after linear detrending. The two-tailed Student’s t test with the effective degrees of freedom is used to test for significance. The effective sample size is defined as neff=n1+ρXX,1ρYY,11−ρXX,1ρYY,1−1, where n is the sample size and ρXX,1 and ρYY,1 are the lag-1 autocorrelations of the time series.39

Model

This study uses the latest version (v.41) of the simplified parameterizations primitive-equation dynamics (SPEEDY) model to verify the impact of SST gradients on the atmospheric response.40 SPEEDY is a hydrostatic, spectral-transform atmospheric model developed at the Abdus Salam International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Trieste, Italy. The SPEEDY model incorporates streamlined physical parameterizations resembling those of operational weather forecasting systems. The physical parameterizations are designed for processes such as radiation, convection, large-scale condensation, cloud formation, and surface fluxes.41 With the simplified parameterizations, the SPEEDY model captures the large-scale circulation response to SST forcing and essential air-sea coupling processes while avoiding the substantial uncertainty in the complex nonlinear feedbacks of coupled general circulation models. The SPEEDY model has a T30L8 resolution, where T30 denotes the triangular truncation of wavenumber 30, which is approximately 3.75° (longitude) × 3.75° (latitude), and L8 denotes 8 vertical levels in the model’s terrain-following σ coordinate. Post-processing results in the model-level data being interpolated onto eight standard pressure surfaces: 30, 100, 200, 300, 500, 700, 850, and 925 hPa.

In the SST-forced experiments, the SPEEDY model is integrated for 31 years with superimposed SSTAs over the tropical Atlantic and/or tropical Pacific for the period July-October (July-August-September-October [JASO]). The first year of the simulation is treated as the spin-up period. The model results from the last 30 years are used for the analysis.

Results

Link between the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship and SST gradients

The boreal summer Atlantic Niño-subsequent winter ENSO relationship displays significant multidecadal variability (Figure 1). In detail, the 31-year sliding correlation between the ATL3 and Niño3.4 indices is significant at the 95% confidence level during the periods 1894–1925 (P1) and 1984–2009 (P3) but is not significant during the period 1928–1981 (P2). The multidecadal changes in the ATL3-Niño3.4 correlation are associated with distinct ATL3-related SSTAs (Figure S1). During the periods of significant ATL3-Niño3.4 correlations (P1 and P3), positive SSTAs over the tropical Atlantic coexist with negative SSTAs over the tropical Pacific from late summer to autumn. During the period of a non-significant ATL3-Niño3.4 correlation (P2), the SSTA pattern of Atlantic Niño persists from late summer to winter, whereas the tropical Pacific SSTAs remain weak. The results suggest that a persistent Atlantic Niño from late summer to winter does not strengthen the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection. To compare SSTA patterns between the significant and non-significant periods, we combined P1 and P3 for the composite analysis.

The composite pattern of tropical Pacific SSTAs, based on one standard deviation (STD) of the ATL3 index, shows pronounced negative anomalies during P1/P3 (Figure S2). The composite pattern of tropical Atlantic SSTAs is more zonally uniform during P1/P3 than during P2. Consequently, the zonal SST gradients over the tropical Atlantic are not statistically significant during P1/P3 but exhibit a zonal dipolar pattern from late summer through autumn during P2 (Figures 2A–2F). The pronounced negative SSTAs in the tropical Pacific are characterized by a meridional dipolar pattern of the meridional SST gradients, with positive (negative) anomalies north (south) of the equator during P1/P3 (Figures 2G, 2I, and 2K). This meridional dipolar pattern is absent during P2 (Figures 2H, 2J, and 2L). Variations in the large-scale circulation response to the Atlantic Niño

The 10-m horizontal wind reflects changes in the atmospheric circulation during the development of La Niña. When the ATL3-Niño3.4 correlation is statistically significant, anomalous easterlies prevail over the tropical Pacific from late summer through autumn (Figure S3). The pronounced easterly anomalies favor the development of La Niña through the wind-SST feedback. When the ATL3-Niño3.4 correlation is not statistically significant, the 10-m wind anomalies are rather weak.

The multidecadal variation in the tropical Atlantic-Pacific connection is characterized by the anomalous cross-basin Walker circulation from the tropical Atlantic to the tropical Pacific during P1/P3 (Figures 3A, 3C, and 3E). This cross-basin Walker circulation intensifies the low-level easterly wind over the tropical central Pacific, promoting the formation of La Niña. In contrast, the cross-basin Walker circulation is absent during P2 (Figures 3B, 3D, and 3F). Anomalous ascent occurs only over the tropical Atlantic, suggesting a decoupling of the dynamical linkage between the tropical Pacific and tropical Atlantic. As such, the cross-basin Walker circulation strengthens the tropical Atlantic-Pacific connection during P1/P3, leading to a significant Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship.Figure 3Composites of the zonal vertical circulation from the JAS to the SON seasons between positive and negative ATL3 anomaly years(A and B) Composites of zonal vertical circulation (vectors; units of m/s) averaged over 5°S–5°N for the JAS season during (A) P1/P3 and (B) P2.(C–F) Same as (A) and (B) but for the (C and D) ASO and (E and F) SON seasons, respectively. Model results

The tropical Atlantic-Pacific SSTAs act as equatorially symmetrical thermal forcings, which trigger a Gill-Matsuno-type response.42,43 In the tropical Pacific, the SSTAs characterized by pronounced meridional gradients resemble the equatorially symmetric SST pattern.44 The Gill-Matsuno-type response to the equatorially symmetrical cooling drives symmetrical meridional circulations that intensify anomalous descent over the equator. The zonal gradients of heating regulate anomalous ascent over the west of the heat zone through the combined effect of Rossby and Kelvin waves (Figure 4; see the mathematical derivations in the supplemental information). Specifically, the heating without zonal gradients enhances the ascending motion over the western boundaries of the heat zone (Figures 4E and 4F). During P1/P3, the absence of SST gradients enhances clockwise zonal circulation with the ascending branch over the western tropical Atlantic (Figure 5A). The absence of stronger SST gradients during P2 intensify clockwise zonal circulation, leading to a more vigorous ascending branch (Figure 5B). The anomalous ascent in the lower-middle troposphere is linked to upper-level divergence with significant easterly anomalies over the tropical eastern Pacific. The combination of anomalous ascent and easterlies matches the variation in the Gill-Matsuno-type responses to the zonal gradients of the heat source. The difference between Figures 5A and 5B shows a weak cross-basin Walker circulation under a slight decrease in SST gradients (Figure 5C). In contrast, the difference in circulation patterns due to the shift in SST mean values between P1/P3 and P2 is not statistically significant over the tropical Atlantic (Figure 5D). The tropical Pacific SST gradients intensify the anomalous descent from the tropical eastern Pacific to the tropical western Atlantic (Figure 5E). The anomalous descent is associated with clockwise-rotating Walker circulation over the tropical central Pacific. This Walker circulation favors the development of La Niña via the Bjerknes feedback. However, the absence of cross-basin Walker circulation disconnects the Atlantic Niño from La Niña development. The tropical Atlantic SST gradients cause anomalous ascent and upper-level easterlies that converge with the anomalous descent over the equatorial eastern Pacific to form the cross-basin Walker circulation (Figure 5F). The cross-basin Walker circulation couples with the Pacific Walker circulation, promoting La Niña formation and strengthening the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship.

Discussion

This study investigates how tropical Atlantic and Pacific SST gradients modulate the relationship between the boreal summer Atlantic Niño and the subsequent winter ENSO. The zonal SST gradients over the tropical Atlantic have a key role in connecting the Atlantic Niño with ENSO. When the zonal SST gradients are weak, the Gill-Matsuno-type response to the Atlantic Niño reinforces the ascending branch of the cross-basin Walker circulation over the tropical western Atlantic. Concurrently, the tropical Pacific meridional SST gradients strengthen the descending branch of the cross-basin Walker circulation over the tropical eastern Pacific. The enhanced cross-basin Walker circulation further amplifies the Pacific Walker circulation, promoting La Niña development and strengthening the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection. When the zonal SST gradients over the tropical Atlantic are strong, the ascending motion weakens over the tropical western Atlantic, inhibiting the cross-basin Walker circulation and disrupting the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection.

Multidecadal variations in the SST gradients are driven by the joint effect of atmospheric and oceanic processes via air-sea feedbacks.45,46,47 For the tropical Atlantic and Pacific, the dominant air-sea coupled modes are the Atlantic Meridional Mode (AMM) and Pacific Meridional Mode (PMM), respectively.48,49,50,51,52 Both the AMM and PMM are established by the wind-evaporation-SST (WES) feedback, for which the LHF is the thermodynamic driver.

In the tropical Atlantic, the relationship between the LHF and zonal SST gradients is modulated by nonlinear WES feedback processes. The correlation between the STDs of the LHF and zonal SST gradients within (20°S–20°N, 70°W–15°E) exhibits multidecadal changes that coincide with the evolution of the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection (Figure 6). In detail, the LHF-zonal SST gradient correlation is not statistically significant during P1/P3 but is statistically significant during P2. This multidecadal change is driven by the strength of the WES feedback. During P1/P3, the variations in surface wind speed, LHF, and SST are weak and not statistically significant, which dampens the WES feedback (Figure S5). During P2, significant variations in surface wind speed, LHF, and SST over the equatorial eastern Atlantic result in robust WES feedback. The mechanism underpinning the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection also explains the non-stationary effect of ENSO on global climatic variability. For example, the synergistic coupling between the Atlantic Niño and ENSO during P1/P3 amplifies the effect of ENSO on precipitation over North America and South Asia and near-surface air temperatures over North America, East Asia, and Australia in boreal winter (Figure S6). Given that the Atlantic Niño leads ENSO by two seasons, their linkage can be used for seasonal climate predictions.

Conclusion

The multidecadal variation in the Atlantic Niño-ENSO relationship is jointly modulated by the SST gradients in the tropical Atlantic and Pacific. Specifically, the zonal SST gradients in the tropical Atlantic, driven by the WES feedback, link the Atlantic Niño to ENSO. When the zonal SST gradients are weak, the Gill-Matsuno-type response to the Atlantic Niño intensifies anomalous ascent over the tropical western Atlantic. The Pacific SST gradients enhance anomalous descent over the tropical eastern Pacific. Their combined effect strengthens the cross-basin Walker circulation, which further intensifies the Pacific Walker circulation, favoring La Niña development and reinforcing the Atlantic Niño-ENSO connection.
