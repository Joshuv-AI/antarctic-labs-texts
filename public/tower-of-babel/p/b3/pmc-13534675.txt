Future changes in climate suitability, yields, and calorie optimization of four global staple food crops
Authors: Lucia Mumo, Christian L. E. Franzke, Vecchia P. Ravinandrasana, Moses Ojara, June-Yi Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Achieving “zero hunger,” the second sustainable development goal, is challenging due to climate change, weather and climate extremes, and unabated human population growth. Understanding likely changes in spatial crop suitability and yield for maize, rice, wheat, and soybean using the FAO-Ecocrop and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models is imperative for sustainable agri-food systems. Our results reveal a northward shift in climate suitability as the optimal and suitable regions decrease and the unsuitable and marginal areas expand more profoundly in the far future (2061-2100) than in the near future (2021-2060) relative to the 1970-2000 baseline for all crops under all considered emission scenarios. Heterogeneous yield changes are observed in most parts of the globe, though with consistent regional outcomes. Across all emission scenarios, projections for all four staple food crops show spatially consistent declines in climate suitability and yield within major tropical producing zones, contrasted by yield gains in extratropical regions. Currently, rice can potentially be optimized in over 40% of the agricultural land, followed by wheat (30%), maize (25%), and soybeans under the integrated food systems. Future climate is anticipated to heterogeneously alter these land fractions by ~12%, ~8%, and ~13% in maize, rice, and wheat by the end of 21st century, consequently affecting the global food trade and food security. To reduce crop yield uncertainties, climate-smart agricultural interventions, improved agronomic practices, and the optimization of technological advancements are paramount in the global south. Introduction

The environmental footprint of the emerging agri-food systems to feed the increasing human population with diverse diets has raised concerns about appropriate ways to achieve the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals 2030 while also protecting the environment under climate change. The increasing human population will likely lead to an increased food demand by 50% by the mid-21st century1. Concurrently, continuing global warming will likely exceed 1.5°C in the near term (2021-2040), potentially triggering complex concurrent climate extremes, altering precipitation regimes, and growing crop seasons2, consequently exacerbating food insecurity. Unequivocally, elevated carbon dioxide (CO2) levels are projected to deprive zinc micronutrients to 138 million people globally and reduce wheat and rice proteins by 7.8% and 7.6%, respectively, by 2050, causing protein deficiency to 1.5 billion people3. These consequences of climate change are heterogeneous in spatial coverage and magnitude and disproportionately occur in the global south4.

The various impacts of climate change have and will continue to alter the climate suitability of crops, yield, and nutrients by exacerbating biotic (pest and diseases) and abiotic (heat and water) stresses5. For instance, a third of observed global yield variance can be attributed to climate variability6. Additionally, climate extremes have reduced crop yields by 9-10%7, with a significant loss of up to 50% in South Africa8. However, some places have observed yield gains due to increased cropland, improved farm management, and the carbon dioxide fertilization effect9. Nonetheless, the aggravated negative impacts of climate change and extremes outweigh those yield gains, hence the observed yield gaps10–13. Additionally, the elevated CO2 compromises the future of nutritious food as it will alter the grain quality and nutritional composition of the crop3.

Globally, the leading four staple food crops are rice (Oryza sativa L.), wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), maize (Zea mays L.), and soybean (Glycine max L. Merr), and they contribute to two-thirds of the total calorie intake of 50% of the world population and 37% of global protein intake14,15. These crops also contribute significantly to the total global cereal production, i.e., maize contributes 43%, wheat 27.7%, and rice 18.5%15. Additionally, rice has medicinal and cosmetic values and can be converted into a functional food product by 2050, likely increasing its demand by 25%16. The synchronous edge of the “Triple burden of malnutrition,” i.e., inadequate calories (hunger), over-intake of calories (obesity), and micronutrient deficiency (hidden hunger)17, remains challenging in the developing world due to overdependency on cereals and legumes for calories, proteins, and micronutrients15. This calls for optimizing crop total calorie output, and these four staple food crops play a significant role in food nutrition. For instance, maize is rich in calories, and it exhibits a favorable water footprint per kilocalorie as compared to most cereals like rice and wheat18, hence it is viable for environmental sustainability.

Global climate models have played a significant role in understanding future food security. Previous studies using various species distribution modeling consensually depict shrinkage of the optimal/highly suitable regions as the marginal and unsuitable regions expand for some food crops in the future19–21. Heterogeneous yield changes have been observed around the globe, with a high likelihood of this trend persisting in the future12,22. However, a paucity of comprehensive global studies exists in modeling changes in climate suitability, yields, and probable total calorie optimization under the improved parametrization of the current state-of-the-art climate models that participated in the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). Additionally, continuous monitoring of climate change impacts on crop systems is imperative for a food-secure world.

Based on this backdrop, we show possible ways of alleviating global hunger by systematically modeling changes in crop climate suitability, yields, and total calorie optimization of the four global staple food crops under three emissions scenarios in CMIP6 to account for scenario uncertainty. Here, we use the mechanistic FAO-Ecocrop model and the XGBoost model, assuming that crop cultivars, agronomic management practices, technological advancement, and other farm management practices are held constant. However, to account for their changes, the first-differencing method is used on crop yields to remove their trends. This method was used successfully to account for interannual climate variability as it removes the effects of the stationarity assumptions in regression analysis10,23.

Data and methodology

Data

We used downscaled and bias-corrected total monthly rainfall (mm), solar radiation (w/m2), minimum, and maximum temperature (°C) data from thirteen CMIP6 climate models at a resolution of 2.5 arc-min, courtesy of WorldClim. These datasets are bias-corrected using the Delta method that reduces model bias by 50-70%24. The data is freely available at: https://www.worldclim.org/data/cmip6/cmip6_clim2.5m.html. The choice of the 13 models (ACCESS-CM2, CMCC-ESM2, CNRM-CM6-1, CanESM5, FGOALS-g3, GFDL-ESM4, HadGEM3-GC31-LL, INM-CM5-0, MIROC6, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, MRI-ESM2-0, NESM3, and MRI-ESM2-0) was guided by the availability of the models’ data and the same variables for the three emissions scenarios: SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. Two projection periods, 2021-2060, hereafter termed the “Near-future,” and 2061-2100 as the “Far-future,” with 1970 - 2000 as the reference period, are used. Since the worldclim datasets are averaged within a specific period, we obtained the same datasets from the CMIP6 portal at https://esgf-node.ipsl.upmc.fr/search/cmip6-ipsl/ for the development of the XGBoost regression model.

The crop yield data were derived using the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations country yield data and satellite-derived crop-specific vegetation index25. The data spans from 1981 to 2016 and is freely available at https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.909132.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

Quantitatively understanding the future changes in crop yield is a prerequisite for ensuring global food and nutritional security. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), a machine-learning technique that bootstraps decision trees to build non-parametric regression models, was used following33. The XGBoost iteratively combines the weak prediction model output with one strong learner that is used as the final predictive model (Fig. 1). This increases the prediction accuracy, credibility, and robustness of XGBoost over parametric methods. For consistency purposes, we used 34 years (1981-2014) of yield and climate data to train the model, and the data points were bootstrapped to create more data points. This method has been successfully deployed in previous studies to understand the impacts of climate variability on crop yields around the globe12.Fig. 1Diagrammatic depiction of the extreme gradient boosting model sequential processes that reduce errors through regularization of the decision trees.

Calorie optimization

The total crop yield is directly proportional to the number of calories that a certain crop produces. However, the final food energy output depends on the diversified processing method and cooking styles, as biofortification and industrial fortification alter the nutritional components of food, as well as the energy output34. For consistency purposes, we consider calories produced by uncooked food. For instance, 100 grams of maize produces approximately 356 kilocalories (kcal), rice (paddy rice) 280kcal, wheat 334kcal, and soybean 335kcal35. Here, we compared three scenarios to explain the future changes in calorie output per grid. Results

Current and future climate suitability of rainfed maize, wheat, rice, and soybean

Understanding spatial changes in the climate suitability of crops is paramount for optimal land utilization. Maize is one of the most widely cultivated crops around the globe due to its usage versatility, ranging from animal feed and biofuel energy in the United States of America (USA) and China to being a dominant food crop in Latin America and Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)41. Currently, a significant portion of climatically optimal and suitable land for rainfed maize is located in the USA, south Brazil, northwest Argentina, India, east Asia, and SSA (Fig. 2a). Currently, the most suitable land for rainfed rice is located in China, India, the Amazon catchment area, Southeast Asian countries, Central Africa, the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), and the southern part of the USA (Fig. 2b). In future climate scenarios, tremendous shrinkage of optimal, highly suitable, and suitable rainfed rice regions is predicted globally. For instance, suitable paddy rice-growing land reduces by 10.8% (47.2%) under the lowest (highest) emission scenarios, respectively, by the end of the 21st century (Table 3). At the same time, the unsuitable land is projected to expand by 16.3% (23.6%) under SSP1.2-6 (SSP5.8-5), respectively.

Soybeans are one of the drought-resistant crops without much agricultural engineering. Currently, it is potentially highly suitable/optimal over South America, Mexico, the southeastern USA, southeastern to central China, India, and most countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 3c). Future projections show a loss of optimal land for soybean as the highly suitable, suitable, and marginal zones increase in all the scenarios and timespans (Table 3). For instance, climatically suitable soybean growing zones are likely to expand by 71.3% (49.3%) under SSP1.2-6 (SSP5.8-5), respectively, by the end of the 21st century. Presently, rainfed wheat is among the most widely cultivated crops around the world, characterized by climatically highly suitable and optimal zones in India, Argentina, eastern Brazil, western Europe, Ukraine, central USA, east Asia, Australia, and the savanna agroecological zone, East and Southern African countries (Fig. 3d). The grave consequences of climate change are projected to significantly reduce wheat arable land with a marginal increase over the high latitudes, primarily in the far-future and under SSP5-8.5 as compared to other scenarios and timespans (Fig. 3d). For instance, the suitable rainfed wheat growing zones are projected to reduce by 71.3% (49.3%) under SSP1.2-6 (SSP5.8-5), respectively by the end of the 21st century. This emphasizes the need for adoption of climate-smart agricultural practises like the use of ozone-resistant cultivars for wheat43, and the upscale of irrigation, mostly over SSA, where it is underutilized.

Spatial superposition between the observed crop yield and the probable crop climate suitable regions

Unveiling underutilized yet climatically suitable land for crop farming offers valuable insights to farmers in optimizing the potential arable land. In addition, it guides policymakers in tailoring farming practices and adaptation measures for sustainable agricultural production over a given area. Here, we spatially overlay the probable climatically suitable land against the reported global crop yield. Our results show that modelled suitable land for maize is optimized except in some patches over central Africa and northern Australia, and most of the maize yield is produced under rainfed systems over the global south but under irrigation in the global north (Fig. 4a). Paddy rice production is under extensive irrigation schemes in most parts of the globe (Fig. 3b). However, this current flood rice irrigation system is unsustainable in the long run due to the depletion of groundwater, soil, and land degradation45. This highlights the need for adaptive climate-smart agricultural practices and strategic agricultural intensification to meet the projected food demand with little environmental footprint. Rainfed rice systems are also practiced in some parts of the world, such as the Amazon basin, the Yangtze River basin in China, and some parts of central and western Africa (Fig. 4b). This can be linked to the adoption of aerobic rice farming in those regions as it does not require standing water fields46,47. Conclusively, most of the climatically suitable land for rice is optimized except over the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Southeastern USA (Fig. 3b), due to the dominance of corn farming, and over Brazil due to soybeans’ eminence.

There is also a high overlap between climatically suitable areas for soybean in most parts of the globe, except for small patches in Europe, the USA, South America, and northern Australia (Fig. 3c). However, despite extensive climatically suitable land for soybean over Africa between 20°S and 10°N, only a few countries have optimized its farming due to the dominance of subsistence maize production in the region. Notably, optimizing climatically suitable agricultural land for soybean in the DRC has the potential to alleviate poverty, hunger, and malnutrition that dominate the country and Africa at large48. A quantifiable sum of soybean production systems is under irrigation in the global north but under-rainfed systems in the global south (Fig. 3c).

The current climatically suitable demarcated wheat growing zones conform to the observed global wheat yield distribution. Nevertheless, in some places in eastern Brazil, SSA, the USA, northern Australia, and Europe are underutilized for wheat (Fig. 3d). This can be attributed to the dominance of horticulture over those regions49.

This highlights the need for adoption of non-climatic practices (i.e., supplemental irrigation, improved agronomic practices, and utilization of advanced agricultural technology) as it counteracts the impacts of climate change on agriculture by reducing heat and water stress and maintaining soil moisture that consequently increases crop yield, though the impacts are spatially heterogeneous2. For instance, supplemental irrigation can reduce climate-induced total yield losses by 4 times, varying amongst crops in the major global food basket, i.e., those that contribute 40% to the global cereal crops50.

Quantification of climate and crop models’ uncertainties

Climate models inherently have uncertainties stemming from parameterization schemes, future scenarios, dynamical downscaling, and internal variability58,59. These climate models’ uncertainties are transferred to the crop models, which also have their own uncertainties arising from the input parameters (i.e., climate and agronomics) and the Machine Learning (ML) prediction model’s uncertainties arising from hyperparameter tuning, randomness in data selection, and out-of-distribution prediction.

First, we ascertain climate models’ projection uncertainties under SSP585 by first calculating anomalies in each variable relative to the historical baseline, then estimating the uncertainty across models through inter-model standard deviation. Our results show that precipitation is the most uncertain climate variable, with high uncertainty along the tropics (Amazon Basin, Central Africa, and South and South East Asia) (Fig. 6), and low uncertainty over the subtropical dry zones. Input data uncertainties are assessed by perturbing precipitation, solar radiation, maximum and minimum temperature of the 13 models to create plausible climate extremes tails under the 10th (cooler/drier) and above the 90th percentile (hotter/wetter) as the lower and upper plausible range, respectively. We then propagated these plausible climate ranges into the XGBoost framework to assess the sensitivity of projected crop yields to climate input data forcing uncertainty. Our results show non-linear yield-climate response to input data uncertainty, with maize exhibiting the highest sensitivity to climate uncertainty of 36.1%, then rice (26.9%), wheat (14.51%), while soybeans have the weakest climate response with yield uncertainty of 12.3% respectively (Table 5). The global mean maize yield under SSP585 is projected to decrease by 12.2 %, rice decreases by 10.6%, soybeans by 9% and wheat by 19.4%. Under extremely higher temperature and wetter conditions above the 90th percentile, all the crop yield decreases heterogeneously due to stunted growth compounded by heat stress, and increased abiotic stress (Table 5). However, under the low climate stress, the impact is opposite, though with varying uncertainty. The relative yield uncertainty is calculated as (absolute differences between the lower and upper plausible divided by the MME prediction).

Increase in wheat and soybeans yield may be due to precipitation abundance dominating over heat stress, and the benefits of wheat over the extratropical regions. The decrease in maize and rice signifies the likelihood of heat stress overshadowing rainfall gains over the major producing zones and crop dominance areas like maize in SSA. The plausible range of future crop yield outcomes may be wider due to the different types of uncertainties; therefore, these results should be treated as conditional predictions that highlight trends rather than definitive predictions for global food security policies.

Reconciling the FAO-eco crop and XGBoost yield predictive model

The FAO-Ecocrop and XGBoost models use fundamentally different assumptions of mapping biophysical crop climate suitability envelopes and predicting non-linear yield response to climate variability, respectively. As they serve complementary purposes for understanding food security in this study, we reconcile the two methods by assessing how they predict future signs of change in climate suitability and crop yield at the grid level relative to the baseline under SSP585. Regions predicting similar signs of change are classified as zones of “Positive/Negative Agreement”, while regions with contrary signs of change are marked as “No Agreement” as they indicate a divergent outcome of the two modelling approaches. Agreement of the two modelling frameworks increases confidence in the projected crop outcomes, and most likely, the climate factor plays the key role in modulating the yield-climate response.

Our results indicate substantial consistency in major crop growing zones using the two modelling approaches under all the emission scenarios. Conclusion and discussion

Our study systematically analyses changes in climate suitability, rainfed crop yield, and total calorie optimization for the four global staple food crops using downscaled and bias-corrected state-of-the-art climate models from CMIP6 under three climate-change emission scenarios. With the ultimate goal of sustainably curbing food insecurity within planetary boundaries, adopting integrated food calorie-optimized approaches may realistically quantify the available calories for effective food policies. Our results show good agreement between simulated crop climate suitability under the FAO Ecocrop model and observed crop yield, indicating optimal land utilization except over tropical rainforests. However, limited irrigation usage is observed over the global south, explaining the inherent yield gaps. The rainfed crops considered here are projected to lose climatically optimal and suitable areas as the marginal and unsuitable regions enlarge globally more in the far-future (2061 - 2100) than in the near-future (2021 - 2060) relative to the baseline (1970 - 2000).

A substantial heterogeneous spatial change in these crops’ yields is projected by the end of the 21st century, with the tropical areas predicted to experience the highest wheat and rice yield loss, though accompanied by a slight increase in maize and soybean. The extratropical regions anticipate yield gain in all the crops, with pronounced gain in wheat and rice. Spatial consistency between FAO-Eco crop and the XGBoost models’ outputs shows coherent regional crop outcomes in most parts of the globe across all four food crops under the three emission scenarios. The two modelling approaches agree on a decrease in crop production and suitability over the major grain-producing zones due to climate stress, and yield gain over the extra-tropics. This gain can be attributed to favourable crop biophysical temperature thresholds resulting from global warming, as temperature is the main crop-limiting factor in the extra-tropics9. Solar radiation and precipitation are the major drivers of yield uncertainty across the crops. Disagreement areas show regions where climate change may not explain high yield variance, but other non-climate factors play a significant role. Therefore, more stringent agricultural adaptation and mitigation policies are advocated over those regions.

Sensitivity analysis of calorie optimizations shows that optimizing total calories is likely to overestimate the edible calories by 62% (maize), 85% (soybean), 25% (wheat), and 12% in rice. However, soybeans have the highest livestock-feed recovery percentage of 11.25%, with wheat having the least (2.25%). The integrated food pathways show that currently rice has the highest land optimization fraction of 40%, followed by wheat, rice, and soybeans, respectively. Future changes are likely to alter this pattern, with maize optimization emerging highly over the Asian continent under rainfed agriculture systems. While over the tropics, it is imperative to swiftly shift to new proposed optimal calories due to heterogeneous calorie optimization outcomes. Notably, adjusting this staple food calorie optimization is not a mere agronomic decision; it is a socio-economic policy tool with cascading effects. Therefore, the proposed rainfed crop shift pattern should be adopted with caution, as it may pose unexpected changes in global food trade shifts, destabilize local food availability, and increase malnutrition risks. For instance, context-specific adjustments should prioritize diversification, food nutrition, differential regional utilization of crops, nutritional education, social safety nets, cultural and consumer preferences, integrated global food policies, political goodwill in global food supply chains, and climate-smart agricultural practices.

Future studies should incorporate other non-climatic factors in crop modelling, such as soil, farm management practices, irrigation, and carbon dioxide fertilization effects that are limited in the current study. Nevertheless, following this proposed crop pattern adjustment will increase resource use efficiency as there will be increased calories per unit water/energy, increased climate resilience, and enhanced livelihoods and nutrition safeguards. However, since there is no single plausible collective solution to fully shield crop production against the negative impacts of climate change due to their differential impacts, tailored, specific climate-smart agricultural practices for a given area remain a key adaptation and mitigation technique.
