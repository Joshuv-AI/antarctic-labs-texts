Advances, challenges, and future directions in trauma‐related Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable (FAIR) data efforts
Authors: Maya O'Neil, Anke de Haan, Yaara Sadeh, Meghan L Marsac, Nancy Kassam‐Adams, Antonio Morgan‐Lopez, Jessica Hamblen, Kate Clauss, Denise Hien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFindable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) data advances are becoming more common and more important across research fields given the large amount of research data in need of synthesis and application. Many novel methods improve the efficiency and accuracy of data reuse, combination, and synthesis, which is necessary given that there are over 500 published randomized controlled trials of posttraumatic stress disorder treatments in adults; however, these methods are still relatively new to the field of traumatic stress research. We provide a brief overview of relevant FAIR data efforts from other fields and within trauma health care and research; share examples of trauma‐related FAIR data efforts to demonstrate recent advances and challenges; and suggest potential next steps to continue making trauma data more FAIR. These topics were originally presented as part of an International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) Pre‐Meeting Institute organized by the Global Collaboration on Traumatic Stress FAIR Data Workgroup and conducted in conjunction with the 2024 ISTSS 40th Annual Meeting in Boston (MA, USA), titled, “How to Find, Re‐use, and Share Data for Broader Impact: Practical Tools and Tips for FAIR Data.” Our discussion underscores the need for ongoing research and collaboration in trauma data science to enhance the effectiveness of interventions for individuals facing the challenges of traumatic stress and its comorbidities. Advancing FAIR data methods can serve as the groundwork for more efficient, accurate, and evidence‐based health care for the field of traumatic stress research, advancing precision medicine and learning health systems approaches to care.

Recent efforts to make research data Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) are becoming even more important as the rate of research data continues to increase and computing capabilities advance. New methods now allow for the efficient synthesis of larger‐scale and more detailed data. Brain and behavioral health care and research, and, more specifically, trauma‐related health care and research, are no exception. Publications of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) research data have progressively increased in number over the past four decades since PTSD was first included in the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM‐III; American Psychiatric Association, 1980). There are currently over 500 published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating treatments for adults with PTSD (O'Neil et al., 2023).

Efforts to synthesize these vast trauma data are common (e.g., Cipriani et al., 2018; Forman‐Hoffman et al., 2018). There are increasing numbers of meta‐analyses and network meta‐analyses. These reviews often serve as the basis for widely disseminated and used clinical practice guidelines (e.g., American Psychological Association, 2025; Department of Veterans Affairs [VA]/Department of Defense [DoD], 2023). Despite their importance, study‐level evidence synthesis efforts are costly and time‐consuming, and they frequently utilize different methodologies that make it hard to compare reviews. Similar issues exist with efforts to assemble individual participant‐level data (Michelson & Reuter, 2019). It is difficult to access individual participant‐level data. Even when data access is possible, the data often require harmonization across studies from projects that are not necessarily methodologically comparable. FAIR data efforts are essential to improving the field's ability to synthesize data and address important questions, including those related to advancing precision medicine and learning health care systems.

Fortunately, there are numerous examples of recent advances in making trauma data more FAIR. Our goals are (a) to provide a brief overview of FAIR data efforts relevant to trauma health care and research, (b) share examples of trauma‐related FAIR data efforts to demonstrate some of the achievements and challenges, and (c) suggest potential next steps to continue making trauma data more FAIR. These topics were originally presented as part of an International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) Pre‐Meeting Institute organized by the Global Collaboration on Traumatic Stress (GCTS) FAIR Data Workgroup and conducted in conjunction with the 2024 ISTSS 40th Annual Meeting in Boston (MA, United States) entitled “How to Find, Re‐use, and Share Data for Broader Impact: Practical Tools and Tips for FAIR Data” (Kassam‐Adams et al., 2024).

FAIR DATA EFFORTS RELEVANT TO TRAUMA HEALTH CARE AND RESEARCH

The field of trauma health care and research has been able to build on FAIR data efforts in other fields, such as cancer, pharmacogenomics, and molecular genetics (Collins & Varmus, 2015; National Institutes of Health [NIH], 2025; National Research Council, 2011). Similarly, the field has learned important lessons from other broad, often national‐ and international‐level efforts, such as the U.K. Biobank (n.d.), VA Million Veteran Program (n.d.), and the NIH's All of Us Research Program (NIH, n.d.), focused on gathering, curating, and synthesizing large‐scale data into meaningful clinical guidance.

Though there are trauma data are included in some of these efforts, FAIR data advances with a specific focus on traumatic stress and PTSD are still in their relative infancy. Brain and behavioral health data compilation efforts, including the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) Data Archive (NIMH, n.d.), Metapsy.org (Meta‐Analytic Psychotherapy Databases, n.d.), the University of Michigan's Institute for Social Research (n.d.), and the Federal Interagency Traumatic Brain Injury Research Informatics System (FITBIR; n.d.), have served as important models, collaborations, and jumping‐off points for FAIR data work more closely related to traumatic stress and PTSD.

Advances in traumatic stress and PTSD FAIR data have largely occurred over the past decade and are diverse in scope and methods (Kassam‐Adams & Olff, 2020; Sadeh et al., 2023). The GCTS FAIR Data Theme, initiated in 2019, provides an organizing home for researchers interested in advancing FAIR data practices in the traumatic stress field. One of the recent GCTS FAIR data projects was a 2022 survey of FAIR data practices conducted among researchers (Prakash et al., 2023). A total of 222 researchers from 28 countries participated in this online survey, which was made available in seven languages. Researchers reported on their views on data sharing and reuse, their current data practices, and perceived facilitators and barriers to adopting FAIR data principles and practices. Most participants had positive views of data sharing and reuse, endorsing items indicating strong scholarly altruism, ethical considerations that support data sharing, and data reuse as a way to improve research quality and advance the field. Many respondents reported having shared or reused data, but gold‐standard practices, such as formally depositing data in established repositories, were infrequent. Reported barriers for increased adoption of FAIR data practices included time constraints, funding, and a need for more familiarity with the FAIR principles.

The GCTS FAIR Data Workgroup has also produced tipsheets on several key topics to aid researchers in making data more FAIR. Topics include ethics and consent for FAIR data, preparing data for sharing and reuse, deidentifying or anonymizing data for sharing, how and where to share data, and finding existing data resources to help address new research questions. The tipsheets are freely available online (GCTS, 2025).

FAIR DATA PROJECTS: CHALLENGES AND LESSONS LEARNED

The GCTS FAIR Data Theme brings together leaders of a growing number of FAIR trauma data projects to share ideas and methods and increase the visibility of these projects for the field. These projects are at various stages of development and dissemination. All are advancing FAIR trauma data science, and all provide important information for the field in terms of original findings, challenges overcome, methods developed, and remaining gaps to be addressed. Here, we provide four examples of these FAIR trauma data projects and present key challenges faced and lessons learned to inform future directions in FAIR trauma data science.

PTSD Repository

Overview

The PTSD Trials Standardized Data Repository (PTSD Repository; National Center for PTSD [NCPTSD], n.d.) is a comprehensive database of PTSD treatment RCTs in adults that has been developed and disseminated over the past 8 years through a collaboration between the VA's NCPTSD, the Pacific Northwest Evidence‐Based Practice Center, and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). Updated annually, this publicly available database of over 550 studies is located on the NCPTSD's website. Data were extracted following gold‐standard methods used by the AHRQ and include hundreds of variables focused on study characteristics, sample characteristics, PTSD and related outcomes (e.g., standardized effect sizes), and Cochrane's risk‐of‐bias tool (Version 2; RoB 2) for each study. Data are freely available and appropriate for meta‐analyses. A subset of the data on psychotherapy interventions is integrated with Metapsy.org, which offers the ability to run web‐based meta‐analyses.

Achievements

The PTSD Repository website provides tailored information to researchers, clinicians, and the public. The research section focuses on the data itself and methodological resources, such as how the data are coded and organized. The clinician section provides an easy search tool to identify basic information about all treatments of a specific type, as well as “data stories” that summarize information on various topics (e.g., “What types of PTSD treatments have been studied?”). The public section offers a number of simple data visualizations and explanations.

The PTSD Repository has already been used in numerous systematic reviews and meta‐analytic publications (Hamblen et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2024; Raines et al., 2024; Swerdlow et al., 2023), with more projects using the database underway. Additionally, the database has been used to provide evidence for clinical practice guidelines (e.g., American Psychological Association, 2025), and it has provided evidence for other national clinical tools (e.g., the NCPTSD's Consultation Program) and been used to determine loss‐of‐diagnosis ratings for the online PTSD Decision Aid (Hamblen et al., 2022). However, to ensure its relevance and continued use, ongoing support for updates, maintenance, and dissemination will be needed. Finally, because some questions cannot be answered with aggregate data, future updates will ideally be expanded to include the compilation and standardization of individual participant‐level data from PTSD clinical trials in adults.

Challenges

Key successes are related to the collaborative partnerships, including experts in both PTSD and systematic review methods, as well as the robust and sustained funding to establish and expand this resource; notably, sustained funding to ensure maintenance and accessibility is an ongoing challenge. Because the database currently includes only study‐level data (i.e., aggregate data extractable from published studies), there have not been significant challenges related to protected health information, internal review boards, or human subject protections, which are common with raw datasets containing identifiable data. However, study‐level data limit the types and nuances of analyses that can be completed; for example, study‐level data cannot be used to examine what interventions work best for which subgroups unless the results for each subgroup are reported separately, which is uncommon. Additionally, even study‐level data compilation is rife with challenges, including deciding on clear inclusion criteria, determining which variables to extract, and making many other decisions related to the extraction of nuanced and comprehensive data while simultaneously ensuring the data are similar and well‐defined enough to be appropriately combined into meaningful groups across many studies.

Project Harmony virtual clinical trial

Overview

Project Harmony, funded in 2018 by the NIH's National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), conducted an individual participant‐level data meta‐analysis (IPD‐MA; Cooper & Patall, 2009; Stewart & Parmar, 1993) of studies identified by a systematic review of the published and gray literature up to 2019 to compare the effectiveness of behavioral and pharmacological treatments for adults with comorbid PTSD and substance use disorders (SUDs). ​​Studies were included if they utilized RCTs, reported pre‐ and posttreatment PTSD and substance use data, and recruited adult participants with current clinician‐determined diagnoses of PTSD and SUD. Access to raw, individual‐level patient data from 36 of 42 identified international clinical trials was given, such that data from a combined total of 4,046 participants were acquired, harmonized, and analyzed. Using an expert consensus panel, treatments were categorized into trauma‐focused, integrated PTSD and substance use treatments, pharmacotherapies targeting PTSD or substance use, behavioral treatments for substance use, and nonmanualized community‐based treatments (Hien et al., 2022). ​Propensity score weighting to adjust for covariate imbalances (Brincks et al., 2018; Saavedra et al., 2021), integrative data analysis (IDA; Curran et al., 2008; Morgan‐López et al., 2023) using moderated nonlinear factor analytic approaches (Bauer, 2017), and propensity‐weighted multilevel linear mixed‐modeling methods allowed for a robust comparison of comparative treatment effectiveness across a large and diverse sample, providing insights into the most effective treatment strategies for comorbid PTSD and SUD.

Achievements

At its most basic level, Project Harmony successfully integrated raw individual‐patient data from 36 randomized controlled trials, enabling a comprehensive analysis of treatment effectiveness for comorbid PTSD and SUD that represents the largest pooling of data in the traumatic stress and addiction fields (Hien et al., 2023). Project Harmony has also successfully utilized network meta‐analysis to compare the efficacy of various treatments, providing a robust method to synthesize data from multiple published studies and outcomes and enhancing the field's understanding of psychotherapy and pharmacological interventions for individuals with comorbid PTSD, alcohol, and other drug use disorders (Hien et al., 2024). The Project Harmony primary outcome analyses also definitively demonstrated that trauma‐focused therapies combined with pharmacotherapy resulted in significant improvements in both PTSD and alcohol use outcomes. In terms of methodological advances, Project Harmony applied a combination of innovative statistical methods, including integrative data analysis (measure harmonization), propensity score weighting, measurement nonequivalence, and meta‐analysis of individual patient data, ultimately allowing for a more precise estimation of treatment effects across diverse studies.

Challenges

The Project Harmony team encountered several challenges, including variability in the inclusion criteria across studies, which raised questions about how to combine intervention conditions across trials, potentially undercutting random assignment, leading to statistically significant heterogeneity in outcomes (e.g., Hill et al., 2024). All meta‐analyses are constrained by the available trials, and in the context of comorbid PTSD and SUD, many existing studies were noted to be lacking diversity, thereby demanding further replication for external validity. Variation in the measurement of PTSD and a lack of consensus on treatment categorization were additional challenges (Hien et al., 2022; Morgan‐López et al., 2022), as were missing data and inconsistencies across studies, which could affect the reliability of the findings. Finally, in some cases, largely due to institutional review board (IRB)–related restrictions or availability, investigators could only share summary data rather than individual patient data; for these studies, summary information was used to generate synthetic individual‐level data (Reiter, 2005; Reiter et al., 2014), which uses technology similar to multiple imputation.

Child Trauma Data Archives

Overview

The Child Trauma Data Archives (CTDA; 2025) represents a pioneering international collaboration that aggregates and standardizes individual participant‐level data from trauma‐related studies worldwide (Kassam‐Adams et al., 2020). Currently, it encompasses two major components: the established Prospective Studies of Acute Child Trauma and Recovery (PACT/R) Data Archive and the new Child Trauma Prevention and Treatment (CTPT) Data Archive. The PACT/R Archive has successfully compiled over 30 datasets representing more than 5,500 children exposed to acute trauma (i.e., injury, disaster, accident, violence, and medical events), with 80% of cases having two to five assessment points and utilizing 22 different measures of child posttraumatic stress.

Achievements

Building on PACT/R's methodology and structure, the CTPT Archive expands the scope to include both child trauma prevention and treatment studies (i.e., RCTs as well as other intervention studies), with an initial set of 20 studies comprising approximately 1,500 children. The CTDA is developing metadata standards and methodological frameworks to facilitate data reuse while establishing clear procedures for both contributing data and requesting archive access.

Challenges

For meaningful cross‐study analysis and interpretation, harmonizing data from diverse trauma studies is one of the key challenges to using data from these archives. Data from different sources must be transformed to make them comparable, for example, when dealing with many different measures of symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety in children and caregivers. Semantic standardization at the item (i.e., symptom) level for these mental health measures is crucial for the research utility of both archives; they are the most commonly assessed outcomes across studies. To address this challenge, researchers developed a comprehensive three‐step methodology to establish expert‐reviewed semantic harmonization at the item level. First, the team aligned symptom‐level diagnostic criteria across four different classification systems: the DSM‐IV (American Psychiatric Association, 1994), DSM‐5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), International Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (10th ed.; ICD‐10; World Health Organization [WHO], 2016), and ICD‐11 (WHO, 2019). As an example, the DSM‐IV PTSD symptom “irritable behavior” is aligned with the DSM‐5 symptom “irritable behavior and angry outbursts” based on the conceptual similarities between the two items (American Psychiatric Association, 1994, 2013). The second step involved mapping individual items from various assessment measures onto these consolidated symptom categories (i.e., semantically similar items measuring the same construct). For instance, different questions about loss of interest (e.g., “nothing is fun,” “I didn't enjoy anything”) from multiple scales might all assess the “diminished interest” symptom in various diagnostic conceptualizations of depression (Sadeh et al., 2024).

As a final step, the CDTA expert panel, along with colleagues with relevant expertise, reviewed the proposed symptom concepts and item wordings in a consensus review process. As part of this review, the expert panel carefully considered various challenges, such as how best to handle reverse‐valanced items like “I am easily cheered up” in assessing depressed mood, or whether to retain items that are highly related but may be insufficient on their own to assess a specific construct, such as the “I feel bored” item for diminished interest. Through multiple rounds of expert review, the consensus group achieved strong agreement regarding the semantic harmonization of items from disparate measures. The harmonization process does not dictate analytical strategies but rather establishes a strong building block for researchers to use in designing analyses. This work provides a foundation for integrated cross‐study analyses by future researchers using CTDA data.

International Collaborations of Individual Participant Data in Child Trauma Research

Overview

In addition to the ongoing projects described, collaborations on individual participant‐level data can be used in a time‐limited manner to address individual research questions. The CPTCI International Data Set (de Haan et al., 2020) and the IPD‐MA on cognitive behavioral therapies with a trauma focus (CBTs‐TF; de Haan et al., 2021, 2024) are examples of these single‐project FAIR data efforts. Both projects combined and harmonized raw data across international studies in trauma‐exposed school‐aged children and adolescents. In contrast to data archives, these projects were created to answer prespecified research questions in close ongoing collaboration with the original study authors. The CPTCI International Data Set combines 17 data sets from eight different countries, including children and adolescents aged 6–18 years who have been exposed to trauma (N = 2,313). Each participant had data from the Child Post‐Traumatic Cognitions Inventory (CPTCI; Meiser‐Stedman et al., 2009) assessing dysfunctional posttraumatic cognitions about permanent and disturbing change and being a fragile person in a scary world. The project aimed to explore the role of dysfunctional posttraumatic cognitions in the recently proposed contrasting approaches to PTSD in the ICD‐11 and DSM‐5. The IPD‐MA on CBTs‐TF included 25 randomized trials from 17 different countries. Participants (N = 1,686) were 6–18 years of age and treated for PTSD. The aims were to determine the efficacy of CBTs‐TF relative to passive and active control conditions and to explore individual‐level and treatment‐level moderators.

Achievements

These projects strengthened the child trauma research community by building on close, ongoing collaboration with and across the original study authors. Both projects demonstrate how FAIR data methods can be leveraged to answer questions that would not be possible with single studies alone. Findings from the CPTCI International Dataset indicate that a PTSD diagnosis reduced to its core symptoms could help to disentangle PTSD, depression, and dysfunctional posttraumatic cognitions (de Haan et al., 2020). The IPD‐MA on CBTs‐TF also met its intended aim, supporting the efficacy of CBTs‐TF for symptoms of PTSD, depression, and anxiety, irrespective of age, gender, trauma characteristics, or carer involvement in treatment, with particular benefits for individuals with higher initial levels of distress (de Haan et al., 2024).

Challenges

Across both projects, the biggest challenge was to harmonize different measures of PTSD symptoms. In child trauma research, there is no gold‐standard PTSD assessment. The variability in measurement is driven by different requirements regarding scope, language, and developmental capacity, and by changes in PTSD diagnosis across the DSM‐IV, ICD‐10, DSM‐5, and ICD‐11. Another level of complexity arose regarding self‐report versus proxy‐report versus clinician‐rated outcomes. Datasets with scarce or no labels or metadata, and those in languages other than English, added complexity to the data harmonization. These challenges were addressed by closely collaborating with study authors, reanalyzing study publications, utilizing a large team of established child trauma researchers and statisticians, and building on previous harmonization attempts (Kassam‐Adams et al., 2012). Everyone across the meta‐analysis and contributing author teams devoted considerable time to successfully conducting both projects.

DISCUSSION

FAIR data efforts in the field of traumatic stress research have been buoyed by work in both related and relatively unrelated fields, yet these efforts simultaneously face challenges unique to the field of traumatic stress. Continuing to leverage the most recent advances in other fields, including early‐stage forays into machine learning and alternative intelligence approaches relevant to FAIR data science, will likely offer sustained benefit to traumatic stress research. However, dedicated support for the time and effort needed to tailor and adapt these approaches to traumatic stress research is also necessary to establish the building blocks of sustainable traumatic stress FAIR data resources in the future. The traumatic stress FAIR data efforts described herein provide examples of the time and skills needed to overcome the challenges of initiating FAIR data resources. Key challenges encountered in these FAIR data projects include: restrictions in sharing IPD; obtaining sustained funding; variability across studies in inclusion criteria, treatments, and outcome measures; limited descriptions and meta‐data availability; data harmonization and semantic standardization, including selecting variables and standardizing data entry, creating templates, and training personnel for data extraction; and a lack of expert or field consensus requiring investigators to conduct such consensus activities prior to classifying treatments or outcome domains. Addressing these challenges requires expertise across clinical, research, statistics, and data management disciplines, along with significant time and resources.

FAIR data resources can provide significant potential benefit in terms of improved accuracy, efficiency, and granularity of findings—cornerstones of learning health science and precision medicine. Fortunately, even these limited examples demonstrate this potential. For example, the question of the efficacy of PTSD and PTSD/SUD treatments has been hampered for decades by small sample sizes within individual trials, mixed findings across trials, and the inability to compare treatment efficacy findings across treatment types. By categorizing existing treatments and comparing them to a single comparator, Project Harmony has been able to redirect the field toward focusing on the most promising behavioral treatments and highlight the synergy achieved through adding substance use–targeting medications to support behavioral therapies. In a more targeted approach, the IPD‐MA on CBTs‐TF leveraged FAIR data methods to answer prespecified research questions. Through close collaboration to address complex data harmonization, this international project was able to show the efficacy of CBTs‐TF in young people relative to PTSD, depression, and anxiety, irrespective of individual‐ and treatment‐level moderators (e.g., age, gender, caregiver involvement). The PTSD Repository's comprehensiveness and detailed, standardized extraction of a very broad range of variables across PTSD trials in adults enables a much faster comparison of outcomes, accounting for factors such as self‐reported versus clinician‐assessed outcomes and active versus passive comparators—factors that previously could not be meta‐analyzed efficiently in the short time frames often necessary for policymaking or clinical practice guideline development. Complementing these adult‐focused efforts, the Child Trauma Data Archives have shown how prospective data compilation and semantic harmonization can establish research infrastructure that supports multiple investigative teams, moving beyond single‐project approaches to create sustainable resources for the broader research community.

Although some advances in precision medicine are focused on advances like detailed genetic analyses, precision medicine and learning health systems are often also advanced through nuanced research methods demonstrating what works for whom. An example of how IPD‐MA can aid in these efforts is seen in Project Harmony. By leveraging existing patient‐level data and cutting‐edge quantitative methodologies, Project Harmony improved treatment‐matching and addressed some of the unmet needs of diverse PTSD/SUD populations (e.g., Morgan‐Lopez et al., 2022; Saavedra et al., 2021).

Continued evolution of frameworks that prioritize FAIR principles is essential for advancing research and improving clinical practices in trauma care. We recommend analyzing individual differences among PTSD and PTSD/SUD participants to better understand treatment‐matching and optimize effectiveness. Traditional effect size analyses likely mask hidden variations in treatment response, including nontrivial proportions of patients who worsen. A more granular, patient‐centered approach can improve specificity in treatment recommendations. Toward that aim, efforts to update and expand the FAIR data project datasets described in this paper should be undertaken. For example, the PTSD Repository could be expanded to include IPD. Project Harmony could be expanded to integrate the 32 or more additional trials that have taken place since 2018, increasing the dataset to over 8,400 participants. These efforts would allow for the introduction of new treatment combinations and underrepresented populations, improving the generalizability of findings. The comprehensive three‐step semantic harmonization methodology developed by the Child Trauma Data Archives, which successfully aligned symptom‐level diagnostic criteria across classification systems and mapped individual items from various assessment measures onto consolidated symptom categories, could serve as a model for standardizing adult PTSD and trauma measures. Applying this expert‐reviewed semantic harmonization approach to adult measures would address the significant challenge of measure variability across studies and greatly enhance future harmonization efforts for adult trauma research, similar to how it has facilitated cross‐study analyses in child trauma populations.

We recommend expanding evidence synthesis to include additional patient‐centered outcomes for both PTSD and PTSD/SUD FAIR data projects. Historically, meta‐analyses have focused on clinical severity without examining improvements in psychosocial functioning. Future research should compare functional recovery outcomes across treatment modalities, addressing long‐term patient well‐being. These approaches could help address the mismatch between researcher‐defined treatment success and patient‐identified recovery goals, ensuring patient‐centered outcome measures. Some examples include incorporating psychosocial recovery indicators (e.g., employment, housing stability, relationships) for both PTSD and PTSD/SUD trials and adding outcomes beyond consumption outcomes in SUD research.

Data sharing, a cornerstone of FAIR data practices, should be emphasized in future traumatic stress research, including both clinical trials and nonexperimental study designs. This includes creating accessible data dictionaries with variable definitions and labels and study protocols to enhance collaboration. Buchanan and colleagues (2021) provide a tutorial with recommendations related to selecting metadata for inclusion, data organization, and software. Establishing core data elements for key areas of traumatic stress research (e.g., child trauma research), including specific PTSD measures and methods of data collection and reporting (e.g., self‐report, proxy, clinician‐report, electronic health record), would greatly enhance future harmonization efforts. In addition, data sharing can be incentivized in the form of contributorship or coauthorship, integrated as a new category in curriculum vitae templates and recognized by awards.

More time and funding need to be allocated for data repository development and expansion, data combination and harmonization, and secondary data analysis. The work of preparing and analyzing FAIR data represents significant opportunities to advance clinical research, data science, learning health science, precision medicine, and evidence‐based policymaking; however, the process of implementing sustainable FAIR traumatic stress data resources requires substantially more time and investment in larger teams with more diverse qualifications than any individual study.

Recent advances in FAIR data methods and applications across fields demonstrate the necessity and utility of these significant research infrastructure investments and serve as the basis for advances in traumatic stress research and clinical care. Our review of these recent and ongoing traumatic stress FAIR data projects not only summarizes their numerous achievements and challenges but also provides a roadmap and applied examples for advancing trauma data science within the framework of FAIR principles. Our insights collectively underscore the need for ongoing research and collaboration in trauma data science to enhance the effectiveness of interventions for individuals facing the challenges of traumatic stress and its comorbidities. Innovations in FAIR data methods are paving the way for expanding definitions of recovery; promoting more equitable and effective diagnostic practices; and serving as the groundwork for more efficient, accurate, and evidence‐based health care.
