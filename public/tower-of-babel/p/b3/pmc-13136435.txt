ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the failure of a coil spring suspension system in a locomotive using an experimental and numerical approach. Fractography of the failure spring utilizing scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is an initial phase in the evaluation process. The result ensures material characteristics by examining surface morphology and identifying chemical composition analysis. The connected coil spring assembly subjected to a finite element analysis (FEA) enables the assessment of spring behavior under typical rail track conditions, including active effort and track curvature; static stress analysis is performed. The dynamic analysis was utilized to analyze the effects of variable loading due to static analysis; it was unable to effectively identify the failure. The middle axle’s inner spring was determined to be the significant failure area by fatigue life assessment using FEA results identified a substantially less extensive fatigue life under dynamic loads. Based on research, dynamic loads improve a fatigue damage and provides significant data to enhance spring design and assist reliability in locomotive suspension systems.Keywords: SEM fractography, Tractive effort, straight and curved track, Fatigue life lifespanSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The coil spring suspension system, an integral component of bogie vehicles, provides comfort, stability, and the ability to support loads by absorbing shocks and vibrations while in operation. These systems are widely used in railroads and other transportation-related sectors due to their robustness to environmental conditions and dynamic loads. With any mechanical system, coil springs can wear out and eventually break, which might jeopardize a vehicle’s functionality, safety, and performance. Bogies are components of a railroad vehicle’s system, suspension parts, bushings, bearings, and all of its parts. Additionally, the bogies’ intricate systems include wheel sets and frames that allow for movement mechanically. The wheel sets are connected to the vehicle’s chassis by the main suspensions, which permit them to freely spin on their own axis. Coil spring suspensions can fail for an assortment of reasons, such as material fatigue, design flaws, manufacturing flaws, and environmental variables like corrosion and temperature changes. In addition to the significant issues that could arise from these types of failures, including derailments, increased maintenance costs, and possible passenger safety risks. The safety link, auxiliary dampers, and extra suspensions are that connect the vehicle’s frame to its body. As the connectivity of the system continues to increase, it is more crucial than ever to protect its dependability and security. The operational effectiveness of rail vehicles is significantly impacted by multi-body models, including the way wheels and rails interact as well as the suspension components1. A study on suspension spring failure in metro vehicles2 found that suspension coil springs indicate resonance. For identifying the modal frequency of the coil spring within the permissible range, FE analysis and testing were conducted. Coil springs’ intrinsic frequency and dynamic stiffness were established3. In WAG-9 locomotives, coil springs are essential suspension system parts that absorb shocks and vibrations during locomotive operation. For the finite element study, a higher-order 3D 10-node SOLID187 element with three translational degrees of freedom per node was employed. In order to guarantee mesh quality, static structural analysis was carried out, and the Jacobian ratio, aspect ratio, skewness, and warpage were all checked within the permissible limits. In important high-stress regions, mesh refinement was used, and quality was confirmed to meet the specified boundary constraints. Through several iterations with increasingly refined meshes, a mesh convergence analysis was carried out, confirming convergence when additional refinement produced insignificant changes in stress and deformation values. Based on accessible mechanical data along with specifications, structural material parameters with an elastic modulus of 210 GPa, Poisson’s ratio of 0.3, density of 7850 kg/m³, and yield strength of 250 MPa were utilised to model the coil spring material.

A 10-node quadratic tetrahedral SOLID187 element was selected for structuring the model, as it is suitable for modelling stress variations and curved design. A global element size of 4 mm was used in an adaptive mesh technique; in high-stress areas like coil bending and contact zones, this size was reduced to 1.5 mm. Standard parameters are utilised to evaluate mesh quality: skewness < 0.5, aspect ratio < 3, and Jacobean ratio > 0.6, all of it within acceptable industry norms. Through continually optimising the mesh and analysing the maximum von Mises stress, a mesh convergence analysis was carried out. The convergence was verified when subsequent refinements resulted in a change below 2%, indicating sufficient accuracy and computational efficiency.

Conclusion

This investigation analysed the failure of a coil spring suspension system in a locomotive using a thorough experimental and numerical analysis. The chemical composition of the failure coil spring was revealed to be consistent with typical needs following SEM examination. Fractography analysis and stiffness testing confirmed material integrity and design compliance. Static stress analysis performed under various types of functional conditions includes active effort, curved track, and straight track. Static loading proved to be the root cause of failure, as the results revealed that the induced stress levels were significantly lower than the materials’ yield strength. A dynamic analysis ensued to find out how the system behaved under different conditions of load. The simulation performed showed that resonance effects occurred when excitation frequencies were near to the system’s natural frequency, which strengthened the spring’s displacement and force. The middle axle’s inner spring, which has a lifespan of nearly 1.89 × 10⁴ cycles and whose fracture initiates at the inner coil diameter, was determined to be the failure identified by fatigue analysis. The outcomes reveal the significance of dynamic loading in fatigue failure and the necessity of more effective spring design.
