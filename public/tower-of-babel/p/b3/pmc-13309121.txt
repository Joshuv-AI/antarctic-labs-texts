Two-point deep serratus anterior plane block for modified radical mastectomy – A case series assessing axillary coverage using structured sensory mapping
Authors: Sachin Bansal, Manisha Hemrajani, Pushplata Gupta, Adhiraj Baruah, Akanksha Dutt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSerratus anterior plane block (SAPB) provides chest wall analgesia in modified radical mastectomy (MRM) surgeries by targeting T2–T6 intercostal nerves, but conventional single-injection techniques show inconsistent T2 and axillary coverage, failing to reliably block the intercostobrachial nerve – a key contributor to postoperative axillary pain and postmastectomy pain syndrome. MRM patients experience significant axillary pain from dissection-related intercostobrachial nerve (ICBN) injury, necessitating multimodal analgesia that ensures reliable T2-axillary desensitisation. There is inadequate cephalad/axillary spread in standard SAPB, confirmed by prior studies showing <20% T2 blockade and variable ICBN involvement despite high local anaesthetic volumes. This prospective case series included 10 patients in whom ultrasound-guided deep two-point SAPB was administered with 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine at the 5th rib midaxillary line and 10 mL at the 3rd rib anterior axillary line. Sensory mapping via grid, cold/pin-prick Likert scores, and photography 30 min post-block showed reliable T2–T6 dermatomal desensitisation (70% success: composite score >4) confirmed by heat maps. It is concluded that two-point injection at 3rd and 5th rib enhances T2/ICBN blockade over single-level SAPB; nevertheless, larger randomised controlled trials are needed for opioid-sparing validation.

INTRODUCTION

Fascial plane blocks form an integral part of multimodal analgesia for modified radical mastectomy (MRM) by blocking the intercostal nerves T2–T6.[1] However, significant postoperative axillary pain commonly occurs due to intercostobrachial nerve (ICBN) injury and axillary dissection which if not addressed can contribute to postmastectomy pain syndrome.[23]

Conventional serratus anterior plane block (SAPB), superficial or deep, provides reliable chest wall analgesia but inconsistent ICBN blockade and axillary coverage as shown on standardised dermatomal mapping.[45] More cephalad injection at the third rib within the serratus–intercostal plane has been suggested to effectively target the axilla.[67]

We therefore evaluated ultrasound-guided deep SAPB using a two-point injection at the third and fifth ribs in patients undergoing MRM, aiming for more consistent T2 coverage. We mapped sensory changes systematically and recorded them as digital photographic documentation.

CASE SERIES

We included in this case series 10 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade I-II female patients, undergoing MRM surgery at a tertiary care oncology centre, from January to December 2025. Pre-operative written informed consent for participation, clinical information, and photographic documentation was obtained from each patient. We adhered to CARE: consensus-based clinical case reporting guidelines. Prior approval from the institutional ethics committee (BMH/2024-25/1885) was obtained.

Before surgery, standard ASA monitors (electrocardiogram, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry) were applied and baseline parameters were recorded. Patients were given lateral decubitus position with the operative/block side up, and the non-dependent arm flexed, abducted, and raised over the head. Grid-like markings were drawn using a surgical marker to standardise sensory mapping: vertical lines along the posterior, mid, and anterior axillary lines and the midclavicular line, and horizontal lines at each rib level after counting ribs in the caudo-cranial direction,[8] creating intersection points for assessment. The skin was prepared with povidone-iodine while keeping the markings visible.

Ultrasound-guided SAPB was performed using a high-frequency linear probe (6–13 MHz) (SonoSite M-Turbo™) and a 22G blunt-tip needle (Stimuplex™). The operator stood by the patient’s trunk, and the ultrasound machine was placed on the opposite side facing him. All blocks were performed by one experienced anaesthesiologist using 30 ml of 0.375% ropivacaine. The transducer was placed aseptically with a sterile probe covering in the coronal plane in the midaxillary line at the 5th intercostal space. After subcutaneous infiltration with 2 mL 2% lignocaine and a 1-minute wait, the needle was advanced in-plane, caudo-cranially in the deep serratus anterior plane. After negative aspiration, 20 mL local anaesthetic was injected in increments here. The probe was then moved to the anterior axillary line at the level of the 3rd rib. After subcutaneous infiltration, a second injection of the remaining 10 mL ropivacaine was given in the same deep plane.

Sensory block assessment performed 30 minutes later was assigned to two senior female anaesthesiologists. The patient’s eyes were covered to reduce visual bias. Temperature sensation was tested using a cold spirit swab, and pin-prick sensation using a blunt 26G needle. A 3-point Likert scale was used: 0 = intact/normal, 1 = impaired, and 2 = lost. Sensation was assessed at the grid intersection points, then extended to the medial arm, and the distribution was marked for photographic documentation. A composite dermatome score (0–8) for each modality was calculated by summing four site scores within each dermatome (e.g., T2: maximum 8, minimum 0).

After assessment, general anaesthesia was administered using a laryngeal mask airway and standard anaesthesia technique for induction and maintenance. All patients received 0.15 mg/kg intravenous dexamethasone after induction and 1 g intravenous paracetamol at the end of surgery. Postoperatively, pain was assessed using the numerical rating scale (NRS). Patients received oral paracetamol 500 mg 6 hourly and fentanyl through intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (25 μg bolus, 10-minute lockout). Descriptive analysis was done using International Business Machines Statistical Package for the Social Sciences v23; data were reported as number (%), mean ± standard deviation, and median values for ordinal sensory scores, displayed as heat maps.

All 10 patients completed the planned assessments without protocol deviation, and their demographic and perioperative characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Heat maps [Figure 1a] indicate predominant score-2 responses from T2 to T6 along the anterior, mid, and posterior axillary lines for both modalities, indicating reliable anterolateral thoracic desensitisation. A localised cluster of median score 0 was noted at the midclavicular line at T2 for pin-prick. Figure 1b summarises patient-wise composite sensory scores across dermatomes. Block success was predefined as a composite score >4 (Maximum 8) at each dermatome, from T2 to T6 for both cold and pin-prick modalities. Figure 1c is a photographic documentation of the mapped cutaneous sensory distribution in all the 10 cases.

DISCUSSION

This case series suggests that a two-point (5th and 3rd rib levels) deep SAPB technique produces consistent reduction of pin-prick and cold sensation over the anterior (T3–T6) and lateral (T2–T6) chest wall dermatomes. The additional cephalad injection at the 3rd rib improves axillary distribution of the drug. Uniform sensory attenuation along anterior, mid, and posterior axillary lines, together with sensory loss over the medial upper arm, confirms ICBN block, which is important to cover axillary pain in MRM.

The conventional single-injection SAPB, typically performed at the fourth or fifth rib level, often blocks mid-thoracic dermatomes but shows variable cephalad reach to T2 and inconsistent axillary coverage. Although increasing injectate volume may enhance overall spread, Kunigo et al.[4] reported that even with 40 mL local anaesthetic, T2 and axillary analgesia remained unreliable, and patients required rescue blocks for axillary pain, supporting the concept that injection level (and not volume alone) is a key determinant of lateral (T2–T6) chest wall dermatomes coverage.

Bhoi et al., in a randomised controlled trial comparing superficial and deep SAPBs, administered 30 mL of local anaesthetic at the fifth rib level and performed systematic dermatomal sensory mapping. Although both approaches produced consistent sensory loss from T3 to T7 dermatomes, desensitisation of the T2 dermatome was observed in fewer than 20% of patients, irrespective of the plane of injection. In contrast, Jain et al. demonstrated more reliable blockade of the T2–T5 dermatomes using a comparable volume of local anaesthetic when SAPB injection was performed more cephalad, at the third rib level. This indicates that level of injection, rather than injectate volume, is a critical determinant of cephalad drug spread and effective T2/axillary coverage, supporting the rationale for incorporating a targeted third-rib level injection in our two-point technique.[59]

Cadaveric evidence also provides a mechanistic basis for our approach. Biswas and colleagues demonstrated that a high-volume double-injection strategy yielded broader and more consistent dye distribution over the anterolateral chest wall and axilla, with particularly reliable axillary staining when a separate injection was performed at the third rib level. Recent cadaveric observations also describe inconsistent cephalad reach of single-level SAP injections.[1011]

We also observed a consistent area of relative sparing at the mid-clavicular T2 region, where pin-prick was preserved and cold sensation only partially reduced. This finding may reflect overlapping sensory supply of the upper anterior chest with supraclavicular nerves (cervical plexus), which would not be blocked by serratus anterior plane techniques and represent a clinically relevant limitation when surgery involves the upper-inner quadrant or parasternal territories. This situation would require additional anterior chest wall block (e.g., parasternal intercostal plane techniques) with cervical plexus block.[1112]

We defined block success as a score >4 (maximum score 8) for loss of both modalities at T2 to T6 dermatomes. Block success was achieved in 7 out of 10 cases (70%). In 2 cases (20%), (cases 7 and 10), the block score was ≤4 in terms of both modalities at T2 dermatome, while in one case (case 4), the score was ≤4, with pin-prick sensation intact at T2 dermatome. A common feature in all three cases was body mass index >25 kg/m2. A single high-volume injection has the disadvantage of the injectate infiltrating the surgical field and also hampering the electrocautery.

Our mechanism-based case series evaluates the two-point deep SAPB technique designed to improve axillary and ICBN block in patients undergoing MRM. Systematic dermatomal assessment across the axillary lines and medial upper arm, using both pin-prick and cold modalities with photographic documentation, strengthened the sensory evaluation. However, the limited sample size and assessment of sensory blockade at a single time point are important limitations of this case series.

CONCLUSION

Administering the “two-point SAP block” at the 3rd and 5th rib levels can achieve relatively consistent axillary coverage for MRM surgeries as opposed to single injection conventional SAP because it is able to block the T2 dermatome and ICBN territory in the axilla.
