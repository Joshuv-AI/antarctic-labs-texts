Effect of different targeted tidal volumes during high-frequency oscillation with volume-targeted ventilation on cerebral blood flow velocity and cardiac output: study protocol for a randomised crossover study
Authors: Ourania Kaltsogianni, Mahesh Nanjundappa, Grace Okoye, Anne Greenough, Theodore Dassios
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh frequency oscillation (HFO) is a lung protective ventilation strategy for newborn infants due to the small tidal volume delivery at fast frequencies, but has been associated with an increased risk of brain injury. High-frequency oscillation with volume-targeted ventilation (HFO&VTV) is a new mode of HFO that applies a targeted tidal volume (VThf) set by the clinician and achieves less fluctuations of carbon dioxide levels and therefore reduces the risk of brain injury. The optimal starting VThf values, however, have not been explored to date.This study investigates the optimum starting settings in newborn infants receiving HFO by comparing the cerebral blood flow velocity and cardiac output at different tidal volume targets of HFO&VTV.MethodsThis randomised crossover trial performed at a single tertiary neonatal unit will be recruiting 25 infants of any gestation and at any postnatal age receiving HFO. Each infant will receive three targeted tidal volumes on HFO&VTV in random order with control periods in between. The resistive index (RI) as an indicator of cerebral blood flow in term-born infants and the cardiac output for all infants in the study will be assessed in each period. The primary outcome will be the reduction in the RI that will be measured on term-born infants using the anterior cerebral artery Doppler. The secondary outcome will be the improvement in cardiac output that will be assessed in all infants with bedside ECHO. Background

One in seven infants born in the UK each year is admitted to a neonatal unit [1], and the most frequent cause for admission is the need for respiratory support [2]. Mechanical ventilation, although life-saving, is associated with considerable mortality [3] and morbidity [4]. Preterm ventilated infants can suffer chronic respiratory morbidity, including lung function abnormalities and exercise intolerance persisting in adolescence and adulthood [5]. Term infants can also suffer from complications related to mechanical ventilation. In one study, mortality and severe neurodisability at 2-year follow-up were common among term-born infants who required mechanical ventilation at birth, affecting more than a quarter of the cohort and particularly those who were ventilated for longer than 3 days [6]. Therefore, optimisation of neonatal ventilation could help reduce respiratory morbidity.

Infants with severe respiratory failure are often supported by high-frequency oscillation (HFO) when small tidal volumes are delivered at fast frequencies. Of particular concern during HFO is the risk of brain injury due to fluctuations and extremes of carbon dioxide (CO2) levels, which may occur either due to very efficient CO2 clearance or impaired venous return secondary to raised intra-thoracic pressure [7]. A new mode of HFO has now been developed that is high-frequency oscillation with volume-targeted ventilation (HFO&VTV), during which the clinician sets a target tidal volume (VThf), and the ventilator automatically adjusts the amplitude pressure to supply the targeted VThf. A maximum allowed level of the pressure amplitude (Amplmax) is also set as a safety feature [8]. This mode allows using high frequencies combined with low VThf (in comparison to conventional ventilation) to decrease the risk of lung trauma related to the ventilator [9]. In addition, HFO&VTV reduces fluctuations of high frequency tidal volumes, achieves better control of partial arterial pressures of carbon dioxide (pCO2) levels [8, 9], and reduces hypoxic episodes compared with HFO [10]. Consequently, HFO&VTV may be particularly useful when the respiratory mechanics change rapidly, such as following the administration of surfactant [9, 11], drainage of pneumothorax or treatment with sedation and muscle relaxation [8]. No recommendation has been made on optimal starting VThf values and monitoring parameters [12], and yet a recent national survey demonstrated that 54% of neonatal intensive care units apply this mode [13].

Doppler ultrasound has been used extensively in neonates to evaluate cerebral haemodynamic changes. There is no consistent evidence to support the routine use of Doppler-derived parameters in preterm infants to predict brain injury or neurodevelopmental outcomes [14]. In term-born infants, the resistive index (RI) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), measured with Doppler ultrasound, is inversely associated with cerebral blood flow velocity, and extreme values (RI < 0.55 or > 0.75) support the diagnosis of hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE) [15]. The RI values of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) are also a non-invasive measure of cerebral blood flow. Low RI of the ACA correlates with worse brain injury in infants with HIE under therapeutic hypothermia [16]. Doppler imaging with cranial ultrasound is widely used in neonatal intensive care, and it is readily available by the bedside. Moreover, bedside echocardiography (ECHO) is the current standard measurement of cardiac output (CO) in infants with cardiovascular instability [17]. A ventricular output of 150 to 300 ml/kg/min is considered normal in preterm and term neonates without transductal or interatrial shunting, whereas values of less than 150 ml/kg/min have been associated with adverse outcomes [18].

The aim of this study is to investigate the safety profile of HFO&VTV compared to HFO by assessing the effect on cerebral blood flow velocity and cardiac output, as well as determining the optimum starting settings. Our primary outcome is the reduction in the resistive index that will be measured on term-born infants using the ACA Doppler on cranial ultrasound. The secondary outcome is the improvement of the cardiac output that will be assessed with bedside ECHO in all infants.

Methods

This is a randomised crossover trial.

Setting

A single tertiary neonatal unit at King’s College Hospital NHS Foundation Trust, London, UK. The unit has previous experience in the use of HFO as a rescue mode of ventilation.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria are as follows:

Newborn infants of any gestation and at any postnatal age on HFO.

Newborn infants of any gestation and at any postnatal age on HFO.

Exclusion criteria

Infants with known intracerebral pathology (stroke, hydrocephalus, intracerebral haemorrhage, severe HIE)

Infants with known intracerebral pathology (stroke, hydrocephalus, intracerebral haemorrhage, severe HIE)

Infants with congenital cardiac abnormalities.

Infants with congenital cardiac abnormalities.

Recruitment

Parents or legal guardians of all eligible infants will be initially approached by the clinical team taking care of the infant, and if they agree, then by a researcher. The parents are provided with an information sheet about the study. The researchers answer questions and respond to any concerns in a face-to-face meeting and obtain written informed consent. The study was approved by the Health Research Authority and by the London-Hampstead Research Ethics Committee.

Randomisation

The randomisation sequence in which participants receive the different levels of tidal volumes is generated by using an excel random number generator and kept in sealed envelopes. This is done by a person independent of the research team who is not involved in the study. A member of the research team will enrol participants in their allocation sequence.

Enrolment

Infants will be enrolled in the study by the research team following parental consent. Infants are randomised to the sequence of the different levels of VThf by opening the next envelope.

Blinding

The study is not blinded.

Intervention

All infants will be oscillated using SLE6000 ventilators. Ventilation settings will be adjusted by the clinical team as per the unit’s protocol.

Infants will be studied when clinically stable, and a blood gas will confirm that the pCO2 is in the target range (4.5 to 6.5 kPa). Participants will receive HFO&VTV at targeted tidal volumes of 1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 ml/kg in random order [8] for a period of 10–20 min each. Additionally, there will be a control/washout period of 20 min between each tidal volume when infants will be switched to HFOV without volume guarantee at the settings applied prior to the study.

Transcutaneous CO2 monitoring will be applied to the infants during the study by using the Masimo Set Radiometer TCM5 as neither carbon dioxide diffusion coefficient (DCO2) nor weight-corrected DCO2 values correlate with pCO2 levels and tidal volumes during HFO&VTV [10].

The resistive index of the anterior cerebral artery as an indicator of cerebral blood flow in term-born infants and the cardiac output for all infants in the study will be assessed at the last 5 min of each 20-min period. Measurements of the RI and the cardiac output will also be made during the last 5 min of the control periods, and the results will be averaged. For the first five participants, two investigators will be measuring the CO and RI of the ACA in the same participant to assess intra- and inter-observer variation in ultrasound measurements.

Measurement of cardiac output

All echocardiography studies will be performed using a Philips EPIQ model cardiovascular ultrasound machine. The left and right ventricular CO will be calculated by measuring the ventricular outflow tract cross-sectional area (CSA) and velocity time integral (VTI) as described below. The CSA of the left or right ventricular outflow tract will be determined by measuring the end-systolic internal diameter of ventricular outflow tracts at the hinge point of the aortic valve or pulmonary valve annulus, respectively.

The diameter of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) will be measured in the parasternal long axis view (PLAX), and the diameter of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) will be measured in the PLAX RVOT view. CSA will then be calculated as CSA = (D∕2)2 × π (π = 3.14), where D is the diameter.

The left ventricular VTI will be measured in the apical five-chamber view, and the right ventricular VTI will be measured in the PLAX RVOT view with pulse wave Doppler spectral at the three locations across both LVOT and RVOT: 1 mm below (proximal) the valve level, at the level of valve hinges, and the tip of the aortic and pulmonic valve (distal to the valve). The nurse-to-patient ratio will be according to the unit’s protocol that is determined on the patient’s acuity.

No concomitant treatments will be prohibited during the trial.

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome will be the reduction in the Resistive Index that will be measured on term-born infants using the anterior cerebral artery Doppler on cranial ultrasound [19].

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome will be the improvement of the cardiac output that will be assessed with bedside ECHO in all infants [20].

Sample size

Term infants

The sample size for the term born infants was estimated based on the primary outcome measure, which is the change in the Resistive Index (RI) measured using the anterior cerebral artery Doppler on cranial ultrasound. The standard deviation of the RI has been reported to be 0.057 in 40 term infants [19] and a clinically significant difference of 0.11 has been observed between infants with hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy and healthy controls [15]. To detect such a difference by comparing these two independent means with > 90% power and 0.01 significance, ten infants will be required. Recruitment of 12 infants would allow for data quality assurance and drop out from the study after recruitment.

Adequate recruitment within 18 months is feasible with a modest consent rate, as in a recent chronological year we had 14 term infants that were treated with HFO.

Preterm infants

The sample size for the preterm infants was estimated based on the secondary outcome measure, which is the change in cardiac output assessed with bedside ECHO. The standard deviation of the cardiac output measured by echocardiography has been reported to be 56 ml/kg/min in preterm infants [22], and a clinically significant difference of 71 ml/min/kg corresponds to the difference between the mean normal value of 221 ml/min/kg in preterm infants [22] and a cutoff for low abnormal values of less than 150 ml/min/kg [18]. To detect such a difference by comparing these two independent means with a power of 90% and a significance level of 5%, a sample of 13 preterm infants studied crossover would be required.

Data collection

Demographic and outcome data will be collected from the clinical records and recorded under a unique study identifier number. Basic epidemiologic parameters such as sex, gestational age at birth, birth weight, mode of delivery, Apgar scores, doses of surfactant administered, day of life at study, mechanical ventilation starting time and HFO starting time, and diagnoses at enrolment will be recorded for each infant. Paper data will be stored in a locked filing cabinet. Patient de-identified electronic data will be stored in password-protected computers and on encrypted devices.

Statistical analysis

Sub-group analyses will be performed for term and preterm infants. Descriptive statistics will be used to describe infant demographics. These will include standard statistical summaries, e.g. medians and ranges, means and variances, or proportions and percentages, dependent on the distribution of outcomes.

The main analysis will investigate resistive index (for term infants) and cardiac output (for term and preterm infants) at baseline and at each targeted tidal volumes during HFO&VTV.

Data will be assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) will be used for normally distributed data, with the paired T-test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. If data are not normally distributed, non-parametric tests for related samples will be used (Friedman test). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons will be performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for related samples. Linear mixed models will also be used to estimate mean differences and confidence intervals.

Since extreme values of cardiac output or cerebral perfusion might be harmful for ventilated infants, an interim analysis will be undertaken aiming to minimise unnecessary exposure to inadvertent targeted tidal volumes if a strong treatment effect is detected before the trial is completed. The interim analysis will be undertaken by an independent researcher as planned above and halfway through the recruitment of the full intended population. Analysis will be performed using SPSS software.

Safety

All participants will receive standard care and monitoring by clinical staff. Potential risks include fluctuations of the pCO2 (hypocarbia and hypercarbia), but these will be minimal as transcutaneous CO2 monitoring will be applied to the infants during the study. In addition, the targeted tidal volumes selected (1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 ml/kg) are relatively small and unlikely to cause overventilation. All serious adverse events will be recorded on a serious adverse event (SAE) form and will be emailed to the sponsor within one working day of the Chief Investigator (CI) becoming aware of the event. When the event is unexpected and thought to be related to the use of the device, this will be reported by the CI/Sponsor to the ethics and Health Research Authority within 15 days.

Protocol compliance

Any accidental protocol deviations will be appropriately documented and reported to the Chief Investigator and Sponsor immediately. The Chief Investigator and Sponsor will monitor and audit the conduct of this research.

Data protection and patient confidentiality

The General Data Protection Regulation and Data Protection Act 2018 will be adhered to. Data will be de-identified before being entered in a secure database. A unique study identifier number will be issued to each participant on enrolment into the study. That number will be used in all subsequent data collection forms. Patient paper data will be stored in a locked filing cabinet in a room that is only accessible to the research team and that is based at the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit facilities at King’s College Hospital. Patient de-identified electronic data will be stored on encrypted university computers or memory stick devices, both of which require user identification and password verification.

Discussion

This study will explore the optimal volume targeting values on HFO with regard to cerebral blood flow and cardiac output in newborn infants. To date, optimal VThf parameters have not been identified.

In a retrospective cohort study among 53 very preterm infants (mean (SD) gestational age: 26.8 (2.3) weeks) with respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) that received HFO&VTV within 72 h of life, the mean (SD) VThf to provide normocapnia (5.3–7.3 kPa) was 1.64 (SD 0.25) ml/kg, but there was no significant correlation between VThf and pCO2 levels. Therefore, a specific volume targeting range could not be recommended. The authors concluded that optimal VThf levels are dynamic and vary within the same patient over time and between patients with different baseline characteristics. That study, though, was limited by its retrospective design, the lack of continuous CO2 monitoring, and the fact that they included only very preterm infants with RDS within the first 72 h of life [10]. A retrospective service evaluation that included 17 infants receiving HFO&VTV with a median corrected gestation of 28 weeks (range 24–40 weeks) suggested starting with volumes of 2–2.5 ml/kg, but with close monitoring of carbon dioxide levels, as most infants will require volumes of less than 2 ml/kg to achieve normocapnia [8].

This prospective randomised crossover trial will provide more robust evidence on the optimal VThf target range with regard to cerebral blood flow and cardiac output across all gestations and at any postnatal age in infants with a variety of underlying diagnoses. The tidal volumes we have chosen (1.5, 2.0 and 2.5 ml/kg) are within the ranges observed in previous studies, and therefore it is unlikely to cause overventilation and subsequently hypocarbia. High frequency tidal volumes exceeding 3 ml/kg have been used before with normal arterial CO2 levels [8]. In addition, transcutaneous carbon dioxide monitoring will be applied for the whole duration of the study to alert the researchers about significant changes in CO2 elimination [23].

If this study establishes the optimal safe volume targeting range, it will help achieve tighter carbon dioxide control and less fluctuations of cerebral blood flow during HFO&VTV and reduce the risk of brain injury related to high-frequency oscillation. This could help reduce respiratory morbidity and improve clinical outcomes in the neonatal population.
