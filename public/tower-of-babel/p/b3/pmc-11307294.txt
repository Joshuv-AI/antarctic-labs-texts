Insights on Using Solid Bitumen Reflectance as a Thermal Maturity Proxy in the Bakken Formation, Williston Basin, USA
Authors: Paul C Hackley, Clint Scott, Justin E Birdwell, Jennifer L Nedzweckas, Brett J Valentine, Tongwei Zhang, Timothy O Nesheim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo further refine the use of solid bitumen reflectance (BRo in %) as a measurement of thermal maturity in source-rock reservoirs, we examined its relationship to other thermal proxies in the Bakken Formation. Comparisons included criteria from programmed temperature pyrolysis, gas chromatography (GC), and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Thirty-two organic-rich samples from the lower and upper shale members of the Devonian–Lower Carboniferous Bakken Formation were collected from eight cores across the Williston Basin, USA, at depths (∼7575–11,330 ft) representing immature through post peak oil/early condensate thermal maturity conditions based on proximity to current hydrocarbon production. Unmodified BRo values were correlated to programmed temperature pyrolysis parameters (hydrogen index, production index, and Tmax), normal hydrocarbon and isoprenoid analysis of extractable organic matter (pristane/n-C17 and phytane/n-C18) from GC analysis, and peak ratios from FTIR spectroscopy (branching ratio and A-factor). Strong correlations between unmodified BRo values (not corrected to a vitrinite reflectance equivalent, VRe) and other thermal proxies suggest that BRo can be used as a direct thermal proxy in marine Paleozoic source-rock reservoirs where vitrinite is rare or absent. Moreover, an apparent overestimation of VRe at the lowest thermal maturity investigated herein may argue against the application of BRo conversion to VRe in the Bakken Formation. Solvent extraction caused a consistent decrease in BRo when average post-extraction values from a given well were compared to BRo prior to extraction, although the decrease in mean value was not statistically significant. These results are discussed in the context of advocating for the use of unmodified BRo values as a best practice for thermal maturity determination in Paleozoic marine source-rock reservoirs.

Introduction

The term “solid bitumen” is used to describe a solid or semisolid viscous petroleum phase which is observed petrographically and has well-defined optical and spectroscopic characteristics that allow it to be distinguished from other types of sedimentary organic matter.1−3 The measured mean reflectance of solid bitumen (symbolized BRo, in %) is increasingly used as a thermal maturity proxy in source-rock reservoirs since the advent of the shale revolution in petroleum production.4−6 This is particularly the case in North America, where source-rock reservoir production predominantly occurs from Paleozoic marine facies in which vitrinite is rare or absent.6 However, despite its now widespread use, many workers still attempt to link measured BRo values to an equivalent vitrinite reflectance value (symbolized VRe in %).7−13 Vitrinite reflectance (symbolized VRo in %) is determined by measuring light reflectance from the woody remains of vascular land plants. The conversion of measured BRo values to a calculated VRe value is performed using one of multiple published empirical expressions which relate BRo to VRo, as summarized in a recent study on the Bakken Formation.14 This conversion is typically performed because VRo measurement has a much longer historical usage in petroleum systems research and is globally regarded as the standard thermal proxy technique in sedimentary basins. For example, VRo is used for the placement of prospective boundary limits on hydrocarbon-related thermal maturity windows, e.g., immature, oil, and wet and dry gas.15−18 Moreover, there are issues with the usage of BRo due to the sometimes heterogeneous textures and presentations of solid bitumen, the possibility of its migration, the confusion of its identification with that of other sedimentary organic matter types, including vitrinite, and the sometimes absence of its strong relationship to other thermal proxies in the same sample or sample set.19−23 Nevertheless, the observation that most of the currently produced source-rock reservoirs globally occur in distal marine strata, where vitrinite is scarce, or are Lower to Middle Paleozoic in age from before the evolution and radiation of vascular land plants means that VRo measurement is not a universally useful thermal proxy and that other proxies, such as BRo are necessary.

Thermal maturity proxies are important for understanding burial history and the thermal stresses present in a sedimentary basin.18 The thermal regime is relevant not only to petroleum exploration but also to the evolution of reservoir porosity for applications like geothermal24 and energy gas storage,25 CO2 sequestration,26 and rock–water interactions relevant to critical mineral resources.27 Herein, we examine the thermal proxy BRo in the Bakken Formation as a case study because these rocks lack substantial vitrinite, and the petroleum system is reasonably well understood. This is often not the case in petroleum systems research, so the availability of a well-calibrated BRo data set provides substantiation for the direct use of BRo in other black shales, particularly those predating land plant evolution. The petroleum geology of the world-class source rocks in the Devonian–Lower Carboniferous Bakken Formation of the Williston Basin has been widely investigated in the last decades, including studies of sedimentology,28,29 paleoenvironment,30 reservoir characterization,31,32 organic and inorganic geochemistry,33−35 and petroleum production.36−38 Thermal maturity in the shale members of the Bakken Formation has been historically investigated through VRo studies,39,40 some of which have claimed that the phenomenon of vitrinite reflectance suppression appears to impact VRo measurement.41−45 VRo suppression is reported when measured reflectance values are lower than expected relative to thermal maturity as predicted through other means such as comparisons to depth, temperature, or the type of produced petroleum product. Experimental work has shown that VRo suppression can indeed occur under certain conditions, such as in the presence of abundant hydrogen-rich organic matter, and the suppression is postulated to operate through a free radical mechanism which enhances termination reactions and thereby slows the aromatization and rate of increasing structural order in vitrinite.46 To address the presence of VRo suppression in the Bakken, workers have used measured BRo values to compute VRe.14,47−49 However, new evidence suggests similar free radical-induced reflectance suppression appears to impact BRo, although the molecular mechanism operating in solid bitumen may be somewhat different than for vitrinite.50 This observation suggests that the computation of VRe values from BRo measurement may be a flawed approach to thermal maturity determination, necessitating an alternative or the use of multiple thermal proxies in solid bitumen-containing source-rocks such as the shale members of the Bakken Formation or other Lower Paleozoic marine source-rock reservoirs.

Accurate information on thermal maturity in the Bakken Formation is critical for North American petroleum exploration and development due to the importance of the Bakken petroleum system for current oil production51−54 and its potential use as an analog or case study to inform exploration and development of other shale formations. In this study, we investigated BRo properties in the Bakken Formation, comparing this thermal proxy to other thermal maturity criteria from programmed temperature pyrolysis, gas chromatography (GC), and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, to illustrate the interrelationships among thermal proxies and to outline best practices for thermal maturity determination in solid bitumen-rich source rocks where vitrinite is scarce or absent. In addition, we examined BRo values before and after organic solvent extraction in an effort to refine the understanding of potential compositional controls on its optical properties and used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and low-pressure nitrogen (LP-N2) adsorption to explore microscale rock properties across the immature-early condensate thermal gradient represented by the samples of this study.

Materials and Methods

Samples were collected from the core repositories of the North Dakota Geological Survey (NDGS) Wilson M. Laird Core and Sample Library in Grand Forks, ND (6 wells in North Dakota, see Table 1 for sample identifications that include well number and depths in feet) and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) Core Research Center in Denver, CO (2 wells in Montana; library numbers F190 and F194, see Table 1 for depths). In total, one hundred and ninety-six core samples were collected from the informal lower and upper shale members of the Bakken Formation from the eight wells (Figure 1). All samples were crushed and homogenized using an automated mortar and pestle. Rock powders were used for the determination of total organic carbon (TOC) content at the USGS Petroleum Geochemistry Research Laboratory in Lakewood, Colorado (USGS-Lakewood). Following carbonate removal at room temperature with 6 mol/L HCl, TOC content was determined using a LECO C744 carbon analyzer.55 A Wildcat Technologies Hydrocarbon Analyzer with Kinetics (HAWK; Humble, TX) instrument was used for programmed temperature pyrolysis following typical procedures.56,57 Geochemical screening analyses included internal laboratory standards and procedural duplicates (minimally every 20 samples) for all runs and were performed according to the instrument manuals.

Based on TOC content >10 wt %, indicating high-quality organic-rich source rocks, a sample subset was chosen for detailed petrographic analysis at the USGS-Organic Petrology Laboratory in Reston, Virginia. Four samples were selected from each of the 8 wells, 2 each from the lower and upper Bakken shale members, for a total of 32 samples. These 32 samples were selected to contain approximately equivalent TOC content (∼12 wt %, although this was not always achievable), so that geochemical and petrographic comparisons between samples would not be biased by high or low organic matter concentrations. Petrographic samples were crushed to approximately 1 mm in top size, mounted as randomly oriented particulate in poly(methyl-methacrylate), and mechanically polished according to ASTM D2797.58 Organic petrography analyses were conducted on a Leica DM 4000 microscope equipped with LED illumination and a monochrome camera for light detection operated by the computer program DISKUS-FOSSIL from Hilgers Technisches Buero. For each sample, the dominant population of solid bitumen14,17 was measured for mean random reflectance using incident white light with a 50× 0.85 numerical aperture oil immersion objective per ASTM D7708.59 Microscope calibration used a yttrium-aluminum-garnet (0.908% Ro) reflectance standard. The samples also were qualitatively evaluated in blue light epi-fluorescence.

The 32-sample subset was non-quantitatively extracted using a chloroform rinse procedure via a vortex and a centrifuge. Bitumen was extracted by adding ∼6 mL of chloroform to 0.5–1.0 g of crushed rock, vortexing, centrifuging for ∼5 min, and decanting the supernatant. This rinsing procedure was repeated at least 3 times until the extract was clear. Chromatograms of the whole extracts were determined using an Agilent model 6890 Gas Chromatograph with a 60 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 μm ZB-1 dimethyl-polysiloxane capillary column and a flame ionization detector (GC-FID) at USGS-Lakewood. The temperature program started at 40 °C and increased at 4.5 °C/min to 325 °C, where it was held for 20 min. The helium carrier gas flow rate was 2.5 mL/min, and the injection volume was 1 μL using a splitless injection mode with an injection temperature of 325 °C.60 Following the methods described above, the extracted rock residues were reanalyzed for TOC content, and programmed temperature pyrolysis and BRo values also were redetermined on the extracted rock residues. BRo reanalysis of extracted rock residues was on the same microscope system and by the same petrographer as had conducted petrography on the original samples, and without consultation of the BRo values that were determined on the original samples.

The 32-sample subset was analyzed via a screening-type whole-rock FTIR spectroscopy approach. A Bruker ALPHA II spectrometer equipped with a platinum attenuated total reflectance sampling module (model A220/D-01) and diamond internal-reflection element was used for all measurements.61 Absorbance mode spectra were collected between 4000 and 400 cm–1 by coadding 16 scans for each sample at a 4 cm–1 resolution. After background and baseline corrections using the Bruker OPUS software package (version 8.2.28), spectra were exported for processing in Microsoft Excel (v. 2308). A combination of check standards and replicate sample spectra was used to confirm that variability in peak positions and absorbance intensities were within the acceptance criteria defined by the manufacturer. Thermal and compositional proxies CH3/CH2 branching ratio62 and A-factor63 were calculated for each spectrum. The CH3/CH2 branching ratio was calculated using the sum of intensities between 2915 and 2935 cm–1 (CH2-asymmetric stretch) and the sum of intensities between 2950 and 2970 cm–1 (CH3-asymmetric stretch). A-factor values were calculated as the ratio of the sum of intensities between 2915 and 2935 cm–1 divided by that area added to the aromatic C–H stretch between 1580 and 1615 cm–1. An additional baseline correction beyond that applied to the overall spectrum was determined to be unnecessary.

From within the 32-sample subset, one sample was selected from the lower Bakken shale from each of the 8 wells for SEM evaluation and surface area and pore size analysis via LP-N2 adsorption. SEM samples were milled in a Fischione Instruments model 1060 Ar+ ion mill using 6 keV, 2 beams, 2° tilt, 50% focus, and 175° rocking, at 3 rpm for 30 min, after which the samples were rotated 180° and milled for an additional 30 min under the same conditions. A conductive coating of iridium (∼3 nm thickness) was applied to all milled sample examination surfaces. A Hitachi SU5000 variable pressure field emission SEM operated at 5–10 keV, 15–30 spot intensity, at a working distance of 3 mm, was used for imaging. LP-N2 adsorption analysis was performed at the Bureau of Economic Geology in Austin, Texas, on the eight sample lower Bakken shale subsets with an Autosorb-iQ-MP instrument (Quantachrome Instruments) equipped with a vacuum capable of reaching 5 × 10–7 Pa. Crushed (20 to 50 mesh, 500 to 800 μm) sample masses of ∼200 mg were placed into 6 mm stem quartz sample cells and degassed for 8 h at 250 °C under vacuum. Nitrogen at 77 K was used as the probe gas for all experiments. Surface areas were calculated using the best linear range between 0.05 and 0.3 P/Po, with a minimum of 5 points used in the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis. Pore size distributions were obtained using both the density functional theory method and the Barrett, Joyner, and Halenda method, both of which are available within the ASiQwin instrument software package (v. 2.02).

All data generated from the 32-sample subset and used within this manuscript are available from Hackley et al.64

Results

Screening Geochemistry

The TOC content ranges from 10.3 to 21.8 wt % (Table 1), with an average of 13.2 wt %, as selected by design (n = 32) from the larger overall sample set (n = 196). The S1 values range from 2.4 to 8.8 mg hydrocarbon (HC)/g rock, with an average of 5.2 mg/g, whereas the S2 values range from 12.7 to 122.8 mg/g, with an average of 42.1 mg/g. Tmax values range from 420 to 458 °C, indicating thermal maturities from immature to late mature.65 The computed parameter hydrogen index [HI, (S2 × 100)/TOC] ranges from 104 to 662 mg HC/g TOC, whereas the computed parameter production index [PI, S1/(S1 + S2)] ranges from 0.04 to 0.35, also consistent with immature to late mature thermal conditions.

Following the chloroform extraction, the TOC content ranges from 9.4 to 20.4 wt %, with an average of 12.5 wt %. Programmed pyrolysis parameter S1 ranges from 0.10 to 0.71 mg/g (average 0.23 mg/g) and S2 ranges from 9.7 to 118.1 mg/g (average 42.2 mg/g). As a percentage of the original value, 1.3 to 19.3% of TOC content is extracted, not considering three samples in which the measured post-extraction TOC content is slightly greater than pre-extraction, possibly due to sample variability or measurement error. The LECO TOC standard deviation has been shown to range between 0.1 and 0.5% for shale reference materials used in interlaboratory testing studies,66 and the variability generally increases with organic richness. For S1, 83.2 to 98.5% of the original value is extracted (avg. 95.0%) by the chloroform rinse, whereas 3.8 to 36.1% of the original S2 content is extracted (avg. 18.9%). As expected, Tmax values for extracted samples show a small but consistent increase (1 to 5 °C) relative to pre-extracted values due to the removal of heavy oils in the S2 component.67 Computed HI and PI values show consistent decreases post-extraction. Generally, these shifts in bulk organic geochemical parameters after chloroform treatment are consistent with observations from mature source rocks of the Late Cretaceous Eagle Ford Shale.68

GC and FTIR Spectroscopy

Chromatograms from GC-FID show smooth and consistent n-alkane envelopes, with the strongest response generally at n-C9–15 and smooth decay out to n-C27 in lower maturity samples (wells F190, F194, and 26661), and out to n-C34 in higher maturity samples (Figure 2). Samples from the immature to early mature and early peak oil thermal maturity wells (based on proximity to hydrocarbon production69) generally show the presence of unresolved complex mixture (UCM) at higher carbon numbers, whereas UCM is absent in post peak oil to early condensate samples (see Section 3.3 for interpretation of thermal maturity zones). Chromatograms of the upper and lower Bakken shale samples are essentially indistinguishable at a given thermal maturity. Pristane/phytane ratios range from 0.81 to 1.91 (Table 2), whereas terrestrial to aquatic ratios [TAR, (n-C27 + n-C29 + n-C31)/(n-C15 + n-C17 + n-C19) or n-C27/(n-C15 + n-C17 + n-C19) for lower maturity samples] range from 0.01 to 0.05, consistent with marine-sourced organic matter deposited under anoxic conditions.70,71 Isoprenoid ratios Pr/n-C17 and Ph/n-C18 plot in a strongly correlated linear relationship (r2 = 0.94) at the boundary between mixed organic and marine organic sources (Figure 3) in the discriminant chart of Shanmugam,72 also consistent with the known marine depositional environment of the Bakken.73

Branching ratio values from bulk-rock FTIR spectroscopy range from 0.58 to 0.76, whereas A-factor values range from 0.52 to 0.80. These values are consistent with other studies of source rocks containing primarily type II marine kerogen, including a limited evaluation of shale members of the Bakken Formation by Lee et al.74 over a similar maturity range. Differences with some studies applying micro-FTIR methods7 or other FTIR measurement approaches, such as transmittance or diffuse reflectance, may lead to differing ranges of FTIR ratio values due to the differences in instrumentation, acquisition method, data processing and reduction, and the specific peak integration approach applied.75,76

Organic Petrography and Reflectance

BRo values are 0.31 ± 0.05 to 0.53 ± 0.06% (n = 14) for wells F190, F194, and 26661; 0.61 ± 0.08 to 0.78 ± 0.11% (n = 4) for well 20844; and 1.05 ± 0.15 to 1.29 ± 0.14% (n = 16) for wells 22388, 29426, 24123, and 19918 (Table 3). Proximity to current hydrocarbon production69 and the locations of the sampled wells with respect to prior thermal maturity modeling54 place the samples into 3 thermal maturity groupings: immature to early mature in wells F190, F194, and 26661; early peak oil in well 20844; and post peak oil to early condensate in 22388, 29426, 24123, and 19918. Therefore, BRo values of 0.31 ± 0.05 to 0.53 ± 0.06% suggest immature to early mature conditions, whereas BRo of 0.61 ± 0.08 to 0.78 ± 0.11% suggests early peak oil, and BRo values of 1.05 ± 0.15 to 1.29 ± 0.14% suggest post peak oil to early condensate conditions (Figure 1). Samples from the lower and upper Bakken shale members are separated by only approximately 60 to 90 vertical ft, and thus the entire formation should be at a consistent thermal maturity in each well. Samples from immature thermal conditions (wells F190, F194, and 26661) contain abundant heterogeneous bituminite grading to solid bitumen of variable reflectance (sensu Hackley et al.77) and scattered algal organic matter with strong fluorescence intensity (Figure 4A–D), including sparse Tasmanites. Heterogeneity of the bituminite to solid bitumen continuum at low maturity is considered to arise from differences in mineral inclusions and the relative degradation of the original organic matter, from which the visually most homogeneous and most abundant organic matter type was selected for the BRo measurement. Solid bitumen is weakly fluorescent, whereas local blebs of higher reflectance (∼0.1 to 0.2% higher than the dominant population) solid bitumen show little or no fluorescence (cf. Figure 4A,B). Samples from interpreted early peak oil conditions contain a secondary, less abundant population of strongly fluorescent homogeneous solid bitumen present as fracture-filling veins and lenses (Figure 4E,F), in addition to the dominant population of relatively heterogeneous solid bitumen. Samples from interpreted post peak oil/early condensate thermal conditions contain abundant solid bitumen as an interconnected network (light gray material present throughout Figure 5A–D) acting as a groundmass for the rock mineral matrix. Solid bitumen also occurs as larger, sometimes lamellar, accumulations (Figure 5B). Algal organic matter and bituminite (including bacterial biomass) are absent at post peak oil/early condensate conditions, and the solid bitumen is more homogeneous in presentation. Granular micrinite is present at all thermal conditions as a low-abundance highly reflective component (e.g., Figure 4A) that is thought to occur as a refractory residue remaining from the volatilization of lipid-rich organic compounds during diagenesis.78 Scattered, nonstructured terrigenous organic matter consisting of laths (Figure 4A) and rounded fragments of inertinite (Figure 4C) are present at all thermal conditions; vitrinite was not identified. These descriptions are consistent with the organic petrology of the Bakken and its relationship to thermal maturity, as reported by others since the advent of the shale revolution.6,14

SEM Petrography and LP-N2 Adsorption

SEM imaging shows the relative absence of resolvable organic matter porosity in the eight examined samples from the lower Bakken shale (Figure 6). SEM visualization of porosity generally is limited to interparticle porosity (per Loucks et al.79) occurring interstitially between organic matter and minerals (Figure 6A–E) and intraparticle porosity in the interstices of clay booklets (Figure 6F). Minor intraparticle organic pores are observed at post peak oil to early condensate thermal maturity (Figure 6D,E). The interconnectedness of solid bitumen as a sample groundmass at post peak oil to early condensate thermal maturity is made evident at the scale of SEM resolution (i.e., 20,000× magnification) in Figure 6D. However, we see no evidence for the interconnectedness of organic pores in solid bitumen80 and note that some of the SEM-visualized pores may be artifacts of sample preparation.81 The low abundance of SEM-visualized porosity in the immature to early condensate samples of this study is generally consistent with prior works which have evaluated porosity in Bakken shale samples via SEM.23,82−84

Discussion

Comparison of BRo to Other Thermal Proxies

In light of prior reports of VRo suppression in the Bakken Formation,41 experimental evidence for BRo suppression,50 and issues with the use of VRe conversions,14 we investigated whether BRo measurements could be used directly as an unmodified thermal proxy. No recalculation to a corresponding VRe value or correction for anticipated suppression85 appears to be necessary. In this section, we compare unmodified BRo values from unextracted samples to thermal proxies from programmed temperature pyrolysis (HI, PI, and Tmax), isoprenoid analysis of extractable organic matter (Pr/n-C17, Ph/n-C18), and FTIR spectroscopy peak ratios (branching ratio, A-factor) to make the case that BRo values can be used without correction for suppression and without recalculation to a VRe value in marine source-rock reservoirs such as the Bakken Formation.

Thermal proxies from programmed temperature pyrolysis are widely used for thermal maturity estimation, primarily due to their low cost and time efficiency relative to other methods.86,87 In particular, Tmax,87 HI,88 and PI57 have widespread application as thermal proxies, with certain values recognized as boundaries for hydrocarbon thermal maturity windows for source rocks containing primarily oil-prone Type II marine kerogen, e.g., immature Tmax < 435 °C, early mature 435 to 445 °C, peak mature 445 to 450 °C, late mature 450 to 470 °C, and overmature >470 °C.65 Unmodified BRo values show a strong positive correlation to Tmax values in this work, with linear and logarithmic r2 values of 0.91 (Figure 7A). Similarly, BRo shows strong logarithmic (r2 = 0.94) and linear (r2 = 0.92) correlation to HI (Figure 7B), and to PI (logarithmic r2 = 0.93, linear r2 = 0.90, Figure 7C). Conversion of the BRo values to VRe values using, for example, the widely cited empirical conversion of Jacob89 does not change the correlative relationships, but it does result in calculated VRe values of 0.59 to 0.70% for samples from wells F190 and F194 in Montana. These VRe values are indicative of thermally mature source rock, which could be presumed to source commercial hydrocarbon accumulations,18 particularly in the organic-rich Bakken shale, which contains hydrogen-rich Type II organic matter. Wells F190 and F194 are in Sheridan County, which has overall produced little oil associated with the lower and upper Bakken shale and adjacent strata69 relative to elsewhere in the Williston Basin. Examination of available production data indicates that F190 (BRo values 0.31 to 0.38%, VRe values 0.59 to 0.64%) is approximately 25 miles from active Bakken-Three Forks production, suggesting a potential overestimation of thermal maturity through the VRe conversion. Moreover, low Tmax (420 to 427 °C), low PI values (0.04 to 0.05), and the absence of SEM-visualized thermally generated organic porosity (Figure 6A) confirm the immature conditions. However, well F194 (BRo values 0.41 to 0.48%, VRe values 0.65 to 0.70%) is only approximately 2 mi from active Bakken-Three Forks production. Lateral migration in the overall impermeable Bakken section is generally thought to be negligible,90 so the presumption for active wells nearby to well F194 is that the produced hydrocarbons are sourced from the shale members of the Bakken Formation. Here, Tmax values of 432 to 438 °C also suggest early oil window thermal conditions. Therefore, based on proximity to active production, unmodified BRo values of 0.31 to 0.38% appear to indicate immature conditions, whereas BRo values of 0.41 to 0.48% suggest early oil window thermal maturity. There are no studies which have examined correction criteria for BRo suppression, and the only work to provide correction criteria for VRo suppression85 relies on the estimation of an original HI value, introducing additional uncertainty. Therefore, in light of the strong correlation between unmodified BRo values and the thermal proxies from programmed temperature pyrolysis, as well as the potential overestimation of VRe at low thermal maturity in the Bakken system for well F190, our results suggest the use of unmodified BRo values (along with other thermal proxies from geochemical analysis) as a best practice for estimation of thermal maturity in the Bakken Formation when such data is available. Based on the data of this study, unmodified BRo values >0.41% appear to define the approximate threshold value for the earliest possible entrance to the commercial oil window in the Bakken Formation.

As noted earlier, isoprenoid ratios Pr/n-C17 and Ph/n-C18 show a strongly correlated linear relationship (r2 = 0.94) interpreted as a thermal effect from the liberation of isoprenoids during maturation, which results in a ratio decrease.91,92 When isoprenoid ratios are evaluated in relation to unmodified BRo values, moderate logarithmic correlations are observed, with r2 values of 0.70 for Pr/n-C17 (Figure 7D) and 0.65 for Ph/n-C18 (Figure 7E). Isoprenoid ratios also show moderate correlation to the previously discussed thermal proxies from programmed temperature pyrolysis (Tmax, HI, and PI), with logarithmic r2 values ranging from 0.53 to 0.68 (e.g., Figure 7F,G). Finally, unmodified BRo values show a moderate positive linear correlation to the branching ratio (CH3/CH2) values from bulk-rock FTIR spectroscopy (r2 = 0.64, Figure 7H) and a moderate negative linear correlation to A-factor values (r2 = 0.68, Figure 7I). We earlier noted arguments against the usage of BRo as a thermal proxy, including heterogeneous textures, the possibility of solid bitumen migration, and the confusion of its identity with that of other sedimentary organic matter types.20,21 However, in this study, we selected the dominant solid bitumen population for measurement to reduce heterogeneity and confusion of organic matter identity, and the shale members of the Bakken Formation are widely regarded as “tight formations”,36,37,93 hedging against migration of extraneous solid bitumen into the petroleum system. As noted above, the apparent overestimation of VRe in well F190 using the influential conversion equation of Jacob89 suggests against the conversion of BRo measurements to an equivalent vitrinite reflectance value. Other less influential conversion equations, such as those cited in Sanders et al.,50 give lower values for VRe when applied to our BRo data but are not as widely used or may not be relevant to the Bakken Formation. As noted by Zain and Rimmer,14 even conversion equations which appear relevant to a particular petroleum system may give erroneous or unsuitable results. Therefore, the observance of moderate to strong correlative relationships between measured BRo values in this study and thermal proxies from a suite of other approaches suggests the usage of unmodified BRo as a best practice for the determination of thermal maturity in the Bakken Formation, as opposed to the transformation of BRo to VRe. Future work in the Bakken and similar solid bitumen-rich Paleozoic marine source rocks could help to better define and establish thermal maturity thresholds based on unmodified BRo values—a thermal proxy which can be directly measured—rather than applying empirical conversions that may or may not be appropriate and which can introduce additional uncertainty. To restate, usage of a calculated VRe-based approach to thermal maturity determination: (1) may introduce errors that are present in the calibration data set, (2) may not be appropriate given the applicability of the original data, (3) may introduce an obvious overestimation, as shown herein, and (4) may simply be unnecessary when BRo can be used as a direct thermal proxy without transformation.

Extraction Effects on Solid Bitumen

It is widely recognized that solvent extraction prior to programmed temperature pyrolysis analysis can help improve interpretation, particularly in cases where samples may be contaminated by oil migration or by exposure to oil-based drilling mud.67 Solvent extraction is therefore a common approach in geochemical screening analysis for petroleum system studies.7,45,68,94,95 On the other hand, petrographic studies of organic-rich source rock samples pre- and post-extraction to observe extraction effects on organic matter are less common.96 Prior work by Barker et al.97 tested the hypothesis of bitumen impregnation as an agent of reflectance suppression by measuring VRo pre- and post-extraction in a global suite of approximately 60 samples, including source rocks, coals, and pyrolysis residues of coal. Note that the term “bitumen” has an operational definition, meaning a component of sedimentary organic matter which is extractable via typical organic solvents, whereas “solid bitumen” is a petrographically identified solid or semisolid viscous petroleum which may have variable extraction properties, i.e., a given solid bitumen population in a sample may or may not be extractable, or an individual solid bitumen accumulation may have microscopic domains which are extractable and other domains which are not.7 The work by Barker et al.97 concluded solvent extraction made no discernible difference in VRo that could be detected outside of the intrinsic uncertainty in reflectance measurement. Similar work by Wenger and Baker98 also found no detectable difference in the reflectance of pre- and post-extracted Pennsylvanian shale samples, whereas, studies by Pickel and Götz99 and Veld et al.100 measured clear increases in VRo in Pennsylvanian coals caused by solvent extraction, although only one of four coals in the latter study showed a post-extraction VRo increase. In this study, we performed organic solvent extractions to observe the effect on measured BRo values and geochemical screening data. As previously noted, BRo values for extracted samples are generally slightly lower than BRo values determined on the same sample prior to extraction, e.g., BRo in the extracted sample F190 7576.7 is 0.09% lower than in the original unextracted sample. Although the BRo of the extracted sample is not always lower than the BRo of the unextracted sample (Table 3), we found that when BRo values are averaged from a given well, the mean BRo of the average for the extracted samples is consistently lower than the mean BRo of the unextracted samples (Figure 8). However, mean values overlap within the standard deviation in most cases, and values from the t-test suggest the pre- and post-extraction BRo means from each well are not significantly different. As noted above, BRo values from unextracted and extracted samples were measured by the same petrographer in this study using the same methodology on the same microscope system within a time frame of about 1.5 years. Moreover, BRo data from the extracted samples were collected using a blind experiment methodology, wherein the BRo value for the unextracted sample was not consulted during measurement of the extracted sample. Nevertheless, although the systematic small decrease in average BRo value from each well (Figure 8) may be a real and measurable effect of solvent extraction, sample heterogeneity and measurement error prevent recognition of a statistically significant difference in BRo caused by the extraction procedure.

Comparison of pre-extracted (BRo) to post-extracted (BRo ext.) average BRo values for each well. Wells are listed in order of increasing depth. Error bars show the standard deviation of the average BRo value for each well.

As previously described, solvent extraction causes significant effects on parameters measured by the carbon analyzer and programmed temperature pyrolysis, including decreases in TOC, S1, and S2 values, and an increase to Tmax.101 We note that these changes are due to the removal of organic components—i.e., mostly n-alkanes and isoprenoids, as measured by GC—from solid bitumen, which is the dominant organic component as observed petrographically. Moreover, extraction is presumably from discrete submicroscopic domains within the overall solid bitumen component and is not a consequence of the extraction of a certain microscopically observable population of solid bitumen. That is, although some pre-extraction heterogeneity of solid bitumen was observed, the extraction did not cause a certain population of solid bitumen to disappear or cause a certain presentation of solid bitumen to become more abundant7 at least as resolvable by optical microscopy. We also note that extraction effects vary with thermal maturity, as determined by BRo, with the percentage of S1 extracted showing a moderately positive logarithmic relation to BRo (r2 = 0.58), which improves to r2 = 0.70 when the single sample with the lowest percentage of original S1 extracted (83.2%) is removed from consideration. The percentage of original S2 extracted also shows a moderately positive logarithmic relationship to BRo (r2 = 0.54), which improves to 0.71 when the average percentage of S2 extracted for samples from a given well is compared to the average BRo from that well. These final observations support and reaffirm our earlier observations of the usage of unmodified BRo as a valid method for thermal maturity determination in Paleozoic marine source rocks.

The kerogen H/C ratios were estimated based on the relationship between this parameter and HI using data collected by Lewan et al.102 and used for comparison to Eagle Ford Shale kerogens by French et al.68 This summary figure demonstrates how bulk organic geochemical and kerogen elemental parameters scale with BRo through maturation into the late oil window in the Bakken Formation. No discernible difference was observed between source rocks from the informal lower and upper members of the Bakken Formation, so the same scales are expected to apply to both. Summary

The use of BRo as a thermal maturity proxy in the shale members of the Bakken Formation was investigated through an integrated petrographic and geochemical study, including measurement of BRo, geochemical screening analysis (TOC and HAWK), isoprenoid/n-alkane analysis via GC, FTIR spectroscopy, SEM visualization, and low-pressure gas adsorption. Data from these approaches resulted in the following observations:

Unmodified BRo values (not corrected to a VRe value) defined three thermal maturity groupings: immature to early oil window (BRo 0.31 ± 0.05 to 0.53 ± 0.06%); early peak oil (0.61 ± 0.08 to 0.78 ± 0.11%); and post peak oil to early condensate (1.05 ± 0.15 to 1.29 ± 0.14%).
