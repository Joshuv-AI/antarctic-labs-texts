Afebrile Culture-Negative Vertebral Osteomyelitis in a Healthy Korean Male
Authors: Sung-Woon Kang, Joon Seon Song, Hyo Joo Lee, Yong Shin, Sung-Han Kim
Published: 2026

Dear Editor:

A previously healthy 53-year-old male presented with a two-month history of hip and lower back pain. He lived in Jecheon but worked nationwide delivering animal feed. At symptom onset, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed high signal intensity in the L5 vertebra. Although the symptoms did not clearly worsen over the next two months and the patient was managed with observation alone without specific treatment, repeat MRI demonstrated an increased marrow signal at L5 with an epidural abscess (Fig. 1A). Labs were notable only for leukocytosis (10,900/mm3); C-reactive protein (CRP) was normal, and interferon-gamma releasing assay was negative. Core needle biopsy was negative for Gram/acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stains and cultures, Xpert MTB/RIF, Grocott-Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) staining, and 16S rRNA sequencing. Histopathological examination revealed chronic inflammation without granuloma formation, consistent with chronic osteomyelitis.

After three weeks of empirical cefazolin treatment, back pain worsened (Numeric rating scale, NRS 7–8). MRI showed progression of osteomyelitis and an epidural abscess. The patient underwent surgical decompression and was switched to piperacillin/tazobactam therapy. Gram/AFB strains and cultures, GMS, 16S rRNA sequencing, and Xpert MTB/RIF performed on the specimens were still negative. After 18 days of piperacillin/tazobactam treatment, the patient presented with fever and rash, accompanied by elevated CRP (5.03 mg/dL) and eosinophilia (517/mm3). MRI showed no further disease progression. Drug fever was suspected and the antibiotic was changed to meropenem. After 10 days of meropenem treatment, the patient reported symptomatic improvement with a CRP 0.62 mg/dL.

However, six weeks later, he presented again with fever and back pain. MRI revealed worsening of the vertebral osteomyelitis and an epidural abscess (Fig. 1B). Labs showed leukocytosis (11,300/mm3) and CRP 3.49 mg/dL. Epidural abscess aspiration yielded negative bacterial, fungal, and mycobacterial cultures as well as 16S rRNA sequencing results. Repeat biopsy and serological tests were performed.

Diagnosis: Q fever vertebral osteomyelitis

Serology for Coxiella burnetii revealed a phase I IgG titer of 1:8,192 and a phase II IgG titer of 1:2,048, whereas serology for Brucellosis was negative. Histopathology revealed doughnut-shaped granulomatous inflammation without necrosis, suggestive of Q fever (Fig. 1C). polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detected C. burnetii DNA in the tissue samples (Fig. 1D). No extravertebral lesions were identified on positron emission tomography/computed tomography or transthoracic echocardiography. After three months of doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine treatment, the MRI findings (Fig. 1E) and pain improved (NRS 1).

Q fever primarily affects domestic ruminants, and humans typically acquire the infection through inhalation of aerosolized particles shed by infected animals [1]. Osteoarticular involvement is the third most common manifestation of chronic Q fever [2]. In adults, osteoarticular Q fever most commonly affects the lumbar spine, with 30% of cases arising from the extension of adjacent vascular infection [34].

In addition to serologic testing, the most useful diagnostic tool for osteoarticular Q fever is PCR testing of the affected bone or joint tissue, followed by PCR testing of blood and, to a lesser extent, direct culture of the infected tissue. Although next generation sequencing is not yet a part of the standard diagnostic approach for Q fever, emerging reports indicate that it may be a useful adjunctive modality in selected culture-negative and diagnostically challenging cases, particularly when unusual or fastidious pathogens are suspected [5]. The prognosis of osteoarticular Q fever is generally favorable in isolated cases but is markedly worse when accompanied by endocarditis or vascular involvement [24]. Treatment follows the standard regimen for chronic Q fever: doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine for 18 months (or 24 months when prosthesis is present) [1].

According to the Korean national surveillance data, the highest prevalence of Q fever was observed in Chungcheong province, followed by Jeolla and South Gyeongsang provinces [6]. Only three cases of osteoarticular Q fever were reported [6], suggesting that the disease may be substantially underdiagnosed. This underscores the importance of thorough history-taking, particularly regarding occupational, residential, and animal exposure. In patients with culture-negative vertebral osteomyelitis, this information may provide important clues for suspected Q fever and prompt appropriate serological or molecular testing.

In conclusion, osteoarticular Q fever should be considered in patients with culture-negative vertebral osteomyelitis in regions where Q fever is endemic.
