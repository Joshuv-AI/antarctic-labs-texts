Alcohol addiction and Alzheimer’s disease: a molecular collision course
Authors: Jia-Sheng Chang, He-Zhou Huang, Mei Yuan, Yuan Zhou, Dan Liu, Ke-Bin Zhan, Ling-Qiang Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic alcohol consumption is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), contributing to cognitive decline through multiple biological pathways. Excessive alcohol intake accelerates neurodegeneration by impairing the brain’s ability to clear toxic proteins, disrupting neurotransmitter balance, and exacerbating inflammation and oxidative stress. These effects collectively weaken neuronal resilience, making the brain more vulnerable to AD-related damage. Emerging therapeutic strategies focus on mitigating alcohol-induced harm through neuroprotective drugs, inflammation-targeting treatments, and neurotransmitter modulators. Lifestyle-based interventions, including early abstinence, cognitive training, and precision nutrition, also show promise in reducing risk and slowing disease progression. Future research should prioritize personalized treatment approaches and novel drug delivery methods to improve outcomes for individuals affected by both conditions.Subject terms: Drug discovery, Physiology, Addiction

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and alcohol use disorder (AUD) are two major contributors to cognitive decline, and their pathological interactions have garnered increasing attention. In this review, we will explore the pathological mechanisms and intervention strategies related to alcohol misuse. It is important to note that the term ‘Alcohol Addiction’ typically denotes a severe, chronic condition characterized by compulsive alcohol use, craving, loss of control, and continued drinking despite significant negative consequences [1]. However, contemporary medical diagnostics employ the more precise and encompassing term ‘Alcohol Use Disorder’ (AUD). AUD is a spectrum diagnosis (according to DSM-5) [2], categorized as mild, moderate, or severe based on the severity of a constellation of behavioral, cognitive, and physiological symptoms (e.g., loss of control, craving, tolerance, withdrawal, and social or occupational impairment due to drinking). Therefore, ‘Alcohol Addiction’ can be understood as representing the core features or serving as a synonym for the severe subtype of AUD. This review will predominantly employ the standardized term ‘AUD’. However, the concept of ‘addiction’ may be invoked when specifically discussing the core pathological features of severe dependence and loss of control [1].

Beyond the shared molecular pathology highlighted below, AUD and AD exhibit significant convergence at the level of neural circuitry dysfunction and clinical manifestations. Both disorders prominently impact prefrontal cortical regions critical for executive function and inhibitory control, leading to overlapping deficits in decision-making, planning, and behavioral regulation [3, 4]. Critically, beyond these shared features and pathological interactions, accumulating epidemiological evidence strongly suggests that AUD itself constitutes a significant independent risk factor for the development of both Alzheimer’s disease and AD-like dementia [5–7]. Furthermore, large-scale brain networks, such as the default mode network (DMN) involved in memory and self-referential thought, show altered connectivity in both conditions [8]. Clinically, while core symptoms differ (e.g., amnesia in AD vs. craving in AUD), significant overlap exists in domains like executive dysfunction, emotional dysregulation (e.g., apathy, irritability), and sleep disturbances [9, 10]. Critically, both disorders are associated with systemic and central nervous system low-grade inflammation, a shared pathophysiological mechanism contributing to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline [11].

Recent studies have confirmed that prolonged alcohol exposure may accelerate neurodegeneration by modulating key pathological hallmarks of AD, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, and neuroinflammatory pathways [12, 13]. Clinical biomarker studies indicate that AUD patients exhibit significantly elevated levels of AD-related biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A prospective cohort study involving 252 elderly individuals found that alcohol consumption was negatively correlated with CSF Aβ42 levels and positively associated with phosphorylated tau (P-tau) levels, suggesting that alcohol may exacerbate AD pathology by impairing Aβ clearance and promoting tau hyperphosphorylation [14]. In APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mouse models, ten weeks of moderate ethanol exposure not only increased the number of cortical amyloid plaques but also shifted plaque size distribution toward smaller aggregates, potentially providing a pathological basis for subsequent plaque proliferation [12]. Notably, chronic ethanol consumption-induced excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) imbalance may synergistically promote Aβ deposition, as evidenced by significantly increased hippocampal N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) mRNA levels and decreased γ-aminobutyric acid type-A receptor (GABAAR) mRNA expression [12].

There is considerable controversy regarding the dose-dependent effects of alcohol exposure. Moderate ethanol exposure (20–30 mM) has demonstrated neuroprotective effects by mitigating Aβ-induced neuronal calcium overload and upregulating heat shock proteins (HSP70, HSP27), thereby reducing oxidative stress-related damage [15]. In APP/PS1 mice, low-dose ethanol exposure significantly reduced Aβ plaque burden by lowering cortical ApoE levels and inhibiting the IKK-α/β-NF-κB pathway [16]. However, chronic excessive alcohol consumption (≥100 mM) impairs microglial phagocytosis of Aβ by activating the TLR4/p38 MAPK pathway, while its metabolite acetaldehyde induces significant upregulation of BACE1-AS expression in astrocytes, leading to increased Aβ1-42 production [13, 17]. This dose-dependent effect suggests a complex molecular regulatory network underlying alcohol’s impact on AD pathology.

The induction and perpetuation of chronic, low-grade neuroinflammation represent a core shared pathological axis through which AUD critically exacerbates AD progression [18]. Neuroinflammation and metabolic dysregulation play pivotal roles in alcohol-mediated AD pathology. Acute ethanol exposure significantly reduces perivascular AQP4 polarization, leading to decreased Aβ clearance efficiency, whereas chronic intake exacerbates neuroinflammation by inducing astrocytic GFAP expression [13, 19]. Metabolic-immune dysregulation exhibits sex-specific differences; ethanol exposure enhances perivascular microglial Aβ phagocytic activity in aged female rats, but this effect is absent in males [20]. These findings suggest that alcohol addiction exacerbates AD pathology through multidimensional molecular mechanisms, including E/I imbalance, glial cell polarization abnormalities, and epigenetic modifications. Integrating insights across these molecular mechanisms with the broader dysfunctions in neural circuits, networks, and clinical manifestations is essential for developing effective interventions targeting the complex AUD-AD comorbidity. This study systematically explores the molecular regulatory network of alcohol addiction in AD pathology, aiming to provide a new theoretical framework and research direction for targeted interventions.

Core mechanisms of alcohol-mediated AD pathology

Hippocampal degeneration and neuronal loss

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction

Alcohol exposure induces excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and disrupts antioxidant defense mechanisms, leading to significantly elevated oxidative stress levels in the hippocampus (Fig. 1). Chronic alcohol intake (10 g/kg/day for 10 weeks) increases the levels of lipid peroxidation marker malondialdehyde (MDA) while reducing total antioxidant capacity [21]. When ethanol (5 g/kg) is co-administered with manganese (30 mg/kg) for 35 days, oxidative/nitrosative stress is further exacerbated, as evidenced by significantly elevated levels of 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT) [22].

The mechanisms underlying alcohol-induced neuronal damage involve mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 1). Binge alcohol consumption (4 g/kg per session) disrupts oxidative phosphorylation by inhibiting mRNA expression of mitochondrial complex IV and V, leading to reduced ATP production [23]. Continuous ethanol exposure (15% v/v for 28 days) significantly upregulates dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) activity, increasing mitochondrial fission frequency while downregulating mitofusin 2 (Mfn2) expression [24]. This imbalance in mitochondrial dynamics ultimately results in mitochondrial membrane potential reduction and impaired calcium buffering capacity [23]. This metabolic crisis impairs synaptic plasticity within the hippocampus, a region critical for memory formation and consolidation, processes severely disrupted in AD.

At the molecular level, alcohol exposure activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, which acts synergistically with the dysregulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant axis. Ethanol combined with restraint stress (6 h per day for 28 days) upregulates NF-κB expression while downregulating Nrf2 and HO-1 levels in the hippocampus [25]. In ALDH2-deficient mouse models, binge alcohol consumption (4 g/kg) increases 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)-modified protein levels, exacerbating mitochondrial DNA oxidative damage [26]. However, conflicting evidence exists: melatonin (20 mg/kg) reverses ethanol-induced glutathione (GSH) depletion and suppresses excessive NF-κB activation, but neither melatonin nor U83836E could prevent Purkinje cell loss in neonatal rats exposed to alcohol, suggesting that oxidative stress may not be the sole pathogenic mechanism [25, 27].

Inhibition of neurogenesis

Alcohol addiction significantly suppresses hippocampal neurogenesis through multiple molecular mechanisms, involving three interrelated biological processes (Fig. 1).

First, neuroinflammation plays a crucial role by inducing progenitor cell apoptosis. The adolescent intermittent ethanol exposure (AIE) model demonstrates a significant reduction in the number of doublecortin (DCX)-positive neurons in the dentate gyrus (DG) region, accompanied by an increased proportion of cleaved caspase-3-positive apoptotic cells [28]. This process is closely associated with the dose-dependent upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors such as CCL2, HMGB1, and TNFα, wherein aberrant activation of M1-type microglia directly inhibits neurogenesis by disrupting the self-renewal microenvironment of neural stem cells (NSCs) [28, 29].

Second, the dynamic balance between cholinergic and glutamatergic systems plays a key role in regulation. In a prenatal ethanol exposure model, there is a significant reduction in reelin-positive interneurons in the DG hilus, accompanied by aberrant upregulation of the NMDA receptor NR2B subunit, which is directly linked to the suppression of progenitor cell proliferation [30]. In vitro experiments confirm that when astrocytes are exposed to 75 mM ethanol, neurite outgrowth in co-cultured neurons is significantly inhibited, while choline pre-treatment completely reverses this neurotoxic effect [31].

Additionally, epigenetic regulation and specific receptor activation are also involved. Acute ethanol binge (5 g/kg) reduces the number of DCX-positive immature neurons and enhances apoptotic signaling via cannabinoid CB1 receptor activation, an effect that is specifically reversed by the CB1 receptor antagonist AM251 [32]. Notably, intervention with the RPTPβ/ζ inhibitor MY10 suggests that ethanol-induced neurogenesis impairment may involve a regulatory pathway independent of glial cell responses [33]. The suppression of hippocampal neurogenesis contributes to the volumetric loss observed in this region in AD and is thought to underlie aspects of learning and memory deficits.

Regarding intervention strategies, pharmacological studies provide crucial evidence. The cholinesterase inhibitor galantamine (4 mg/kg) prevents and reverses AIE-induced reductions in DCX-immunoreactive cells while significantly downregulating caspase-3 activity [28]. Moreover, dexmedetomidine pre-treatment (125 μg/kg) effectively restores neural progenitor cell numbers following ethanol exposure by inhibiting microglial activation [34].

Neurotransmitter system imbalance

Synergistic effects of cholinergic deficiency

Alcohol addiction impairs cholinergic system function through multiple pathways, exhibiting a synergistic effect with cholinergic deficits observed in AD.

One key mechanism involves abnormal cholinergic enzyme activity. Long-term ethanol exposure (0.5% concentration for 7 or 14 days) significantly inhibits choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity in the zebrafish brain [35]. In an ethanol-induced dementia mouse model, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity increases, whereas cholinergic supplementation (e.g., fermented Protaetia brevitarsis larval extract) reverses this effect by reducing AChE activity and increasing acetylcholine (ACh) levels [36].

Regarding neuronal degeneration, both AD patients and AUD patients exhibit reductions in basal forebrain cholinergic neurons and fiber networks [37]. Furthermore, epigenetic cholinergic deficits can be observed across generations: offspring (F1/F2) of ethanol-exposed mother mice display reduced ACh synthesis and memory impairments [38].

Intervention strategies indicate that soy isoflavones (40 mg/kg) improve cognitive impairment by modulating AChE activity and enhancing synaptic plasticity protein expression [39]. Similarly, Cajanus cajan ethanol extract (200 mg/kg) exerts its effects by inhibiting AChE activity (IC50 = 0.001 μL/min) and enhancing cholinergic signaling, with pinostrobin as an active component exhibiting a binding affinity of −8.7 kcal/mol to muscarinic receptors [40].

Notably, ethanol’s effects on the cholinergic system vary depending on dosage and duration. Short-term exposure (7 days) inhibits ChAT activity, whereas no significant changes are detected after 28 days. Moreover, some interventions (e.g., FL50 extract) exhibit greater efficacy in ethanol-induced models compared to scopolamine-induced models [41].

The key pathways underlying these effects involve ethanol-induced direct inhibition of ChAT, upregulation of AChE activity, and epigenetic regulation (e.g., disruption of NGF metabolism), forming a synergistic interaction with Aβ toxicity in AD [37, 38]. This synergistic cholinergic deficit disrupts the function of the septohippocampal pathway and cortical projections from the basal forebrain, key circuits for attention and episodic memory, which are profoundly impaired in AD.

Disruption of GABA/Glutamate homeostasis

Alcohol disrupts the dynamic balance of the GABA/glutamate system through multi-level neural regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 1). The main characteristics of this disruption include the following: acute alcohol exposure significantly enhances GABA release in the central amygdala (CeA), whereas chronic intake leads to a sustained increase in baseline GABAergic tone [42, 43]. This process is dependent on the activation of corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) type 1 receptors, as CRF antagonists completely block ethanol-induced GABAergic enhancement [44].

In the ventral tegmental area (VTA), chronic ethanol intake inhibits GABAergic input via presynaptic GABAB receptors and reduces the activity of G-protein-gated inwardly rectifying potassium (GIRK) channels [45]. Notably, alcohol’s effects on glutamatergic transmission exhibit regional specificity: acute exposure decreases the amplitude of glutamatergic postsynaptic potentials in the CeA, whereas chronic intake increases glutamatergic transmission via upregulation of NMDA receptor function [42]. However, in ethanol-dependent rat models, the density of glutamatergic synapses in the nucleus accumbens (NAc) is significantly reduced, accompanied by a weakening of NMDA receptor currents [46]. In VTA dopamine neurons, ethanol enhances glutamate release via D1 dopamine receptor activation, forming a positive feedback regulatory loop [47].

Existing studies have revealed the complexity of molecular regulation in this process. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) inhibits GABA release in the CeA via Y2 receptors, while interleukin-1β (IL-1β) enhances GABAergic transmission only in ethanol-dependent individuals [48, 49]. In the glutamate regulatory pathway, GABAergic synapses in the dorsolateral striatum mediate ethanol effects via δ-opioid receptors, whereas ethanol-induced glutamatergic inhibition in the CeA depends on the interaction between nociceptin and the NOP receptor system [50, 51]. During prolonged withdrawal, presynaptic GABA release in the CeA develops tolerance, while compensatory increases in glutamate release occur in the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus [43].

Collectively, available evidence suggests that alcohol primarily disrupts excitatory-inhibitory balance through CRF-GABA interactions, NMDA receptor sensitization, and presynaptic receptor modulation (D1/GABAB/Y2). Furthermore, neuropeptide system dysregulation during withdrawal exacerbates the irreversible progression of pathological changes. This dysregulation within limbic circuits, particularly the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, manifests clinically as impaired executive function, emotional lability, and heightened anxiety, symptoms commonly overlapping in AUD and AD.

Effects of alcohol on dopamine, serotonin, and other neurotransmitters

Alcohol exacerbates neurotransmitter imbalances by disrupting the dynamic equilibrium of the dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT) systems, thereby accelerating the pathological progression of Alzheimer’s disease. Experimental evidence demonstrates that chronic ethanol exposure significantly reduces dopamine release in the NAc, accompanied by an increased dopamine reuptake rate and enhanced D2 autoreceptor activity [52]. At the receptor level, adolescent intermittent ethanol exposure (AIE) has been shown to impair the ability of medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) D1 receptors to regulate glutamatergic signaling in adulthood, while D2 receptor function remains unaffected [53]. Genetic studies further indicate that reduced striatal D2 receptor availability in alcohol-dependent individuals is significantly correlated with the severity of clinical symptoms [54].

In the 5-HT system, the 5-HTTLPR gene polymorphism has been found to influence drug response: individuals with the S/S genotype exhibit poorer anxiolytic responses to SSRIs, whereas those with the L/L genotype are more sensitive to 5-HT3 receptor antagonists [55]. Animal studies show that Tph2-deficient mice, which have impaired central 5-HT synthesis, consume significantly more ethanol than wild-type mice, suggesting an inhibitory role of the 5-HT system in alcohol consumption behavior. Studies on prenatal alcohol exposure indicate that alcohol suppresses placental SERT mRNA expression while upregulating TPH1 protein levels, leading to abnormally elevated fetal circulating 5-HT concentrations [56, 57].

Notably, different experimental conditions may yield conflicting results. In zebrafish models, short-term binge drinking (3 weeks) significantly enhances DAT activity, whereas long-term exposure leads to compensatory downregulation of DAT function [58, 59]. Additionally, sex differences have been observed in ethanol intake: female SERT-knockout (SERT-/-) rats consume significantly more alcohol than males, suggesting a sexually dimorphic regulation of the 5-HT system [60].

Neuroinflammation and Glial Cell activation

Microglial M1 polarization

Alcohol addiction significantly exacerbates neuroinflammatory responses by activating the M1 polarization phenotype of microglia (Fig. 1). Studies have shown that chronic alcohol exposure activates microglia through the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to increased release of pro-inflammatory factors. Alcohol exposure upregulates TLR4 expression, increasing the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the hippocampus, accompanied by an increase in Iba-1 positive microglial cell density [61, 62]. Pharmacological intervention with sodium butyrate has been shown to significantly reduce the expression of the M1 marker CD86 and the levels of pro-inflammatory factors, while increasing the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 [61]. Further studies revealed that alcohol-induced microglial secretion of MCP-1 can activate GSK3β via the CCR2 receptor, promoting neuronal apoptosis. Blocking MCP-1 synthesis (Bindarit) or CCR2 (RS504393) effectively reduces apoptotic body numbers [62]. At the mechanistic level of polarization, alcohol-activated GSK3β has been found to drive microglial transition to the M1 phenotype, with minocycline significantly reducing pro-inflammatory factor release by inhibiting GSK3β activity [63].

It is worth noting that there are dose-dependent effects in related studies: short-term alcohol exposure (4 days) only induces partial activation of microglia, without causing an upregulation of IL-6 or TNF-α levels. In contrast, chronic ethanol exposure over 6 months increases IL-15 expression in the hippocampus, yet no significant changes in classical inflammatory markers are observed [64]. Regarding intervention strategies, the PDE4b inhibitor Rolipram can reverse ethanol-induced microglial activation and reduce TLR2/NF-κB pathway activity [65]. More in-depth research has shown that depletion and subsequent regeneration of microglia completely eliminates TNF-α accumulation induced by chronic ethanol exposure while restoring baseline IL-10 levels [66]. Existing evidence suggests that alcohol drives microglial M1 polarization through multiple synergistic pathways, with its effects regulated by the duration and dose of exposure. This highlights the need for a stratified analysis approach when assessing alcohol-related neuroinflammatory risks. Sustained M1 microglial activation contributes to synaptic stripping and neuronal damage within vulnerable brain networks, such as the default mode network, whose dysfunction is a hallmark of cognitive impairment in AD.

Reactive astrogliosis

Alcohol exposure significantly regulates reactive astrogliosis in a dose- and sex-dependent manner, with mechanisms involving inflammation pathway inhibition and cytoskeletal remodeling (Fig. 1). In male mouse models, chronic ethanol exposure results in a decrease in GFAP-positive cell density in the hippocampus, a phenomenon related to LRP1-mediated suppression of the IKK-α/β-NF-κB pathway, accompanied by a reduction in IL-1β and TNF-α levels [16]. In female mice, a decrease in S100β protein levels in the anterior cingulate cortex is observed, along with upregulation of TNF expression and compensatory increases in the neuroprotective factor TGF-β1, suggesting that sex differences may influence the functional homeostasis of astrocytes [67]. In terms of dose effects, acute binge ethanol exposure (4 days, 3 g/kg) enhances GFAP immunoreactivity in the brainstem trigeminal spinal tract but completely abolishes GFAP signaling in cerebellar Bergmann glial cells [68]. Chronic low-dose exposure (10% ethanol for 4 weeks) induces hypertrophy of dorsal hippocampal astrocytes and an increase in GFAP expression, but after 36 weeks of long-term exposure, GFAP levels decrease [69].

Intervention studies have shown that activation of the Gq signaling pathway in the nucleus accumbens astrocytes can specifically reverse the extinction deficit in ethanol-dependent female mice, with therapeutic effects related to GFAP expression levels [70]. During withdrawal, a significant increase in glutamine synthetase-positive astrocyte density in the anterior cingulate cortex was observed, suggesting that astrocytes may influence withdrawal symptoms by regulating glutamate metabolism [71]. It is important to note that conflicting dose-response phenomena exist between different species models: after 21 days of intervention with 20% ethanol, GFAP levels in the cortex of APP/PS1 mice decreased, while the same dose increased the GFAP-positive area in the prefrontal cortex of Wistar rats [16, 72]. These phenotypic differences may be due to species-specific regulatory mechanisms.

Indirect regulation of Aβ/Tau pathology

Mechanisms of Tau hyperphosphorylation

Ethanol exposure regulates the phosphorylation process of tau protein through multiple converging pathways (Fig. 1). Chronic excessive ethanol exposure (≥3 g/kg/day) significantly upregulates glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK-3β) and cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5) activity in a dose-dependent manner, directly inducing tau phosphorylation at Ser396/Ser404 [73, 74]. Although low-dose ethanol (≤1 g/kg/day) does not immediately enhance tau phosphorylation, prolonged exposure leads to a sustained increase in baseline kinase activity [74].

Acute ethanol intoxication (blood ethanol concentration ≥80 mg/dL) activates microglia via the TLR4/NF-κB signaling axis, triggering the release of extracellular cold-inducible RNA-binding protein (eCIRP), which subsequently induces a p38 MAPK phosphorylation cascade in neurons, markedly increasing tau phosphorylation at Thr231 [75]. In chronic exposure models, ethanol upregulates hippocampal IL-1β and IL-6 expression, activating the JNK pathway while inhibiting protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), thereby establishing a self-sustaining positive feedback loop for tau hyperphosphorylation [76].

Notably, perinatal ethanol exposure (50 mM for 5 days) results in persistent phosphorylation of tau at Thr231 in the developing hippocampus without a concurrent change in total tau expression levels [77]. Clinically, CSF analysis indicates that phosphorylated tau (p-tau181) levels in 11% of alcohol-dependent individuals with cognitive impairment meet the diagnostic criteria for AD, suggesting that alcohol may accelerate AD-specific tau pathology [78].

Regarding therapeutic interventions, compounds that modulate the GSK-3β/PP2A dynamic balance, such as ethyl acetate extract from Aralia elata, have demonstrated dual effects of reducing phosphorylated tau levels while inhibiting the JNK/BAX apoptotic pathway [79]. The accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau disrupts axonal transport and synaptic function in corticolimbic circuits, contributing to the progressive cognitive decline and memory failure characteristic of AD.

Dysregulation of Aβ metabolism

Ethanol exposure exhibits a dose-dependent influence on β-amyloid (Aβ) metabolism. Studies indicate that low-dose ethanol0o-p5 (<1.5 g/kg) decreases hippocampal neuronal sensitivity to Aβ toxicity, mitigating synaptic damage induced by Aβ oligomers via enhanced insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) signaling [73]. However, chronic excessive ethanol intake (≥3 g/kg/day) significantly increases amyloid plaque burden in the cortex and hippocampus of APP/PS1 transgenic mice, altering plaque size distribution and suggesting that ethanol may accelerate early Aβ aggregation [12].

Ethanol disrupts Aβ homeostasis through two primary mechanisms: first, it upregulates astrocytic β-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), promoting β-secretase-mediated cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) to generate Aβ1-42; second, it impairs low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1)-mediated Aβ efflux across the blood-brain barrier while suppressing microglial phagocytic activity, leading to reduced Aβ clearance [13, 16, 20].

Importantly, sex differences significantly influence ethanol’s effects on Aβ regulation. Chronic ethanol exposure enhances perivascular microglial phagocytosis of Aβ1-42 in aged female rats, whereas no such changes are observed in males [20]. Additionally, moderate ethanol consumption (equivalent to approximately two drinks per day) has been linked to cognitive improvement in Aβ-negative individuals but does not confer neuroprotection in Aβ-positive individuals [80].

Current research highlights two key contradictions: first, while both low-dose and moderate-dose ethanol exposure have been reported to ameliorate Aβ pathology, high-dose and prolonged exposure exhibit the opposite effect; second, peripheral metabolic dysregulation may exacerbate central Aβ deposition by disrupting peripheral-central Aβ homeostasis, with ethanol-induced hepatic dysfunction leading to impaired peripheral Aβ clearance being a proposed mechanism [12, 13, 16, 73, 74]. This interplay is further complicated by sex differences in central Aβ clearance pathways [20, 74].

Therapeutic strategies targeting Alcohol-AD interactions

Inhibitors of key signaling pathways

Dual inhibitors of p38 MAPK and GSK-3β

The roles of p38 MAPK and GSK-3β in mediating alcohol-induced AD pathology have been extensively studied. The selective p38α MAPK inhibitor MW150 (2.5 mg/kg/day, intraperitoneal injection for 14 days) has been shown to enhance long-term potentiation (LTP) induction and improve object-location associative memory in AD model mice [81]. Phenylsulfonamide derivative Sulfo (I), is N-(2-formylphenyl) benzene sulfonamide, inhibits p38α activity with a high binding free energy (−62.24 kcal/mol), and molecular dynamics simulations indicate superior conformational stability (RMSD < 2.0 Å) compared to existing co-crystal structures [82]. The nanodrug PH797804, administered intranasally, significantly reduces p38 MAPK activity and increases synaptophysin expression [83, 84].

Regarding GSK-3β modulation, flavonoid compound sanggenon C has been shown to inhibit GSK-3β activity (IC50 = 2.3 μM) and reduce Aβ-induced tau hyperphosphorylation [85]. Additionally, 2,4-dihydropyrano[2,3-c]pyrazole derivatives act as multitarget compounds, inhibiting GSK-3β (IC50 = 1.8 μM) while activating the Nrf2 pathway, leading to increased neuronal survival under oxidative stress conditions [86]. Magnesium supplementation has also demonstrated significant improvement in tau pathology by enhancing GSK-3β Ser9 phosphorylation [87].

Although research on dual p38 MAPK/GSK-3β inhibitors is still in its early stages, experimental evidence suggests their potential synergistic regulation: p38α MAPK activation enhances GSK-3β-mediated tau hyperphosphorylation, and a PTML-ANN model predicts an 85% accuracy for combined inhibition effects [88, 89]. Current research challenges primarily revolve around optimizing blood-brain barrier permeability and subtype selectivity (e.g., p38α vs. p38β), which may be addressed through nanodelivery approaches and structural optimization strategies to enhance clinical translation potential [82, 83].

TLR4 antagonists (TAK-242)

TLR4 antagonists, as key regulators of neuroinflammatory pathways, exhibit unique therapeutic potential in the interaction between alcohol addiction and AD. TAK-242 (Resatorvid) specifically binds to the lipopolysaccharide-binding domain of myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD-2), effectively blocking TLR4 dimerization and downstream NF-κB signaling activation. Experimental data indicate that this compound reduces microglial M1 polarization by up to 67% [90]. A structurally optimized derivative, (+)-N-phenethyl desmethylmorphan (1j), demonstrates significantly enhanced activity in BV-2 microglial cells, exhibiting 75-fold greater antagonistic efficacy than the classical antagonist (+)-naltrexone (IC50 = 1.4 μM), while maintaining a cytotoxicity ratio of 100:1 (IC50 = 140 μM) [91].

In the realm of structural modifications of natural products, tanshinone IIA biotransformation product 2 significantly inhibits lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced nitric oxide (NO) production (IC50 = 0.16 μM) and suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, displaying 20-fold higher anti-inflammatory activity than the parent compound [92]. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that its mechanism of action involves blocking the phosphorylation of JNK and p38 proteins in the MAPK pathway [92].

Structural optimization studies of dihydroartemisinin (DHA) indicate that compound 2k, modified at the C-10 position, exhibits a 14-fold increase in TLR4 antagonistic activity compared to DHA (IC50 = 0.354 μM) and prevents NF-κB activation by inhibiting TLR4 endocytosis [93]. Among chalcone derivatives, compounds 7w/7x form π-π interactions with the Phe151 residue of MD-2 (KD = 31.2–96.2 μM), significantly suppressing LPS-induced MAPK phosphorylation (JNK/ERK/p38) and pulmonary inflammatory infiltration [94].

However, existing studies present significant contradictions: clinical trials of Eritoran were terminated due to insufficient efficacy, and C16 chain-length compounds lost in vivo activity due to serum albumin binding, highlighting the importance of optimizing pharmacokinetic properties [95, 96]. Furthermore, different compounds exhibit distinct pathway selectivity; for instance, the DHA derivative 2k selectively inhibits NF-κB without affecting the MAPK pathway, whereas tanshinone IIA derivative 2 simultaneously targets both MAPK and NF-κB pathways [92, 93]. These findings underscore the necessity of designing targeted strategies tailored to the specific inflammatory phenotype of AD in future research.

Neurotransmitter system regulation

Cholinergic-GABAergic Co-regulation

The imbalance in cholinergic and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic co-regulation plays a crucial role in the pathological interaction between alcohol addiction and AD. The septohippocampal circuitry, composed of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons (BFCNs) and GABAergic neurons, is highly sensitive to alcohol exposure, which manifests as reduced choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) activity accompanied by a decline in the density of parvalbumin-positive GABAergic neurons. Animal studies have demonstrated that maternal choline supplementation alleviates degeneration in both neuronal populations, suggesting the potential value of a co-repair strategy [97].

In the striatal microcircuit, cholinergic interneurons dynamically regulate low-threshold spiking GABAergic interneurons via muscarinic M4 receptors, a mechanism disrupted under dopamine-depleted conditions. Notably, the NKCC1 inhibitor bumetanide restores both cholinergic excitation and GABAergic inhibition by modulating intracellular chloride ion concentration ([Cl-]i) [98].

Evidence at the receptor level suggests promising translational implications. Lorazepam, a benzodiazepine positive allosteric modulator of GABAAR, reduces long-latency afferent inhibition (LAI) by 40% and short-latency afferent inhibition (SAI) by 19%, confirming the cooperative role of these two neurotransmitter systems in motor control [99]. Additionally, dual-target compounds such as butyrylcholinesterase/GABA transaminase (BuChE/GABA-T) inhibitors show potential for simultaneously improving cognition and anxiety symptoms in AD models [100].

Pathological studies further reveal a significant correlation between early-stage reductions in hippocampal GABAAR expression and decreased cholinergic markers in the prefrontal cortex in AD patients [101]. However, the impact of basal forebrain GABAergic neuron damage on memory function exhibits dose-dependent differences: low-dose immunotoxin primarily impairs working memory, whereas high-dose exposure affects both working memory and long-term memory consolidation [102]. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for developing cholinergic-GABAergic co-modulators, though their efficacy in alcohol-related AD comorbidity models requires systematic evaluation.

Glutamate transporter enhancers (Riluzole)

Riluzole, a glutamate transporter agonist, modulates central nervous system glutamate homeostasis through multiple mechanisms, including stimulating EAAT1/EAAT2 glutamate transporter activity, inhibiting voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels, and antagonizing AMPA/NMDA receptors, collectively reducing synaptic glutamate concentrations [103, 104].

Preclinical studies in AβPP-PS1 transgenic AD models have shown that 30-day administration of riluzole (6 mg/kg/day) significantly restores hippocampal glutamate metabolism, as evidenced by the normalization of [1,6-13C] glucose-labeled glutamate C4 and aspartate C3 levels [105]. Clinical studies indicate that six months of riluzole treatment (50 mg/day) slows the decline in brain glucose metabolism, as assessed by positron emission tomography (PET), correlating with improvements in cognitive decline [106].

Animal studies further suggest that initiating treatment during early disease stages (2–6 months of age) prevents cognitive deficits observed at 12 months and restores glutamatergic neurotransmission function [107]. Notably, although riluzole exhibits a dose-dependent biphasic effect in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) treatment, its therapeutic window in AD remains undefined [108]. Current clinical trials generally involve small sample sizes (e.g., one study included only 20 participants), highlighting the urgent need for large-scale, long-term studies to validate its efficacy and safety [109].

Metabolic and epigenetic interventions

Acetaldehyde clearance strategies

As the primary toxic metabolite of ethanol metabolism, effective clearance of acetaldehyde is a critical target for intervening in the alcohol-AD interaction network. Current research strategies focus on regulating and enhancing the activity of aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) and developing multi-system cooperative clearance mechanisms. ALDH2, a key mitochondrial enzyme in acetaldehyde metabolism, is directly associated with acetaldehyde accumulation when its activity is reduced. The ALDH2 rs671 genetic mutation decreases acetaldehyde clearance efficiency by sixfold, significantly increasing neurotoxicity risk. In astrocyte-specific regulatory mechanisms, cerebellar astrocyte-specific ALDH2 knockout models exhibit abnormal decreases in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels, highlighting its essential role in maintaining central neurotransmitter homeostasis [110, 111].

Ethanol metabolism upstream enzymes also critically influence acetaldehyde generation. Alcohol dehydrogenase 1 (ADH1) catalyzes the first step of ethanol oxidation to acetaldehyde, and its activity modulates systemic ethanol-acetaldehyde flux [112]. ADH1 deficiency exacerbates ethanol-induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and liver injury by elevating fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs), independent of acetaldehyde levels [112]. Conversely, tissue-specific overexpression of ADH (e.g., in cardiac tissue) intensifies ethanol’s toxic effects, likely due to accelerated production of toxic metabolites [113]. These findings underscore the dual role of ADH: while essential for ethanol detoxification, its activity must be precisely balanced to avoid downstream metabolic overload.

Pharmacological strategies to enhance ALDH2 activity show potential for intervention. For instance, Alda-1 stabilizes the ALDH2 tetramer structure, significantly increasing its activity. In an ethanol-induced femoral head necrosis model, Alda-1 administration was associated with increased cortical bone thickness in the femoral midshaft and improved trabecular bone volume [114]. The phytochemical quercetin enhances acetaldehyde metabolism via an ALDH1A1-dependent pathway, but its protective effect is markedly diminished in ALDH1A1-deficient models, indicating target specificity [115].

Studies on liver-gut axis cooperative clearance mechanisms reveal that bile excretion transports hepatic acetaldehyde to the intestine for secondary metabolism, whereas inhibition of bile flow results in elevated serum acetaldehyde levels [116]. The probiotic formulation Duolac ProAP4 has been shown to reduce blood acetaldehyde concentration in ALDH2*2 mutation carriers but has no significant effect in wild-type individuals [117].

Notably, acetaldehyde toxicity exhibits organ-specific differences: in a liver injury model, acetaldehyde accumulation did not exacerbate pathology, whereas in an esophageal cancer model, it promoted carcinogenesis by inducing the enrichment of CD44H-positive stem cells [112, 118].

The cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) pathway, another oxidative route for ethanol metabolism, generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) and exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction. Chronic ethanol exposure activates CYP2E1, leading to lipid peroxidation and protein hyperacetylation, which synergize with ALDH2 deficiency to amplify neuronal damage [119].

Existing evidence suggests that targeting ALDH2 activity in combination with multi-system clearance strategies can effectively reduce acetaldehyde burden. However, intervention outcomes must account for genetic polymorphisms and tissue-specific metabolic differences. Integrating ADH modulation with ALDH2 activation may offer a dual approach to mitigate both ethanol and acetaldehyde toxicity, though tissue-specific enzyme expression patterns require careful consideration [112, 120].

HDAC inhibitors (Vorinostat)

Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors exhibit multidimensional intervention potential in the interplay between alcohol-induced neurotoxicity and AD through epigenetic regulation. Vorinostat (SAHA), a hydroxamate-class pan-HDAC inhibitor targeting HDAC classes I, II, and IV, has been shown to alleviate alcohol withdrawal-induced mechanical hypersensitivity, increasing pain thresholds at a dose of 50 mg/kg [121]. This effect is closely associated with the restoration of central histone H3K9 acetylation levels [122].

In metabolic regulation, trichostatin A (TSA), a Streptomyces-derived HDAC1/3/6 inhibitor, significantly alleviates alcohol-induced hepatic steatosis by inhibiting HDAC3 activity and restores histone acetylation defects in the carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1α (CPT1α) promoter [123, 124].

Neuroplasticity studies indicate that sodium butyrate (NaB), a short-chain fatty acid inhibiting class I HDACs (HDAC1/2/3/8) at 600 mg/kg, reverses alcohol exposure-induced overexpression of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor GluN2B subunit during adolescence, while also improving deficits in hippocampal long-term synaptic depression and novel object recognition memory [125]. In neurodegenerative mechanisms, HDAC inhibitors demonstrate dual effects on oxidative stress: TSA reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in SK-N-MC neurons through HDAC2 inhibition while inhibiting pathological overexpression of HDAC2, suggesting a protective role against alcohol-related neurodegeneration [126].

It is worth noting that different HDAC inhibitors exhibit dose- and subtype-dependent differences in regulating alcohol consumption. Decitabine, a DNA methyltransferase inhibitor (DNMTi) rather than HDAC inhibitor, significantly reduces ethanol intake in a chronic intermittent drinking model, whereas SAHA does not show similar effects under the same experimental conditions, likely due to HDAC subtype selectivity [121, 127]. Current evidence indicates that HDAC inhibitors restore histone acetylation homeostasis, regulate synaptic plasticity, and inhibit oxidative stress, offering multi-target intervention strategies for alcohol exposure and AD pathology. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular interactions of specific HDAC subtypes—such as HDAC1/3 in amyloid-beta metabolism and HDAC2 in tau hyperphosphorylation—to advance precision therapeutic approaches.

Targeting brain insulin resistance

Emerging evidence indicates that brain insulin resistance (BIR) induced by chronic alcohol exposure serves as a critical nexus promoting Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology [128]. The pathogenic mechanisms are manifested through three primary pathological mechanisms: BIR impairs β-amyloid (Aβ) clearance by suppressing insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) activity [129]; activates glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) (as discussed in Section 3.1.1) to drive Tau hyperphosphorylation [130]; and disrupts neuronal glucose uptake mediated by GLUT4, precipitating energy metabolic crisis [130]. Significantly, intervention strategies proposed in this review exhibit synergistic effects against BIR: Acetaldehyde scavenging (Section 3.3.1) mitigates oxidative damage to insulin signaling molecules such as IRS-1 [131]; and precision nutritional interventions (Section 3.4.2) involving ω-3 fatty acids or curcumin have been demonstrated to enhance cerebral insulin sensitivity through PPARγ pathway activation [132].

Integrated management strategies

Early abstinence combined with cognitive training

Neuroplasticity interventions during early abstinence mitigate AD pathology progression by promoting multimodal synaptic remodeling and neurotransmitter homeostasis regulation. Synaptic restructuring in the NAc is a key target for abstinence interventions. Optogenetic activation of the basolateral amygdala–NAc D1 dopamine receptor pathway has been shown to reverse morphine withdrawal-induced depressive-like behaviors, while the paraventricular thalamus–NAc D2 dopamine receptor pathway modulates acute withdrawal symptoms [133].

During early abstinence, supplementation with the dopamine precursor levodopa (L-DOPA) restores NMDA/AMPA receptor subunit ratios in the NAc shell and reverses chronic drug exposure-induced reductions in dendritic spine density [134]. Pharmacological intervention timing studies indicate that administration of ketamine at the onset of ethanol withdrawal (day 0) prevents aberrant GABAergic synaptic function in the central amygdala (CeA) while enhancing long-term potentiation effects in the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) [43, 134]. However, delaying ketamine administration until withdrawal day 2 results in a loss of neuroprotective effects [135].

The cannabinoid CB1 receptor antagonist rimonabant inhibits the 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) signaling pathway, preventing nicotine withdrawal-induced dendritic spine loss in the hippocampal CA1 region [136]. Behavioral studies indicate that alcohol-dependent individuals exhibit significant recovery of hippocampal CA2/3 gray matter volume after two weeks of abstinence, with the extent of recovery positively correlated with the duration of cognitive training [137]. Matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) inhibitors locally infused into the CeA block the reinforcement of self-administration behavior induced by ethanol withdrawal, suggesting that extracellular matrix remodeling interventions may enhance cognitive training outcomes [138].

Notably, sex-specific neuroadaptive mechanisms should be considered in intervention design. During nicotine withdrawal, glutamatergic synaptic transmission in the NAc shell exhibits an increasing trend in female rats, whereas this phenomenon is absent in males, indicating potential sex differences in withdrawal-associated neural adaptations [139].

Precision nutritional intervention

Precision nutritional intervention demonstrates multidimensional therapeutic potential by regulating metabolic imbalances in the alcohol-Alzheimer’s disease (AD) interaction network. Studies show that supplementation with ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) presents a dose-dependent effect: long-term supplementation with EPA/DHA (3.36 g/d) in patients with clinical coronary artery disease slows cognitive aging by approximately 2.5 years [140]. Cohort studies indicate that sustained ω-3 supplementation reduces the risk of AD onset by 64%, although some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have not observed significant clinical benefits, potentially related to APOE ε4 gene polymorphism and the timing of intervention [141, 142]. In the field of vitamin D research, a deficiency with serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels below 30 nmol/L increases the risk of all-cause dementia by 25%, while supplementation can reduce AD incidence by 17% [143]. However, a meta-analysis of nine RCTs showed no significant improvement in various cognitive assessment markers with vitamin D supplementation [144].

Notably, curcumin intervention studies have shown that daily supplementation with 180 mg significantly lowers circulating GSK-3β levels (Δ = −2.4 ng/mL), while also reducing islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) concentrations (Δ = −2.0 ng/mL), suggesting that it may improve insulin resistance by inhibiting tau hyperphosphorylation pathways [145]. In alcohol-dependent populations, the deficiency rate of thiamine (vitamin B1) is significantly elevated, and timely supplementation can reverse neurofunctional deficits associated with Marchiafava-Bignami disease [146]. Additionally, plant-based antioxidants such as walnut extract can significantly reduce brain tissue levels of free radicals in AD transgenic animal models (lipid peroxidation reduced by 29%) while inhibiting Aβ fibrillation [147]. Similarly, flavonoids like quercetin—abundant in fruits and vegetables—target the Keap1-Nrf2 pathway to activate vitagenes (e.g., heme oxygenase-1 and glutathione enzymes), enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses against alcohol- and AD-associated oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [148, 149]. This aligns with the hormesis principle, where mild stressors induce adaptive cytoprotection [148, 149]. Current research controversies primarily include the heterogeneity of ω-3 intervention effects influenced by the APOE ε4 genotype and inconsistent clinical translation evidence of vitamin D supplementation [141–144]. These findings suggest that precision nutritional interventions need to integrate multidimensional information, including genetic characteristics, metabolic phenotypes, and disease progression stages, in order to develop individualized intervention strategies.

Conclusion and prospects

Current evidence suggests that alcohol addiction and the pathological progression of AD share a synergistic exacerbation mechanism involving multiple molecular pathways. Chronic alcohol exposure significantly increases ROS levels in the hippocampus by enhancing oxidative stress responses, while also inducing microglial polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [150, 151]. This dual effect accelerates neuronal degeneration. In terms of intervention strategies, blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration efficiency remains a critical limiting factor. Research has shown that nanoparticle delivery systems, such as lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles, can increase brain drug accumulation by 6.5 times, significantly improving the brain bioavailability of N-acetylcysteine [151, 152]. For characteristic AD pathological changes, chitosan-hyaluronic acid nanoparticles have demonstrated dual functions: both detecting Aβ fibrils (detection limit 0.1 nM) and inhibiting aggregation, while gold nanorod-antibody conjugates dissociate Aβ aggregates via near-infrared photothermal effects, significantly delaying paralysis progression in transgenic nematode models [153, 154].

The cutting-edge breakthrough directions focus on optimizing delivery systems. For example, targeting the p38 MAPK/GSK-3β pathways with dual-functional nanoparticles constructed using PLGA (poly lactic-co-glycolic acid) carriers, with a particle size of 101.5 ± 10.3 nm, can optimize BBB penetration efficiency [155]. At the clinical translation level, a combined biomarker model for AD risk stratification in alcohol use disorder (AUD) patients may be established using phosphorylated tau protein at the 217th site (p-tau217) and neurofilament light chain protein (NfL) [156]. Notably, the safety evaluation of nanomaterials needs to address dose-dependent paradoxical effects. For instance, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) inhibit Aβ fibrillation at low concentrations, but accelerate deposition at high concentrations, suggesting the need to establish species-specific toxicity threshold standards [157].

Conflicting research evidence currently shows that gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) exhibit both inhibitory effects on Aβ aggregation and may trigger microglial overactivation via the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathway [158, 159]. This emphasizes the need for systematic exploration of the spatiotemporal dynamic interactions between alcohol exposure and Aβ/tau pathology using organoid models and in vivo imaging techniques, providing a theoretical foundation for developing safe and effective individualized intervention strategies.
