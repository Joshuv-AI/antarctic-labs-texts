Intrathoracic fetus in fetu cases found in paediatric population: a systematic review
Authors: Ali Samady-Khanghah, Anahita Abdolzadeh, Mahdi Parvizi-Mashhadi, Khashayar Atqiaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe fetu in fetu (FIF) expression refers to an extremely rare congenital phenomenon characterized by a second fetus growing into the body of the healthy twin. This study aimed to review the few cases located in the thoracic cavity.MethodUsing the central medical databases PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, as well as the references of the articles, we collected only 9 cases of FIF in pediatric patients’ thorax. The inclusion criteria were full-text literature and participants aged 19 years or younger. Intra-abdominal or extra-thoracic cases and the presence of malignant components were our exclusion criteria.ResultsOnly one case was diagnosed in adolescence. The remaining participants were younger than 10 months at the time of the intervention. There were 4 male, 3 female, and 2 non-declared sex cases. The entire mass was found in the mediastinum, but one was located intrapulmonary. All cases had undergone the thoracotomy. The minimum survival reported in the literature was 5 months. Adipose tissue was the most frequently reported tissue among the resected masses, with 8 cases, followed by the vertebral column and bony tissues, with 6 and 5 cases, respectively.ConclusionAn infant suffering from respiratory distress or even recurrent infections, particularly with a mass revealed in its chest radiography, should raise a suspicion of FIF. This doubt will be strengthened if an axial skeleton or bony densities are found.Keywords: Fetus in fetu, Intra-thoracic, Paediatric, Systematic review

Introduction

The prevalence of thoracic masses in children remains unclear. Additionally, the spectrum encountered in radiology practice differs substantially between pediatric and adult populations. The proportion of malignant tumors among pediatric mediastinal tumors is up to 50% [1]. The majority of them indicate excision [2]. The typical “fetus in fetu” (FIF) is an extremely rare congenital pathologic phenomenon characterized by the parasitic development of a malformed fetus inside the body of the normal second twin. However, FIF is defined as a fetal-like or fetiform mass with a calcified vertebral axis surrounded by other organs or limbs. The abdominal cavity is basically the main location from which FIF most often derives. FIF found in the thoracic cavity is rare; to the best of our knowledge, only nine cases have been reported.

Materials and methods

A systematic review of the literature on cases of FIF found in the thoracic cavity has been done. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not sought because only a literature search and non-human-subject research were conducted.

Search strategy

A systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses). PubMed, Embase, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Scholar Google, along with the references of included studies, were used to collect cases. The search process was done, including the search terms” Intrathoracic Fetus in fetu”, “Mediastinal Fetus in fetu”, and “Pulmonary Fetus in fetu” with all available search years from 2004 to 2022.

Selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were cases for which full texts were available, in English or non-English, and cases found in the thoracic cavity of pediatric patients aged under 19 years. Masses out of the rib cage, and with malignant components, were excluded.

The evidence level, according to the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine classification system, was level 1.

Data extraction

The variables were the year of publication, age at diagnosis, sex, signs and symptoms at referral, imaging findings, therapeutic interventions, macroscopic mass features, and outcome. The organs and tissues detected in the masses are also described.

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, Washington, USA).

Age: Data were available for all patients (range, 21 st week of intrauterine life to 12 years).

Sex: Data were found for only seven cases. Four male patients were present in front of three females.

Clinical presentation: Data were available for all patients. All manifestations fell into two categories: respiratory infection symptoms, such as cough and fever [3–8] and those found incidentally during prenatal ultrasonography [9–11].

Diagnostic measurements: X-ray was the primary diagnostic modality. However, prenatal ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging were used. Outcome

The outcomes were uneventful for all of the patients. The maximum follow-up period was 2.5 years for a three-month-and-a-half male infant [4].

Discussion

In 1800, Johann Friedrich Meckel, a German anatomist, first described the term FIF. Young, in 1809, published it in the form of the first case report, and by Willis in 1953, an approach to definition was made [12]. If an abnormal embryogenesis of a diamniotic monochorionic twin pregnancy leads to asymmetric division and abnormal anastomosis of the yolk circulation, the FIF phenomenon occurs. This rare pathology has a prevalence of 1/500,000 births; fewer than 200 cases have been reported worldwide until 2019 [13]. FIF occurs in different age groups, with a predominance in early childhood, and in the majority of cases, is diagnosed in patients younger than 18 months, with a presumed male predominance [13]. Although few asymptomatic cases have been reported so far, the typical clinical symptoms of FIF are related to the compression of adjacent organs by the mass, involving abdominal distension, feeding difficulties, vomiting, and respiratory distress.

It is predictable that the majority of our cases present with respiratory symptoms. FIF can be diagnosed prenatally; however, a limited number of cases (7%) of FIF were diagnosed before birth [13]. Grossly, the FIF appears as a sac containing liquid, or vernix caseosa, as a mass suspended by a vascular pedicle. An anastomosis with the host’s vasculature is also identifiable. It generally manifests as anencephaly or acardia. When known, the FIF’s sex is always the same as that of the host. Although mainly retroperitoneal, it can also be mediastinal, mesenteric, cranial, or gonadal [14–17]. A varying degree of organogenesis, including an axial skeleton and rudimentary limbs, is seen. This complex weighs between 13 and 2000 g, primarily reflecting the host’s blood supply [7]. Due to the Spencer’s recommendation, a FIF should meet one or more of the following criteria: (a) enclosed in a separate sac containing liquid or vernix caseosa, (b) covered with normal skin whether partially or completely, (c) have almost recognizable anatomical parts, and (d) connected to the host’s body by one or a few relatively large blood vessels [7]. The FIF is mainly used in the differential diagnosis of teratoma. Thus, with respect to the risk of future malignancy, it is essential to establish a definitive diagnosis. The presence of a spine is a strong differentiator between a FIF and a teratoma. However, radiologists and pathologists believe that FIF can be retained even in the absence of a vertebral axis. The absence of a vertebral axis accounts for approximately 9% of FIF cases [5, 12, 18]. According to them, the suggestion that the FIF definition should be broadened to include any structure in which the fetal form exhibits highly developed organogenesis or a vertebral axis [17] receives greater attention. There are two theories of FIF: “a highly differentiated mature teratoma” and “identical twin theory”. The “teratoma theory” claims that FIF represents a specialized type of highly well-differentiated teratoma, whereas the “identical twin theory” proposes that FIF arises from abnormalities in the process of twinning. Under normal circumstances, twins develop symmetrically; however, in FIF, one twin remains underdeveloped and becomes encapsulated within the body of the other twin during gestation [19]. Its main differential diagnosis is teratoma, which is particularly important given the high risk of malignancy associated with teratoma. Analysis of genetic markers, including blood group, karyotype, and DNA, reveals no genetic differences between the host and the FIF. Therefore, this result is compatible with both the theory of “parasitic twins” and the “monozygous teratoma” theory [20]. The final diagnosis of FIF is made solely on histopathology. The recommended surgical intervention is complete excision. It is widely recognized that surgical approach design is an important preoperative consideration. Close postoperative follow-up with ultrasound and serology (AFP or beta-HCG) is recommended to exclude even rare recurrence [21].

Although the hypothesis that vertebral axis differentiation distinguishes FIF from teratoma must be accepted, prenatal diagnosis in mediastinal localization may be problematic. In particular, among relatively prevalent anterior mediastinal teratomas, only three cases in our survey were diagnosed prenatally by ultrasound. Regarding localization, six cases involved the left hemithorax [3–6, 11, 22], two in the posterior mediastinum [8, 9], and on the right para-cardiac region [10]. All cases benefited from complete excision via thoracotomy. In rare cases, FIF may be considered malignant if any of the membranes or tissues are not completely excised [23].

Conclusion

During the assessment of respiratory distress in infants, a mass centered on the axial skeleton on imaging may suggest intrathoracic FIF. However, our survey revealed that the presence of the vertebral column is not a reliable index. Intrathoracic FIF must meet most of Spencer’s criteria. CT or MRI yields an accurate preoperative diagnosis. Complete excision is curative even in the intrathoracic cases.
