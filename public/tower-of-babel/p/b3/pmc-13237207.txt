Two decades of tropical soil analyses for a better understanding of soil organic carbon
Authors: Marie Tella, Vincent Freycon, Frédéric Feder, Nour Souki, Fabrice Bénédet, Jean-Baptiste Laurent, Nathalie Choty, Patrice Autfray, Hervé Guibert, Rabah Lahmar, Graziëlla Albaut, Vivien Baudassé, Naëma Bouarfa-Hachkar, Maxime Geniez, Laura Lopez, Christelle Rostagno, Evelyne Séguret, Gaël Sénécal, Mathilde Valette, Julien Demenois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil organic carbon is essential for soil fertility, water retention and nutrient storage. Although crucial for climate regulation and food security, tropical soils remain insufficiently documented. The DATA4C database includes 31,980 soil samples collected in 58 countries in tropical regions between 2002 and 2022. Each sample could be characterised by 41 variables covering the soil’s physical and chemical properties, such as texture, pH, carbon and nitrogen content, and cation exchange capacity. These samples were analysed in an ISO 9001-certified laboratory according to standardised protocols. The data has been validated and harmonised over two decades. DATA4C is a valuable resource for modelling tools, agricultural and environmental planning, and the monitoring and assessment of tropical soils. Its aim is to facilitate the reuse of data in various scientific and operational contexts.Subject terms: Environmental impact, Geochemistry

Background & Summary

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is a critical component of soils1, primarily because of the role of soil organic matter (SOM) in sustaining soil fertility by contributing to soil water-holding capacity for plant growth and in storing carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and other nutrients2. About 95 percent of our food nutrients come from soils, which have a natural capacity to provide nutrients to support crop growth3. Besides, soils hold three times the amount of carbon in the atmosphere4 and are the singular most biodiverse habitat on Earth5. Sustainable management of SOC can therefore play a significant role to tackle several Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) like food security (SDG 2), climate change (SDG 13), land degradation and biodiversity loss (SDG 15)6.

Wiesmeier et al.7 reported a large number of SOC drivers at various scales, from climate to soil physico-chemistry, including land-use and management. SOC stock fluctuates with latitude and longitude with greater stocks at higher latitudes, decreases in the mid-latitudes, and increases in the humid tropics8. The high value in the humid tropics is due to the high precipitation9 and net primary production10. Simultaneously, SOC stocks in the tropics are subject to several threats: soil erosion11, land conversion12 or unsustainable intensification of crop production13. For example, the conversion of primary forest to cropland in the tropics caused a 25–30% loss of SOC stocks12. Yet, SOC stocks in the tropics are poorly documented in contrast to their global importance14,15.

To contribute to fill this gap, in this study we provide a field-sampled SOC database (DATA4C) from the tropics. The database contains 31,980 soil samples collected between 2002 and 2022 in 58 tropical countries (Fig. 1). All soil samples were analyzed at the ISO certified 9001 soil laboratory of CIRAD at Montpellier, France. Each soil sample can be described by 41 variables covering 7 soil properties. Soil properties are: texture (Clays, FineSilt, CoarseSilt, TotalSilt, FineSand, CoarseSand, TotalSand), nitrogen and carbon contents (Ntot, Ctot, Cinorga, Corg), exchangeables and cationic exchange capacity (ExchCaAcetate, ExchKAcetate, ExchMgAcetate, ExchNaAcetate, CECAcetate, ExchCaCobalti, ExchKCobalti, ExchMgCobalti, ExchNaCobalti, ExchAlCobalti, CECCobalti, pHCobalti), pH (pHwater, pHKCl), total contents of major elements (Altot, Fetot, Mntot, Sitot), available phosphorus (ExtPOlsen) and the exchangeable and extractable contents of the major elements (ExchAlKCl, ExchHKCl, AlDCB, FeDCB, SiDCB, AlOx, FeOx, FeOx, SiOx, AlPyro, FePyro, SiPyro).

The database (called DATA4C for “Data for soil carbon”) was designed to increase the reuse of soil datasets from research and developments experiments and projects involving CIRAD scientists. Our aim was to make available a dataset acquired in a single laboratory that allows for pan-tropical comparisons. Additionally, DATA4C is a contribution to the FAIR movement16, the “4 per 1000 Initiative: Soils for Food Security and Climate” (www.4p1000.org) and the Soil Carbon International Research Consortium (https://soilcarbonfutures.earth/). CIRAD aims to progressively provide an open-access to DATA4C to facilitate the synthesis of soil data in agronomy and forestry, and especially soil carbon. The database can be used in modelling tools requiring soil information. It provides data of interest for land management, agricultural and environmental planning, as well as risk assessment and monitoring. It complements other regional and international databases (e.g WoSIS).

Methods

All data contained in this dataset were acquired by the CIRAD soil analysis laboratory in Montpellier between 2002 and 2022 from samples provided mainly by CIRAD’s researchers in agronomy, agroforestry, and soil science and its partners as part of research and expertise projects. The analyses were carried out in accordance with ISO 9001 certification, and all data have been rigorously validated and recorded in the laboratory database using the same procedure since 2002. All samples associated with similar analysis request are grouped together in a single file, which is linked to a year of analysis and has a unique name. Two types of metadata were retained for all samples of the dataset: the year of analysis and the country where the soil samples were collected. Two other types of metadata were retained for some samples of the dataset: the land use at the time of sampling and the geographical coordinates of the samples. Land use was listed according to the IPCC classification6. As this information was not required when an analysis was requested, we collected it from analysis applicants who are still active. Finally, this information concerns 11,464 soil samples (36% of the dataset). Geographical coordinates of the samples (e.g., GPS readings) was not systematically recorded or could not be mentioned in order to maintain the confidentiality of the expertise project. Finally, this information concerns 804 soil samples (3% of the dataset). In addition, in order to offer a database with a “tropical” specificity, the original database was limited to soils from countries that were entirely (e.g., Cameroon, French Guiana, etc.) or partially located in the intertropical zone (e.g., Saudi Arabia, Burma, and Argentina) (Fig. 1).

All acquisition methods used to quantify variables comply with French soil analysis standards or reference protocols for the scientific community (see the Dictionary.pdf file in the Dataset17). Additionally, each variable is derived from an analytical serie that is systematically validated by analysing reference samples (local reference from laboratory or certified reference materials) with conventionally true target values. This approach validates all data in this database, which spans over two decades. Furthermore, the French Ministry of Agriculture requires most of the variables in this database to obtain soils analysis certification in France (exchangeable bases, cation exchange capacity of the exchange complex, texture, organic matter, pH, and available phosphorus). Thanks to the accuracy of its results, which is proven by its participation in the Bipéa Interlaboratory Test, the soil analysis laboratory of CIRAD has received external recognition for the accuracy of its analyses throughout the period in which this database was acquired.

Among the 41 variables in this database, three have been calculated: organic carbon, as well as the sums of sand and silt (see Dictionary.pdf in the Dataset17). Over 20 years of data acquisition, the SI reference units evolved, so we harmonized them according to current standards (see Dictionary.pdf in the Dataset17).

The laboratory has expressed a limit of detection (LOD) for each variable (Table 1), which is the value or output signal above which it can be stated with a certain level of confidence that a sample differs from a blank containing no element to be determined. Thus, all results below the LOD have been retained in the database in a non-numerical format. For example, if the result is 0.12 g kg−1 and the LOD is 0.30 g kg−1, the database will contain (0.12) < LOD = 0.3 g kg−1.

Where Xblank is the mean of n blank measurements, Sblank is the standard deviation of n blank measurements, and k is a numerical factor chosen according to the confidence level desired18,19. In this study, a multiplier factor of 3 corresponds to a 0.13% chance of incorrectly concluding that a substance is present when it is not (p = 0.01).

Over the course of 20 years of variable acquisition, the laboratory’s measuring instruments were replaced and certain methods evolved. These changes led to shifts in the LODs of the variables over time. The LODs used for all variables in the database are based on the averages of the blanks and their distributions on the laboratory’s control charts from 2016 to 2022.

For calculated variables, it is impossible to systematically compare their value with the LODs of measured variables. If all measured variables are below the LOD, then the calculated variable will also be below the LOD. However, if one measured variable is below its LOD, the calculated variable may be above or below the LOD. Since it is impossible to examine the calculated variables individually, and to avoid misinterpreting the data, the decision was made not to express an LOD for the latter (NA).

Technical Validation

We validated the DATA4C database by (i) verifying that the data fell within the expected ranges; (ii) studying some bivariate relationships. In addition, we compared the distribution of values for some variables in this database with that from the WoSIS database.

Firstly, for 17 variables, we studied the percentage of analyses that were above the maximum threshold value and below the minimum threshold value defined by Fürst et al.20 for European forest mineral soils (Table 1). These threshold values corresponded to the 97.5% and 2.5% percentiles of the database for these soils, respectively. When the LOD of measurements in our study was higher than the minimum threshold value, we were unable to calculate the percentage of analyses that were below the minimum threshold value.

Six within the 17 variables in our DATA4C database had between 64% and 20% of their values above the maximum threshold: Altot, Fetot, Mntot, AlOx, FeOx, and Clay. This result is consistent with the fact that tropical soils are generally rich in Al, Fe, and Mn. Due to the intermediate ionic potential of these three elements (in ferric form for Fe), their cations are very poorly soluble in water and precipitate by hydrolysis21 in the form of aluminium, iron, and manganese oxide and oxyhydroxide. Furthermore, it is not uncommon to find tropical soils with a clay content greater than 51% (i.e., the maximum threshold value for European forest soils), for example in Ferralsols22,23, which represent 23% of tropical soils24.

Among the 17 variables, AlCobalti had more than 20% of values below the minimum threshold, which was 0.018 cmol+ kg-120. This is certainly explained by a LOD in our study (i.e., 0.005 cmol kg−1) that is better than that determined by Fürst et al.20 and by the fact that exchangeable aluminium can be present in very small quantities when the soil pH is higher than the acidity constant of aluminium (i.e., pKa = 5.5). In this case, aluminium precipitates as aluminium oxide and is rarely found in soluble or exchangeable form.

Secondly, we specifically studied four bivariate relationships: pHKCl vs pHwater, Fetot vs FeOx, Altot vs AlOx, and CECAcetate vs CECCobalti (Fig. 2). 98.8% of the samples in the DATA4C database had negative ΔpH (= pHKCl – pHwater) values, which is to be expected, since the potential for proton extraction in soil mixed with KCl solution is stronger than in soil mixed with water. The 1.2% of samples that had positive ΔpH values certainly correspond to Geric Ferralsols25, since this is one of the criteria for the Geric property. 82% of the samples in the DATA4C database had Fetot values greater than 4 times FeOx, which is generally observed in temperate forest soils20. 16% of the samples had Fetot values between FeOx and 4 times FeOx, which certainly corresponds to soils rich in amorphous or weakly crystallized iron oxides (e.g., ferrihydrite). Finally, 2% of the samples had Fetot values lower than FeOx, which is inconsistent. This inconsistency is discussed in § Usage note. 85% of the samples in the DATA4C database had Altot values > 10 times AlOx, which is generally observed in temperate forest soils20. 15% of the samples had Altot values between AlOx and 10 times AlOx, which certainly corresponds to soils rich in amorphous or weakly crystallized aluminium oxides. Finally, 82% of the samples in the DATA4C database had CECAcetate values (at pH 7) higher than CECCobalti (at soil pH), which is expected for soils with a pH < 7 with constituents that have variable negative charges (i.e., organic matter, clays).

We compared soil textural triangles texture triangles and boxplots for four variables, clay, organic carbon, CECAcetate, and pHwater, from our DATA4C database, which contains only tropical soils, and the global WoSIS database, which contains both tropical and non-tropical soils. Bimodal soils are soils in which the silt fraction in weight percentage is systematically smaller than the clay and sand percentages26. The DATA4C database contains a higher proportion of bimodal soils than the WoSIS database (52% vs. 19%; Fig. 3). This confirms that bimodal soils are common in tropical and subtropical regions, unlike temperate soils26. However, this DATA4C database also contains 48% of non-bimodal soils, which are therefore also common in tropics, where they can be found in Ferralsols, Alisols, Nitisols, Podzols, Fluvisols, and Gleysols (e.g.23,25). Soils in the DATA4C database are generally more clayey than those in WoSIS (Fig. 4), which is consistent with the fact that 23% of tropical soils are Ferralsols, soils that can have high clay content. The soils in the DATA4C database have organic carbon contents and CEC values that are broadly similar to those in WoSIS, although they have fewer very high values. This does not validate two of the myths associated with tropical soils, namely that they have low organic C contents and a low capacity to retain nutrients. Finally, the soils in the DATA4C database have a wide pH range, although it is less extensive than that of WoSIS, which contains very acidic and very basic soils. This does not validate the myth of acidic tropical soils. The invalidity of these three myths had already been showed in other studies (e.g.24).

Usage Notes

All data in this dataset were acquired from samples prepared according to the standard NF ISO 11464. As stated previously, all methods used to quantify the variables comply with French soil analysis standards or reference protocols for the scientific community (see Dictionary.pdf in the dataset). Consequently, the data can easily be aggregated with data acquired using similar protocols. However, caution is required when combining data from different analytical protocols. While some variables can be measured similarly using different protocols, other protocols cannot be compared. For instance, values measured with barium ions and hexaminecobalt(III) ions as index cations are highly comparable, and these two methods can be considered equivalent27. However, they differ from values measured with acetate ions.

Despite all the precautions taken during data extraction and technical validation, some samples had a total element content that was lower than the extractable or exchangeable content. For example, Fetot < FeOx for 2% of the samples in the database. This finding is probably due to a conflict of units between the laboratory database and the individual results entered by operators. This illustrates the difficulty of matching units for old, unverifiable acquisitions, and highlights that there may be some inconsistencies in the data. However, these would not exceed a few percent of the dataset.

Information on the geographical location of samples is generally only available at the level of the country from which they were taken. Moreover, the heterogeneity of the countries is mainly explained by the geographical areas of intervention, collaborations and projects in which CIRAD is involved. The over-representation of samples in certain areas results from the presence of agronomic experimentation, regular monitoring or the small size of farms. Finally, as information on sampling depths was not systematically provided at the time of analysis, we have not considered them. However, we can estimate that the majority of samples come from surface horizons (<30 cm).
