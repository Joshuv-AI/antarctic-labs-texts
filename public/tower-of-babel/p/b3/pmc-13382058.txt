Best evidence summary of exercise rehabilitation in patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy
Authors: Ye He, Ping Song, Ling Li, Tingting Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate and summarize existing evidence regarding exercise rehabilitation for patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy to inform the development of evidence-based clinical practice.MethodsAccording to the “6S” evidence pyramid model, evidence on exercise rehabilitation in patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy was retrieved, including clinical decisions, guidelines, evidence summaries, best practices and recommended practices, expert consensus, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, and high-quality randomized controlled trials. Searches covered major domestic and international databases as well as professional websites, with literature collected up to 8 January 2026. Two independent researchers evaluated the quality of the literature and extracted and summarized the included evidence.ResultsA total of 15 articles were selected, including one clinical decision, four guidelines, one expert consensus, three systematic reviews and six randomized controlled trials. Thirty-five pieces of evidence were summarized in nine areas: exercise purpose, multidisciplinary team formulation, health education, exercise assessment, exercise preparation, exercise timing, exercise type, exercise parameters, and exercise supervision.ConclusionsThis study summarizes the latest evidence on exercise rehabilitation for patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy, while emphasizing the cautious use of extrapolated evidence when pancreaticoduodenectomy-specific data are lacking. Prior to applying the evidence, specific safety issues for these patients should be fully considered, and interventions should be tailored to individual needs and preferences. Future research should prioritize high-quality randomized controlled trials on exercise rehabilitation in this population to generate direct evidence and thereby refine exercise rehabilitation programs.Systematic review registrationThis study was registered at the Fudan University Centre for Evidence-based Nursing (Registration No. ES20244076).

Introduction

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) remains the standard treatment for lesions in the pancreatic head and periampullary region. Tumors in the pancreatic head and ampulla of Vater present a complex anatomical challenge due to their proximity to numerous blood vessels. The procedure necessitates the reconstruction of the digestive tract, pancreatic duct and biliary tree, which may lead to multiple procedure-specific complications, such as postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) and delayed gastric emptying (DGE).1,2 Consequently, PD is regarded as one of the most complex and high-risk operations in hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery. The intricacy of the procedure and its potential sequelae necessitate a robust preoperative health and functional status.3 However, patients undergoing PD are generally older and at an elevated risk for sarcopenia and cachexia.4,5 Additionally, the increasing use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy prior to surgery may further impair patients’ functional status, thereby heightening the risk of postoperative mortality and complications.6 With advances in surgical techniques and perioperative care, the mortality rate following PD has been reduced to less than 4%.5 However, the incidence of postoperative complications ranges from 10% to 50%.7, 8, 9 Therefore, it is crucial to implement effective strategies to enhance preoperative physical function and reduce postoperative complications, thereby expediting recovery.

Exercise rehabilitation is a fundamental component of perioperative patient management within the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) framework. A growing body of evidence indicates that exercise training is an effective non-pharmacological intervention for patients with cancer, enhancing quality of life by restoring muscle strength, improving physical function, and alleviating cancer-related symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety and depression.10, 11, 12 Indeed, exercise is now recommended throughout the cancer care continuum. Preoperative exercise can optimize patients’ physical functional reserves, thereby increasing their adaptability to the surgical stress response. Concurrently, to stimulate the expedited recovery of respiratory, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal systems, patients need to engage in physical activity in the early postoperative period.13

The demonstrated benefits of exercise for patients with cancer have led to various international guidelines. For instance, the Exercise Guidelines for Cancer Survivors published by the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) in 2019 recommend that cancer survivors perform at least 30 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise at least three times a week and perform strengthening exercises for major muscle groups twice a week.14 However, achieving these recommendations may be challenging for PD patients, especially those experiencing cancer-related or procedure-specific complications. Research quantifying exercise levels of patients after PD revealed that only 16% of participants exercised sufficiently to meet the ACSM Exercise Guidelines for Cancer Survivors.15 In the past, research on exercise among cancer survivors has predominantly focused on more prevalent cancers, such as breast, lung, colon, and prostate cancers.16, 17, 18, 19 While a growing number of exercise trials involving patients undergoing PD have shown preliminary effectiveness,10,20, 21, 22, 23, 24 there is considerable heterogeneity in the exercise rehabilitation programs employed across different studies. Standards for exercise type and exercise parameters (including initiation timing, duration, frequency, and intensity) have not yet been unified. Although existing guidelines and consensus statements have mentioned and described exercise intervention for PD patients,13,25,26 the evidence remains scattered, lacking highly practical exercise rehabilitation guidelines specifically tailored to PD patients.

Evidence summary provides an overview of available evidence on health care interventions and activities, primarily encompassing guidelines, systematic reviews and high-quality original studies, centered around a specific theme or set of themes.27,28 It is focused, reliable, comprehensive, concise, easy to understand and disseminate, particularly suitable for preparation of evidence resources prior to evidence transformation.29 With this in mind, this study systematically searched for relevant studies on exercise rehabilitation for PD patients, screened, extracted and integrated existing scientific evidence, with the aim of providing a basis for formulating and implementing the best exercise rehabilitation program for PD patients.

Methods

This study adopted the evidence summary reporting standards by the Fudan University Evidence-based Nursing Center according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) evidence summary principles, which include six areas: problem establishment, structured search of the literature, literature screening, literature quality appraisal, the summary and grading of evidence and the formation of practical suggestions.25,26,28

Establishment of the problem

The PIPOST model was used to construct the evidence implementation questions.30 (i) Population (P): patients undergoing PD with age ≥ 18 years; (ii) Intervention (I): Preoperative and postoperative exercise rehabilitation; (iii) Professional (P): health care workers; (iv) Outcome (O): improvement in clinical outcomes; (v) Setting (S): specialist wards, rehabilitation centers, communities, homes; (vi) Type of evidence (T): clinical decision making, guidelines, evidence summaries, best practices, recommended practices, expert consensus, systematic reviews and meta-analyses and high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Evidence sources and retrieval strategy

According to the “6S” evidence pyramid model,31 we searched the following databases from the top down: UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, Joanna Briggs Institute, National Guideline Clearinghouse, Guidelines International Network, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, Yi Mai tong Guidelines Network, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, European Society for Medical Oncology, American Society of Clinical Oncology, American Association for Cancer Research, Oncology Nursing Society, American College of Sports Medicine, Chinese Medical Journal Full-text Database, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, CINAHL, CNKI, Wanfang database, SinoMed, VIP database.

The search was conducted using a combination of subject headings and free-text terms, and the search words were ‘pancreaticoduodenectomy / pancreatoduodenectomy / Whipple / pancreatectomy / duoden∗ / head of pancreas / pancreatic head / periampullary / pancreat∗ / pancreatic resection / pancreatic surgery’, ‘exercise / rehabilitation / walking / bicycling / resistance training / endurance training / enhanced recovery after surgery / yoga / exercise therapy / preoperative exercise / mobilization / activity / ambulation / cycling / training / aerobic exercise / aerobic fitness / physical activity / physical therapy / physiotherapy∗ / bridge exercise / rehabilitation exercises / preoperative exercise / postoperative exercise / prehabilitation’, ‘clinical decision making / decision making / consensus / guideline / meta-analysis / systematic review / randomized controlled trial / evidence summary / decision-making’. The search period was from the establishment of the databases to 8 January 2026. Inclusion and exclusion criteria of evidence

Inclusion criteria: (1) The study population was patients undergoing PD who were ≥ 18 years; (2) Literature on the preoperative or postoperative exercise rehabilitation in patients undergoing PD; (3) Literature types included clinical decisions, guidelines, evidence summaries, best practices, recommended practices, expert consensus, systematic reviews and meta-analyses and high-quality RCTs. (4) The languages of research were English and Chinese literature.

Exclusion criteria: (1) literature with incomplete information or unavailability of full text; (2) duplicate publications; (3) guideline abstracts, guideline interpretations or conference abstracts (4) Eligible publications were limited to English and Chinese.

Literature screening

Retrieved records were imported into Endnote, and duplicates were removed. Two researchers trained in evidence-based medicine independently screened the literature. Prescreening was performed by reading the title, abstract and keywords of the literature, and then rescreening was performed by reading the full text. During literature screening if the inclusion of retrieved literature was controversial, the third researcher was consulted to confirm.

Criteria for assessing the quality of the literature

Guidelines were evaluated using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II).32 The scale consists of 6 dimensions and 23 items with 2 additional overall evaluation items. Each item was rated on a scale of 1–7 (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Standardized percentages in each field = [(actual score - lowest possible score) / (highest possible score - lowest possible score)] × 100%. Quality grades were divided according to the percentage of standardization in each field: Grade A (highly recommended) when all 6 domains achieve scores of ≥ 60%; Grade B (recommended) when at least 3 domains score between 30% and 60%; and Grade C (not recommended) when at least 3 domains score below 30%.

The systematic review was evaluated using the 2016 version of the systematic review methodology quality assessment tool by the JBI Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care,33 which consists of 11 evaluation items, each of which contains four options, ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, and ‘Not Applicable’.

The expert consensus was evaluated using the 2016 version of the expert consensus methodology quality assessment tool by the JBI Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care,33 which consists of 6 evaluation items, each of which contains four options, ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, and ‘Not Applicable’.

Clinical decisions, evidence summaries, best practices and recommended practices were evaluated by tracing the original literature, and using appropriate JBI evaluation tool.

RCTs were evaluated using the Randomized Controlled Trial Evaluation Tool by the JBI Centre for Evidence- Based Health Care in Australia,34 which has 13 evaluation items, each with four options, ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’, and ‘Not Applicable’.

Quality appraisal

Literature quality appraisal was completed independently by two researchers who had trained in evidence-based medicine, and if there was a disagreement, a third researcher was invited to assist in the evaluation. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to determine inter-rater agreement. When evidence conclusions conflicted, the principle of ‘high-quality evidence first, newly published evidence first’ was followed.

Evidence extraction and summary

Two researchers who possessed evidence-based medicine training and work experience in hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery extracted and summarized the evidence according to the following principles: (1) combine evidence with consistent content, and prioritize concise, logical evidence; (2) combine evidence with complementary content according to linguistic logic; (3) when the content from multiple sources conflicted, integrate according to the principles of high grade, high quality, newly published evidence.

According to the JBI evidence pre-classification and evidence rank system (2014 Edition),35 the level of evidence was divided into 1 to 5 levels from high to low, 1a was the highest level and 5c was the lowest level. In addition, the recommendation level was divided into A (strong recommendation) and B (weak recommendation) according to the JBI evidence FAME structure, which includes the feasibility (F), appropriateness (A), meaningfulness (M) and effectiveness (E).

Literature quality evaluation results

Clinical decisions

A clinical decision36 was included. After tracing the original literature, we obtained a guideline37 that was already included in this study.

Quality evaluation of guidelines

A total of four guidelines were included,13,25,37,38 all with ICC values > 0.75, with good consistency of evaluation. The quality evaluation results are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Quality evaluation of expert consensus

An expert consensus article was included.26 After quality appraisal, all items were rated ‘Yes’ and thus met the inclusion threshold.

Quality evaluation of systematic reviews

Three systematic reviews published within the past 5 years were included.39, 40, 41 All three were considered methodologically acceptable for inclusion, and their quality evaluation results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Quality evaluation of the randomized controlled trials

Six RCTs were included in this study.10,20, 21, 22, 23, 24 The quality evaluation results are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Notably, due to the nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and interventionists was not feasible in any of the included studies. Additionally, only three studies20, 21, 22 implemented blinding of outcome assessors, while the other three10,23,24 had unclear or no blinding. The completeness of follow-up was unclear in four studies,20,22, 23, 24 and two studies10,23 did not include all randomized subjects in the outcome analysis.

Discussion

Main findings

Exercise rehabilitation addresses the unique physiological challenges of pancreaticoduodenectomy

Preoperative exercise: establishing energy reserves for surgical stress

Evidence 1 emphasizes the purpose of preoperative exercise. Preoperative exercise aims to establish adequate physiological and functional reserves to support the mobilization of reserves induced by surgical stress.13,25,26,42 This is particularly important for patients undergoing PD, as they typically face a triple burden: sarcopenia, cachexia driven by tumor-induced metabolic derangement, and the cumulative toxicities of neoadjuvant chemotherapy,4, 5, 6 which leads to a significant decline in physical function in patients before surgery. Both the guidelines and expert consensuses43, 44, 45 emphasize the critical role of exercise in treating sarcopenia and cachexia in patients with cancer. Accumulating evidence24,39, 40, 41 further shows that preoperative exercise can improve both preoperative and postoperative physical function in PD patients, with gains most consistently observed in muscle mass and cardiopulmonary fitness.

Early postoperative mobilization: accelerating multi-system recovery

Early mobilization is perceived to be the key for successful enhanced recovery. Evidence 2, derived from patients undergoing PD and other pancreatic surgery, supports the benefits of early postoperative mobilization in accelerating multi-system recovery, such as respiratory system, gastrointestinal system, and musculoskeletal system. In fact, the rationale for early mobilization is deeply rooted in the pathophysiology of surgical stress. PD triggers a systemic inflammatory response, characterized by cytokine release, insulin resistance, and catabolism, which impairs tissue oxygenation and delays wound healing.42 Postoperative mobilization counteracts these effects by enhancing cardiopulmonary efficiency, promoting gastrointestinal motility, and stimulating anabolic signaling pathways in skeletal muscle.

Multidisciplinary team collaboration and pre-implementation training

Team composition and systematic training

Evidence 3 emphasizes the necessity of forming a multidisciplinary team. The development and implementation of exercise rehabilitation programs require the integration of expertise from multiple disciplines.46 This approach aligns with the broader ERAS philosophy, which recognizes that optimizing surgical outcomes requires coordinated interventions across multiple domains rather than isolated efforts by individual disciplines.13,25 What that team should look like, however, is not standardized. Given the anatomical and physiological complexity of PD, the team needs to span clinical, rehabilitative, nutritional, and psychological expertise, including surgeons to assess anatomical constraints and fistula risk, physical therapists to design progressive mobilization programs, nutritionists to match caloric intake with energy expenditure, psychologists to address anxiety surrounding drain management and wound integrity, and nurses to deliver exercise interventions and coordinate the work of various specialties.46

Evidence 4 addresses an easily overlooked prerequisite: systematic training of team members on PD-specific exercise rehabilitation prior to exercise program initiation to ensure maximum therapeutic benefit and safety. This training is clinically important. In fact, there is wide variability in staff knowledge about how to modify mobilization for specific complications. Therefore, general exercise rehabilitation training is not applicable. PD-tailored training should include: identifying specific exercise contraindications of PD patients, such as signs of intra-abdominal hemorrhage and anastomotic fistula; managing drainage tubes during activities; modifying exercise prescription for patients with DGE; and establishing emergency response protocols for adverse events during exercise.

The role of nurses in multidisciplinary teams

Nurses occupy a distinctive position in this framework due to their continuous patient contact throughout the perioperative period. They are well-positioned to coordinate multidisciplinary collaboration, deliver health education, conduct dynamic assessments and continuous supervision, identify early warning signs of complications or adverse events, and adjust exercise program based on patient response.47,48 However, the evidence remains insufficient regarding how nurses can optimize their contribution to motor rehabilitation in PD patients through specific mechanisms. Further targeted empirical studies are needed to clarify the framework of responsibilities and the allocation of tasks across the various perioperative stages.

Comprehensive education, assessment and preparation: establishing the safety foundation

The health education, exercise assessment and pre-exercise preparation covered in evidence 5–19 form the safety foundation on which exercise implementation rests.

Health education

Evidence 5 and 6 emphasize the importance of health education. Targeted preoperative counseling and health education can enhance patients' understanding of the principles and benefits of exercise rehabilitation, thereby stimulating their intrinsic motivation and promoting active participation. Studies47,48 have shown that patients who understand the physiological mechanisms underlying exercise benefits (such as enhanced oxygenation, accelerated gastrointestinal recovery, and increased musculoskeletal mass) demonstrate higher adherence compared to those who receive prescription instructions without explanatory context. It is recommended to provide health education in oral, written, and multimedia forms during the perioperative period, tailored to patients' health literacy levels and cultural backgrounds. This multimodal approach accommodates different learning preferences and facilitates retention of complex medical information.

Comprehensive assessment

A comprehensive baseline assessment is necessary to determine the optimal timing for initiating the exercise program and to provide the basis for formulating the exercise plan and the required level of supervision.37 Evidence 7 provides a framework for this. Evidence 8 emphasizes repeated evaluations at the end of exercise training. Such longitudinal assessment is reasonable as it allows for monitoring the effectiveness of the exercise.

During the postoperative period, PD patients face an increased risk of condition deterioration. Evidence 9 calls for dynamic monitoring of hemodynamic indicators, physical symptoms, incision integrity, and drain status before and during exercise. Exercise should be stopped immediately if any stopping criterion is met,11,22 for example, unstable hemodynamics (elevated or low blood pressure, rapid or slow heart rate, decreased oxygen saturation), altered mental status, uncomfortable physical symptoms (vertigo, nausea or vomiting, uncontrollable pain, fatigue or muscle weakness), signs of active bleeding or occurrence of adverse events such as falls or accidental drainage-tube dislodgement.22 Although one included study reported operational criteria for hemodynamic instability during early mobilization after pancreatic surgery, standardized PD-specific hemodynamic thresholds for stopping postoperative exercise have not yet been established. In that study, mobilization was modified or discontinued according to blood pressure, respiratory rate, heart rate, arousability, and patient symptoms.

Although the above evidence is classified as high-level (1b and 1c), it is derived not only from PD patients but also from a broader surgical population. Given the disease-specific characteristics of PD patients, a more targeted assessment framework remains to be established. At the same time, a comprehensive assessment requires specialized equipment, trained personnel, and sufficient time, posing feasibility challenges in resource-constrained clinical settings. Future research should explore streamlined assessment protocols that preserve safety while reducing implementation barriers, for instance, validating abbreviated fitness batteries or nurse-administered risk stratification tools that can be integrated into routine perioperative workflows.

Preparation before exercise

Evidence 10-19 address the preoperative and postoperative pre-exercise preparation strategies that create the physiological and psychological conditions necessary for safe exercise participation.

Evidence 10 advocates the adoption of multimodal prehabilitation. Exercise, nutrition and psychological intervention are currently the commonly used triple prehabilitation strategies.24 Broader prehabilitation also involves smoking and alcohol cessation, correction of anemia, control of inflammation, improvement of cognitive function and frailty.26,36 This multimodal approach is grounded in the recognition that PD patients are burdened by multidimensional deficits that cannot be addressed by exercise alone.

Evidence 11 emphasizes the importance of preoperative smoking and alcohol cessation to decrease wound healing complications and respiratory complications.

Nutritional insufficiency restricts the anabolic response to exercise, and training performed under malnourished conditions may promote muscle catabolism rather than enhance physical performance. Preoperative nutritional risk is observed in 61.9% of PD patients, with 25.9% at high risk,49,50 while postoperative malnutrition reaches 69% due to digestive tract reconstruction and metabolic disorders following pancreatic resection.38,51, 52, 53 Therefore, nutritional screening and support are necessary throughout the perioperative period. Evidence 12 recommends using Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002) for preoperative nutritional screening of PD patients. Patients who screen positive (NRS 2002 ≥ 3) require a full nutritional workup, and based on this, nutritional treatment plans should be formulated.38,54 Evidence 13 sets out the indications for preoperative nutritional support, and evidence 14 and 15 provide the timing and specific goals of preoperative and postoperative nutritional support for PD patients. Particularly worth mentioning is that the standard recommendation is 7–14 days of preoperative nutritional support.38 When malnutrition persists but surgery cannot wait, no consensus currently exists on the optimal approach—whether to provide abbreviated nutritional support or to accept the oncological risk of surgical delay.

Evidence 16–19 summarizes a series of optimization strategies before postoperative exercise, including health education, alleviating patient anxiety about activity causing wound separation, early tubes removal, multimodal analgesia (MMA), and prevention of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV).13,22,25,26 This evidence highlights the most frequently cited barriers to mobilization in clinical practice. First, the anxiety about wound dehiscence is particularly salient in PD patients. Nurses can address this anxiety through targeted education that explains closure techniques, demonstrates gentle mobilization methods that minimize abdominal strain, and provides reassurance through frequent incision inspection and documentation of intact healing. Second, indwelling nasogastric tubes, urinary catheters, and abdominal drains limit patient activity, and early removal is advised by guidelines.26 Notably, while drain removal on day 3 is considered safe for PD patients,25 it may need to be extended to 7–14 days in cases of pancreatic fistula or biliary leak. Hence, nurses should offer pre-mobilization education on drainage tube care during walking to promote mobility and avoid inadvertent removal. Last, Evidence 18 and 19 highlight MMA and PONV prevention, which are based on expert consensus and clinical guidelines and are graded as Level 5b. Despite the low evidence level, these measures are critical for early ambulation, as they can lessen hemodynamic instability and discomfort in PD patients.22,25,26

Individualization as a core principle: formulating targeted exercise rehabilitation programs

Exercise timing

Evidence 20 and 21 address the timing for initiating exercise. Preoperative exercise should be started as early as the surgical pathway allows. The ideal window is 3–6 weeks before surgery,13,25 but surgical urgency often compresses this in practice,36 creating a fundamental tension between the need for sufficient conditioning duration to induce meaningful physiological adaptations and the imperative to avoid delaying cancer treatment. The evidence from Yuan et al.24 is reassuring on this point, their two-week program produced functional gains comparable to longer interventions, suggesting that even a brief, intensive preoperative course can be worthwhile. The neoadjuvant treatment period deserves particular attention here: most regimens run 3–6 months, offering a substantial and largely untapped window for preoperative conditioning.20,39,40

Postoperative mobilization should begin as soon as the patient awakens from anesthesia, which is consistent with ERAS principles13 and aligns with the findings of Olsén et al.22 that same-day mobilization produces better oxygenation than next-day mobilization. It is worth noting that the prerequisite for early activity is hemodynamic stability and the absence of major complications (such as pancreatic fistula, DGE, intra-abdominal hemorrhage). However, these conditions are not always guaranteed in PD patients, where significant blood loss, fluid shifts, and vasopressor use are not uncommon. Although supported by high-level evidence (1c), the clinical priority is not to enforce immediate mobilization, but to dynamically evaluate physiological and logistical readiness, balance benefits and risks, and tailor decisions to each patient—that is, delaying mobilization if conditions are unfavorable, yet not abandoning the objective entirely.

Exercise type

Evidence 22–24 summarize the types of exercise for PD patients, including respiratory training, aerobic exercise, and resistance exercise, and suggest combined application.37 This is theoretically reasonable because impaired respiratory mechanics, reduced cardiopulmonary reserve, and muscle wasting independently or jointly compromise surgical tolerance, and no single modality addresses all three. The most clinically pertinent concern is resistance training. Although it is the most effective countermeasure against muscle atrophy, high-intensity postoperative resistance training raises a legitimate concern about elevated intra-abdominal pressure and the risk of anastomotic disruption or pancreatic fistula exacerbation. Fortunately, recent evidence23 confirms that early postoperative progressive resistance exercise does not increase these risks, which confirms the safety of including it in early postoperative exercise. A prudent approach is to avoid high-intensity resistance training in the early postoperative period, and instead adopt moderate-intensity regimens targeting major muscle groups, with particular attention to lower extremity and core stabilization exercises that enhance functional mobility while minimizing abdominal strain.

Exercise parameters

No standardized exercise parameters (frequency, intensity and duration) exist for PD patients, but relevant references are available. Based on the quality of the literature, evidence 25–32 was selected as recommendations.

Preoperative exercise parameters need to be individualized to each patient's baseline, Evidence 26–29 provides specific parameters. While these benchmarks offer a starting point, they are sourced from both PD patients and the general surgical population,20,24,37,39,40 and thus should be adopted with caution. For PD patients with poor baseline physical conditions, severe complications or advanced age, downward adjustments are warranted. For instance, PD patients experiencing baseline dyspnea due to pleural effusion, ascites, or anemia may not tolerate even 15 minutes of continuous inspiratory muscle training, thus necessitating an interval-based protocol (e.g., three 5-min sessions).

Evidence 30 and 31 emphasize that postoperative exercise parameters follow the principles of individualization and progressive advancement, and Evidence 24 provides a progression framework—from in-bed to out-of-bed activities. A key limitation is that no high-quality evidence offers PD-specific progression benchmarks. The field endorses individualization as a principle while providing no standardized criteria to operationalize it, leaving clinicians and nurses to navigate by general principles and clinical experience. Future prospective studies should develop validated progression criteria grounded in PD-relevant clinical markers (drain status, anastomotic integrity indicators, inflammatory markers and nutritional parameters) rather than the generic functional milestones borrowed from other surgical populations.

Evidence 32 advocates the principle of “frequency over intensity” for early postoperative exercise. Although derived from the hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) surgical population26 and rated as Level 5b, this evidence precisely captures a critical insight specific to the early postoperative catabolic state, where exercise serves a permissive rather than conditioning function, reflecting a paradigm shift from exercise as physiological stressor to exercise as metabolic facilitator.

Hybrid supervision models: balancing efficacy and feasibility

Evidence 33 emphasizes that supervised exercise programs produce better engagement and better outcomes than unsupervised ones,21,22,37 and the evidence on this is consistent. Supervision serves multiple functions: providing real-time feedback on exercise form to prevent injury, adjusting intensity based on patient response, delivering motivational support to overcome discomfort and fatigue, and monitoring for adverse events. For PD patients, however, there are unique supervisory challenges. The prolonged recovery trajectory, the geographic dispersion of patients often traveling from distant regions for surgery at specialized centers, and the high symptom burden create logistical barriers to sustained in-person supervision. A hybrid supervision model that combines initial face-to-face supervision with subsequent remote monitoring might be a practical solution to balance the efficacy and feasibility.

Home-based exercise has real advantages: flexibility, cost-effectiveness, and the ability to continue rehabilitation beyond the hospital stay,55,56 but it comes with conditions. Evidence 34 supports its feasibility, while noting three critical prerequisites: patient self-efficacy for independent program completion, mechanisms for remote support, and availability of home exercise equipment.37,39,40 The handoff from supervised hospital exercise to unsupervised home continuation is where adherence most commonly breaks down. Evidence 35 proposes strategies to enhance adherence: wearable devices (sports bracelets, accelerometers), exercise diaries, telephone follow-ups, and family involvement.10,21,39,40 Family members play a core role in home exercise.21 Their day-to-day encouragement and willingness to participate alongside the patient can make the difference between a program that continues and one that quietly stops. Nurses are well placed to bring families into the rehabilitation process, teaching them what to look for, how to support safe exercise, and why their involvement matters.

Finally, a key issue also needs to be considered: Can nurses detect early warning signs of exercise-related adverse events through remote monitoring? Digital health technologies, such as remote video monitoring, mobile health applications, wearable sensors,21,40 offer promising solutions. However, the evidence supporting their efficacy and safety specifically in PD exercise rehabilitation is limited. Large-scale implementation should therefore be approached cautiously until further evidence is available. Targeted clinical trials are needed first.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This evidence summary provides a reference for the formulation and implementation of exercise rehabilitation programs for PD patients in clinical practice. However, in the absence of high-quality PD-specific evidence, extrapolated evidence should be applied with caution. The application of evidence requires integration with the clinical experience of health care professionals, the actual physical condition of patients, and patient preferences. Future research should prioritize conducting high-quality RCTs focused on exercise rehabilitation for PD patients, so as to generate direct evidence for formulating targeted and individualized exercise rehabilitation programs.

Nurses can undertake multiple important roles in the exercise rehabilitation process for PD patients: (1) coordinators among the multidisciplinary team, ensuring smooth communication among members; (2) primary implementers and supervisors of exercise interventions, guiding and monitoring patient exercise to ensure effectiveness; (3) health and exercise assessors, responsible for baseline assessments before exercise to guide the formulation of exercise strategies, and for dynamic assessments during exercise to promptly adjust strategies and ensure patient safety; (4) health educators, helping patients understand the goals, content, expected benefits, and necessity of exercise rehabilitation, thereby promoting active patient participation. In the future, more emphasis should be placed on systematic training of nurses' evidence-based awareness and practical skills, so as to fully leverage their unique advantages.

Furthermore, the optimal model for nursing staff involvement in exercise rehabilitation for PD patients (including specific duty frameworks, certification pathways, and implementation strategies) still requires further research, as it is not yet a mature best practice supported by current evidence. Establishing certification pathways within professional nursing organizations, such as a “Certified Oncology Exercise Rehabilitation Nurse,” may be a future direction worth considering, to formalize this specialized competence and incentivize skill development.

Strengths and limitations

This research has several strengths. Firstly, our retrieval method follows the “6S” evidence pyramid model, and retrieval strategy was formulated by the research team after discussion, ensuring a thorough search of the evidence. Secondly, given the scarcity of relevant literature on exercise rehabilitation for PD patients, this study systematically integrated high-quality evidence in this field and provided a practical framework covering nine areas of evidence, which serves as a reference for the formulation of clinical exercise programs.

However, this study has some limitations. First, only Chinese and English literature are included in this study. Despite efforts to conduct a comprehensive literature search, there is a possibility of missing relevant evidence in non-English languages. Second, the evidence in this study is derived from various countries, where differences in cultural background, cognition and concept may influence research results. Therefore, the evidence requires continuous updating in future research, and clinical transformation and application should consider the specific context. Third, and most importantly, there remains a shortage of high-quality studies specifically addressing exercise rehabilitation for PD patients. The included studies were not exclusively focused on PD patients but also involved mixed populations including those undergoing HPB surgery and a broader group of surgical patients. Although we carefully adopted evidence based on common pathophysiological mechanisms, the heterogeneity of the research subjects weakened the applicability of the research results to the PD patient population, as extrapolated evidence may not fully cover the risks of specific surgeries. Finally, while we have provided safety considerations based on available evidence, standardized, validated, PD-specific safety parameters and exercise progression criteria have not yet been established in the literature.

More large-sample, multicenter RCTs specifically targeting PD patients still need to be conducted in the future to enrich the evidence base and develop tailored evidence-based exercise rehabilitation programs for this specific patient population.

Conclusions

Through a systematic and scientific evidence-based approach, this study summarized 35 pieces of best evidence in nine dimensions, including exercise purpose, multidisciplinary team formulation, health education, exercise assessment, exercise preparation, exercise timing, exercise type, exercise parameters, and exercise supervision. These findings provide a reference for clinical health care professionals in formulating and implementing exercise rehabilitation programs for PD patients, and highlight the need for cautious application of indirect evidence where PD-specific data are lacking. Future research should prioritize high-quality RCTs to provide stronger direct evidence for constructing targeted and individualized exercise rehabilitation programs, ultimately promoting the rapid recovery of this high-risk surgical group.
