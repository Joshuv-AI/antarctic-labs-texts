Early-life gut microbiome composition and rotavirus vaccine-induced IgA responses in U.S. infants: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Janiret Narváez-Miranda, Michael B Sohn, Daniel Velasquez-Portocarrero, Ann L Gill, Robert Beblavy, Darline Castro-Melendez, Kelechi Ejiofor, Xing Qiu, Nathan Laniewski, Brandon Groff, Jessica Brunner, Meghan Ras, Alena Leger, Allison Macomber, Sarah L Caddy, Baoming Jiang, Tom O'Connor, Steven R Gill, Kristin Scheible

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRotavirus remains a leading cause of childhood mortality worldwide, despite the widespread introduction of oral rotavirus vaccines. Evidence linking the gut microbiome to vaccine response is inconsistent and limited in U.S. populations. This study investigates the development of the infant gut microbiome and its association with immunogenicity following RotaTeq administration in U.S. infants.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal analysis of infants in Rochester, New York, using 16S rRNA sequencing to assess microbiome composition at one (M1), sixth (M6), and twelfth (M12) months of age. Rotavirus-IgA serologies were measured at M6 and M12 to assess RotaTeq vaccine seroresponse. Clinical metadata were used to assess factors associated with microbial diversity and rotavirus-IgA titres over the first year of life. We examined associations between (1) M1 microbiome and M6 rotavirus-IgA; (2) M6 microbiome and M6 rotavirus-IgA; and (3) M6 microbiome and M12 rotavirus-IgA.FindingsHigher gut microbial alpha diversity at M1 was associated with higher rotavirus-IgA titres at M6 (N = 47, β: 2·06, 95% CI: [0·31–3·99], p = 0·024). Alpha diversity at M6 was not associated with concurrent rotavirus-IgA responses (N = 56, β: 0·73, 95% CI: [−0·856, 2·313], p = 0·36) but was associated with higher rotavirus-IgA at M12 (N = 52, β: 1·47, 95% CI: [0·127, 2·805], p = 0·033). Rotavirus-IgA responses were associated with specific microbial taxa across timepoints, with both positive and negative associations observed.InterpretationIn a healthy U.S. infant cohort, early-life gut microbiome diversity and composition were associated with rotavirus-IgA responses following RotaTeq vaccination. This study advances understanding of microbiome–vaccine interactions in high-income settings.FundingOffice of the Director of the National Institutes of Health, National Institute of Mental Health of the National Institutes of Health, and the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences of the National Institutes of Health.Keywords: Immunoglobulin A, Microbiota, Infant microbiome, Rotavirus vaccines, Rotavirus immunogenicity, Early-life microbiome

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Oral Rotavirus vaccines have significantly reduced child mortality due to rotavirus worldwide, but there is significant variability in immune responses across geographic regions. One proposed driver of this variation has been the developing infant gut microbiome. A broad search on PubMed, from August 2021 to May 2025, using the search terms (“rotavirus” OR “rotavirus vaccine”) AND (“microbiome” OR “microbiota”) AND (“immunogenicity” OR “immunoglobulin A”), identified eight published studies that directly assessed the relationship between the infant gut microbiome and oral rotavirus vaccine (ORVs) immune responses. The majority of these studies were conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where diminished ORV immunogenicity is common. Findings evaluating the association between the infant gut microbiome and ORV immunogenicity have been inconsistent, with studies reporting infant microbiome features associated with ORV immunogenicity, whereas others have found no clear relationship. Multiple factors present in LMIC, including high prevalence of circulating rotavirus, higher maternal antibody levels, variable nutritional status, environmental exposures, and co-infection, may shape both microbiome development and infant immune responses, potentially confounding the observed associations. Only a few studies have included infants from high-income countries (HICs), and studies investigating this relationship in U.S.-based cohorts using both microbiome profiling and serological outcomes remains limited.

Added value of this study

This study provides an analysis of the infant gut microbiome and rotavirus-IgA titres in a U.S.-based cohort. By leveraging a well-characterised longitudinal birth cohort with prospectively collected metadata and low rotavirus infection prevalence, we minimised many of the confounding factors present in previous studies. We measured rotavirus-IgA as a measure of ORV immunogenicity. We conducted a bivariate analysis to identify potential confounders to the microbiome-ORV immunogenicity association in our population. Through 16S rRNA gene sequencing and statistical approaches robust to limitations posed by compositional microbiome data, we systematically assessed the independent contributions of the microbiome while accounting for other potential influences. We identified a significant association between early-life microbiome composition and rotavirus-specific IgA responses following RotaTeq vaccination, strengthening the evidence for the role of the gut microbiome in shaping oral vaccine serologic outcomes longitudinally.

Implications of all the available evidence

Together with prior work, our findings support a role for the developing gut microbiome in shaping ORVs immunogenicity. This relationship may be obscured by interacting factors present in previous studies conducted in LMIC, such as high prevalence of natural rotavirus infection and maternal antibodies. Our results suggest that in HICs, the impact of the microbiome on vaccine responses may be more readily detected. These findings strengthen the rationale for considering the infant gut microbiome as a modifiable determinant of vaccine performance and underscore the importance of cross-geographic studies to disentangle universal from context-specific drivers of ORV immunogenicity.

Introduction

Diarrhoeal diseases remain a leading cause of child mortality worldwide, and rotavirus is a major contributor across all age groups.1 Global introduction of the oral live-attenuated rotavirus vaccine (ORVs) to infants 2–8 months of age resulted in a drop in severe paediatric gastroenteritis and child death cases.2 ORV's immunogenicity exhibits geographical variability,3 with lower responses reported in low-middle-income countries (LMICs) compared to high-income countries (HICs).

Several factors can influence ORV immune responses, including the prevalence of rotavirus, transfer of maternal antibodies, and co-administration of other oral vaccines.3, 4, 5 For example, maternal antibodies transferred through breastmilk associate with lower ORV immune responses in high rotavirus-prevalence LMICs.6 In contrast, no effect of breastmilk antibodies on ORV responses has been observed in the US.7 Another factor hypothesised to influence ORV immune responses is the gut microbiome. ORV replication in the gut stimulates mucosal immunity that protects against natural infection; a healthy microbiome is essential for immune development, evidenced in several studies demonstrating adverse immune-related outcomes such as allergies and diabetes in dysbiotic conditions.8,9 While the microbiota-immune systems interaction is incompletely understood, bacterial members of the microbiome produce metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids)10 and antigens (e.g., polysaccharide A)11 that can augment innate and adaptive immune cell signalling, ultimately influencing IgA production in the gut.

Several studies have aimed to identify an association between the developing infant gut microbiome and antigen-specific responses, such as to ORV. Most studies have been conducted in LMICs, where the ORV immunogenicity is markedly variable and confounded by high rotavirus prevalence. Only three studies have included HIC as comparison groups, and the single study among those three that characterised both ORV immune responses and gut microbiome had null findings.12, 13, 14 Heterogeneity between study populations and limitations posed by the compositional nature of microbiome data have led to inconclusive results regarding the independent effect of gut microbiome on ORV immunogenicity.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 We hypothesised that by leveraging a US population with high vaccine uptake, low rotavirus infection prevalence, and prospectively collected metadata and breastfeeding behaviour, we could better isolate the effect of the infant gut microbiome on ORV immune responses. In this study, we employ a rigorous longitudinal approach using analytic methods that address the limitations of relative abundance measures to characterise microbiome composition, vaccine responses, and their relationship in early life.

Methods

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the University of Rochester School of Medicine and Dentistry Institutional Review Board (#58456). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. All data were de-identified prior to analysis to protect participant confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

From December 2015 to April 2019, pregnant participants were recruited from outpatient obstetrics clinics affiliated with the University of Rochester Medical Center (URMC). After informed consent, pregnant participants and their newborns were enrolled as part of the Understanding Pregnancy Signals and Infant Development and Environmental Influences on Child Health Outcomes (UPSIDE-ECHO) study.20 Eligibility criteria for pregnant participants included: <14 weeks of gestation, age 18 or older, singleton pregnancy, no known substance problems or a history of psychotic illness, and ability to communicate in English. Exclusion criteria included women with major endocrine disorders (e.g., polycystic ovary syndrome, pre-pregnancy diabetes), high-risk pregnancies, or significant obstetric problems. Infants born before 37 weeks of gestation were excluded from the postnatal study phases. Study visits, including physical exams, biospecimen collection, and questionnaire completion, were completed at each trimester and at birth, one (M1), six (M6), and twelve (M12) months postnatally.

Rotavirus vaccination was administered by primary care providers in accordance with CDC guidelines and standard URMC practice. Consistent with U.S. standard practice, two types of rotavirus vaccine were offered in the cohort: RotaTeq, which is given in a three-dose series at two, four, and six months of age, or Rotarix, which is a two-dose series given at two and four months of age. The first dose is recommended before 15 weeks, with full vaccination completed before eight months of age.

Study population

We utilised overlapping subsets of the UPSIDE-ECHO cohort, summarised in Fig. 1A, to characterise longitudinal shifts in gut microbiome composition and ORV immune responses. Rotavirus vaccination history (vaccine type, number of doses, vaccination dates) was pulled from participant electronic medical records and verified using the New York State Immunisation Information System (NYSIIS). RotaTeq was the predominant vaccine administered (84·2%); therefore, we restricted the final analytical sample to infants vaccinated with RotaTeq to ensure homogeneity. Of a total of 353 ECHO participants, 287 had microbiome measures. Samples analysed for alpha diversity, beta diversity, and individual taxa abundance that met quality criteria are indicated in Fig. 1A and further detailed in Supplemental Methods (Appendix p.4). Of the 287 participants with microbiome analysis, 284 had information on rotavirus vaccination, and 177 of those were both vaccinated with RotaTeq and had samples available for serology. Eighty-eight subjects had both microbiome and serology data for at least one of the microbiome-rotavirus IgA combined analyses. Exclusion criteria included lack of microbiome sequencing data (N = 66), plasma (N = 87) or vaccination records (N = 3).

Biospecimen collection

Cord blood was collected immediately after delivery by trained staff and placed in K2-EDTA tubes (Becton Dickinson; Franklin Lakes, NJ). For postnatal M6 and M12 visits, 10 mL of blood was collected through venipuncture and placed in K2-EDTA tubes. The collected blood was centrifuged for 10 min at room temperature at 2000 G to extract plasma. Rectal swabs were collected at birth and postnatally at the M1, M6, and M12 visits. A sterile flocked swab (Copan Diagnostics; Murrieta, CA) applicator was dipped into sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Corning; Manassas, VA) and inserted into the child's anal orifice up to the flocked portion of the swab and placed inside a sterile conical tube (Corning; Manassas, VA). All biospecimens were stored at −80 °C until the time of the given assay.20

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from rectal samples by homogenisation using FastPrep mechanical lysis (MPBio, Solon, OH) followed by the Quick-DNA™ Fecal/Soil Microbe Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research; Irvine, CA). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of 16S rRNA were amplified with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) using dual-indexed primers specific to the V3–V4 regions (319F: 5′ ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG 3′; 806R: 3′ GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT 5′). (Holm, JB. 2019) Amplicons were then normalised using SequalPrep™ plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA) and pooled. Pooled libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq (Illumina; San Diego, CA) at the University of Rochester Genomics Research Center. Each sequencing run included the following controls: 1) positive control containing a 1:1:1:1:1 mixture of Staphylococcus aureus, Lactococcus lactis, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Streptococcus mutans, and Escherichia coli; 2) negative control consisting of sterile water; and 3) extraction controls of buffers only.

Taxonomical classification

Raw read data from the Illumina MiSeq was first converted into a FASTQ format 2 × 312 paired-end sequenced file using bcl2fastq version 1·8·4, provided by Illumina. Reads were multiplexed using a configuration described previously by Holm JB.1 The first 24–29 bases in each paired read represented an adaptor, followed by an 8-base barcode, an overlapping tag sequence, and a heterogeneity spacer. After that, a gene-specific primer was present, leading to the target 16S rRNA sequence.

Demultiplexed reads were then imported into QIIME2 (version 2022·2) for subsequent analysis.21 Reads were demultiplexed, requiring exact barcode matches, and 16S primer sequences were removed, allowing 20% mismatched and requiring at least 18 bases. Next, the data were cleaned, joined, and denoised using DADA2. The totality of the infant samples spanned five sequencing runs. For all sequencing runs, forward and reverse reads were trimmed based on median quality score of 20 in order to optimise the number of error-free reads during denoising and therefore, detect true sequence variants. Error profiles were learnt with a sample of one million reads, and a maximum expected error of two was allowed. All batches were then merged before taxonomical classification. The taxonomical classification was performed using a custom Bayesian classifier trained with full-length 16S sequences from the SILVA classifiers.22,23 Samples with read numbers less than or equal to those of negative controls were eliminated.

Microbiome data were processed to reduce noise and improve the reliability of subsequent analysis. We applied a minimum sample read-depth cutoff and a minimum taxa-prevalence cutoff. Depending on the analysis, taxa were aggregated to a defined taxonomic level, collapsing features present in fewer than a predetermined percentage of samples into their parent taxon while retaining more prevalent taxa as separate features (Supplemental Table S1, Appendix p.3).

Rotavirus vaccine immune outcomes

Rotavirus-IgA was used as a measure of ORV immunogenicity. A total of N = 177 unique participants were both vaccinated with RotaTeq and had samples available for Rotavirus-IgA measurements. Rotavirus-IgA titres from plasma were measured through a RotaTeq antigen-specific Enzyme-Linked Immunoassay (EIA).21,24 In brief, 96-well microplates were coated with 100 μl/well of rabbit hyperimmune serum to the rhesus rotavirus strain (RRV) (Source: CDC) at a 1:10,000 dilution and then incubated overnight. The microplates were incubated with 200 μl Blocking Buffer (5% wt/vol BD Difco Skim milk in PBS) at 37 °C for 1 h. Microplates were then incubated for 1 h in duplicate with 100 μl/well of clarified lysates of RotaTeq G1 strain-infected MA104 cell cultures in working dilutions of 7·2 × 10ˆ5 FFU/mL for RotaTeq antigen. A 100 μl/well of serially diluted test plasma (1:20–2:10,240) was added and incubated for 2 h. Followed by adding 100 μl/well of biotin-conjugated goat anti-human IgA (KPL, USA) diluted 1:2000 and incubated for 1 h. Extravidin (100 μl/well) (Sigma, USA) diluted 1:3000 was added to the wells and incubated for 1 h, and then the reactions were developed with 100 μl/well 3,30,5,50-tetramethylbenzidine (Sigma, USA) and stopped with 100 μl/well 1 N HCl. Plate optical density was measured using a BioTek 800 TS Plate reader set to 450 nm reading reference wavelength. Rotavirus-IgA titres in plasma were calculated as the reciprocal of the highest dilution that gave a mean OD greater than the cutoff value (3 standard deviations above the mean OD of the negative control serum wells).

Viral antigen was expressed and produced in MA-104 cells (RRID:CVCL_3845). Cellosaurus flags MA-104 as a problematic cell line due to a misidentification of its species origin: this line was initially thought to be of rhesus macaque origin but is now identified as African green monkey/vervet monkey (Chlorocebus pygerythrus) and is registered by ICLAC as ICLAC-00077. Since MA-104 cells were used exclusively as a permissive substrate for antigen expression and production, and because our conclusions do not rely on the rhesus macaque origin or any MA-104-specific cellular biology, this does not affect the interpretation of the results.

The rotavirus-IgA seroresponse to RotaTeq or Rotarix was assessed by seroconversion and rotavirus-IgA titres, expressed as log2-transformed values. We defined rotavirus-IgA measured at birth (cord blood) as the baseline for seropositivity and seroconversion. Seropositivity was defined as a titre ≥40, and seroconversion was defined as either (a) seronegative at baseline (titre <40) and seropositive at follow-up, or (b) seropositive at baseline with a ≥4-fold rise in titre at follow-up. Seroresponse was evaluated at M6 and M12 visits using log2-transformed titres.

Measurement and selection of covariates

Following study enrolment, trained staff administered questionnaires to the infant's parents to gather information on lifestyle, past pregnancies, medical conditions, medication use, and sociodemographic characteristics. Information was updated at postnatal visits.

Potential confounding factors were determined by literature review,3,5,6,9,14,17,18,25 and included demographics, potential sources of rotavirus exposure, social determinants of health, and diet-related variables. Covariates considered infant sex (female or male), infant or maternal ethnicity-race (White or Non-White), mode of delivery (vaginal or caesarean section), maternal education (<high school or high school, some college, bachelors, post-graduate degree), season of sampling (winter, spring, summer, fall), beneficiary of Medicaid during pregnancy (yes or no), infant age (weeks), birthweight (z-score), pregnancy body mass index (BMI) (kg/mˆ2), number of vaccine doses, maternal parity, and feeding type. Maternal parity was defined as a binary indicator of prior live or stillbirths before the index pregnancy (yes/no) and served as a proxy for infant exposure to older siblings. Antibiotic exposure (yes/no) and history of gastroenteritis were assessed based on reported use between study visits and confirmed through electronic medical records.

In this study, race and ethnicity are treated as separate variables, reflecting US federal data standards and conceptual differences between the two constructs. In this framework, race is treated as a social category tied to perceived physical traits and assigned social position, while ethnicity refers to shared culture, language, and heritage.26,27 Both are used as distinct demographic variables that can capture differences in the population based on their genetic and cultural backgrounds.

Feeding type was evaluated in two ways: categorically as exclusive breastfeeding, formula, or combination, and continuously as breastfeeding duration, which was summarised as the age of the infant at the time of cessation (in weeks); formula-fed infants were encoded as zero weeks for breastfeeding duration. We derived a proportional breastfeeding duration variable by dividing the breastfeeding duration reported at the M12 visit by the infant's age at the corresponding visit to approximate cumulative breastfeeding exposure at earlier timepoints. For example, a parent reporting 24 weeks of infant breastfeeding total in the first year would be coded as 100% at the M6 visit and 50% at the M12 visit. Using statistical variable selection based on bivariate associations with the outcome and predictor, the minimally sufficient set of variables for inclusion in our models were: infant age, feeding type, and history of gastroenteritis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise population characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were analysed by non-parametric or χ2 tests as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0·05, with multiple testing adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

For gut microbiome analyses, alpha diversity was quantified using Shannon's index and compared across visits and covariates. Longitudinal diversity was modelled using linear mixed-effects models (LMER) to account for repeated measures, and estimated marginal means were used for pairwise contrasts. Beta diversity was calculated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, and overall compositional differences by visit were assessed with permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) blocking for participant repeated measures. Beta diversity dispersion was evaluated using a permutation-based test of multivariate homogeneity of group dispersion (PERMDISP).

Bivariate analysis was performed to examine associations between each covariate and microbiome diversity, using non-parametric tests (Table S3; Appendix p.15) and regression models adjusted for age and feeding type. To further investigate the individual taxa associated with covariates significantly related to alpha diversity, we performed a probit log-contrast model for binary variables and a log-contrast model for both categorical and continuous variables. (S.Methods; Appendix p.4).

To longitudinally evaluate rotavirus-IgA responses over the first year of life, we employed LMER adjusting for gestational age. Additionally, we employed a cross-sectional approach to explore the association between rotavirus-IgA titres and different covariates (demographic, dietary, clinical, and environmental) using a univariate approach (Table S4; Appendix p.21), linear models, while adjusting for post–conception age to account for sample collection variability. M12 models were additionally adjusted for a history of gastroenteritis.

To investigate links between early microbiome features and vaccine immune outcomes, we evaluated the following relationships: (1) M1 microbiome with M6 rotavirus-IgA; (2) M6 microbiome with M6 rotavirus-IgA; and (3) M6 microbiome with M12 rotavirus-IgA.

For each comparison, three analyses were performed: (1) Associations with alpha diversity and rotavirus IgA titres were modelled using multivariate linear regressions, adjusting for post–conception age and feeding type. (2) Relationships between overall microbial community composition and rotavirus IgA titres were tested using kernel-based association methods. (3) Taxa-level associations with rotavirus IgA titres were evaluated using penalised regression models designed for compositional data, with multiple testing correction applied. All penalised regression models included post–conception age as a covariate. Additionally, when the outcome was M12 rotavirus-IgA, the variable for a history of gastroenteritis was included in the models of each comparison. Significance on the log contrast analysis was defined as q < 0·2.

Missing data were addressed using complete-case analysis. Participants missing microbiome, sequencing, serology, or vaccination records were excluded from relevant analyses, and samples with incomplete metadata were dropped on a per-analysis basis. Additional information regarding post-processing methods and statistical analysis parameters used can be found in Supplemental Methods (Appendix p.4). All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 4·5·0) with the following R packages: lme428, emmeans29, rstatix30, vegan31 and MiRKAT.32

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Study population

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the unique infants included in the overall study are summarised in Table 1. The analysed population consisted of exclusively term infants (gestational age of 39·62 ± 1·19 weeks), the majority of whom were delivered vaginally (214/278, 77%). Infant sex had a balanced distribution, with 142 of 278 (51·1%) male at birth. Most of the infants were identified by their parents as white race (175/278, 62·9%), followed by black race (55/278, 19·8%). In the microbiome analysis subset, feeding patterns shifted from a majority who were exclusively breastfeeding at M1 (133/224, 59·6%) and M6 (86/197, 43·7%) to a majority who were no longer breastfeeding by M12 (98/181, 54·1%) (Table S2, Appendix p.8). Antibiotic use was reported in a minority of participants with available data (31/274, 11·3%).

Microbiome 16S rRNA analysis and bacterial taxa comparisons over time

Alpha diversity increased from birth to M1 (p < 0·0001), from M1 to M6 (p < 0·0001), and from M6 to M12 (p < 0·0001) (Fig. 2A). Ordination analyses revealed that infant gut microbial communities became increasingly similar over time, as indicated by reduced variation in community composition (beta diversity) among samples and decreased distances between group centroids (Fig. 2B). Age was a significant determinant of overall community composition during the first year of life, as assessed by PERMANOVA (R2 = 0·114, p < 0·0001). Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons between consecutive time points revealed significant compositional differences at each transition. The largest effects occurred between Birth vs. M1 (R2 = 0·096, p < 0·0001), followed by M1 vs. M6 (R2 = 0·034, p < 0·0001) and M6 vs. M12 (R2 = 0·018, p < 0·0001), indicating a decelerating rate of community change over time. Multivariate dispersion also differed significantly across timepoints (PERMDISP p < 0·0001), with M1 showing the greatest inter-individual variability.

Longitudinal profiling of bacterial taxa reflected established successional patterns8,33 in mean relative abundance of specific microbial taxa from birth to M12 (Fig. 2C). Bacterial genera were grouped by phylum: Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria. At birth, genera such as Staphylococcus and Escherichia-Shigella were the most abundant. By M1, the relative abundance of these pioneer taxa were decreased compared to birth. Genera from the phylum Actinobacteriota, specifically Bifidobacterium, increased with time. Compositional patterns remained relatively stable from M6 to M12. Over time, the relative abundance of the Bacteroidota phylum increased, including Bacteroides and Prevotella. Between M1 and M12, the proportion of genera classified as “Other” also increased within Actinobacteriota and Firmicutes. A comprehensive list of the genera aggregated to the “Other” categories can be found in Table S5 (Appendix p.28).

Sex, ethnicity, breastfeeding, and socioeconomic status influence the infant gut microbiome

Clinical variables associated with the gut microbiome varied across time points (Fig. 3). Overall, female infants had higher alpha diversity when compared to males at all time points, however, significance was not reached at M6 (Fig. 3A). Higher maternal education was associated with significantly lower alpha diversity at M1 and M6, and similar trends were observed at M12; no differences were found at birth (Fig. 3B). At birth, infants born to non-white mothers had significantly lower alpha diversity, which reversed to positive, though not significantly, at M1, M6, and M12 (Fig. 3C). Medicaid enrolment was associated with lower alpha diversity at birth, higher alpha diversity at M1 and M6, and no significant differences were found at M12 (Fig. 3D).

Infants who were exclusively breastfed at M1 and M6 had lower alpha diversity than combination- or formula-fed infants; infants who were still being breastfed at M12 had lower alpha diversity than those who had stopped breastfeeding (Fig. 3E). Additionally, infants who were exposed to breastmilk at M1 or M6, exhibited lower alpha diversity compared to those not exposed to breastmilk (Fig. 3F). Similarly, breastfeeding duration was negatively associated with alpha diversity at M1 and M6 (Fig. 3G). Detailed univariate (Table S3; Appendix p.15) and multivariate analysis (Table S6; Appendix p.32) are provided in the appendix. Pre-pregnancy BMI was positively associated with alpha diversity at M1 (Fig. 3H). No differences were observed in alpha diversity and history of gastroenteritis at any time point (Fig. S1, Appendix p.6).

ORV immunogenicity in a healthy US cohort

We observed a significant rise in rotavirus-IgA titres from birth to M6 (β = 2·47, p < 0·0001), followed by a decrease from M6 to M12 (β = −3·65, p < 0·0001) (Fig. 4A). Eleven of 146 (7·5%) of infants were seropositive at birth, which could reflect either technical or true biological variation.34, 35, 36 Seropositivity rose to 58 of 65 (89·2%) at M6 and then declined to 45 of 60 (75%) at M12 (Fig. 4B). No differences in rotavirus-IgA titres between fully or partially vaccinated individuals were observed at either M6 or M12 (Fig. 4C). Similarly, rotavirus IgA titres at M6 and M12 did not vary based on the number of doses or time between vaccine and rotavirus-IgA measures (Fig. S2; Appendix p.6). Additional sensitivity analyses supporting these results can be found in Tables S7 and S8 (Appendix, p.33) Thirty-seven of 44 M6 participants (84·1%) qualified as rotavirus-IgA seroconverters, while 18 of 20 (90%) were seroconverters at M12 (Fig. 4D).

Multivariate analyses were used to test the effect of clinical variables on rotavirus-IgA in our population (Fig. 5). Notably, at M6, infants born to non-white mothers had lower rotavirus-IgA titres compared to those born to white mothers (β = −1·53, 95% CI: [−3·02, −0·046], p = 0·044) (Fig. 5A; Fig. S3A; Appendix p.6). At M12, infants who reported gastroenteritis at M12 visit had higher rotavirus-IgA titres compared to those who did not (β = 1·62, 95% CI: [0·13, 3·10], p = 0·033) (Fig. 5B; Fig. S3B; Appendix p.6). Rotavirus vaccine response and microbial alpha and beta diversity

The M1 microbiome, which represents a baseline gut ecosystem closest to the time of vaccine schedule initiation, is likely a critical window for immune system priming. To evaluate the influence of the M1 microbiome on M6 rotavirus-IgA (N = 47), we first assessed the microbiome alpha diversity. Higher alpha diversity at M1 was associated with higher rotavirus-IgA titres (β: 2·06, 95% CI: [0·31–3·99], p = 0·024) (Fig. 6A). In contrast, no evidence of an association between the taxonomic microbial composition (beta diversity) at M1 and rotavirus-IgA responses was found (p = 0·937) (Fig. 6B).

Next, we investigated the association between the M6 microbiome and rotavirus-IgA titre at M6 (N = 56). Given that all infants had received at least one RotaTeq dose by M6, we first examined whether the number of doses, vaccine schedule completion, or the number of weeks since the last dose influenced alpha diversity; no significant differences were observed for any of these vaccine-related variables (Fig. S4; Appendix p.7). We found no association between the M6 alpha diversity and rotavirus-IgA titres at M6 (β: 0·73, 95% CI: [−0·856, 2·313], p = 0·36) (Fig. 6D). We again observed no significant association between the M6 beta diversity and rotavirus-IgA at M6 (p = 0·24) (Fig. 6E).

To explore the potential influence of the gut microbiome on post-vaccination maintenance of rotavirus-IgA, we examined the association between the M6 microbiome and rotavirus-IgA at M12 (N = 52). We observed a positive association between the M6 alpha diversity and M12 rotavirus-IgA (β: 1·47, 95% CI: [0·127, 2·805], p = 0·033) (Fig. 6G). No significant association was found between the M6 beta diversity and M12 rotavirus-IgA (p = 0·24) (Fig. 6H).

Microbial taxa associated with vaccine response

We performed a debiased L1 penalised log-contrast regression (LCM) to assess the associations between individual taxa and rotavirus-IgA (Fig. 6C,F I). Rotavirus-IgA titres were significantly associated with multiple taxa at M1 including: positively with Collinsella (β = 0·243, 95% CI: [0·076, 0·392], q = 0·037), Atopobium (β = 0·262, 95% CI: [0·066, 0·458], q = 0·062), and Schaalia radingae (β = 0·28, 95% CI: [0·116, 0·458], q = 0·018); and negatively with Bifidobacteria (β = −0·204, 95% CI: [−0·323, −0·085], q = 0·012), Klebsiella (β = −0·195, 95% CI: [−0·331, −0·058], q = 0·042), Lactobacillus (β = −0·17, 95% CI: [−0·314, −0·035], q = 0·087), Escherichia-Shigella (β = −0·128, 95% CI: [−0·245, −0·012], q = 0·162), Streptococcus salivarus (β = −0·229, 95% CI: [−0·359, −0·098], q = 0·012), and Peptostreptococcus anaerobius (β = −0·176, 95% CI: [−0·338, −0·014], q = 0·162) (Fig. 6C).

While M6 diversity metrics were not significantly associated with M6 rotavirus-IgA, LCM identified individual taxa that were significant. Specifically, positive associations were found with Anaerococcus (β = 0·33, 95% CI: [0·217, 0·446], q < 0·0001), Prevotella (β = 0·22, 95% CI: [0·064, 0·36], q = 0·096), and Bifidobacterium breve (β = 0·16, 95% CI: [0·046, 0·28], q = 0·096); and negative associations with Eggerthella (β = −0·17, 95% CI: [−0·306, −0·032], q = 0·160), Prevotella buccalis (β = −0·17, 95% CI: [−0·312, −0·034], q = 0·160), and Bifidobacterium bifidum (β = −0·22, 95% CI: [−0·336, −0·109], q = 0·0039) (Fig. 6F).

Consistent with the significant longitudinal positive association observed between the M6 alpha diversity and M12 rotavirus-IgA titres, log-contrast modelling identified multiple individual taxa associated with M12 rotavirus-IgA titres (Fig. 6I). We observed a positive association with Dialister propionicifaciens (β = 0·21, 95% CI: [0·107, 0·309], q = 0·0034), P. buccalis (β = 0·18, 95% CI: [0·067, 0·284], q = 0·023), Anaerococcus (β = 0·15, 95% CI: [0·054, 0·238], q = 0·023), and Streptococcus (β = 0·14, 95% CI: [0·028, 0·247], q = 0·11). Conversely, Peptoniphilus (β = −0·15, 95% CI: [−0·252, −0·043], q = 0·061), Murdochella asaccharolytica (β = −0·16, 95% CI: [−0·291, −0·034], q = 0·11), Campylobacter ureolyticus (β = −0·23, 95% CI: [−0·373, −0·085], q = 0·023) and Peptococcus (β = −0·23, 95% CI: [−0·36, −0·103], q = 0·013) were negatively associated with M12 rotavirus-IgA titres. The models also revealed that a history of gastroenteritis at M6 was an important predictor of rotavirus-IgA at M12 (β = 0·32, 95% CI: [−0·101, 0·733], q = 0·14) (Fig. 6I). A detailed summary of the prevalence and mean relative abundance for all identified taxa is provided in Table S10 (Appendix p.36).

To assess potential covariation or confounding, we performed additional analyses to determine the impact of clinical variables on individual taxa associated with rotavirus-IgA. A small number of these taxa were significantly associated with specific variables. B. breve, which was positively associated with M6 rotavirus-IgA, was higher in breastfeeding participants at M6 (β = 0·081, q = 0·0372), which was positively associated with exposure to breastmilk at M6 (Table S11, Appendix p.37).

Discussion

Vaccines, including ORVs, elicit variable immune responses across individuals, with particularly pronounced differences observed between children from LMICs and HICs.3The gut microbiome has emerged as a potential contributor to this variability, especially in early life when microbial colonisation and immune system development are tightly intertwined.37 Several studies have investigated the microbiome-ORV relationship, but findings have been inconsistent, likely reflecting differences in study design, geography, and host microbial context.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 To date, all eight published studies have focused on LMICs, where perinatal factors, environmental exposures, nutritional status, and baseline microbial profiles strongly influence both ORV immunogenicity and gut ecology. Yet, understudied HIC cohorts, such as those in the U.S. exposed to relatively low viral prevalence and high vaccine uptake, may offer greater precision when examining microbiome-ORV relationships.

Here, we present a U.S.-based study reporting an association between the infant gut microbiome and ORV immune responses. Among vaccinated infants, rotavirus-IgA titres varied considerably, despite overall high seroconversion rates. Given the high proportion of seroresponders, our analyses primarily capture inter-individual variability in a higher range of antibody responses rather than determinants of vaccine non-response. While seroconversion thresholds are clinically relevant,38 treating rotavirus-IgA as a continuous variable allowed us to leverage this variability to examine relationships between the microbiome and vaccine immunogenicity. While titre thresholds are useful in describing the level at which a given proportion of the population is likely to be protected from disease, individuals with higher vaccine-specific titres may also benefit from even greater protection from severe disease against vaccine-preventable diseases.38, 39, 40 Understanding the factors that enhance vaccine responses, even among seroresponders, can offer further directions to maximise protection.

In our population, microbial alpha diversity showed a time-dependent association with ORV immune responses. Higher alpha microbial diversity at M1 was positively associated with ORV-induced immune responses, results that align with a recent multi-country LMIC study reporting positive associations between microbial diversity, IgA seroconversion, and vaccine shedding.17 Interestingly, higher alpha diversity at M6 positively associated with ORV-immune responses at M12, suggesting that a more diverse microbiome in early life may facilitate both immune priming and the maintenance of ORV-immune responses. In contrast, we observed no association between overall microbial community composition and rotavirus-IgA titres, suggesting that within-sample microbial features may be more relevant to vaccine-induced immune responses than broad shifts in community composition.

In support of this, specific microbial taxa were also significantly associated with increased Rotavirus-IgA titres across different analyses. Anaerococcus, which was positively associated with rotavirus-IgA at M6 and M12, has similarly been linked to tetanus toxoid immune responses in a population from New Hampshire.41 The positive association between Anaerococcus and vaccine responses across similar cohorts further supports the notion that species in this genus may play a role in early-life immune priming. Other positively associated taxa, including those belonging to the Collinsella and Prevotella genera have also been implicated in antigen-specific responses in previous studies. Collinsella is linked to enhanced IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cell responses in a rotavirus infection mode42 and to increased intestinal permeability and IL-17A induction.43 These features of mucosal immune activation, particularly Th17 responses, are known to support differentiation of IgA-secreting plasma cells,44 providing a plausible mechanistic link between the microbe and enhanced Rotavirus-IgA production. Similarly, we identified negatively associated taxa aligning with Wagner and colleagues’ observations, who found that Streptococcus and Klebsiella impaired IgA seroconversion and reduced vaccine shedding in infants from Malawi and Indonesia.17

Some results were inconsistent with previous reports. For example, Harris and colleagues reported positive correlations between Escherichia and Rotavirus-IgA titres in Ghana,13 whereas we identified a negative association with Escherichia-Shigella. This may reflect variation in results that are species-specific but are aggregated at the genus level. Evidence supporting this effect can also be seen in the Bifidobacterium results. While Bifidobacterium has been positively correlated to higher rotavirus-IgA in Australian infants,45 we observed a negative association with Bifidobacterium at the genus level overall. However, associations were positive with Bifidobacteria breve but negative with Bifidobacteria bifidum. Different directionality of taxa from the same genus can reflect the potential of individual taxa to exert disproportionate effects through non-redundant metabolic or immunomodulatory functions. More broadly, bacterial taxa may influence ORV immunogenicity via the secretion of bacteria-derived adjuvants such as flagellin or lipopolysaccharide, or via microbial metabolic products such as short-chain fatty acids that enhance IgA class switching.46

Discrepancies with other studies regarding the association between individual taxa and ORV immune responses may reflect geographic- and immunological-context-specific effects, highlighting the complexity of host–microbiome–vaccine interactions and the importance of studying population-specific effects. For example, breastmilk-associated Bifidobacterium species showed opposite associations in our cohort. Although exposure to breast milk shapes the infant gut microbiome, its impact on ORV immunogenicity may differ across populations based on predominance of specific strains or species of microbiota.14,25 The variation in microbial diversity and rotavirus-IgA observed across host factors, such as race and sex, underscores the need for larger cohorts in areas with low rotavirus prevalence to statistically resolve these complex mediating effects.

A key strength of our study lies in its controlled design and relatively homogeneous cohort, allowing us to minimise biological and environmental confounders that can bias microbiome–immune associations. By focussing on healthy infants of similar demographic backgrounds who uniformly received the RotaTeq vaccine, we were able to reduce residual effects despite the modest sample size. Nevertheless, we were unable to account for maternal IgG and FUT2 secretor status, both of which are known to influence susceptibility to rotavirus infection and vaccine response in populations similar to ours.25 Additionally, our analysis was restricted to bacterial composition and a single immune endpoint. Expanding to a larger number of participants, additional neonatal longitudinal timepoints, and applying metagenomic and metabolomic approaches will be important next steps in exploring the mechanism.

In conclusion, this study highlights a critical window of early-life microbial composition which primes the mucosal immune system and influences vaccine-specific responses. By linking early-life microbiome diversity and composition to ORV antibody responses in U.S. infants, this study establishes the microbiome as a measurable factor influencing rotavirus-IgA in HICs. Larger cohorts using multi-omics approaches will be key to unlocking actionable microbial targets to enhance vaccine-specific responses.
