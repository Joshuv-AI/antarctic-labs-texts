De novo pediatric choroidal Osteoma: A longitudinal observation since inception and through treatment
Authors: Brian Soetikno, Purna Nangia, Prithvi Mruthyunjaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a pediatric case of choroidal osteoma documented from its earliest detectable stage, characterize its growth pattern, and evaluate the impact of photodynamic therapy (PDT) on tumor progression.ObservationsA 4-year-old female underwent cataract surgery in the right eye and was consequently followed regularly. At age 8.8 years old (4 years of follow-up), a subtle, amelanotic choroidal juxtapapillary lesion was detected in the left eye on fundus examination. A retrospective review of fundus photographs prior to this examination revealed no readily visible lesion at age 6.7 years. Subtle changes on fundus photography in intermediate timepoints were apparent only on close inspection. Multimodal imaging confirmed the diagnosis of choroidal osteoma. The lesion's effective basal diameter growth rate before PDT was 1.94 mm/year, faster than reported in pediatric cases. After PDT, the growth rate was 1.78 mm/year, which was not statistically different (p = 0.558). Over a five-year follow-up period, the patient maintained 20/20 visual acuity in the affected eye despite subfoveal involvement of the tumor.Conclusions and importanceThis case represents a rare instance of photographic documentation of choroidal osteoma during its earliest identifiable stages, providing unique insight into this rare tumor's natural history and progression. While PDT was performed, it neither halted the tumor's progression completely nor prevented it from extending sub-foveally. These findings underscore the importance of early detection, longitudinal imaging, and continued exploration of management strategies for pediatric choroidal osteomas.Keywords: Choroidal, Growth, Growth rate, Osteoma, Pediatric, Photodynamic therapy, PDT

Introduction

Choroidal osteoma is a rare, benign, ossifying tumor of the choroid, typically affecting young, healthy individuals with a predilection for females.1 It is characterized by the presence of mature bone replacing the choroid. Visual impairment may occur in the presence of outer retinal atrophy from tumor decalcification or secondary choroidal neovascular membrane (CNVM) with fluid exudation, secondary fluid without CNVM, or secondary polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV).2 While the etiology of choroidal osteoma remains largely unknown, it is crucial to document and analyze individual cases to better understand the natural history, clinical presentation, and management strategies for this uncommon condition. Here, we present a unique case of a de novo pediatric choroidal osteoma, which was observed since inception and documented from its earliest detectable stage. We characterized the tumor's growth rate and discussed our treatment strategy and approach. To our knowledge, this is a rare case report of a pediatric choroidal osteoma that was longitudinally followed from a timepoint before being visually detectable.

Case report

A 4-year-old female presented to our pediatric ophthalmology clinic for a posterior subcapsular cataract in the right eye. The patient underwent extracapsular cataract extraction with posterior chamber lens implantation in the right eye and a complete examination under anesthesia, which revealed no other abnormalities at that time. The patient was followed at least every six months in the pediatric ophthalmology clinic and subsequently developed lens proliferation underneath the intraocular lens of the right eye. Visual acuity at that time was 20/200 in the right eye and 20/16 in the left eye. She underwent a 25-gauge removal of lens re-proliferation and pars plana vitrectomy in her right eye at age 8. At 4 years of follow-up (age: 8.8 years), indirect ophthalmoscopy, and fundus photography in the left eye revealed a small, yellow, juxtapapillary, sub-retinal lesion. Consequently, she was referred to our retina and ocular oncology clinic for further evaluation.

Multi-modal imaging revealed an amelanotic juxtapapillary lesion in the left eye (Fig. 1A) associated with lamellar choroidal changes on optical coherence tomography (OCT) (Fig. 1B). Fluorescein angiography (FA) showed early patchy hyperfluorescence and late diffuse staining (Fig. 1C and D). A hyperechogenic focus without back-shadowing was seen on B-scan ultrasonography (Fig. 1E). The workup was consistent with a choroidal osteoma, and the initial plan was to observe at 3-month intervals.

Given that the patient had been previously followed for the cataract surgery in the right eye, we were able to retrospectively review fundus photographs prior to the detection of the choroidal lesion at age 8.8 years. At age 6.7 years (Fig. 2A) and 7.3 years (Fig. 2B), the fundus photographs appear normal; however, upon close comparison with 7.8 years, there may have been a small lesion at the superotemporal arcade (Fig. 2A–C, green arrows). The lesion was first detected via ophthalmoscopy, and the patient was referred to us at 8.8 years. By this time, the tumor had grown significantly (Fig. 2D). Noting the rapid growth and progression to the fovea, the differential was also expanded to inflammatory choroidal granuloma. Laboratory workup for uveitis, including angiotensin-converting enzyme, lysozyme, complete blood count, calcium, phosphorus, QuantiFERON tuberculosis gold testing, syphilis rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test, and complete metabolic panel, was within normal limits.3 Given the lesion's rapid growth and yellow choroidal appearance, a choroidal granuloma remained a strong consideration. As such, the patient underwent empirical treatment with two cycles of intravenous methylprednisolone (20 mg/kg/day for 3 days), administered three months apart at ages 9.3 and 9.6 years respectively. Despite this intervention, the lesion continued to enlarge (Fig. 2E), and in the absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of inflammation, a diagnosis of choroidal osteoma was confirmed based on multimodal imaging and lack of steroid responsiveness.

As the lesion exhibited progressive growth toward the fovea (Fig. 2F), posing an increasing risk of CNVM development and subsequent vision loss,4 treatment was pursued to attempt to slow its advancement. Given the documented foveotrophic growth pattern and the proximity to the foveal center, verteporfin photodynamic therapy (PDT) was administered at age 9.9 years to mitigate the risk of central vision compromise. Verteporfin was administered intravenously at 6.6 mg (6 mg/m2; Visudyne) followed by light stimulation using laser at 689 nm with half-fluence PDT, i.e., 25 mJ/cm2 over 83 seconds. The application of light was applied at two spots, one supratemporal to the optic nerve and one at the inferotemporal aspect of the lesion closest to the fovea, but avoiding the fovea. At 2-months follow-up post-PDT, the patient had vitreous cells that were suggestive of intraocular inflammation secondary to PDT. This was treated with a course of oral prednisone, starting at 25 mg/day and tapered every 5 days, over a month (Fig. 2G). At the post-treatment month 3 visit, a focal area of subretinal hemorrhage was observed at the inferonasal aspect of the tumor (Fig. 2H, yellow arrow), raising concern for early CNVM development. Swept-source OCT imaging was performed, which revealed no associated subretinal or intraretinal fluid. No additional imaging modalities, such as OCT angiography or indocyanine green angiography, were conducted at that time. Isolated subretinal hemorrhage may also occur as a transient adverse effect of PDT as previously reported by Palamar et al.5 Subsequent follow-up images (Fig. 2I–K) demonstrated progressive tumor enlargement without new hemorrhage or exudation. By age 11.8 years (Fig. 2N), the lesion had extended subfoveally, as captured in serial photographs (Fig. 2L–N).

Fundus photographs before the detection of the lesion, and serial follow-up after, enabled the estimation of the lesion's effective basal diameter growth rate at 1.94 mm per year (Fig. 3). The area of the lesion was measured using manual segmentation of the fundus photographs in ImageJ.6 The effective diameter was calculated from the area by taking the square root, dividing by π, and multiplying by 2.7 After PDT (dashed vertical line, Fig. 3), the lesion continued to grow and progress; however, the growth rate was noted to be 1.78 mm per year, which was lower compared to the rate before PDT. However, using analysis of covariance modeling, this rate of growth was not statistically significantly different (p = 0.558).

Now at age 12.5 years, and 8 years of follow-up, the best corrected visual acuity in the eye with the choroidal osteoma remained 20/20. There was no evidence of CNVM including sub-retinal hemorrhage or fluid. The patient continues to be monitored closely at 3 to 4-month intervals with multi-modal imaging, including OCT and fundus photography. Repeat PDT or intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) injection would be considered in the case of visual acuity decline due to new subretinal fluid or hemorrhage.

Discussion

We report a rare case with photographic documentation of pediatric choroidal osteoma during its earliest identifiable stages, providing valuable insights into the natural history of choroidal osteoma. The etiology of choroidal osteoma remains elusive, and both primary and secondary origins have been proposed. Secondary ossification may result from inflammatory or systemic processes, as supported by reports of choroidal osteomas in patients with conditions such as orbital inflammatory pseudotumor, uveitis, vitritis, Harada's disease, and histiocytosis X.8,9 These associations suggest that secondary heterotopic bone formation could occur in response to inflammatory triggers or other acquired conditions. In our patient, there was no history of uveitis, trauma, or other medical conditions, thus suggesting a possible congenital etiology. The tumor's detection coincided with regular ophthalmic evaluations for unrelated cataract surgery in the contralateral unaffected eye, and the review of fundus photography showed no obvious lesion at age 6.7 years. This observation appears consistent with the choristoma hypothesis outlined by Noble,10 where embryonic mesenchymal tissue may have been present in a microscopic, or dormant, state since birth and subsequently grew, potentially triggered by hormonal changes during puberty. Further research is needed to explore these mechanisms.

Vision loss in choroidal osteomas may stem from decalcification of the tumor or secondary complications, such as CNVM or PCV, which are often associated with subretinal fluid or hemorrhage. In our patient, no secondary CNVM developed, and no subfoveal decalcification was observed, likely contributing to the preservation of 20/20 visual acuity over time. The impact of decalcification depends on its location. Sub-foveal decalcification is particularly harmful, as it is associated with outer retinal atrophy and progressive vision loss. Specifically, decalcification imparts a relative risk of 3.6 for final visual acuity of 20/200 or worse.4 Conversely, extra-foveal decalcification can be beneficial, as it often leads to scarring and tumor involution, thereby preventing further progression toward the fovea. Decalcification coincides with choriocapillaris loss, which has been confirmed in a previous study using OCT angiography.11 The loss of the choriocapillaris precedes disruption of the RPE and damage to the outer retina.

Decalcification of choroidal osteomas is hypothesized to be due to the activity of osteoclasts. Osteoclasts, which resorb bone and release calcium, reverse the mineralization process initiated by osteoblasts. Decalcification can occur spontaneously or secondarily after laser photocoagulation or PDT. Prior reports have investigated whether modulating the osteoclast activity with receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa beta ligand (RANKL) inhibitors or calcium supplementation may provide benefits. Hébert et al. reported the use of denosumab (a RANKL inhibitor) and alendronate (a bisphosphonate) to halt progression in a case of bilateral choroidal osteomas, with clinical stability observed over nine years of treatment.12 Additionally, calcium supplementation has been proposed as a potential strategy to prevent decalcification. Voluck et al. reported the use of recommended daily calcium intake in a case of bilateral sub-foveal tumors.13 Due to the rarity of choroidal osteoma, these medical therapies have not been validated, and their risk-to-benefit ratio should be considered carefully.

The growth behavior of choroidal osteomas is variable (Table 1). Aylward et al. reported that 40 % of observed osteomas demonstrated growth, while 50 % remained unchanged over time.10 In this case, the lesion exhibited a growth rate in effective basal diameter of 1.94 mm per year prior to PDT, which is slightly higher than the largest growth rate reported by Shields et al.,4 however, the measurement technique differed, as in their study, indirect ophthalmoscopy was used to make measurements of the tumors. Zhang et al. provided another measurement of growth rate at 6.2 mm per year, which was much larger than our patient and Shields et al.’s study.4 Their case report was unusual in that the tumor was detected in a male patient at post-pubertal age. Future studies are needed to better characterize the growth rates of these tumors with longitudinal imaging.

Verteporfin PDT was selected to attempt to halt the growth of the lesion and secondarily address the risk of choroidal neovascularization and vision loss as the lesion encroached upon the foveal region. In a retrospective study by Mazloumi et al., eight out of nine eyes with extrafoveal choroidal osteoma that were treated with PDT did not progress to becoming sub-foveal.15 In our patient, PDT treatment resulted in localized posterior intraocular inflammation and a transient area of subretinal hemorrhage, which raised concern for possible early CNVM; however, no associated subretinal fluid or neovascular complex was detected on imaging. Despite undergoing PDT, the lesion continued to enlarge over time and ultimately extended into the subfoveal region. Furthermore, serial multimodal imaging demonstrated no evidence of extrafoveal tumor decalcification following PDT, with the lesion maintaining its ossified appearance throughout follow-up. We did measure a decrease in the basal diameter growth rate after PDT (1.78 mm/yr vs. 1.94 mm/yr prior), but this was not statistically significantly different. PDT releases free radicals, which interact with the vessel endothelium, leading to an inflammatory response and vessel thrombosis. The benefit of PDT is that it may stimulate the decalcification of the tumor and inhibit growth.15,16 However, once the tumor progresses to becoming sub-foveal, the risk of decalcification includes vision loss from outer retinal atrophy, and PDT is not recommended.17 In addition, the benefits of PDT must be carefully balanced with the risks of encouraging CNVM development as it may also induce choroidal ischemia or disrupt Bruch's membrane and the retina pigment epithelium.18

While anti-VEGF therapy has proven effective in managing secondary CNVM arising from choroidal osteomas, its use is generally reactive rather than preventive. Anti-VEGF agents, such as ranibizumab and bevacizumab, have been shown to reduce subretinal fluid and stabilize or improve vision in cases complicated by CNVM formation.19,20 However, to date, there are no reports supporting the use of anti-VEGF therapy as a primary intervention aimed at halting osteoma growth or preventing lesion progression. In our patient, the absence of CNVM and the lack of evidence for the primary benefit from anti-VEGF therapy supported the decision to forego anti-VEGF injections. Moreover, the treatment burden associated with repeated intravitreal injections in a young child—including the potential need for anesthesia—further influenced our choice to pursue PDT as the initial intervention.

This case underscores the importance of early detection and meticulous longitudinal follow-up in patients with choroidal osteoma. Measuring the growth rate of choroidal osteomas could serve as a marker of treatment response. Further studies are needed to elucidate this rare condition's natural history and optimal management strategies.
