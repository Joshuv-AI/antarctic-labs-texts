Cumulative C-reactive protein exposure links to COPD risk in adults with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3 (no overt CVD): A prospective cohort study
Authors: Yunyu Liu, Jun Gong, Jian Zhang, Juntao Tan, Chao Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, characterized by interconnected metabolic, cardiovascular, and renal dysfunctions, is linked to chronic low-grade inflammation, a key driver of comorbidities like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). C-reactive protein (CRP), a sensitive inflammation biomarker, may predict COPD risk in CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD), but longitudinal patterns of CRP remain underexplored.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, we included 4,887 participants aged ≥45 years with CKM stages 0–3 and without prior COPD from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). CRP was measured in 2012 and 2015. Longitudinal change patterns were identified using k-means clustering. Cumulative CRP exposure (cumCRP) was calculated as the area under the curve. Incident COPD was defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis plus objective evidence of airflow limitation (peak expiratory flow <80% predicted) in 2018. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess associations.ResultsFour CRP change patterns were identified: stable low-risk (Class 1), rapidly increasing (Class 2), significant improvement (Class 3), and persistently high-risk (Class 4). Compared to Class 1, Class 4 was associated with a 69% higher COPD risk (adjusted OR=1.69, 95% CI: 1.28–2.23). High cumCRP (tertile 3 vs. 1) increased COPD risk by 65% (OR=1.65, 95% CI: 1.32–2.08). The association was stronger in advanced CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD), particularly Stage 3.ConclusionsLongitudinal CRP change patterns and cumulative CRP exposure are independently associated with incident COPD in individuals with CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD). Persistently elevated or rapidly increasing CRP may help identify high-risk individuals for targeted monitoring and early intervention.Keywords: C-reactive protein (CRP), cumulative CRP exposure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, inflammation, longitudinal change pattern

1. Introduction

In 2023, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced the concept of Cardiovascular - Kidney - Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a systemic disorder characterized by the complex interplay of obesity, metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, and cardiovascular dysfunction.1–3 Epidemiological studies have shown that this syndrome is highly prevalent. Approximately 90% of US adults meet the criteria for at least stage 1 CKM syndrome, with 15% qualifying for advanced stages. 4 In South Korea, stage 2 CKM syndrome has been reported to have the highest prevalence. 5 Moreover, there are significant disparities in the burden of CKM syndrome across different social determinants of health (SDOH) and genders, highlighting the importance of addressing these health inequities.6,7

CKM syndrome has far - reaching effects on almost all major organ systems, leading to a variety of severe clinical complications. These include kidney failure, 8 early - onset cognitive deterioration, 9 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD),10–12 obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), 13 a graded risk for cardiovascular mortality, 14 and an elevated risk of cancer. 15 The progression of CKM syndrome is driven by the convergence of multiple comorbidities, such as hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome (MetS), type 2 diabetes, and CKD. 16 Over time, these overlapping conditions contribute to the development of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis (as indicated by coronary artery calcification) and subtle abnormalities in myocardial structure and function. Concurrently, kidney function progressively declines, further exacerbating the risk of clinical cardiovascular disease (CVD), renal failure, disability, and mortality. Given the asymptomatic nature of CKM syndrome in its early stages, early identification and intervention are crucial for mitigating its long - term adverse consequences. 2 The progression of both CKM syndrome itself and associated conditions like COPD is intimately linked to chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation.17,18 Consequently, biomarkers reflecting this inflammatory state, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), are of significant interest for understanding and predicting COPD risk, particularly within the high-risk CKM population. 19

Chronic low-grade inflammation, a hallmark of CKM syndrome progression, manifests through elevated CRP—an acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver that directly reflects systemic inflammation severity. 20 As a sensitive indicator of systemic inflammation, CRP is intricately linked to the pathophysiological cascades driving both CKM syndrome and COPD. In COPD, elevated CRP levels—including its monomeric form (mCRP), which more potently reflects low-grade chronic inflammation—are consistently associated with impaired metabolic function, reduced exercise capacity, and worse respiratory symptoms, underscoring its role as a marker of disease severity and functional impairment.21,22 Importantly, CRP’s significance extends beyond mere disease characterization: it predicts critical clinical outcomes in COPD, such as increased risk of acute exacerbations and higher mortality, making it a pivotal tool for risk stratification and prognosis assessment.19,23 Notably, the interplay between CRP, COPD, and CKM syndrome is further reinforced by their shared inflammatory underpinnings. CKM syndrome, characterized by metabolic dysregulation (e.g., metabolic-associated fatty liver disease), cardiovascular dysfunction, and renal impairment, involves systemic inflammation that exacerbates COPD progression; conversely, COPD-related chronic inflammation may amplify metabolic and cardiovascular derangements in CKM. This bidirectional relationship is reflected in the association between elevated CRP (and CRP/albumin ratio, a composite marker of systemic inflammation) and the coexistence of COPD with metabolic abnormalities, highlighting CRP as a key link between these conditions. 20 Additionally, the responsiveness of COPD patients with high CRP levels to anti-inflammatory therapies, such as mesenchymal stromal cell infusions, further supports CRP’s role as a functional marker of inflammatory activity that could modulate disease trajectories in the context of CKM syndrome. 24 Together, these observations—spanning CRP’s utility in characterizing COPD severity, predicting outcomes, and connecting inflammatory pathways across CKM-related comorbidities—justify its selection as a central biomarker for investigating the intricate relationships between CKM syndrome and COPD. 18

However, most of these prior studies have limitations. They mainly rely on single - point measurements of CRP, which fail to capture the dynamic nature of inflammation over time. In the context of CKM syndrome, the longitudinal patterns of inflammation, which could potentially better reflect the development of inflammation - related diseases like COPD, remain largely unexamined. Additionally, confounding factors such as lifestyle, comorbidities, and medications are often not comprehensively adjusted for, potentially leading to inaccurate results. In this study, we aim to overcome these limitations. We will use a longitudinal design, collecting data at multiple time points. By employing k - means clustering, we can identify distinct longitudinal CRP change patterns. 25 Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression will be utilized to comprehensively account for various confounding factors.

The primary objective of this research is to explore the relationship between longitudinal patterns of systemic inflammation, measured through cumulative CRP exposure (cumCRP) and change patterns - based classifications, and the incidence of COPD in individuals with CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD). By doing so, we hope to gain new insights into the role of inflammation in the development of COPD within the context of CKM syndrome. These findings may have significant implications for the development of early intervention and prevention strategies.

2. Method

2.1. Study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative survey targeting middle-aged and elderly individuals aged 45 and above, managed by the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, Peking University’s National School. 26 The study employed a longitudinal design with data collection waves in 2011– 2012 (baseline, wave 1), 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), 2018 (wave 4), and 2020 (wave 5). Biological samples, including fasting blood, were collected during waves 1 (2011–2012) and 3 (2015). Further details on the study population have been described in prior publications. The eligibility criteria for participants in this study were as follows: (1) age ≥ 45 years; (2) complete data on C-reactive protein (CRP); (3) a physician-confirmed diagnosis of chronic lung disease with objective evidence of airflow limitation, defined as a measured peak expiratory flow (PEF) to predicted PEF ratio of less than 80% prior to 2015; and (4) complete information regarding CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD).

We utilized data from Waves 1 (2011--2012), 3 (2015), and 4 (2018) because serum CRP measurements were only available in Waves 1 and 3, and the COPD outcome was first comprehensively assessed in Wave 4. Wave 5 (2020) was not used as the primary outcome because CRP was not measured in that wave, precluding the calculation of cumulative CRP exposure. Participants were selected based on the availability of complete biomarker and follow-up data within the CHARLS cohort, rather than by consecutive or random sampling. Eligibility was determined by the presence of complete CRP data across Waves 1--3 and complete CKM staging information at baseline. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2024). The CHARLS was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent before enrolling in the study. This manuscript does not contain any individual person’s identifiable data, images, or videos.The current analysis was conducted under the data use agreement for CHARLS. To evaluate potential selection bias, baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants were compared (Table S8a), and excluded participants were further stratified by specific exclusion reason (Table S8b). The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 27

2.2. Data assessment

2.2.1. Assessment of CRP, cumCRP, and CRP change categories

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured in fasting serum samples using immunoturbidimetry (Hitachi 7180 analyzer). To minimize the influence of acute inflammation or infection (e.g., CRP > 100 mg/L), we first performed winsorization by setting values below the 1st percentile and above the 99th percentile to missing. The remaining CRP values were then natural-log transformed [ln(CRP)] to normalize their right-skewed distribution—a standard approach for inflammatory biomarkers, where most individuals have low baseline concentrations while a minority exhibit orders-of-magnitude elevations. 28 This transformation stabilizes variance, reduces the undue influence of extreme outliers, and aligns with established methods for analyzing non-Gaussian biomarker data. For longitudinal assessment, cumulative CRP exposure (cumCRP) was subsequently calculated as the area under the curve estimate, representing mean inflammation burden over time: (ln(CRP2012) + ln(CRP2015))/2* time(2015-2012).29–31 Thus, the cumulative metric represents the time-integrated log-transformed inflammatory burden, which is less susceptible to distortion by extreme raw concentrations and avoids artificial linear dose-response relationships that could be driven by a few outlying values in subsequent analyses. Using k-means clustering applied to these log-transformed values, participants were stratified into four distinct longitudinal change patterns: Class 1 (Stable Low-Risk: sustained minimal inflammation), Class 2 (Rapidly Increasing: progressive inflammatory escalation), Class 3 (Significant Improvement: pronounced inflammation resolution), and Class 4 (Persistent High-Risk: chronically elevated inflammation). 32

2.2.2. Assessment of CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD)

CKM syndrome is defined as a systemic health disorder characterized by pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors (such as obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and diabetes), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and the cardiovascular system, which collectively increase the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes and multiorgan dysfunction[1]. In alignment with the American Heart Association Presidential Advisory, CKM syndrome is staged from 0 to 4 to reflect a spectrum of risk and progression[1]. In this study, we applied the CKM staging framework at baseline (Wave 1, 2011) as follows: (1) Stage 0: No CKM risk factors present. Individuals exhibit normal body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, blood pressure, lipid profile, glucose metabolism, and kidney function, with no evidence of subclinical or clinical cardiovascular disease (CVD). (2) Stage 1: Presence of excess or dysfunctional adiposity, defined as prediabetes (HbA1c 5.7%–6.4% or fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL) without other metabolic risk factors or CKD. (3) Stage 2: Presence of metabolic risk factors or moderate-to-high-risk CKD. Metabolic risk factors included hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥135 mg/dL), hypertension (blood pressure ≥130/80 mmHg or use of antihypertensive agents), dyslipidemia (treated or laboratory-confirmed), or type 2 diabetes (self-reported, medication use, HbA1c ≥6.5%, or fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL). Moderate-to-high-risk CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) between 30 and 59 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the CKD-EPI creatinine equation. (4) Stage 3: Presence of very-high-risk CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or overweight/obesity (BMI ≥23 kg/m2 for Asian population or waist circumference ≥90 cm in men/≥80 cm in women), in the absence of clinical CVD. (5) Stage 4: Clinical CVD (self-reported heart disease or stroke) coexisting with any of the above CKM risk factors (Stage 1–3). Stages 1–3 were considered as having CKM risk, whereas Stage 0 represented no CKM risk. This staging approach facilitates risk stratification and aligns with the preventive and prognostic framework recommended by the American Heart Association.33,34

We acknowledge that our operationalization of CKM Stage 3 deviated from the original AHA Presidential Advisory, which defines Stage 3 as the presence of subclinical cardiovascular disease (e.g., coronary artery calcium >100 Agatston units, or elevated cardiac biomarkers) or very high-risk CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or albuminuria ≥300 mg/g) among adults with metabolic risk factors. Because CHARLS does not include coronary artery calcium scoring, cardiac biomarker data, or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio measurements, we adapted the staging criteria using available variables: Stage 3 was defined as BMI ≥23 kg/m2 (Asian population cutoff) or waist circumference ≥90 cm in men/≥80 cm in women in the absence of clinical CVD. This modification aligns with prior CKM studies in Asian populations using CHARLS data and represents a pragmatic adaptation given data availability. A supplementary table (Table S7) summarizing the staging criteria and their mapping to AHA definitions is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

2.2.3. Outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) during the follow-up period (Wave 4). Case ascertainment was based on a composite criterion integrating both self-reported physician diagnosis and objective lung function measurement. Specifically, a confirmed incident COPD case was defined as a participant who met both of the following criteria: 1) self-reported a prior physician diagnosis of “chronic lung disease”,35–37 and 2) demonstrated objective evidence of airflow limitation, defined as a ratio of measured to predicted peak expiratory flow (PEF%) of less than 80%. Lung function was assessed using a peak flow meter, with the highest of three measurements obtained by trained technicians following a standardized protocol used for analysis. Predicted PEF values were calculated using the validated Zhongnan Mountain formulas for Chinese adults: for men, the formula is 75.6 + 20.4 × age − 0.41 × age2 + 0.002 × age3 + 1.19 × height, and for women, the formula is 282.0 + 1.79 × age − 0.046 × age2 + 0.68 × height. 38 Rigorous quality control measures, including standardized technician training, regular equipment calibration, and adherence to consistent measurement protocols, were implemented to ensure data reliability and integrity.

While spirometric confirmation using post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 represents the gold standard for COPD diagnosis, PEF <80% predicted has been validated as a screening tool in large epidemiological studies. A PEF cutoff of <80% predicted demonstrates approximately 90% sensitivity for detecting airflow limitation, with a negative predictive value exceeding 95% for excluding severe COPD (GOLD stages III–IV). Given the logistical constraints of conducting spirometry in a nationwide cohort and the absence of bronchodilator reversibility testing in CHARLS, our composite definition—combining self-reported physician diagnosis with objective PEF measurement—represents a pragmatic approach that balances diagnostic accuracy with feasibility. Previous CHARLS-based COPD studies have employed similar definitions, supporting its validity in this population. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that this approach may introduce some outcome misclassification, particularly for participants with restrictive lung diseases or mild airflow obstruction. This limitation is addressed in the Discussion.

2.3. Data collection

At baseline (Wave 1, 2011–2012), trained interviewers collected sociodemographic and health data via standardized questionnaires. Sociodemographic details encompassed gender, age, educational attainment (Primary school and below, or Junior high school and above), body mass index (BMI). Health-related information included self-reported smoking and drinking habits (yes/no) and physiciandiagnosed conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia. Additionally, laboratory test results, including Blood urea nitrogen, Cystatin C, White blood cell, Hematocrit, C-reactive protein (CRP),triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), Hemoglobin, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c), Uric acid, estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate and total cholesterol (TC). 39

2.4. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Inter-group comparisons utilized χ2 tests for categorical variables, ANOVA for normally distributed continuous variables, and Kruskal-Wallis tests for non-parametric data.

Firstly, participants were stratified into four distinct CRP change pattern groups using K-means clustering based on longitudinal CRP measurements (2012-2015). This unsupervised learning technique optimally partitions observations into K clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance, thereby capturing nuanced temporal patterns in inflammatory dynamics relevant to COPD pathogenesis. Cluster number optimization employed the elbow method, which identified K=4 as the inflection point where marginal reduction in within-cluster distortion plateaued—balancing model parsimony with explanatory power while mitigating overfitting (Figure 2(a)–(c)). This four-cluster solution aligned with established inflammatory change pattern typologies: Class 1 (Stable Low-Risk), Class 2 (Rapidly Increasing), Class 3 (Significant Improvement), and Class 4 (Persistent High-Risk). 40

Clustering of changes of CRP from wave 1 to wave 3. Changes in CRP classified into four classes using the K-means algorithm (a and b); Mean CRP for the four classes in 2012 and 2015 (c); class 1 (red)—stable low-risk group; class 2 (green)—rapidly increasing group, class 3 (cyan)- significant improvement group; class 4 (purple)—persistent high-risk group.

Secondly, we also evaluated associations using both continuous cumulative CRP (cumCRP) and tertile-based categorization (Q1: low; Q2: medium; Q3: high). Multivariable logistic regression quantified relationships between CRP change pattern, cumCRP, and incident COPD across hierarchically adjusted models: Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, education. Model 3 was adjusted for age, gender, BMI, education, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, antidiabetic agents, antihypertension agents, antihyperlipidemic agents. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots explored potential non-linear dose-response relationships between cumCRP and COPD risk. Prespecified subgroup analyses assessed effect modification by age (<60/≥60 years), sex, education (≤primary school/>junior high), smoking status (current, former, never), alcohol use, CKM stage (0-1/2/3), diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, with full covariate adjustment. 25

To address confounding, sensitivity analyses systematically excluded users of antidiabetic, antihypertensive, antihyperlipidemic agents, and smoking status. All analyses were conducted using R 4.4.2, with two-tailed P<0.05 defining statistical significance.

3. Result

3.1. Study population selection

The selection process for the study population is outlined in Figure 1. From an initial 17,708 participants in Wave 1 of the CHARLS cohort, 5,613 were excluded due to insufficient data for classifying CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD), resulting in 12,095 eligible individuals with confirmed staging at baseline. Subsequent exclusions were made based on follow-up data availability: 2,185 subjects lacked COPD-related data at Wave 4, 1,114 had a prior COPD diagnosis or incomplete COPD data during Waves 1–3, and 3,909 had incomplete C-reactive protein (CRP) data across Waves 1–3. The final analytical sample comprised 4,887 participants, who were categorized into four distinct CRP change pattern classes: Class 1 (n=1,436), Class 2 (n=1,388), Class 3 (n=1,219), and Class 4 (n=844).

Flowchart of the study population.

To assess potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants (Table S8a). Excluded participants (n=7,432) were slightly older, had lower BMI, and exhibited lower prevalence of metabolic comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and lifestyle risk factors (smoking and alcohol use) compared with the analytical sample, largely attributable to higher proportions of missing data. Importantly, baseline ln-transformed CRP levels were comparable between included and excluded participants (0.07 ± 0.95 vs. 0.11 ± 1.08 mg/L, p=0.90), suggesting minimal differential selection with respect to the primary exposure of interest. Table S8b further characterizes excluded participants by specific exclusion reason, revealing heterogeneous baseline profiles: individuals lacking CKM staging data were younger with lower BMI, whereas those with incomplete CRP data were older with higher BMI. This variability indicates that no single exclusion mechanism dominated the selection process, thereby supporting the generalizability of our findings.

3.2. Clustering of CRP change pattern

Longitudinal changes in CRP levels from Wave 1 (2012) to Wave 3 (2015) were characterized using K-means clustering. The elbow method determined the optimal number of clusters as four (Figure 2(a)). The resulting change patterns and mean CRP levels per wave are shown in Figure 2(b) and (c). The four classes were defined as: Class 1 (Stable Low-Risk; red), with consistently low CRP (Wave 1: -0.74 ± 0.47 mg/L; Wave 3: -0.65 ± 0.63 mg/L); Class 2 (Rapidly Increasing; green), showing a marked rise (Wave 1: -0.38 ± 0.47 mg/L; Wave 3: 0.91 ± 0.58 mg/L); Class 3 (Significant Improvement; cyan), with a pronounced decline (Wave 1: 0.65 ± 0.53 mg/L; Wave 3: 0.11 ± 0.50 mg/L); and Class 4 (Persistent High-Risk; purple), maintaining elevated CRP (Wave 1: 1.37 ± 0.71 mg/L; Wave 3: 1.43 ± 0.63 mg/L).

3.3. Baseline characteristics by CRP change categories

Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of the 4,887 participants stratified by CRP change pattern class. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed across classes for most variables, except sex and drinking status (P > 0.05). The proportion of participants aged ≥60 years increased from Class 1 (36.42%) to Class 4 (43.36%; P = 0.008). Educational attainment, smoking history, and CKM syndrome stage distribution also differed significantly (all P < 0.05). The prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, along with the use of related medications, rose progressively from Class 1 to Class 4 (all P < 0.001). Similarly, BMI and several laboratory parameters (e.g., white blood cell count, triglycerides, HbA1c) were highest in Class 4, while HDL-C and eGFR were lowest (all P < 0.001). Cumulative and wave-specific CRP levels differed as expected (P < 0.001). COPD incidence varied significantly (P < 0.001), ranging from 8.22% in Class 1 to 14.36% in Class 4.

3.4. CumCRP, CRP change categories and COPD incidence among individuals with CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD)

Logistic regression analyses revealed that, compared to the stable low-risk CRP change pattern (Class 1), all other change patterns were associated with a significantly higher incidence of COPD, with the strongest association observed for the persistent high-risk group (Class 4; fully adjusted OR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.41--2.47, P<0.001) (Table 2). Furthermore, each unit increase in cumulative CRP was linked to a 7% higher COPD risk (OR=1.07, 95% CI: 1.04--1.11, P<0.001), demonstrating a dose-dependent relationship where participants in the highest cumulative CRP tertile had 1.61-fold greater odds (95% CI: 1.27--2.04, P<0.001) of developing COPD compared to the lowest tertile (Table 2). Restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a linear positive dose-response relationship between cumulative CRP and COPD risk in the overall population (P-overall<0.001) (Figure 3). This relationship was most pronounced among participants with advanced CKM syndrome (Stage 3: P-overall<0.001), weaker in Stage 2 (P-overall=0.042), and non-significant in early stages (Stages 0--1: P-overall=0.197). Consistently, the median cumulative CRP level increased progressively with advancing CKM stages (from -0.2 in Stages 0--1 to 1.3 in Stage 3), indicating that greater chronic inflammatory burden accompanies CKM progression and may underpin the elevated COPD risk observed in advanced stages. 3.5. Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses assessed the consistency of the association between CRP change patterns and COPD incidence (Table 3). No significant interaction was observed between CRP change categories and key demographic, lifestyle, or clinical factors, including smoking status (P-interaction > 0.05 for all), indicating the association was generalizable across subgroups. Stratified analyses for cumulative CRP are presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Nonetheless, the association's strength varied. Among former smokers, the persistent high-risk CRP change patterns (Class 4) was associated with a pronounced increase in COPD risk (OR = 3.48, 95% CI: 1.26--10.72), showing a significant positive trend (P-trend = 0.015). Similarly, in never smokers, both the significant improvement (Class 3) and persistent high-risk (Class 4) change patterns were linked to significantly elevated COPD risk (ORs = 1.68 and 1.80, respectively; P-trend = 0.001). The association was most robust in advanced CKM stages, with significant positive trends in Stage 2 (P-trend = 0.008) and Stage 3 (P-trend < 0.001), but was non-significant in the early stages (Stages 0-1; P-trend = 0.627). When analyzing cumulative CRP, stratified analyses confirmed a dose-dependent relationship with COPD, which was strongest in CKM Stage 3 (P-trend < 0.001). The interaction between cumulative CRP (as continuous or tertile variable) and smoking status remained non-significant (P-interaction > 0.05), further supporting that the inflammatory exposure contributes to COPD risk independently of smoking status.

A series of sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Tables S3--S5). After sequentially excluding participants using antidiabetic, antihyperlipidemic, or antihypertensive medications, the core associations remained materially unchanged. Specifically, the persistent high-risk CRP change patterns (Class 4) consistently demonstrated the highest risk for COPD (adjusted OR range: 1.86--1.93, all P < 0.001), and a significant positive association was maintained for each unit increase in cumulative CRP (OR range: 1.07--1.08, all P < 0.001). Crucially, an analysis confined to current and former smokers (excluding all never smokers) was performed to further interrogate the role of inflammation beyond smoking (Supplementary Table S6). In this smoker-only cohort, the dose-dependent relationship between continuous cumulative CRP and COPD risk remained significant (OR per unit increase = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.03--1.13, P < 0.001), with individuals in the highest cumulative CRP tertile facing a 49% greater risk (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.06--2.10, P = 0.022). While statistical significance was attenuated for individual non-referent CRP change pattern classes in this smaller subgroup, the overall positive trend aligned with the main analysis. Collectively, these sensitivity analyses substantiate that the identified associations between adverse CRP change patterns, elevated cumulative CRP, and increased COPD incidence are robust and not driven by concurrent medication use or confounded by the inclusion of never smokers.

4. Discussion

This study provides novel evidence that longitudinal patterns of systemic inflammation, quantified through cumulative CRP exposure (cumCRP) and classifications based on change patterns, are independently associated with the risk of incident COPD among individuals with CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD). Our analysis of a nationally representative cohort demonstrated that, compared to a stable low-risk pattern, persistently high-risk (Class 4: adjusted OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.28–2.23) and rapidly increasing (Class 2: OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.12–1.87) CRP change patterns were associated with a significantly elevated risk of COPD. Furthermore, cumCRP showed a positive dose-response relationship with COPD incidence, with high cumulative burden (highest vs. lowest tertile: OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.32–2.08) amplifying risk most substantially in advanced CKM stages (Stage 3).

These findings align with the American Heart Association’s emphasis on CKM syndrome as a multidimensional health threat necessitating early intervention. 41 Chronic inflammation is a recognized pathophysiological bridge between metabolic dysregulation and end-organ damage,42,43 with CRP serving as a validated biomarker of systemic inflammatory burden. Previous studies have established associations between elevated CRP and respiratory decline, though most relied on single-point measurements. Our work extends this paradigm by demonstrating that longitudinal inflammatory patterns—particularly dynamic escalation or sustained elevation—hold predictive value for COPD development in at-risk CKM populations. The clinical relevance is underscored by the escalating COPD risk observed across advancing CKM stages, highlighting a critical synergy between the severity of metabolic dysfunction and inflammatory exposure. This supports emerging frameworks positioning COPD within the spectrum of CKM-related target organ damage, where inflammatory pathways may mediate pulmonary compromise in metabolically vulnerable individuals. Our classification based on change patterns provides a pragmatic tool for identifying high-risk subgroups that may warrant intensified surveillance or preventive strategies.

By applying k-means clustering analysis to hs-CRP measurements at two time points (2012 and 2015), we identified four distinct inflammatory change patterns among individuals with CKM stages 0–3 (no overt CVD): stable low-risk, rapidly increasing, significant improvement, and persistently high-risk. Crucially, after comprehensive adjustment for confounders including age, sex, BMI, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and medication use, these patterns showed differential associations with incident COPD. Compared to the stable low-risk pattern (Class 1), participants with the persistently high-risk pattern (Class 4) had a 69% increased risk of COPD, while those with the rapidly increasing pattern (Class 2) showed a 45% risk elevation. Notably, even individuals in the significant improvement pattern (Class 3) retained a 35% residual increase in COPD risk, potentially indicating irreversible inflammatory lung damage or persistent subclinical inflammation despite a decline in the biomarker level.

The present study significantly advances the understanding of the interplay between systemic inflammation, CKM syndrome progression, and COPD risk, building upon foundational work on mechanisms of inflammation-related chronic diseases. While prior research has established CRP as a biomarker of systemic inflammation and a predictor of cardiovascular events,44–46 its longitudinal role in COPD pathogenesis across CKM stages remained underexplored. For instance, cross-sectional studies in specific populations demonstrated correlations between elevated CRP levels and COPD severity, 47 but these findings were limited by an inability to capture inflammatory dynamics. In contrast, our longitudinal analysis of CHARLS data enabled the identification of distinct CRP change patterns, each exhibiting a graded association with COPD incidence. This pattern-based approach, applied via k-means clustering, reveals nuanced inflammatory pathways that static CRP measurements cannot discern. Notably, this study addresses a critical gap in CKM research by quantifying how stages of metabolic dysfunction modulate inflammation-COPD relationships. Previous work linked systemic immune-inflammation indices to CKM progression but did not stratify outcomes by organ-specific complications. 48 Our findings demonstrate that the inflammatory burden, particularly in advanced CKM stages, amplifies COPD risk in a dose-response manner. The 65% increased odds of COPD in individuals with high cumCRP aligns with observations from cohorts linking persistent systemic inflammation to accelerated lung function decline.49,50 However, our study extends these insights by integrating cumCRP as a metric of time-integrated exposure, revealing a dose-response trend not apparent in analyses restricted to single CRP measurements.

Methodologically, this study introduces analytical frameworks that address limitations in previous epidemiological designs. Using the area under the curve to estimate cumCRP, rather than single time-point measurements, mitigates regression dilution bias and provides a more robust proxy for long-term inflammatory exposure. Combined with restricted cubic spline modeling, this approach offers superior resolution in characterizing risk relationships compared to conventional logistic regression. For example, while prior studies identified CRP as a cardiovascular risk factor,51–53 its application to respiratory outcomes required domain-specific validation—a gap addressed by our CKM-stratified analyses. The finding that the predictive power of cumCRP was strongest in Stage 3 CKM patients has profound implications for risk stratification, suggesting that metabolic comorbidities may synergistically enhance inflammatory pathogenicity.

From a clinical perspective, these results advocate for considering CRP monitoring within CKM management protocols, particularly for high-risk subgroups. Current CKM syndrome guidelines emphasize metabolic control but lack components for inflammatory surveillance.54,55 Our data indicate that even moderate elevations in cumCRP correspond to clinically significant increments in COPD risk, suggesting CRP as a potential target for preventive interventions. This aligns with the direction of emerging evidence from trials like CANTOS, where IL-1β inhibition reduced cardiovascular events in patients with high CRP, 56 and extends this paradigm to pulmonary outcomes. 57 Furthermore, the persistently high-risk CRP pattern identified herein represents a phenotype that may require consideration of anti-inflammatory strategies, contrasting with traditional COPD management focused primarily on bronchodilation and corticosteroids.58,59

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables may influence the observed associations. Second, the definition of COPD relied on a composite of self-reported physician diagnosis and PEF <80% predicted rather than spirometric confirmation with post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70. While PEF is a validated screening tool with high sensitivity for detecting clinically significant airflow limitation, it cannot differentiate obstructive from restrictive ventilatory defects and may misclassify mild COPD cases. This non-differential misclassification would likely bias our effect estimates toward the null, suggesting that the true associations may be stronger than observed. Third, smoking is the strongest known risk factor for COPD. Although we adjusted for smoking status and performed subgroup analyses, detailed smoking information—including pack-years, smoking intensity, smoking duration, and passive smoking exposure—was unavailable in CHARLS. Residual confounding from these unmeasured smoking characteristics may therefore partially influence the reported associations, though the persistence of significant associations in never-smokers and sensitivity analyses excluding never-smokers suggests that the inflammatory-COPD relationship extends beyond smoking effects. Fourth, the longitudinal “trajectories” were derived from only two time points (2012 and 2015), which may not capture the full complexity of chronic inflammatory dynamics. We have therefore reframed these as CRP change categories rather than true longitudinal trajectories. Fifth, although the reported associations are statistically significant, the magnitude of association for continuous cumulative CRP (OR ≈ 1.07 per unit increase) is relatively modest. Whether these effect sizes justify CRP-based screening strategies requires careful consideration of statistical significance versus clinical relevance, and our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence for clinical implementation. Sixth, the CHARLS database collects participants’ geographical information, but access to these data is strictly controlled. As our access request was not approved, we were unable to adjust for important environmental confounders such as ambient air pollution (PM2.5) and biomass fuel exposure. In China, ambient particulate matter pollution accounts for approximately 22% of COPD-related disability-adjusted life years, and indoor air pollution from solid fuels contributes an additional 19.5%. These environmental factors drive both systemic inflammation and COPD risk; their omission may have led to residual confounding, potentially inflating the observed associations between inflammatory markers and COPD incidence. This limitation is particularly relevant given the urban-rural disparities in environmental exposures across China. Seventh, regarding CKM staging, our Stage 3 definition differed from the original AHA framework, which specifies subclinical cardiovascular disease (e.g., elevated coronary artery calcium) or very high-risk CKD (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2). Because CHARLS did not include coronary artery calcium scoring or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio data, we operationalized Stage 3 using BMI ≥23 kg/m2 (Asian cutoff) or waist circumference thresholds in the absence of clinical CVD. This modification, while pragmatic for this cohort, may have introduced some misclassification in CKM staging. Eighth, we lacked detailed medication data regarding duration, dosage, and treatment adherence, which could have influenced both CRP levels and COPD risk. Finally, the excluded participants (n=7,432) differed systematically from the analytical sample in ways that may introduce selection bias (Table S8a). Compared with included participants, excluded individuals were older, had lower BMI, and lower prevalence of metabolic comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and lifestyle risk factors (smoking and alcohol use), largely attributable to higher rates of missing data. However, baseline CRP levels were comparable between included and excluded participants (ln-transformed CRP: 0.07 ± 0.95 vs. 0.11 ± 1.08 mg/L, p=0.90), suggesting that selection bias concerning the primary inflammatory exposure is unlikely to have materially influenced our findings. Table S8b further demonstrates that characteristics of excluded participants varied by specific exclusion reason (e.g., those lacking CKM staging data were younger with lower BMI, whereas those with incomplete CRP data were older with higher BMI), indicating that no single mechanism dominated the exclusion process.

Future investigations should prioritize intervention trials targeting CRP reduction in patients with CKM syndrome, ideally combining pharmacological (e.g., canakinumab) and lifestyle modifications. Mechanistic studies employing multi-omics platforms could elucidate how CKM-related metabolic perturbations (e.g., insulin resistance or lipotoxicity) amplify inflammatory signaling in pulmonary tissues. Longitudinal imaging studies integrating lung function metrics with CRP dynamics would further clarify causal pathways. Finally, harmonizing CKM staging criteria across international cohorts will be essential for validating the observed stage-specific effects and establishing universal risk prediction models.

5. Conclusion

In this nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults with CKM syndrome stages 0–3, we found that longitudinal CRP change patterns and cumulative CRP exposure were significantly associated with an increased risk of incident COPD. Participants exhibiting persistently high-risk or rapidly increasing CRP patterns had substantially elevated COPD risk, independent of traditional risk factors. The strength of this association intensified with advancing CKM stages, suggesting a synergistic interplay between metabolic dysfunction severity and chronic inflammatory burden in driving pulmonary compromise.

These findings highlight the potential utility of serial CRP monitoring as a risk stratification tool in CKM populations, particularly for those in advanced stages. While our study reinforces the role of systemic inflammation in the pathogenesis of COPD within the CKM context, further research is needed to establish causality and to explore whether interventions aimed at reducing inflammatory burden could mitigate COPD risk in this vulnerable population. Future studies should also incorporate comprehensive environmental exposure data and clinical validation of COPD to refine these associations and inform targeted prevention strategies.
