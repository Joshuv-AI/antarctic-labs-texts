Abdominal mouse: Floating intra-abdominal cystic lymphatic malformation – An exceedingly rare case report

Introduction

Lymphangiomas are benign, rare lymphatic malformations characterized by thin-walled cystic lesions. Most of them are located in the head and neck region (75 %), the axillary region (20 %), and, less than 5 % of cases, the intra-abdominal region. Furthermore, most cystic lymphatic malformation (CLM) is diagnosed in childhood and are rarely discovered in adulthood [[1], [2], [3]].

Intra-abdominal CLM account for 7 % of all abdominal cystic lesions in adults, with a very rare prevalence of about 1 in 250,000 people [4]. They are found in the mesentery, greater omentum, and retroperitoneum. However, most cases are asymptomatic and identified incidentally by imaging modalities such as CT scans or ultrasound [3]. Nevertheless, a definitive diagnosis by biopsy should be undertaken after the surgical removal of cysts to optimize the management plan and prognosis. On the other hand, intra-abdominal CLM can present with symptoms of bowel obstruction, including abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting [5,6].

However, our case presents with multiple, floating, and variably sized intra-abdominal CLM not attached to the mesentery, greater omentum, mesocolon, or retroperitoneum. This unusual and interesting finding is novel and, as far as we know, has not been reported in any other case.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old female admitted to our hospital, referred from her obstetrician, complaining of dull aching right upper abdominal pain for 2 months of duration. Her past medical and surgical history was unremarkable. No associated symptoms, such as recent changes in bowel habits, diarrhea, or association with fatty meal, were reported. Vital signs were normal. Abdominal examination showed upper abdominal tenderness without guarding or rigidity, and a mobile lump inside her abdomen.

Routine laboratory test was unremarkable, including pancreatic and liver enzymes. Ultrasonography showed large, multiple cysts from pelvis to sub-hepatic region of undetermined origin. Contrast-enhanced CT scan showed (Fig. 1) multiple complex cysts, possibly of ovarian origin. Therefore, we performed pelvic ultrasound with no evidence of ovarian cystic lesion.

Contrast enhanced computed tomography, axial view of the abdomen and pelvis showing; (A) multiple cystic lesions at the subhepatic region, white arrow. (B) Paracolic gutter, yellow arrow and (C) pelvis, green arrow. Multiple cystic lesions at the subhepatic lesion (white arrow).

During an exploratory laparoscopy, whole abdomen and pelvis were explored; multiple variable-sized cysts were observed throughout the abdominal cavity, ranging from 1 cm to 10 cm in size. Notably, the ovaries appeared normal, as confirmed by laparoscopy and imaging studies. Due to the presence of these cysts in different locations and their size diversity, we decided to perform an upper midline open laparotomy to resect the large cysts.

These cysts were either found floating freely (Video 1) or attached to the transverse colon mesentery, sub-hepatic area and greater omentum, right paracolic gutter (Video 2), retrocecal region, pelvis, and near the stomach and duodenum. Intraoperative period was uneventful with successful removal of cystic lesion. There was no need for bowel resection as the cysts were only loosely attached to the mesentery, not to the bowel itself. Over 50 cysts were removed (Fig. 2) and sent for histopathology, which showed multilocular cysts, covered by flat endothelium containing collection of lymphoid tissues, foam cells, and smooth muscle cells in the wall of the cysts, confirming the diagnosis of intra-abdominal CLM.

Showing gross multiple, variable size cysts.

On postoperative day 2, patient's course was uneventful, so she was discharged home with follow-up plan. 3 months later, the patient was in good health and was able to resume her daily life activities with no persistence or recurrence of the symptoms.

Discussion

Lymphangiomas are rare benign fluid-filled cystic malformations of lymphatic vessels. The cause of lymphangiomas is related to abnormal lymphatic system development during fetal embryology. This occurs when primary lymphatic sacs fail to fuse properly with the main lymphatic system. Some may be acquired, resulting from inflammation, trauma or lymphatic obstruction. Despite this, the exact cause of cystic lymphangiomas is still not clearly understood. [1,7].

Abdominal CLM are a very rare type of lymphangioma, accounting for less than 5 % of all cases. More than 80 % of lymphangioma cases are diagnosed within the first few years of life [1,3]. Intra-abdominal CLM are mostly attached to intra-abdominal structures, including the mesentery, greater omentum, mesocolon, and retroperitoneum [3,4,8]. A few cases of abdominal CLM have reported cystic lesions in the pancreas [9] and stomach [10]. However, in contrast to these studies [3,4,9,11,12], a floating isolated intra-abdominal cyst was incidentally noted by laparoscopy during the operation in our case. Moreover, the presence of multiple variable-sized cystic lesions in distributed locations, which is a very rare finding according to the literature, makes the diagnosis of abdominal CLM challenging.

Preoperative diagnosis of abdominal CLM is complex and requires a high degree of clinical suspicion. In addition to being asymptomatic [3,5], the clinical features of abdominal CLM vary significantly depending on size and location, including symptoms such as abdominal pain, mass, and bowel obstruction [3,6,11]. In our case, the patient presented with vague, unspecified abdominal pain without any characteristic signs on examination, making the diagnosis challenging.

Beyond clinical manifestations, radiological findings using abdominal ultrasound, CT scan, or magnetic resonance imaging are important for diagnosing abdominal CLM. Additionally, these imaging modalities help determine the location, size, and association of abdominal CLM with surrounding organs, which are essential for proper surgical management. However, histopathological examination of a biopsy remains the definitive diagnosis.

The management of abdominal CLM is variable and includes multiple treatment modalities depending on abdominal CLM features and patient symptoms, including surgical and conservative treatment. As some abdominal CLMs spontaneously regress [4], asymptomatic patients are usually managed conservatively with imaging and clinical follow-up. On the other hand, symptomatic patients may require percutaneous intervention, such as aspiration or sclerotherapy, or surgical intervention, including laparoscopy or open laparotomy [3,4]. Despite the treatment modality used, complete removal of abdominal CLM should be achieved due to the significant risk of relapse, which can reach 100 % according to previous studies [4]. Despite the high rate of recurrence and injuries to surrounding tissue reported in previous studies, we achieved a successful, uncomplicated complete resection of multiple intra-abdominal cystic lymphangiomas.

Conclusion

Despite being rare, abdominal CLM must be considered as possible diagnosis especially if the cysts are multiple and complex. Being without attachment and freely floating in the abdominal cavity, abdominal CLM may cause the symptoms of a moving lump described by the patient, which must be taken in consideration.
