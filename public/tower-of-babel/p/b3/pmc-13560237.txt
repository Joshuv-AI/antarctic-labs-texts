Parity differences and inflammatory signatures relative to neonatal nutritional status in a community near the Atlantic Ocean coastline of Lagos, Southwest Nigeria: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Bamgboye Morakinyo Afolabi, Titilola Mitsuko Afolabi, Victor Inem, Jane Ogoamaka Okwuzu, Susan J Holdbrooke, Abideen Salako, Olusola Ajibaye, Oluwagbemiga O Aina, Veronica Enya, Chinedum T Oparaugo, Tolulope Uzoka, Toyosi Raheem, Alero Ann Roberts, Adeola Yetunde Olukosi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal malnutrition (NM) remains a major public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), driven by complex maternal, placental, and fetal factors. This study determined the prevalence of neonatal malnutrition and examined the potential influence of placental Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) and Interferon-gamma (IFN-gamma) on maternal and neonatal characteristics in Lagos, Nigeria.MethodA cross-sectional, facility-based study was conducted from March to November 2024 among 254 pregnant women in their first, second, or ≥third pregnancies. Participants were enrolled in the late third trimester and followed through delivery. Maternal sociodemographic and obstetric data were collected using semi-structured questionnaires. Placental characteristics and neonatal anthropometry were recorded postnatally. Neonatal nutritional status was classified using the Ponderal Index Percentile (PIP). Data was analyzed using NCSS 2022.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 29.3 ± 5.0 years, with significant variation across groups. The mean age of mother with severely malnourished neonates (26.2 ± 4.1 years) was significantly lower than that of mother of moderately malnourished babies (29.2 ± 3.8 years), healthy (29.2 ± 4.9 years), or overweight neonates (31.9 ± 6.0 years). The prevalence of severe and moderate NM was 4.7% and 5.9%, respectively, with the highest burden among primigravida women. Placental TNF-α concentrations were significantly higher among first-pregnancy mothers (29.7 ± 7.8 pg/mL) than among second-pregnancy mothers (12.1 ± 4.2 pg/mL) who delivered severely malnourished neonates.ConclusionSevere neonatal malnutrition was most common in 1st and 2nd pregnancies and elevated placental TNF-α may contribute to its pathophysiology. These findings highlight the need for targeted nutritional and medical interventions for first-time mothers and underscore the importance of multi-center research to better characterize the biological mechanisms underlying NM in SSA.Keywords: Birthweight, Birth length, Birth head circumference, Neonatal malnutrition, Nigeria, Ponderal Index, Small-for-gestational-age, Sub-Saharan Africa, Tumor Necrosis Factor-α, Interferon-γ

What is already known on this topic

Neonatal malnutrition reflects a nutritional deficit that begins in utero and becomes clinically evident at birth. Established contributors include inadequate maternal nutrition, placental insufficiency, maternal anemia, intrauterine infections such as Plasmodium falciparum malaria, and physiological stress during pregnancy. Previous studies have examined these determinants from clinical, epidemiological, and pathophysiological perspectives. However, limited research has examined neonatal malnutrition using a cross-sectional, descriptive approach that integrates maternal, placental, and inflammatory biomarkers.

What this study adds

This study is among the first in sub-Saharan Africa to examine the potential role of cytokines — specifically TNF-α and IFN-γ — in fetal malnutrition. A key finding was a negative correlation between birthweight and birth length among malnourished neonates, in contrast to the strong positive correlation observed in healthy and overweight neonates. The findings raise important questions about whether elevated cytokines contribute to fetal malnutrition or whether malnutrition itself triggers cytokine release, highlighting a critical area for future investigation.

How this study might affect research, practice, or policy

The findings have implications for maternal and child health (MCH) programs at the Federal and state levels in Nigeria and support progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The study underscores the need for multicenter, multidisciplinary investigations of placental and cord blood cytokines to clarify their roles in fetal malnutrition. Such evidence could inform improved antenatal screening, targeted nutritional interventions, and enhanced clinical monitoring for at-risk pregnancies.

Introduction

Malnutrition in newborns most often results from intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), a condition arising from a complex interplay of maternal, placental, and fetal factors. Earlier studies have demonstrated that fetal malnutrition may occur across a wide range of birth weight or gestational age. [1–4] Although the World Health Organization (WHO) has established guidelines outlining nutritional requirements throughout pregnancy, [5] persistent gaps exist addressing women’s nutritional needs throughout the entire reproductive continuum. [6] Fetal malnutrition was first described over six decades ago as an “intrauterine failure to acquire a normal amount of subcutaneous fat and muscle”. [7] It is now broadly understood as inadequate supply of nutrients and oxygen to the developing fetus leading to impaired growth and increased susceptibility to chronic diseases later in life. [8] Normal fetal growth is influenced by genetic potential and the regulation of intrauterine hormones and growth factors. [9] Evidence indicates that both low and high birthweight are associated with adverse long-term outcomes. Low birthweight has been linked to reduced cognitive ability, shorter adult stature, immune dysfunction, chronic lung and kidney disease, and increased risks of hypertension, impaired glucose tolerance, diabetes, and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. [8] Conversely, higher birthweight has been associated with increased risks of obesity, diabetes, and certain cancers in adulthood. [9] Maternal nutrition is arguably the most critical determinant of optimal fetal growth. Adequate intake of macronutrients—including protein and essential fatty acids, particularly Omega-3 fatty acids—and key micronutrients such as folic acid, calcium, and iodine are essential for organogenesis, neurodevelopment, and tissue accretion. [10] Despite improvements in obstetric and neonatal care, fetal malnutrition and IUGR remain major public health challenges in sub-Saharan Africa. [11] Infants born with moderate to severe malnutrition face lifelong risks of multisystem complications and require ongoing surveillance to support early prevention and intervention. [12] Globally, the burden of childhood malnutrition remains substantial. In 2022, the WHO estimated that 149 million children under five were stunted, 45 million were wasted, and 37 million were overweight or obese. [13] According to UNICEF’s 2025 report, approximately 43 million children (6.6%) under five years of age suffer from wasting, including 12.2 million who are severely wasted, with South Asia bearing the highest prevalence. [14] A meta-analysis of 5,356 newborns from 13 African studies reported a pooled prevalence of fetal malnutrition of 19%, indicating that nearly one in five neonates in Africa is affected. [15] In Nigeria, an estimated 2 million children are born with severe malnutrition, with 18% experiencing wasting, particularly in the northern regions. [16] Several maternal and environmental factors have been implicated in severe neonatal malnutrition, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preexisting maternal vascular disease, and adverse social or behavioral exposures such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, and substance use. [17] Placental thickness and volume have also been associated with neonatal nutritional status. [18] Histopathological abnormalities at the maternal–fetal interface—such as defective placentation, inadequate trophoblast invasion, and incomplete spiral artery remodeling—can impair oxygen and nutrient delivery to the fetus. [19] Despite the high burden of fetal malnutrition in sub-Saharan Africa, limited studies have examined its underlying biological and immunological mechanisms. Studies from Ethiopia identified placental weight and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status as key determinants of fetal malnutrition, [20] while research from Nigeria reported a high prevalence of SGA among preterm infants. [21] However, the potential roles of inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) in neonatal malnutrition remain poorly understood. Although Takele et al. demonstrated altered cytokine profiles and immune dysfunction in malnourished adults, [22] similar investigations in neonates are scarce. [23–25] Therefore, the objectives of this study were to determine the prevalence of neonatal malnutrition on the first day of life using the Ponderal Index and its percentiles, [26] and to evaluate the possible association between neonatal malnutrition and specific cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, prospective cross-sectional study was conducted at a public health facility in Ikorodu Local Government Area (LGA), a semi-urban community located near the Atlantic Ocean coastline of Lagos State, southwestern Nigeria. Ikorodu is one of the five LGAs comprising the Lagos East Senatorial District (Fig. 1). Eligible participants were recruited from the antenatal clinics of Ikorodu General Hospital and its annex, the Ita-Elewa Primary Health Center, between March and November 2024.

Flowchart of recruitment of study population by simple random sampling. [24]

Study population and eligibility criteria

Pregnant women attending antenatal care were eligible if they had a singleton pregnancy, were in the late third trimester, and had no medical conditions likely to adversely affect pregnancy outcomes. Exclusion criteria included refusal to provide informed consent, critical maternal illness, and stillbirth. Participants were selected using simple random sampling from the antenatal clinic attendance register. Enrolled women were interviewed and followed until delivery.

Sample size determination

Sample size was calculated using an estimated malaria prevalence of 20% in pregnancy, a 5% margin of error, and a design effect of 1.0. At a 95% confidence level, the minimum required sample size was 246, computed using Epi Info version 7.1.26. [27] To account for potential attrition, the sample size was increased by 6.5% to 262.

Training of data collectors

Data collection procedures were standardized through training sessions conducted at the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research in Lagos and at Ita Elewa Primary Health Center between February 12 and 19, 2024. Training included orientation on data collection instruments, procedures for timely specimen delivery to the laboratory, and retraining of 15 midwives, nurses, and health information officers. Specific emphasis was placed on immediate clamping of the umbilical cord after delivery, identification of the umbilical artery, proper aspiration and preservation of arterial cord blood, and accurate measurement of neonatal anthropometry, including head circumference, which is not routinely recorded.

Accurate and timely record-keeping was emphasized throughout. Maternal data collection included socio-demographic characteristics, anthropometric indices, and obstetric and gynecological histories, obtained using structured questionnaires. Clinical examination findings, delivery histories, and neonatal profiles were retrieved from facility records maintained by the head matron/midwife.

Delivery procedures

Deliveries followed standard obstetric practice. Most neonates were delivered via spontaneous vaginal delivery with cephalic presentation, while a minority were breech or delivered by cesarean section. Following delivery of the neonate, uterine contractions facilitated expulsion of the placenta, umbilical cord, and amniotic membranes.

Placental processing

Immediately after delivery, the placenta was weighed, and a wedge-shaped biopsy was collected. Tissue samples were placed in sterile containers with 20 mL of 10% formalin and transported to a specialist laboratory for histopathological examination.

Sections approximately 8 μm thick were stained using routine hematoxylin and eosin procedures and examined under a Leica DM 500 light microscope at 100× magnification. Placental malaria was diagnosed using the Rogerson criteria, [28] considered the gold standard, which include detection of Plasmodium falciparum parasites, presence of hemozoin pigment and fibrin deposition in the intervillous space.

Placental blood was collected from the cut surface and stored in EDTA bottles for hematological and cytokine analyses. Thick and thin smears were prepared and stained with Giemsa for malaria microscopy.

Cord blood collection

At delivery, the umbilical cord was double-clamped 10–15 cm from the neonate’s abdomen and then severed. The umbilical artery was identified, and 5 mL of arterial blood was aspirated with a sterile syringe. Arterial cord blood sampling was preferred because it: preserves neonatal blood volume, is non-invasive and painless, minimizes the risk of blood loss, especially in preterm infants, provides clinically relevant neonatal information, and prevents maternal–fetal blood admixture.

Approximately 2–3 mL of cord blood was spotted onto Whatman filter paper for malaria DNA analysis, and the remainder was stored in EDTA bottles.

Neonatal data collection

Following standard neonatal resuscitation, birthweight was measured on a digital weighing scale and recorded to the nearest 10 gm, birth length was measured from crown to heel, using a non-stretchable cloth tape, and birth head circumference was measured across the temporal protuberances using the same cloth tape. Apgar score was assessed at 1 and at 5 min after birth.

Hematological and cytokine assays

Placental hematological parameters were analyzed using a Sysmex XN-550 hematology analyzer (Kobe, Japan). Parameters included: red blood cell count, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, platelet count, total and differential white blood cell counts, and immature granulocyte count.

Placental TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations were measured using commercial sandwich ELISA kits (Sunlong BioTech, China). IFN-γ results are reported separately.

Definitions used: Anemia: hemoglobin < 9.5 g/dL Thrombocytopenia: platelet count < 150 × 10³/µL Thrombocytosis: platelet count ≥ 450 × 10³/µL, Immature granulocytes were categorized as low, normal, or high using established thresholds. [29].

Data management and statistical analysis

Field data were recorded manually, then transferred to Excel spreadsheet, cleaned, and exported to Stata version 16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) for analysis. NCSS version 20 (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) was used to construct age-frequency distributions.

Neonatal nutritional status was assessed using the Ponderal Index (PI):

Classification: Severe neonatal malnutrition was categorized as PIP <5th percentile, moderate neonatal malnutrition as PIP 5th–10th percentile, healthy neonates as PIP > 10th–90th percentile and overweight as PIP > 90th percentile. [30].

Data analyses included: Frequencies and proportions, bivariate cross-tabulations, multivariate regression, Shapiro–Wilk’s test for normality, Pearson correlation coefficients, ANOVA or Non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis) tests for group comparisons, chi-square tests for proportions, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Significance was set at p < 0.05. No missing data were reported.

Dependent variable was neonatal malnutrition. Independent variables were maternal socio-demographic characteristics such as age, marital status, educational level, residence (urban or rural), family size, monthly income, alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, antenatal care (ANC) visits, number of ANC visits, use of Long-lasting Insecticide-treated bednets (LLIN), Intermitent Preventive Treatment during pregnancy (IPTp) gestational age, and duration of labor. Placental variables were placental weight, placental TNF-α and IFN-γ and hematological indices. Fetal variables were birthweight, birth length, birth head circumference, birth sex.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Nigerian Institute of Medical Research (IRB Protocol No. IRB/23/096; 20 November 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality was maintained by de-identifying data and restricting access to research personnel. Given the malaria endemicity in Nigeria, placental hemoglobin values of 9.5–15.0 g/dL were considered normal in third-trimester pregnancies. [31, 32].

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Of the eligible women approached, eight declined to participate because of cultural concerns about placental handling. A total of 254 pregnant women were enrolled, including primigravidae (n = 91; 35.8%), secundigravidae (n = 92; 36.2%), and multiparous women (n = 71; 28.0%) (Fig. 1). Maternal and placental data were collected for all participants, and neonatal anthropometric measurements were obtained at birth. Placental blood samples from 80 randomly selected women, primarily in their first or second pregnancy, were analyzed for TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations.

Maternal demographic and gestational characteristics

Among the 254 neonates, 12 (4.7%) were severely malnourished, 15 (5.9%) moderately malnourished, 202 (79.5%) healthy, and 25 (9.9%) overweight. The mean maternal age was 29.3 ± 5.0 years, with significant variation across nutritional groups (F = 3.65, p = 0.02). Mothers of severely malnourished neonates were the youngest (25.2 ± 4.1 years), whereas those of overweight neonates were the oldest (31.9 ± 6.0 years).

Most mothers of severely malnourished neonates were 26–30 years old (41.7%), had a normal BMI (58.3%), and lived in urban areas (83.3%). They also had higher mean systolic (126.8 ± 14.5 mmHg) and diastolic (83.4 ± 1.1 mmHg) blood pressures. In contrast, mothers of healthy neonates had lower systolic (118.2 ± 13.3 mmHg) and diastolic (77.8 ± 8.4 mmHg) pressures and were predominantly urban residents (82.7%). By occupation, mothers of severely malnourished neonates were mostly artisans (41.7%), whereas mothers of healthy neonates were mostly traders (50.5%). In all, 101 (39.8%) of mothers in this study had a daily income of ≥ 10,000 Naira, equivalent to approximately US15.7 Dollars, mainly among 7 (46.7%) of moderately malnourished and least among 4 severely malnourished babies. Neonatal nutritional status by parity

Firstly, the overall prevalence of severe malnutrition, moderate malnutrition, healthy and overweight neonates were 4.7% (12/254), 5.9% (15/254), 79.5% (202/254) and 9.8% (225/254) respectively, with a combined prevalence of severe and moderate malnutrition as 10.6% (27/254) (Fig. 2). Secondly, 66.7% (8/12) of severely malnourished babies were born to women in their 1st pregnancy but none was delivered by women in their ≥3rd pregnancy (Table 2; Fig. 3). Further, 41.7% (5/12) of mothers whose neonates were severely malnourished had fever during their pregnancy, especially during the 1st trimester. Gestational age, duration of labor, and placental weight did not differ significantly across nutritional groups. However, significant differences were observed in birthweight (F = 35.3, p < 0.000001), birth length (F = 9.5, p = 0.00001), and head circumference (F = 2.87, p = 0.04).

Percentage distribution of various categories of malnutrition relative to type of pregnancy (2024)

One minute after birth, 8.3% (1/12) of severely malnourished neonate had an APGAR Score of 6, 41.7% (5/12) had an APGAR score of 8 and another 8.3% (1/12) recorded an APGAR score of 1. Five minutes after birth, no malnourished child had an APGAR score less than 8 but 50.0% (6/12) recorded an APGAR score of 10. Means of labor duration, gestational age and placental weight were evenly spread among mothers, regarless of the nutritional status of the neonates. Notable variations were observed in the means of birthweight (F-ratio = 35.3, P-value < 0.000001), birth length (F-ratio = 9.5, P-value = 0.00001) and birth head circumference (F-ratio = 2.87, P-value = 0.04) relative to nutritional status of the study neonates (Table 2.).

Maternal hematological parameters and cytokine concentrations

Significant parity-related differences were observed in: Hemoglobin concentration (F = 3.43, p = 0.04), Hematocrit (F = 3.17, p = 0.047), and Monocyte count (F = 3.49, p = 0.04). The mean placental platelet count indicated generalized thrombocytopenia across participants (all subjects: 77.9 ± 76.2 × 10³/µL; selected subjects: 77.7 ± 87.2 × 10³/µL). Placental TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations were highest in primigravid women (TNF-α median = 24.6 pg/mL; IFN-γ median = 7.7 pg/mL). Placental malaria parasite density was also highest in first pregnancies (geometric mean = 484.0 parasites/µL) (Table 3 and 4).

Placental hematological, cytokine, and malaria parameters by neonatal nutritional status

Among the 80 mother–infant pairs with cytokine data, no significant differences were observed in placental hematological parameters across nutritional groups. Mean platelet counts were similar between severely/moderately malnourished neonates (55.3 ± 40.5 × 10³/µL) and overweight neonates (52.5 ± 41.5 × 10³/µL). Placental malaria parasites were detected in mothers of moderately malnourished neonates (GMPD = 520.0) and in those of healthy neonates (GMPD = 453.5).

Cytokine differences by parity among malnourished neonates

Only primigravid and secundigravid women delivered severely malnourished neonates. Among these women, TNF-α levels were marginally higher in primigravidae (median = 30.7 pg/mL) than in secundigravidae (median = 12.1 pg/mL) (Z = 1.94, p = 0.05). IFN-γ levels did not differ significantly (Z = 0.39, p = 0.70). Similarly, among moderately malnourished neonates, IFN-γ levels differed marginally by parity (Z = 1.94, p = 0.05), whereas TNF-α levels did not.

Cytokine differences among healthy neonates

There was a significant difference (χ² = 13.86, p = 0.001) in the placental TNF-α concentration (pg/ml) among women in their 1st (20.0 pg/ml), 2nd (9.5 pg/ml) and 3rd (16.8 pg/ml) pregnancy. There was also a notable variation (χ² = 19.24, p = 0.00007) in their placental IFN-γ concentrations in the 1st (7.6 pg.ml), 2nd (5.8 pg/ml) and 3rd (7.4 pg/ml) pregnancy.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence of neonatal malnutrition and its associations with maternal parity, placental hematological indices, and inflammatory cytokines in a semi-urban population in Lagos, Nigeria. The findings demonstrate that neonatal malnutrition remains a significant public health concern, with a combined prevalence of severe and moderate malnutrition of 10.6%. Notably, severe malnutrition occurred predominantly among primigravid women, underscoring the heightened vulnerability of first pregnancies to intrauterine nutritional and inflammatory disturbances. The predominance of severe malnutrition among neonates born to primigravidae aligns with established evidence that first pregnancies are associated with increased susceptibility to placental malaria, impaired placental perfusion, and heightened inflammatory responses. These factors may collectively compromise the transfer of nutrients and oxygen to the fetus. The younger mean age of mothers of severely malnourished neonates further suggests that biological immaturity, limited nutritional reserves, or reduced access to antenatal care may contribute to adverse fetal outcomes.

The study also identified significant differences in neonatal anthropometric indices—birthweight, birth length, and head circumference—across nutritional categories. Among malnourished neonates, birthweight and birth length showed no significant correlation, in sharp contrast to the strong positive correlations observed among healthy and overweight neonates. This disruption of expected growth proportionality may reflect asymmetric intrauterine growth restriction, a hallmark of fetal malnutrition.

A key contribution of this study is the evaluation of placental cytokines, particularly TNF-α and IFN-γ, in relation to neonatal nutritional status. In this study, placental TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations were highest among mothers with severely and moderately malnourished newborns, especially among those in the first pregnancy. Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha determines hormone synthesis, placental architecture and embryonic development during pregnancy, [33] and a high concentration of this cytokine which leads to pregnancy loss, [34] and pre-eclampsia, [35] may have disrupted materno-fetal interaction, interrupted the required perfect synergy between maternal immune system and inflammatory response and simultaneously weakened the immune adaptation of the fetus. Elevated TNF-α concentrations among primigravid mothers who delivered severely malnourished neonates suggest a potential role for pro-inflammatory pathways in the pathophysiology of fetal malnutrition. TNF-α is known to impair placental vascular development, reduce nutrient transport, and promote catabolic processes, all of which may restrict fetal growth. The initial release of TNF-α and IFN-γ may be an innate immune response aimed at early malaria parasite clearance and placental preservation. [36, 37] Excessive or dysregulated cytokine production may have contributed to placental pathology, such as placental fibrosis, which probably impaired nutrient transfer to the fetus. Dysregulated cytokine signaling has been implicated in hyperinflammatory states and tissue remodeling, [38] which may potentially explain the restricted fetal growth observed in this study. Although IFN-γ levels did not differ significantly among severely malnourished neonates, marginal differences among moderately malnourished neonates warrant further investigation.

Primiparity and low daily income were more common among mothers who gave birth to malnourished children, a finding similar to what was reported in an earlier study. [39] Fetuses of primiparous women may have to contend with maternal naivety in cyesis and thus exposed to various inflammatory challenges.

In this study, the overall prevalence of fetal malnutrition (both severe and moderate) was 10.6% below the 20.3% reported from Ethiopia [40], the 54.8% from Turkey, [41] the 52.9% from India, [42] the 31.0% from Iraq, [43] and the 33.9% from another part of Nigeria. [21] Yet, the prevalence of severe neonatal malnutrition was 4.7%, similar to the 4.0% prevalence reported by Obani in Northeastern part of Nigeria, [44] though from children aged 6–59 months and not specifically day-old babies.

The generalized placental thrombocytopenia observed across participants may reflect underlying inflammatory or infectious processes, including malaria, which remains endemic in the study area. The presence of placental malaria parasites among mothers of moderately malnourished and healthy neonates underscores the complex, multifactorial nature of fetal growth restriction, in which infection, inflammation, and maternal nutritional status interact dynamically.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research from Ethiopia [20] and Nigeria, [21] which identified placental weight, small-for-gestational-age status, and preterm birth as important determinants of fetal malnutrition. However, few studies in sub-Saharan Africa have examined the immunological mechanisms underlying neonatal malnutrition. [45–47].

The altered cytokine patterns observed in this study parallel findings from adult malnutrition research, [48] suggesting that immune dysregulation may begin in utero.

Data from this study are essential not only for identifying infants at heightened risk for future cardiovascular, metabolic, and other long-term health consequences associated with early-life malnutrition but also could be valuable resources for interventions and preventive strategies to curb fetal and neonatal malnutrition. Furthermore, these findings may inform public health policy makers to promulgate beneficial decision aimed at advancing better maternal and child health outcomes in alignment with national priorities and the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly in a malaria-endemic setting with persistently high maternal, neonatal and infant mortality rates.

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, standardized data collection procedures, and integration of maternal, placental, and neonatal indicators. The use of the Ponderal Index Percentile provided a sensitive measure of neonatal nutritional status on the first day of life. However, the study also has limitations. Cytokine analyses were conducted on a subset of participants, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of cytokine involvement in fetal malnutrition.

Despite these limitations, the findings underscore the need for enhanced antenatal surveillance, particularly for primigravid women, who appear to be at greatest risk of delivering malnourished neonates. Routine assessment of maternal nutritional status, screening for infections, and early identification of placental dysfunction may help mitigate adverse outcomes. Furthermore, the potential role of inflammatory cytokines in fetal malnutrition highlights the importance of multidisciplinary research to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study shows that severe neonatal malnutrition was most prevalent among primigravid women, highlighting the heightened vulnerability of first pregnancies to intrauterine nutritional and inflammatory disturbances. Elevated placental TNF-α concentrations among primigravid mothers who delivered severely malnourished neonates suggest a potential role for pro-inflammatory pathways in the pathophysiology of fetal malnutrition. Although IFN-γ patterns were less consistent, the observed cytokine variations underscore the need for further investigation into immunological mechanisms that contribute to impaired fetal growth.

The findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive antenatal assessment of maternal and fetal nutritional status, particularly for first-time mothers. Strengthening routine screening for maternal infections, nutritional deficiencies, and placental dysfunction may help reduce early neonatal morbidity and mortality. Moreover, the study highlights the urgent need for multicenter, multidisciplinary research in sub-Saharan Africa to better elucidate the biological, environmental, and immunological determinants of neonatal malnutrition and to inform targeted preventive and therapeutic strategies.
