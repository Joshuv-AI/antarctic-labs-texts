Cultural empathy development through simulation-based education: a qualitative exploration of Saudi nursing students’ and academics’ experiences
Authors: Ashwaq A Almutairi, Abdulaziz M Alodhailah, Shorok Hamed Alahmedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCultural empathy development among nursing students requires innovative educational approaches that facilitate deep understanding and perspective-taking. While simulation-based interventions show promise, the lived experiences and perceptions of students and educators regarding cultural empathy development remain underexplored in Middle Eastern contexts, particularly where healthcare increasingly serves diverse populations.MethodsGuided by an interpretivist–constructivist paradigm, this qualitative descriptive study was conducted in 2022 at the College of Nursing, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, as part of a larger mixed-methods project. Eight focus groups were held with 58 first-year nursing students following a cultural empathy simulation activity, and six semi-structured interviews were conducted with nursing academics who observed the simulation. Focus groups were conducted in Arabic and translated into English; interviews were in English. Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework guided data analysis.ResultsStudent focus groups generated four themes: (1) Growing awareness of culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) patient needs, (2) Imagining walking in CALD patients’ shoes, (3) Exploring barriers and solutions when working with CALD patients, and (4) Reflecting on the value and importance of cultural sensitivity in nursing. Participants described the simulation as transformative, offering experiential learning beyond traditional didactic approaches.ConclusionsCulturally focused simulation experiences can facilitate profound shifts in students’ cultural awareness and empathetic understanding. Background

Preparing nursing students to deliver culturally responsive, person-centred care requires more than factual knowledge about customs or language differences; it demands the cultivation of cultural empathy—the capacity to recognise, understand and appropriately respond to patients’ lived experiences across cultural boundaries. Simulation-based education (SBE) has become a prominent strategy for developing such non-technical capabilities because it provides psychologically safe, authentic contexts for practice, feedback and reflection. Recent evidence syntheses show SBE can improve empathy in pre-registration nursing cohorts, albeit with small-to-moderate pooled effects and heterogeneity that reflects variation in design features and outcome measures [1, 2]. More generally, SBE enhances a spectrum of socio-emotional competencies (communication, teamwork, ethical sensitivity) that underpin empathic practice [3, 4].

The design of SBE matters. Reviews highlight that immersion, scenario authenticity, opportunities to take the patient role, and structured debriefing are key drivers of attitudinal change, including empathy [5–7]. Debriefing supports learners to connect emotions elicited in scenario work to real-world behaviours, while prebriefing and clear psychological safety guard against defensive reactions that can blunt learning [8–12]. These insights situate SBE as a pedagogical approach aligned with interpretivist–constructivist learning, where meaning-making through guided reflection is central to developing professional identity and empathic dispositions.

Digital modalities further extend empathy-oriented simulation. Immersive virtual reality (VR) scenarios can heighten perspective-taking by placing learners “in the patient’s shoes,” with qualitative studies reporting compassion, concern and recognition of patients’ emotions during VR gameplay [13], and feasibility studies demonstrating gains in cross-cultural communication and engagement [14]. Although VR’s optimal design parameters and transfer to clinical behaviour require continued evaluation, converging findings suggest that well-scaffolded virtual simulations can complement high-fidelity manikin and standardized-patient work to support empathic growth in novice learners.

These trends intersect with pressing needs in Saudi Arabia, where health-system transformation under Vision 2030 emphasises patient-centred care, workforce development and the adoption of innovative educational technologies [15]. The Saudi health sector serves culturally and linguistically diverse populations—including expatriate residents and multinational health workforces—where culturally attuned communication and accommodation of beliefs and practices are routine requirements of safe care [15, 16]. Recent Saudi and regional studies document variable baseline empathy and cultural competence among learners and clinicians, underscoring an education gap and the need for formative, experiential approaches early in training [17].

Despite growing global literature on empathy training and cultural competence interventions, several gaps remain. First, meta-analytic evidence confirms that education can move the needle on empathy and cultural competence but also notes modest effect sizes and low-to-very-low certainty for some outcomes, especially when interventions rely on self-report measures [18, 19]. Second, much of the extant scholarship originates in Western or high-income settings, with comparatively fewer qualitative accounts that unpack how SBE fosters cultural empathy in Middle Eastern nursing education, and how contextual factors (e.g., gender dynamics, language, religious practices) shape learning processes and outcomes. Third, few studies juxtapose learner and educator perspectives to illuminate alignment—and tensions—between curricular intent, scenario design and debriefing practices, even though facilitation skill is pivotal to empathy development [20]. Addressing these gaps requires contextually anchored qualitative inquiry that traces students’ and academics’ meaning-making around culturally focused simulation activities [21, 22].

In the Saudi context specifically, curricular efforts to embed cultural competence have advanced, yet the literature still indicates mixed readiness and variable skill in navigating cross-cultural encounters, particularly among early-year students [23]. Qualitative explorations can add needed depth by capturing the emotional and cognitive work learners undertake during simulation (e.g., discomfort, moral reasoning, perspective-taking) and by identifying facilitation practices that help convert those moments into durable empathic habits. Such insights are essential for aligning SBE with Vision 2030’s educational priorities, informing faculty development for culturally sensitive debriefing, and guiding resource allocation (e.g., standardized-patient casting, language supports, VR content) to scale culturally focused simulations within Saudi nursing programmes.

Accordingly, this study adopts an interpretivist–constructivist stance and explores Saudi first-year nursing students’ experiences in a cultural-empathy simulation, alongside academics’ reflections on curriculum integration and facilitation. By situating findings within ongoing health-system reform and educational innovation, the study aims to provide actionable guidance for designing and delivering SBE that nurtures empathic, culturally responsive practice in Saudi Arabia and comparable non-Western contexts.

Methods

Study design

This investigation adopted a qualitative descriptive design to capture and interpret participants’ accounts of their experiences with a cultural empathy simulation. The approach was selected for its suitability in generating rich, contextually grounded descriptions of complex educational phenomena while remaining close to the participants’ own language and meanings. The qualitative strand formed part of a larger convergent parallel mixed-methods study, allowing integration of experiential narratives with quantitative measures for a comprehensive understanding.

Philosophical orientation

The study was underpinned by an interpretivist paradigm, which assumes that reality is socially constructed through interaction and shared meaning-making. This orientation recognises that understanding the development of empathy is contingent upon exploring how individuals perceive, interpret, and ascribe significance to educational experiences, rather than solely measuring predefined constructs. A constructivist epistemology informed the research process, positioning knowledge as co-created between participants and researchers through dialogue and reflection.

Setting

The study took place at the College of Nursing, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, during the first semester of the 2022–2023 academic year. The cultural empathy simulation formed part of a scheduled educational intervention within the undergraduate nursing curriculum.

Participants and recruitment

Two participant groups were purposively sampled to ensure information-rich perspectives.

Undergraduate nursing students: Eligible participants were first-year Bachelor of Nursing students who had completed the cultural empathy simulation. This cohort was targeted due to their receptiveness to formative educational experiences and minimal prior clinical exposure, which reduced the influence of entrenched practice habits. Recruitment was conducted via classroom announcements and voluntary sign-up sheets. No incentives were provided, ensuring voluntary participation without coercion.

Undergraduate nursing students: Eligible participants were first-year Bachelor of Nursing students who had completed the cultural empathy simulation. This cohort was targeted due to their receptiveness to formative educational experiences and minimal prior clinical exposure, which reduced the influence of entrenched practice habits. Recruitment was conducted via classroom announcements and voluntary sign-up sheets. No incentives were provided, ensuring voluntary participation without coercion.

Academic staff: Nursing faculty members who had observed or been directly involved in the simulation were invited to participate in individual interviews. The sample reflected variation in nursing specialties, teaching experience, and familiarity with simulation pedagogy. All academic participants had observed at least one complete simulation cycle, providing them with direct and informed insights.

Academic staff: Nursing faculty members who had observed or been directly involved in the simulation were invited to participate in individual interviews. The sample reflected variation in nursing specialties, teaching experience, and familiarity with simulation pedagogy. All academic participants had observed at least one complete simulation cycle, providing them with direct and informed insights.

Among the academics, two had facilitated the simulation sessions, while the remaining four acted solely as observers. This distinction was considered when analysing perspectives to account for potential differences in interpretive stance.

Data collection

Student focus groups

Eight focus groups were conducted with a total of 58 students, with 6–8 participants per group to balance diversity of views with effective discussion management. Focus groups were selected over individual interviews to encourage interaction, stimulate reflection, and elicit shared or divergent viewpoints. Sessions, lasting 45–60 min, were held in Arabic to facilitate authentic emotional expression and minimise linguistic barriers. A semi-structured guide explored students’ simulation experiences, comparisons with prior learning, perceived benefits and challenges, emotional reactions, and suggestions for improvement. Guides were developed specifically for this study, informed by literature on empathy education and simulation-based pedagogy. An English version of the guide is available in Supplementary File 1.

Academic interviews

Six semi-structured interviews, each lasting 30–45 min, were conducted in English, reflecting the multilingual academic environment. Interview topics included observations of student engagement, perceived educational value of the simulation, comparison with traditional teaching, feasibility, and recommendations for curricular integration.

Both data collection formats were facilitated by researchers independent of participants’ formal assessment to minimise power imbalances and encourage candid discussion.

Data management and transcription

All sessions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Arabic focus group recordings were transcribed verbatim in Arabic before being translated into English by a certified translator experienced in healthcare terminology. Back-translation was performed by an independent bilingual healthcare educator to verify semantic and conceptual equivalence. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussion between translators and the research team. Thematic coding was undertaken with reference to both the original Arabic and translated English transcripts to preserve meaning. All transcripts were anonymised, assigned participant codes, and stored on password-protected devices accessible only to the research team.

Data analysis

Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis framework: (1) immersion in the data through repeated reading, (2) systematic generation of initial codes, (3) collation of codes into potential themes, (4) review and refinement of themes for internal coherence and distinction, (5) definition and naming of themes, and (6) production of an analytic narrative supported by illustrative quotations. Coding was conducted inductively using NVivo 12 software, ensuring findings emerged from participants’ accounts rather than from predetermined theoretical categories.

Data saturation

was assessed using the Comparative Method for Theme Saturation (CoMeTS) approach, with saturation deemed reached when no new codes or themes appeared in two consecutive focus groups and in the final academic interview.

Ethical considerations

Results

The qualitative analysis revealed profound insights into how simulation-based cultural empathy education impacts student learning and perspective development. The thematic structure reflects the experiential journey students underwent, from initial awareness through emotional processing to actionable understanding.

Participant demographics

A total of 58 first-year nursing students and 6 nursing academics participated in the study. Student participants were predominantly female, reflecting the overall gender distribution in the nursing programme (Table 1). The mean age of student participants was 19.8 years (SD = 0.9), while academic participants had a mean of 11.3 years (SD = 4.2) of teaching experience. Academics represented a range of nursing specialties, and two had direct facilitation roles in the simulation, while the remainder were observers.

Student focus group findings

Analysis of the eight focus groups with first-year nursing students revealed a rich set of experiences and reflections on participating in the cultural empathy simulation. Students described a transformative learning process that moved beyond technical skill acquisition to encompass emotional engagement, critical self-awareness, and a deeper appreciation of culturally responsive care (Table 2). The discussions illuminated how immersive simulation prompted shifts in perspective, encouraged empathy through lived patient experiences, and stimulated practical problem-solving for cross-cultural challenges. Four overarching themes emerged from the analysis, each comprising distinct but interconnected subthemes that collectively depict the developmental trajectory of cultural empathy among novice nursing students.

Themes and subthemes from student and academic participant narratives

Theme 1: Growing awareness of CALD patient needs

Participants consistently described heightened consciousness of cultural diversity’s impact on healthcare experiences following the simulation activity. This awareness manifested as recognition of previously unconsidered challenges faced by culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) patients within the Saudi healthcare system.

Students frequently contrasted the cultural empathy simulation with their previous simulation experiences, which focused primarily on technical nursing skills without cultural dimensions:

Yes, I have tried simulation training, but not with regard to cultural empathy… all the training in the nursing labs was focused on simulating nursing skills only. There was no simulation or scenario that allowed us to feel empathy or experience different cultural situations. (P3, FG6)

The simulation experience awakened students to the reality of Saudi Arabia’s changing demographics and the implications for healthcare delivery:

We are now approaching the stage in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia where there is cultural diversity and diversity of nationalities, so I believe that this is very important… Saudi Arabia has opened its tourist visas with ease, and one day they may need care, so our nursing staff should be able to understand people of all nationalities. (P4, FG2)

This newfound awareness extended beyond theoretical understanding to practical recognition of how cultural factors influence patient experiences:

…and how it has increased our awareness as future nurses for patients who come from different backgrounds… we need to understand their feelings and what they’re going through. (P2, FG1)

Theme 2: Imagining walking in CALD patients’ shoes

The simulation’s immersive nature enabled students to engage in perspective-taking that transcended intellectual understanding, facilitating genuine emotional connection with CALD patients’ experiences. This theme captures the transformative process of moving from observational learning to experiential understanding.

Students described visceral responses to witnessing patient struggles from multiple perspectives:

When I saw the patient crying and suffering because she couldn’t communicate… I felt like I was in her place. I imagined how scared and lonely she must feel in that moment. (P6, FG3)

The 3D simulation technology enhanced this perspective-taking by allowing students to literally see through different viewpoints:

The 3D experience made it so real… I could see from the patient’s eyes, from the family’s view, and from the nurse’s position. Each view showed me something different about the same situation. (P8, FG4)

Students recognised the emotional labour required for empathetic care:

Before the simulation, I thought caring was just about doing the right medical procedures. But now I understand that caring means feeling with the patient, understanding their emotions and their cultural needs. (P5, FG7)

Theme 3: Exploring barriers and solutions when working with CALD patients

Participants demonstrated sophisticated understanding of systemic and interpersonal barriers that impede culturally sensitive care whilst proposing practical solutions based on their simulation experiences.

Students identified communication as the primary barrier to effective cross-cultural care:

The biggest problem is language… when patients can’t explain their symptoms or understand our instructions, everyone becomes frustrated and the care suffers. (P7, FG1)

Proposed solutions emphasised both systemic and individual approaches:

We need more professional interpreters in hospitals, but we also need to learn basic phrases in common languages spoken by our patients… even simple greetings can make patients feel more comfortable. (P3, FG5)

Students recognised the importance of accommodating diverse religious and cultural practices:

Some patients have different prayer times, dietary needs, or modesty requirements. We learned that asking about these needs shows respect and improves the relationship. (P4, FG8)

Theme 4: Reflecting on the value and importance of cultural sensitivity in nursing

This theme encompasses students’ deepened appreciation for cultural competence as an essential nursing competency, moving beyond technical skills to embrace holistic, person-centred care approaches.

Students expressed transformed understanding of professional nursing identity:

Being a nurse is not just about giving medications or doing procedures… it’s about understanding each patient as a unique person with their own background, beliefs, and needs. (P8, FG2)

The simulation connected cultural empathy to patient outcomes:

When patients feel understood and respected, they trust us more. They’re more likely to follow treatment plans and share important information about their symptoms. (P5, FG3)

Many students advocated for curricular integration:

Every nursing student should experience this kind of simulation… it should be part of our regular curriculum, not just a one-time activity. (P7, FG5)

Academic interview findings

Theme 1: Expanding simulation in the Saudi curriculum

Academics expressed enthusiasm for simulation’s potential to address long-standing gaps in cultural competency education within Saudi nursing curricula. They viewed the cultural empathy simulation as an innovative solution to traditional pedagogical limitations.

One academic noted the contrast between theoretical and experiential learning:

We can lecture about cultural diversity for hours, but students don’t truly understand until they experience it themselves. The simulation creates that ‘aha moment’ that transforms theoretical knowledge into personal understanding. (Academic 3)

Academics recognised simulation’s unique ability to create safe learning environments for culturally sensitive topics:

Students can make mistakes, ask questions, and explore their biases in the simulation without harm to real patients. This safety allows for honest learning and growth. (Academic 1)

The immersive nature of simulation was particularly valued:

The 3D technology takes students beyond reading about cultural differences to actually experiencing them. They see facial expressions, hear tone of voice, and feel the emotional impact of cultural misunderstandings. (Academic 5)

Theme 2: Learning to connect and empathise with CALD patients’ feelings

This theme reflects academics’ observations of students’ emotional engagement and empathetic development throughout the simulation experience.

Academics described witnessing genuine emotional responses:

I saw students with tears in their eyes during the debriefing. They weren’t just intellectually understanding the patients’ struggles—they were feeling them. (Academic 4)

The progression from cognitive to affective learning was frequently observed:

Initially, students approached the scenarios analytically, trying to solve problems. But as the simulation continued, I watched them begin to emotionally connect with the patients’ experiences. (Academic 6)

Academics noted improved communication skills following the simulation:

Students began using different language when discussing patients—more empathetic, more person-centred. They started saying ‘the person with diabetes’ instead of ‘the diabetic patient. (Academic 1)

Theme 3: Preparing first-year students for culturally empathetic practice

Academics engaged in nuanced discussions about optimal timing and sequencing of cultural empathy education within nursing curricula, considering developmental readiness and curricular integration.

Several academics supported early introduction of cultural empathy concepts:

First-year students come with open minds and fewer preconceptions. This is the ideal time to shape their understanding of culturally sensitive care. (Academic 2)

Others emphasised the need for developmental scaffolding:

Cultural empathy should be introduced early but reinforced throughout the programme. Students need multiple exposures to different cultural scenarios as they develop clinical expertise. (Academic 5)

Academics discussed integration with existing curricula:

Rather than adding another course, we should embed cultural empathy simulations within existing subjects—medical-surgical, paediatrics, mental health. Each specialty has unique cultural considerations. (Academic 4)

Academics recognised the need for faculty development:

We need to prepare faculty to facilitate these simulations effectively. Cultural empathy education requires skilled debriefing and cultural sensitivity from educators themselves. (Academic 1)

Discussion

This qualitative exploration offers an in-depth understanding of how simulation-based education (SBE) can foster cultural empathy among first-year nursing students in Saudi Arabia, as perceived by both learners and academic staff. The integration of these perspectives highlights the multifaceted nature of empathy development and reinforces the value of immersive, culturally focused simulation within undergraduate nursing curricula.

Cultural empathy as a developmental process

The findings affirm that cultural empathy emerges through a staged process that includes awareness of diversity, perspective-taking, recognition of barriers, and integration of culturally sensitive practices into one’s professional identity. This trajectory aligns with models of professional empathy development in healthcare, which propose a progression from cognitive recognition to affective engagement and, ultimately, behavioural adaptation [24, 25]. The students’ descriptions of “seeing through the patient’s eyes” and “imagining being in their place” illustrate the transformative potential of experiential learning, echoing earlier work demonstrating that immersive scenarios can generate emotional resonance not achievable through didactic methods alone [26].

The role of immersive simulation and guided reflection

Both students and academics emphasised the critical role of immersion—particularly the use of 3D visualisation—in enhancing perspective-taking. Recent studies indicate that high-fidelity simulation and virtual reality environments can trigger embodied experiences that foster empathy and cultural understanding, provided these are accompanied by structured debriefing [27]. In our study, debriefing was repeatedly cited as the space where emotional reactions were processed and linked to professional values. This supports the argument that simulation without reflective dialogue risks remaining a technical exercise, whereas debriefing transforms experience into deeper learning [28].

Faculty facilitation as a determinant of empathy outcomes

The academics’ observations underscore that the educational impact of cultural empathy simulations depends heavily on the facilitator’s ability to create a psychologically safe environment, encourage self-reflection, and challenge assumptions without alienating learners. This mirrors international findings that faculty skill in managing sensitive cultural content is pivotal for learner engagement and for translating insights into practice [29, 30]. In Saudi Arabia, where sociocultural norms shape both patient–provider relationships and classroom interactions, faculty competence in culturally sensitive debriefing is especially important. Faculty development programmes should therefore prioritise training in facilitation strategies tailored to culturally diverse scenarios.

Contextual relevance in Saudi Arabia

The Saudi healthcare context is characterised by increasing cultural and linguistic diversity due to workforce globalisation and Vision 2030 reforms [31]. Our participants’ recognition of the need for culturally competent care resonates with recent regional studies reporting gaps in nurses’ readiness to provide such care [32]. The congruence between Islamic principles of compassion and the goals of empathy education may facilitate learner receptiveness, as found in other Middle Eastern settings where cultural and religious values support humanistic care principles [33]. However, traditional hierarchical pedagogies may limit learners’ willingness to challenge biases or engage openly in reflective dialogue [34]. This highlights the need to blend culturally congruent values with learner-centred pedagogies in empathy training.

Comparison with non-Western contexts

While much of the literature on cultural empathy in nursing stems from Western contexts, emerging evidence from East Asia, the Middle East, and Africa shows that simulation can be adapted successfully to diverse cultural and educational systems [35, 36]. For example, studies in Korea and Jordan demonstrate that immersive simulation addressing cultural and linguistic barriers improves both empathy and communication confidence [37]. Our findings are consistent with these reports but add the dimension of academic perspectives, which shed light on curricular integration challenges and faculty preparedness. This dual perspective may be particularly valuable for informing the scaling of cultural empathy simulations in non-Western contexts.

Barriers and solutions for culturally competent care

Students identified communication difficulties, religious and dietary needs, and cultural practices as central barriers to providing culturally competent care—issues widely documented in cross-cultural healthcare literature [38]. The proposed solutions in this study, including interpreter services, basic language skills, and proactive inquiry into patient preferences, align with best-practice guidelines for culturally responsive care [39]. Notably, participants emphasised the importance of showing respect through simple gestures, such as greetings in the patient’s language, reflecting evidence that even small acts can improve trust and rapport [40, 41].

Study limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these qualitative findings. The single-site design and cultural specificity may limit transferability to other educational contexts or cultural settings. The immediate post-intervention timing of data collection captured vivid experiences but prevented assessment of long-term impacts or changes in perspective over time. The voluntary nature of focus group participation may have introduced selection bias, with more engaged or satisfied students choosing to participate. Additionally, the group-based nature of student focus groups may have introduced social desirability bias, with some participants potentially offering more socially acceptable responses in the presence of peers. The perspectives reflect a single institutional context, which may limit transferability to other nursing programmes or cultural settings. Despite rigorous translation and back-translation procedures, some subtle meanings or culturally embedded expressions may have been altered or lost in translation.

Implications for practice and policy

The qualitative findings have immediate implications for nursing education practice and policy development. The demonstrated effectiveness of culturally-focused simulation for empathy development supports investment in simulation technology and faculty development programmes to support widespread implementation.

The themes reveal specific requirements for successful empathy education including skilled facilitation, structured debriefing, and safe learning environments that encourage honest reflection and exploration of cultural biases. Educational institutions should consider these requirements when planning empathy education initiatives.

The academic perspectives on curricular integration suggest that empathy education should be embedded throughout nursing programmes rather than isolated in standalone courses. This integration requires systematic curricular planning and coordination across different nursing specialties and educational levels.

Conclusions

This qualitative study highlights the powerful impact of culturally-focused simulation-based education on empathy development among Saudi nursing students. It reveals how carefully designed simulations foster shifts in cultural awareness, emotional engagement, and professional identity, effects that go beyond traditional learning outcomes. The alignment between Islamic values of compassion and the goals of empathy education demonstrates that cultural foundations can enhance, rather than hinder, empathy development when integrated thoughtfully.

By combining student and academic perspectives, the study offers a deeper understanding of empathy as a transformative process requiring experiential learning and reflection. These findings complement existing quantitative evidence, offering insight into how students experience and interpret empathy education. The implications extend beyond Saudi Arabia, offering practical principles, such as cultural responsiveness and guided reflection, that can inform empathy training in diverse healthcare contexts globally. As patient populations grow more diverse, equipping nurses with culturally attuned empathy is increasingly essential for quality care and better outcomes.
