Successful Treatment of Primary Extramammary Paget Disease with a Combined Ablative Carbon Dioxide Laser and Topical Imiquimod
Authors: Shekhar Neema, D. Senkadhir Vendhan, Biju Vasudevan, A. W. Kashif
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Extramammary Paget disease (EMPD) is a rare intraepithelial adenocarcinoma primarily affecting apocrine-rich areas, most commonly the vulva in elderly women. While surgical excision is the gold standard, its limitations in extensive disease necessitate alternative therapies. We report a case of a 75-year-old woman with a 2-year history of progressive vulvar erosion diagnosed histologically and immunohistochemically as primary EMPD. Owing to the lesion’s extent and the patient’s preference to avoid surgery, she was treated with a combination of ablative carbon dioxide (CO2) laser therapy and topical 5% imiquimod. Four biweekly sessions of CO2 laser therapy yielded significant resolution in the upper vulva, while residual disease in the lower vulva responded completely to a three-month course of imiquimod cream applied thrice weekly. Follow-up biopsy at six months confirmed histological remission, and no recurrence was observed at 12 months. This case highlights a successful, non-invasive treatment approach combining laser ablation with immunomodulatory therapy, offering a promising alternative to surgery in extensive EMPD with favorable outcomes and cosmetic acceptability.

Introduction

Extramammary Paget disease (EMPD) is a rare malignant condition affecting areas rich in apocrine sweat glands such as genital skin and axilla. The incidence of EMPD ranges from 0.1 to 2.4 patients per 10,00,000 person-years, affecting individuals between 50 and 80 years, with peak incidence at 65 years. Women are affected more commonly, with the vulva being the most common site of involvement.[1] Surgical excision of the lesion remains the gold standard in the management of EMPD. We hereby report a case of EMPD managed successfully with an ablative mode carbon dioxide (CO2) laser and topical imiquimod cream with no recurrence after six months of therapy.

Case Report

A 75 years old female, presented to the dermatology out-patient department with genital erosion of 2 years. The lesion started over the lower third and gradually progressed to involve the entire vulva over 2 years. These lesions were associated with mild discomfort, oozing and did not respond to topical antibiotics, steroids and antifungals. No history of pain, weight loss, bowel or bladder disturbances, or any constitutional symptoms were noted. Dermatological examination revealed the involvement of the vulva in the form of well-defined erythematous erosive plaque measuring 25 × 15 cm [Figure 1]. Dermoscopy of the lesion shows milky red areas, dotted vessels and white structureless areas [Figure 2]. Gynaecological and breast examinations were normal and there was no regional lymphadenopathy. Biopsy taken from the vulva showed large, rounded cells with abundant pale cytoplasm and a large nucleus with prominent nucleolus (Paget cells) in the basal layer of the epidermis. These cells were seen extending into the pilosebaceous units [Figure 3]. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), Epithelial membrane antibody (EMA), Cytokeratin (CK7, CK 20) [Figures 4-6]. Haematological and biochemistry panels, tumour markers, ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis, contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan of the abdomen, hysteroscopy were within normal limits. Considering the extensive involvement of the vulva and the patient’s personal preferences, the CO2 laser was preferred over surgical excision. Ablative CO2 laser treatment (Dermaindia, continuous wave mode at 5W) was administered every two weeks. During each visit, ablative laser treatment in continuous mode was administered until the patient tolerated the pain and superficial skin charring. The upper two-thirds of vulva showed excellent clinical response after four sessions while lower one-third remained symptomatic. Topical imiquimod cream 5% was prescribed thrice weekly for 3 months, with which remission was obtained and patient continues to be in remission six months after stopping treatment [Figure 7]. A repeat biopsy was done from the lower 1/3rd of vulva after 6 months that showed no residual disease activity [Figure 8]. Patient continues to be on regular follow-up for a period of 12 months after stopping treatment.

Discussion and Conclusion

Extramammary Paget Disease (EMPD) is a rare form of cutaneous adenocarcinoma that primarily affects areas rich in apocrine glands, such as the vulva, perianal region, and axilla. EMPD is mostly seen in the postmenopausal woman with a red scaly plaque which may be pruritic or painful sometimes mimicking inflammatory and infective skin conditions such as psoriasis, eczema, and moniliasis. Later the lesions may become erosive, weepy, or rarely with infiltrated nodules, vegetating lesions, and regional lymphadenopathy.[2] EMPD is classified into two types based on its origin. Primary EPMD originates from the epidermis or apocrine gland-bearing skin, predominantly affecting areas like the vulva, perianal region, and axilla. It typically manifests as an intraepithelial adenocarcinoma without an underlying malignancy. Secondary EPMD arises from an underlying malignancy, commonly colorectal, urinary, or genital cancer. Malignant cells extend from deeper tissues to the epidermis in secondary EMPD.[3] EMPD is characterized histopathologically by intraepidermal proliferation of Paget cells which are large cells with centrally situated large nuclei and abundant basophilic cytoplasm. Paget cells in primary EMPD usually stain for markers of Cytokeratin (CK) 7+/20− while the secondary EMPD has expression of CK7+/20+, though the rare primary EMPD cases may show expression of CK20 as seen in our case. Other markers of importance include carcinoembryonic antigen, epithelial membrane antibody, HER-2/neu, and gross cystic disease fluid protein-15.[4] Dermoscopy is helpful in the diagnosis of EMPD and in differentiating it from eczema and Bowen’s disease. It shows milky red area, erosions, dotted vessels, structureless white areas and shiny white lines.[5] Surgery is considered the gold standard in the management of patients with EMPD; however, there are high frequencies of recurrences after excision. A low recurrence rate is achieved using Mohs microscopic surgery. Radiotherapy can be utilized for inoperable lesions or as an adjuvant treatment to surgical excision, especially in the case of postoperative recurrence. Systemic chemotherapeutic agents such as vincristine, docetaxel, carboplatin, 5-FU, and mitomycin-C have been tried. Local applications of cytotoxic drugs such as five fluorouracil help in decreasing the margins of the lesions. Imiquimod 5% cream, a topical immune response modifier, has been successfully used in the management of superficial EMPD.[6] Other interventions in the management of EMPD include topical and systemic photodynamic therapy, Laser therapy, or a combination of these modalities. Carbon dioxide laser ablative mode can be used to treat non-invasive forms of EMPD.[7] We achieved remission in our case by combining CO2 laser with topical imiquimod.
