PRMT5 in mitochondria regulates mtDNA stability through TFAM arginine methylation
Authors: Sangheeta Bhattacharjee, Sayan Das, Banhi Chowdhury, Benu Brata Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5) catalyzes arginine methylation and regulates cellular functions such as proliferation, RNA splicing, and nuclear DNA damage response. This study uncovers that a fraction of nuclear-encoded PRMT5 localizes to the mitochondria, which is critical for maintaining mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) homeostasis. PRMT5 knockout (PRMT5-/-) cells had reduced nucleoid counts, diminished mtDNA copy numbers, disrupted the balance of the mitochondrial fission-fusion cycle, impaired mitochondrial plasticity, and nucleoid trafficking. PRMT5-/- cells are hypersensitive to mtDNA-damaging agents, exhibit reduced mitochondrial transcripts, oxidative phosphorylation, and respiratory capacity that triggers cell death. We identify TFAM as a previously unrecognized interacting partner of PRMT5, which catalyzes symmetric dimethylation of TFAM at R82 residue, which is crucial for mtDNA binding and protection. Defective R82-methylation destabilizes TFAM, which is then degraded by LonP1. Together, we establish that PRMT5 is a mitochondrial enzyme and a key regulator of TFAM in mtDNA maintenance.Subject terms: DNA damage and repair, Methylation

Introduction

Arginine methylation is a key post-translational modification (PTM) responsible for the addition of the methyl group to arginine residues. It is increasingly recognized as a pivotal PTM orchestrating a variety of cellular processes, including epigenetic regulation, DNA repair, and nuclear genome maintenance1,2. Protein arginine methylation is an irreversible modification in which the guanidinium group of arginyl residues becomes methylated in a reaction with S-adenosylmethionine as the methyl donor. PRMTs are grouped into three types: type 1 (including PRMT1-4, PRMT6, PRMT8), type II (comprising PRMT5 and PRMT9), and type III (represented by PRMT7) enzymes3. This classification is determined by their ability to catalyze the formation of asymmetric dimethylated arginine (ADMA), symmetric dimethylated arginine (SDMA), and monomethylated arginine (MMA), respectively. PRMT5 has emerged as a major symmetric dimethylating enzyme involved in the arginine methylation of a myriad of substrates4–7. PRMT5 knockout in mice causes early embryonic death, while its depletion leads to defects in cell proliferation8,9. PRMT5 is primarily known for its role in regulating gene expression and chromatin structure through the methylation of histone proteins8. PRMT5 preserves genomic stability by methylating arginine residues on key DNA damage response (DDR) proteins, including 53BP1, FEN1, RAD9, RUVBL1, and TDP14,6,7,10–13. But how PRMT5-mediated arginine methylation regulates mitochondrial function is still unknown.

The mitochondrial genome lacks the genes essential for mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) replication, transcription, repair, and maintenance, compelling it to rely only on proteins expressed in the nucleus for mtDNA integrity. Nuclear encoded mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) belongs to the high-mobility group (HMG) of structural proteins; it acts as a transcription factor, replication initiator, and mtDNA packaging factor, thus becoming the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis14,15. Human mtDNA, which is circular DNA (~16.5 kb), is a crucial component of eukaryotic cells that encodes a critical subset of mitochondrial proteins required for cellular ATP generation16–18.

TFAM binds mtDNA and plays a crucial role in the formation of mitochondrial nucleoids. TFAM wraps around mtDNA and plays a crucial role in the formation of mitochondrial nucleoids for mtDNA maintenance and ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)14,19–21. TFAM binds mtDNA in both a sequence-dependent and sequence-independent manner and contributes to mtDNA compaction and nucleoid formation14,19,21. Sequence-dependent binding to mtDNA promoter regions is required for initiating mitochondrial transcription, which may also serve as the RNA primer for replication initiation21,22. In addition, TFAM binds mtDNA in a sequence-independent manner to compact the genome and protect it from mtDNA damage14,20,23,24.

TFAM also regulates the dynamics of the mitochondrial network, which involves intricate processes of fission and fusion, which are essential for maintaining its structural integrity and functional efficacy25,26. Mitochondrial fusion has been proposed to compensate for mtDNA damage by facilitating the transfer of nucleoids, ensuring the preservation of mtDNA copies27–29. Reductions in mtDNA copy number and the accumulation of mtDNA damage have been linked to various neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, and Huntington’s diseases, which have been correlated with TFAM; its exact role is elusive23,30. There is limited evidence for the post-translational regulation of TFAM31–33. While studies have investigated the phosphorylation and acetylation of TFAM, which are implicated in its dissociation from mtDNA31,32, other PTMs of TFAM that play a role in its selective binding to mtDNA and its role in fission-fusion dynamics are not known.

This study is the first to demonstrate the localization of catalytically active PRMT5 within mitochondria and its functional coupling with TFAM. In this article, we identify that PRMT5 catalyzes dimethylation of TFAM at R82, which promotes the mitochondrial fission-fusion cycle, stimulates its recruitment to mtDNA, shields the mitochondrial nucleoid, regulates mitochondrial gene transcription and oxygen consumption, providing cellular protection against mitochondria-targeted DNA-damaging agents.

Results

Nuclear-encoded PRMT5 is translocated to the mitochondria

While performing immunofluorescence staining, we observed that PRMT5 was primarily located in the cytoplasm (Fig. 1A), consistent with previous reports8. To determine whether PRMT5 localizes to the mitochondria, we co-stained MCF7 cells with endogenous PRMT5 and the mitochondria-specific marker HSP60 and analyzed them by super-resolution microscopy. Upon immunofluorescence, we detected significant colocalization of PRMT5 (red) with mitochondrial matrix protein HSP60 (green), suggesting the presence of a substantial fraction of PRMT5 in mitochondria (Fig. 1A, B). Under identical conditions, immunofluorescence using the anti-PRMT5 antibody in PRMT5 knockout (PRMT5-/-) cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A) revealed no detectable PRMT5 signal in the mitochondria. Next, we examined the expression of the exogenous FLAG-PRMT5 fusion protein using super-resolution microscopy. Figure 1C, D demonstrates the colocalization of the FLAG-PRMT5 (red) fluorescence pattern with HSP60 (green) upon ectopic expression of PRMT5 in MCF7 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Quantitative image analysis (Fig. 1A, C) demonstrates significant colocalization between PRMT5 and HSP60, as evident by the Pearson correlation coefficient (Fig. 1E). Together, these immunofluorescence data indicate that PRMT5 localizes to mitochondria.

Next, we performed cellular fractionation and Western blotting to confirm the immunofluorescence findings. We detected the presence of PRMT5 as a single band with a similar molecular weight (72.7 kDa) in the mitochondrial (M) fractions together with nuclear (N), cytoplasmic (C) of PRMT5-competent cells (Fig.1F and Supplementary Fig. 1C). It is unlikely that the mitochondrial PRMT5 signal was the result of nuclear contamination because nuclear Lamin B1, which is an abundant nuclear protein, was undetectable in the mitochondrial fraction (Fig.1F and Supplementary Fig. 1C). The relative purity of the mitochondrial and nuclear fractions was further assessed by the analysis of TOM20, Cyt C, COX IV and LONP1 which were only detectable in the mitochondrial fractions (Fig.1F and Supplementary Fig. 1C). The cytoplasmic fraction is checked by GAPDH analysis.

To examine PRMT5 function in the mitochondria, we developed CRISPR-Cas9 to knock out PRMT5 expression and developed the PRMT5-/- MCF7 isogenic cell line (Supplementary Fig. 1D). The knockout efficiency was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Notably, we failed to detect the PRMT5 signal in any of the subcellular fractions in the PRMT5-/- cell line (Fig. 1F), suggesting PRMT5 is translocated to mitochondria.

To further confirm that PRMT5 is present inside mitochondria rather than adventitiously associated with the outer surface of the organelle, we performed proteinase K (ProK) protection assays. ProK treatment of purified mitochondria has often been used to assess the mitochondrial localization of a protein34. We observed a significant fraction of PRMT5 resistant to ProK-treatment. Furthermore, upon addition of Triton X-100 to disrupt the mitochondrial membrane, PRMT5 in the mitochondrial fraction was ProK-sensitive (Supplementary Fig. 1F, compare lanes 2 and 3), suggesting the presence of PRMT5 within the mitochondria. To precisely determine PRMT5’s mitochondrial localization, we conducted a ProK protection assay under iso-osmotic and hypo-osmotic conditions using isolated mitochondria (Fig. 1G). Under iso-osmotic conditions, ProK degraded the outer membrane (OM) protein TOM20, while PRMT5 remained intact, confirming it is not OM-associated (Fig. 1G, lanes 1 and 2). Hypo-osmotic swelling further disrupted the OM, yet PRMT5 remained resistant to ProK digestion (Fig. 1G, lanes 5 and 6), mirroring the matrix protein LonP1. These results indicate that PRMT5 is localized within the mitoplast, protected by the inner membrane (IM). To further validate PRMT5’s mitochondrial matrix localization, we isolated mitoplasts by treating ultra-purified mitochondria with digitonin, which selectively disrupts the cholesterol-rich outer membrane (OM) while preserving the inner membrane (IM). Following ProK/digitonin treatment, TOM20 was completely degraded, whereas the IM protein COXIV showed partial resistance to ProK but was fully degraded upon digitonin treatment. Notably, PRMT5, like the matrix protein LONP1, remained entirely resistant to ProK/digitonin digestion, reinforcing its localization within the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 1H).

Finally, we performed an in vitro mitochondrial import assay using recombinant His-PRMT5 and ultra-purified mitochondria in the presence of ProK and the mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) (Fig. 1I). The results revealed that His-PRMT5 was transported to a protease-protected compartment, and its import was strictly dependent on membrane potential (Fig. 1I), a defining characteristic of proteins localized to the inner membrane (IM) and mitochondrial matrix35.

Further, the presence of PRMT5 was established in the tissue lysates obtained from mitochondria isolated from the heart, brain, and liver tissue of mice, which have an abundance of mitochondria (Fig. 1J). The presence of PRMT5 in the mitochondria was confirmed by the symmetric dimethylation of arginine residues of mitochondrial proteins detected in PRMT5 proficient mitochondria extract (Fig. 1K), which was markedly abrogated in PRMT5-/- cells. Notably, complementation with the catalytic dead mutant PRMT5R368A in PRMT5 -/- (PRMT5-/-/R368A) cells failed to restore SDMA levels, whereas PRMT5WT effectively rescued the methylation in PRMT5-/-/WT cells (Fig. 1K). Together, these results reveal the first evidence for the presence of a catalytically active PRMT5 enzyme in the mitochondria.

PRMT5 knockout cells are more susceptible to mitochondrial DNA damage

Mitochondrial nucleoids play a crucial role in protecting mtDNA from intrinsic or extrinsic damage. To determine if nucleoid counts were altered by the loss of PRMT5, we used PicoGreen staining and further compared in PRMT5+/+, PRMT5-/-, PRMT5-/-/WT cells using live cell microscopy. We observed a ~4-fold reduction in nucleoid count and enlarged nucleoids in PRMT5-/- cells within elongated mitochondrial networks (Fig. 2A, B; untreated, and Supplementary Fig. 2A). This phenotype was rescued in PRMT5-/-/WT and PRMT5+/+ cells under comparable PRMT5 expression without exogenous DNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To rule out PicoGreen binding bias due to altered mtDNA supercoiling, we quantified nucleoids in fixed cells using an anti-dsDNA antibody, which yielded similar results (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D).

Given PRMT5’s involvement in nuclear DNA damage response6,7, we explored its role in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage and repair. MIL-Iri, which induces mtDNA damage by trapping mitochondrial Topoisomerase 1-mtDNA covalent complexes (Top1mtcc)36,37, showed that PRMT5-/- cells had a ~ 3-fold increase in susceptibility to mtDNA damage compared to PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 2A, B; panel MIL-Iri treated).

The reduction in nucleoid count prompted us to assess the mtDNA copy number using qPCR in PRMT5+/+, PRMT5-/-, PRMT5-/-/WT and PRMT5-/-/R368A cells. Our results reveal statistically significant rescue of mtDNA copy number in PRMT5-/-/WT cells, whereas no such rescue was observed in PRMT5-/-/R368A cells, demonstrating that PRMT5’s catalytic activity is essential for mtDNA maintenance (Fig. 2C).

MtDNA replication and transcription is a coupled process38,39. As a result, we assessed the mRNA expression of genes transcribed by mtDNA. PRMT5-/- cells showed a significant decrease in mRNA expression of mtDNA-encoded OXPHOS subunits (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Next, we measured the extent of mtDNA damage and repair in the presence of mtDNA-damaging agents (MIL-Iri and H2O2) using a gene-specific long-range quantitative PCR assay, which is an established method34,36,37. We found that PRMT5-/- cells exhibited rapid mtDNA depletion (~2-fold) and slower repair kinetics (~1.5-fold) compared to PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 2D, E and Supplementary Fig. 2F, G) upon inducing mtDNA damage. This suggests PRMT5 protects mtDNA from damage and aids in its repair. Cell survival assays revealed increased sensitivity to mtDNA-damaging agents (MIL-Iri, H2O2, and menadione) and respiratory chain inhibitors (rotenone, antimycin, and oligomycin) in PRMT5-/- cells compared to PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 2H–L). This increase in cytotoxicity in PRMT5-/- cells was consistent across different types of mitochondria-targeted damage. We performed PI/Annexin V flow cytometry to more accurately assess cell viability and apoptosis with MIL-Iri, independent of mitochondrial enzyme activity. MIL-Iri treatment induced significantly higher apoptosis in PRMT5-/- cells, which was rescued by PRMT5WT complementation, consistent with the survival assay (Supplementary Fig. 2M–P). Finally, we assessed mitochondrial health using the Seahorse XF24 flux analyzer. PRMT5-/- cells showed reduced respiration, further diminished by MIL-Iri treatment compared with PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 2G). ATP-linked, basal, maximal, and spare respiratory capacities were significantly reduced in PRMT5-/- cells and further decreased with mtDNA damage compared with PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 2H). Mitochondrial respiration assays revealed that only wild-type PRMT5 restored respiration in PRMT5-/-/WT cells, while the catalytically inactive R368A mutant (PRMT5-/-/R368A) failed, underscoring the essential role of PRMT5 enzymatic activity (Fig. 2I, J). These findings demonstrate that PRMT5 plays a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial nucleoid numbers and protects mtDNA from extrinsic damage.

PRMT5 knockout cells produce hyperfused mitochondria

To investigate the role of PRMT5 in regulating mitochondrial plasticity, we measured mitochondrial length in PRMT5-/- cells and compared them with cells expressing PRMT5 variants (PRMT5+/+ or PRMT5-/-/WT) using MitoTracker Red (Fig. 3A). Mitochondrial length was categorized into three groups: hyperfused mitochondria (>5 µm), moderate length (2–5 µm), and fragmented mitochondria (<2 µm) as done previously25. Notably, in the absence of mtDNA damage, PRMT5-/- cells exhibited a significant increase (~ 2-fold) in hyperfused mitochondrial networks and a concurrent ~1.5-fold decrease in fragmented mitochondria, which could be rescued in PRMT5-/-/WT or wild-type PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Accordingly, we observed increased expression levels of Mfn1 and Mfn2 in PRMT5-/- cells (Fig. 3C). However, this increase was reversed in PRMT5-/-/WT and in PRMT5+/+ cells (Fig. 3D).

Next, we measured mitochondrial mass by staining cells with MitoTracker Green dye and performing flow cytometry analysis. Indeed, we detected a marked increase in mitochondrial volume (Supplementary Fig. 3B) consistent with hyperfused mitochondria (Fig. 3A, B), suggesting that PRMT5-/- cells employ an alternative mechanism to regulate mitochondrial dynamics. This supports the concept that PRMT5 protects mitochondrial structure and dynamics and is closely linked to proper nucleoid distribution and the removal of intrinsically damaged mtDNA, consistent with previous studies25.

Next, to explore PRMT5’s role in mitochondrial network dynamics, particularly in fission and fusion rates, we utilized live-cell microscopy and the Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) technique37. Cells were transfected with mitochondrial matrix-localized Mito-YFP (yellow fluorescent protein) and the fission/fusion rate of mitochondria was analyzed by measuring the rate of redistribution of Mito-YFP, ectopically expressed in PRMT5+/+, PRMT5-/-, or PRMT5-/-/WT cells (Fig.3E). In the absence of MIL-Iri, PRMT5+/+ cells showed moderate FRAP recovery, reaching a maximum intensity of ~71% at 60 s (Fig. 3E, F), indicating that, under steady-state conditions, there is a balance between the diffusion of Mito-YFP due to the existence of a moderately interconnected mitochondrial network (Fig. 3A, B). Under similar conditions, the PRMT5-/- cells exhibited a 10% increase in FRAP recovery compared to the PRMT5+/+ cells, with a maximum intensity of ~85–90% at 60 s (Fig. 3E, F), indicating elevated levels of the close mitochondrial network due to the presence of hyperfused mitochondria (Fig. 3A, B). Intriguingly, complementation of FLAG-tagged human PRMT5 (PRMT5-/-/WT) restored the fission-fusion cycle to levels similar to PRMT5+/+ cells (maximum intensity of ~75% at 60 s) (Fig. 3F), indicating PRMT5 knockout triggers hyperfused mitochondrial network (Fig. 3B). To further assess PRMT5’s role in mitochondrial fission-fusion dynamics, we performed a FRAP assay in the presence of the DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-140. In the presence of Mdivi-1, PRMT5+/+ cells showed rapid FRAP recovery, reaching ~95% fluorescence intensity at 60 seconds, consistent with a hyperfused mitochondrial network (Supplementary Fig. 3C, D). Under the same conditions, PRMT5-/- cells exhibited similar recovery (~85–90%), indicating a comparably interconnected mitochondrial network due to impaired fission.

Next, in the presence of mtDNA damage with MIL-Iri, we detected that the hyperfused mitochondria in PRMT5-/- cells (Fig.3A) were more prone to fragmentation compared to PRMT5-proficient cells (PRMT5+/+ and PRMT5-/-/WT) (Fig. 3G, H). Accordingly, Mil-Iri treatment markedly delayed (15–10%) the FRAP recovery of YFP-mito in PRMT5-/- cells compared to PRMT5-proficient cells (PRMT5+/+ and PRMT5-/-/WT) (Fig. 3I-J), suggesting the existence of a dispersed mitochondrial network due to increased mitochondrial fission in PRMT5-/- cells. Collectively, these data indicate that PRMT5 plays a crucial role in maintaining the fission-fusion dynamics of mitochondria (Fig. 3K), which is essential for proper nucleoid distribution within cells.

PRMT5 catalyzes TFAM dimethylation at R82

To investigate the significance of PRMT5 localization in mitochondria, we co-immunoprecipitated FLAG-PRMT5 ectopically expressed in HEK293 cells and conducted an unbiased proteomics study using tandem mass spectrometry (MS). MS and PSM (Peptide Spectrum Match) analysis identified TFAM and other mitochondrial matrix-associated proteins (PHB1, TOP1mt, and LONP1) interacting with FLAG-PRMT5 (Fig. 4A). Gene enrichment confirmed a strong mitochondrial association, with TFAM ( ~ 19 unique peptides) emerging as a top hit (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B).

To examine the mass spectrometry results, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) of ectopic FLAG-PRMT5 in HEK293 cells that pulled down endogenous TFAM, confirming the association of PRMT5 with TFAM (Fig. 4B). Reverse co-IP in cells expressing Clover-TFAM further confirmed the presence of PRMT5 in the TFAM complex (Fig. 4C). We further validated PRMT5 interactions with endogenous mitochondrial matrix proteins—including TOP1mt, LONP1, PHB1, COX4, and TOM20, supporting the mass spectrometry findings (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

Next, we performed immunostaining in MCF7 cells expressing ectopic FLAG-PRMT5 with anti-FLAG antibodies and co-stained with anti-TFAM antibodies to detect its colocalization with endogenous TFAM. Indeed, Fig. 4D, E shows a significant colocalization of PRMT5 with TFAM, which is in keeping with PRMT5-TFAM binding in co-IP experiments (Fig.4B, C). To determine if the PRMT5-TFAM association occurs within the mitochondria, we extracted mitochondria from HEK293 cells and performed a co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) of endogenous TFAM with an anti-TFAM antibody (Fig.4F). Figure 4G detected PRMT5 in the TFAM-immune complex in the mitochondrial fraction, supporting the plausibility that PRMT5 binds with TFAM in the mitochondria.

Because PRMT5 binds to TFAM, we examined TFAM arginine methylation using MS analysis. MS analysis of the FLAG-PRMT5 immunoprecipitation complex identified TFAM-R82 as a potential dimethylated arginine residue (Supplementary Fig. 4B). The R82 residue of TFAM is highly conserved among species and is located within the conserved DNA-binding HMG Box A domain, adjacent to the mtDNA (Fig.4H)21.

Prompted by the MS findings, we immunoprecipitated ectopic Clover-TFAM with an anti-GFP antibody from HEK293 cells, and probed with antibodies that recognize symmetrically dimethylated arginine residues (anti-SDMA)6. Fig. 4I shows that Clover-TFAMWT reacts with the SDMA-specific antibody and recognizes bands (*band indicated the precursor form of TFAM) with a similar molecular weight as that of Clover-TFAM, suggesting the possibility of TFAM arginine methylation. To validate R82 as an arginine methylation site on TFAM, we immunoprecipitated the ectopically expressed methylation mutant Clover-TFAMR82K from HEK293 cells, probed with an SDMA-specific antibody. Fig. 4I shows a marked reduction (~80-90%) in the methylation signal in R82K mutant TFAM, confirming that R82 is the primary site of TFAM methylation in cells.

To establish whether PRMT5 is responsible for TFAM arginine methylation, we immunoprecipitated ectopically expressed Clover-TFAMWT in PRMT5 +/+ and PRMT5-/- cells with an anti-GFP antibody and probed with an anti-SDMA antibody. Fig. 4J shows that PRMT5 knockout resulted in a marked decrease (80%) in arginine-methylated TFAM, showing that TFAM not only physically interacts (see Fig. 4B–E) but is also arginine-methylated in vivo by PRMT5. To explore TFAM-methylation in mitochondria, we performed PLA assays using SDMA- and TFAM-specific antibodies with MitoTracker Green staining. TFAM-SDMA PLA signals primarily colocalized with mitochondria, confirming that symmetrically dimethylated TFAM is present predominantly within this compartment. (Supplementary Fig. 4E–G).

Finally, to obtain direct evidence for TFAMR82 methylation by PRMT5, we conducted in vitro methylation assays using recombinant His-tagged TFAM variants (His-TFAMWT and His-TFAMR82K) as substrates and immunoprecipitated PRMT5 in the presence of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). Methylation of TFAMWT by PRMT5 was abrogated in the TFAMR82K mutant (Fig. 4K), demonstrating that R82 is the major residue for PRMT5-mediated TFAM arginine methylation (Fig. 4L). Symmetric arginine dimethylation is primarily catalyzed by PRMT5, with PRMT9 also proposed for catalysis of SDMA41.To test PRMT5’s specificity for TFAM methylation, we performed in vitro methylation assays using immunoprecipitated PRMT9 (Supplementary Fig. 4H). TFAM was methylated by PRMT5, but not by PRMT9, confirming PRMT5 as the specific methyltransferase (Supplementary Fig. 4I). Because TFAMR82 as well as PRMT5 are conserved along the phylogeny, we conclude that R82 of TFAM is the plausible cellular target for PRMT5 across species.

R82-methylation of TFAM shields mtDNA from exogenous damage

To assess the functional impact of R82 methylation of TFAM, we generated a TFAM knockout isogenic clone in the MCF7 cell line (TFAM-/-) using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. The knockout efficiency was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). Because TFAM preserves and packages the mitochondrial genome14,20,21, next, we used TFAM-/- cells to complement with human Clover-tagged wild-type TFAM (TFAM-/-/WT) or a methylation mutant R82K-TFAM (TFAM-/-/R82K), which was also confirmed by Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Live cell microscopy with DNA-specific PicoGreen dye shows that TFAM-/- cells have a marked reduction in mitochondrial nucleoid count (~7 fold) as compared to TFAM+/+ cells (Fig.5A; quantification in Fig. 5B), which is in keeping with previous reports25. Under similar conditions, TFAM methylation mutant (TFAM-/-/R82K) cells were defective to rescue the nucleoid count by ~2–3 fold as compared to TFAM+/+ cells or TFAM-/-/WT cells (Fig. 5B), suggesting the role of TFAM-R82 methylation in the maintenance of mitochondrial nucleoids akin to PRMT5-/- cells (Fig. 2A, B). Because TFAM plays a critical role in the replication of mtDNA19. Therefore, we hypothesized that TFAM-/- cells or TFAM-/-/R82K would be more susceptible to the mtDNA damage associated with MIL-Iri-induced Top1mtcc (Fig. 5C). Upon treatment with MIL-Iri, we observed that TFAM-/- cells show a lower mt-nucleoid count (~3 fold) as compared to the wild-type cells which is consistent with TFAM’s protective role (Fig. 5C; quantification in Fig. 5D). However, the mt-nucleoids in TFAM-/-/R82K cells exhibited higher susceptibility (~2 fold) to mtDNA damage as compared to TFAM-/-/WT cells. (Fig. 5C; quantification in Fig. 5D). Accordingly, we detected a marked reduction in mitochondrial copy number in TFAM-/- cells or TFAM-/-/R82K compared to its wild-type counterpart (Fig. 5E).

To further investigate the role of TFAM-R82 methylation in regulating mitochondrial dynamics, we measured the mitochondrial length in TFAM-/- cells complemented with the R82K methylation mutant (TFAM-/-/R82K) and compared it to the wild-type counterpart (TFAM-/-/WT) (Fig. 5A; quantification in Fig. 5F; left panel). TFAM knockout cells showed a significant increase in hyperfused mitochondria (~3 fold) as compared to TFAM proficient cells (TFAM+/+ and TFAM-/-/WT), which could not be rescued by R82K mutant (Fig. 5A; quantification in Fig. 5F; left panel). Accordingly, we detected an increase in mitochondrial volume in R82K methylation mutant (TFAM-/-/R82K), which were reversed in TFAM-proficient (TFAM+/+ and TFAM-/-/WT) cells (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Next, following mtDNA damage with MIL-Iri, we observed that the hyperfused mitochondria in TFAM-/- and TFAM-/-/R82K cells (Fig. 5C) were more prone to fragmentation compared to those in TFAM-proficient cells (TFAM+/+ and TFAM-/-/WT) (Fig. 5C; quantification in Fig. 5F; right panel). This implies that TFAM-R82 methylation is essential for proper nucleoid maintenance and distribution.

TFAM is a transcription factor that activates transcription by binding to the two main promoters of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): the light strand promoter (LSP) and the heavy strand promoter 1 (HSP1)21. To investigate the role of R82 methylation, we measured the transcript levels in cells expressing different TFAM variants (TFAM+/+, TFAM-/-, TFAM-/-/WT, and the methylation mutant TFAM-/-/R82K). The TFAM-/-/R82K cells showed a significant decrease in mRNA expression of OXPHOS subunits encoded by polycistronic heavy- and light-chain transcripts of mtDNA, including 12srRNA, ND1, ND2, ND6, CYTB, COX2 (Fig. 5G). This suggests that PRMT5-mediated TFAM-R82 methylation not only protects mtDNA nucleoids but is also crucial for mtDNA transcription.

R82-methylation of TFAM increases mtDNA binding

TFAM-mtDNA binding is critical for mtDNA compaction and transcriptional activation14,20. In its role in mtDNA compaction, TFAM is believed to interact with mtDNA globally14,20,21. Circular dichroism spectroscopy comparing WT and R82K TFAM showed no structural changes, indicating that the R82 mutation does not alter TFAM function independently of PRMT5-mediated dimethylation (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Next, to assess the binding abilities of TFAM-R82 methylation mutations to mtDNA, we performed both in vivo and in vitro mtDNA binding assays. First, we used chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) with an anti-TFAM antibody, followed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis targeting the D-loop site containing HSP1 and NCR (non-coding regions) and coding regions containing ND4, CYTB and COX1 (Fig. 6A–C). As shown in Fig. 6B, the enrichment of endogenous TFAM on the HSP1 sequence of mtDNA was significantly reduced (~7 fold) in PRMT5-/- cells compared to PRMT5+/+ cells. Accordingly, TFAM defective in R82 methylation (TFAMR82K) exhibited a marked reduction in mtDNA enrichment (~3 fold) to the HSP1 sequence compared to the TFAMWT counterpart (Fig. 6C) in ChIP assays. These findings were further confirmed by measuring the intrinsic fluorescence of TFAM-R82K mutant in the presence of increasing concentrations of a double-stranded Light Strand Promoter (LSP) specific oligo (Supplementary Fig. 6A). The R82-methylated His-TFAMWT exhibited increased DNA binding activity compared to its non-methylated counterpart or methylated His-TFAMR82K (Supplementary Fig. 6B–G). The defective DNA binding ability in the HSP and LSP regions may attribute to the marked reduction in transcriptional activation of OXPHOS subunits encoded by polycistronic heavy- and light-chain promoters of mtDNA in TFAM-/-/R82K cells (Fig. 5G).

Next, we examined the effect of R82 methylation on TFAM’s global DNA binding activity using linearized plasmid DNA and recombinant His-tagged TFAM variants42. We conducted in vitro methylation of recombinant TFAM with immunoprecipitated PRMT5 in the presence of SAM, followed by DNA binding assays (Fig. 6D). Figures 6E and F demonstrate that, with increasing concentrations of recombinant TFAM, the percentage of band retardation is higher (~2.5 fold) for the R82-methylated His-TFAM (Rmet TFAM) compared to the non-methylated His-TFAM. Furthermore, the DNA binding activity of methylated His-TFAMR82K was significantly reduced (~2 fold) compared to the R82-methylated wild-type TFAM (Rmet His-TFAMWT) at higher DNA concentrations (Fig. 6G, H), which may facilitate mtDNA compaction and protection from mtDNA damage. Together, we provide evidence that R82-methylation of TFAM promotes its DNA binding ability (Fig. 6I).

Loss of R82-methylation in TFAM leads to LonP1-mediated degradation and induces mtDNA Damage

The mtDNA-unbound form of TFAM is more sensitive to proteolytic degradation by Lon protease 1 (LONP1)32,33 and arginine methylation of downstream target proteins such as KLF4, 53BP1, and TDP1 protects it from degradation4,6,7. Therefore, we tested endogenous TFAM half-life in cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments using PRMT5+/+ and PRMT5-/- cells. In the absence of PRMT5, the half-life of endogenous TFAM is significantly decreased (Fig. 7A, B), which is consistent with the increase of mtDNA-unbound TFAM (Fig. 6B). Considering LONP1’s direct involvement in TFAM degradation32, we investigated its role in regulating TFAM half-life in PRMT5 knockout cells using a specific LONP1 protease inhibitor, CDDO-Me (Fig. 7C, D). The relative extent of TFAM accumulation in PRMT5-/- was markedly increased upon CDDO-Me treatment (Fig. 7C, D) similar to PRMT5+/+ wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B) suggesting PRMT5-mediated arginine methylation not only increases mtDNA binding abilities but also protects TFAM from LONP1-mediated degradation.

To further explore the impact of TFAM arginine dimethylation on its stability, we assessed the half-lives of exogenously expressed Clover-tagged TFAMWT and a Clover-tagged arginine methylation mutant TFAMR82K in the CHX chase experiments in the presence and absence of CDDO-Me. Figures 7E and F show that in the absence of CDDO-Me, TFAMWT exhibits an increased half-life compared to TFAMR82K. However, CDDO-Me treatment restored the half-life of TFAMR82K (Fig. 7G, H), similar to the wild-type (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D), suggesting that the R82-methylation not only enhances the binding capacity of TFAM to mtDNA but also protects TFAM from LONP1-degradation.

To investigate the biological significance of TFAM-R82 methylation, we measured the extent of mtDNA damage and repair in cells expressing TFAM variants using gene-specific long-range quantitative PCR assays34,36,37. Interestingly, both the methylation-defective TFAM-/-/R82K cells and the TFAM-/- cells exhibited rapid mtDNA damage (~1.5 fold) and slower recovery (~1.2 fold) as compared to their wild-type counterparts (TFAM+/+ or TFAM-/-/WT) (Fig. 7I, J). Next, to assess endogenous mtDNA breaks in PRMT5-/- or TFAM-/- mitochondria we directly measured in vivo trapping of Top1mtcc in isolated mitochondria using ICE (immunocomplex of enzyme) assays using antibodies that detects Top1mt (Supplementary Fig. 7E–H). Topoisomerase 1 cleavage complex (Top1cc) intermediates are trapped by endogenous DNA lesions, including abasic sites, mismatches, oxidized bases, nicks, and other endogenous DNA adducts37,43,44. We observed increased accumulation of Top1mtcc in mitochondria from PRMT5-/- cells under basal conditions compared to wild-type, indicating elevated endogenous mtDNA breaks in the absence of PRMT5 (Supplementary Fig. 7E, F). Notably, Top1mtcc trapping was further exacerbated by MIL-Iri, a Top1mtcc poison that induces mtDNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 7G–H). Similarly, TFAM-/- and methylation-defective TFAM-/-/R82K cells exhibited heightened Top1mtcc trapping both at baseline and following DNA damage, whereas TFAM+/+ and TFAM-/-/WT cells showed significantly reduced mtDNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 7E–H).

Finally, mitochondrial health was assessed using the Seahorse XF24 flux analyzer. TFAM-/- cells showed reduced respiration, further diminished by MIL-Iri treatment as compared to TFAM+/+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 7I). Both basal and maximal respiratory capacities were significantly lower in TFAM-/- cells, with additional reductions following mtDNA damage (Supplementary Fig. 7J). The methylation mutant TFAM-/-/R82K cells demonstrated a ~2-fold reduction in oxygen consumption rate, both in the absence and presence of MIL-Iri, compared to TFAM-/-/WT cells (Fig. 7K and Supplementary Fig. 7K). Additionally, TFAM-/-/R82K and TFAM-/- cells were more sensitive to MIL-Iri and H2O2 in survival assays, indicating that TFAM-R82 methylation is crucial for cell survival under DNA damage with Top1mtcc and oxidative stress (Fig. 7L and Supplementary Fig. 7L). This sensitivity aligns with the observed decline in oxygen consumption rate in TFAM-/-/R82K cells (Fig. 7K and Supplementary Fig. 7L). Annexin V/PI staining showed that MIL-Iri treatment markedly increased early and late apoptosis in TFAM-/-/R82K cells (Supplementary Fig. 7M–P). Clonogenic assays revealed that TFAM knockout heightened MIL-Iri sensitivity, which was rescued by TFAMWT, wild-type but not by TFAM R82K, indicating that methylation-deficient TFAM fails to restore survival (Supplementary Fig. 7Q, R).Together, our results indicate that deficiencies in TFAM or PRMT5 cause comparable mitochondrial dysfunctions, including reduced mtDNA content, impaired repair kinetics, and compromised respiration in response to mtDNA damage (Fig. 8). This establishes a functional link between PRMT5 and TFAM through R82 methylation.

Discussion

The present study provides unique evidence for the presence of PRMT5 in the mitochondria, which is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function and nucleoid integrity. PRMT5-knockout (PRMT5-/-) cells show reduced nucleoid counts and mtDNA copy numbers, disrupting mtDNA homeostasis. PRMT5-/- cells exhibit hyperfused mitochondria, affecting the fission-fusion balance necessary for proper nucleoid distribution. Consequently, PRMT5-/- cells are more susceptible to mtDNA-damaging agents, such as Top1mt poisons, hydrogen peroxide, ETC inhibitors, that display impaired oxidative phosphorylation and reduced respiratory capacity, leading to cell death. We identify TFAM as a previously unreported interacting partner of PRMT5. PRMT5 catalyzes the symmetric dimethylation of TFAM at residue R82, which promotes mtDNA binding and protects mtDNA from damage. Defective R82-methylation in TFAM or PRMT5-/- cells results in decreased mitochondrial transcripts due to impaired enrichment of TFAM at mtDNA promoters. R82-dimethylation stabilizes TFAM in the mitochondria, protecting it from LONP1-mediated degradation. This study establishes PRMT5 as a critical regulator of mitochondrial stress responses.

PRMT5 is a symmetric arginine di-methylating enzyme residing predominantly in the cytoplasm8,45 and involved in multiple cellular functions, including DNA repair pathways6,10,46. Its genetic inactivation in mice leads to early embryonic lethality47, and its depletion impairs cell proliferation6,7. In humans, PRMT5 regulates cellular proliferation by adding SDMA marks to core histones H3 and H4. PRMT5 also targets non-histone proteins, including p53, E2F1, and DNA repair proteins such as FEN1, RAD9, TDP1, 53BP1, and RUVBL1, which protect the nuclear genome4,5,8,10,13. We show that catalytically active PRMT5 is localized within mitochondria matrix (Fig.1) and provide evidence that TFAM undergoes arginine methylation, establishing it as a bona fide PRMT5 substrate (Fig.4).

PRMT5 localization in mitochondria is consistent with reports that SAM-dependent methyltransferases regulate mitochondrial bioenergetics48,49. DNMT3a and DNMT3b48,50 mediate de novo mtDNA methylation to protect against oxidative stress, while NDUFAF7 dimethylates NDUFS2 to stabilize complex I51. We show PRMT5 can enter mitochondria without a canonical targeting sequence52,53, pointing to the existence of still unknown mechanisms of intracellular trafficking (Fig. 1). We performed extensive validation, including super-resolution microscopy (Fig. 1A–D), mitoplast protection assays (Fig. 1G–H), and mitochondrial import assays (Fig. 1I), confirming PRMT5’s membrane potential-dependent mitochondrial importation.

We demonstrate that PRMT5 knockout disrupts the balance of the mitochondrial fission-fusion cycle, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional, hyperfused mitochondria devoid of mtDNA, as evidenced by increased mitochondrial mass and reduced nucleoid count (Figs. 2 and Fig.3). This disruption impairs mitochondrial plasticity and nucleoid trafficking, essential for responding to cellular stress and metabolic demands (Figs. 3G and 7K). This phenotype aligns with the effects of DRP1 inhibition, which similarly leads to the formation of enlarged, clustered nucleoids within a hyperfused mitochondrial network27,54,55. PRMT5 might regulate mitochondrial plasticity through direct interaction and post-translational modification of fission or fusion regulators because complementation of wild-type PRMT5 successfully rescues the structural dynamics defects caused by PRMT5 loss, underscoring the essential role of PRMT5 in mitochondrial function. DRP1 inhibitor (Mdivi-1) treatment induced comparable hyperfused mitochondrial networks in both PRMT5+/+ and PRMT5-/- cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C–D), suggesting an additional role of PRMT5 in the regulation of the mitochondrial fission pathway. Our findings link PRMT5 to mtDNA and mitochondrial dynamics, highlighting its role in mitochondrial homeostasis.

The mechanistic role of PRMT5 in mitochondrial function is closely tied to its interaction with TFAM, a core nucleoid-associated protein critical for mtDNA organization and protection15,19,56. TFAM, an HMG architectural protein, binds mtDNA approximately every 16 base pairs and is regulated by its nuclear-encoded importation into mitochondria, where it can be degraded by LONP1 when unbound from mtDNA32,33. PRMT5 catalyzes symmetric dimethylation (SDMA) of TFAM at the conserved R82 residue within the HMG box domain A, a site crucial for its DNA binding56 (Fig. 4).

Our study reveals that TFAM deficient in R82 methylation cannot fully restore mtDNA and mitochondrial transcript levels in TFAM-/- cells (Fig.5), suggesting impaired TFAM-mtDNA binding and nucleoid packaging, transcription, and replication20,57. Chromatin immunoprecipitation with R82K-mutant TFAM demonstrates deficient binding to promoter sites, leading to reduced mitochondrial transcription and oxidative phosphorylation, mtDNA copy number, and heightened vulnerability to mtDNA-damaging agents (Figs. 5–7).

TFAM’s intracellular levels are finely balanced by transcription, regulation, and degradation, with phosphorylation and acetylation facilitating its dissociation from mtDNA31. We established that TFAM deficient in R82 methylation remains mtDNA unbound and is subsequently degraded by LONP1 (Fig. 7), resulting in mitochondrial genome decompaction14,21. The packaging of mtDNA into nucleoids likely requires specific and sequential TFAM binding, a process stimulated by TFAM-R82 methylation (Fig. 6).

Our results reveal phenotypic similarities between PRMT5-/- and TFAM-/-/R82K cells. Similar to PRMT5-/- cells (Fig. 2A, B), TFAM knockout cells exhibited a ~3-fold increase in hyperfused mitochondria compared to PRMT5/TFAM-proficient cells, a phenotype not rescued by the R82K mutant (Fig. 5A, F). Consistently, mitochondrial volume was elevated in TFAM-/-/R82K cells but restored to normal levels in PRMT5+/+; TFAM+/+ wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Upon mtDNA damage with MIL-Iri, hyperfused mitochondria in TFAM-/- and TFAM-/-/R82K cells were significantly more prone to fragmentation than in PRMT5+/+; TFAM+/+ cells (Fig. 5C and F). Our data support PRMT5’s role in maintaining mtDNA integrity, as PRMT5 knockout cells exhibit reduced TFAM binding and mtDNA instability (Figs. 2 and6). Notably, TFAM-/- cells expressing TFAM R82K show significant mtDNA depletion (~2-fold) and an impaired ability to recover mtDNA, even after 12 h in a drug-free medium. These findings indicate that TFAM-R82 methylation is essential for nucleoid maintenance and distribution. Collectively, the shared mitochondrial defects in PRMT5-/- and TFAM-/-/R82K cells underscore the critical role of PRMT5-mediated TFAM-R82 methylation in safeguarding mtDNA nucleoids.

Inhibiting mitochondrial DNA repair offers a promising anticancer strategy, as mitochondrial metabolism is central to tumorigenesis. PRMT5 inhibitors such as GSK3326595, AZD3470, and AMG193 are under clinical evaluation as potential anticancer agents across multiple cancers58,59. Our study suggests that combining PRMT5 inhibitors with the mitochondria-targeted Top1 inhibitor (MIL-Iri) may exert synergistic anticancer effects by amplifying mitochondrial dysfunction and promoting tumor cell death. TFAM’s role in neuroprotection is suggested, and PRMT5 downregulation is linked to neurological disorders like Alzheimer’s and Huntington’s diseases30,60. Together, our findings provide mechanistic insights into PRMT5-mediated regulation of TFAM via R82 symmetric dimethylation (Fig. 8), with potential implications for understanding diseases such as neurodegeneration.

Methods

Reagents, antibodies and oligonucleotides

A tabulated list of reagents, antibodies, and oligonucleotides used in this study is available in Supplementary Table 1.

Treatment and transfections

Cells were exposed to different concentrations of MIL-Iri for the designated durations as mentioned in the figure legends. For cycloheximide (CHX) experiments, cells at 60–70% confluence were cultured in six-well plates, and 24 h later, treated with CDDO-Me (1μM) for 24 h immediately followed by CHX at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL. For the FRAP assay, cells were treated with Mdivi-1 at a concentration of 2.5 μm, 4 h. Transfections were carried out using plasmid DNA and either Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) or Transit Transfection Reagent (Mirus), following the manufacturer’s instructions. All experiments were conducted 48 h post-transfection61–64.

Cell culture, plasmids, and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout

Cell cultures were maintained in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS obtained from Life Technologies at 5% CO2 and 37 °C temperature. The HEK293 and MCF7 cell lines were procured from ATCC. The cloning procedure for the pCMV-Tag2B-FLAG-PRMT5 construct was previously described6. Clover-tagged-TFAM and pET28A His-tagged-TFAM plasmids were purchased from Addgene.Clover-tagged-TFAMR82K and pET28A His-tagged-TFAMR82K constructs were generated using the ‘QuickChange’ protocol (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA). The His-tagged PRMT5 constructs were generated by polymerase chain reaction amplification using full-length PRMT5 (FLAG-PRMT5WT) as template and cloned in the bacterial expression vectors pET28A. All constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

The CRISPR-based PRMT5 and TFAM gene knockout was performed in MCF7 cells as described previously7,12. Briefly, the pSpCas9(BB)-2A-GFP (PX458) vector containing the PRMT5 guide RNA (5’ CCTGAATTGCGTCCCCGAAATAG 3’) targeting exon 1 and TFAM guide RNA (5’ AGGTGGCCTCGCTACCGCAAAGA 3’) targeting exon 1 was used to transfect MCF7 using lipofectamine 2000. GFP-expressing cells were sorted using a FACS Cell Sorter, and an isogenic clone was generated from a single cell. The loss of PRMT5 and TFAM protein expression in these clones was confirmed by Western blot analysis. The pYFP-Mito construct37, which was used for FRAP analysis, was a kind gift from Dr. Richard J. Youle (NIH). Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) was used for transfection purposes following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell extracts and immunoblotting

Cell lysates were prepared using a previously described method6,7,64. Briefly, the cells were trypsinized, pelleted, and lysed using a standard lysis buffer. The lysis buffer consisted of 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% NP40, and 0.5% Na-deoxycholate, supplemented with complete protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was then subjected to intermittent vortexing and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 m to collect the supernatant, which was then stored at -80 °C for further use. Gel electrophoresis was performed using 10% Tris-glycine gels, followed by standard immunoblotting procedures. For Western blot analysis, following SDS–PAGE it was transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in PBS containing 0.1% Tween for 1 h, followed by incubation with the primary antibody (1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 0.1% Tween-PBS, secondary antibodies were applied at a 1:10,000 dilution and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Detection of immunoreactivity was achieved using an ECL chemiluminescence reaction (Amersham) in the ChemiDocTM MP System (Bio-Rad, USA), and densitometric analysis of the immunoblots was conducted with ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation

For immunoprecipitation, cells were lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 300 mM NaCl, 0.4% NP40, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.5 mM dithiothreitol, along with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysate was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 20 m at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein A/G-PLUS agarose beads (Santa Cruz, CA, USA) were added to approximately 50 μL of the lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C with precleared lysate and the specified antibodies (2–5 mg/ml). Immunocomplexes were isolated by centrifugation, washed three times with the lysis buffer, and analyzed via immunoblotting.

Preparation of mitochondria

The mitochondria were isolated using a previously described protocol34. In summary, cells were scraped and pelleted, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in a buffer containing 10 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM CaCl2, and 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) for 5 m to induce osmotic shock. After that, the cells were homogenized for 1 h using a glass Dounce homogenizer, followed by the addition of a stabilizing buffer (2 M sucrose, 35 mM EDTA, and 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5). Next, the lysate was centrifuged at 750 × g for 5 m to remove nuclei (N) and cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was subjected to a second round of centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 m to isolate the mitochondria pellet, while the supernatant contained the cytoplasm (C). The mitochondria pellet (M) was then washed three times with mito buffer (250 mM sucrose, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 5 mM DTT, and 20 mM Hepes-KOH, pH 7.4) and eventually resuspended and stored in mito buffer at −80 °C for further use.

Ultrapure mitochondria were isolated as previously described65. Briefly, crude mitochondria were washed in mito buffer and further purified by centrifugation (95,000 × g, 30 m, 4 °C) on a 30% Percoll density gradient using an SW41 rotor in a Beckman Coulter Optima L-100 XP ultracentrifuge. Ultra-Clear centrifuge tubes (14 × 89 mm; Beckman Instruments) were used for separation. The purified mitochondrial fraction, appearing as a dense band near the tube bottom, was carefully collected using a Pasteur pipette. Mitochondrial pellets were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C until further use.

For the isolation of mitochondria from mouse tissue, mice were euthanized by decapitation according to approved ethical guidelines, and heart, brain, and liver tissue were promptly collected. The tissue was rinsed with ice-cold MSE-A buffer (225 mM Mannitol, 75 mM Sucrose, 1 mM EGTA, 20 mM MOPS, and 0.5 mM DTT, pH 7.5) to remove blood and extraneous tissue as described previously66. Next, the tissue was minced with scissors in a small volume of ice-cold MSE-A buffer for 5 m, using a ratio of 5 ml of MSE-A per 1 gram of tissue. The minced tissue was transferred to a glass Teflon homogenizer and homogenized further in ice-cold MSE-A. The homogenate was centrifuged at 500-600 g for 10 m at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet was designated as the whole cell extract (W). The supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 900–1200 g for 10-15 m at 4 °C. The pellet, which is the nuclear fraction (N), was saved, and the supernatant was filtered through a cheesecloth. The filtered supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15-20 m at 4 °C. The resulting pellet contained the mitochondrial fraction (M), while the supernatant held the cytoplasmic fraction (C). The mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in MSE-A containing 0.02% digitonin. Following an additional centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 10 m in MSE-A, the resulting pellet was re-suspended in MSE-B, composed of 225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 20 mM MOPS, 0.1 mM EGTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and ddH2O, adjusted to pH 7.4.

Proteinase K protection assay

Proteinase K protection assays were performed as done previously34. In brief, equal amounts of freshly isolated mitochondria from cells were incubated with 0.5 mg/ml proteinase K (Sigma) for 15 m at 25 °C, either with or without 1% Triton-X-100 (Sigma). Proteinase K activity was halted by adding 2 volumes of 20% trichloroacetic acid at 0 °C for 20 m. The resulting protein pellets were washed with ice-cold acetone, resuspended in 2× Laemmli sample buffer, and analyzed by Western blotting as described.

Mitoplast analysis

Isolated mitochondria were resuspended in either 250 mM sucrose buffer (250 mM sucrose, 20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4) or 5 mM sucrose buffer (5 mM sucrose, 20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4) and incubated on ice for 30 m as done previously67. For proteinase protection assays, aliquots were treated with 25 μg/mL proteinase K for 5 m on ice, followed by PMSF inhibition. Samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 m at 4 °C, with supernatant proteins recovered by pyrogallol red precipitation. Pellets were washed with 100 μL sucrose buffers, re-pelleted, and solubilized in urea sample buffer containing 50 mM DTT for SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis.

For digitonin-based mitoplasting, ultrapure mitochondria were treated with 0.2% digitonin (10 m, ice) to disrupt the outer membrane, followed by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 10 m at 4 °C. Mitoplasts were divided for proteinase K treatment (25 μg/mL, 37 °C, 30 min) to assess protein localization.

Live cell confocal microscopy and Immunocytochemistry

Live cell imaging was conducted following previously established protocols36,37. Briefly, cells were plated on confocal imaging dishes (Genetix) 24 hours prior to transfection. Live-cell imaging was subsequently performed using either conventional confocal microscopy (Leica SP8 system) or super-resolution imaging (Leica STELLARIS 8 FALCON) operating in Lightning mode with a 63×/1.4 NA oil-immersion objective. The white light laser lines UV (Diode 405 nm), 488 nm (Argon), 561 nm (DPSS561), or 633 nm (HeNe633) wavelengths were used to excite DAPI/ Hoechst, MitoTracker red, PicoGreen, and RFP/GFP/YFP fluorophores. The confocal dish stage is set within a chamber that maintains a temperature of 37 °C and has a 5% CO2 supply. Fiji ImageJ (NIH) was used to analyze the fluorescence pattern of transfected cells.

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted following previously established protocols6,7,37,68. In summary, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 m at room temperature. Primary antibodies targeting PRMT5, COXIV, FLAG, dsDNA, and TFAM were detected using Alexa Fluor 488-labeled anti-mouse, Alexa Fluor 568-labeled anti-mouse, Alexa Fluor 488-labeled anti-rabbit, and Alexa Fluor 568-labeled anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (all from Invitrogen). The cells were mounted in an anti-fade solution containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Invitrogen) and examined with a Leica TCS SP8/STELLARIS laser scanning confocal microscope. Images were captured and processed using Leica LAS X software and then resized in Adobe Photoshop. Colocalization was assessed by measuring fluorescence intensities for each channel in the LAS X software.

Analysis of nucleoid area and mitochondrial length

The nucleoid area and perimeter of the mitochondria were assessed using Fiji ImageJ software. After applying scale information from the LASX (Leica), the images were converted to binary format, and the area of each nucleoid or the perimeter of each mitochondrion was measured using the ‘Analyze Particles’ function in ImageJ. The nucleoid area within each mitochondrion in every cell was analysed and categorized into groups as mentioned in the figure legend according to previous work25. The perimeter of each mitochondrion within every cell was analysed and categorized into hyperfused, intermediate, and fragmented as done previously25. This was done for approximately 50 cells and plotted as a percentage of mitochondria based on different types for each cell.

Quantification of mitochondrial transcripts and mtDNA by real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the specified cells (1 × 106) using the TRIzol reagent (15596018, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions, including deoxyribonuclease (AMPD1; Sigma-Aldrich) treatment for each sample. A 1 µg aliquot of RNA was reverse-transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (4368814, Applied Biosystems)37,69. Real-time PCR was then carried out with SYBR Green PCR master mix on the ABI 7500 thermocycler (Applied Biosystems). Each reaction mixture contained 5 µl of 2× SYBR Green PCR master mix and 2 µl of cDNA, generated by reverse transcription and diluted 10-fold, in a final volume of 10 µl with primers at 25 nM. The thermocycling conditions were set at 95 °C for 5 m, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 50 s, a variable 50 °C for 50 s, and 72 °C for 60 s. Relative gene expression was calculated as the ratio of the expression level of the gene of interest to that of β-actin RNA, with the expression levels in wild-type cells defined as 100%.

For mtDNA copy number estimation, total DNA was extracted from cell samples using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, including treatments with proteinase K and RNase. To quantify mtDNA copy number, the ratio of the expression level of the gene of interest was calculated to that of β-actin DNA, with the expression levels in wild-type cells defined as 100%.

Mitochondrial fission-fusion analysis using live cell microscopy and FRAP analysis

A confocal laser-scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8) was used for the photobleaching experiments64 to assess the mitochondrial network in live cells as described previously37. Briefly, cells were transfected with mitochondrial matrix-targeted protein (pYFP-Mito) in PRMT5 proficient and deficient cells. For FRAP (Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching), photobleaching of YFP-mito at a specific ROI (region of interest) within the cell was done using a 514 nm laser for 30 ms. Thereafter, successive images were taken at intervals of 500 ms for 60 s. The fluorescence intensity of the bleached region was calculated using LasX software, and the background signal was adjusted. The fluorescence signal within the ROI was normalized to the pre-bleach signal, and the FRAP curve was obtained using the equation: ROI = (It - Ibg) / (Io - Ibg) × 100. Here, Io is the pre-bleaching intensity, It is the intensity at a time point (t), and Ibg is the background signal determined in a region outside the mitochondria.

Measurement of mitochondrial mass

Mitochondrial mass was assessed using MitoTracker Green FM (Molecular Probes) staining. Cells were trypsinized, resuspended in PBS, and incubated with 200 nM MitoTracker Green FM for 30 m at 37 °C in the dark. The stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSAria III system.

Cell survival and Apoptosis assays

Cytotoxicity assays were performed as described previously7,37,61. Briefly, PRMT5+/+, PRMT5-/-, TFAM+/+, TFAM-/-, TFAM-/-/WT, and TFAM-/-/R82K MCF7 were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells in 96-well plates (BD Biosciences, USA) on the first day. The cells were treated with the specified drugs (H2O2, MIL-Iri, rotenone, oligomycin, menadione, antimycin) after 24 h. 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was added after an additional 48hrs and colorimetric detection was done at 570 nm using a Molecular Devices Spectra Max M2 Microplate Reader. The percentage of cell survival was calculated by normalizing with the untreated control. The data presented represent the mean values ± S.D. obtained from three independent experiments. To evaluate cell apoptosis, PRMT5 and TFAM variant cell lines were plated at 5 × 105 cells/mL and were treated with vehicle control (DMSO) or MIL-Iri. Then, cells were washed and stained by Annexin V–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) and propidium iodide (PI) (BD Pharmingen). Data acquisition was conducted by flow cytometry using a BD FACSAria III system.

Clonogenic assays

TFAM +/+ and TFAM -/- cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 50% confluency. TFAM -/- cells were transfected with Clover-TFAM WT or Clover-TFAM R82K using the TransIT-X2 Dynamic Delivery System (Mirus), following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48 hours, the cells were treated with MIL-Iri at the indicated concentrations for 3 hours. Cells were then trypsinized and re-seeded at 1 × 10³ cells per well in 6-well plates. Following two weeks of incubation at 37 °C, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. The washed cells were fixed with ice-cold methanol for 10 minutes and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 1 hour. Excess stain was removed by rinsing with water, and the plates were air-dried. Colony counts were obtained using ImageJ software.

Mass spectrometry analysis of PRMT5

Immunoprecipitation of ectopic FLAG-PRMT5 complexes was performed using an anti-FLAG antibody, as detailed earlier. The immunoprecipitated samples were then subjected to tryptic digestion at 37 °C overnight. Subsequently, the digested samples were lyophilized, reconstituted, and fractionated using strong cation exchange (SCX) liquid chromatography (LC). Finally, the fractions obtained were subjected to mass spectrometry analysis, following previously described methods6,61. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE repository with dataset identifier PXD057645. Raw MS files were processed using Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and database searches were performed with MS Amanda (v2.5.0.16129) against a merged database containing the human UniProt reference proteome and MitoCarta 3.0 mitochondrial proteins70.

Quantification of mtDNA damage

MtDNA damage and repair were quantified using a PCR-based assay known as Long-range PCR, as described previously34,36,37. Briefly, indicated cell variants were seeded in a 12-well plate and treated with mtDNA-damaging agents (MIL-Iri and Hydrogen peroxide) for specific durations as described in the figure legend. Alternatively, after drug treatment, cells were grown in drug-free media for an indicated time to study the repair kinetics. Total DNA was extracted from cell samples using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions, including treatments with proteinase K and RNase. MtDNA-specific Long-range primers were used to amplify a mtDNA fragment of 9 kbp and COX-2 specific primer was used to amplify a smaller fragment of 110 base pairs for normalization. The PCR cycle was restricted to 18 cycles so that the amplification occurs within an exponential phase. The mtDNA damage was quantified by the ratio of the long-range PCR product (LR) to the short-range PCR product (SR) (LR/SR).

ICE bioassay

The isolation of Top1mt-DNA cleavage complexes was performed using an in vivo complex of enzyme (ICE) bioassay as previously described37. Briefly, cells (5 × 10⁶) expressing PRMT5 and TFAM variants were either left untreated or exposed to MIL-Iri (2.5 μm, 3 h). Mitochondria were isolated using the aforementioned protocol and lysed in 6 M guanidinium thiocyanate buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.5), 20 mM EDTA, 4% Triton X-100, 1% N-lauroylsarcosine, 1% DTT]. Lysates were precipitated with 0.4 mL ice-cold 100% ethanol (−20 °C, 5 min), followed by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min). Pellets were washed twice with ethanol, then resuspended in freshly prepared 8 mM NaOH and briefly sonicated (20% amplitude, 10–20 sec). For detection, mtDNA samples were slot-blotted onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore) using a Bio-Rad vacuum manifold. Top1mt-DNA complexes were immunodetected with anti-Top1mt antibodies, while anti-dsDNA antibodies served as loading controls. Protein-DNA adducts were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence (ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, Bio-Rad).

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Proximity ligation assays were performed using a commercial PLA kit (Olink Bioscience) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For each experimental condition, a minimum of fifty cells were analyzed to ensure statistical reliability. Cells were counterstained with Mitotracker Green.

Circular dichroism analysis

All circular dichroism data were collected using a Jasco Circular Dichroism Spectrophotometer J-1500 with a 1-mm path length cell. The protein sample of WT or R82K mutant was 10 μM in 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT. Far-UV circular dichroism spectra were collected at 25 °C, with a data averaging time of 5 s. The data were collected every 1 nm from 195 to 260 nm.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and RT-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed following previously described protocol71. Briefly, PRMT5+/+, PRMT5-/- and TFAM-/- cells (1 × 107) expressing TFAM variants; TFAMWT and TFAMR82K were fixed in formaldehyde (1.1%) at room temperature for 10 m, followed by quenching for 5 m by addition of glycine (125 mM). The cells were then washed with PBS twice, scraped, and lysed in 0.5 ml of ChIP lysis buffer (50 mM Hepes-KOH (pH 7.5), 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8), 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 1× protease inhibitor) for 30 m in ice. Sonication was done ten times for 30 s at 30% power to fragment the DNA corresponding to 200 to 300 base pairs size, followed by centrifugation at 13000 rpm for 30 m at 4 °C. The remaining supernatant was diluted in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA (pH 8), 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and 1× protease inhibitors. It was then precleared by incubation with protein-G-sepharose beads /salmon sperm DNA at 4 °C for 1 h. For each immunoprecipitation, 500 μl of the precleared chromatin was incubated with 5 µg of GFP and TFAM-specific antibodies on a revolving platform at 4 °C for 1 h. A control reaction containing an equivalent amount of Goat/Rabbit IgG was included to assess background levels. Additionally, 10% of the precleared chromatin was reserved as an input control. Following antibody incubation, 150 μl of protein-G-sepharose beads/salmon sperm DNA was added and incubated overnight under the same conditions. The next day, the beads were washed sequentially with a high-salt buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl; pH 8.1, 500 mM NaCl), a LiCl wash buffer (250 mM LiCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl; pH 8.0), and finally TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl; pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). DNA was then eluted using an elution buffer containing 1% SDS and 100 mM NaHCO₃. The elutes were incubated overnight at 65 °C in the presence of 5 M NaCl, reaching a final concentration of 200 mM, to reverse formaldehyde cross-linking. Following this, samples were digested with proteinase K (50 mg/ml) at 55 °C for 3 h and further treated with RNase (20 mg/ml) at 37 °C before undergoing DNA isolation using the phenol-chloroform method. For qPCR analysis, the input sample was diluted 50-fold. PCR reactions were performed in triplicate using an ABI 7500 thermocycler with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 m, followed by 40 cycles of amplification at 95 °C for 15 s, variable 50 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with signal acquisition at the end of each cycle. TFAM enrichment on mtDNA was quantified using the percentage input method.

In vitro methylation assays

The in vitro methylation assays were performed as previously described6. In brief, PRMT5 and PRMT9 were immunoprecipitated and incubated with either recombinant His-TFAMWT or His-TFAMR82K proteins (1 μg). The methylation reactions were conducted in a buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 4 mM DTT) containing 100 μM unlabeled S-(5’-adenosyl)-L-methionine chloride dihydrochloride (SAM) (A7007) (Sigma) for 2 h at 30 °C. The reactions were stopped by adding 2 × SDS loading buffer (Invitrogen) and boiling the samples for 5 m. The resulting methylation products were separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred onto a PVDF membrane, and analyzed by Western blotting using an anti-SDMA antibody.

Recombinant protein purification

TFAM purification was done using pET28a-TFAM expression vector (Addgene), which encompasses residues 43–246, representing the full-length TFAM protein as described previously21.PRMT5 purification was done using the pET28a-PRMT5 expression vector.BL21 (DE3) E. coli was transformed with either pET28a-TFAM or pET28a-PRMT5 plasmids, and a single colony was inoculated in 20 ml of LB media containing 50 μg/ml Kanamycin and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C. The next day, the culture was scaled up to 1 litre until the OD600 reached 1.0. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and the culture was grown overnight. Thereafter, the cells were harvested and lysed in a 20 ml resuspension/lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) by subsequently vortexing at an interval of 30 m for 4 h. The lysate was then sonicated using a probe sonicator on ice for an additional 30 m with 30% amplitude pulse for 30 s (10 s on and 20 s off) and centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 30 m at 4 °C. The supernatant was then subjected to Ni-NTA-based affinity chromatography to isolate the His-tagged TFAM and His-tagged PRMT5. The protein was eluted in an elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, pH 7.5) and then concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-15 10kDA-cut-off concentrator (Millipore). The protein was aliquoted and stored in −80 °C.

Mitochondrial Protein Import Assay

Protein import assays were performed as previously described35,72 with modifications. Purified mitochondria were resuspended in import buffer (250 mM sucrose, 10 mM MOPS-KOH [pH 7.2], 80 mM KCl, 2 mM KH₂PO₄, 5 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM methionine, 3% fatty acid-free BSA) supplemented with 2 mM ATP, 2 mM NADH, 5 mM creatine phosphate, and 100 μg/mL creatine kinase. Recombinant His-tagged PRMT5 (His-PRMT5) was incubated with mitochondria at 25 °C to initiate import. Reactions were terminated by transferring samples to ice and dissipating the mitochondrial membrane potential using 0.2 mM CCCP. Non-imported precursors were removed by proteinase K treatment (25 μg/mL, on ice), followed by protease inhibition with 1 mM PMSF. Mitochondria were re-isolated by centrifugation, washed with isolation buffer, and lysed under denaturing conditions. Imported His-PRMT5 was detected by using anti-His antibodies.

EMSA

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed as described previously42. The pBluescript SK (+) plasmid, approximately 3 kb in size, was subjected to Xho1 digestion at a single site to linearize the DNA. Linearized plasmids (125.5 ng) were then incubated with varying amounts of hTFAM (500, 1000, 1250, 1500, 1750, and 2000 ng) in a reaction mixture, which corresponds to a ratio of 1 hTFAM per 11.3, 5.6, 4.5, 3.8, 3.2, and 2.8 base pairs of plasmid DNA, respectively42. This mixture was incubated in an EMSA buffer (35 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 72 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 5 mM DTT, 5 mM spermidine, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 10 mM EDTA) at 37 °C for 30 m. After incubation, the protein-DNA complex was subjected to electrophoresis in agarose gel (1%) in the absence of ethidium bromide (EtBr). The TFAM-mtDNA binding was visualized in a ChemiDoc instrument (BioRad) after staining with EtBr. The percentage retardation was calculated as indicated in the figure legend by measuring the migration, which is equal to the distance between the lane and the DNA-protein band in each lane, and normalizing with the only plasmid DNA lane.

DNA binding measurements

The intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence of TFAM was utilized as the basis for a binding assay to study its interaction with DNA. Among the four tryptophan residues present in TFAM, three of them are located within 3.6 Å from the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The intrinsic fluorescence of only TFAM protein (25 nM) in a quenching buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT) was initially measured. Subsequently, a double-stranded oligo corresponding to the LSP (light strand promoter) region (5’ GTGTTAGTTGGGGGGTGACTGTTAAAAGTG 3’) was added in increasing concentrations. This measurement was done in a FluoroMax-3 spectrofluorimeter (Jobin–Yvon–Horiba). Excitation wavelength of 280 nm was used and emission spectra were taken in a range of 320–400 nm. The slit width of the excitation and emission of the instrument was set at 4 nm and 10 nm, respectively. Each binding measurement was carried out in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. To assess the binding affinity, the percent reduction in fluorescence (% quenching) was determined and plotted against the total concentration of DNA used in the assay.

Mitochondrial bioenergetic profiling

Cellular respiration was analyzed using the Seahorse XF 24 analyzer (Seahorse Biosciences, Agilent Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocols. In brief, 30,000 cells were seeded into assay microplates the night before the experiment. On the following day, the cells were incubated in 500 μl of assay media at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator for 1 h before the assay. The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured over time using the XF 24 analyzer after the sequential addition of various respiratory inhibitors. The respiratory inhibitors used were oligomycin (0.5 μM), FCCP (1 μM), and rotenone (0.5 μM), all obtained from Seahorse mito stress kit (Agilent). Following the assay, the media was removed, and the cells were lysed for further analysis. The data were normalized based on protein concentration, which was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad).

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were conducted independently at least two-three times. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.). The number of experiments and the quantified cells per field are detailed in the figure legends. Each experiment (n) was performed using different cell variants. Statistical significance (P values) was determined using Sidak’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons two-way ANOVA and t-test, as indicated in the figure legends. Data analysis and graphing were performed using Prism 8 (GraphPad Software) and OriginLab. No specific method was applied to pre-determine sample size, and no data were excluded from the analysis. Images were randomly selected, and data analysis was carried out in a blinded manner.
