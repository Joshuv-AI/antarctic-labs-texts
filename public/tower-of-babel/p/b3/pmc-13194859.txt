Assessment of food hygiene knowledge and practices among food handlers in Ondo West Local Government Area, Ondo State, Nigeria
Authors: Aisha Adesola Falade, Damola Olanipekun Ajisafe, Idowu Peter Adewumi, Abisola Esther Babatope, Queensly Opeyemi Adeyemi, Oluwafunbi Ajoke Fadipe, Temitope Oluwaseun Odeyemi, Adebimpe Priscilla Akinola, Lifting Oluwapamilerinayo Jeff-Agboola, Omolara Ojuolape Adeoye, Yemisi Adefunke Jeff-Agboola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFoodborne illnesses remain a major public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where food hygiene practices may be inadequate. Food handlers play a pivotal role in preventing food contamination and ensuring safe food supply. This study assessed food hygiene knowledge and practices among food handlers in Ondo West Local Government Area (LGA), Nigeria, and examined associations with socio-demographic factors and training status. A community-based cross-sectional descriptive study was conducted among 220 food handlers across six randomly selected wards in Ondo West LGA. Respondents were stratified by type of food handling (street vendors, market stalls, bakeries, catering services, and butchers/fishmongers). Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, comprising sections on socio-demographics, knowledge (12 items), practices (10 items), and training availability, scored on a 5-point Likert scale, and analysed using SPSS version 27. Knowledge and practice scores were categorized as high/low and good/poor based on the median. Associations between socio-demographics, training, and knowledge/practice levels were assessed using Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. Pearson correlation evaluated relationships between knowledge and practices, while logistic regression determined predictors of good hygiene practices. Overall, 53.6% of respondents demonstrated high food hygiene knowledge, and 79.1% reported good hygiene practices. Core practices included frequent handwashing (85%), wearing protective clothing (75.9%), supporting regular inspections (87.7%), and valuing hygiene training (90%). Knowledge was significantly associated with education (p < 0.05) and workplace (p < 0.05), but not with age, gender, or experience. Training status was not significantly associated with knowledge or practices (p > 0.05). Knowledge positively correlated with hygiene practices (r = 0.47, p < 0.001), and logistic regression showed that food handlers with high knowledge were 6.45 times more likely to maintain good hygiene (p < 0.001). Food handlers in Ondo West LGA demonstrated generally good knowledge of food hygiene and adherence to recommended hygiene practices. However, limitations remain in the availability and consistency of formal food hygiene training, and knowledge levels vary across workplace settings. Strengthening structured training programs, workplace supervision, and enforcement of hygiene standards is necessary to improve safe food handling and reduce foodborne disease risks in the community. Introduction

Foodborne diseases impact millions of people each year and place a significant financial burden on communities and healthcare systems, making food safety a crucial global public health concern1. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates2, contaminated food causes 420,000 deaths and 600 million cases of foodborne illness worldwide each year, with children under the age of five accounting for 30% of these deaths. Annually, nearly 33 million healthy years are lost worldwide owing to the intake of unsafe food2. Food handlers, who are directly involved in food preparation, service, and distribution, are critical to maintaining food safety across the supply chain, as their food hygiene practices, attitudes, and understanding have a direct impact on the safety and microbiological quality of food3.

Food safety in Nigeria remains a major public health concern due to inadequate sanitary infrastructure, limited regulatory oversight, and insufficient formal training and knowledge among food handlers4. Nigeria, Africa’s most populous country and a growing economy, faces huge challenges related to its food safety culture4. It is estimated that there are 173 million cases of diarrhea and about 33,000 deaths caused by foodborne illness in Nigeria annually, resulting in an annual treatment cost of approximately USD 1.7 billion (excluding lost productivity)5.

Food hygiene practices comprise a variety of methods designed to ensure food safety and prevent foodborne illnesses, including using uncontaminated water and fresh ingredients, maintaining personal and kitchen cleanliness, preventing cross-contamination between raw and cooked foods, cooking foods thoroughly to recommended temperatures, and storing foods under appropriate temperature conditions6. Even though these concepts are extremely important, different Nigerian restaurants continue to follow them in different ways. Previous research shows significant variations in practice levels based on a range of factors, including the type of food facility, the degree of education of food handlers, and the availability of necessary equipment and resources7.

In this study, food hygiene knowledge is defined as food handlers’ understanding of key principles related to food contamination, personal hygiene, cross-contamination, temperature control, and foodborne disease prevention8. Food hygiene practices, on the other hand, refer to the routine behaviors and actions adopted during food preparation, handling, storage, and service that directly influence food safety outcomes9. The persistent occurrence of foodborne illnesses in Nigeria suggests a disconnect between knowledge acquisition and consistent application in practice, particularly within informal food handling settings where regulatory oversight and structured training are limited10. This mismatch represents a critical public health problem that necessitates empirical investigation.

The importance of food handlers’ understanding of food hygiene cannot be over-emphasized, as poor practices that endanger food safety arise directly from a lack of knowledge. A study on Nigerian perspectives on food safety showed that 66.7% of research depended on knowledge evaluation, while just 12.6% used observations to investigate actual practices11. There is an urgent need for a study due to the gap between knowledge assessment and practice evaluation. Some factors influence food handlers’ knowledge, including formal food safety training, access to information via various media outlets, and educational attainment12.

Despite the documented burden of foodborne diseases in Nigeria10, existing studies largely emphasize self-reported knowledge of food safety with limited integration of practice-based assessments and predictive analysis. Few studies have quantitatively examined how food hygiene knowledge translates into actual hygiene practices at the local government level, particularly using multivariable approaches to identify key predictors of safe practice4,13. Moreover, evidence from Ondo State remains sparse, despite its high reliance on informal and semi-formal food vending systems. This gap limits the ability of policymakers to design targeted, context-specific interventions. The present study addresses this gap by jointly assessing food hygiene knowledge, practices, and their associated factors among food handlers in Ondo West Local Government Area.

Similar studies conducted in West Africa have revealed similar issues. For example, a survey performed in Ghana found that 51% of street food vendors had negative attitudes toward food safety and cleanliness, whereas 70.4% had adequate awareness of food safety14. Training programs can significantly improve the understanding of food hygiene among food handlers. Recognizing this necessity, the Nigerian government has developed standardized food safety training guides aimed at improving the skills of food producers, handlers, and sellers throughout the food supply chain15.

However, there are significant disparities in the accessibility and availability of this training programs at the local government level, necessitating research to determine the effectiveness of the program on actual practice. As a result, this study aims to assess the food hygiene knowledge and practices among food handlers in Ondo West Local Government Area.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a community-based cross-sectional descriptive design, which allowed for the assessment of food handlers’ food hygiene knowledge and practices at a single point in time within their routine work environments16. A community-based approach was appropriate due to the large number of informal food handlers operating outside formal health facilities (Fig. 1).

Study area

This study was conducted in Ondo West LGA, located in Ondo State, South-West geopolitical zone of Nigeria, with the headquarters of the LGA residing in the town of Ondo. The current estimated population of Ondo West LGA is put at 288,868 inhabitants, with the area mostly populated by members of the Yoruba ethnic group. The Yoruba and English languages are commonly spoken in Ondo West LGA, with the religions of Christianity and Islam widely practised in the area17.

Population

The study population comprised food handlers who lived within the Ondo West LGA, including street vendors, market stall workers, bakers, caterers and butchers/fish mongers and individuals who are currently employed as food handlers in any capacity (cooks, food vendors, kitchen staff) within the Ondo West LGA.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be eligible, participants must be food handlers aged 18 years and above, currently employed in any capacity within Ondo West LGA (e.g., street vendors, bakers, caterers, butchers), have no less than 3 months of experience in their current role, and provide informed consent. Food handlers were excluded if they declined consent, had medical conditions influencing responses (e.g., cognitive impairments), were not directly involved in food handling, or worked on a temporary or part-time basis with inconsistent food handling exposure.

Sample size and sampling techniques

A total of 240 samples was calculated using the Leslie Kish formula (n = Zα2pq/d2), which is appropriate for estimating proportions in cross-sectional studies18. The formula was applied because the study aimed to estimate the prevalence of adequate food hygiene knowledge and practices among food handlers. A prevalence of 17.1% from a previous study on foodborne pathogens was used10, with a 5% margin of error, 95% confidence interval and 10% non-response rate. However, a total of 220 respondents participated in the study, giving a 91.6% response rate. A two-stage sampling technique was employed to select 220 food handlers in Ondo West LGA. First, six out of twelve wards (the smallest political unit of government) were randomly selected. Then, within each ward, food handlers were stratified into categories—street vendors, market stall workers, bakers, caterers, and butchers/fishmongers—and randomly selected proportionally.

Study instruments and data collection

Data were collected using an interviewer-administered structured questionnaire designed in line with the study objectives and informed by existing literature on food hygiene knowledge and practices among food handlers19–21. The instrument comprised four sections.

Section A captured respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, educational level, marital status, years of experience, and place of work; Section B assessed knowledge of food hygiene using 12 statements covering personal hygiene, cross-contamination, food storage, cooking temperatures, and health risks associated with poor hygiene; Section C evaluated food hygiene practices through 10 items reflecting self-reported hygiene behaviors, workplace practices, and perceptions of inspection and training, and Section D examined the availability and effectiveness of food safety training programs, including frequency, content, institutional support, and applicability of training to daily practice.

Sections B, C, and D were measured using a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly Agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, Strongly Disagree), allowing standardized assessment of respondents’ knowledge, practices, and perceptions. The questionnaire was administered face-to-face by trained research assistants across food vending sites in Ondo West LGA, including street vending points, markets, bakeries, catering outlets, restaurants, and fast-food establishments. This approach ensured clarity of responses and accommodated varying literacy levels. The instrument was pretested among 22 food handlers in Akure LGA. Reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.850, indicating high internal consistency.

Data analysis

The data collected for this study were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. Frequency distribution tables and chart were used to describe variables. For inferential analysis, associations between variables (e.g., training status vs. knowledge and practice levels, level of knowledge vs. socio-demographic characteristics) were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-square test, where the assumptions of Chi-square were not met (e.g., expected cell counts < 5), Fisher’s exact test was applied. Binary logistic regression was examined to test for statistical significance between the variables of interest, with the level of statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Knowledge scores were computed by summing responses to the 12 knowledge items. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), giving a maximum possible score of 60. Respondents scoring ≥ the median score were classified as having “1 = high knowledge”, while those scoring below the median were classified as having “0 = low knowledge”. Practice scores were similarly computed using the 10 practice items. Respondents scoring ≥ the median practice score were categorized as having “1 = good practice”, while those scoring below were categorized as having “0 = bad practice”.

Ethical consideration

An ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC), University of Medical Sciences, Ondo, with the approval number: NHREC/TR/UNIMED-HREC-OndoSt/22/06/21. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment into the study. It was clearly explained that participation was purely voluntary with no penalty for non-participation and that participants could choose to withdraw from the study at any time. All the information collected was handled as strictly confidential22.

Results

The respondents represented a diverse workforce (Table 1). Most were aged 26–35 years (31.8%), with females constituting a slight majority (56.8%). Educational attainment was relatively high, as one-third had tertiary education (33.2%) and over one-quarter had secondary education (27.7%). Marital status was evenly distributed, with 42.3% married and 40.0% single. Over one-third (35.5%) had more than five years of work experience. Food handlers were almost evenly distributed across bakeries, street vending, market stalls, catering services, and butcher/fishmongering activities, reflecting broad representation of food handling settings within Ondo West LGA.

Discussion

The demographic characteristics of the participants show a mostly young and female food handling population, which revealed similar trends in food sectors across Nigeria23. The relatively high level of formal education contrasts with previous research that often linked low education and poor food hygiene24. While the participants exhibit high experience on the job, scholars opined that experience by itself does not ensure safe practices without ongoing training, hence regular interventions are necessary25.

The results show that the participants have significant knowledge of food hygiene, particularly in areas including handwashing, contamination, and the hazards presented by rodents and spoilt food. This corroborates the findings of Adel-Hakim et al.26, who documented high knowledge of hygiene among food handlers. Likewise, Charity et al.27 discovered great agreement on the part hygiene plays in avoiding foodborne diseases. Though general knowledge was high, lower awareness of technical aspects like correct cooking temperatures and refrigeration (72.7% and 73.2%) reflects a gap noted in previous studies, underscoring the need for constant, practical training25. Although 53.6% of food handlers were classified as having high knowledge based on the study’s scoring criteria, this did not necessarily translate into reduced foodborne disease risk. The high level of reported hygiene practices may reflect social desirability bias, leading to an overestimation of actual compliance28. Additionally, food safety outcomes in Ondo West LGA may be influenced by contextual factors such as inadequate sanitation, limited regulatory enforcement, and inconsistent monitoring9.

The high level of food hygiene practices recorded among the participants corresponds with previous research, which noted great compliance with food hygiene practices among urban food handlers29. The great confidence (79.1%) and handwashing adherence (85%) point to a good behavioural perspective, supporting previous findings on the impact of awareness on safe practices30. Self-reported data, as revealed in previous research28, could thus distort real compliance rates. The focus on sanitation and training reflects WHO’s guidelines for hygienic food preparation in food handling facilities31. Still, the 5–15% of respondents with opposing opinions draw attention to ongoing disparities, which emphasise the need for regular monitoring, focused instruction, and more stringent law enforcement30.

In line with earlier research, the handlers have a moderate level of access to food hygiene training, which may be attributed to irregular training schedules, limited enforcement of mandatory certification, cost and time constraints for informal food vendors, and the absence of structured, government-led continuous training programs, particularly at the local government level32. About 39.1% of respondents said they had received official training, which is consistent with a previous report that structured hygiene education was not widely available in Nigeria4. Furthermore, the neutrality of 51.8% towards training comprehensiveness suggests insufficient content depth or delivery efficacy33. Although certain government and commercial training initiatives have been recognised, the WHO’s request for regular, practical, and mandatory food safety training is not being adequately reinforced31, as evidenced by the low confidence in applying gained knowledge (35.5%). Employer support is still insufficient to encourage long-term hygiene improvements, despite being noted by 45% of respondents.

The results showed that knowledge of food hygiene and sociodemographic factors had conflicting relationships. Education level revealed a startling inverse trend, while age and gender did not show any significant relationships, which is consistent with earlier research reporting that knowledge of food safety is not always dependent on age or gender34. In contrast to the widely held belief that greater education is associated with higher knowledge12, this study found that people without formal education knew more about hygiene. This runs counter to research35, which highlighted the role that formal education plays in influencing behaviour related to food safety. This discrepancy may be explained by the predominance of informal, experience-based learning among food handlers in Ondo West LGA, where individuals with lower formal education often acquire food hygiene knowledge through prolonged hands-on practice, peer learning, and on-the-job exposure rather than formal training programs3. This suggests that structured food safety education programs should be implemented across all categories of food handlers, and should not rely solely on formal education, but should be tailored to the practical needs of food handlers to ensure consistent hygiene knowledge and practices36.

Previous studies reported that organized food handlers (such as caterers and bakers) frequently receive more systematic hygiene guidance than informal handlers, like butchers9,37, workplace type had a substantial impact on knowledge levels. The lack of a significant correlation between years of experience and hygiene knowledge contrast previous study, which recorded that experience increases knowledge37. These discrepancies can result from variations in informal learning frameworks and regional food safety training regulations33. Overall, the results highlight that formal education and experience alone might not be enough without focused, continuous hygiene training catered to working situations, even though the relationships offer important insight38.

Findings of the regression analysis revealed that food hygiene knowledge strongly predicts hygienic practices (OR = 6.45, p < 0.05), corroborating the notion that knowledge has a positive influence on practice39. This finding aligns with the Theory of Planned Behaviour, which posits that food handlers’ knowledge enhances perceived behavioural control, thereby shaping positive attitudes and increasing the likelihood of engaging in appropriate food hygiene practices40. According to previous research, knowledgeable food workers are more likely to follow safety procedures25. However, some researchers warn that without supportive conditions and supervision, knowledge alone might not guarantee appropriate practice41,42. Therefore, systemic reinforcement through monitoring is still necessary to maintain safe food handling behaviour, even though education is crucial.

Limitations of the study

This study is limited because individuals may exaggerate their knowledge or hygienic habits, which could lead to social desirability bias in self-reported results. Additionally, the knowledge domain in the questionnaire included some awareness questions, and certain practice items reflect attitudes rather than actual behaviors. This distinction is acknowledged as a limitation and is considered in interpreting the findings. Furthermore, the causal inference between practices and knowledge is limited by the cross-sectional design16. Notwithstanding these limitations, reliability was ensured, and bias was reduced through skillful data collection, thorough sampling, and tool pre-testing. Overall, these limitations did not impair the validity or applicability of the study’s outcomes in comparable contexts.

Conclusion and recommendations

Food handlers in Ondo West Local Government Area generally exhibit good knowledge and adherence to food hygiene practices. High awareness of key hygiene principles, including handwashing, safe storage, and cross-contamination prevention, contributes to overall safe food handling. Knowledge was found to positively influence practice, underscoring the importance of continuous education and training to maintain and improve hygiene standards. Strengthening food hygiene awareness remains crucial for preventing foodborne illnesses and promoting public health in the community.

Notwithstanding, there are still gaps, especially for handlers with less education and less access to training. Food hygiene education must therefore be focused and ongoing. The study suggests that all types of food handlers, particularly those working in the unorganised sector, should be required to participate in frequent and mandatory food safety training programs20. Access to sanitary facilities and clean water should be guaranteed, and regular inspections should be enforced by government and public health officials. To reach people with lower levels of formal education, food safety education should also be incorporated into community health outreach initiatives. Public health authorities should implement regular and structured food hygiene training programs for food handlers regardless of their formal educational background, ensuring that essential food safety knowledge is consistently communicated across different workplace settings36. In Ondo West and comparable settings, filling these gaps will improve public health outcomes and further lower the risks of foodborne illness.
