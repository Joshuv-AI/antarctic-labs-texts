The prevalence of depression among patients with primary hyperhidrosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoyu Wang, Lipeng Xie, Shuai Wang, Shushi Meng, Yuqing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA well-established clinical association is recognized between primary hyperhidrosis (PH) and depression. However, the evidence about the prevalence of depression among people with PH is inconsistent, and there is currently no systematic review on the subject.MethodsThis study systematically searched PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science, and PsycINFO for studies reporting on the prevalence of depression in people with PH from database inception to December 19, 2025. We included cross-sectional and cohort studies reporting depression prevalence, as well as case–control studies reporting depression proportions, in patients with primary hyperhidrosis (PH). Two reviewers independently performed literature screening and data extraction, and assessed methodological quality using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool. Following the resolution of any discrepancies by discussion or a third reviewer, the pooled prevalence was derived using a random‑effects model with the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Subsequently, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, and publication bias was assessed.ResultsA total of 18 studies published between 2001 and 2024 were included, encompassing 47,053 participants. The overall pooled prevalence of depression was 18.0% (95% CI: 14.3%–22.0%) under the random‑effects model, with substantial between‑study heterogeneity (I2 = 90.24%). Subgroup analyses identified that the reported prevalence of depression varied significantly based on the assessment instrument employed (p < 0.001 for between‑subgroup heterogeneity).ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that depressive symptoms are highly prevalent among patients with primary hyperhidrosis (18.0% as assessed by validated scales), which is substantially higher than the estimated 4.4% global prevalence of clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder in the general population. Although this comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as these estimates are based on different definitions and are not directly comparable, the magnitude of the difference highlights the substantial burden of depressive symptoms in this population. The variation in reported prevalence rates attributable to different assessment tools highlights the necessity of adopting standardized and comparable measurement methods in clinical practice and future research. These findings provide updated evidence on the prevalence of depression in the PH population, identify assessment tools as a major contributor to heterogeneity, and offer valuable guidance for clinical assessment and research design.Trial registrationThe study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025642265; 24 January 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05109-w.

Background

Primary hyperhidrosis (PH) is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by excessive sweating beyond physiological thermoregulatory needs [1–3]. According to epidemiological research, between 2% and 4.8% of the general population has it [4, 5]. Primary hyperhidrosis usually starts in childhood or adolescence, peaks in late adolescence, and can last a lifetime [6, 7]. The palms (palmar hyperhidrosis), axillae (axillary hyperhidrosis), soles of the feet (plantar hyperhidrosis), and face are the most often affected regions. Sweating is enough to drip, soak through clothes, and seriously interfere with day-to-day activities [8]. The quality of life is impacted by hyperhidrosis, which is linked to social and mental stress [9–11].

Chronic physical illnesses are associated with an increased risk of mental health disorders, particularly depression [12]. This relationship has a variety of pathological and physiological processes, including long-term psychological and social stress brought on by the disease, as well as biological pathways including chronic inflammation and neuroendocrine changes brought on by the disease itself [13]. In addition to the ongoing burden of symptoms and the stress of therapy, people with chronic illnesses frequently experience a deterioration in their quality of life, all of which play a significant role in the development of depression [14]. Based on this, we put out a theoretical hypothesis framework regarding the relationship between depression and hyperhidrosis. Due to its visibility, functional impairment, and chronic course, hyperhidrosis is not an isolated symptom but rather a persistent psychosocial stressor that mediates the causal pathway from social avoidance, low self-esteem, and finally developing into clinical depression [11, 15]. As a chronic, visible, and frequently uncontrolled physiological condition, hyperhidrosis significantly increases the risk of depression for patients through multiple parallel and intertwined pathways [11, 15, 16].

There are substantial gaps and limitations in the current evidence, despite the fact that the aforementioned theories and clinical observations strongly suggest that patients with PH are a high-risk group for depression and that some preliminary studies have reported significant depressive symptoms or higher depression scores in this population [9, 10, 15–30]. First, most available data derive from observational studies with limited sample sizes, compromising the robustness and generalizability of their findings. Second, heterogeneity in assessment instruments and diagnostic thresholds across studies generates substantial variability in results, hindering direct comparison or meta-synthesis. Most critically, a precise, pooled estimate of depression prevalence in this population globally is absent. This lack of a definitive prevalence estimate impedes the integration of depression screening into hyperhidrosis management and undermines awareness among clinicians and policymakers about the scale of this comorbidity. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis are warranted to generate a reliable pooled prevalence and to elucidate the sources of heterogeneity.

It is important to note that the term ‘depression’ in the literature encompasses both clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder and subthreshold depressive symptoms assessed by self-report scales. Throughout this review, we use ‘depression’ to refer to the outcome as defined by the original studies—either by validated self-report scales or by ICD-based diagnostic codes—and we explicitly distinguish between these approaches in our analysis and interpretation.

We aim to address this gap by undertaking the first systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the association between hyperhidrosis and depression, thereby providing robust epidemiological evidence for the theoretical link. Our specific objectives are twofold: 1) Primary: To determine the global pooled prevalence of depression among patients with hyperhidrosis by synthesizing all available observational studies. 2) Secondary: To investigate potential sources of between-study heterogeneity, particularly through subgroup analysis of assessment tools. These findings may help translate evidence into clinical practice by raising awareness of psychiatric comorbidity and informing the development of comprehensive management strategies.

Methods

The reporting of this systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020 statement) [31]. The conduct of this review adhered to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for prevalence reviews, and the methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies [32].The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42025642265; 24 January 2025) had the study protocol pre-registered. The registered protocol can be accessed in full at: [https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42025642265]. No substantial amendments were made to the pre-registered protocol after registration. All analyses and subgroup investigations (e.g., by assessment tool) were conducted as planned.

Literature search and study selection

A systematic research search was conducted to identify all relevant English-language studies from database inception until December 19, 2025. The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Ovid MEDLINE, PsycINFO, and Web of Science. The search strategy was designed to capture studies on both hyperhidrosis and depression. It integrated free-text keywords associated with these two principles with controlled vocabulary (such as MeSH terms in PubMed and Emtree terms in Embase). The search strategies for all databases are provided in Appendix 1. Two reviewers separately examined the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records in accordance with the predetermined eligibility criteria after eliminating duplicates. The same two independent reviewers then conducted a full-text review of potentially eligible studies. At any point, disagreements were settled by consensus or by seeking advice from a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) included patients with clinically diagnosed primary hyperhidrosis (i.e., physician-diagnosed, not based solely on self-report); (2) reported the prevalence or proportion of depression, or provided sufficient data for its calculation; and (3) were published in English. We included cross-sectional studies, cohort studies (including baseline prevalence data), and case–control studies that reported the proportion of depression in the PH group. Depression was identified either by validated self-report scales (using published cut-off scores), structured diagnostic interviews (e.g., SCID, MINI), or ICD-based diagnoses (e.g., registry-based or electronic medical record data). Throughout this review, we distinguish between studies that assessed depressive symptoms via self-report scales and those that identified clinically diagnosed depression via ICD codes. This distinction is maintained in the interpretation of our findings.

Excluded studies were those that were: (1) Reviews, commentaries, case reports, animal studies, letters, conference abstracts, books, editorials, or notes. (2) Studies for which the full text or essential data were unavailable were also excluded. Conference abstracts that reported full, extractable data meeting the inclusion criteria were not excluded and were retained for the meta‑analysis, as they provided valid prevalence estimates.

Data extraction and quality assessment

A standardized form was used by two independent reviewers, Xiaoyu Wang and Lipeng Xie, to gather data from the included research. First author, publication year, sample size, participant characteristics (e.g., mean age, gender distribution), depression evaluation instrument, number of depression cases, and prevalence rate were among the extracted data. Two reviewers independently evaluated each included study's methodological quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Prevalence Studies [32]. Each of the nine elements on this checklist has a rating of “Yes” (1 point), “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable” (0 points). Studies were classified as high (8–9), moderate (6–7), or low (≤ 5) quality based on the overall score (range 0–9). Although the AXIS tool was pre-specified in our PROSPERO protocol, we opted to use the JBI Checklist for Prevalence Studies, as it is specifically designed for the appraisal of studies reporting prevalence data and is therefore more appropriate for our research question. This deviation from the protocol has been transparently reported.

Data synthesis and analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 16.0; StataCorp). The primary outcome was the pooled prevalence of depression among patients with primary hyperhidrosis, expressed as a proportion with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Given the anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, the pooled prevalence estimate was calculated using a random-effects meta-analysis model. To stabilize the variances of proportions, study-specific prevalence rates were transformed using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation prior to pooling, and the results were back-transformed for interpretation. Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test and quantified by the I2 statistic. For the random-effects meta-analysis, between-study heterogeneity was further estimated as the variance of the distribution of true effect sizes (τ2). Significant heterogeneity was deemed to be shown by an I2 value higher than 75%. Based on the kind of depression evaluation tool utilized, a pre-specified subgroup analysis was carried out to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. To determine whether any one study had a significant impact on the total pooled estimate, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted. This was accomplished by recalculating the aggregate prevalence after progressively eliminating each study. Publication bias was evaluated both statistically using Egger's linear regression test and visually using a funnel diagram. In Egger's test, a p-value of less than 0.10 was thought to indicate significant small-study effects.

Results

Study selection

Three thousand two hundred thirty records were found in the first comprehensive database search for title and abstract screening. After excluding 5 retracted papers and removing 476 duplicate records, there are 2749 distinct records remaining. Of these, 2,702 were eliminated because they had no bearing on the subject of the study. The remaining 47 articles’ complete texts were obtained, and their eligibility was evaluated. Nineteen studies were eliminated because they did not provide data on the prevalence of depression. Ten further studies were disregarded because they were review articles. As a result, the quantitative synthesis contained 18 papers that satisfied all eligibility requirements [9, 10, 15–30]. The included studies varied in their primary aims and recruitment settings. Most studies were conducted in dermatology or surgery outpatient clinics and recruited patients seeking treatment for hyperhidrosis. Two register-based studies (Klein 2020; Heiskanen 2022) drew participants from nationwide healthcare databases, while one additional case–control study (Bahar 2016) recruited participants from two dermatology outpatient clinics in Canada and China. One study (Bragança 2014) specifically focused on patients with severe hyperhidrosis, whereas the remaining studies included patients across all severity levels. Regarding PH diagnosis, all included studies required a clinician-confirmed diagnosis of primary hyperhidrosis per our eligibility criteria, although the specific diagnostic criteria and severity assessment tools varied across studies. The Hyperhidrosis Disease Severity Scale (HDSS) was the most commonly used tool for assessing disease severity, where reported.

Depression was assessed using diverse instruments. The most frequently employed tool was the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [33], used in seven studies, followed by the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [34] in four studies. Other instruments included the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory–Second Edition (MMPI-2) [35], the Patient Health Questionnaire–9 (PHQ-9) [36], the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) [37], and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale–Revised (CESD-R) [38], each used in one study. Three studies did not use standardized rating scales or structured diagnostic interviews; instead, they identified depression cases based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic codes from administrative claims or registry data. Although Glaser (2019) and Ryguta (2023) were published as conference abstracts in supplement issues of the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, they were included in this review because they reported complete and extractable prevalence data that met our eligibility criteria.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of the 18 included studies, as assessed using the JBI checklist, is presented in Appendix 2. Based on total scores, 11 studies (61.1%) were rated as high quality (score 8–9), 6 (33.3%) as moderate quality (score 6–7), and 1 (5.6%) as low quality (score 4).

Studies rated as high quality typically demonstrated strengths in several domains: a clear sample frame, appropriate sampling methods, adequate description of subjects and setting, use of standardized measurement instruments, and appropriate statistical analysis. A notable proportion of studies employed robust designs, including prospective (n = 3), case–control (n = 5), or multicenter (n = 4) approaches. Furthermore, the majority of high-quality studies utilized validated psychological assessment scales (e.g., HADS, BDI) to ascertain depression status.

Common limitations across studies were also identified. These included: insufficient sample size justification or small samples (e.g., Ruchinskas, 2002; Wang, 2018); inadequate description of the sampling methodology (e.g., Manav, 2022; Kristensen, 2020). Studies using ICD codes (Klein 2020; Heiskanen 2022) were considered to reflect clinically diagnosed depression, which may represent a methodological strength compared with scale-based cut-off scores that capture a broader spectrum of depressive symptoms rather than clinical diagnoses. In summary, the overall methodological quality of the evidence base is satisfactory to good. However, heterogeneity in measurement tools and concerns regarding sample representativeness in some studies should be considered when interpreting the pooled results.

Meta-analysis: pooled prevalence of depression

The meta-analysis, using a random-effects model with the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation, yielded a pooled prevalence of depression among patients with hyperhidrosis of 18.0% (95% CI: 14.3%–22.0%). The forest plot (Fig. 2) illustrates the prevalence estimates and their 95% confidence intervals for each individual study, as well as their respective weights in the overall pooled estimate. Heterogeneity assessment

Significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies. The Cochran’s Q test was highly significant (Q = 174.11, df = 17, p < 0.001), with an I2 statistic of 90.24%, indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity. The estimated between-study variance was τ2 = 0.031. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were carried out to investigate possible sources of variation due to the high heterogeneity.

Subgroups analysis

Sensitivity evaluation

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the pooled prevalence estimate. The recalculated estimates on the transformed scale remained tightly clustered between 0.178 and 0.197 (see Appendix 3). More importantly, all corresponding 95% confidence intervals showed extensive overlap with the primary meta-analysis interval (14.3%–22.0%). This indicates that the overall finding of an 18.0% prevalence of depression was not unduly influenced by any single study.

To further explore the potential impact of studies with unclear sampling methods (Q2 in the JBI checklist), we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding three studies (Gross 2014; Manav 2022; Ryguta 2023) that had unclear sampling methods (Q2) and notably higher prevalence estimates (60.0%, 44.4%, and 32.0%, respectively). The recalculated pooled prevalence was 14.5% (95% CI: 11.1%–18.2%), compared with the primary estimate of 18.0% (95% CI: 14.3%–22.0%). Although the point estimate was lower, the confidence intervals overlapped, heterogeneity remained substantial (I2 = 89.3%), and the overall effect remained statistically significant (z = 13.26, p < 0.001), indicating that our findings are robust to this methodological concern (Appendix 4).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis, synthesizing data from 18 observational studies, provides robust quantitative evidence that depression is highly prevalent among patients with primary hyperhidrosis. The pooled prevalence was 18.0% (95% CI: 14.3%–22.0%). This figure is substantially higher than the global estimated prevalence of major depressive disorder in the general population, which is approximately 4.4% according to the World Health Organization (WHO, 2017) [39]. Although this comparison should be interpreted with caution, as the WHO estimate reflects clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder whereas our pooled estimate primarily represents depressive symptoms assessed by validated self-report scales, the observed difference underscores the substantial psychological burden associated with PH. As noted above, the WHO estimate and our pooled estimate are based on different definitions—clinical diagnosis versus self-reported symptoms—yet the magnitude of the difference remains clinically meaningful. This finding underscores that PH is not merely a physical condition but also a significant risk factor for mental health, with the associated depression burden reaching clinically meaningful levels. Despite this, psychological comorbidity in PH patients is frequently overlooked in clinical practice. Consequently, our results highlight a critical need for integrating routine depression screening and early psychological assessment into the standard care pathway for patients with PH.

Our findings are consistent with a recent meta-analysis by Henning et al. (2025) [11], which reported that primary hyperhidrosis was associated with increased odds of depression (odds ratio 2.4, 95% CI: 1.4–4.0). While Henning et al. focused on the relative risk of psychiatric diseases in PH patients compared with controls, our study specifically quantifies the absolute prevalence of depressive symptoms within this population, providing complementary evidence on the mental health burden of PH. Notably, two large studies included in Henning et al.’s review—Wong (2022) [40] and Henning (2023) [41]—were not included in our meta-analysis because they did not report raw prevalence data or used outcome definitions that were not directly comparable with our inclusion criteria. Wong (2022) is a population-based cohort study examining the bidirectional association between hyperhidrosis and depressive disorders, while Henning (2023) is a retrospective cohort study focusing on sleep disturbances and depression in PH patients.

The link between PH and depression can be conceptualized through intertwined psychosocial and physiological pathways. Psychosocially, the visible and often socially stigmatizing symptoms of PH can induce profound social anxiety. This anxiety frequently manifests as avoidance of common interactions (e.g., handshakes, group activities), thereby creating a self-reinforcing cycle of isolation. Furthermore, PH causes tangible functional impairments, damaging documents, electronics, and personal belongings, which can undermine occupational and academic performance [8, 26, 42, 43].

The cumulative burden of social embarrassment and functional limitation often erodes self-esteem, establishing a detrimental psychological cascade that can be summarized as “Hyperhidrosis—Social avoidance—Depression” [11, 44, 45].

This systematic review also highlights critical avenues for future mechanistic research. A key priority is to elucidate the interplay between established psychosocial mediators (e.g., social avoidance, functional impairment, low self-esteem) and emerging neurobiological substrates. From a physiological perspective, accumulating evidence points to shared neuroendocrine and autonomic pathways between PH and depression [46, 47]. Both conditions are frequently associated with sympathetic nervous system hyperactivation and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation [3, 46]. Furthermore, preliminary findings implicate systemic inflammation and deficiencies in micronutrients (such as vitamin D and magnesium) in the pathophysiology of both disorders [21]. These potential common biological mechanisms offer promising targets for future etiological research.

While substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I2 = 90.2%), sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimate. Subgroup analysis identified the type of depression assessment instrument as a major source of this heterogeneity. As noted by Thombs et al. [48] and Levis et al. [49], self-report screening questionnaires may overestimate depression prevalence compared to structured diagnostic interviews. Notably, studies employing the Beck Depression Inventory reported a significantly higher prevalence, a finding that may be attributable to the instrument’s specific emphasis on cognitive and somatic symptoms commonly associated with chronic physical conditions. This critical observation underscores the necessity of adopting standardized, validated assessment tools in future epidemiological studies of PH. Such standardization is paramount for generating more consistent and directly comparable prevalence estimates, thereby enhancing the evidence base for clinical and public health.

The high prevalence of depression identified in this study underscores an urgent need to integrate mental health assessment and management into the standard care of patients with PH. We propose a multifaceted clinical approach encompassing routine screening, integrated treatment, and patient education. First, routine screening for depression using validated scales (e.g., HADS) should be incorporated into outpatient evaluations, particularly for adolescents and young adults at a critical developmental stage. Second, clinical management should adopt a dual-track strategy: actively optimizing first-line treatments for hyperhidrosis (e.g., antiperspirants, iontophoresis, botulinum toxin, oral medications, or surgery), as evidence suggests that effectively reducing sweating can lead to significant improvements in anxiety and depression scores [20]; combined with timely referral for evidence-based psychological interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy) and psychiatric consultation when indicated. In addition to cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), which has well-established efficacy for depression in general populations, other promising treatment approaches warrant consideration in the context of PH. Pharmacological interventions, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), have demonstrated effectiveness for depressive symptoms in patients with chronic dermatological conditions and may be considered when depressive symptoms are moderate to severe. Furthermore, integrated care models that co-locate dermatology and mental health services have shown promise in improving both skin and psychological outcomes by facilitating early detection, reducing referral barriers, and enhancing treatment adherence. Finally, proactive patient education about the well-established link between PH and depression is essential to reduce shame, normalize the experience, and improve long-term treatment adherence.

Strengths and limitations

This study has a number of advantages. It presents the first systematic meta-analysis measuring the prevalence of depression in individuals with primary hyperhidrosis. The methodological rigor was enhanced by pre-registration, adherence to PRISMA guidelines, detailed subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and thorough assessment of publication bias.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most included studies were cross-sectional, precluding causal inference regarding the direction of the association between hyperhidrosis and depression. Second, the high statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 90%), although explored through subgroup analysis by assessment tool, indicates other unmeasured sources of variation, such as differences in cultural context, hyperhidrosis severity, and comorbidities, were not fully accounted for. Third, some studies had small sample sizes, which may limit the precision of their individual estimates. Fourth, the methods used to identify depression varied across studies and may have introduced different biases. Consistent with Thombs et al. [48] and Levis et al. [49], self-report screening questionnaires may overestimate depression prevalence compared with structured diagnostic interviews. Conversely, the three studies that relied on ICD diagnostic codes from administrative databases may have underestimated the true prevalence, as this approach depends on clinical documentation and coding practices and may miss cases that are not formally diagnosed or coded as depression. Nevertheless, ICD-based identification reflects clinically diagnosed depression, which provides complementary information to scale-based estimates. These factors may affect the generalizability of the pooled prevalence estimate.

Future studies should address these limitations by employing longitudinal designs to clarify the temporal and causal relationship between PH and depression, examining depression prevalence across clinically relevant subgroups (including patients with different disease severity, sweat locations, and treatment histories), and elucidating the underlying psychosocial and biological mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis demonstrates that primary hyperhidrosis is associated with a high prevalence of depressive symptoms (18.0% as assessed by validated scales), which is substantially higher than the estimated 4.4% prevalence of clinically diagnosed major depressive disorder in the general population. Although this comparison should be interpreted cautiously given the different definitions used, the observed difference highlights the considerable psychological burden faced by PH patients and indicates that PH is a condition with substantial mental health comorbidities rather than merely a physical symptom. These findings advocate for the adoption of a biopsychosocial model in clinical management, integrating routine mental health screening and intervention into standard PH care to holistically improve patient outcomes.
