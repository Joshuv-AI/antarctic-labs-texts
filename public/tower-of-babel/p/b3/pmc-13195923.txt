Post-thaw embryo culture and the risk of large-for-gestational-age offspring: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: N Vilk Ayalon, N Omer Vilk, Y Sompolinsky, R Azulay, M Godin, Y Bentov, E Esh-Broder, C Adler Lazarovits, M Ketzinel-Gilad, R Hizkiyahu, A Hershko Klement
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrozen embryo transfer (FET) is widely used due to its safety and effectiveness. However, it has been associated with an increased risk of large for gestational age (LGA) infants. Proposed mechanisms include effects of embryo thawing and post-thaw culture duration on developmental programming. Current evidence remains limited regarding the impact of post-thaw culture on birthweight or LGA risk.MethodsA retrospective cohort study conducted in a university affiliated IVF unit including all IVF cycles resulting in singleton live births between 2021 and 2023. Data incorporated maternal characteristics, cycle details, and embryonic data. LGA was defined using national birthweight charts stratified by gestational age and sex. The cohort was classified accordingly: fresh, thawed and transferred and thawed with post-thaw culture ≥ 24 h.ResultsA total of 493 singleton births were analyzed. Mean birthweights were comparable across groups: 3192.7 ± 470.4 g (fresh), 3207.5 ± 540.8 g (thawed and transferred) and 3244.9 ± 458.7 g (post-thaw culture) (P > 0.5). LGA rates were also similar: 7.4%,10.6% and 10.1% respectively (P > 0.5). Stratifying by post-thaw culture duration (0, 1, 2, and 3 days), LGA rates were 10.6%, 8.6%, 12.8%, and 5.6% (P > 0.5). Univariate analysis showed no association between LGA and maternal age, parity, number of embryos transferred, endometrial thickness, or treatment group. However, maternal BMI was significantly (P = 0.04) associated with LGA. In a conditional regression analysis, maternal BMI demonstrated a modest association with LGA (P = 0.05).ConclusionsPost-thaw culture duration does not significantly affect LGA risk; however, maternal BMI is a modest predictor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02322-z.

Background

Frozen embryo transfers (FET) have become increasingly common owing to the high success rates achieved with vitrification, due to their safety profile in terms of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) and due to the policy favoring a single embryo transfer [1–3]. Worldwide, the number of cryopreservation cycles is increasing, and accordingly the frequency of thawed embryos transfers (1). Following these trends, many studies reported the special complications attributed to thawed embryo transfers, and specifically the increased odds for a large for gestational age (LGA) offspring [4–7].

Pregnancies complicated by large-for-gestational-age neonates, particularly those with macrosomia, are associated with a higher incidence of both maternal and fetal complications [8]. Maternal outcomes include a higher frequency of cesarean deliveries, postpartum hemorrhage, significant perineal lacerations, and obstetric anal sphincter Injuries. For the fetus, the risks include shoulder dystocia, which may lead to fractures and nerve injuries, as well as an elevated likelihood of NICU admissions, respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, polycythemia, and meconium aspiration syndrome.

Follow-up studies on FET related neonatal outcomes have found a correlation between the embryo culture duration and the risk of LGA. Fetal weights following day 5–6 transfers, blastocyst stage, were reported as more commonly large for gestational age compared to cleavage stage transfers (day 2–3) [9–11] whereas other studies [12, 13] did not demonstrate a significant difference in the rate of LGA between blastocyst and cleavage stage transfers. The impact on fetal weight is influenced by multiple factors, related to both genetic factors and intrauterine environment. However, in IVF treatments additional exposures such as the treatment protocol, endometrial thickness, day of embryo transfer, and whether the embryo was frozen or further cultured—may also contribute through epigenetic mechanisms [9].

Post thaw culture of thawed embryos provides an additional perspective over the possible impact of culture conditions on neonatal outcome, though only a few studies have investigated the post-thaw embryo culture on neonatal birthweight; Some reported no significant difference between the birthweights of neonates from embryos that were cultured from day 3 to day 5 (after thawing) and then transferred, and those thawed and transferred immediately on day 3 or day 5 [14, 15]. In contradiction, another study showed that embryos cultured from day 3 to day 5 had lower birthweights compared to embryos that were cryopreserved and transferred at day 5 [16].

In order to further analyze the possible impacts of post thaw culture, we constructed a study design comparing fresh and frozen-thawed embryos, as well as embryos that were thawed and further cultured before the transfer. Considering that the duration of culture might also affect fetal weight, we further stratified the study by the post thaw culture duration.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was performed at a single university-affiliated IVF unit and included all IVF cycles resulting in singleton live births between 2021 and 2023. In our setting, IVF is covered by the national social insurance program for all patients. Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A) is not covered and is therefore rarely performed; no PGT-A cycles were included in the present analysis.

Embryo transfer study groups were as follows:

A total of 493 singleton births were analyzed, after excluding 30 twin pregnancies. Egg donation cycles and surrogacy cycles were also excluded. Data collected included factors associated with the risk of large for gestational age (LGA): maternal characteristics (age at oocyte retrieval, height, weight, BMI, parity), treatment details (stimulation protocol, endometrial preparation method for thawed embryos, endometrial thickness), and embryo-related data (fresh vs. frozen, embryonic age at vitrification, and embryonic age at transfer). Maternal diabetic status (pre-gestational or gestational diabetes) was available for 296 women who gave birth in our institution.

Due to local work regulations, hospital elective services are not operational during weekends (Friday–Saturday). Consequently, day 5 fresh transfers are possible only for retrievals performed Tuesday–Thursday. Retrievals on Sunday or Monday are designated for day 2–4 embryo culture, either transferred or frozen. In the case of thawed embryo transfer, embryos may be transferred on the same day or, in the case of day 2–4 embryos, further culture for ≥ 24 h post-thaw may be pursued. For example, a thawed day 3 embryo can be transferred on the same day of its thawing or cultured further to day 4 (1-day culture) or day 5 (2-day culture). The exact transfer protocol for thawed embryos is determined through a discussion between the treating physician and the patient, based on medical recommendations and patient preference.

Birth data, including birthweight, gestational week, and fetal sex, are recorded in the IVF unit, as part of the national registry. LGA was defined as birthweight > 90th percentile for gestational age and sex using national birthweight charts [17].

Statistical analysis

The primary outcomes were mean birthweight and the standardized rate of LGA in each study group. In addition, potential risk factors for LGA—including maternal age, maternal BMI, parity, infertility diagnosis, endometrial thickness, embryo developmental stage at transfer, number of embryos transferred, and endometrial preparation protocol for frozen–thawed embryos—were compared between LGA and non-LGA births, as well as across the three study groups (fresh embryo transfer, immediate post-thaw transfer, and post-thaw culture ≥ 24 h).

Comparisons between LGA and non-LGA births for continuous variables were performed using the Student’s t-test; comparisons for continuous variables across study groups were completed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare rates and proportions. To assess culture type as an independent risk factor for LGA, we performed multivariable conditional logistic regression adjusting for potential confounders.

All p-values were two-tailed, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 28 (IBM Corporation).

The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board and conducted in compliance with applicable regulations regarding human subject research (approval number 0209-24-HMO).

Results

A total of 493 singleton live births were analyzed. Of these, 108 (22%) resulted from fresh embryo transfers, 226 (46%) from frozen embryo transfers performed immediately after thawing, and 159 (32%) from frozen embryos that underwent post-thaw culture prior to transfer.

Maternal and cycle characteristics across the three study groups (fresh, frozen-thawed, and frozen-thawed with post-thaw culture) are presented in Table 1. There were no significant differences in maternal age, parity, or height/weight among the groups. Notably, our population is characterized by a relatively young maternal age (early 30s for all groups). Significant differences were observed in the primary indication for treatment (P = 0.01), the endometrial preparation protocol )P < 0.01) and the embryonic stage at transfer (P = 0.01) (Table 1). Blastocyst-stage transfer was predominant in the post-thaw cultured group, whereas cleavage-stage transfers were more frequent in the fresh and immediate post-thaw groups. Artificial endometrial preparation using estrogen and progesterone was more common in the post-thaw cultured group. Although mean endometrial thickness was approximately 9 mm in all groups, this variable showed a statistically significant difference (P = 0.04), across the cohorts (Table 1).

Mean gestational age at birth and mean birthweight were comparable among the groups: 3192.7 ± 470.4 g (fresh), 3207.5 ± 540.8 g (thawed and transferred) and 3244.9 ± 458.7 g (post-thaw cultured) (Fig. 1; P > 0.05). Gender distribution and gestational age at delivery were also similar between groups (P > 0.05 for both) (Table 1).

The other primary outcome measure, rate of large for gestational age (LGA) neonates, did not differ significantly among groups: 7.4% in the fresh group ,10.6% in the immediate post-thaw transfer group and 10.1% in the post-thaw cultured group (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2A). When stratified by post-thaw culture duration (0, 1, 2, and 3 days), LGA rates were 10.6%, 8.6%, 12.8%, and 5.6%, respectively (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Rates of LGA and fetal weights were comparable across the different endometrial preparation protocols used for thawed embryo transfers (supplementary Table 1). To further clarify potential differences in outcome measures, specifically among day-5 blastocyst transfers, we compared fetal birth weight and large-for-gestational-age (LGA) rates between groups. The mean birth weight was 3337.6 ± 472.3 g in the fresh transfer group, 3199.9 ± 666.7 g in the immediate post-thaw transfer group, and 3227.6 ± 447.9 g in the post-thaw cultured group (P = 0.48). The corresponding LGA rates were 13.3%, 11.9%, and 9.0%, respectively (P = 0.60).Univariate analysis revealed no association between LGA and maternal age, parity, number of embryos transferred, endometrial thickness, or embryo transfer group (Table 2). However, maternal BMI was significantly (P = 0.04) associated with LGA (Table 2). In multivariate conditional logistic regression incorporating culture type, maternal age, BMI and parity, embryonic age, endometrial thickness, fresh versus frozen-thawed transfer and treatment indication, maternal BMI demonstrated a modest association with LGA (P = 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that LGA rates were similar among fresh embryos, frozen embryos, and frozen embryos that underwent post-thaw culture prior to transfer. Neither post-thaw culture nor culture duration significantly affected the risk of LGA. While most maternal and cycle parameters showed no association with LGA newborns, maternal BMI was identified as an independent predictor in a univariate analysis.

Cryopreservation has been linked to an elevated incidence of large-for-gestational-age (LGA) neonates. Multiple factors may underlie the observed differences in fetal growth between offspring resulting from frozen and fresh embryo transfer, among them the underlying cause of parental infertility, laboratory procedures, the composition and duration of the culture medium, and embryo selection strategies. Endometrial receptivity is also a critical determinant, shaped by endometrial thickness, the presence and function of the corpus luteum, associated hormonal secretions, and the pharmacological regimens administered prior to embryo transfer. In addition, cryopreservation and related laboratory manipulations may induce epigenetic modifications and alterations in DNA methylation within the embryo [18, 19].

Previous studies were conducted to explore the mechanisms underlying the higher large-for-gestational-age (LGA) incidence following frozen embryo transfer (FET). In an elegant study design [20], birth-weights were compared in the same mother following a fresh embryo transfer from an egg donation as compared to a thawed embryo of the donor within the same mother. The researcher demonstrated no significant differences in birthweight, thereby suggesting that maternal factors or possibly the uterine preparation environment may have influenced the findings of earlier studies, rather than intrinsic embryonic properties. Additional studies [18, 19] have examined the epigenetic differences between fresh and frozen embryos. Ghosh et al. [18] examined placental DNA methylation levels at repeated sequences (LINE1 elements) and at all CCGG sites (the LUMA assay), in order to evaluate the impact of modifiable clinical and laboratory procedures associated with in vitro fertilization. The study showed that global methylation levels differed between placentas from natural conceptions and those from IVF. Specifically, placentas from fresh embryo transfers exhibited significantly altered LINE1 methylation compared to in vivo conceptions, whereas placentas from frozen embryo transfers did not differ from controls. Barberet et al. [19] compared the expression of imprinted genes (IGs) and transposable elements (TEs) among fresh, frozen, and naturally conceived embryos. In their study, placental tissue and umbilical cord blood samples were analyzed using pyrosequencing to assess DNA methylation profiles of three IGs and two TEs. The results demonstrated that fresh embryos exhibited the most pronounced alterations, suggesting that ovarian stimulation may contribute to changes in placental epigenetic regulation.

Regarding the possible effects of the culture, previous researchers have studied post thaw culture as a possible factor affecting growth patterns. Xiong et al. [16] reported that neonates derived from post-thaw cultured blastocysts had significantly lower birthweights compared with those from conventional frozen–thawed blastocyst transfers (2611.90 ± 618.65 g versus 2931.86 ± 546.52 g, P < 0.01), but similar birthweights to those resulting from fresh transfers. Notably, the incidence of large-for-gestational-age (LGA), defined as a birthweight greater than the 90th percentile for gestational age, did not differ significantly among the three groups. Zhu et al. [14] analyzed 10,464 day-3 embryos that underwent cryopreservation, comparing outcomes between those transferred after a short post-thaw culture period (0.5–8 h) and those transferred following extended culture of 48–72 h (994 embryos). Their study found that the duration of post-thaw culture did not significantly affect birthweight. However, it was not specified whether adjustments were made for gestational age, and no specific analysis of large-for-gestational-age (LGA) incidence was provided.

Rahav-Koren et al. [15] evaluated 463 embryos, comparing frozen blastocyst transfers on day 5 with embryos thawed on day 3 and cultured to day 5 prior to transfer (n = 232). The two groups demonstrated comparable neonatal birthweight (thawed day 5: 3181.2 ± 578.1 g versus thawed and cultured day 3–5: 3209.5 ± 639.9 g, P = 0.43). As in previous studies, no adjustment for gestational age was reported, and there was no specific analysis of large-for-gestational-age (LGA) incidence.

Previous studies investigating the association between embryo transfer day and birth weight have reported conflicting results. Ling et al. [11]. compared frozen blastocyst (n = 3,364) and cleavage-stage (n = 1,471) transfers and observed higher birth weights following blastocyst transfer, with an increased risk of large for gestational age (LGA; adjusted OR 1.329, 95% CI 1.149–1.536) and very large for gestational age (VLGA; adjusted OR 1.317, 95% CI 1.092–1.590), particularly in late pregnancy. In contrast, Maheshwari et al. [12], analyzing fresh cleavage-stage (n = 18,249) and blastocyst (n = 42,677) transfers, reported comparable rates of both small for gestational age (SGA) and LGA infants between groups. Similarly, Yin et al. [13], using a matched-pair design to compare frozen cleavage-stage and blastocyst transfers (n = 173), found no significant differences in birth weight. Consistent with these latter studies, our findings also demonstrated no difference in LGA rates following day-5 embryo transfer.

Our study has several strengths. As a single-center analysis, it minimizes variability arising from differences in laboratory practices across institutions. Neonates were classified according to national birthweight percentiles adjusted for both gestational age and fetal sex, thereby accounting for two key determinants of growth. Furthermore, we stratified outcomes by the number of culture days following thawing, allowing a more detailed examination of the potential impact of post-thaw culture duration. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size reduce the robustness and generalizability of the findings and the results of the adjusted analysis should be interpreted with caution; The study was powered to detect an absolute increase of approximately 10% in LGA rates. Consequently, smaller differences may not have been detected. Although such differences are unlikely to be clinically meaningful, larger studies would be needed to identify more modest effects. In addition, unmeasured variations in laboratory conditions, such as culture media or embryo handling, may have introduced residual confounding not captured in our analysis. Importantly, our study did not directly assess epigenetic or placental parameters; therefore, conclusions regarding the biological mechanisms underlying culture effects should be interpreted with caution. Future research is required to further elucidate the molecular and epigenetic mechanisms underlying variability in IVF treatments that influence fetal growth, both in the context of frozen versus fresh transfers and with regard to the presence and duration of post-thaw culture. Future studies incorporating in-vivo conceptions as a comparator may provide additional context for interpreting IVF-related findings.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study comparing fresh, frozen–thawed, and post-thaw cultured embryo transfers, birthweight and LGA rates were similar across groups. Instead, maternal anthropometric factors—specifically BMI—was the sole predictor of fetal overgrowth, suggesting a possible limited impact of laboratory culture conditions compared with maternal characteristics.

These findings provide clinically relevant reassurance for both patients and clinicians regarding the safety of post-thaw culture in relation to neonatal growth outcomes.
