Screening for mood disorders after stroke: feasibility of a community-based pathway
Authors: Octavio Garcia Silva, Dianne Lesley Marsden, Shalini Rajan, Vinodkumar Raveendran, Beng Lim Alvin Chew, Md Golam Hasnain, Christopher R Levi, Mark W Parsons, Neil Spratt, Carlos Garcia Esperon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the prevalence of anxiety and depression 4–6 months poststroke, examine whether demographic factors were associated with response status, and evaluate feasibility outcomes, including acceptability and utility, of a poststroke mood disorder screening programme.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included patients aged ≥18 years discharged with ischaemic stroke, transient ischaemic attack or intracerebral haemorrhage between October 2022 and February 2024 from a comprehensive stroke centre in New South Wales, Australia. Exclusion criteria were (a) current or recent patient of a multidisciplinary rehabilitation team, (b) receipt of postdischarge mental health services, (c) discharge to a residential aged care facility, (d) end-of-life care or (e) residence outside the health district. Eligible patients were mailed validated mood screening tools. High-scoring patients meeting predefined criteria were referred to a rapid-access psychiatry clinic.ResultsOf 825 patients identified, 485 were eligible and 236 (49%) responded. Responders were older than non-responders (median 75 vs 71 years, p<0.01), and there was a trend towards fewer males (58% vs 66%, p=0.052). Among responders, 84 (35%) scored above thresholds for anxiety and/or depression. Of these, 18 (21%) were referred to psychiatry; 14 attended, and 10 (71%) received interventions, including initiation of medication and/or referral to psychology.ConclusionsAnxiety and depression were highly prevalent after stroke in our population. This pilot poststroke mood disorder screening was feasible, identified one in three responders with symptoms and led to targeted psychiatric interventions in the majority of those reviewed.Keywords: STROKE, Anxiety, DEPRESSION, CEREBROVASCULAR, PSYCHIATRY

Introduction

Anxiety and depression are commonly identified after stroke. Among stroke survivors, anxiety has a prevalence of 18%–24% within the first 10 years, while depression affects approximately 31%–33% within the first 5 years.1 2 These rates are higher than those reported in the general population, where anxiety and depression prevalence ranges between 10%–14% and 10%–13%, respectively.3 4 Mood disorders after stroke not only affect recovery and quality of life5 but also contribute to cognitive impairment, functional decline, increased mortality and higher recurrence risks of stroke.26,8

Thus, identification of mood disorders after stroke is of importance. There is a myriad of tools in the literature, but the Hospital Anxiety (A) and Depression (D) Scale (HADS) is commonly used. This scale has a strong prognostic value, with a score ≥7 associated with a 41% higher 5-year mortality risk.9,11 Although lower cut-off scores are typically recommended, higher values (eg, >11) increase specificity and identify individuals with more pronounced symptoms, reducing false positives and the risk of straining clinical resources.12 13 For people after a stroke with cognition or communication limitations, there are screening tools that can be completed by someone who knows the patient well, such as a carer. The Stroke Aphasic Depression Questionnaire-10 (SADQ-10) allows the carer to assess depressive symptoms with high sensitivity,14 while the Behavioural Outcomes of Anxiety (BOA) scale can be used to effectively evaluate anxiety.15 16

Currently, there is uncertainty regarding optimal timing, frequency and ongoing management of poststroke mood disorder screening. Poststroke depression follows a fluctuating trajectory, with variation in timing of onset, course and duration. While approximately one-third of episodes are identified within the first 3 months, a substantial proportion is detected later, during the subacute or chronic phases of recovery.17 This variability highlights the risk of failing to detect delayed-onset symptoms if screening is limited to the acute phase.

This lack of consensus is reflected in international guidelines. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (UK) recommends screening at least three times within 6 months, followed by an annual review,18 while the American Heart Association advises screening during key care transitions, without specifying frequency.19 In Australia, inpatient screening has improved over the past decade from 53% to 63%.20 However, only 9% of stroke patients identified with mood disorders during admission received psychological care, highlighting the need for more sustainable pathways of care.21 Additionally, approximately 20% of inpatients are unable to complete standardised assessments due to aphasia or cognitive impairment.22 23 These findings underscore the challenges of detecting mood disorders during hospitalisation and the importance of extending screening initiatives to community-dwelling stroke survivors, with adapted tools suitable for individuals with cognition and communication difficulties.

Therefore, we developed and piloted a structured mood screening initiative for community-dwelling stroke survivors. The pathway was developed to address limitations of inpatient screening by enabling postdischarge assessment and incorporating proxy-reported tools for patients with cognition and communication impairments. It employed validated instruments selected for their brevity, self- or carer-administered format and validation across community settings, supporting their feasibility and clinical applicability. The pathway included referral of high-scoring patients meeting predefined criteria to a dedicated poststroke psychiatry clinic established as part of this pilot initiative.

The aims of this study were to (1) describe the prevalence of anxiety and/or depression among stroke survivors who completed the mood screening pathway, (2) determine whether demographic factors were associated with response status (responders vs non-responders) and (3) describe the (a) acceptability (responder rate to the questionnaires) and (b) utility (percentage of referrals to psychiatry and percentage of interventions made by psychiatry).

Material and methods

Study design and study population

We prospectively reviewed consecutive patients admitted under the Stroke service at the John Hunter Hospital (Newcastle, Australia) between October 2022 and February 2024. Patients were eligible for inclusion in the mood disorder screening pathway if they were (a) aged ≥18 years and (b) discharged with a principal or secondary diagnosis of transient ischaemic attack, intracerebral haemorrhage or cerebral infarction (ischaemic stroke), as defined by International Classifications of Diseases-10 codes G45, I61 and I63, respectively. Exclusion criteria were (a) patients currently admitted to, or discharged from a multidisciplinary rehabilitation team within the previous 4 weeks, (b) receiving mental health services postdischarge, defined as documented follow-up with psychiatry or psychology services in the medical record or reported by the patient or carer, including active pharmacological treatment for depression and/or anxiety prescribed by a general practitioner (GP), (c) discharged to residential aged care facility, (d) receiving end of life care or (e) residing out of the local health district.

Inpatient mood screening was not performed consecutively in all patients. Instead, screening was undertaken at the discretion of treating clinicians when mood disorders were clinically suspected.

Patients were contacted by mail 4 to 6 months after the index event. This timeframe was chosen to capture individuals in the early community phase following completion of acute care and, where applicable, structured rehabilitation. This period reflects a transition in the continuum of care, during which patients move from hospital-based services to community settings. Given the recognised variability in onset and longitudinal course of poststroke depressive symptoms, including delayed emergence beyond the first 3 months,17 24 this period was considered appropriate to identify symptoms that may not have been evident during hospitalisation.

Patients received a study package containing: (1) an introductory letter; (2) mood screening tools—the HADS-A/D for the patient, or the SADQ-10 and BOA for the primary carer; (3) a reply-paid envelope and (4) a two-page information sheet with contact details for local mental health services. There was no predefined time limit for questionnaire return; responses received up to the time of data extraction were included in the analysis.

Participants who returned a completed questionnaire were classified as responders; those who did not were considered non-responders. Responders were categorised into three subgroups: (1) patient-only respondents (patient questionnaire completed without carer questionnaire), (2) dyad respondents (both patient and carer questionnaires completed) and (3) carer-only respondents (questionnaire completed by a carer without patient self-report).

Returned questionnaires were scored by a member of the research team using the following cut-offs: HADS-A≥11 points (anxiety), HADS-D≥11 (depression), BOA≥14 (proxy-rated anxiety) and SADQ-10≥14 points (proxy-rated depression).

A summary letter was sent to the GP of all responders, outlining the screening results compared with the cut-off scores for each tool completed and providing recommendations for continued mood surveillance.

All participants who screened positive for anxiety and/or depression were individually reviewed by a multidisciplinary team, including neurologists and psychiatrists, to determine eligibility for referral to the dedicated poststroke psychiatry clinic established as part of the pathway. As is common in many rural and regional areas across Australia and internationally, psychiatric services were limited. Therefore, referrals were triaged according to eligibility criteria co-designed by the stroke and psychiatry teams following a trial period prior to the study. Referrals prioritised patients under 60 years of age, those under 65 who were employed, or individuals with a psychiatric history or recent emergency department presentations for mental health concerns (eg, suicidal ideation) (figure 1).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics and questionnaire results.

Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD), an area-based measure developed by the Australian Bureau of Statistics. IRSAD summarises information about the socioeconomic conditions and households within a geographic area. IRSAD scores were assigned based on residential postcodes.25 Stroke severity was routinely assessed at admission using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) as part of standard stroke care.26 However, NIHSS scores were recorded in the study database only for patients who screened above the cut-off scores.

Continuous variables were expressed as median and IQR due to non-normal distribution, and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between responders and non-responders were performed to identify demographic differences. For continuous variables (eg, age, IRSAD), the Mann-Whitney U test was used. For categorical variables (eg, sex, stroke diagnosis), chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were applied, as appropriate. For subgroup analyses, patient-reported scores were compared between patient-only respondents and patients within dyads, while caregiver-reported scores were compared between carers within dyads and carer-only respondents, using the Mann-Whitney U test. Within dyads, associations between patient-reported HADS scores and caregiver-reported BOA and SADQ-10 scores were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r). A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R-medic software.27

We used the SQUIRE (Standards for QUality Improvement Reporting Excellence) checklist when writing the manuscript.28

Results

From October 2022 to February 2024, a total of 825 patients were screened, and 485 eligible participants were subsequently mailed the study package. The most frequent reasons for exclusion were residence outside the health district (n=127) and patient deceased at the time of review (n=101) (figure 2).

Among the 485 patients, 236 (49%) returned at least one completed questionnaire and were classified as responders, while 249 (51%) were non-responders. Among the 236 returned packages, 227 (96%) questionnaires were completed by patients and 147 (62%) by carers.

The total eligible study population had a median age of 74 years (IQR 64–82), and 300 of 485 participants (61%) were male. The majority were admitted with an ischaemic stroke (80%, table 1). The median IRSAD score was 973 (IQR: 947.3–989.2), slightly below the national average of 1000, indicating that participants predominantly resided in areas with modest socioeconomic disadvantage.

Patients in the responder group were significantly older than non-responders (75 years vs 71 years; p<0.01) and there was a trend towards fewer males (57% vs 65%; p=0.052). The distribution of stroke diagnoses was comparable between responders and non-responders, with most patients in both groups diagnosed with ischaemic stroke (p=0.67). IRSAD scores were very similar between the two groups (median 973 for responders vs 976 for non-responders; p=0.33).

Among the 236 responders, the median scores for the scales were: HADS-A=5 (IQR 2–9) (n=227), HADS-D=4 (IQR 1–9) (n=227), BOA=12 (IQR 7–18) (n=147) and SADQ-10=9 (IQR 5–15) (n=147) (table 2). Of the 236 responders, 89 were patient-only respondents, 138 were dyads and 9 were carer-only respondents. Compared with patient-only respondents, dyad patients had significantly higher anxiety symptoms (HADS-A median 6 (IQR 2–10) vs 5 (2–8); Mann-Whitney U=5177, p=0.045) and higher depressive symptoms (HADS-D median 5 (2–9) vs 3 (1–6); U=4600.5, p=0.002). Among carers’ questionnaires, BOA and SADQ-10 scores did not differ significantly between dyad carers and carer-only respondents (BOA: U=719.5, p=0.38; SADQ-10: U=624.5, p=0.92). Within dyads, patient-reported questionnaires showed strong correlations with caregiver-reported ones. HADS-A correlated with BOA (Spearman’s r=0.74, p<0.0001) and HADS-D correlated with SADQ-10 (r=0.66, p<0.0001).

A total of 84 patients (35%) scored above the cut-off scores (≥11 for HADS-A or HADS-D; ≥14 for BOA or SADQ-10) for anxiety and/or depression. Of these, 77 patients (32%) scored positive for anxiety alone, 55 patients (23%) for depression alone, and 48 patients (20%) for both conditions. The median age was 75 years (IQR 65–81), 46 (55%) were male and most of them were admitted after an ischaemic stroke (77%), with a median admission NIHSS of 4 (IQR 2–7).

Those 84 patients scoring positive for mood disorders were individually reviewed by the multidisciplinary team. Only a minority, 18 patients (21%) met the study’s predefined eligibility criteria and were referred to the poststroke psychiatry clinic. 14 attended the appointment. Four patients cancelled their appointments; all were offered the opportunity to reschedule but declined (figure 3). The median age in the referred group was 53 years (IQR 43–69) and 12 (67%) were male. A total of 66 patients (78%) were not referred to the poststroke psychiatry clinic as they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The most frequent reason was age above 65 years (n=61), followed by age 60–65 years without current employment (n=3), and linguistic barriers that prevented arranging follow-up (n=2). In the non-referred group, the median age was 77 years (IQR 70–83) and 34 (52%) of them were male.

Among those reviewed, 10 (71%) patients had a specific intervention: 3 were referred to a psychologist, 2 initiated psychiatric medication and 3 received both interventions. The remaining four patients did not require additional pharmacological or psychological intervention at the time of assessment. Discussion

This single-centre pilot study evaluated the feasibility and utility of a structured postdischarge mood disorder screening programme for stroke survivors. Nearly half of contacted patients returned completed questionnaires (49%), with over a third (35%) of responders scoring above thresholds for anxiety and/or depression. By incorporating both patient self-report (HADS) and caregiver proxy-report tools (BOA, SADQ-10), the initiative was able to include individuals with communication impairments and provide a broader perspective on emotional outcomes within stroke survivor–carer dyads.

The prevalence of depression in our cohort (23%) was similar to previously reported (27% in a recent meta-analysis),29 while the anxiety rate (32%) was higher than previous literature (20%–25%).5 30 This difference may reflect variations in screening tools, timing of questionnaire administration during the subacute phase, or heightened awareness among patients and carers postdischarge. The choice of a ≥11 cut-off in HADS aligns with the aim of identifying those patients with clinically significant levels of anxiety or depressive symptoms. Although lower thresholds2 7 could maximise combined sensitivity and specificity, recent evidence suggests that conventional cut-offs (≥8) lead to a higher prevalence of false-positive cases, placing unnecessary strain on clinical services.12 In contrast, a higher threshold of ≥11 increases specificity, reducing false positives. In the context of stroke, where cognitive changes and fatigue frequently elevate HADS scores without representing diagnosable anxiety or depression, a conservative threshold may improve the clinical meaningfulness of screening results. We acknowledge that using a higher threshold may reduce sensitivity and potentially miss some individuals with milder symptoms; however, for a pragmatic screening initiative with limited follow-up capacity, optimising specificity was prioritised. Consequently, our prevalence estimates likely represent individuals with more substantial anxiety or depressive symptoms.

Response rates in our study (49%) were lower than those reported in other countries, ranging between 60% and 70%. This is possibly influenced by age, mode of questionnaire delivery and engagement strategies.31,33 It is also possible that non-responders were more likely to experience low mood, apathy, cognitive impairment or anxiety, which may have reduced their motivation or capacity to engage with the screening process. This potential non-response bias could have led to an underestimation of the true prevalence of mood disorders in this population.

In our subgroup analysis, dyad patients—those for whom both patient and caregiver questionnaires were available—showed higher anxiety and depression scores compared with patient-only respondents. This likely reflects that individuals able to complete questionnaires independently have greater functional independence and a lower symptom burden. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between dyad carers and the carer-only subgroup. Patients requiring caregiver proxy reporting, often due to cognitive impairment or aphasia, may be inherently more vulnerable to mood disturbances.

Despite a substantial proportion of patients scoring above the pre-specified threshold, only one in five was ultimately referred to the poststroke psychiatry clinic. A substantial proportion of referred patients received evidence-based interventions, including initiation of psychiatric medication, referral to psychology services or both.

Key strengths of this study include the use of validated instruments (HADS, BOA, SADQ-10), integration of caregiver perspectives, and embedding of the programme into routine clinical practice with existing resources. Limitations include (a) potential response bias; there was a large proportion of non-responders, and these were younger than the responders, suggesting that the mail approach in isolation is not a good approach and use of technology might be required to engage with the younger stroke community. In addition, no reminder phone calls or follow-up contact were undertaken after the initial mail-out, which may have contributed to the observed response rate and (b) strict eligibility criteria for referral to the psychiatry clinic, excluding patients over 65 and those aged 60–65 who were not employed. This limited the number of patients who received direct specialist care; almost 80% of patients with positive scores did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria for clinic referral. These thresholds were developed to ensure that the workload of this study was able to be managed through existing resources of the public system. However, future iterations of this programme may include a broader inclusion criteria. Furthermore, difficulties related to language barriers in a small number of cases emphasise the importance of accessible and culturally appropriate mental health pathways within diverse stroke populations.

In conclusion, this pilot mood disorder screening pathway demonstrates that structured poststroke mood disorder screening is feasible, and it can identify a high proportion of patients living in the community with anxiety or depression. The proposed criteria for referral to psychiatry were able to identify a small subset of patients requiring psychiatry review, but potentially a larger group of patients might have benefited. Future initiatives should focus on improving response rates, potentially through digital platforms, removing age cut-offs and revisiting scoring thresholds for referral to psychiatry.
