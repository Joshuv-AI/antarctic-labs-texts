Insights from the WHO Tricycle-aligned Fleming Fund EQASIA External Quality Assessment (EQA) programme detecting ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing Escherichia coli in South and South-East Asia
Authors: Hiba Al-Mir, Praissy Zefi Jeyakumar, Tomislav Kostyanev, Faisal Ahmad Khan, Lone Brink Rasmussen, Maimoona Emmad, Freshwork Ayalew Abegaz, Patrícia Teixeira Dos Santos, Soo-Young Kwon, Rangsiya Prathan, Taradon Luangtongkum, Pattrarat Chanchaithong, Pitak Santanirand, Watcharaporn Kamjumpho, Ondari D Mogeni, Tobin Guarnacci, Marianne Holm, Wantana Paveenkittiporn, Nimesh Poudyal, Rungtip Chuanchuen, Rene S Hendriksen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Escherichia coli, notably those producing AmpC, ESBL and carbapenemases among food-producing animals, poses a significant public health threat, especially in low- and middle-income countries. With support from the Fleming Fund and in alignment with the WHO, FAO and WOAH tripartite Tricycle project, the EQASIA Matrix External Quality Assessment (EQA) scheme aimed at evaluating laboratories’ proficiency from different One Health (OH) sectors in detecting resistant E. coli from food-like matrices in South and South-East Asia.MethodsBetween 2021 and 2024, four Matrix EQA rounds were implemented across OH sectors. Each round involved four lyophilized simulated meat samples spiked with well-characterized E. coli strains expressing β-lactam resistance phenotypes. Participating laboratories performed selective isolation, species identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and phenotypic classification using routine methods. Results were submitted via a secure online platform and evaluated against expected outcomes, using CLSI-based interpretive criteria and a standardized error categorization system.ResultsTwenty-one laboratories from 10 countries participated across the four rounds. Accuracy in E. coli identification declined from 75% in 2021 to 23.1% in 2024. Only one laboratory achieved full identification accuracy in any round. AST performance showed inter-laboratory variation, with deviation rates ranging from 0.5% to 41%. Misclassification of resistance phenotypes, particularly AmpC and combined ESBL + AmpC, was common. Quality control testing improved modestly over time but remained incomplete.ConclusionsThe Matrix EQA revealed critical insights into regional laboratory capacity and highlighted persistent technical gaps in detecting complex resistance mechanisms from food matrices. With the conclusion of the Fleming Fund, maintaining these gains will require sustained national investment, EQA integration into AMR strategies and continued regional coordination to support Tricycle-aligned surveillance.

Introduction

The global burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) continues to rise, undermining the efficacy of critical antimicrobials and threatening public health and food safety worldwide.1,2 Among priority pathogens, Escherichia coli producing ESBLs, AmpC and carbapenemases have emerged as important indicators of AMR dissemination across human, animal and environmental sectors.3 These resistance mechanisms pose significant clinical and agricultural challenges and are key targets of global surveillance initiatives such as the WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) and the FAO’s monitoring of foodborne AMR.4 The WHO Tricycle protocol specifically promotes surveillance of ESBL-producing E. coli as a sentinel organism for integrated AMR monitoring across One Health sectors,5 offering a harmonized framework that also serves as a proxy for MDR emergence and transmission.

Despite these initiatives, gaps in laboratory capacity persist in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including inconsistent methods for selective isolation, limited standardization and challenges in interpreting complex phenotypes.6–8 External Quality Assessment (EQA) data from WHO’s Global Foodborne Infections Network (GFN), ARBAO-II, WHO Collaborating Centres and technical guidance from the European Union Reference Laboratory for Antimicrobial Resistance (EURL-AR) underscore the urgent need for harmonized, high-quality laboratory practices and reliable AMR reporting systems.9–12 EQAs play a central role in addressing these needs by benchmarking laboratory performance, supporting continuous improvement and ensuring comparability across sectors and countries critical for effective One Health surveillance.

In this context, the UK International Development programme’s Fleming Fund–supported EQASIA project was launched in 2020 to improve the quality of bacteriology diagnostics across One Health sectors in South and South-East Asia.6,8,13 Among its portfolio of multiple EQA types, the Matrix EQA specifically assesses laboratory proficiency in isolating and characterizing ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli from food-like matrices.8 While broader in scope than the Tricycle protocol, the Matrix EQA offers a practical platform to evaluate laboratories’ readiness to contribute high-quality data to Tricycle-aligned surveillance, while also encompassing additional clinically relevant resistance mechanisms. The Matrix EQA builds on technical foundations established by EURL-AR and leverages a digital informatics platform for harmonized data submission and performance reporting.8

The primary objective of this study is to assess and strengthen participating laboratories’ capacity to isolate, identify and perform antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) on presumptive ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli from complex matrices. Here, we present consolidated results and lessons learned from four Matrix EQA rounds conducted between 2021 and 2024 across participating countries in South and South-East Asia, highlighting technical variability and laboratory performance. These findings provide insights to inform regional capacity building and contribute to global AMR surveillance initiatives including WHO GLASS, EURL programmes and the WHO Tricycle framework. The findings also underline the importance of such an EQA programme in South and South-East Asia and highlight the need for it to become sustainable.

Materials and methods

Foundation and framework of the Matrix EQA

The EQASIA EQA trials are based on validated methodologies developed by the European Union Reference Laboratory for Antimicrobial Resistance (EURL-AR), including protocols for the isolation and characterization of E. coli producing ESBLs, AmpC and carbapenemases from animal and food sources (Figure 1).12,14 The WHO Tricycle protocol itself builds upon these EURL-AR methods and the EU’s monitoring framework, providing an international reference for integrated AMR surveillance.11 The Matrix EQA applies these principles to assess laboratory proficiency under routine conditions and promote harmonization of methodologies across participating countries, supporting the comparability of surveillance data with global initiatives such as WHO GLASS and WHO Tricycle.4,11

EQASIA Matrix EQA cultures preparation and handling, following the official EURL-AR protocols (https://www.food.dtu.dk/english/topics/antimicrobial-resistance/eurl-ar/protocols) (https://www.eurl-ar.eu/protocols.aspx). Prior to spiking, minced pork meat was screened to ensure the absence of naturally occurring ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli. Portions of 25 g were incubated in 225 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW) at 37°C ± 1°C for 18–22 h. A loopful of the enriched suspension was then plated onto MacConkey agar supplemented with 1 mg/L cefotaxime and incubated overnight. Only batches negative for target-resistant E. coli but containing background flora were selected for subsequent use. Four meat portions (25 g each) from the validated batch were individually spiked with phenotypically and genotypically characterized E. coli strains. Following incubation and selective plating as above, the resulting bacterial growth—comprising both target organisms and commensals—was harvested and lyophilized. These cultures were designed to simulate realistic sample complexity and were distributed as ready-to-use EQA test materials. The E. coli isolates used for spiking were phenotypically characterized at the National Food Institute, Technical University of Denmark (DTU Food), and externally verified by the Peter Doherty Institute for Infection and Immunity (Australia). WGS was performed to confirm the presence of relevant antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes and chromosomal mutations. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined by broth microdilution and used to define expected values for quality assessment. Reference control strains, E. coli ATCC 25922 and E. coli NCTC 13846, were included in earlier trials and recommended for ongoing internal quality control. Interpretative ranges for AST quality assurance were based on CLSI M100-34th Ed. and EUCAST QC guidelines.

Design and workflow of the Matrix EQA trials

Each Matrix EQA round distributed four lyophilized cultures prepared from pork meat samples spiked with well-characterized E. coli strains exhibiting ESBL, AmpC or carbapenemase phenotypes. Lyophilization was selected to address logistical challenges in shipping and cold chain maintenance across the region (Figure 1). The isolates were thoroughly characterized at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU Food) and validated by external referee reference laboratories, including the Peter Doherty Institute (Australia) and Chulalongkorn University (Thailand). Characterization involved phenotypic MIC determination by Sensititre broth microdilution and Illumina WGS for detection of AMR genes and chromosomal point mutations.

The preparation of lyophilized cultures followed a defined procedure. First, minced pork samples were tested to confirm absence of target-resistant E. coli. Selected portions were then spiked with individual E. coli strains, incubated in buffered peptone water and plated on selective media (MacConkey agar with 1 mg/L cefotaxime). The resulting bacterial mixture was harvested and lyophilized, creating stable test material mimicking naturally contaminated food samples (Figure 1).

Reference material, shipment and laboratory engagement

To ensure comparability and reliability of results, all spiked strains were benchmarked against EURL-AR MIC protocols and quality control (QC) standards (e.g. E. coli ATCC 25922 and NCTC 13846 for colistin). Participating laboratories received protocols referencing the EURL-AR website, instructions for reviving lyophilized samples and procedures for selective isolation and AST.12,14 Communication with laboratories was facilitated through EQASIA country coordinators and central communication hubs at DTU Food and CUVET Thailand.

Lyophilized cultures were shipped under IATA-compliant UN3373 (biological substance, Category B) conditions by Chulalongkorn University (Thailand). Laboratories were required to confirm receipt and perform testing within a designated timeframe. Technical support was provided throughout the process to ensure correct handling, storage and subculturing of the reference material. Participants were encouraged to use their routine laboratory methods, but reference protocols for media validation and phenotypic confirmation (e.g. double disk synergy test, combination disk test, cefoxitin resistance and carbapenem MIC) were made available for harmonization purposes. Laboratories were instructed to store samples in a cool, dry and dark environment until analysis.

Selective isolation and identification

Participants were instructed to perform selective isolation of resistant E. coli using MacConkey agar with 1 mg/L cefotaxime, following EURL-AR protocols. Species identification was carried out using each laboratory’s standard methodology, which could include biochemical tests, chromogenic media, MALDI-ToF MS or PCR. All participating laboratories submitted metadata on isolation and identification methods via the EQASIA informatics module.

AST

AST was conducted on one confirmed E. coli isolate per sample using the laboratory’s routine method (e.g. disk diffusion, broth microdilution, gradient diffusion). Antimicrobials were selected from a recommended panel covering clinically relevant drug classes, with priority agents including cephalosporins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones and colistin. Breakpoints for interpretation were based on CLSI (M100, 31–34th Ed.); where unavailable, EUCAST clinical breakpoints (v13.0, 2021–24) or epidemiological cut-off values were applied.

Confirmatory tests (e.g. clavulanic acid synergy) were included for phenotypic classification but not scored. Interpretation of presumptive resistance mechanisms followed EFSA guidelines.

Data collection and scoring system

A key component of the Matrix EQA was the use of a customized, secure online ‘One-Stop-Shop’ informatics module developed by DTU Food to manage all EQASIA EQAs. Laboratories were provided with individual login credentials to access the portal, which allowed for structured submission of data on selective isolation, species identification and AST interpretation. Participants also reported the specific methods and media used. Following submission, the module generated individualized performance reports comparing observed and expected results using a standardized four-point scoring system: 4 = correct, 3 = minor deviation, 1 = major deviation and 0 = very major deviation (Figure 2). In line with standard AST error definitions, reporting a resistant isolate as susceptible is classified as a very major error due to the risk of ineffective treatment, whereas reporting a susceptible isolate as resistant is considered a major error (Figure 2). AST interpretation (S/I/R) was scored, while MIC or zone diameter values were treated as Supplementary data (available as Supplementary data at JAC Online) (Table S1).8,15

Distribution of scores according to the reported results and their agreement with the baseline expected results.

In alignment with EURL and CLSI guidelines, no predefined thresholds were applied for pass/fail outcomes. Instead, a threshold of 95% performance score (5% deviation in the AST results compared to the baseline results) was set across each of the Matrix EQAs, to identify laboratories that need attention and capacity building in order to further improve their performance in EQAs and in general. In cases of substantial deviation, participants were encouraged to conduct internal root cause analyses and technical online consultation meetings were conducted with them. This feedback-oriented scoring system aimed to build long-term quality assurance capacity rather than rank laboratories.

Results

Participation and engagement

Between 2021 and 2024, a total of 21 laboratories from 10 countries in South and South-East Asia—Bangladesh, Bhutan, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Timor-Leste and Vietnam—participated in at least one of the four Matrix EQA rounds. Participating laboratories primarily represented national reference laboratories (NRLs) or designated sentinel laboratories involved in national AMR surveillance programmes across both the human health (HH) and animal health (AH) sectors (Figure 3). These participating laboratories represent a portion of the larger EQASIA network, which includes a total of 67 laboratories involved in various EQA initiatives across the region until 2024. Participation varied by year, with 9 laboratories (2 HH and 7 AH) in 2021, 5 laboratories (2 HH and 3 AH) in 2022, 9 laboratories (3 HH and 6 AH) in 2023 and 13 laboratories (5 HH and 8 AH) in 2024 (Figure 1). Only two laboratories (#22 and #40) maintained continuous participation across all four Matrix EQA rounds. One laboratory (#03) that participated in 2021 subsequently withdrew from the EQASIA programme due to logistical challenges.

Countries participating in the EQASIA Matrix EQAs between 2021 and 2024. Colour indicates sector affiliation of the participating laboratory as animal health laboratory (yellow), human health laboratory (blue) or both human and animal health laboratories (green).

Methodological approaches

The majority of participating laboratories across all four Matrix EQA rounds adhered closely to the recommended protocols for selective isolation of presumptive ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli. In 2021, eight of nine laboratories used the protocol without modifications, while one reported modification. This pattern was similar in subsequent years, with unmodified protocol adherence observed in 4 of 5 laboratories in 2022, 6 of 9 in 2023 and 10 of 13 in 2024. A small number of laboratories reported protocol modifications in each round (ranging from 1 to 3 laboratories per year). Selective isolation specifically targeting carbapenemase-producing E. coli was infrequently performed and was reported by only one laboratory in each round from 2021 to 2024. Species identification methods varied among laboratories, but the laboratories showed a general preference for traditional biochemical tests, which were employed by seven laboratories in 2021, five in 2022, six in 2023 and seven in 2024. Use of chromogenic media increased over time, reported by one laboratory in 2021, two in 2023 and three in 2024. MALDI-ToF was performed in 2023 (one laboratory) and used by two laboratories in 2024. PCR-based species confirmation was employed only once, by a single laboratory in 2024. Other identification methods were rare and included automated biochemical identification systems reported by only one laboratory in 2021.

Isolation and identification performance

All four Matrix EQA samples were designed to contain E. coli isolates expressing ESBL, AmpC, carbapenemase or combined ESBL + AmpC phenotypes. The accuracy of isolation and identification of these resistant E. coli varied across the Matrix EQA rounds. In 2021, four laboratories reported all samples as positive for presumptive resistant E. coli, but only one laboratory (#33) correctly identified all four samples as pure cultures of typical E. coli. In 2022, three laboratories (#01, #33 and #40) reported all samples as positive, with one laboratory (#05) reporting identification for only three samples; however, none correctly identified all four isolates. In 2023, four laboratories (#01, #27, #37 and #42) reported all samples as positive, but none achieved complete correct identification. In 2024, eight laboratories (#12, #14, #22, #33, #37, #40, #46 and #55) reported all samples as positive, with two laboratories (#40 and #55) correctly identifying all four isolates. Incorrect identification outcomes were primarily due to one of the following: reporting mixed cultures without typical E. coli colonies, mixed cultures containing typical E. coli colonies, pure cultures without typical E. coli morphology or absence of growth.

Overall, the proportion of laboratories obtaining results in accordance with the expected outcome fluctuated across samples and years, ranging from 20% to 100% (Figure 4). The highest agreement was observed for sample M1 in 2022 (100%), while the lowest was for M3 in 2022 (20%). These results highlight variability in laboratory proficiency for isolation and identification of presumptive resistant E. coli from complex matrices across the region.

Percentage of correct isolation and identification results across Matrix EQA rounds, 2021–24. The figure shows the percentage of laboratories reporting results in accordance with the expected outcome for each of the four Matrix EQA samples (M1–M4) in each round (2021–24). Each bar represents the proportion of participating laboratories that correctly identified E. coli isolates from spiked food-like matrices, reflecting their proficiency in isolation and species identification of ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli. Variability across years and samples illustrates differences in laboratory performance and highlights technical challenges associated with detection from complex matrices.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing performance

Participating laboratories submitted AST results for positive isolates. Disk diffusion was the predominant method across the 4 Matrix EQA rounds, while a few laboratories used the broth microdilution method for selected antimicrobials. The AST performance of participating laboratories was evaluated across four Matrix EQA rounds (2021–24), focusing on three analytical dimensions: sample-level deviation, antimicrobial-level deviation and laboratory-level deviation. Performance was assessed based on categorical interpretations (susceptible, intermediate or resistant; S/I/R), with deviations defined as any discrepancy from the expected interpretation derived from reference laboratory results.

At the sample level, deviation rates varied between test samples within and across rounds. Overall deviation per sample ranged from 2.2% to 26% across the four rounds. Sample M2 and M3 showed the highest deviation in 2023 and 2024 respectively, while sample M5 had the lowest deviation in 2022 (Figure 5).

Percentage of deviation in the AST interpretation (R/I/S) per sample in EQASIA Matrix over years.

At the antimicrobial level, deviation rates differed substantially between agents. Antimicrobials such as amikacin, ampicillin, cefotaxime, cefoxitin, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, doripenem, ertapenem, gentamicin, imipenem, levofloxacin, tetracycline, tigecycline and tobramycin demonstrate deviation rates below 10% at least across two rounds of EQAs, while others such as azithromycin, cefepime, ceftazidime, colistin, meropenem, nalidixic acid, piperacillin/tazobactam, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole exhibited deviation rates above 10%. The frequency and extent of deviations varied across rounds (Figure 6).

Percentage of deviation in the AST interpretation (R/I/S) among the E. coli strains used to spike the matrix samples in EQASIA Matrix EQA over years. Results are categorized according to antimicrobial agent.

At the laboratory level, individual laboratory performance ranged from 0.5% to 41.2% deviation per round, with seven laboratories achieving deviation rates below 5% in all rounds. Laboratories with higher deviation rates were identified in each round (Figure 7).

Percentage of deviation in the AST interpretation (R/I/S) among the E. coli strains used to spike the matrix samples in EQASIA Matrix over years. Results are categorized by laboratory ID numbers.

Phenotypic classification accuracy

Phenotypic classification performance varied across Matrix EQA rounds and among different resistance phenotypes. In 2021, correct classification rates were 66.7% for ESBL producers, 40% for AmpC producers and 33.3% for carbapenemase producers. In 2022, classification accuracy improved, with 100% correct for ESBL and carbapenemase producers, 75% for ESBL+AmpC producers and 33.3% for AmpC producers. In 2023, correct classification rates were 71.4% for ESBL producers, 80% for ESBL+AmpC producers, 50% for AmpC producers and 62.5% for carbapenemase producers. In 2024, performance was 88.9% for ESBL producers, 75% for AmpC producers, 75% for carbapenemase producers and 0% for ESBL+AmpC producers.

Overall, correct classification rates varied by phenotype and year, with the highest accuracy generally observed for ESBL and carbapenemase producers and the lowest for AmpC and ESBL+AmpC producers (Figure 8).

Percentage of obtained classification of ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli strains used to spike the matrix samples in accordance with the expected results.

QC performance

Reference strains from ATCC were provided free of charge to all participants until 2023, along with instructions for storage and maintenance to support internal quality assurance and future EQA participation. AST results for reference strain E. coli ATCC 25922 were submitted by all participating laboratories only in Matrix EQA 2024; deviations outside expected QC ranges were most frequent for ertapenem, doripenem and cefepime (Figure 9).

Percentage of deviation in the AST of the quality control strain E. coli ATCC 25922 by the laboratories across Matrix EQA rounds. Results are categorized by laboratory ID numbers.

Colistin susceptibility for E. coli NCTC 13846 was tested by three laboratories (one out of three outside the expected ranges) in 2021 and two laboratories (all within expected ranges) in 2024.

Discussion

The four Matrix EQA rounds conducted between 2021 and 2024 provided a regional assessment of laboratory capacity to detect ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli from complex food-like matrices across South and South-East Asia. The findings highlight both encouraging progress and ongoing challenges in laboratory performance, protocol adherence and surveillance readiness in line with the WHO Tricycle and GLASS frameworks.16,17

Participation across rounds reflected broad regional engagement, with higher and more consistent involvement from the AH sector. Information on barriers to participation was collected through routine communication with laboratories and EQASIA country coordinators, which commonly identified logistical constraints, limited reagent availability and competing laboratory priorities. Completion rates improved over time, despite recurring constraints related to access to selective media, confirmatory reagents and shipping delays. Most laboratories were able to submit complete data covering selective isolation, identification, AST and phenotypic classification. However, only two laboratories participated in all four rounds, and several others withdrew or engaged intermittently. This inconsistency, despite a supportive and learning-focused EQA approach, points to structural challenges such as limited prioritization of food-source surveillance, constrained resources and administrative hurdles that continue to limit sustained participation.13,18 Consequently, trends over time should be interpreted cautiously, as changes may reflect both evolving laboratory capacity and differences in participant composition rather than a fixed cohort.

Methodological variations were self-reported by laboratories through the EQASIA informatics platform, mainly in selective isolation steps, including substitution of cefotaxime disks for selective agar or adjustments in incubation temperature. These changes were often resource-driven and underscore the ongoing need for practical, flexible protocols that still maintain comparability. Selective isolation targeting carbapenemase-producing E. coli (CPE) was rarely performed, with only one laboratory per round reporting its use. Given the increasing emergence of CPE in both human and animal reservoirs, including terrestrial food animals, this represents a gap in laboratory awareness and capacity, particularly within the AH sector.16,17,19 However, protocol modifications were infrequent and heterogeneous; therefore, their impact on performance could not be systematically assessed.

Species identification methods remained largely dependent on conventional biochemical tests, with two reports of MALDI-ToF use across the four rounds. This limited uptake suggests barriers to advanced diagnostic adoption, including cost, infrastructure and technical expertise. Bridging this diagnostic gap will be essential to improving result accuracy and strengthening the microbiological foundation of national AMR surveillance systems.

Overall, a gradual improvement in laboratory performance was observed, but key challenges persisted. These included misidentification of mixed phenotypes (notably ESBL+AmpC), difficulties in colistin susceptibility testing and inconsistent use or interpretation of QC strains. Variation in isolation and identification accuracy was observed across samples and years. In some instances, all laboratories correctly identified target isolates; in others, particularly with more complex resistance phenotypes, performance dropped markedly. Common errors included misclassification due to mixed flora, absence of growth or incorrect colony selection. These findings suggest that even with well-characterized test material, routine isolation of resistant E. coli from food-like matrices remains technically demanding, particularly in low-resource settings, and may challenge implementation of the WHO Tricycle protocol.11

AST performance also varied substantially. Although 21 laboratories participated in at least one round, only 19 submitted AST datasets suitable for inclusion in the laboratory-level analysis shown in Figure 7. While several laboratories achieved high concordance, others showed persistent deviations across antimicrobial agents and EQA rounds. The number of participating laboratories and diversity of testing platforms did not allow a meaningful comparison of reliability between AST methodologies. Sample-level deviation rates ranged from 2.2% to over 25%, with no uniform trend in improvement across all participants. At the antimicrobial level, lower deviation rates (<10%) were observed for commonly used agents such as amikacin, cefotaxime, gentamicin and imipenem. Higher deviation rates were associated with agents like azithromycin, colistin, trimethoprim and piperacillin/tazobactam compounds often associated with interpretive difficulty, reagent variability or limited routine use. These trends align with prior findings from WHO GFN and EURL-AR EQAs and reinforce the need for strengthened guidance, quality-controlled reagents and targeted training.10,12

Phenotypic classification accuracy also varied by resistance mechanism. ESBL and carbapenemase producers were more reliably identified, while AmpC and combined ESBL + AmpC phenotypes were frequently misclassified, particularly in 2024, where no correct classifications were recorded for the ESBL + AmpC strain. These errors raise concerns about the reliability of phenotype-based surveillance and highlight the limitations of routine phenotypic testing without molecular confirmation.

QC performance was generally acceptable, though deviations were most common for ertapenem, doripenem and cefepime, known for technical complexity. Colistin susceptibility testing was rare; in 2021, only three laboratories conducted QC testing with E. coli NCTC 13846, with one result outside the acceptable range. By 2024, two laboratories performed the test, and both were within range. These trends suggest modest improvement in technical precision but continued limitations in test coverage and capacity. A formal correlation between QC deviations and routine isolate testing was not assessed due to the limited number of laboratories and variability in reporting completeness.

Collectively, the Matrix EQA served not only as a performance assessment tool but also as a mechanism for identifying critical gaps in the generation of reliable AMR data from food sources. The trials indirectly evaluated laboratory preparedness to implement Tricycle-aligned surveillance, while supporting broader goals of GLASS reporting. However, sustained improvements will require more than periodic testing. Addressing persistent issues in reagent access, confirmatory capacity and institutional commitment is essential to translate EQA insights into long-term capacity gains.

Future directions should include the expansion of the Matrix EQA to additional matrices (e.g. retail meat, clinical or environmental samples), greater standardization of phenotypic confirmation protocols and improved access to advanced methods such as whole-genome sequencing. National integration of EQA participation into AMR strategies and procurement systems, alongside continued regional coordination, will be critical for enabling laboratories in LMICs to contribute reliable, timely data to the global AMR response. In conclusion, the EQASIA Matrix EQA provided a coordinated regional mechanism to evaluate and strengthen laboratory capacity for detecting ESBL-, AmpC- and carbapenemase-producing E. coli from food-like matrices. Across four rounds, laboratories demonstrated progressive improvement; however, performance remained variable, including among NRLs, reflecting differences in infrastructure, resources and routine diagnostic capacity across sectors and countries. The low accuracy in isolating and identifying carbapenemase-producing E. coli (as low as 23.1% in 2024) is particularly alarming from a public health perspective. These organisms are often resistant to most, if not all, available antibiotics. A failure to detect them in a clinical or food sample means a missed opportunity to implement life-saving infection control measures and allows for the silent dissemination of these ‘superbugs’ within healthcare networks and the food chain.

Regular participation in EQA programmes is essential to ensure the reliability of surveillance data and the quality of diagnostic services. The Matrix EQA model is particularly relevant to the WHO Tricycle framework, which relies on consistent and accurate detection of ESBL-producing E. coli as a One Health indicator. By testing laboratories’ ability to isolate and characterize resistant E. coli from complex matrices, the Matrix EQA provides a practical measure of readiness to contribute high-quality data to Tricycle and GLASS reporting.

With the closure of the Fleming Fund regional programme, there is a substantial risk that recently strengthened laboratory capacity, especially for technically demanding procedures such as selective isolation and phenotypic mechanism characterization, may not be sustained. To preserve these gains, routine EQA participation must be embedded within national AMR strategies, supported by stable funding, coordinated technical assistance and access to validated materials and protocols.

Sustained regional collaboration and continued reinforcement of quality systems will be critical to ensure that laboratories remain able to detect high-priority resistance mechanisms and contribute meaningfully to One Health AMR surveillance efforts.
