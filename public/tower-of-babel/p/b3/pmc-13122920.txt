Association of serum uric acid and serum uric acid/creatinine ratio with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease in young adults: a retrospective study
Authors: Yue Li, Zixian Li, Siyu Wu, Ling Cao, Beibei Qian, Junxian Tao, Zhimin Qian, Li Xie, Xiaoli Cai, Xiaofang Sun, Ying Li, Zhenwen Zhang, Caifeng Yan, Dunmin She
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Metabolic dysfunction-associatedsteatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide. Its incidence continues to rise with a marked trend toward younger age groups, posing dual challenges for early screening and standardized diagnosis and treatment. There is an urgent clinical need for simple-to-operate, highly sensitive serological markers to enable early disease identification.Purpose This study aims to investigate the correlation between serum uric acid (SUA) and serum uric acid to creatinine ratio (SUA/Cr) levels in young adults and MASLD and its components.Methods A retrospective analysis was conducted on 5,224 individuals aged 20–45 years who underwent physical examinations at Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital in 2019. Measured the patient’s body mass index(BMI), systolic blood pressure(SBP), diastolic blood pressure(DBP), fasting blood glucose(FBG), creatinine(Cr), triglycerides(TG), total cholesterol(TC), serum uric acid(SUA), high-density lipoprotein(HDL-c) and other indicators. Logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the correlations among SUA, SUA/Cr, and the components of MASLD in the young adults while adjusting for confounding factors. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate discriminatory ability.Results The levels of SUA and SUA/Cr in the MASLD cohort were significantly higher than those in the non-MASLD cohort. Elevated SUA and SUA/Cr levels were associated with components for MASLD. Both SUA (OR: 1.014, 95% CI: 1.013–1.015) and SUA/Cr (OR: 2.570, 95% CI: 2.368–2.788) were significantly associated with MASLD (P < 0.001). The Area Under Curve(AUC) values were 0.807 and 0.724, respectively.Conclusion SUA and SUA/Cr levels are significantly associated with MASLD and its components in young adults.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, Serum uric acid, Serum uric acid/creatinine ratio

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is defined as fatty liver disease with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (such as prediabetes, type 2 diabetes, obesity, hypertension, or dyslipidemia), excluding other known causes of steatosis, and with low alcohol consumption [1]. MASLD has now become the most common cause of chronic liver disease worldwide, affecting up to 38% of the global adult population [2].

In recent years, the prevalence of MASLD has been steadily increasing due to unhealthy lifestyles and accelerated urbanization [3]. Hepatic steatosis affects approximately two-thirds of individuals with type 2 diabetes and elevates the risk of overall liver-related mortality [4]. A meta-analysis involving 116,975 adults indicated that hypertension was associated with approximately a 1.3-fold increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes over 15 years among adult patients with MASLD [5]. Additionally, a study analyzing data from 3,684 participants demonstrated a positive correlation between the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio and MASLD [6]. Most current MASLD research has focused on the elderly population [7]. with little attention paid to the association between MASLD and younger individuals. However, the imbalance between high caloric intake and insufficient physical activity is increasingly leading to metabolic disorders among young people [8], and MASLD is showing a trend toward affecting younger age groups. An analysis of data from 195 countries and territories revealed that the number of young people globally, particularly those under 50, living with MASLD increased by approximately 94% from 1990 to 2021 [9].In the Middle East and North Africa region, the prevalence of MASLD has increased by 13.8% over the past 30 years [10]. In China, over the past decade, the prevalence of MASLD among individuals under 45 has surpassed that of the elderly population [11]. In the United States, mortality rates from chronic liver disease due to MASLD are rising among residents aged 35–44, with rates of approximately 28 deaths per 100,000 men and 23 deaths per 100,000 women [12]. In addition to chronic liver disease, MASLD can significantly increase the risk of severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in young populations by accelerating arterial wall elasticity loss [13]. In addition, available epidemiological evidence suggests substantial geographic and ethnic variation in the prevalence of MASLD among young populations worldwide [14]. Population-based studies and meta-analyses have reported higher prevalence in North America, the Middle East, and parts of East and South Asia, while lower prevalence has been reported in certain regions of Africa and South America [15]. Beyond geographic differences, marked ethnic disparities have also been observed. MASLD prevalence is higher among Hispanic and South Asian youth compared with non-Hispanic White populations, whereas lower prevalence has been consistently reported among non-Hispanic Black youth [16]. These disparities likely reflect complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, lifestyle factors, body composition, and socioeconomic determinants. The increasing complexity and younger onset of MASLD pose significant challenges to current diagnosis and management. It is estimated that MASLD affects 30–35% of Americans, yet only 6% receive a diagnosis. Therefore, early identification of newly diagnosed MASLD and prompt initiation of treatment are urgently needed.

Serum uric acid(SUA)is a metabolic byproduct primarily synthesized by the liver, intestines, and vascular endothelium [17]. It serves both as the final metabolic product of dietary purines and as an endogenous substance produced during cellular damage and death within the body [18]. Although SUA is not currently defined as a component of MASLD, some studies indicate a strong correlation between elevated SUA levels and MASLD or its components [19]. Notably, SUA can directly induce hepatic steatosis through NLRP3-mediated inflammation, thereby increasing disease risk and potentially serving as the sixth cardiometabolic marker for diagnosing MASLD [20]. Given that renal clearance of SUA is typically influenced by renal function, SUA/Cr is considered a more accurate indicator of renal function status than SUA levels alone [21]. Therefore, we will use both SUA and SUA/Cr to predict correlation. The rising prevalence of MASLD among young adults in China underscores the critical importance of its early diagnosis in this demographic. Previous studies have established a link between SUA, SUA/Cr, and metabolic syndrome (METS) in obese populations [22, 23]; however, scant research has focused on the relationship between these biomarkers and MASLD specifically in the young adult population. The objective of this study is to investigate the correlation between MASLD and SUA, as well as SUA/Cr, in the youth population. Given the simplicity and convenience of measuring SUA and SUA/Cr, which can be performed in community hospitals, exploring the relationship between MASLD and these parameters in Chinese youth may hold significant practical value.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study included 5,224 healthy individuals aged 20–45 years (2,568 males and 2,656 females) who underwent physical examinations at Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital Affiliated with Yangzhou University Health Examination Center in 2019. All enrolled participants have been excluded if they have severe liver or kidney disease, malignant tumors, pregnancy, or other major systemic diseases. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Northern Jiangsu People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval: No.2022ky012).

Data collection

The following data were collected: sex, body height(BH, m), body weight (BW, kg), systolic blood pressure༈（SBP, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure(DBP, mmHg), and blood-related tests. Participants were measured for BH and BW wearing light clothing and without shoes. Blood pressure (BP) was measured three times by nurses according to relevant standards. Blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein after an 8-hour fast and immediately sent to the clinical laboratory of Jiangsu Provincial North People’s Hospital for processing. An automated biochemical analyzer (Cobas 8000; Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland): fasting blood glucose (FBG): 3.9–6.1 mmol/L (70–110 mg/dL), creatinine (Cr) [reference range: 44–133 µmol/L (0.5–1.5 mg/dL)], triglycerides (TG) [reference range: <1.7 mmol/L (< 150.5 mg/dL)], total cholesterol (TC) [reference range: <5.17 mmol/L (200 mg/dL)], serum uric acid (SUA) [reference range: 143–339 µmol/L (2.4–5.7 mg/dL)], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c) [reference range: 1.29–1.55 mmol/L (50–60 mg/dL)]. Fatty liver is diagnosed using abdominal ultrasound imaging modes based on findings such as increased liver echogenicity relative to the renal cortex, blurred intrahepatic vascular borders, and ultrasound attenuation. All imaging results are interpreted by sonographers who were blinded to laboratory data, and any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Definition

The BMI calculation formula was as follows: BMI (kg/m²) = weight (kg) / height² (m²). Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula: eGFR = 142 x min(Scr/κ, 1)α x max(Scr/κ, 1)-1.200 × 0.9938^Age x 1.012 [if female][ κ = 0.7 (females) or 0.9 (males); α= -0.241 (female) or -0.302 (male)]. MASLD was diagnosed according to the Asia-Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Metabolic-Associated Steatohepatitis [1]: Imaging evidence of hepatic steatosis plus at least one of the following five criteria: (1) BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² (≥ 23 kg/m² for Asians) or waist circumference > 94 cm in males, > 80 cm in females; (2) Fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL (≥ 5.6 mmol/L) or 2-hour postprandial glucose ≥ 140 mg/dL (≥ 7.8 mmol/L) or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7% or current antidiabetic medication use; (3) Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or current antihypertensive medication use; (4) Plasma triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL (≥ 1.7 mmol/L) or currently receiving lipid-lowering medication; (5) Serum high-density lipoprotein (HDL) < 40 mg/dL (< 1.0 mmol/L) in males or < 50 mg/dL (< 1.3 mmol/L) in females, or currently receiving lipid-lowering medication.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software. For quantitative data, normally distributed variables were described as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup differences assessed using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were described as quartiles and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were described as proportions and analyzed using chi-square tests. Pearson’s correlation analysis and logistic regression analysis were performed on statistically significant indicators to determine correlations between SUA, SUA/Cr, and MASLD and its components, plot the receiver operating characteristic curve(ROC ) and calculate the AUC value. Further compute the Youden index to determine the optimal cut-off value. To assess potential multicollinearity among covariates, variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics were calculated for all variables included in the regression models. Model calibration and goodness of fit were evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. SUA and SUA/Cr were divided into quartiles based on total population size, and appropriate statistical tests were applied to assess differences in central tendency between quartiles for SUA and SUA/Cr. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline measurements of BMI, SBP, DBP, FPG, TC, TG, LDL-C, ALT, AST, ALB, TBIL, DBIL, IBIL, SUA and SUA/Cr were also significantly higher in the MASLD group than in the non-MASLD group.

This association persisted after gender-specific analysis. In the predictive model, both SUA and SUA/Cr were associated with the prevalence of MASLD. SUA (OR: 1.014, 95% CI: 1.013–1.015; p < 0.001) and SUA/Cr (OR: 2.570, 95% CI: 2.368–2.788; p < 0.001). Results of the multiple logistic analysis of MASLD and SUA are shown in Table 4. After adjustment for age and sex, an independent association between SUA and MASLD remained. Ultimately, after adjustment for age, sex, ALB, TBIL, DBIL, and IBIL, the OR of occurrence of MASLD was 1.012 (95% CI: 1.011–1.013; p < 0.001). In Table 5, after adjustment for age and sex, an independent association between SUA/Cr and MASLD remained. Ultimately, after adjustment for age, sex, ALB, TBIL, DBIL, and IBIL, the OR of occurrence of MASLD was 2.564(95% CI: 2.343–2.805; p < 0.001). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed acceptable fit.

Table 6; Fig. 2 present the ROC curve analysis results for SUA and SUA/Cr in MASLD diagnosis. The areas under the ROC curves were 0.807 and 0.724, respectively, with corresponding p-values < 0.001, indicating that both indicators are statistically significant for MASLD diagnosis. The diagnostic threshold for SUA was 339.5, with sensitivity and specificity of 75.7% and 72.7%, respectively. The diagnostic threshold for SUA/Cr was 4.1535, with sensitivity and specificity of 71.4% and 62.3%, respectively.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between SUA, SUA/Cr, and MASLD and its components in a sample of young population in China. We conducted an analysis of medical examination data from 5,224 Chinese young population aged 20–45. Our study found that the prevalence of MASLD among young examinees in the Yangzhou region was 25.65%, with a prevalence rate of 42.99% among young males and 8.89% among young females. SUA and SUA/Cr levels were closely associated with MASLD in this young population. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the relationship between SUA, SUA/Cr, and MASLD in the young population.

The prevalence of MASLD is steadily increasing, emerging as a prominent health concern. Among the known drivers of MASLD, obesity, hypertension, elevated FPG, reduced HDL-C, and elevated TG promote the development of MASLD through distinct mechanisms. Obesity induces adipose tissue dysfunction by causing adipocyte hypertrophy, leading to insulin resistance, inflammation, and adipokine imbalance, thereby promoting excessive release of nonesterified fatty acids (NEFA) [24]. Hypertension activates the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) and sympathetic nervous system (SNS), leading to adipose tissue inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [25]. Elevated FPG activates carbohydrate responsive element binding protein (ChREBP), accelerating de novo lipogenesis (DNL) and synergistically upregulating sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c(SREBP-1c) in the context of hyperinsulinemia. This promotes hepatic triglyceride accumulation and induces lipotoxicity [26]. The decrease in HDL-C weakens its cholesterol-reversing, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cell-protective functions, leading to lipid accumulation in hepatocytes and exacerbated inflammatory responses [27]. Elevated blood triglycerides induce lipotoxicity and inflammatory responses by increasing hepatic free fatty acid uptake, promoting hepatic triglyceride re-esterification, and facilitating lipid droplet accumulation [28].

SUA has become a prominent health issue in daily life. Regarding the relationship between SUA and MASLD, some scholars suggest that elevated SUA levels may increase insulin resistance through multiple pathways. Under conditions of insulin resistance, further increases in SUA levels directly promote hepatic steatosis in individuals with hyperuricemia [29]. Beyond its traditional interpretation as a metabolic or renal marker, SUA is increasingly discussed in the context of oxidative stress, inflammation, and redox homeostasis [30, 31]. SUA is the primary endogenous antioxidant in human plasma. However, when oxidative stress persists and increases, xanthine oxidase (XO) activity intensifies, leading to heightened SUA synthesis. Once this exceeds the antioxidant threshold, SUA transforms into a pro-oxidant, inducing NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β release. This process subsequently impairs hepatic energy metabolism and promotes lipid accumulation within the liver [32]. Thus, a vicious cycle of mutual reinforcement develops among SUA, insulin resistance, hepatic steatosis, and oxidative stress [33]. Emerging evidence suggests that SUA may indirectly influence hepatic metabolism through modulation of the gut–liver axis. Elevated SUA levels have been associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by alterations in microbial composition and reduced microbial diversity. Such dysbiosis may impair intestinal barrier integrity, leading to increased gut permeability. Consequently, bacterial-derived endotoxins, particularly lipopolysaccharide (LPS), can translocate into the portal circulation and reach the liver. Hepatic exposure to LPS may activate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)–mediated signaling pathways, including nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), thereby promoting inflammatory responses and contributing to hepatic lipid accumulation [34]. Chang investigated the association between SUA and MASLD defined by ultrasound elastography(USE) parameters, concluding that a linear relationship exists between SUA and MASLD risk [35]. Results from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey indicate that SUA is positively correlated with MASLD and all-cause mortality, serving as an independent predictor of MASLD [36]. Furthermore, in our study, the prevalence of MASLD was significantly higher among young men than women, and they also tended to have higher levels of SUA. Indeed, current research indicates that the occurrence of MASLD exhibits significant gender differences [37]. From a biological perspective, sex hormones influence uric acid metabolism, renal uric acid excretion, and fat distribution. Estrogen promotes uric acid clearance and offers protective effects against hepatic lipid deposition and inflammation; conversely, androgen-related metabolic characteristics may contribute to higher serum uric acid levels and earlier onset of MASLD in males [38, 39]. These mechanisms may partially explain the stronger association observed in males and underscore the necessity for gender-stratified analyses in future studies.

Cr may be influenced by muscle mass, metabolic activity, and systemic redox balance. Therefore, the SUA/Cr ratio may represent an integrated biomarker reflecting oxidative and inflammatory status [40]. Given that changes in renal function may affect SUA excretion, SUA/Cr provides a more accurate representation of endogenous SUA levels [17]. In Japan, a study involving 9,104 subjects who underwent annual health examinations and were not receiving medication indicated that SUA/Cr may serve as a useful marker for distinguishing non-drinking subjects with high insulin resistance (IR) from those with dyslipidemia [41]. A 4-year retrospective cohort study involving 5,107 individuals undergoing health examinations indicated that SUA/Cr is closely associated with new-onset MASLD [42]. A study involving 228 subjects found that elevated SUA/Cr levels correlated with lower liver CT values (β = -0.133, P = 0.039) and lower liver-to-spleen CT ratios (β = -0.154, P = 0.016). SUA/Cr demonstrated the ability to distinguish MAFLD severity (AUC: 0.623) [43]. A separate study using transient elastography measured controlled attenuation parameters (CAP) in a total of 34,981 individuals and followed up 2,179 individuals without MASLD. It concluded that elevated SUA/Cr levels correlate with the severity of hepatic steatosis in MASLD and constitute an independent risk factor for MASLD [44]. In summary, although increasing recognition exists that SUA and SUA/Cr are factors associated with metabolic diseases related to MASLD, few studies have specifically investigated the relationship between SUA, SUA/Cr, and MASLD in the adolescent population. This study is the first to demonstrate the association between SUA and SUA/Cr with MASLD in a young population. Given that individuals diagnosed with MASLD before age 45 exhibit a significantly elevated risk of developing gastrointestinal tumors and lung cancer [45, 46], early screening and intervention have become critical for reducing the cancer burden [47]. Therefore, incorporating SUA and SUA/Cr into youth health screening metrics holds promise for enabling early diagnosis and treatment of MASLD, thereby halting precancerous lesions at their source. Beyond its statistical association with MASLD, SUA/Cr also has some advantages. SUA/Cr demonstrates notable potential for clinical translation. Both SUA and Cr are routinely measured in standard biochemical panels, making SUA/Cr a cost-effective, readily available, and easily reproducible index that does not require additional testing burden [48]. This feature is particularly advantageous for large-scale screening and risk stratification in general populations.

In the context of existing MASLD screening strategies, several commonly used markers have inherent limitations that may restrict their applicability in large-scale or early-stage risk identification, particularly among young and asymptomatic populations. The controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) has high professional requirements and testing costs [49], and liver enzymes are prone to interference [50]. Composite inflammatory markers, such as the hs-CRP/HDL-C ratio, reflect inflammatory status but are not specific indicators of hepatic fat accumulation. In contrast, SUA and SUA/Cr are routinely measured, low-cost laboratory parameters that impose minimal additional burden on clinical workflows. Importantly, SUA/Cr partially accounts for interindividual variability related to renal function and body composition, thereby offering a more stable surrogate of endogenous uric acid burden [51]. Beyond convenience and cost-effectiveness, uric acid–related markers may capture early metabolic disturbances and redox imbalance associated with insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation [52]. Accordingly, SUA and SUA/Cr can serve as complementary indicators to enhance the feasibility of early risk stratification in large-scale and resource-limited settings.

In recent years, MASLD has become a focal point for researchers, with an increasing number of studies turning their attention to this field. Alarmingly, the prevalence of illness among young people is quietly on the rise, drawing widespread attention. Currently, several studies have indicated a strong association between MASLD and young adults. As a primary driver of CLD, studies indicate that the 15–29 age group accounts for 17.2% (290 million) of MASLD cases, 11.2% (n = 232,072) of CLD incidence cases, and 3.8% (n = 55,515) of CLD mortality cases [53]. Jin et al. also found that younger patients (aged 20–50 years) under conditions of hyperglycemia and hypertension had a significantly higher risk of MASLD progression compared to older individuals (HR = 2.08 vs. 1.47) [54]. This study, based on a survey of young adults (aged 20–45), suggests that higher levels of SUA and SUA/Cr are more likely to lead to MASLD. Compared to cardiac metabolic parameters such as HOMA-IR and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, SUA and SUA/Cr do not require complex testing and can be widely adopted at the primary care level [55]. If incorporated into existing diagnostic systems, they could enable “risk re-stratification” during youth, significantly enhancing the efficiency of comprehensive management throughout the disease course. However, its clinical incremental benefits still require ongoing validation through multicenter, prospective studies before they can be fully implemented in practice [56].

In summary, our findings indicate a positive correlation between SUA and SUA/Cr levels in young Chinese adults and their risk of developing MASLD. Both SUA and SUA/Cr levels can indeed serve as risk factors for MASLD in this population. Therefore, clinicians should monitor SUA and SUA/Cr levels in the general population, taking into account the presence of hepatic steatosis, and implement appropriate interventions in a timely manner. The incorporation of new auxiliary diagnostic criteria could broaden treatment approaches for MASLD. For instance, studies indicate that inhibiting xanthine oxidase (XO) activity significantly reduces uric acid production in HepG2 cells and lowers FFA-induced fat accumulation levels [57], thereby preventing hepatic steatosis and improving MASLD [58]. Currently, XO inhibitors have emerged as therapeutic targets for MASLD [59]. Future research will focus on optimizing management strategies for patients with varying degrees of disease severity and further refining the feasibility of treatment approaches.

The primary strength of this study is its large sample size. However, limitations exist. First, the diagnosis of fatty liver did not involve liver biopsy or elastography; it was based on ultrasound imaging which may be subject to misclassification. Second, given the cross-sectional design of this study, causal relationships and temporal sequencing between SUA, SUA/Cr and MASLD cannot be established, and reverse causation cannot be excluded. Prospective cohort studies and predictive modeling are required to clarify temporal relationships and to determine whether these biomarkers have clinical value in risk prediction or early identification of MASLD. Third, we were unable to control for other lifestyle-related variables, such as dietary habits, alcohol consumption history and smoking, which may represent confounding factors. Fourth, the study population was derived from a single-center health check-up cohort, limiting their generalizability.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that compared with young individuals without MASLD, SUA and SUA/Cr levels were significantly higher in those with MASLD. SUA and SUA/Cr levels are associated with MASLD and its components. Given the low cost, wide availability, and routine inclusion of SUA and SUA/Cr in community health screenings, their use may be considered for preliminary risk stratification in large-scale health screening populations or individuals with metabolic risk factors. Currently, multiple drug therapies for MASLD have been developed. Future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm the temporal relationship and evaluate their potential role in risk stratification. External validation of these findings across multicenter studies and diverse populations is required to enhance their generalizability.
